## 12. The Second Storm

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday, and by Thursday the gulls had gone inland.

Ansel Thackeray noticed the birds first. They came off the water in loose, anxious skeins, wheeling over the town without settling anywhere — gulls do not abandon the sea for nothing — and he stood in the doorway of the lighthouse with his coat collar turned up and watched them pass overhead like a rumor of trouble traveling south. Maren had taught him that in their first winter here, when he was still a mainland man learning the grammar of the coast. *Gulls are honest*, she'd said, her hands buried in her sleeves against the wind. *They know a lie before it's told.* The barometer confirmed what the birds already knew: the glass had sunk so low that the brass scale seemed embarrassed to show it.

By dusk on Thursday the horizon had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind arrived not as a gust but as a presence, a thing that leaned against the tower and stayed.

He climbed the stairs at half past four, an hour early, because his hands wanted something to hold and there was nothing else. The routine was older than grief now, worn smooth as a stair stone: trim the wick, wind the clockwork, polish the lens until the great drum of glass threw its long white arm out over the water. He spoke aloud as he worked, though there was no one to hear. He had never quite stopped doing that — narrating his tasks to the empty gallery, to Maren, to whoever kept house among the shadows when he wasn't looking. *Wind's rising from the southeast. Glass at twenty-eight and falling. We'll have a night of it.*

The first storm, back in October, had come out of the west and spent itself in a night of noise. This one was different. This one came up from the south like something summoned, and it did not spend itself. It gathered.

By nine o'clock the sea had turned white.

Not gray, not black — white, all the way to the horizon, the whole surface of the bay torn into driven foam so that the distinction between water and air dissolved entirely, and the light swept across a world without edges. Ansel stood in the lamp room with his palm flat against the cold glass and felt the tower breathe under him, a slow flexing of masonry and iron, the structure taking each wave of wind the way a man takes a blow — braced, absorbing, holding. The light went round and round, six seconds dark, two seconds bright, six seconds dark, and every time it swung its beam across that churning whiteness he found himself counting, without meaning to, the things it might be shining for. Boats. Men. Names.

The ledger was gone — confiscated in November, locked in Captain Marsh's strongroom with its names filed away as evidence of unregistered persons — but the reconstructed pages sat downstairs on his table, forty-one entries written in his own hand by lamplight after his shifts, and he had learned something in the writing of them that he could not unlearn: that remembering was not sentiment. It was work. It was a kind of seamanship, keeping souls aboard by keeping their names.

At eleven, the sound began.

He heard it before he understood it — a change in the wind's voice, a new note threaded through the roar, high and thin and wrong. It took him a full minute to place it, because he had never heard it from this shore before, and then his stomach dropped through the floor of the tower.

It was a whistle. A steam whistle, sounding over and over, three short blasts, pause, three short blasts, thin as a needle and frayed at every edge, coming from somewhere out in all that white.

Three shorts. *We are making no way. We need assistance.*

* * *

Ansel came down the stairs three at a time with the lantern swinging wild in his fist and crossed the yard to the garrison barracks through rain that struck sideways and hurt. The sentry at the door had his rifle slung and both hands jammed in his pockets and the particular misery of a soldier assigned to weather.

"Boat," Ansel shouted over the wind. "Out there. Do you hear it?"

The sentry listened, and his face changed. "That'll be the *Wren*."

"Then she's in trouble. Get your officers."

What happened next happened with the terrible slowness of institutions. Lights bloomed in the barracks windows one by one. Men stumbled out into the yard in greatcoats buttoned wrong. A runner was dispatched; a runner returned; there was discussion, visible even at distance as shapes gesturing under a swaying lantern, about signals, about procedure, about whose authority extended to what. Ansel stood in the rain at the edge of the garrison's little kingdom and felt something old and cold move in his chest — not fear, not yet, but the recognition of a shape he knew. He had seen men die of delay before. The sea did not wait on paperwork.

Finally Captain Marsh came out of the headquarters building at a run, bareheaded, her hair plastered dark against her face, and her voice cut through the yard like the whistle out at sea.

"The *Wren*'s aground or disabled somewhere off the point. She has eleven aboard — eight crew and three passengers, including Lieutenant Hargreaves." A beat. "And the December pay chest."

Someone actually laughed at that, briefly, horribly. Marsh didn't look at him.

"Can we launch anything?" That was the sergeant, Corrigan, shouting into the wind.

"No." It was Ansel who answered, stepping forward into the lantern light, and every face in the yard turned to him — the civilians' faces with resignation, the soldiers' with the sudden attention of men realizing the ground had shifted. "No boat launched from this shore tonight comes back. You've got no harbor, no slipway, no crews that know this water, and the sea will take anything you put on it inside thirty yards of the beach. There is one thing you can do for those men, and only one."

Marsh looked at him. Rain ran off her chin. "Say it."

"Burn coal in the lamp room and keep the light true. If they can't see the point, they're dead. If they can see it, they've got a chance to hold off, or to run for the lee side of the headland where your boat might live till morning. Everything depends on whether they know where the land is." His voice cracked once, and he mastered it. "I keep the light. But I'll need hands. Coal, oil, my stores are low and the clockwork will want winding twice tonight, and I'm sixty-one years old."

There was a silence of perhaps two seconds, in which the entire question of what these men were doing on this coast was asked and answered by nobody, and then Marsh said, quietly, in a voice meant for him alone: "You'll have every hand you want."

* * *

The night that followed, Ansel would remember afterward not as a sequence of events but as a single unbroken act, the way a drowning man remembers water.

The wind rose past anything in his experience of the point. At midnight the anemometer's cups were spinning too fast to read, and the rain stopped being rain and became the sea flung upward, so that walking from the tower to the coal shed — eighty yards, with four soldiers carrying buckets — was an immersion rather than a journey. The tower shook. Not breathed now but shook, groaning at certain frequencies like something alive being hurt, and twice the whole structure lurched hard enough to stagger him where he stood on the gallery stairs.

But the light burned.

It burned because Ansel trimmed the wick with hands that had done it ten thousand times, and because Private Dunmore, a boy of nineteen with a face green as weed, hauled coal up ninety-three steps without stopping, and because Corporal Vane held the lantern steady while Ansel worked the clockwork, and because Sergeant Corrigan — who had served on gunboats and knew what a sea could do — stood at the gallery rail with a telescope pressed to his eye, sweeping the white chaos for any sign.

"I've got her!" Corrigan roared at half past one. "Off the south shoal — masthead light — she's driving!"

Ansel took the telescope. Between sweeps of the lamp he caught her: a smudge of darkness in the whiteness, a mast leaning at an angle no mast should lean, and around her the foam breaking in long combers. She was dragging. Whatever anchor held her was chewing along the bottom of the bay, and the bottom of the bay ended in the shoal, and beyond the shoal was the point, and the point was rock.

"They can see us," he said. "They're holding off. Good." And then, because there was nothing else in the world to do: "More coal."

Through the small hours the *Wren* fought her anchor and the tower fought the wind and Ansel fought the lamp, and the three struggles were really one struggle, conducted at different ends of the same darkness. Twice the light guttered when spray drove up through the ventilators and he had to shield the burner with his own body, kneeling in front of the great lens with his coat spread like a wing while the salt water ran down his neck. Once the wind tore the gallery door open with a bang like artillery and Corrigan and two soldiers threw themselves against it to close it again. Once — the moment he would carry longest — the *Wren*'s whistle sounded four blasts instead of three, and he did not know the signal, and for ten seconds he thought it meant they were going down.

It meant, Corrigan translated later, hoarse with shouting, *we are drifting toward danger.*

"Well," Ansel said, "they've read the situation correctly," and went back to the wick.

Toward dawn the wind began, very gradually, to lose its argument. It did not stop — storms at this latitude rarely stop, they sulk and shift and renew the quarrel — but the shriek dropped to a roar, the roar to a mutter, and the eastern sky lightened from ink to iron to the pale washed gray of old linen. And there, in the lee of the headland, riding out the slack water with her anchor finally biting and her sails reduced to ribbons, lay the *Wren* — battered, dismasted by half, but whole. Eleven men crowded her deck. Eleven faces turned toward the lighthouse as the sun came up behind the clouds, and even at that distance Ansel could see them raise their arms, all together, in a gesture that needed no flag code.

In the lamp room, Private Dunmore sat down suddenly on the coal sacks and wept, without shame, the way boys cry. Sergeant Corrigan looked at the wrecked sea, and then at Ansel, and said nothing at all, which from Corrigan was an oration.

* * *

They came down the tower stairs in the gray morning, and the yard was full of soldiers who had spent the night watching the light from below, and the way those men looked at him was a thing Ansel felt on his skin like weather. He was the man who had been requisitioned. He was the widower whose ledger sat locked in the strongroom as evidence. He was the obstacle on the point, the irregularity in the survey, the name on the eviction order. And he had just spent seven hours keeping eleven soldiers alive with a lamp and two hands.

Lieutenant Hargreaves, wrung out and salt-stiff, waded ashore from the gig that brought the *Wren*'s boat party in, crossed the yard, and stopped in front of Ansel with the awkward intensity of a man who had rehearsed a speech during a shipwreck and found it all gone.

"We'd have gone on the shoal," he said simply. "Every man of us. We couldn't see anything. Then your light — " He stopped. Started again. "Thank you."

Ansel nodded. His throat was too full of salt and sleep to manage more.

Captain Marsh stood apart, near the headquarters door, and she did not come to him and she did not speak. But she looked at him for a long moment across the ruined morning — at the keeper soaked to the bone, coal-black to the elbows, sixty-one years old, standing straighter than any man in her command — and something passed over her face that Ansel recognized, because he had seen it in mirrors for two years. It was the look of a person revising an account. Recalculating a sum they had believed settled.

She turned and went inside, and the door closed behind her.

The debt was plain now, undeniable as the sunrise: the army owed its dead-not-dead to the man it had come to displace. Ansel understood, with the tired clarity of a man who has been awake for twenty hours, that such debts are never allowed to stand. Institutions pay them or bury them. And he suspected — watching the headquarters windows, where her silhouette moved behind the frosted glass — that within a day, perhaps within hours, Elowen Marsh would come to him with the price in her hand.

He went upstairs to check the mechanism, and found, when he got there, that his legs were shaking, and let them.

Below, the sea was already calming itself into innocence, and the *Wren* rode at anchor in the bay like a patient animal, and somewhere in the strongroom under the captain's lock sat a book of names that was about to matter far more than any of them yet knew.
