## 14. Sabotage or Salvage

The frost came in off the sea that morning like a creditor, settling on everything the tide had left and everything it hadn't. Ansel Thackeray felt it first in his knuckles, which had never been the same since the winter Maren died — a deep, arthritic honesty in them that no almanac could match. He stood at the window of the workshop with his palms flat against the cold glass and watched the light come up grey over the point, and for the first time in weeks he did not go straight to the timber.

He made himself wait. That was the discipline now: not the sawing, not the fitting, but the waiting. Maren had been the patient one. She had planed a board until it sang and then set it aside for a day to see if it remembered its shape in the morning. *Wood has opinions,* she used to say, blowing a curl of shaving from her sleeve. *You don't argue with wood. You give it time to change its mind.*

So he waited while his tea went lukewarm, and he looked out at the half-built thing that had grown along the rocks below the workshop like something living — the footbridge's spine now reaching eleven feet toward the mainland path, its skeleton of salt-bleached oak held together with trenails and stubbornness and, in two joints that still shamed him, his own impatience. Eleven feet of thirty. It was not much to look at yet. It was everything to look at.

Behind him, on the workbench, under an oilcloth and then under a sacking apron and then under a false lid of old lighthouse stores — spare wicks, a rusted fog-bell hammer, three tins of paint gone to skin — lay the ledger. Not the ledger itself, of course; the army had that, locked in Captain Marsh's quarters or already crated for the transport south. This was the second ledger, the one built from memory in ninety-one nights of lamplight, its pages soft-cornered from handling, its ink sometimes brown where he'd stretched the bottle too far. Two hundred and six names restored. Forty-one still missing, hovering somewhere in the fog of his mind like ships that hadn't yet made harbor.

He had taken to saying good morning to it. He did not say it aloud — a man who talks to books alone in a workshop is one step from talking to gulls — but he touched the oilcloth as he passed, the way you'd touch a doorpost leaving a house.

Then he went up the tower and lit the lamp and put the night behind him, because the light did not care about bridges, and neither, for those hours, did he.

* * *

It happened because of the sergeant.

Ansel had learned the garrison's rhythms the way he learned weather — by watching, by counting, by noticing what changed. The sentries changed at six and at two. The supply wagon came Thursdays. And Sergeant Brody Quill, a broad red-faced man out of some inland county where the tallest thing was a hedgerow, took his morning walk along the cliff path every day at half past seven, boots ringing on the frozen ground, breath steaming like a locomotive in miniature. Quill was not cruel. Quill was not clever. Quill was thorough, which in a soldier is often more dangerous than either.

That Thursday, the frost had hidden the path's edge, and Quill — whether by accident or by some animal instinct for the unexplained — stopped at the bend above the cove. Ansel, coming down from the tower with his coat collar up, saw him stop. Saw him lean. Saw him stand very still for the length of ten heartbeats, looking down at the rocks where the bridge frame rose out of the morning shadow like the ribs of something beached.

Ansel did not run. Running would have been a confession. He walked, evenly, the way a man walks when he has nothing in the world to hide, and he was still forty yards off when Quill turned, and even at that distance Ansel could see the transformation come over the sergeant's face — the slow sunrise of self-importance, the dawning certainty that this morning, at last, contained something that mattered.

"Thackeray." Quill's voice carried flat across the cold air. "What in God's name is that?"

"That," said Ansel, "is my workshop below."

"You know damned well I can see it's not a workshop. There's a structure on the shore. Timberwork. Beams and uprights." Quill came toward him, and his breath came with him, cloud after cloud. "Unauthorized construction within the requisitioned perimeter. Do you know what that is, keeper? That is Article Fourteen. That is sabotage of works undertaken for the defense of the realm."

"It's a footbridge," Ansel said. "Forty feet of it, when it's done. My wife drew the plans. It goes to the mainland path."

"Your wife is dead." Quill said it without malice, which somehow made it worse, the way a stone is worse than a blade because it doesn't know what it's doing. "I've read the file. And dead women don't build bridges."

Ansel felt the old cold move through him, colder than the morning, the cold that lived under his ribs since the night he'd closed Maren's eyes. He kept his voice level because a level voice was the only weapon they couldn't confiscate.

"No," he said. "They don't. They leave them to be finished."

Quill looked at him a long moment, and there was something in the look that wasn't entirely the sergeant's native dullness — a flicker of uncertainty, as if he had stepped on ground that sounded hollow and wasn't sure yet whether it was a cellar or a grave. Then the training reasserted itself, the comfort of procedure closing over doubt like water over a dropped coin.

"You'll come with me to the captain," he said. "Now. And nobody touches that timber until it's been seen to."

* * *

They walked him through the town, and Ansel hated that most of all — not the grip on his arm, which was perfunctory, a gesture rather than a restraint, but the walking. Past the Harrow Inn where Tam Osei stood in the doorway with a mug halfway to his mouth. Past the chandlery where Widow Fenn was scraping ice from her window with the flat of a knife, and her eyes followed him the whole length of the street, and her hand never stopped moving, because a woman who has buried two fishermen learns early that grief and labor must share a body. Past the notice boards with their white requisition papers going grey at the edges, curling like leaves in a season that hadn't earned them.

Word would reach the town by noon. By noon they would all know the keeper had been marched through his own streets under a soldier's hand, and by evening there would be a version of it told in every kitchen, and the version would grow hands and feet until it became a story about all of them. Ansel found, to his surprise, that this thought steadied him rather than frightened him. Let them tell it. A story that belongs to everyone is hard to tear down. He had learned that from a book of names.

At the garrison headquarters — the old customs house, commandeered, its brass fittings still bright under the army's neglect — they put him in a chair in the passage and left him. The chair was Windsor-made, local, probably bought off some family now scattered inland by the requisition, and Ansel sat in it and felt the ghosts of the household all around him in the wallpaper's faded roses and the worn hollows in the floorboards. The whole coast was haunted now, and not only by the drowned.

He waited an hour. He counted it by the clock on the mantel of the empty office opposite, whose tick came through the door in small dry increments, each one a plank he would not lay today.

When the door at the end of the passage opened, it was not Captain Marsh.

It was a lieutenant he knew by sight — Pell, young, conscientious, the sort of officer who ironed his greatcoat — and behind him two privates, and behind them the machinery of procedure moving on greased wheels. Lieutenant Pell had a paper in his hand and did not quite meet Ansel's eye.

"Ansel Thackeray," he read, "you are detained pending investigation into unauthorized construction on the shoreline of the point, contrary to Article Fourteen of the Requisition Order. The premises known as the lighthouse workshop are hereby sealed. No person may enter or remove materials therein until the investigation is concluded."

"And who conducts the investigation?" Ansel asked. His voice came out quieter than he intended, and in the quiet he heard the real question underneath it: *is it her?*

Pell glanced down the passage, toward the stairs, toward the rooms above where Elowen Marsh kept her maps and her lists and, Ansel knew, at least one page of Maren's handwriting pressed somewhere among the army's paperwork like a flower in a Bible.

"The captain will conduct it," Pell said. "She's been notified."

* * *

They sealed the workshop with him standing outside it, which was a cruelty precise enough to seem deliberate, though Ansel believed afterward that it was only efficiency. A corporal drove nails into the doorframe while another strung cord and fixed a printed notice to it — SEALED BY ORDER, date, signature — and Ansel watched the little ceremony with the detached attention of a man watching his own funeral procession pass a window.

The nails went in with a sound like knuckles cracking. Each one was an argument he was losing. Behind that door lay forty-one names he could not finish retrieving, and eleven feet of bridge he could not defend, and Maren's original drawings folded in the drawer of the bench — her hand, her compass-work, the little annotations in the margins where she'd argued with herself about load and span. *Wind shear at the third piling,* she'd written, and beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *A. will say it's too much timber. A. will be wrong.* He had laughed aloud the first time he found that, alone in the workshop at midnight, laughter coming out of him like something exhumed.

All of it behind a door full of nails.

He understood, standing in the frost with his hands in his pockets so no one would see what they wanted to do, that this was how it would be lost. Not in one grand storm, not in fire or flood, but in paper and procedure — a seal, a hearing, a form filled out in triplicate, and then the demolition detail arriving on an ordinary Tuesday with their crowbars, and the whole fragile architecture of memory coming down to be burned as scrap because no form existed anywhere that gave it a name worth saving.

He thought: *an hour.* That was all it would take. One hour with a crowbar and the bridge frame on the rocks would follow the workshop. The tide would take the oak out to sea, and the sea, which kept everything, keeps nothing you can hold.

The despair came on him then, finally, standing in front of the sealed door, and it did not come like a wave. It came like the tide itself — patient, level, covering things he had thought were above its reach. He had rebuilt the ledger name by name through ninety-one nights because grief needed somewhere to go, and now grief stood in the road with nowhere to go at all, and the clock on the mantel back at headquarters went on ticking in its small dry voice, ticking through the hours in which everything he loved could be made to disappear lawfully, in daylight, by men who were not evil, which was the worst of it. Quill would sleep soundly tonight. Pell would iron his greatcoat. The forms would be filed correctly.

And somewhere above him, behind a window in the customs house, Captain Elowen Marsh sat with the power to make it otherwise, and Ansel did not know — could not know — whether the woman who had let him go once without reporting him, whose face by lamplight in the interrogation room had carried something that was not yet mercy but might have been on its way, would sign the order or burn it.

* * *

They put him in a room, not a cell — the distinction mattered to someone in the quartermaster's chain, and Ansel blessed whatever pedant it was. A bed, a stool, a window with a view of the parade ground and, beyond it, the tower, still standing, its gallery rail furred with frost. The lamp was dark in the daylight, as lamps are, but he watched it anyway the way you watch a sleeping child, checking the rise and fall of a thing you cannot afford to lose.

Evening came down over the water the color of pewter. Somewhere below, the garrison ate; he heard the clatter of mess tins, a snatch of a song from the ranks, laughter with that particular edge men put on laughter when the wind is rising and they'd rather not think about it. The barometer at headquarters — he'd seen it in the passage, a good instrument, aneroid, brass-cased — had been falling all afternoon. He had noted it without meaning to, the way a man notes the smell of smoke. Thirty years on this point had given him instruments no case could hold. The swell was coming in long and wrong. The gulls had gone silent over the cove by four o'clock. Whatever was building out past the shelf, it was building slowly, and it was large, and it did not care about articles or orders or hearings.

He lay on the bed with his coat over him and did not sleep. He named the missing names instead, silently, in order, the way a shepherd counts sheep in a storm — not because it helps the sheep but because counting is a kind of faith. Aldous Renn. The twin Coram boys, lost in '81. The Dutch pilot whose name he had nearly, nearly reached twice and lost again, a name shaped like a wave breaking. Forty-one. Forty-one souls in a drawer under oilcloth behind a nailed door, waiting on the conscience of one soldier.

Near midnight the boots started in the corridor — not the sentry's measured round, but quicker, several pairs, voices pitched low and urgent. A door banged. Someone called for the duty signalman. Ansel sat up in the dark, and his ears, tuned by decades of night watches to the grammar of emergency, sorted the sounds: running feet, the storeroom unlocking, someone shouting about boats, about the moorings, about the *Larkspur* — the garrison's own supply boat, out since Tuesday, overdue since dusk.

And beneath it all, faint but gathering, the sound of the sea changing its mind — the long drag and surge of a swell standing up off the shelf, the prologue of a storm that would make the October gale look like weather's rough draft.

His window rattled in its frame. Out on the point, the tower stood black against the flying scud, and Ansel Thackeray, detained, sealed away from his lamp and his ledger and his wife's last work, pressed his hands to the cold glass and understood with terrible clarity what the next hours were going to ask of everyone on this coast — and that the hearing he was owed might arrive wearing a very different face than the one Lieutenant Pell had promised.

The door latch turned.
