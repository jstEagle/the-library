## 16. Working Nights

The frost came early that December, silvering the workshop windows like breath held against glass. Ansel had learned to read the weather in those panes — the thickness of the rime, the way it feathered toward the sea — and on the fourth night after the hearing, he read in them the approach of footsteps on the frozen path, measured and even, boots with a soldier's heel.

He did not turn around. He kept his hands on the adze, shaping the curve of the stringer beam, letting the shavings curl away pale as fish flesh. The lamp above his bench threw his shadow long across the floorboards, and when the door opened at last, letting in a knife of cold air and the smell of the tide flat, the shadow trembled but did not break.

"You'll want to shut that," he said. "The cold's good for the timber. Not so good for what's left of my knuckles."

Elowen Marsh closed the door behind her with her elbow, because both her arms were full. Army timber — good Baltic pine, straight-grained and kiln-dried, still bearing the broad arrow stamp burned into its ends — stacked in bundles bound with cord. Under one arm, wrapped in sacking, a set of tools that clinked when she set them down: augers, a brace and bits, chisels in a leather roll that had seen better decades.

"Requisitioned," she said, and there was something in her voice that was almost humor — a dry note, careful as a hand placed on thin ice.

"From whom?"

"From an army that has more timber than sense." She unwound her scarf, and the lamplight found her face — thinner than when she'd arrived in autumn, he thought, though perhaps he was seeing what he wanted to see. "The stores sergeant owes me a favor I intend to spend before either of us regrets it."

Ansel set down the adze. He looked at the timber a long moment. Pine of that quality did not grow on this coast; Maren had specified it in her drawings, written *Baltic pine or nothing* in her slanted hand beside the joint detail, because she had known, the way she knew most things, that the bridge would have to outlast everyone who built it.

"That's her stamp," Elowen said, nodding at the drawing pinned above the bench — the footbridge rendered in ink and wash, every measurement annotated twice, once in figures and once in words, as if Maren had not trusted numbers alone to hold the thing together. "On the timber. The broad arrow."

"The Crown marks what it owns." Ansel ran his thumb along the stamped end of a plank. "Maren always said the bridge would be made of stolen goods one way or another. She only hoped it'd be stolen from people who deserved to lose it."

"Did she." Elowen unrolled the tool roll on the bench, and her fingers paused over one of the chisels — a mortise chisel, handle worn to the shape of a grip not hers, not anyone's now. "Whose were these?"

"My father-in-law's. Then mine. Now nobody's, until somebody picks them up." He slid the roll toward her. "You've handled tools before. I saw the calluses when you signed the hearing papers. Sailing? Farming?"

"Carpentry." She said it flatly, the way she reported casualties. "My father believed a girl should know a trade in case the commission failed. It failed more slowly than he expected, but it failed."

"And the army took you instead."

"The army didn't take me. I walked in." She picked up the mortise chisel, tested its edge against her thumbnail the proper way, and Ansel felt something loosen in his chest that he had not permitted to move since October. "What do you need cut first?"

He showed her.

* * *

They fell into it the way water finds a channel — not quickly, but completely, so that within three nights there was no question anymore of whether she would come, only of when, and how much timber, and whether the wind off the point would let the lamp burn steady enough to work by.

She came after evening muster, when the garrison settled into its cards and its complaints and its slow fermentation of resentment toward the weather, the posting, and the sea. She came without escort, in a civilian coat she had presumably requisitioned from herself, and she never spoke of what it cost her to walk past the sentry post at the head of the path where, on any given night, some corporal with more suspicion than sense might mark the lighthouse keeper's workshop lit at all hours and say something to someone.

Ansel did not thank her for the risk. He understood, from his own long acquaintance with dangerous silences, that gratitude named aloud can frighten a thing into flight, the way a raised hand frightens birds off wire. Instead he taught her joinery.

It went against every instinct she had, he discovered by the second night. She approached wood the way she approached everything — as a problem to be mastered, forced, brought to heel. When the first tenon wouldn't seat, she reached for the mallet.

"No." His hand covered hers on the chisel, and neither of them moved for a moment. Sawdust hung in the lamplight between them, fine as pollen. "You're fighting it. The wood's already told you where it wants to go. You felt it — that grain runs off to the left, so your paring goes left. Work with the lie of it."

"And if the lie of it is wrong?"

"It isn't wrong. It's just true. Your job is to find out what's true and build on it." He eased her hands through the cut, and the shaving came away whole and translucent, and he heard her breath change. "There. That's the whole trade, right there. Everything else is measuring."

"Maren," Elowen said, not looking up from the joint, her voice very level, "was she like this? Teaching you?"

"She never taught me anything in her life. She'd stand there while I did it wrong, and then she'd ask me questions until I worked out myself that I'd done it wrong." He laughed — short, surprised at the sound, rusty from disuse. "Infuriating woman. Twenty-two years married and she never once told me my business. Just asked me whether I thought that was really the best way to go about it."

"And you'd say yes."

"I'd say yes, and then I'd lie awake doing it differently in my head, and in the morning the work would be different, and she'd never say a word about it." He fitted the tenon home with the heels of his hands, and it seated with a soft wooden sigh, tight and true. "That's how she argued. Through the work."

Elowen set down her chisel. For a while she studied the joint — the clean shoulder, the hidden strength of it, the way two separate pieces became one thing indistinguishable at the seam.

"My orders say this is demolition pending," she said finally. "Every plank in this building is scheduled for burning or salvage by order of the Southern Coastal Command. I've read the inventory. I signed half of it myself."

"I know."

"And yet here I am, teaching myself to commit it properly." She turned the joint over in her hands, testing it, finding no play in it anywhere. "Do you ever think about what you're asking me to become?"

"I don't ask you anything, Captain. You walk down that path on your own legs."

"Yes," she said quietly. "That's rather the trouble with it."

* * *

The nights lengthened. They talked the way people talk when their hands are busy — obliquely, sideways, the important things said only in passing, disguised as remarks about timber or weather, so they could be denied later if denial became necessary.

She told him about Harwich, where she'd grown up between a boatyard and a chapel, and how her father had built pilot cutters with the same disregard for shortcuts that Maren had brought to bridges. She told him about Woolwich, and the examination boards, and the particular silence that falls around a woman who scores higher than the men expect — not hostility exactly, but a recalibration, a settling of accounts. She told him none of it looking at him; she told it to the work, and Ansel understood that this was the only way she could tell it at all.

He told her about Maren — not the grief of her, which he kept as he kept the ledger, private and complete, but the living fact of her. How she'd come to the point as the daughter of the previous keeper and stayed as the wife of the next one, and how the townsfolk had never decided which of them had married which. How she'd rowed out in weather that kept grown fishermen ashore, because someone had to see the light was lit and Ansel couldn't be two places at once. How she'd started the ledger the first winter of their marriage, after the *Providence* went down with eleven men, because — she'd said — God keeps accounts, but God doesn't keep names, and names are what the widows need.

"She was right about that too, I suppose," Elowen said. It was near midnight; they were sanding the rail stock, and the sound of it filled the workshop like slow surf.

"She was right about everything. That's the hell of it." Ansel blew dust from the grain. "You spend years thinking, one day I'll catch her out, one day she'll be wrong about something, just so I'll know the world's fair. And then she dies, and she's still never been wrong, and you understand the world isn't fair at all."

The sanding stopped. Elowen looked at him across the rail between them, and in the low light her face was stripped of rank the way a shore is stripped at low tide — everything usually covered, laid bare and gleaming wet.

"My brother died at Khartoum," she said. "There's a name on a memorial in Cairo. Cut in stone by a man who never met him. Some subaltern spelled it wrong — Elowen, they carved, as if it were a surname." Her jaw tightened. "Nobody has ever corrected it. Nobody will. It will stand wrong for a hundred years, because correcting it would require someone to care, and the machinery that cut it has moved on to other stones."

Ansel set down his paper. He crossed the workshop, took the reconstructed ledger from its hiding place beneath the floorboards where he'd built a false bottom the army's searches had never found, and laid it open on the bench between them.

"Then you understand," he said, "what this is."

Elowen looked down at the pages — his hand, not Maren's, but shaped to hers, each entry copied from memory and confirmed by the town, dates and vessels and fates in columns as straight as drill. Page after page. Forty years of names.

"It's a casualty list," she said slowly.

"It's a rescue record. Every name in the lost column has three saved beside it somewhere — sometimes thirty." He turned the pages. "Your army calls these unregistered persons. Persons who drowned, Captain. Persons who washed up on my beach and were buried by my wife with more ceremony than the Admiralty ever spared them. That's what got confiscated in October. That's what sits in your strongbox under seal."

She did not touch it. But she looked, and she kept looking, and Ansel watched something happen in her that he had seen happen once before — in himself, standing over Maren's open grave, when the ground gave way beneath everything he'd believed load-bearing and something else rose to take the weight.

"I could be court-martialed for reading this," Elowen said.

"You could be court-martialed for carrying that timber down the path tonight."

"Yes. Well." She straightened. The officer came back into her spine by visible degrees, but her eyes stayed soft, and that contradiction — that impossible coexistence — was the truest thing he had learned about her yet. "Hand me the smoothing plane. If we're going to commit treason against the Southern Coastal Command, we'll do it properly. No tear-out in the rail stock. Maren's drawings don't allow for it."

"No," Ansel said. "They don't."

* * *

On the ninth night, the wind backed to the northeast and the temperature fell so hard the sea smoke rose off the water in columns, ghostly in the moonlight, and the workshop's iron stove worked itself red trying to hold the warmth for the glue pot. They worked close together at the bench, closer than the job strictly required, shoulders nearly touching, and neither of them remarked on it, because remarking would have broken it.

She was learning the drawknife. Her hands had remembered more than she'd claimed — the farm childhood surfacing through the officer the way water surfaces through chalk — and the shavings came off the spar in long curls that dropped and coiled on the floor around their boots.

"My husband," Ansel said, without deciding in advance to say it, "would have liked you."

"Don't." The word came fast and quiet. She didn't stop the drawknife. "Don't do that. Don't make me into a consolation. I've seen men do that — take the first warm thing after the cold and call it love, and hate themselves for it by spring."

"And what would you call it, then? Since you've had time to form a view."

The drawknife stilled. Outside, the wind moaned in the tower vents, a sound he had lived with for thirty years and barely heard anymore, and inside there was only the stove ticking and the sea smoke pressing white against the windows and the two of them not looking at each other over a half-finished rail.

"I'd call it," she said at last, carefully, as if walking out onto ice she could hear cracking, "the first thing in eleven years I've built instead of demolished. And I'd want it to survive inspection. Which means going slowly. Checking every joint." A pause. "Working with the lie of the grain."

Ansel let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding. "Maren would have said you'd done it wrong."

"Maren would have asked me whether I thought so."

And he laughed, and after a moment — surprising them both, he thought — so did she, low and brief, a sound the workshop had never held before, and the frost on the windows caught the lamplight and threw it back doubled, and for an hour the work went on in a silence that was not empty anymore.

It was near two in the morning when she wound her scarf and gathered her coat, and stood a moment at the door with her hand on the latch, not opening it.

"There's a transport due Thursday," she said. "Bringing the winter garrison rotation. And equipment." She was looking at the door, not at him. "Heavy equipment. Demolition charges among it, per the original survey schedule. The surveyor's report lists this workshop's footings as — " she paused, and he heard her choose the exact words — "as obstructing the bridge approach alignment. The new bridge. Theirs."

Ansel felt the cold come into the room ahead of any draft. "When?"

"The schedule says January. But schedules change when reports are filed early." She pulled the door open, and the wind took her scarf and streamed it seaward. "I'll delay the paperwork as long as I can. Weeks, perhaps. Not months. Whatever this becomes — " she touched the doorframe, the workshop, the century of salt soaked into its timbers — "it needs to be finished before the equipment lands."

She went out into the dark. Ansel stood in the doorway watching her lantern shrink down the path toward the garrison lights, small and steady against the wind, and behind him the footbridge's skeleton filled the workshop from wall to wall — ribs and stringers, the bones of Maren's design taking shape at last after four years of drawings and dreams.

Weeks, then. Perhaps less.

He went back inside, banked the stove, took up the pencil, and wrote in the margin of the drawings, beside her measurements, in his own hand: *Work faster.*
