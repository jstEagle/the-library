## 17. The Third Storm — the Great Storm

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday, and by the morning of the solstice it had fallen so far that the needle lay against the stop like a bird with a broken wing. Ansel Thackeray had kept lamps burning through thirty winters on this point, and he had learned to read weather the way other men read letters — but he had never seen the glass lie down flat before noon.

He said nothing to Elowen when she came up from the garrison at dusk with army bread and a tin of mutton under her arm. She had taken to arriving in the last hour of light most evenings now, ostensibly to inspect the light's log for the Ministry, actually to hold the lamp glass while he polished it and to talk about nothing while the sea talked about everything below them. Tonight she looked at the horizon once, the way a soldier looks at an empty road that will not stay empty, and set her tin down without a joke.

"It's coming," she said.

"It's coming."

They ate quickly. By six o'clock the wind arrived ahead of itself — a long moan out of the northeast that made no pretense of gathering strength gradually but fell upon the point as if it had been running all the way from Norway. The windows of the cottage flexed in their frames. Rain came not in sheets but in solid gray lengths, like rope thrown end over end.

Ansel climbed the tower at seven and lit the lamp while the stairwell shuddered around him. He trimmed the wick, checked the oil feed twice, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and wrote in the log: *Wind rising NE, hard. Barometer 28.1 and falling.* His hand was steady. His hand had learned steadiness the way his hands had learned everything else on this rock — by doing the necessary thing so many times it forgot how to do anything else.

Below him, the town was shuttering itself. He could see lamplight in a dozen windows go out one by one as families banked their fires and went to sit together in inner rooms. On the half-built footbridge — two-thirds finished now, its beams tarred and braced against the winter — the ropes he had lashed across the decking sang three different notes as the gusts crossed them. Maren's bridge, holding. He thought of her drawing it in chalk on the workshop floor, her hair falling across the paper, saying *a path is just a promise you can walk on, Ansel,* and he put the thought away carefully, the way you put away something breakable before the shaking starts.

At nine the sea began to arrive.

Not waves — arrivals. Whole sections of ocean lifted off their foundations and hurled themselves at the headland, and each impact came up through the rock into the tower's bones so that Ansel felt it in his teeth, his wrists, the sockets of his eyes. Spray cleared the lantern gallery entirely, a hundred feet above high water, and struck the glass like gravel. The lens turned. The beam leaned into the dark and was beaten back and turned again.

Elowen came up at ten, soaked through her officer's coat, her collar up, her face streaming.

"The sergeant wants to know if you need men," she shouted, close to his ear.

"Send me two strong backs and no opinions. I'll want relief carrying oil if this goes past midnight."

She went down the stairs sideways against the roll of the tower. Ansel turned back to the lamp. In the glass he saw his own face doubled and refracted — one Ansel in every facet of the lens, all of them tending, all of them watching — and he thought, not for the first time, that Maren would have understood that. A man in pieces, all of him doing his work.

* * *

At eleven-forty the first crack came.

He heard it before he felt it — a sound like a great green tree splitting somewhere below the floor, deep in the masonry where no one had stood since the tower was raised — and then the floor moved under him in a way floors are not meant to move. Not a shudder. A *shift*. The whole column of the tower leaned, a finger's width, perhaps less, and settled, and the clockwork that turned the lens jammed with a shriek of stripped brass.

Ansel dropped to his knees at the mechanism. The gear train had bound; the drive weight hung slack on its chain; somewhere inside the works a tooth had sheared off and lodged itself between two others like a bone in a throat. The beam now pointed fixed out to sea on one heading, sweeping nothing, warning no one approaching from the south and west.

*Hand-turn it,* said some voice in him that was forty years old and calm. *You've done it in drills. Hand-turn.*

He put both hands on the spare wheel and hauled. The lens resisted, then gave, and began to turn — slowly, groaningly, at maybe half its proper rate — under nothing but his shoulders and his arms and his refusal to stop. One revolution every ninety seconds when it should have been forty-five. Half a light. It would have to be enough.

The second crack came at midnight, and with it the lightning.

The storm had found its full voice now. Thunder rolled continuously out of the northeast, not separate reports but one long demolition, and between flashes the rain came down so thick that the beam seemed to end a ship's length from the gallery, drowned mid-reach. Through the lantern glass Ansel watched the sea mount the headland itself — water coming over the top of the point where no water had come in living memory, pouring across the parade ground in brown cataracts, finding the garrison's stores.

He saw the powder store flood because the powder store's roof came off. It rose whole into the wind like a hat snatched from a head, cartwheeled once over the wall, and vanished, and the sea went straight down into the magazine beneath. Even over the storm he thought he heard the soldiers' shouting from the barracks — a thin human sound, very far below, men realizing that several tons of black powder were sitting in rising saltwater next to their own sleeping quarters.

There was nothing he could do about that. There was nothing anyone could do about that. What there was to do was here: the wheel, the lamp, the oil, the turning. He turned.

Elowen came back up at half past twelve with the two soldiers — boys, both of them, white-faced and green — and took one look at the jammed mechanism and put her hands on the wheel beside his without asking what it was.

"Relieve him," she told the soldiers. "Four hands on, four hands off. Mr. Thackeray stays with the lamp."

"Captain—" one of them started.

"You heard the order."

They turned the lens through the small hours. Ansel lost count of time. The log entries, which he made whenever his hands were free, drifted apart: *1:10, tower cracked again, lean perceptible in the stair, still standing.* *2:40, sea over the point entire. Powder store flooded. Lamp OK.* *3:15, oil low, sent boy down.* The boy came back grey and shaking and said nothing except "the sarge says the magazine's under four feet and the fuses are soaked through, thank God," and then put his hands on the wheel.

At four in the morning, at the height of it, the third crack came, and this one they all felt in their spines — a long tearing groan out of the masonry, and the lamp flame bent sideways in the draught of a new airway that had opened inside the wall. Ansel pressed his palm flat against the stone and held it there, listening with his hand the way Maren used to listen to timber, and what the stone told him was this: the tower had cracked through the old bedding joint on the seaward side, the flaw the builders had papered over sixty years ago with mortar and hope, and it was now leaning — barely, measurably, unmistakably — toward the sea.

It would stand tonight. Stone does not hurry. But it would never stand another season unwatched, and everyone who touched that wall knew it.

"Mr. Thackeray." Elowen's voice, close, level. "Tell me what you need me to hear."

"It holds till dawn. After dawn, it needs an engineer's eye and a report that can't be buried." He looked at her. Rain or spray had gotten into everything; they were both of them salted white to the eyebrows, like figures left on a shore by a wave. "And it needs writing down tonight, Captain. Before anyone has a chance to say the keeper cracked it."

Something moved behind her eyes — he watched it move, quick as a fish under ice — and she said only, "I'll write it myself. Every hour. Get back to the wheel."

They turned the light until the sky went the color of wet slate and the storm, having spent everything it had, drew back over the sea growling. At eight minutes past seven in the morning of the shortest day, Ansel shut the lamp down, and the four of them stood a moment in the sudden ordinary grey quiet of the lantern room, deafened by the absence of wind, swaying slightly the way sailors do on land.

Below them, the point looked as if it had been fought over. The parade ground was a lake rimmed with weed. Two outbuildings were simply gone. The workshop — Maren's workshop, with the bridge timbers stacked inside and the drawings pinned to the wall — stood ankle-deep in flood but standing, and Ansel counted the distance from the tower door to it twice before he could breathe evenly again.

The fishing fleet had gone out nothing the night before and stayed in harbor; the garrison's supply boat, refitted after the second storm, had not sailed. No one had needed saving. For the first time in the history of the worst nights on this point, the ledger would record losses of stone and powder and timber only, and Ansel found that his hands shook now — only now, only after — at the size of the mercy.

* * *

They came down into the courtyard as the garrison was mustering, and the sight of them stopped the men mid-motion: the keeper and the captain and two soldiers, crusted white with salt, bent like survivors of a wreck, which is what they were. Sergeant Hale — broad, red-faced, the sort of man who mistakes volume for authority — was already pointing at the tower.

"She's cracked! Look at her, she's leaning like a drunkard, and the magazine's under the sea, and I'll tell you who'll answer for—"

"Sergeant." Elowen's voice carried without rising. It always did. "The tower's condition will be established by inspection and entered in the log. The log I kept personally, hourly, through the night." She held up the oilskin-wrapped notebook, and Ansel saw Hale's jaw work and stall. "Every officer and man on this point will confirm that the light never failed. Not for one minute. Whatever else the sea took, the light did not fail. Now get your men out of the magazine approach and get a headcount."

Hale went. The morning assembled itself slowly out of wreckage. And Ansel stood in the churned mud of the courtyard with Elowen at his shoulder, looking up at the tower he had served for thirty years, leaning now — a degree, no more, but leaning — toward the water it had spent its life defying.

"You knew," he said quietly. "Last night. When I asked for it written down. You'd already thought of it."

"I think about blame professionally," she said. "It's most of what my rank is for." She looked at the notebook in her hands, then out at the drowned parade ground, and her voice dropped to something that wasn't for sergeants. "Ansel. The command is going to need somewhere to put what happened here. Cracked towers don't happen; someone cracks them. Flooded magazines don't happen; someone leaves a hatch open. And there's a civilian on this point who has been building an unauthorized structure out of requisitioned timber since October, and whose name every man in this garrison already knows."

"They saved the light with us."

"Yes." She turned to face him fully, and he saw that she was afraid — the first time he had ever seen it, and the last thing he'd expected to see, because he had built his understanding of her entirely out of her fearlessness. "And that won't weigh anything at all with the men who are already composing the report. They're on their way even now. I watched the boat put out from the mainland an hour ago. Command comes today, Ansel. Before noon."

Above them, in the cracked tower, the great lens sat still in its housing, holding its thousand facets of morning light, waiting to be lit again at dusk. Ansel looked at it a long moment, and then at the workshop where Maren's drawings hung, and then at the woman who had spent her autumn learning joinery by lamplight and had written down the truth all night in her own hand.

"Then we'd best be sure," he said, "that everything true is written down before they arrive."
