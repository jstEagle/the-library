## 19. The Last Planks

Three days.

Ansel Thackeray wrote the number on the workshop's north wall in carpenter's pencil, the way a man might write a debt he intended to pay in full, and then he stood looking at it until the candle guttered and the figures seemed to swim — *28.12*, the twenty-eighth of December, 1899 — as though the year itself had been chalked up against him and was waiting, patient as any creditor, to see whether he would make good.

He had not slept. He had no intention of sleeping. Sleep was a country he could not afford the passage to, not now, with the bridge standing half-born in the frosted light of the workshop lamps: its spine laid, its rails rising, four of its eight balusters still rough-sawn blanks stacked by the door like loaves awaiting the oven. Maren's drawings hung above the bench, inked in her quick sure hand, annotated in pencil so fine it looked like frost — *camber the deck two inches in twelve feet; let the water pass under, not through* — and every time his eyes lifted to them they were her eyes looking back, unhurried, certain that the thing could be done because she had already done it, on paper, in her mind, years before anyone thought to need it.

His hands knew the work even when his mind went grey at the edges. That was the mercy of it. Tenon here, mortise there; the drawknife whispering along the grain; the smell of fresh-cut larch like something almost edible, green and sweet under the tallow-and-iron stink of the army timber Captain Marsh had brought over the wall in the dark. His hands moved and his heart, which for eleven years had been a locked room with the furniture sheeted over, stood open tonight, and grieved, and worked, all three at once, and did not distinguish between them.

*Three days.* He picked up the next blank and began.

* * *

They started coming at first light, and he never learned who sent the first word, or if any word was sent at all.

Tobias Quill came down from the herring sheds with his two grown sons and a chest of tools older than the century, and he did not knock, only shouldered the door open against the wind and said, "Point me," in the voice of a man who had buried a wife off this point and knew precisely what the ledger cost. Ansel pointed him at the balusters. By noon all three Quills were covered in sawdust to the elbows, and the rhythm of four adzes and two planes had become a kind of weather inside the workshop, its own storm, its own tide.

Then Mrs. Penhale arrived with a basket that smelled of hot bread and boiled mutton, and set it on the bench without ceremony, saying only, "You'll eat or you'll fall into the sea, and I've no intention of writing your name down twice." Behind her came the widow Sark and her daughters, who took up positions at the oilstone and the rasp and began, without instruction, to fair the curves Maren had drawn — and here Ansel had to stop, once, and go out into the cold yard and stand until his eyes stopped burning, because the widows were sanding wood to the exact fineness Maren had specified in a margin note none of them could have read from where they stood. They knew her hand. They had watched her measure things for years. Grief, it turned out, was a language spoken fluently by an entire town, and it had opinions about joinery.

By mid-afternoon the workshop was full past capacity. Fishermen who should have been mending nets planed deck-planks on trestles out in the yard, breath smoking, fingers split with cold, taking turns because there were more volunteers than tools. Children too young to be useful ran messages, fetched nails, held lanterns as the short day failed. Old Halloran, who had not left his cottage above the bay since his son was lost in '91, appeared at the door with a coil of rope over one shoulder and said nothing at all, and Ansel showed him where the handrail needed whipping, and Halloran sat down inside the frame of Maren's bridge and worked with his ruined hands until dark.

Nobody spoke much. There was no need. The talk was in the timber — the long shivering note of a good plank tapped true, the dry click of a joint going home tight. Once, when a crossbeam settled into its housing with a sound like a door closing gently in another room, Mrs. Penhale laughed, low and surprised, and the laugh went round the workshop and came back warmer than it left.

Ansel ate standing up, one-handed, the bread Mrs. Penhale had brought, and tasted nothing, and was grateful beyond words anyway.

* * *

The second night he found the plank.

It was near two in the morning when the last of the town went home — Tobias driving them out gently, "Sleep is also work, get gone" — and Ansel remained alone with the lamps and the nearly finished thing. The bridge now stood complete in frame, decked from shore-end to mid-span, cambered exactly as the drawing demanded, so that it rose toward the middle like a held breath. What remained was the final reach: six planks between the last completed section and the far abutment, the stretch that would touch the mainland path, the threshold of the whole design.

And among the seasoned boards leaning against the wall he found one he had not cut.

It was larch, quarter-sawn, longer than the others by a handspan, and planed glass-smooth on both faces — smoother than his own planes left wood. Someone had prepared it and slipped it in among his stock without a word. He turned it under the lamp and found, burned small and neat into the underside of one end, a maker's mark he did not know and beneath it, in careful pyrography, a single word:

*Maren.*

He stood holding it a long time. The lamp hissed. Outside, the sea worked at the rocks below the point with its old patience, the same sound it had made the night the ledger began, the night Maren set down her first name in her first fair hand — *saved, the Gracie May, all souls, praise given* — and Ansel understood that the town had decided this plank before he had, perhaps before he was able to, that her name belonged in the bridge itself, under the rail where walking hands would rest above it without knowing, the way we rest our hands all our lives above the lives that carried us.

He did not ask who had made it. Some gifts are better unclaimed; they belong to everyone that way.

He fitted it last of all.

* * *

The carving took him most of the third day, and he was glad of the slowness of it, because slowness meant his hands stayed steady while the rest of him shook.

Beneath the rail, on the inner face where a palm falls naturally in crossing, he cut the letters with a veining chisel no wider than a straw: *MAREN THACKERAY, 1851–1893,* and under that, because the design asked for it, because her own marginal note asked for it — *she built the way across* — though he had argued with her about the phrasing once, alive, laughing at her modesty, and she had said, *Ansel, nobody crosses a bridge thinking about the builder, and that is the whole point of building.*

The chips came away pale as fingernails. Each letter took minutes. He let them. Around him the workshop filled again — Quill and sons, the Sark women, Halloran at his rope, a dozen others, the boy Jem Carn running the grindstone — and the noise of them wrapped around his silence, and no one interrupted the chisel, and no one needed to ask whose name was going into the wood, because everyone in that room had a page in the ledger too, saved or lost, and understood that the two columns were one column really, all of it one continuous record of what people owed each other across deep water.

Mrs. Penhale brought the food again, and this time she made him sit. She put the bowl in his hands and stood over him until he ate, and while he ate she looked at the bridge — the whole of it, shore to shore, the arc of it clean against the winter sky through the open doors — and her chin trembled once and firmed.

"She'd have had us all measuring wrong within a week," Mrs. Penhale said. "She'd have found fault with my sanding."

"She'd have found fault with everything," Ansel said, "and been right," and Mrs. Penhale laughed the wet laugh of the grieving, and went back to her work.

* * *

The second night ended the way the first had begun, with numbers. He wrote on the wall again, lower on the plaster where the first figure had faded under breath and salt: *27.12*. Then *26.12* appeared beside it in someone else's hand — Jem Carn, he suspected, who had taken to updating the count each evening with terrible solemnity, as though the wall itself were keeping the ledger now.

On the morning of the thirtieth, Ansel walked the bridge.

It was not finished — the final plank lay carved and oiled but unset, waiting for the last fixing, waiting for its moment — and yet the span stood whole enough to bear him, shore to shore, the deck springing faintly underfoot with that living give of well-made timber, the sea sliding beneath through the gap Maren had designed to let the tide pass unstressed. Halfway across he stopped and laid his hand on the rail, and his palm rested directly above the hidden letters, above her name, the way a child's hand rests above its mother's in the dark without knowing why, comforted by proximity alone.

From the middle of the span he could see the town behind him — roofs smoking, nets hanging, the garrison's palisade and the great unfinished skeleton of the army's fortified bridge rearing black against a hard silver sky — and ahead of him the mainland path climbing away through the gorse, empty, white-rimmed with frost, going wherever paths go when the century between them is ending.

He thought of the hearing, and Elowen Marsh staking her commission on a technicality written in salvaged timber. He thought of the tower cracked like a struck bell and the powder store flooded, of the logbooks spread on a table between Elowen and men who wanted a scapegoat. He thought of the order that had come down two days ago, posted on the garrison board where everyone could read it: *the point to be cleared by December 31. No extensions.* No extensions. As if time were a thing you could refuse to lend.

Tomorrow at midnight they would come with axes and writs, and they would find the bridge finished and the town standing on it, and Elowen Marsh would do whatever it was she had spent a year deciding to do. He could not know how it would fall out. He only knew that the wood was true, every joint drawn tight, every plank faired, and that truth in timber, as Maren liked to say, argues better than most men.

He walked back across, slow, learning the deck's temper, and at the shore end he turned and looked once more at the span — her lines, her camber, her letting-in for the tide, finished by forty pairs of hands that loved her — and then he went inside and sharpened the chisels for whatever tomorrow required, and above the bench the drawings stirred slightly in the draft, her pencil notes catching the lamplight, and the wall kept its count: *31.12*, the last day of a thousand years, waiting like all debts and all dawns just beyond the door.

Out on the point, far off through the morning haze, Ansel could see a knot of soldiers coming down the coast road toward the workshop — and at their head, unmistakable even at that distance by her bearing and her grey coat, Captain Elowen Marsh, carrying something flat and official under her arm, walking fast.
