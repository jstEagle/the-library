## 21. The First Light of the Century

Ansel Thackeray woke on the first morning of the century to a silence so complete it seemed rehearsed.

The great storm had spent itself two days before, and the sea off the point lay flat and pewter-colored now, breathing slowly, like a man asleep after a long illness. Frost had come in the night and furred the inside corners of the windows with white ferns. He lay a while in the narrow bed he had shared first with Maren and then, for forty years, with no one, listening to the tower above him tick and settle as it warmed toward the pale sun.

There was a strange arithmetic to waking on such a day. A hundred years had turned in the night, and nothing in the room had changed. The same chair. The same coat on its peg. The same faint smell of linseed oil that had worked itself into the grain of the house and would outlast them all. And yet everything was different, the way a room is different after a coffin has been carried out of it — the same room, but rearranged around an absence that is finally, somehow, a presence too.

He rose, built up the stove, and put the kettle on. His hands did these things without him. They had been doing them for decades — hands busy, heart waiting — and he had long ago stopped resenting the arrangement. But this morning he noticed his hands were not empty the way they used to be. There was something in them even when they held nothing. A weight. A good one.

The ledger lay on the kitchen table where he had left it, open, because Elowen had told him last night, standing on the new bridge in the falling dark with a hundred lanterns burning along its rail: *A record like this should never be closed. Close it and it becomes a monument. Leave it open and it stays alive.*

She had been crying when she said it, though she would have denied it under oath. He had pretended not to see, which was its own kind of tenderness, and perhaps the only kind she could accept just yet.

He ate his bread and dripping standing up, looking out at the water, and thought about dusk. Dusk was when the lamp was lit. It had always struck him as the honest hour — the hour when you could not pretend the day was still yours, nor yet surrender to the dark. On this first day of a new century, dusk would come at ten minutes past four, and he intended to be ready for it, because the light was his, and the century was new, and there was work to do, thank God, always work.

* * *

The morning passed in small devotions.

He climbed the tower at nine and cleaned the lens with a cloth dampened in spirits, wiping the salt haze from the great curved glass until each prism caught the winter light and threw it in pieces across the iron walls — coins of gold sliding over rivets and rust. He checked the wick, trimmed it square, filled the reservoir. He tested the clockwork that turned the apparatus, listened to it tick through one full revolution, counted the flash characteristic against the printed card: *eclipsing every twelve seconds.* Twelve seconds of light, six of dark. He had thought once, in the worst months after Maren died, that this was a cruel ratio — light always outnumbered by its own absence. Later he had come to believe the opposite. Six seconds of darkness existed only to make the twelve seconds of light legible. You needed the gaps to read the message.

Maren would have put that in the ledger somewhere, if ledgers took philosophy among their columns of names and dates. *Darkness: six parts in eighteen. Purpose: emphasis.*

Down again by eleven. He walked the path along the headland, boots crunching frost, down to the workshop — still standing, still his until the paperwork caught up with the world's turning, and Elowen had promised it would catch up slowly now, or not at all. The door swung open on the smell of shavings and pine pitch, the sweetest smell left in his life except one other.

The footbridge stood.

He still could not look at it all at once. He had to take it in pieces, the way you take in a cathedral: the trestles first, doubled and bolted, driven into bedrock at either end; then the stringers, sixty feet of them, spliced with Maren's own scarf joints copied from her drawings; then the rail, oak, planed smooth, running in one unbroken line from the rocks below the lighthouse to the mainland path where the cliff stepped down into gorse and stunted thorn. Gaps in the rail let a fisherman's rope through. Iron rings at intervals for lifelines. She had thought of everything, his Maren, twenty years dead and thinking of everything still.

Her brother's name was carved beneath the third panel from the far end. *Tobias Wren, lost 1879.* And beneath the near rail, added three days ago by Ansel's own stiff hand: *Maren Thackeray, who designed what grief required.*

He ran his palm along the wood. It was cold as stone outside but the timber held a memory of yesterday's fire in it somewhere, deep down, and he stood there in the doorway letting his hand warm against it until his eyes stung and the wind came up off the water and made an honest excuse for it.

* * *

At midday the town began to arrive.

They came across the bridge in ones and twos and whole families, because they could — because for the first time in the history of the point there was a dry, safe, lit passage to the mainland path, and because crossing it had become, overnight, something like a rite. Old Widow Caskie walked it leaning on the arm of two granddaughters and stopped at the middle to look down at the water as if paying a call. The Penhale brothers, who had hauled beams through the freezing nights of the last week, strutted its length like bridegrooms. Children raced it end to end, were scolded, raced it again. Every plank received its feet. That was the thing about a bridge, Ansel thought: it was not finished by carpenters. It was finished by being used.

Mrs. Tregowan brought him a pasty warm from her oven and refused thanks. Young Clem Bowden, whom Ansel had pulled half-drowned from the surf eleven minutes short of death in October, brought a coil of new hemp rope for the lifeline rings and would not leave until he had re-hung every one himself, knotting with the exaggerated care of a boy proving he deserved to be alive.

And they brought names. They were still bringing names. All through the afternoon they came to the lighthouse kitchen where the ledger lay open, and read down the pages, and said things like *my grandfather always said his father was picked up off the Gull Rock in '61, but I never knew the year till now,* and *there — that's my aunt — we thought she'd gone to Plymouth,* and Ansel wrote in the margins in pencil, corrections, confirmations, small repairs to the fabric of the past, while the kettle sang and the room filled and emptied like a tide pool.

By four o'clock the sky in the northwest had gone the color of an oyster shell, bruising slowly toward violet at its edges, and Ansel hung his coat over his shoulders and said, quietly, "It's time."

No one asked time for what. The kitchen emptied behind him, following.

* * *

He lit the lamp at dusk as he had lit it some fourteen thousand times before, and as he would light it tomorrow, and the next day, and — he allowed himself, climbing the stairs, the smallest and most dangerous of hopes — for years yet.

The ritual was older than ritual. Open the glass. Touch the flame to the wick. Turn the flame up slow, watching the mantles take the fire and bloom, adjusting until the burn was even, blue at the base, white at the crown. Then close the glass, start the clockwork, and stand back while the lens gathered the little flame up and multiplied it into a beam that went nineteen miles to sea.

Tonight the beam leapt out over water the color of dark honey, and the flash rolled away toward the horizon, and Ansel stood at the gallery rail with the wind combing his white hair back from his forehead, and looked at the light going out into the new century, and felt — not happiness exactly. Something older than happiness. Continuance. The feeling of being one link in a chain that had no interest in his personal sorrows and carried him anyway.

Below him, gathered on the headland and along the bridge rail, the town watched the flash eclipse and return. Someone cheered. It echoed strangely off the cliff and made the gulls lift screaming from the rocks, and then the crowd was cheering properly, and the sound came up the tower to him like warmth coming up from a hearth.

He stayed only a few minutes more. The lamp wanted nothing from him tonight but witness. He went down the stairs, out the door, and joined the stream of people moving toward the bridge.

* * *

They crossed it together, all of them, at full dark.

Lanterns hung from the rail at intervals the whole sixty feet, so that walking the bridge was like walking through a constellation laid low and made habitable. The tide was halfway up and the sea spoke under the trestles in a voice Ansel had known all his life and had learned, in the last year, to hear differently — not as the thing that took, but as the thing against which every plank and name and rescue was a word spoken.

Halfway across, Widow Caskie took his arm. Her grip was surprisingly strong.

"My Tom is in your book," she said. "Third page. Drowned off the point in 'sixty-eight, before your time. My grandmother wrote to the keeper then and he wrote back and that letter is in a box under my bed, and I never showed a living soul." She patted his sleeve. "You show them all, now. Every day. That's what the book is for."

"Yes," Ansel said. "That's what it's for."

At the mainland end the crowd paused at the top of the cliff path, reluctant to break the spell by dispersing, and it was there, in the ring of lantern light and faces, that Elowen Marsh found him.

She wore her uniform still — she wore it always, it was armor and address alike — but the greatcoat was unbuttoned and there was sawdust, he noticed, ground permanently into the seams of her right cuff, and he found himself absurdly pleased by it. She carried the ledger. She had carried it out of the garrison records herself at midnight, formally deeded into the public register with her own signature and the commandant's unwilling seal, and now she carried it the way a soldier carries a standard, two-handed, careful.

"Keeper Thackeray," she said, and the formality was a joke between them now, and not only a joke.

"Captain Marsh."

"There's an entry," she said. "I made it at three o'clock this morning. It isn't in the register copy. I wanted—" She stopped. Started again, and he understood that this was the hardest maneuver she had performed all year, harder than facing down the commandant, harder than the storm. "I wanted you to read it first."

She opened the ledger to the final written page and put it in his hands, and steadied the lantern close, and stepped back to give him room the way you give a man room at a graveside or a christening.

The entries above hers were in Maren's slanted, confident script, and in his own, and in the penciled hands of a dozen townsfolk. At the bottom of the page, ruled off neat as a drill inspection, in a hand he knew now better than almost any living hand — upright, precise, softened only slightly by fatigue and candle smoke — she had written:

*Saved from the wreck of the supply boat *Merrow*, 21 December 1899, by the light kept burning through the Great Storm: Sergeant R. Quill, Corporal J. Amos, Private T. Hedley, Private W. Fenn, Stoker E. Bright, Lieutenant H. Colm. Six souls. Entered by order of Capt. E. Marsh, Corps of Royal Engineers, who owes the keeper of this light a debt the Crown has no column for.*

Six names. He read them twice. Three times. The garrison men whose boat had gone to pieces in the solstice storm, who had come ashore blue and broken over the very rocks where Tobias Wren had drowned, who owed their breaths to the lamp Ansel tended — entered now in Maren's book, in Elowen's hand, forever part of the same record, the saved and the saved, indistinguishable at last.

"You put them in," he said. His voice did something unfamiliar on the way out.

"Where else would they go?" She was looking at the sea, not at him, her jaw set against whatever her face wanted to do. "Your wife started this book to hold everyone the point gave back and everyone it didn't. The army is part of the point now. Like it or not. We're in the current, Keeper. Same as everybody." A pause, and then, quieter, the sentence underneath the sentence: "Some of us more than others."

Ansel closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping child.

"Maren kept this book because her brother died within sight of help," he said. "Because there was no path between the drowning and the helping. Twenty years I thought the book was her mourning. Then I thought it was mine. Tonight—" He looked down the length of the lit bridge, at the town standing on it, at the lanterns, at the beam sweeping overhead every eighteen seconds, twelve of light and six of emphasis. "Tonight I think it was neither. I think it was an instruction. She wasn't recording the dead, Elowen. She was teaching us how to keep them. And keeping, as any keeper will tell you, is a daily business. Trim the wick. Write the name. Walk the bridge. Again tomorrow."

Elowen turned and looked at him then, fully, and the lamplight was kind to the lines the year had cut into her face.

"Again tomorrow," she said.

They stood a moment longer while the crowd behind them began reluctantly to break apart, lanterns bobbing away down the cliff path toward the houses, voices thinning on the wind. Somewhere below, the Penhale brothers were singing, badly, something about girls who wait by harbors. The tide turned under the trestles with a long sigh, the sea beginning its patient reversal.

Then Elowen straightened, and there was something else in her posture — the particular rigidity she got when duty and desire were pulling opposite bearings, which he had learned to read the way he read weather.

"There's news," she said. "From the War Office. It came on the morning boat." She hesitated, and the hesitation frightened him more than any storm of the year had. "About me. About the garrison. About—"

The lamp above them flashed, eclipsed, returned, painting the ledger in her hands silver and shadow, silver and shadow, and Ansel waited in the six seconds of darkness for whatever the light would show him next.
