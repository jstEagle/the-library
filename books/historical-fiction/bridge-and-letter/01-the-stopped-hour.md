## 1. The Stopped Hour

They came for him at the ninth bell, which was the only hour left in Vellamark that still worked. The church bells had kept their tongues; the tower clocks had all been stopped by decree or by decay, and so the city told time now the old way, by listening for the single bronze voice of Saint Ferro's that no one had dared silence.

Felix Oyelaran was soldering a hinge when the knock came—two measured strikes, then a pause, then two more, the rhythm of men who had never once in their lives been made to wait. He opened the door on a gray morning and two gray uniforms, and behind them the river fog rolling up from the Vellamark like something the city itself exhaled in surrender.

"Felix Oyelaran," said the taller soldier, not asking. "Clockmaker."

"Watchmaker," Felix said. "Clocks when they're brought to me."

"You'll come with us. The observatory. The master clock is stopped." The soldier's mouth did something that might have been a smile on a face built for other purposes. "It will run again before the Governor's procession. That is an order from the Provisional Authority, which means it is a favor you are being permitted to do."

Felix looked past them at his workshop: the bench, the loupe, the little brass universe of springs and escapements he had spent eleven years keeping small and orderly and himself unseen inside. Eleven years in Vellamark, and in all that time he had learned the first law of the city, which every river teaches eventually: what stands out gets torn at the banks. He had a brown face and a foreign grandfather and a trade that required him to lean close over other people's possessions, and he had survived the conquest of his city by being, in every ledger that mattered, nobody.

"I'll need my tools," he said.

He packed the bag slowly, deliberately, while they watched—not out of spite but because haste read as guilt, and guilt invited questions. Then he went with them through streets still wearing their war like wet paint: shuttered arcades, doors chalked with tally marks in a hand he didn't recognize, a wine shop whose sign had been shot through the middle so that the painted mermaid now held her jug aloft like a torch or a plea. Soldiers everywhere, but imperial soldiers, in the slate-blue of the eastern regiments, standing at corners with the particular stillness of men who had been told the city was theirs and were waiting for the city to agree.

The observatory sat on its hill above the river bend like a stone eye that had closed. Felix had seen it his whole life from below—the white dome, the long gallery windows catching dawn—and had never once been inside. It had belonged to the old Duchy, to the astronomers who had drawn the harbor charts and set the tide tables and kept the master clock whose hands, by custom older than anyone living, were the official time of Vellamark. Every clock in the city was set by it. Which meant, Felix understood as he climbed the hill under escort, that for six weeks the city had been living without a true hour, each tower drifting into its own private time, and everyone pretending not to notice, the way a household pretends not to notice a stopped clock in the parlor until one day it becomes furniture.

The hands had stopped, all accounts agreed, on the night the observatory staff disappeared.

* * *

Inside, the great hall smelled of dust and lamp oil and something faintly metallic underneath, like blood remembered rather than spilled. Papers lay where they had fallen. A chair stood overturned by the transit instrument, and no one had righted it, and Felix understood that no one had been permitted to—that the room itself was being kept as evidence, though of what, and against whom, depended entirely on who won the war that had already been won.

Four officers waited near the clock case. One wore the crossed keys of the Provisional Authority on his collar and did not introduce himself, which was its own introduction.

"The clock stopped at 11:47," this officer said. "You will find the fault. You will correct it. You will touch nothing else in this building. Nothing else." He repeated it the way men repeat things they have been ordered to repeat. "The ceremony is in four days. The Governor will pull the winding lever himself, symbolically. The hands will be set forward to noon, to mark—" he consulted nothing, remembered everything— "the beginning of the new era."

"And the forty-seven minutes?" Felix asked, before the wiser Felix could stop him.

The officer looked at him for a moment. Somewhere in the hall a draft moved a paper an inch across the stones with a dry whisper, and every man in the room glanced toward it and away again.

"The past is forty-seven minutes," the officer said. "We are not restoring it. We are replacing it. Fix the clock."

Then they withdrew to the far end of the hall, to a table someone had provisioned with wine taken, evidently, from somewhere, and Felix was left alone with the master clock as if alone meant watched, which it did.

She was a beauty. He thought of her that way immediately, the way all his trade did—a tall case of black walnut gone silver at the edges, brass dial nearly two feet across, the maker's name engraved small and proud: *A. Voss & Daughter, Vellamark.* A daughter. He filed that away without knowing why. The movement behind the dial was eight-day weight-driven, a striking train, a compound pendulum with a mercury jar for temperature compensation—work of real ambition, work by someone who had loved the problem of time itself. And she had not failed. Even wound down and cold, even with the pendulum hanging dead, he could feel in the arrangement of the wheels a machine that had been stopped, not broken. Stopped the way a sentence stops when the speaker is taken mid-word.

He opened the case door. He began, carefully, visibly, to work—oiling here, testing a click there, doing everything loudly enough and in plain view, because the officers at the table were watching the way cats watch a wall.

It was when he leaned into the lower case to trace the strike-train linkage that his knuckles found the board at the back sitting proud of its neighbors. Loose. Not broken—loose by design, held by a catch a finger could find and eyes would never see. He hesitated one full turn of his heart. Then he pressed the catch, and the board swung inward on hidden pivots, and in the shallow space behind it lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string, sealed at the fold with wax.

Three seals, pressed with the same stamp: a ringed planet, small and fine.

Behind him, laughter from the table, the clink of a bottle. Felix's hands knew what they wanted and his whole life knew what it cost. Eleven years of invisibility said: press the board shut, fix the clock, go home, be nobody, live. But the bundle weighed almost nothing and felt heavier than the weights on their cables, and through the oilcloth he could feel the crisp edges of paper, many pages, folded and folded again, written by someone who had gone to the trouble of hiding testimony inside the city's official keeper of time—as if to say: *if you want to know when we vanished, ask the hour.*

He thought of the overturned chair by the transit instrument. Of the maker's name. *Voss & Daughter.*

Felix slid the bundle into the deep tool-bag between his pliers and his oil can, covered it with a chamois, and pressed the false board home. Then he straightened, wiped his hands, and called out in the flattest voice he owned:

"The escapement needs a part. I'll finish at my workshop tomorrow. She'll keep her new hour for your Governor."

The officer with the crossed keys studied him a long moment, the way a man studies a coin he suspects of being shaved.

"See that she does, watchmaker. And see that you bring back only yourself and your tools. The building will be searched after each visit. Everything in it belongs to the record now."

"Everything," Felix agreed, shouldering the bag, feeling the letters ride against his spine like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the fog had lifted off the river, and the bells of Saint Ferro rang the noon—wrong, he knew now, wrong by minutes, wrong by weeks, wrong in a way no one alive could prove anymore except perhaps the woman who had written what now lay against his back.

He walked down the hill a nobody, carrying the whole truth of Vellamark in a canvas bag, and did not let his hands shake until he was through his own door.
