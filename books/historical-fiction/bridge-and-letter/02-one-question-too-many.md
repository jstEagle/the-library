## 2. One Question Too Many

The knock came at the seventh hour of the morning, which was itself an answer of sorts. Felix Oyelaran knew every one of his customers by the hour they kept: the baker's boy at five, the lamplighter with his cracked pendulum weights at six, old Widow Sarr with her grandfather's carriage clock at mid-morning, when her courage and the gin had both had time to settle. Nobody official knocked at seven. Nobody official knocked at all.

He wiped his palms on his leather apron, considered wiping them again, and opened the door.

The man on his step was grey in the way that imperial functionaries achieved greyness—not in hair alone but in cloth, in complexion, in the very quality of his patience. He carried a ledger bound in slate-blue boards and a pen case that clicked when he shifted his weight, which he did often, as though his body were a mechanism that required frequent adjustment.

"Citizen Felix Oyelaran," he said. Not a question. He consulted the ledger anyway, running a fingernail down a column of names. "Clockmaker. Licensed under the guild rolls of Vellamark, prior to annexation. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"Then I'm to take your particulars for the new registry." He stepped inside without being asked, and Felix, whose workshop had never in twenty years held anyone who did not first stoop to admire the orrery in the window, found himself standing aside like a servant in his own house.

The census taker—his badge said MERROW, and beneath the badge, in smaller type, something in the imperial script that Felix could not read and suspected he was not meant to—walked the length of the room once, slowly. He looked at the lathes. He looked at the drawers of jeweled bearings. He looked, for rather longer than a look requires, at the workbench where Felix had laid out a cleaning cloth, a set of pinion wrenches, and a small brass oiler still dark with the observatory's peculiar heavy grease.

"You've been working on something large," Merrow said.

"Carriage clock," Felix said. "Complex movement."

Merrow glanced at the carriage clock on the shelf, finished and gleaming, waiting for Widow Sarr. Then he looked back at the bench, and said nothing, and made a note.

Felix had spent his whole life around mechanisms, and he knew the sound a machine makes just before something shears. It was a high thin singing, mostly below hearing, a tension in the air. The room was making that sound now.

"Name of father?"

"Hallam Oyelaran."

"Trade of father?"

"Cooper."

"Residence unchanged since the registry of the old year?"

"Yes."

Merrow's pen moved in small dry strokes. The questions came in the order Felix expected—age, guild standing, dependents, whether he kept apprentices—and he answered them in the flat voice he had used with the imperial officers at the observatory, the voice of a man who is only his trade. *I am hands. I am eyes. I fix what is broken.* It had worked for eleven days. It had worked since the city fell.

"Where were you," Merrow said, "on the night of the seventeenth of Frostmonth?"

Felix's hands, which had drifted toward the oiler on the bench out of pure nervous habit, stopped.

"The seventeenth," he repeated.

"You'll find the date in your own ledger, no doubt. All good tradesmen keep accounts." Merrow's tone did not change; it was the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest aloud. "We're establishing movements. There was an irregularity at the observatory that night. Staff departures. Unrecorded."

*Unrecorded.* Felix thought of the word *departed*, stamped in red across the duty roster he had seen nailed to the observatory gate. Eleven days ago, before the red stamp, before the empire's banners went up along the Bridge of Chains, he had drunk tea with Magistra Corvane in the instrument room while she argued cheerfully about the equation of time. He had thought her difficult and brilliant and slightly mad about pendulums. Everyone in Vellamark had thought so. It had seemed a safe thing to be thought.

"I was here," Felix said. "Working."

"At night?"

"I keep odd hours. Commission work."

"What commission?"

"A... a striking train. For a client upriver."

"Which client?"

And here was the failure, the terrible mechanical failure, the tooth breaking off the escapement wheel: Felix Oyelaran, who could name every gear in a tower clock by touch alone, who had lied to customs men about undeclared brass for fifteen years without a flicker, heard himself say, "You wouldn't know them," and saw Merrow's pen stop.

"No," Merrow agreed mildly. "I don't suppose I would." He made another note—a longer one, Felix was sure of it—and closed the ledger. "You were at the observatory last week. Repairing the master clock. By order of the governor's office."

"That's public work. Anyone can—"

"Of course it is." Merrow smiled then, and it was the smile that frightened Felix more than anything else, because it was patient, and patient men came back. "The clock is stopped, citizen. A stopped clock is a scandal and a symbol, and symbols are imperial property now. It will run again soon, at a public ceremony, and everything inside that clock will belong to the record." He clicked his pen case shut. "Everything. You understand. Records must be complete."

He left his card on the bench beside the oiler. Grey card, grey letters. At the door he paused.

"The seventeenth," he said. "Think about it. Memory improves with attention."

Then he was gone, and his footsteps faded down the stairwell, and Felix stood in his own workshop listening to the silence reassemble itself, gear by gear, into the shape of fear.

* * *

He waited until full dark. Then he barred the door, shuttered the windows, lit a single candle, and took the bundle out of the flour barrel where he had hidden it like a coward, wrapped in oilcloth, for eleven days.

Nine letters, sealed with wax gone amber with age—or no, not age; he saw now, turning one to the light, that the wax was fresh within the year. Sealed recently. Hidden recently. Each addressed in a small precise hand: *For whoever mends me.*

*Whoever mends me.* She had known. Corvane had known that the clock would stop, that the empire would need it mended, that they would send a clockmaker, because who else would they send? She had built him into her escape plan the way he would build a concealed pivot into a movement—as a necessity no inspector would think to look for.

He broke the first seal.

*I write this in the instrument room by lamplight, though the lamps are meant to be extinguished at nine. They count our lamp-oil now. Small things first—that is how you learn the shape of what is coming. First the oil, then the correspondence, then the students. They called it an audit. I have been audited before; audits end. This one has not ended.*

The second letter described the dismissal of the young astronomers, one by one, each departure dressed in paperwork. The third named the questions they were asked—*not what do you know, but who do you know, and where do they live*—and Felix, reading, heard Merrow's voice saying *we're establishing movements,* and had to set the page down until his hands steadied.

The fourth letter was dated the sixteenth of Frostmonth.

*Tonight Halvene did not come home. His mother came instead, grey as ash, to ask if I had seen him, and I understood finally what I have been refusing to understand: there is no list we can be on safely, and no list we can be off safely either. The observatory will be emptied tomorrow night. I have seen how they do it in the occupied provinces—records burned, persons removed, and then, years later, the victors' historians write that there was never anyone here at all, or that those who were here deserved it, or best of all, that no one remembers. A city that cannot say what happened to its own people will believe anything.*

*So I am leaving testimony. In the one place they will have to open. Let them conquer the sky over Vellamark; they cannot conquer it without instruments, and instruments fail, and failing instruments summon menders.*

The fifth letter listed names. Thirty-one names, written twice—once with dates of removal, once with descriptions, mothers' names, distinguishing marks, the small human details that survive when registries burn. *If these people are dead,* Corvane wrote beneath, *then this is their grave-marker. If they live, this is their summons home. Either way it is not mine. It belongs to whoever reads it.*

Felix read until the candle guttered, and then lit another, and read. Somewhere past midnight he stopped being afraid. Not that the fear departed—he could feel it still, sitting in his chest like a mainspring wound too tight—but it became subordinate to something else, the way fear always becomes subordinate in a craftsman's heart to the discovery of the true fault, the real broken thing, the work that must be done.

He could not unknow this. That was the whole of it. Merrow wanted the record complete, and now Felix understood why: because a complete record, in imperial hands, would be an empty one. And the true record—the thirty-one names, the account of the seventeenth of Frostmonth, the whole vanished household of the observatory—was sitting in his flour barrel, waiting for a mender.

He thought of Magistra Corvane's tea going cold in the instrument room. He thought of Halvene's mother, grey as ash, asking her question of a door that never answered.

There were others who might remember. The housekeeper, Doss, dismissed two years back with a pension and a warning. The apprentice—the quiet girl who used to sweep the dome—left behind when everyone else was taken, as if she were too small to bother with. They would know things. They would confirm things. Between them, perhaps, they could make the testimony undeniable before the historians sealed the archive forever.

Felix blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to his own clocks tick in the blackness like a roomful of small hearts.

In the morning, he would go to Doss's door. And whatever happened after that, he would not be invisible anymore.
