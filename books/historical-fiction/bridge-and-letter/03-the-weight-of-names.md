## 3. The Weight of Names

The housekeeper's name was Marta Venn, and she lived now in a room above a dye-works on the Salt Quarter's western edge, where the river wind carried the smell of mordant and wet wool. Felix found her address the way he found most things—by listening. The lamplighter on Tanner's Row had mentioned that the observatory woman came down the hill every third day to buy coarse soap and never haggled, as if bargaining belonged to another life.

She opened the door on the second knock, and her face did what faces did in Vellamark these days: it went carefully blank, a shutter drawn over a window.

"If you're from the census," she said, "I answered their questions already."

"I'm not." He kept his voice low. "I'm the clockmaker. Oyelaran. I repaired the master clock."

The blankness held for three heartbeats. Then something moved behind it—not trust, exactly, but recognition, the way one recognizes weather about to change.

"You're the one they walked up the hill under guard." She stepped back from the door without inviting him in, which he understood as an invitation anyway. Inside, the room was spare but fiercely ordered: linens folded into squares, a single chair, a windowsill of herbs growing in dented tins. On the wall hung a small brass barometer, and Felix felt his chest tighten at the sight of it—instruments outliving their keepers.

"Madame Astrid kept everything," Marta said, before he had even sat. "Her papers, her correspondence, her observations. She was careful the way careful people are careful, which is to say she thought care would save her." Her hands worked at each other in her lap. "It didn't."

"I found letters," Felix said. "Hidden in the clock case. In her hand."

Marta Venn went very still. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to almost nothing, as if the walls of empire had ears even here. "How many?"

"A dozen. More, maybe—I haven't counted. They're testimony. About the arrests, the night the staff were taken. She names names. She names *officers*."

For the first time, the housekeeper looked afraid in a way she had not permitted herself at the door. "Then you are carrying your own execution warrant, clockmaker, and you brought it into my room."

"Yes," Felix said simply. "I did."

The apprentice was harder to find because the apprentice did not want to be found. Ivo Rask was seventeen and had been junior assistant at the observatory for barely two years when the soldiers came; he had survived that night by hiding in the meridian pit, wedged among the counterweights, while boots crossed the floor above him. Now he slept in the boathouse of a sympathetic aunt and spent his days pretending to be a fish porter, which fooled no one who knew his ink-stained fingers.

Felix cornered him at dawn on the landing stairs, and the boy took one look at the bundle under the clockmaker's arm and went white.

"You shouldn't have those here," Ivo whispered. "You shouldn't have them anywhere."

"They're hers. Astrid's. She wrote down everything—the purge, the names taken, the names given." He watched the boy flinch. "You knew. You saw some of it."

"I saw enough to be dead twice over." Ivo's jaw worked. His eyes, red-rimmed and exhausted, swept the empty stairwell. "What do you want from me?"

"Nothing you don't want to give. But the historians are coming. Imperial historians, to seal the archive—to decide what the record says happened at the observatory. Once they've finished, whatever they leave out will never have existed." Felix held his gaze. "You were there. Your memory is a document too, whether or not anyone writes it down."

The boy stood for a long moment with the grey river light on his face, and Felix watched a war happen behind his eyes—fear against grief, survival against the debt owed to people who had not survived. He recognized the war because he had fought it himself, alone, over a workbench, for three sleepless nights.

"She used to let me wind the sidereal clock," Ivo said finally. "Nobody else. Just me. She said timekeeping was a moral discipline—that if you kept honest hours, you'd want honest everything else." His voice cracked on the last word. "They took her on a Tuesday. She'd just calibrated the transit instrument. Her notes were still on the desk."

"Then help me make sure her notes survive somewhere other than a sealed archive."

The circle, such as it was, met twice in Marta's room before the ceremony. It could not have been called a conspiracy; it lacked the numbers for conspiracy and the confidence for anything so grand. It was a housekeeper who knew which corridors of the observatory the guards neglected, an apprentice who knew which cabinets had false backs, and a clockmaker who knew, better than anyone alive, the interior geography of the master clock itself.

"The restart ceremony is our problem and our door," Felix told them, unrolling a diagram of the clock's case drawn from memory, precise to the quarter-inch. "The whole city will be watching the dial. The officers will be watching the crowd. Nobody will be watching the pendulum hatch—but it opens onto the service stair, and the service stair lets out by the archive corridor."

"The corridor they're sealing," Marta said.

"The very same. The historians arrive tomorrow morning to inventory and seal. The ceremony begins at noon. If I go in early to make final adjustments—which I am officially required to do—and leave the letters inside the case, in the compartment behind the striking train, then we need only one moment of opportunity to carry them out while every eye is on the hour hand."

"And if someone looks in the case?" Ivo asked.

"Nobody looks in the case. That's how the letters stayed hidden through a purge and a conquest. People look at clocks. They don't look *inside* them." He paused. "There's one more thing. The letters need copies. If the originals are lost, the words must survive elsewhere. Marta has the handwriting for transcription; Ivo knows Astrid's shorthand marginalia. By tomorrow noon we should have three fair copies, distributed—one to stay with Marta, one with me, one to travel."

"Travel where?" Marta asked.

"Out of the city. To anyone who will print it while the ink of the conquest is still wet."

They worked through the night. Marta wrote in a copperplate so steady it seemed impossible it belonged to the trembling woman of the first meeting; grief, Felix decided, could hollow a person out and fill the hollow with purpose. Ivo decoded the shorthand by lamplight, murmuring phrases like a litany—"the list of fourteen... the officer with the scarred cheek... she signed the confession they forged or they would have taken the instruments apart bolt by bolt..."—and each phrase landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Toward the small hours Felix found himself weeping quietly over a gear he was polishing, not for the gear, and neither of the others remarked on it. This, too, was part of the discipline Astrid had believed in: keeping honest hours, however heavy.

Noon came bright and cold, and Vellamark turned out in its thousands. The square below the observatory hill was a field of hats and banners, the imperial eagle stitched where last autumn the markgravin's stag had flown. Trumpets. A provost's speech about the empire's gift of *orderly time*. Felix stood at the clock case with his tools laid out like a surgeon's, the letters already sewn into a canvas satchel beneath his apron—he had gone in at dawn, made the transfer, and survived four hours of a census clerk's polite, unblinking presence in the tower, a man who asked about gear ratios and never once looked away from Felix's hands.

At half past eleven, with the crowd roaring at some flourish on the platform, Felix slipped through the pendulum hatch.

The service stair was dark and smelled of dust and old starlight—that faint mineral scent of instruments long aligned to things farther than empires. He moved fast, satchel tight to his ribs, past the archive corridor where he could see, through the wire-glass panel, the historians' crates already packed and stenciled, the seal wax warming over a spirit lamp. Thirty seconds. He counted them like ticks.

He was ten steps from the stair's bottom door when the clerk stepped out of the shadow beside it.

"Master Oyelaran." The man's voice was mild, almost kind. "This wing is sealed until after the ceremony."

"The provost asked me to verify the striking mechanism from below," Felix said, and heard his own lie come out smooth for once, blessedly smooth. "The hammer lift needs weight on the fourth bell."

The clerk's eyes went to the satchel's outline beneath the apron. Somewhere above them, the crowd began a rising chant, counting down the minutes. The clerk's hand drifted toward the whistle chained at his breast—

And the hour struck.

Twelve great strokes rolled down through the tower's bones, through the walls and floor and the air itself, so vast a sound that the clerk flinched toward it, instinct pulling every head in Vellamark upward at once—and Felix Oyelaran was through the door and into the crowd before the twelfth stroke finished dying, swallowed by ten thousand upturned faces, his heart slamming like a broken escapement, the letters against his ribs, and behind him, thin and furious in the tower's shadow, the sound of a whistle beginning to shriek.
