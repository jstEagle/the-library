## 4. On the Record

The clock had been running for six days, and already the city had stopped looking up at it. That was the empire's gift, Felix thought—not to break things, but to make them ordinary again so quickly that no one remembered they had ever been otherwise. The hands moved. The bells rang the quarter hours over Vellamark's rooftops as if nothing had ever interrupted them, as if an astronomer named Ilse Vantorre had not wound her last secret into the case of that same clock and walked out of history through a door no one had found.

Felix heard the striking of ten from his workshop and did not look up. He was copying the letters for the third time.

The first copy he had made slowly, in his careful workman's hand, the hand he used on invoices and repair ledgers—a hand that said nothing about its owner except that he could form letters neatly. The second copy was faster, made at night with the shutters closed and a towel stuffed under the door against sound carrying, though who would be listening in a clockmaker's rooms above a silent shop, he could not say. This third copy was for the correspondents, and it had to be perfect, because imperfection gave the imperial historians their knife: *an unreliable transcription, a forgery, the fantasy of a disgruntled tradesman.*

He wrote until his wrist ached, then blotted each page and stacked it with the others, tied in plain string. No wax. Wax was a signature; wax invited comparison.

*You will have to decide whose name goes at the bottom,* the housekeeper Maren had told him two nights ago, sitting in her narrow room above the tannery with her mended hands folded in her lap. She had served the observatory for thirty-one years. She had carried Ilse Vantorre's tea up the spiral stair every evening of those years, and she had recognized the astronomer's handwriting before Felix finished unfolding the first letter. She had wept once, briefly, and then dried her eyes and begun to remember—dates, names, the carriage that came at midnight, the young assistant who never came back for his coat.

"A testimony without a witness is just paper," she said now—or had said; everything lately felt like one long conversation continuing behind his eyes. "They'll ask: who found this? Who kept it? Who vouches? If you put no name, they'll say it came from nowhere, and things that come from nowhere can be sent back there."

The apprentice, Tam, sixteen years old and left behind when the staff were taken because he had been sent out for lamp oil, had argued the other way. "Sign nothing," Tam had said, jaw set, hands trembling. "They took my mother's brother for less. Sign nothing and stay."

And Felix, who had spent forty-one years practicing invisibility—who had come to Vellamark as a foreign journeyman and learned to walk through the annexation like a man walking through rain, head down, coat collar up—had discovered something in himself that surprised him: he was tired. He was tired the way a mechanism is tired when it has been holding a spring compressed too long. All that stored force had to go somewhere.

He signed the copies.

Not the originals—the originals he had already decided about—but the three fair copies, each bearing at the bottom, beneath the astronomer's transcribed words, a line in his own neat tradesman's hand:

*Found in the case of the master clock of the Vellamark Observatory by Felix Oyelaran, clockmaker, who certifies these pages a true and faithful copy of the documents so recovered, this ninth day of the month of Reaping.*

Then he wrapped the stack in oilcloth, put on his good coat, and went out into a city wearing its new flags.

* * *

The correspondents kept rooms at the Anchorfish, the harborside inn where the empire tolerated foreign eyes because tolerating them looked better than expelling them. There were three: a grey woman from the southern leagues who wrote under a man's name; a thin Merovene with ink-stained cuffs and a memory like a gear-train; and a young one whose nationality shifted depending on who asked, but whose questions never did.

Felix had gone to them one at a time. The grey woman first, because Maren knew her sister. He had sat across from her in the taproom's noise and slid the oilcloth bundle across the table as if passing salt, and watched her read the first page, and the second, and seen her go very still in the way of a person recalculating everything.

"You understand," she had said quietly, not looking up, "that if I print this, they will come for whoever's name is here." Her finger tapped the bottom of the page. "That is your name."

"I know what my name weighs," Felix said. "I've been carrying it lightly for forty years. It's time it did some work."

Now, this morning, he made the rounds again. To the grey woman he delivered nothing—she already had her copy, sewn into the lining of her trunk—but he confirmed to her the location of the bridge, and the hour, and the meaning of a lantern hung from the downstream rail. To the Merovene he passed a single page, one page only, enough to make him hungry for the whole, which would arrive by other hands after Felix was elsewhere. The young one he did not approach at all; approaching the young ones was how informers earned their keep.

By noon the copies were beyond his reach, moving along three different roads out of the city, and Felix felt lighter and more naked than he had ever felt in his life. Somewhere on the southern road a courier's satchel held his name beside the word *testimony*. There was no taking it back. A struck bell cannot un-ring.

* * *

The bridge was called the Weighmaster's Bridge, old stone, older than the empire, older than the kingdom the empire had swallowed. Beneath its upstream arch, at low water, the foundation stones formed a dry pocket where generations of smugglers had cached goods and, Maren had told him, where Ilse Vantorre had met her sources in the last years—ferry men, clerks, a customs officer or two, anyone who could carry a number or a name out past the checkpoints. The astronomer had understood that the truth needed ferries too.

Felix went at dusk, when the fish carts had gone home and the lamplighters were still on the far streets. The original letters rode inside his shirt, against his chest, wrapped in the oilcloth and then in waxed leather, because stone keeps things dry but not forever, and he intended this cache to outlast empires.

He climbed down the iron ring-studs set into the pier, boots finding the grooves worn by decades of smugglers' feet, and crouched in the pocket of dark stone with the river sliding past a hand's breadth below. Somewhere under that water, he thought, was whatever door Ilse Vantorre had walked through. Perhaps she had gone into the river. Perhaps onto a ship. The letters didn't say; they ended abruptly, mid-sentence almost, the way lives end when someone else is writing the schedule.

He worked fast. Loose mortar at the base of the third course—he had pried it two nights before, coming here in the dark just to prepare, feeling absurd and consecrated both. He set the bundle deep, seated the stone, and swept the crumbled mortar into the current with his palm. Then he did the thing he had not planned but could not stop himself from doing: he laid his hand flat on the cold stone, the way a clockmaker rests his palm on a finished movement, listening with his skin for the tick of it.

"Kept safe," he said aloud, and the river took the words.

Above him, faintly, the observatory bell rang seven. The clock he had mended, marking the hour for a city that had already stopped noticing. He climbed back up to the roadway with his hands shaking—not fear, exactly, or not only—and hung the small lantern from the downstream rail, where it swung once and steadied. The signal. *Delivered. Buried. Done.*

* * *

They came for him nine days later, which was longer than he had expected and proved, later, that the correspondents had gotten at least two copies out of the country first.

It was not soldiers. It was the census taker again, the same precise man with the same precise ledger, standing in his workshop doorway among the hanging pendulums and the smell of brass shavings. Two guards waited in the street, studying the sky.

"Master Oyelaran." The census taker consulted his book even though he plainly knew what it said. "Questions have arisen regarding certain documents. Your name has arisen in connection with them."

"Has it," Felix said.

"It has. You will understand that I am required to note your response." The man looked up, and for the first time there was something in his face that was not procedure—something almost like reluctance, or perhaps envy, Felix could never afterward decide which. "How do you answer?"

Felix set down his file. Around him the workshop held everything he had: the tools of his trade, the trade of his hands, the small clean life he had built out of staying small. Down the street, at the corner, he could see Tam loitering badly outside the chandler's, sent by Maren, refusing to leave. Behind the census taker the light of late afternoon lay along the flagstones all the way to the river.

"I answered already," Felix said. "In writing. Under my own name. You may cite the account—it is being printed in three languages."

Something flickered across the ledger page between them: the sound, almost audible, of a record becoming impossible to falsify quietly.

"Then I must ask you to come with us," the census taker said.

"I expected you would." Felix took his coat from the peg, and his hat, and paused at the door to look back at his workshop—at the clocks ticking in their separate faithful rhythms, all of them keeping time without him. "Lock it well," he told Tam, who had abandoned all pretense and stood white-faced at the chandler's corner. "Maren has the key."

They walked him through the city at the hour when the lamps were lit, and the people of Vellamark came out of their doorways to watch him pass, and Felix, who had spent his whole life being nobody, saw in their faces something he had never been given room to see before: recognition. Not of his face—they didn't know his face—but of the act. Word had traveled ahead of him, faster than any guard. *The clockmaker. The one who signed.*

Maren stood at her window above the tannery as he went by. She did not wave. She touched two fingers to her heart and then held them open toward him, palm out—the old observatory gesture, he realized, the one the staff used across the dome when the telescope was aligned and the night's work could begin. *Seen. Recorded. Held.*

He returned it, walking, hands free, head high for the first time in forty-one years, while behind him the great clock rang out eight over the roofs of Vellamark, and the sound rolled down to the river, and past the bridge, and out toward the sea where his name was already landing on foreign shores, arriving, like everything worth keeping, in time.
