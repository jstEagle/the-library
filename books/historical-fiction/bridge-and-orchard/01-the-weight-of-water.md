## 1. The Weight of Water

There is an art to being overlooked, and I have studied it the way other people study scripture.

The first lesson is posture. Stand slightly stooped, as though you were assembled in a hurry from spare parts. Keep your hands busy with something dull — a pen, a blotter, the hem of a sleeve. The second lesson is the eyes. Never meet anyone's gaze for longer than it takes to say *good morning, factor* and mean nothing by it. And the third lesson, the one that took me the longest to learn, is this: be useful in exactly the amount that makes you indispensable to the work and invisible to the men who sign the work off.

I had been practicing these lessons for nine years, since the day the Halvard Trading Company bound me by indenture, and I flattered myself that I had achieved a kind of mastery. In Vessing, that is the closest thing to freedom a diver's child can buy.

The morning bell at the counting-house rang at six, as it always did, and I was at my desk by half past, as I always was, because the factor's clerk Grell — no, not Grell, the *under*-clerk Voss, Grell was far above such small weather — Voss liked to find at least one person already working when he arrived, so that he could feel the particular satisfaction of a man whose subordinates fear him correctly. I was happy to provide that service. Fear, correctly calibrated, is a currency I keep in good supply.

The counting-house smelled of ink, tallow, and old salt, because everything in Vessing smelled of old salt eventually. The tide was out, and through the window I could see the pilings of the lower docks standing naked and dripping, furred with weed, looking like the legs of some enormous animal caught mid-stride. At low tide you could see what the water hid: broken pots, a boot, once — I was told — a man who had owed the company money. The sea keeps Vessing's secrets at the bottom of its pockets and turns them out twice a day for anyone patient enough to look.

I was not patient enough to look. I had learned, early, that looking is how people get noticed.

"Manifests," Voss said, dropping a stack on my desk without breaking stride. "The *Kestrel's* cargo, the *Merrow's* cargo, and the oyster barges. Factor wants them copied clean before noon."

"Before noon," I repeated, in the tone of a man who had no opinion on the matter, because I didn't. Opinions are heavy. I traveled light.

The manifests were the usual litany: salted herring, timber, dye-mordant, and pearls — always pearls, because pearls were Vessing's reason for existing and the company's reason for owning Vessing, which are not the same thing, though the company had spent a hundred years insisting they were. I copied the figures in my best clerk's hand, which is a hand with no personality at all, a hand that could belong to anyone, a hand designed to leave no more trace on the world than a gull's footprint on wet sand.

Copying is a strange kind of work. You read everything and understand nothing, or you understand everything and admit to nothing, and after nine years I had gotten very good at the second kind of reading. A manifest tells you what a ship carried. It does not tell you what the ship was *for*. I had stopped asking, around the age of thirteen, what happened to the pearls between the divers' baskets and the company's strongroom. The asking had gotten a boy named Tovan Rell a broken hand and a month in the company jail, and Tovan had been the factor's own nephew. After that, the numbers and I came to an arrangement: I copied them, and they left me alone.

My scar ached, the way it did on damp mornings.

I should explain about the scar. It sits below my left knee, a pale crescent the length of a finger, and I have spent nine years making sure that no one at the counting-house has ever seen it. It is not a dramatic scar. It is not the kind of scar that wins sympathy or tells a good story in a tavern. It is the kind of scar that tells a very short, very bad story: *this child dove for pearls before the law said a child could be bound to a desk instead.* The oyster beds off the headland took it from me when I was eight, courtesy of a shell edge and a current that wanted to keep me. My mother had already been two years in the water by then, in the permanent sense. The sea had taken her whole, and taken a piece of me as a down payment.

The company, in its mercy, had converted the remainder into an indenture. Nine years of service, they said, in exchange for the debt of my upkeep, and the term would run until my twenty-first year, and the paper said so in language so dense that I suspected even the men who wrote it couldn't untangle it. I had read my contract exactly once, the day they signed me over, and I had read it the way you read a sentence being read *at* you — with your eyes and not your heart, because the heart is a luxury item and the company does not extend credit.

I keep the contract's terms in my head the way a prisoner keeps count of days. Four more years. Four years, and then the paper burns and I walk out of the counting-house and no one in Vessing has ever had occasion to remember my name.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan. A diver's child survives by being unremarkable — my mother's sister told me that the week after the funeral, in the flat voice of a woman reciting arithmetic, and I had built a life on the arithmetic. Don't volunteer. Don't object. Don't be interesting. Be the clerk who is always at his desk, whose sums are always right, whose face is always the face you forget the moment you look away.

It was, I want to be clear, a good plan. It had kept me alive and unbroken for nine years, which in Vessing is a better record than most manage.

It simply had not, in nine years, made me happy. But happiness was not on the manifest.

* * *

At midmorning the light came through the counting-house windows at the angle that made the ink shine, and Voss appeared at my shoulder the way he liked to, without warning, to catch me at nothing. I was at nothing. I was always at nothing. He looked over my copies, found them flawless, and was visibly annoyed about it, because a man like Voss needs flaws the way a fish needs water.

"The oyster barge figures," he said. "Factor wants them cross-checked against the dive-master's tallies. You'll go down to the quay."

"Today?"

"Today. Now. And Nystrom—" he paused, savoring whatever he was about to say, "—try not to smell like a fish when you come back. Some of us work indoors."

I said nothing, because the correct response to Voss was always nothing, delivered promptly. I took the tallies and went out into the wind.

The quay at low tide was a different country from the quay at high. The mud stank sweetly of rot and iodine, and the divers were hauling their baskets up the ladder-rungs, their shoulders scarred white with cold, their hands cracked from the brine. I knew those hands. I had those hands, under the clerk's ink. There is a particular look divers get around the eyes, a permanent squint against the glare off the water, and when I pass a mirror I can still find it in my own, lurking under the clerk's blankness like a drowned thing under ice.

I collected the dive-master's tallies — old Brandt, who had known my mother, and who looked at me a moment too long the way he always did, as though he were about to say her name and thought better of it. He thought better of it every time. It was, in its way, a kindness.

"Your sums are good, little clerk," he said, handing over the slate. He had called me *little clerk* for nine years, and it was the only name anyone used for me besides the company number. "Still writing yourself into their books, then."

"Still diving for their pearls, then," I said, and he laughed, because between the two of us the joke was old enough to have grandchildren.

I checked the tallies against the manifest figures standing right there on the quay, because I am thorough, and thoroughness is a form of invisibility — no one suspects the clerk who checks his work. The herring matched. The timber matched. The pearl baskets from the morning's dive came to four hundred and six, and the manifest said four hundred and six.

Everything matched. Everything always matched. That was the thing about the company's books: they were beautiful. I had heard a visiting factor once describe them as *a cathedral of figures*, and he had meant it as praise, and it was, and it should not have been, though it took me years to understand why.

I walked back up from the quay with the tallies under my arm and the wind pushing at my back, and I did not know yet that the beauty was a lie, that somewhere in that cathedral of figures there was a crack running through the glass. I was still, that morning, a man with four years left on a piece of paper, a scar hidden under a clerk's trouser-leg, and a life folded up small enough to fit in a ledger's margin.

The tide was coming back in. It always does. That is the one promise Vessing keeps.

* * *

The afternoon went the way afternoons went: ink, blotting sand, the scratch of forty pens in a room that smelled of tallow and ambition. I copied the *Merrow's* manifest twice because Voss had lost the first copy, which was Voss's way of losing things he had never had. I sharpened pens. I carried a box of documents to the record room, which is the coldest room in the counting-house and the one I liked best, because in the record room no one checks on the clerk who is shelving files, and I could stand among the company's paper memory and breathe.

It was in the record room, that afternoon, doing nothing more interesting than reshelving, that I first felt it — though *felt* is too strong a word. Noticing, for a man in my position, is a dangerous act, and I had trained myself out of it the way you train yourself out of a stammer. But something snagged. A small thing. The kind of small thing a careful man walks past a thousand times before the thousand-and-first, when for no reason at all, he stops.

An old manifest. Three years back, the *Merrow*, spring season. A pearl shipment entered in the column — and I would swear, standing there in the cold with the dust settling on my sleeves, that I had copied that same shipment that very morning. Same weight, same grade, same dive-master's mark. Entered twice, three years apart, like a coin that had been spent and somehow found its way back into the same purse.

I stood there longer than was safe. Long enough for the record room's cold to get into my bones. And then I did the thing I had been trained to do, the thing that had kept me whole for nine years.

I shelved the manifest, closed the drawer, and thought absolutely nothing about it.

That is what I told myself, anyway, walking back to my desk with my face arranged into its usual blankness. I thought nothing about it through the last hours of the afternoon. I thought nothing about it while Voss criticized my penmanship, which was flawless. I thought nothing about it while I washed my pens, banked the stove, and let myself out into the blue hour when the lamps are lit along the quay and the tide is high enough to hide whatever the sea is keeping.

But a number, once seen, does not go back to being a number. It sits in you like a splinter. You can leave it alone, and the flesh will close around it, and maybe nothing happens. Or maybe the sea gets in, the way the sea gets into everything in Vessing, and works at it, and works at it, until one morning the splinter works its way to the surface and you have to decide what to do with it.

I went home along the harbor road, past the Pearlhouse with its lamps and its noise and its mended nets drying on the rails, past the taverns and the rope-walks, to my room above the cooper's shed, which was cold and narrow and entirely mine for four more years. I ate bread and dried fish. I did not light a lamp, because lamp-oil is money and money is the distance between me and the twenty-first year of my life.

And in the dark, with the tide turning below the window, I found that the number was still there. Four hundred and six. Twice. Three years apart.

I lay on my cot and told myself the story a careful man tells: *clerks err. Columns repeat. It is nothing.* And it was probably nothing. It was almost certainly nothing. Nine years of survival said: walk past it. My mother's ghost, if ghosts bother with advice, would have said the same. The sea takes the curious first — that was the first lesson the oyster beds ever taught me, before they taught me the second one, which is written in a crescent of scar below my knee.

But I was my mother's child before I was the company's, and somewhere under all that trained blankness, in the place the indenture paper never reached, something in me — quiet, patient, and for the first time in nine years, awake — turned the number over in the dark and did not let it go.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I would walk past it. Tomorrow I would be invisible again.

The tide came in below my window, patient as a creditor, and covered everything.
