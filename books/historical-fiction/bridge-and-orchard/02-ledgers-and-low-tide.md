## 2. Ledgers and Low Tide

There is an art to being forgotten, and I have studied it the way other people study music.

The first rule is posture. You must never slouch — slouching draws the eye the way a crooked picture does — but you must not sit too straight either, because straightness reads as ambition, and ambition is remembered. What you want is the posture of furniture. Of a thing that has always been in the room and always will be, and therefore need not be looked at.

The second rule is the hands. A clerk's hands are his signature, and mine have learned to move across a page without flourish, without hesitation, without the small theatrical pauses that say *notice me, I am thinking*. I copy numbers the way rain falls on a roof: evenly, endlessly, asking nothing.

The third rule — the most important one, the one Mama Osk taught me before the company ever did, back when she was still mending nets and I was still small enough to hide behind her — is this: *a diver's child survives by surfacing quietly.* No splash. No ripple. The pearl boats of Vessing are crewed by men who can spot a bubble from thirty feet, and the Halvard Trading Company is crewed by men no less sharp-eyed. Bubbles get followed. Bubbles get people drowned.

Nine years I have kept these rules. Nine years of manifests and tallies and the particular gray light of the counting-house at dawn, and in nine years I have never once been summoned to the factor's office for anything worse than a smudged page. Factor Grell does not know my mother's name. He barely knows mine. In his ledger of people I am filed under *clerks, junior, unremarkable*, and I have worked very hard, every single day, to deserve that filing.

I am telling you all this so you will understand what it cost me, on the morning of the spring tides, when a column of numbers refused to reconcile — and what it cost me again, later, when I could not make myself stop looking at it.

* * *

The counting-house of the Halvard Trading Company sits on stilts above the harbor mud, so that at low tide you can hear the water sucking at the pilings directly beneath your chair, a sound like the port itself breathing in its sleep. There are fourteen desks in the great room, and mine is the fourth from the window, positioned — I once calculated, out of pure professional vanity — to catch the least interesting share of the morning light.

My task that morning was the Vessing outbound manifest for the month of Thaw, fair-copied onto company vellum for the archive. This is work I could do asleep, which is fortunate, because parts of me usually are. You take the working papers, you take the quill the factor allows you, and you transfer column to column: pearls by the grain, dried cod by the barrel, salt, hemp, whale oil, the small honest commerce of a small gray port. The vellum goes to the capital. The working papers go to the furnace-room, per policy, after audit.

Per policy. I want that word on the record, because *policy* is the company's true scripture, and I can recite it the way the divers of the Pearlhouse recite the tides. Policy says clerks copy but do not read. Policy says discrepancies are flagged in red and reported to the under-factor before the hour turns. Policy says the clerk who flags a discrepancy receives a commendation of one silver mark, and the clerk who fails to flag one receives dismissal, and the clerk who *investigates* one —

Well. There is no policy line for the third case, which tells you something about how often it has come up. Ports like Vessing do not survive on curiosity. Curiosity is a luxury import, like cinnamon, and neither has ever cleared the Tide Gate in my lifetime.

At half past the morning bell I reached the pearl entries for the twelfth of Thaw.

Pearls are entered by weight and by consignment, and each consignment carries a lot-number assigned at the receiving floor. The twelfth was a good day for pearls — the *Merrow's Luck* had come in off the beds with a strong haul, four consignments, lots 4-112 through 4-115. I copied them the way I copy everything, my hand moving in its rain-on-a-roof rhythm, and then I turned the page of the working papers and found lot 4-114 again.

This happens. Divers' hands shake, receiving clerks are cold, ink smears, a lot gets entered twice and struck through once. It happens perhaps twice a year, and when it happens there is always a strike-through, always a red marginal note in the under-factor's crabbed hand: *dup. void.*

There was no strike-through. There was no red note.

Lot 4-114 — eleven grains, seed to button grade, consigned to the *Merrow's Luck*, insured value forty-two crowns — appeared twice, both times clean, both times carried forward into the monthly total. Which meant the company had shipped, insured, and been paid against eleven grains of pearls that existed on paper only. Forty-two crowns of air.

Or — said a voice in my head, very quietly, the way you speak near a sleeping animal — it meant eleven grains of real pearls had come off the *Merrow's Luck* and vanished somewhere between the receiving floor and the vellum, and forty-two crowns had gone into a pocket that was not the company's pocket, and the paper trail had been tidied so carefully that even the duplication had been left *deliberately unstruck*, because whoever tidied it knew that a struck entry invites questions and a clean double entry invites nothing at all.

I sat very still. Under my chair, the tide sucked at the pilings.

Here is what the rules required of me: flag it in red, report it, collect my silver mark, and be noticed — loudly, permanently, by name — as the clerk who flagged it. And here is what instinct, nine years deep and bone-solid, required of me: look at nothing, mark nothing, carry the total forward, turn the page.

I turned the page.

Understand that this was obedience. Call it cowardice if you like — I have, often enough, in the dark, with the honesty that comes only after midnight — but I prefer *obedience*, because obedience implies there was someone whose teaching I was keeping faith with, and there was. My mother dove the oyster beds off the headland for eleven years and surfaced every single time without a splash, and she taught me the same skill on land before the fever took her: *be water, Lior. Water fills any shape it's given and keeps none.* The company gave me a shape. I filled it. Lot 4-114 went into my memory the way water takes a dye — silently, thoroughly, and with nowhere visible to point at.

I finished the manifest by noon. I ate my bread in the counting-house yard with the other junior clerks, and I said nothing, because saying nothing is my native language, and while the others complained about the factor and the weather and the price of ale, I sat among them like a fourth kind of silence and let the number turn over in me like a stone in the surf.

Eleven grains. Forty-two crowns. Twice.

Once, at supper, Mama Osk told me that the sea gives up its dead only when it's ready, and that the trick to surviving a drowning is knowing which way the current runs *underneath* the panic. I thought about that a great deal that afternoon, because underneath my well-trained panic — the tidy, policy-shaped fear of the red flag and the silver mark — there was another current running, slow and cold and going somewhere entirely else.

It was asking a question. I did my best not to hear it.

* * *

The trouble with being a good clerk is that you cannot help being a good clerk, even when you are trying not to be.

By the afternoon bell I had fair-copied two more manifests and reconciled the salt accounts, and my hands had done all of this flawlessly while the rest of me was elsewhere — down on the receiving floor, eleven days ago, watching the *Merrow's Luck* unload. Because here was the thing. Here was the splinter working its way under the skin of my trained blankness: *I had been on the yard that morning.* Junior clerks fetch things; it is the tax we pay for our desks. I had carried the tally-slate down to the quay while lot 4-114 came up the ladder in its little canvas bag, and I remembered — now, uselessly, vividly — the receiving clerk weighing it, entering it, and the under-factor's man standing at his shoulder. Not unusual. The under-factor's man stood at everyone's shoulder on pearl days.

But I also remembered this, and I had not thought about it then, because remembering things is not what invisible people do: the bag had come up wet. Not sea-wet. *Brine-vat* wet, the heavy dripping wet of pearls that have been washed and soaked, which is done on the beds, before the lot is bagged — except the *Merrow's Luck* had reported rough water on the grounds that week, and rough water means the washing vats stay lashed shut, and unwashed pearls go up the ladder dry.

Small thing. A wet bag. Eleven grains of nothing.

Except that I had spent my childhood on a pearling deck, before the indenture, before the number they gave me replaced the name, and my hands know pearl cargo the way priests' hands know their beads. Wet bag, dry week. Somebody had opened those washing vats between the beds and the quay, and what you wash pearls to remove is the count of them — the shell-grit, the false bottoms, the little smugglers' deceits divers have practiced since before there were companies to deceive. Or what you do in a brine vat, if you are clever, is soak a second, lesser batch until it *reads* like the first, and sell the first twice, and let the difference walk ashore in a coat pocket.

I am not clever about ledgers. I want to be clear about that. I am a copier, a fourth-from-the-window, a furniture-postured nobody, and I had no proof of anything, and proof would have been worthless to me anyway, because proof is a thing you *show people*, and showing people things is the opposite of everything I have ever survived by.

But I could not strike the thought. That was the trouble. A discrepancy in the books, you flag or ignore. A discrepancy in your own memory — a wet bag, a dry week, forty-two crowns of air wearing the honest face of the *Merrow's Luck* — that kind does not reconcile. That kind sits in you at your desk, fourth from the window, catching the least interesting share of the light, and hums.

* * *

Factor Grell crossed the great room at the evening bell.

He does this most evenings — a slow circuit of the fourteen desks, like a landlord inspecting his rooms — and for nine years I have performed my part in the ritual, which consists of looking busy in a specifically uninteresting way: shoulders slightly rounded, eyes on the middle distance of my own page, quill moving. Grell does not stop at my desk. Grell has never stopped at my desk. Stopping implies expectation, and expectation implies interest, and interest is the one coin I cannot afford to be worth.

That evening he stopped.

Not at my desk — three desks down, at young Pell's, over some question of hemp weights — but close enough that I heard the whole exchange, close enough that I could have reached out and touched the shadow of him on my own page. And I felt it, that animal thing every indentured person carries in the spine: *he is near, be smaller, be smaller.* I bent over my salt accounts and wrote figures I am sure to this day were correct, because my hands have more discipline than my heartbeat, and I waited for the moment to pass.

It passed. Pell answered some question badly, got cuffed verbally for it, and Grell moved on down the row of desks and out the door, and the room exhaled the way it always did, fourteen spines relaxing in sequence like a wave going down a rope.

But in the half-second before he moved on, Grell paused — a hitch no longer than a misplaced stitch — at the stack of working papers waiting for the furnace cart by the door. The Thaw papers. Pearl days among them. He looked at the stack the way a man looks at a shelf he has recently dusted: briefly, proprietorially, satisfied.

Then he left, and I sat there with my heart doing something unsanctioned, understanding two things at once, the way you understand a wave has lifted you only when it starts to set you down.

The first thing: whoever owned the missing forty-two crowns, they were not afraid of the working papers. They were not afraid of clerks, or audits, or the furnace. Whatever the second entry of lot 4-114 was, it was not a mistake that needed hiding. It was a message that had already been delivered to whoever mattered, and the papers were trash to be burned, and the burning was itself part of the tidiness.

The second thing, arriving slower and colder, the way deep water arrives colder than surface water: *I* was the loose thread now. Not because I had flagged anything, or written anything, or breathed a word — but because I had *seen*, and seeing leaves a residue, and residue is what investigations are spun from. If anyone ever pulled that thread — if the capital ever queried the Thaw totals, if the insurers ever asked why forty-two crowns of pearls weighed light — the trail would run backward through the vellum to the working papers to the hand that copied them, and the hand was mine, and the hand had a name, or at least a number.

For nine years I had been safe because I was nothing to anyone. In one morning, one column, one wet canvas bag remembered too well, I had become something to someone, potentially, contingently — a witness who did not yet know what he'd witnessed. And the truly terrible realization, the one I carried home along the low-tide mudflats with the light dying copper over the harbor, was that the fear arrived second.

First — before the fear, before the policy, before the spine-deep training of a lifetime — had come the question. Whose pocket. How many lots. How many years.

Curiosity, Mama Osk would say, shaking her head at me over her mending. The luxury import. And here I was, a diver's child who had never owned two coins to rub together, suddenly rich beyond the dreams of Factor Grell himself.

* * *

The Pearlhouse was loud when I got in, louder than usual, which meant the storm was closer than the sky admitted. Old bones and old scars all ache ahead of weather, and the Pearlhouse is made almost entirely of both. Dagny was already cursing at her net-weights by lamplight, swearing the wind had shifted twice since supper, and the younger ones — the orphaned divers, my successors in everything — were being shooed away from the windows by Mama Osk, who stood a moment in the doorway looking at me with those eyes that miss nothing that matters and pointedly miss everything that doesn't.

"You've got your thinking face on," she said. "I haven't seen that face since you were eight and stole the ferry-boat."

"I don't have a thinking face," I said. "I have the face company policy issued me. One size fits all."

"Mm." She looked at me a moment longer, the way she looks at weather. "Storm's coming up from the southeast. Barometer fell off the table after dark. Get your sleep."

I said I would. I lay in my narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the wind come up the harbor, and below my window the tide turned and began its long patient pull out toward the heads, uncovering the flats, the pilings, the bones of the port — all the things that are only visible when the water stands aside.

Somewhere out in that darkening chop, boats were running for shelter. Somewhere in the counting-house on its stilts, the Thaw papers sat waiting for the furnace cart, carrying their quiet doubled entry like a passenger. And somewhere between those two facts — between the storm coming ashore and the ledger going into the fire — I understood that I was already standing at low tide myself, everything I'd spent nine years keeping covered laid out and gleaming in whatever light was coming.

I did not sleep. I lay there and let the question have its way with me, and it was not about forty-two crowns, not really. It was older than that. It was the question every sold child asks eventually, the one the indenture paper is designed to keep from ever being asked:

*If the numbers can lie about pearls — what else have they lied about?*

What else is written twice, cleanly, unstruck, in the book of me?

Outside, the wind rose to a shriek among the pilings, and the rain came in off the sea like something with intent, and far down the shore, where the docks groaned against their moorings, the long night of the storm had begun — though I did not yet know, could not have imagined, what the sea was about to give back with the morning.
