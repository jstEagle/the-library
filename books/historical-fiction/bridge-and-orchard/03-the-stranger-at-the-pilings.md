## 3. The Stranger at the Pilings

The storm came in off the water the way debts come due—slowly enough that you could pretend it wasn't happening, and then all at once, with nowhere left to hide.

I'd been sent down to the harbor warehouse to reconcile a manifest against the tally-clerk's marks, which was the sort of job they gave clerks who had learned to be quiet. The Halvard Trading Company loved me for exactly that quality. Nine years of being unremarkable had made me very good at it. I could pass through a room of company men the way fog passes through a rigging—present, damp, unnoticed. It was a skill my mother's people would have recognized. Divers learn early that the thing below you survives by not being interesting to the things above.

By the time I finished the count, the sky over Vessing had gone the color of a bruise going bad, and old Tam the warehouse keeper squinted out the door and told me I'd best run for it before the wind decided to keep me.

He wasn't wrong. The first drops hit like thrown gravel. By the time I reached the long walkway between the counting-house and the fish market, the rain had stopped pretending to be weather and become an argument—a full-throated, sideways screaming argument between the sea and everything man had built to hold it back. Lamplighters had given up entirely. The gutters ran brown. Somewhere behind me a shutter banged itself loose and went clattering down the lane like a frightened chicken.

I should have gone straight home. Home, in my case, was a narrow cot in the company dormitory above the salt stores, where I slept among forty other indentured bodies and was grateful, most nights, for the roof. The sensible route was up Harbor Street, head down, hand on the wall, ten minutes of misery and then dry wool and sleep.

Instead I went along the pilings.

I want to be honest about why, because honesty is the one luxury I couldn't afford and took anyway. There was no good reason. The low tide boards under the eastern dock were where the Pearlhouse children stashed things the company men shouldn't find—gull eggs mostly, and once a whole cask of brandywine that Mama Osk pretended she knew nothing about while drinking all of it. When the storms tore loose whatever the boats lost overboard, the tide pushed it into that gap: rope, floats, sometimes a crate worth opening. A diver's child looks at storm-wracked water and sees salvage. It's in the blood, like webbing between the toes we don't quite have.

Also—and this is the part I usually leave out—I'd seen something.

A light. Or the thought of a light. Down beneath the dock where no lamp had any business being, a flicker that might have been lightning playing tricks on wet wood, or might have been a shuttered lantern guttering in a gap where a person might wedge themselves out of the wind. Nobody sane would be under there. But I had grown up among people who dove for pearls in water cold enough to stop a heart, so my definition of *sane* had been set generously from birth.

I got down on my hands and knees on the slick planking at the dock's landward end, where the gap opened under the lowest crossbeam, and I called into the dark.

"Anyone there?"

Wind answered. Water, black and muscular, heaved against the pilings six feet below, slapping the barnacled posts like it wanted to shake them loose and eat them. I was about to write the light off as imagination when I heard it—a sound no storm makes. A cough. Small, human, and utterly spent.

Every instinct I owned, every rule I'd lived by for nine years—*be unremarkable, see nothing, touch nothing, survive*—told me to back away up onto the planks and let the sea finish whatever it had started. Getting involved meant questions. Questions meant names given to company men. Names meant trouble, and trouble, for a person bound in indenture until their twenty-first year, meant losing the only thing worse than freedom: the roof over the small remainder of a small life.

I backed away up onto the planks.

Then I thought about the cough again. About how it hadn't sounded like a stranger's cough at all, really. It had sounded like my mother's, the last winter she worked the oyster beds, when the cold got into her chest and never fully left. And I thought about how, if some clerk had heard *her* coughing under a dock and chosen his own safety, I would not exist to be standing here choosing anything.

Which is a stupid reason to do a dangerous thing. But stupid reasons are the only ones that ever move me, apparently.

I found the coil of salvage line the dock crew kept knotted to a piling, checked it the way you check a rope when your life will depend on it twice—once going down, once coming back—and tied it around my waist under my coat. Then I went over the edge into the dark.

* * *

Under the dock is its own country. The pilings rise out of black water like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, furred with weed, streaming in the surge. Between swells you can hear the whole structure groan above you, the weight of the storm leaning on beams that were old before my grandmother was born. I wedged my boots into the cross-braces and climbed down hand over hand, the line paying out behind me, until the spray soaked me to the skin and my eyes burned with salt and I understood, belatedly, what a fool I was.

"There," I said, to nobody. "Happy? I'm a fool."

The answer came from below and to the left: that cough again, weaker now.

Getting to her was the worst work I've done in my life, and I've done pearl diving. The gap she'd wedged into was barely a body wide, between two pilings where a crossbeam had rotted through, and the surge filled it twice a minute with freezing black water. She'd jammed herself in with her back to the post and her arms around something—a satchel, oilskin, strapped across her chest like a child she refused to put down. Her head lolled. When the water hit her she didn't flinch anymore, and that frightened me more than anything else in that dripping dark. People who've stopped flinching are people who've started leaving.

"Hey." I got a hand on her shoulder and shook her. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

Her eyes opened. Even in that darkness I saw them find me and focus with an effort that must have cost her everything she had left. She looked at my face, then at the company livery collar I still wore—the grey wool with the Halvard stitch—and something went through her expression that I can only call despair. She tried to pull away from me. Toward the water. Toward drowning, apparently preferred to rescue.

"Oh, no you don't." I got both hands into her collar. "That's the wrong direction. That's the *worst* direction."

"M'not—" Her voice was a ruin. "Not going... with you."

"That's fortunate, because I'm not taking you anywhere with sense. I'm taking you somewhere warm and stupid and dangerous, and you're going to hate it. Hold on to me."

She was heavier than she looked, or the water was stronger than it looked, or I was weaker than I'd believed right up until the moment strength mattered. Getting her out of that gap was a nightmare in installments. Her fingers stayed hooked in the oilskin satchel's strap the entire time; when I tried once to take it from her to lighten the load, her grip closed so hard I felt the tendons in her wrist crackle, and she said, with sudden terrible clarity: "Mine. *Mine.*"

"All yours," I promised. "Nobody's taking your bag. Now climb, curse you."

We climbed. I won't pretend it was graceful. Twice the surge nearly peeled us off the braces, and the second time I lost my footing entirely and we swung out over the black water together on the salvage line, her dead weight dragging my shoulder half out of its socket, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that has stayed with me since: *this is the moment. This is where the invisible life ends—one way or the other.*

Then my boots found the brace again, and we went up.

Hauling her over the dock edge onto the planking was less climbing than collapse in reverse. We lay there side by side in the hammering rain, gasping like landed fish, and I laughed—actually laughed—because the alternative was weeping and I've always considered that a private vice.

"Don't," she rasped, when she could.

"Don't what?"

"Don't laugh. You'll cry after. I know the pattern."

"You're a fine one to lecture on patterns, half-dead under a dock in a storm." I got my shoulder under her arm. "Up. There's a place. It's not far, but you're going to hate every step, because you look like someone who hates needing help, and this is going to be help."

She didn't argue. That scared me too. People who are too tired to argue are people running on whatever burns last.

* * *

The Pearlhouse sat—or sits, I should say, though that night I only knew it as refuge—two streets back from the waterline, in the crook where Harbor Lane bends around the old net-yard. It had been a pearl merchant's house once, back when Vessing traded in pearls openly instead of laundering them through company ledgers. Now it belonged, in the way that nothing in Vessing truly belongs to anyone, to Mama Osk and everyone she'd gathered under her roof: net-menders, orphaned divers, a one-eyed cook whose name changed depending on who owed him money. The company called it a boarding house for unlicensed labor. Everyone else called it home.

I'd eaten there maybe twice a month for nine years. Never slept there. Never let myself need it too visibly. But the door had never once been locked against me, and tonight I used that fact like the lifeline it was.

Dagny opened it—Dagny the net-mender, forearms like anchor chain, a woman who mended other people's livelihoods by day and, rumor insisted, moved other people's cargo by night, though never to her face unless you wanted your face rearranged. She took one look at the two of us collapsed against the doorframe and hauled us inside without a word.

"Mama Osk!" she bellowed over the storm rattling the shutters. "Fetch the big kettle! And someone fetch me a blanket before this one drowns on dry land, which would be embarrassing for everyone!"

What followed was a confusion of hands and heat and voices. They stripped Sefa's sodden coat off her—that oilskin satchel staying welded to her body even then; I noticed, filed it away, said nothing—and rubbed her down with rough wool while Mama Osk, a broad soft-spoken mountain of a woman who had run the Pearlhouse longer than I'd been alive, moved through the room dispensing hot broth and orders with equal gravity.

"Fever in her," Osk said, pressing the back of her hand to Sefa's brow. "Lungs full of sea. Lior Nystrom, what in the deep did you haul home?"

"A problem," I said honestly.

"Good." She ladled more broth. "We were short on those."

They put Sefa by the great hearth, wrapped in enough blankets to bury her, and the Pearlhouse's orphans gathered around in the way children do, sitting in a solemn half-circle as though fever were a play. Sefa surfaced from her half-consciousness just enough to register them, and her eyes—dark, sharp, currently swimming—moved from face to face with an attention that struck me as odd. Not the vague drifting of the sick. Something more like a soldier counting exits.

When the children had been shooed to bed and the fire banked, she caught my sleeve with fingers that still shook.

"You wore the collar," she said. Low. Urgent. "Company grey. Under the dock. I saw it."

"I wear it nine years running. It comes off at night, mostly."

"Listen." Her grip tightened. "If they come asking—if the Company comes—you haven't seen me. Swear it. Whatever I say, whoever asks, you found nothing under that dock. Nothing at all. Because if they think you touched me, they won't question you, they'll just—" She stopped. Coughed. Went on thinner. "They'll open you like a ledger and read what they want and burn the rest."

It occurred to me, dimly, that this was a strange speech for a drowned woman. Most people rescued half-dead worry about broth and blankets. This one worried about operational security.

"Who's coming?" I asked. "Who's after you?"

She looked at me for a long time. The firelight moved in her eyes, and I watched her weigh me—watched her decide how much of her secret this grey-collared clerk could carry without breaking. Whatever she saw, it was either enough or she simply had no strength left to hold it alone.

She pulled the oilskin satchel around in front of her, and her hands on it were almost tender.

"Letters," she said. "In a dead man's hand."

* * *

I should have walked out then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. Every survival instinct honed across nine years of careful invisibility stood up in the Pearlhouse firelight and told me, clearly and kindly, to thank Mama Osk for the broth, retrieve my coat, and go back to my cot above the salt stores where I was merely miserable instead of doomed.

Because I knew handwriting. It's a clerk's whole trade. And when Sefa drew the papers halfway from the oilskin—just halfway, guarding them like a wound—I saw the script on the topmost letter, that strong forward-slanting hand with the peculiar crossed double-vee, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

I had copied that signature myself. Apprentices learn the founding documents first; it's tradition, and also the company's little act of worship. Aldric Vane, founder of the Halvard Trading Company, architect of the Vessing port charter, author of the indenture terms under which I had been bound since I was a child pulled half-frozen from a diver's hut.

Aldric Vane, who had drowned seven years ago. Officially, formally, with a sealed record and a memorial plaque in the counting-house that apprentices were required to dust.

"You know it," Sefa said. She was watching my face the way you watch a tide gauge. "You recognize the hand."

"The man's dead," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Seven years drowned. There's a plaque."

"There's a lie," she said. "The plaque is part of it."

And there it was. The crack in the ice under my carefully ordered, carefully invisible life. I stood in Mama Osk's warm kitchen smelling of salt and fish stew, soaked to the bone, shoulder aching, and understood that whatever happened next, I had already crossed the water. There was no climbing back up the pilings to the person I'd been before I heard that cough.

Mama Osk appeared at my elbow with a cup of something hot and alcoholic and said, quietly, so only I could hear: "Whatever she's carrying, child, you brought it through my door. So now it's partly mine. That's how doors work."

Behind her, Dagny leaned in the doorway whittling at a marlinspike she had absolutely no practical need for at midnight, watching Sefa with the flat assessing stare of a woman calculating cargo weights.

Sefa pulled the blanket higher, met my eyes, and held the oilskin packet a little tighter against her chest.

"They're hunting me," she said. "By morning, they'll know I made it to shore."
