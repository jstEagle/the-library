## 4. Handwriting of a Dead Man

I have been chased before. I want to say that up front, because it matters: being hunted is a trade like any other, and I am, if nothing else, a professional. You learn the rhythms of it the way a sailor learns weather. The first day is always the worst—dogs, fresh tracks, men with something to prove. By the third day they grow careless. By the fifth, you grow careless, and that's when people die.

This time it had been eleven days, and I had grown very careless indeed, which is how I came to be drowning under a stranger's wharf in Vessing harbor, tangled in someone's abandoned net, holding an oilskin packet the way a drowning woman holds anything—which is to say badly, out of pure spite, because letting go was not among my options and never had been.

The clerk who pulled me out was the least likely rescuer I could have imagined. Small, gray as gull-feather, moving through the storm like someone who had practiced being unnoticed until the practice became flesh. I remember thinking, as they hauled me up the pilings with hands much stronger than they looked: *this one has survived something. Good. Survivors don't ask questions.*

I was wrong about that, as it happens. But then, I've been wrong about most things that mattered, so at least I'm consistent.

* * *

The Pearlhouse smelled of tar, fish, woodsmoke, and about nine kinds of soup, all cooking at once, none of them agreeing with each other. I lay on a pallet by the stove doing what professionals do best—nothing—and watched the clerk pretend not to watch me.

They were bad at pretending. That was my first clue there was something wrong with Lior Nystrom. A person truly skilled at invisibility doesn't keep glancing at the interesting thing in the room. They look at the wall. They look at their own hands. They make themselves furniture. This one kept looking at me like a cat at a mousehole, all that careful gray stillness shot through with a hunger they clearly hadn't authorized.

"You're going to cough blood," I told them, "if you don't ask whatever it is you're chewing on."

"I wasn't—" A pause. Honest recalibration. "Where did you come from? Before the harbor."

"South." True. "Down the coast." Also true. Vagueness is only lying when you're bad at it.

"And the packet?"

Ah, thought I. There it is.

"Personal correspondence," I said.

"It's oilskin."

"Correspondence that expects rain."

The clerk's mouth did something complicated—not quite a smile, more the memory of one, filed away and rarely accessed. Then, damn them, they sat down on the floor beside my pallet and folded their hands like a schoolmaster waiting out a liar.

"The way you held it," they said. "In the water. When I pulled you up, you'd wrapped your arm through the strap. Your fingers were frozen past feeling. You could have let go and swum easier." Those eyes, pale as weak tea, didn't blink. "Nobody does that for personal correspondence."

I have lied to magistrates. I have lied to men holding knives. I once lied, successfully, to Aldric Vane himself, though he let me believe I had, which is its own kind of lesson. But there is something about a person who has clearly spent their whole life being quiet that makes noise feel almost obligatory. Or maybe I was just tired. Eleven days, cold water, a dislocated shoulder I'd relocated myself, badly.

"It's letters," I said.

"I guessed that."

"From a dead man."

Something moved behind the clerk's face—quick, involuntary, like a fish under ice. "Whose?"

And here is where I should have said *no one you know*, should have rolled over and slept for two days and slipped out with the tide the way I'd slipped out of six ports before this one. Instead I heard myself say his name, out loud, for the first time since the night the hunters picked up my trail at Marrow Point.

"Aldric Vane."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere in the house a pot lid rattled; children's feet thundered overhead; the storm leaned its shoulder against the walls. And Lior Nystrom went very, very still—the stillness of prey, I thought at the time, but I've revised that. It was the stillness of a ledger balancing.

"That's impossible," they said finally, and their voice had gone flat and careful, company-trained. "Vane drowned seven years ago. His body was recovered off the Serpent Rocks. There was a memorial service. The company flies black pennants every year on the anniversary."

"Yes," I said. "The pennants are a nice touch. He watches them from a window in Corvane, forty miles south, and complains that they use cheap dye."

* * *

You cannot understand what those letters are without understanding him, so forgive me while I explain a dead man.

Aldric Vane built the Halvard Trading Company out of three leaky hulls and a genius for arithmetic. That's the version on the plaques. The version I know is older and wetter: a diver's son from exactly this stretch of coast, a boy who counted pearls for other men until he decided counting would be done *his* way or not at all. He was not a good man, whatever the plaques say. He was a precise one, and for a long time precision looked like justice. He paid divers fairly when nobody did. He wrote contracts in plain language when everyone else used fog. For twenty years, the company's ledgers were honest, and honest ledgers in a place like Vessing are worth more than cannons.

Then he got old. And old men build things, and other men inherit the building without inheriting the conscience, and somewhere in the counting-house the numbers began to lie.

He found it the way he found everything—by reading. He read the ledgers the way other men read scripture, looking for sin, and he found it in abundance. Shipments recorded twice. Pearls weighed light and sold heavy. And then, buried deepest, the entry class that made him stop sleeping: contracts. Indentured children, listed line by line like cargo, their debts inflated on paper until no child could ever work free, and then—the elegant part, the part I still can't say without clenching my jaw—the contracts themselves *sold*. Overseas. To plantations, to mines, to buyers who wanted labor that would never walk away, and were willing to pay the Halvard Company for the paperwork of permanent childhood.

He objected. Formally, at first—he was a founder, after all; he expected institutions to have memories of their own rules. What he learned was that institutions have memories only of profit. When his objections escalated, so did the quiet arrangements around him, until one night in autumn seven years ago, a boat went out from Vessing and came back empty, and the Serpent Rocks collected a story about a drowning.

He hadn't drowned. He'd been given a choice, and he took the one that kept him breathing, and the price was his name, his company, and his silence. He kept the silence for five years. What broke it—I'll get to that. What matters is that when he broke it, he needed hands. Couriers. People who could move paper through a coastline crawling with company men, because the letters alone weren't enough. Letters can be dismissed as forgery, madness, spite. Letters are a key. He needed doors.

That was me. Not the only one—there were others, most of them gone quiet now, one way or another—but the last one still walking, which made me either the most important or the most foolish, depending on the day.

* * *

I unwrapped the oilskin that evening, because the clerk had earned that much and because, honestly, I was tired of carrying it alone.

There are twelve letters. He wrote them over two years, in a small fierce hand that slants left, because he broke that wrist diving as a boy and it never healed straight. Each one is signed not with his name but with a string of figures—an old ledger code, a checksum, the kind of thing only a man who dreamed in columns would devise, and only another man who knew his ledgers would recognize.

"These," I said, laying them out on Mama Osk's table in the lamplight, "are addressed to a magistrate. To the circuit court. They are testimony, sworn in everything but ink, naming names, dates, ships, sums. They describe the second ledger—the true one—kept in the Company House vault, against which every falsified entry can be checked. And they describe where the bodies of the accounting are buried. Some of those are children."

Lior had gone white around the mouth. "The vault," they repeated. "The Company House vault."

"You know it."

"I copy manifests into it twice a week." Said slowly, the way a person repeats something they haven't finished understanding. Their fingers were doing something on the tabletop—a tapping, rhythmic, like someone counting without knowing it. Clerks. Even their fear keeps books.

"There's more," I said, and hesitated, and then gave it to them because they'd earned it and because I wanted to see what happened. "The letters name specific contracts. Sold ones. With the children's original names, and the ports they were taken from, and the numbers the company assigned them afterward."

The tapping stopped.

For a long moment the clerk looked at those letters the way a starving person looks through a window at bread. Then they reached out—one finger, careful as touching a hot iron—and traced the signature code.

"This," Lior said, and their voice had gone strange, "is his hand."

"You knew him?" Now it was my turn to be surprised, and I don't love being surprised.

"No." A breath. "But I've seen it. Every indenture in this port gets countersigned by the founding authority—it's archaic, some clause in the original charter nobody ever repealed. Old contracts carry his signature. I've copied it a hundred times." The finger stayed on the page. "It slants left."

"Because he broke his wrist diving."

"Because—" The clerk stopped. Swallowed something. When they spoke again the company-flatness was gone entirely, and underneath it was something young and furious and awake, a door opening in a house I'd assumed was empty. "Seven years ago I was twelve years old. My contract was renewed that year. Renewed early, out of cycle, with new terms I couldn't read and wasn't shown. I remember the clerk who did it. He was in a hurry. He kept checking the door."

The storm chose that moment to throw itself full-weight against the Pearlhouse, and the lamp guttered, and in the dark between flame-steadyings I watched Lior Nystrom arrive at the edge of something enormous and stand there, teetering, refusing yet to fall in.

"Your letters," they said at last, carefully. "They list the sold contracts."

"Some."

"All of them?"

"Every one he could find copies of."

Another silence, but this one belonged to both of us. Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it change under the pilings, the long exhale of the harbor. I thought about sleep. I thought about the road south, and the men on it, and the fact that I had maybe two days before they worked out I'd come north instead, and perhaps four before someone in this town sold my description for the price of a drink.

What I did not yet understand—and would not, until the supper that settled my fate under this roof—was that the packet had already stopped being mine. Somewhere between the wharf and this table, between the oilskin and that left-slanting signature, the thing I carried had changed cargo. It was no longer a dead man's testimony traveling toward a living court.

It was a key. And Lior Nystrom, gray little invisible survivor of the Halvard Trading Company, had just heard a lock turn somewhere inside themselves and was standing very still, listening to it echo.

"Get some rest," the clerk said finally, rising, gathering the letters with a gentleness I hadn't seen them use on anything, including themselves. They squared the edges. Wrapped the oilskin with corners sharp enough to cut. "Tomorrow you should eat. And then you should tell me," and here their voice did the flattening again, but I caught the ember under it, glowing through the ash, "exactly which checkpoints stand between here and the magistrate's circuit."

"Why?" I asked. "Why does a clerk who survives by being unremarkable want to know that?"

Lior paused in the doorway, half in lamplight, half in shadow, and for just a moment they looked neither invisible nor unremarkable at all.

"Because I copy the manifests," they said. "Twice a week. Into that vault. And I think I've been carrying evidence in my own handwriting for nine years without knowing it was evidence at all."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Professionals often don't, before the hard part. I lay listening to the house breathe—Osk's slow footsteps below, the children turning over above, the sea working at the foundations like a patient creditor—and I thought about Aldric Vane in his window in Corvane, watching cheap black dye flap against a sky that had swallowed his name.

Hold on, old man, I thought. Your hand slants left, and it turns out that's not the only crooked thing about you. Hold on. The letters are moving again.

And downstairs, faintly, I heard the scratch of a pen.
