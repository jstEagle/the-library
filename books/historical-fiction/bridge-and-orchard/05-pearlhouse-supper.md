## 5. Pearlhouse Supper

The Pearlhouse smelled, as it always did on a Thursday, of barley soup and tar and the particular sourness of wet wool that had been dried too near a stove. It was a smell I had known since I was eleven years old and carried into it, half-frozen, with nothing to my name but an indenture number and a scar across my palms. You would think a smell could not hold a person. This one had held me for nine years.

I had brought Sefa in through the back, past the rack of drying eels, which she eyed with what I judged to be either hunger or horror. She was still dripping. The oilskin packet was strapped against her ribs beneath her shirt, and she kept one hand pressed over it the way other people press a hand over a wound — automatic, protective, as if the thing inside might bleed out.

"Mama Osk," I said, before I had fully decided to say anything at all. "This is Sefa. She was drowning."

"Was she," Mama Osk said. It was not a question. Mama Osk did not ask questions when she already knew the shape of the answer; she simply stated her knowledge and let you decide whether to lie on top of it, like laying a bad plank over a rotten joist. She was stirring the pot with a wooden spoon older than the harbor wall, and she looked Sefa up and down with the unhurried appraisal of a woman who had fished more than one half-dead stranger out of the tide in her day. "Well. She's drowned now, mostly. Sit her down. Dagny! Bring towels, the ones without holes. Or bring the ones with holes, we're none of us proud."

Dagny emerged from the mending room trailing net twine like a woman trailed by her own opinions. She was sixty if she was forty, hands like knotted rope, and she took one look at Sefa and said, "That's not a drowning. That's a running."

"I know the difference," Sefa said. Her voice was rough, sea-flattened, but steady. "I've done both."

* * *

Supper at the Pearlhouse was served family-style, which is to say it was served fast, in unequal portions, to whoever arrived first with the least shame. There were eleven of us at the long table that night: Mama Osk at the head where the head belonged; Dagny; me; and seven children ranging from Perrin, who was fourteen and thought himself grown, down to little Maren and Isolde, twins aged six who had been left on Osk's doorstep one spring tide by a mother who went diving and did not come back up. The twins ate with their spoons held in their fists, like knives, because nobody had ever corrected them and nobody ever would.

Sefa sat at the far end, between Dagny and the wall, which was not an accident. I had placed her there myself, and I noted with some professional satisfaction that from that chair you could see both the front door and the window, and reach neither quickly. If she ran, we would see her go. If someone came for her, we would see them first. A clerk learns to arrange rooms the way he arranges columns: so that everything is visible and nothing can escape.

The children watched her the way children watch anything new — openly, rudely, with their mouths full. Perrin, who fancied himself a detective because he had once found Dagny's hidden tobacco, leaned across the table and asked, "Are you a smuggler?"

"Perrin," Mama Osk said.

"What? We were all thinking it."

"We were all thinking it politely," Dagny said, "which is a skill you'll want someday, when you have something worth losing." But she said it while looking at Sefa, and there was nothing polite in the look at all. Dagny's eyes went to the hand pressed over Sefa's ribs, and stayed there, and narrowed, and I understood that Dagny had already made her own private inventory and reached conclusions she was keeping in a locked drawer.

"She was under the pilings during the storm," I said. Everyone turned to look at me, which was deeply unpleasant. I am not accustomed to being looked at; I had spent nine years perfecting the art of being furniture. "I pulled her out."

"You pulled her out," Perrin repeated, with the frank astonishment children reserve for watching a fish do arithmetic.

"People pull things out of water," I said. "It's practically our civic industry."

Mama Osk ladled soup. She ladled soup at Sefa last, and fullest, and set the bowl down in front of her with a small decisive click, and then she stood at the head of the table with her own bowl untouched and said, "Right. Before anyone eats another mouthful — Lior, tell them what you told me, or keep your mouth shut forever, your choice."

Which was, I reflected bitterly, exactly how Mama Osk ran everything: offering freedom of choice in the precise shape of having none.

* * *

So I told them. Not everything. I told them the true skeleton with the dangerous meat stripped off: a stranger under the docks, storm-tossed, no papers, hunters asking after her at the Tide Gate within the hour. I did not mention the packet. I did not mention the handwriting. Those things lived behind my teeth, where I had been keeping secrets since I was old enough to understand that a diver's child survives by being unremarkable, and that unremarkable is a discipline, like penmanship.

When I finished, nobody spoke for a moment except little Isolde, who said, "Her hair looks like seaweed," and was shushed by her sister, who agreed with her privately.

Then Dagny put down her spoon. Dagny putting down her spoon mid-meal was roughly equivalent to a ship firing a warning shot.

"I'll say it, since nobody else will," she said. "Company hunters came to the gate asking after her. Company hunters. Which means whatever she's carrying or whoever she's running from, it has the Halvard Trading Company's fingerprints on it, and every soul at this table knows what those fingerprints look like pressed onto their lives." She jerked her chin at the twins. "Their mother dove company beds. Your mother—" this to Perrin "—lost her license to company courts. And him—" the chin swung to me, and I felt it land like a gull hitting a window "—is owned by them until his twenty-first year, inked and sealed and filed. So the question isn't whether we owe her. The question is whether we can afford her. And we can't."

"Dagny," Mama Osk said mildly.

"No, you asked for it said. I'm saying it. I've buried people who couldn't afford things, Osk. I know the price list better than most."

"And yet," Mama Osk said, still mild, and picked up her spoon, and began to eat, which was her way of saying the debate was open and the floor was everyone's and dinner would continue regardless, because the dead do not eat and the living must.

Perrin, drunk on the novelty of being allowed an opinion, said, "We should hide her. In the loft. Nobody checks the loft, it smells like fish guts."

"It smells like fish guts *because you hid a stolen crab pot up there for three weeks,*" Dagny said.

"That's different—"

"The vote," Mama Osk said, "will not be conducted by children who smell of contraband."

And here I should explain something about the Pearlhouse that I did not understand until much later, though it was happening in front of me every week of my life. Mama Osk voted on things. Formally, by raised hands, after argument, whenever a matter touched the whole house — whether to take in a child, whether to spend the winter coal money replacing the roof instead of the stove, whether Dagny's brother Torvald could store "rope" in the cellar that was very clearly not rope. I used to think it was sentimentality, a soft-hearted woman playing parliament among the fishheads. It took me nine years and one storm-tossed night to see what it actually was: armor. Because a thing decided alone can be taken from you alone. But a thing the whole house has voted on — that belongs to the house, and to take it, they must come against everyone, together, at once. Mama Osk built walls out of consensus. The company never figured out how to breach them until, much later, they stopped trying to breach and simply burned.

But that night, I only knew that my stomach hurt, and that Sefa was watching the table with dark, careful eyes, taking our measure the way I took the measure of ledgers: looking for the column that didn't add up.

* * *

The argument lasted through the soup and into the bread.

Dagny laid out the case for turning Sefa out, and she laid it out well, because grief makes excellent rhetoric. She named names. She named the boy, Joren, who had run messages for a smuggler one winter and been taken by company men and returned to the Pearlhouse in a box with his fingers arranged neatly, a message about messages. She named the winter the company had cut the Pearlhouse's wharf-rights in half over an "irregularity" that was punishment for nothing anyone ever identified. She named me — "the boy there has the most to lose, and he's the one dragged her in, which tells you his head needs looking at" — and I sat very still and studied my soup and did not disagree, because disagreement would have required being looked at again.

Against this, Mama Osk said very little. That was her way. She let Dagny empty herself like a net, and when Dagny finally sat back, wrung out, Osk said only:

"How many?"

Dagny blinked. "How many what?"

"How many have we taken in? Count them."

Dagny's jaw worked. She counted, silently, her lips moving faintly, and I counted with her because I couldn't help it — eleven children in nineteen years, plus four adults, plus one mad lighthouse keeper's widow, plus me.

"Sixteen," Dagny said at last, grudgingly. "Seventeen with her."

"And how many came to harm because we took them in?"

"One," Dagny whispered. "Joren."

"One," Mama Osk agreed, gently now, terribly gently, "and Joren came to harm because the company is cruel, not because we were kind. Kindness wasn't the weapon, Dagny. They just want us to believe it was. They want us so frightened of our own mercy that we do their cruelty for them, free of charge, at suppertime." She folded her hands. "I won't give them that discount."

Sefa had not spoken through any of this. She sat with her hands flat on the table — she had moved them off the packet, I noticed, deliberately, as if to say *I am not hiding anything from this room* even though she obviously was — and when the silence stretched, she stood.

"I'll go tonight," she said. "You've fed me. That's more than I had at noon. I won't bring the box down on your heads."

"Sit down," said Maren and Isolde, in perfect unison, with the terrible authority of six-year-olds, and Sefa, to her credit, sat.

"There'll be a vote," Mama Osk said. "Everyone in this house who eats at this table votes. That's the rule and it has always been the rule, and yes, that includes the children, Dagny, don't start. All in favor of sheltering our guest until the roads are safe — hands."

Hands went up. Mama Osk. Perrin, instantly, arm straight as a mast. The twins, both hands each, which Mama Osk ruled counted as one vote each after brief constitutional crisis. The two oldest girls, Britta and Solveig, divers both, thirteen and fifteen, who exchanged one glance and raised their hands together the way they did everything together. Old Ketil from the mending room, who barely surfaced from his deafness to vote and then sank back down.

Dagny did not raise her hand. She looked at the table. Then she looked at Sefa — really looked, the way you look at weather coming in — and said, "Against. For the record. Someone at this table has to remember what we're risking, or it stops being a risk and starts being a habit."

"Noted and respected," Mama Osk said. "Motion carries."

And then Dagny pushed her bowl back, stood, fetched the good towels — the ones without holes — and dropped them in Sefa's lap with the expression of a woman performing an act of war against her own better judgment.

"Loft," she said. "Not because of the smell. Because from up there you can see the lane in both directions and drop out the back window onto the eel racks. If anyone comes, you go over the roof to Torvald's shed and you don't come back here, whatever happens to the rest of us. Understood?"

"Understood," Sefa said quietly.

"Don't thank me. I'll resent it."

* * *

Later — much later, after the children were stacked in their bunks like cordwood and the fire had been banked to red memory — Mama Osk found me in the scullery, where I was washing bowls with more concentration than bowls require.

"You knew," she said. "Before you walked her through my door. You knew she was carrying something worse than a cold."

I scrubbed. "I knew she was afraid in a specific way. Fear with paperwork behind it. I recognize the genre."

"Lior." Mama Osk rarely used my name. To everyone else she was *child*, *girl*, *boy*, *trouble*; to me she was usually nothing at all, just a tilt of the head meaning *come here*. Hearing my name in her mouth was like hearing a bell underwater. "Whatever it is you're not telling me — and you're not telling me quite a lot, I've watched you not tell me for nine years — when you're ready to tell it, this house will still be standing. That's what the vote was for. Not for her. For you. So that when it comes down, you'll know you weren't carrying it alone."

I wanted to tell her then. My hands actually stopped moving in the water. The words were right there — *a dead man's letters, Osk, Aldric Vane writes a fair round hand, I copied his early manifests for three years, I'd know it upside down* —

But nine years of discipline is not undone by one warm kitchen. I said, "There's nothing to tell," and handed her the last bowl, and she took it, and looked at me with those eyes that had seen seventeen strangers arrive at her table, and she said:

"All right. But when there is — and there will be, child, I've never met a secret that didn't eventually demand supper — you'll find the vote's already been cast."

She went to bed. I stood in the scullery with my hands in the cooling dishwater, and through the floorboards above I heard the soft sounds of Sefa settling into the loft, the creak of Dagny's chair in the mending room where she had apparently decided to sleep armed with knitting needles across the threshold.

And that was when the knock came at the front door — three measured raps, unhurried, official, the knock of a man who knows the door will be opened — and through the wall I heard Mama Osk's candle flare, and Dagny rise, and somewhere in my chest a column of numbers that had almost reconciled itself went suddenly, catastrophically wrong.
