## 7. Three Checkpoints

I learned maps before I learned letters. My father drew them on the backs of shipping manifests when I was small—coastlines with too many islands, harbors that didn't exist, a route home marked in ink that was really just an excuse to keep me sitting beside him while he worked. *This is the trick of any journey*, he used to say, tapping the paper. *Anyone can walk. The map is knowing where they'll be watching when you do.*

He'd been drawing maps for seven years from a place he wouldn't name, and I had been carrying them for three, folded against my ribs until the creases wore through the paper and I could have found his route blindfolded, in the dark, bleeding.

Which was fortunate, because on my fourth morning at the Pearlhouse, that was more or less the condition I was in.

* * *

Mama Osk's kitchen smelled of barley and tar, and it had a table scarred by forty years of knives that were never once used for anything you'd serve a magistrate. That morning it held: a loaf, a crock of butter going grey at the edges, Dagny with her mending piled in her lap like a sleeping cat, two diver children who were supposed to be at their lessons and were instead pretending not to listen, Lior Nystrom with a face like a locked drawer, and me.

"Show them," I said.

Lior looked at me the way they always did—like I was a sum that wouldn't reconcile. "Show them what, exactly?"

"The route." I pulled the charcoal stub from my sleeve—I'd taken to carrying it the way other people carry knives—and started drawing on the tabletop, because Mama Osk's table had already survived worse than me. "The magistrate's spring circuit comes up from Corvane on the coast road, sits in Vessing for however long the assizes take, and leaves again before the midsummer tides. If we want those letters heard, they go to the circuit clerk before the court sits. Not after. After, we're just rumors with paperwork."

"And the route?" Dagny didn't look up from her netting, but her fingers had stopped moving, which for Dagny was the same as leaning forward.

"Three doors between this kitchen and that clerk's desk." I tapped the first mark. "The Tide Gate."

Every head at the table knew the Tide Gate; that was the problem with Vessing, everyone knew everything within earshot of the water. The harbor's only deep channel ran beneath it—a stone arch straddling the mouth of the river where it met the sea, with the company customs house squatting on top of it like a gull on a fish head. Anything coming in or out by water passed under the Gate, and anything with value got boarded, measured, taxed, and recorded twice, though only one of those recordings ever made it into the ledgers honestly.

"Second," I said, drawing inland, "the Salt Road tollhouse. Halfway between here and Corvane, the road pinches down through the hills to a single lane, and the company keeps a keeper there who inspects every pack, every cart, every traveler with a face he doesn't know."

"That'll be Brannoch," Dagny said. "Big man. Honest as tide-tables, which is to say honest exactly as far as someone's paid him honest."

"He's been paid dishonest this season. There's a description of me circulating—the hair, the scar, the accent. He's being paid to find a woman traveling alone."

"You are a woman traveling alone," said one of the diver children helpfully.

"I'm considering a hat," I said. The child nodded gravely, as though this solved most things, and perhaps it did.

"Third door." And here my charcoal stopped, because it always stopped there, hovering over the table like it didn't want to commit. "The Company House itself."

Silence of the particular Vessing kind—not empty, but full of everyone deciding how much they already knew. The Company House sat on the market square like a fat man at a funeral, granite-faced, its vault below the waterline. The magistrate's circuit would sit in the square within sight of its windows, and every clerk, guard, and factor inside those windows had one job between now and spring: make sure nothing true reached the court.

"Why would the papers need to go *through* the Company House?" Lior asked. Quietly. They always asked quietly, as if loud questions might get them charged rent.

"Because the circuit clerk keeps his strongroom there. Company hospitality—the magistrate travels cheap, and the company lends him a vault and a guard and, if the factor has his way, a memory. Any evidence deposited with the circuit goes into that vault first. Which means—" I drew a circle around the Company House and then, after a moment, a second circle around myself, "—anything we hand over passes through Grell's hands before it passes into the record."

"So don't hand it over there," Dagny said.

"So we can't *not*. The circuit clerk won't accept testimony without documentary support, and the letters alone prove intent but not theft. We need the ledger pages, or copies, or—something. And whatever we carry has to enter through the front door of the one building in Vessing built specifically to keep people like me out."

I put the charcoal down. Three marks on a scarred table: a gate, a tollhouse, a fortress. Between them, thirty miles of road and one woman who was worth more dead and delivered than alive and asking questions.

"Well," said Mama Osk from the doorway, flour on her hands and no expression at all, "it's a good thing none of you are planning anything. Pass the butter."

* * *

That afternoon Lior found me on the pier behind the Pearlhouse, where I was doing my best impression of a woman who belonged to a fishing village—mending a net badly enough that anyone watching would conclude I was harmless and also terrible at nets.

"You've done this before," Lior said. Not a question. They did that—stated things and let you decide whether to argue.

"Couriers run routes. It's the whole profession."

"How many times?"

"Enough times to know that the route isn't the hard part." I gave the net a yank that made it worse and set it down. "The hard part is papers. Every door wants papers. Tide Gate wants a cargo manifest stamped by customs—which is to say, stamped by the company. Tollhouse wants a waybill or a travel pass sealed by the company or the magistrate's office. Company House wants—" I laughed, and it came out uglier than I intended. "The Company House wants you to *be* the company. Clerks go in and out all day. Nobody else gets past the factor's lobby without credentials, and credentials come from exactly one inkwell."

Lior sat down on the pier edge, knees drawn up, looking out at the gray water like it owed them money. Nine years I'd later learn—that's how long they'd been bound to Halvard. Nine years of learning exactly how the company wrote its own permissions.

"They check seals," Lior said finally. "At all three points?"

"Different men, different checks. The Tide Gate guards mostly read the manifest numbers and wave anything that floats. Brannoch at the tollhouse actually looks—he holds wax to the light, checks the wire marks in the paper. The Company House has a registrar who compares seals against a book of specimens."

"A book of specimens," Lior repeated. Something moved behind their eyes, some gear catching. "So a forged seal has to match not just the die but the pressure, the wax color, the placement. Placement varies by document type."

"Yes. How would you—"

"I copy manifests," Lior said, as if this explained everything, which after a week in their company I was beginning to understand it did. "Six hundred documents a month, four years running. I know what a Halvard cargo seal looks like better than the man who stamps them. I know which clerks press hard and which ones are lazy, which years they changed the wax blend, where the registrar's specimen book is *kept*—third shelf of the strongroom cabinet, because I dusted that cabinet every Tuesday for two years."

I turned to look at them properly. Lior Nystrom, all elbows and careful hands and a voice pitched to never carry across a room. The kind of person the world taught to be furniture. And underneath the furniture: a locksmith.

"You're talking about forgery," I said.

"I'm talking about *accuracy*," they said, with the faintest primness, and then, quieter, the truth leaking out sideways the way truths did with them: "I'm saying none of us can forge alone. Your smugglers can move us and hide us, but they can't write. Dagny can't read half the manifests. And you—" they glanced at me, apologetic and unapologetic at once, "—you're a courier. You carry. You don't make."

"No," I agreed. "I don't make."

"But a clerk makes. A clerk makes papers all day, every day, and nobody watches a clerk's hands." Lior picked a splinter out of the pier boards, examined it, set it down with strange ceremony. "The Tide Gate needs a manifest that reads clean. I can write one. The tollhouse needs a seal that survives lamplight. That's harder. The Company House needs credentials that pass a registrar with a specimen book." A pause. "That's hardest of all, and it's the door we can't avoid."

"Then we find another way into the vault."

"There is no other way into that vault. I've inventoried it." They said it flatly, and I watched them hear their own words—watched the realization arrive in real time that nine years of invisibility had made them the single most dangerous person in Vessing to the company's secrets, precisely because the company never thought furniture was listening.

"Lior," I said.

"Don't." They stood, brushing off their trousers, retreating back into clerk posture, spine curving toward apology. "I haven't agreed to anything. I'm describing obstacles. Describing obstacles is clerical work."

"It's a start."

"It's *inventory*." But they didn't leave. They stood at the pier's end with the wind pulling at their sleeves, and after a while they said, so quietly I almost lost it to the water: "The tollhouse keeper checks faces against descriptions. Yours will be three days old by the time we move. Descriptions change slowly. Papers change fast. If we're going to do this—*if*—we do it soon, and we do it in order, and nobody carries everything at once. You split the packet. Letters separate from anything naming you. If one of us is taken—"

"—the other still walks," I finished. My father's rule. Hearing it in this stranger's mouth did something strange to my chest, like a knot pulled one degree tighter and one degree looser at the same time.

They nodded once, business concluded, and turned back toward the Pearlhouse. Then stopped.

"Sefa. The man at the tollhouse is paid to find a woman with your face." They didn't turn around. "You should know the company also circulates descriptions of its runaway indentures. I've seen the board outside the counting-house my whole life. I know what mine looks like. I've had nine years to memorize it."

And they walked inside, leaving me with a badly mended net, a map scratched into a kitchen table, and the sudden understanding that I hadn't recruited an accomplice at all.

I'd shown the route to someone who had been walking toward these checkpoints their entire life, from the other direction.

* * *

Evening brought the whole Pearlhouse into the kitchen, because Mama Osk's rule was that plans made in her house got made in front of everyone who'd bleed for them. Dagny brought word from her brother's old contacts: passage inland disguised as salt haulage, available within the fortnight, price negotiable and non-refundable. The diver children recited the guard rotations at the Tide Gate from memory, because children see everything and adults forget to be careful in front of them. Mama Osk contributed silence and a pot of fish stew, which in her language was a vote.

I drew the route again on the table, this time for the room. Gate, tollhouse, fortress.

"Three doors," I said. "Each one wants papers none of us can forge alone. So the question isn't whether the route can be walked. Somebody walks routes like this all the time—it's called trade. The question is who in this room becomes somebody else for thirty miles, and what it costs them to take the ink off afterward."

Nobody spoke. The stew steamed. Somewhere below the piers, the tide turned, the long sigh of the harbor changing its mind.

Then Lior Nystrom cleared their throat—the smallest sound, the sound of a person volunteering to exist—and said, "About the seals. I'll need wax, a heat source, and something with a fine point. A knife would do."

Dagny looked at the diver's knife at her belt, and then at Lior, and began, very slowly, to smile.

It was Mama Osk who broke the moment, setting down the ladle with a clack. "Before anyone carves anything," she said, "you should know there was a man at the market today asking after the Pearlhouse. Company coat, but wrong boots. He wanted to know if Osk Blackwood had taken in any drowned women lately."

The kitchen went very still.

"What did you tell him?" I asked.

"I told him I take in whoever the sea sends," Mama Osk said, "and that the sea sends more than he'd believe." She picked the ladle back up. "Eat your stew. It seems we're short on time after all."
