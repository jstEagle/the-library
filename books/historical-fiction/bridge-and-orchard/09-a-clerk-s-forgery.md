## 9. A Clerk's Forgery

There is a particular kind of silence in the Pearlhouse at three in the morning, and I have become an expert on it the way a man drowning becomes an expert on water. It is not the silence of sleep. Sleep has texture to it—Dagny's snore through the wall like a saw working green wood, the orphans' bunkroom creaking as small bodies turn, Mama Osk's kettle ticking as it cools on the stove she never quite trusts to stay lit. No, three-in-the-morning silence is the silence of someone awake who should not be. It is my silence. And lately it smells of candle wax.

I had stolen the wax from my own employer, which felt fitting, though technically I hadn't stolen it so much as misfiled it. The Halvard Trading Company orders sealing wax by the gross—a deep arterial red, stamped with the company's sigil, a three-masted ship over crossed anchors—and every stick that arrives is logged, and every stick that is spent is logged again, because the Company believes with a religious fervor that paper does not lie, provided you are the one holding the pen. A half-stick had cracked during the winter shipment and was recorded as wastage. Wastage goes into a drawer. The drawer does not have eyes.

I am aware of how this sounds. Nine years of perfect invisibility, nine years of being the most forgettable clerk in a building full of forgettable clerks, and now here I was, curled in the cold corner of the net-loft with contraband wax, a candle stub, and the only sharp thing I own that predates my indenture: my mother's diving knife.

It is not a dramatic knife. Divers' knives never are—they're blunt-nosed so they don't snag line, short so they don't catch on current, handled in whalebone worn smooth as soap. It cut oysters from rock for eleven years before it came to me, and it has done nothing since but open letters and, once, in a moment of childish rebellion I still blush over, carve my initials into a beam where no one would ever look. It seemed absurd that this humble object was about to commit a crime.

Because that's what forgery is, when you get down to it. Not a sword-fight, not a masked figure prying open a strongbox. Just wax, heat, pressure, patience. The Company's whole authority—the authority that owned nine more years of my life, that had possibly, if Sefa's letters told true, sold children across the sea like sacks of grain—rested on lumps of red wax pressed by brass dies. And a brass die, I had learned in the counting-house, is just a very expensive stamp.

* * *

Let me back up, because even in my own head I keep trying to make this sound inevitable when it was anything but.

Three nights earlier, Sefa had sat across from me in the loft—we keep her above the salt store now, in the room Dagny uses for mending, which means the Pearlhouse smells permanently of tar and herring and, faintly, of the lavender sachets Osk insists on hiding among the nets—and she had laid out what we needed like a general laying out a campaign. Papers. Three checkpoints, three sets of papers, each requiring seals no honest person could produce.

"The Tide Gate wants a harbor writ," she said. "The tollhouse wants transit passes under company seal. And the Company House—" She'd stopped there, because none of us liked finishing that sentence yet.

"And the Company House wants a miracle," Dagny had said from the doorway, needle paused mid-stitch.

"Company seal," Sefa said, looking at me. "You work there."

"I copy manifests," I said. "I don't touch the seal. The factor keeps the die on his own ring. He sleeps with it, probably. I imagine he talks to it."

"You've seen it used, though."

Of course I had. Hundreds of times. Factor Vessel pressing the brass into wax with a little ritual twist—clockwise, firm, lift straight up—while I stood at the desk's end with my eyes appropriately lowered. That was the trouble with being invisible: people performed their secrets in front of you constantly, because to them you were furniture, and furniture doesn't testify.

"Seen it," I admitted. "Twist, press, lift."

"Can you make it?"

I want it noted, for the record that will never exist, that I said no. I said no with real feeling. I enumerated the ways in which I was unqualified: I had no brass, no engraving tools, no training, and a face that did crimes badly, judging by how hot it got whenever Osk asked if I'd eaten. Sefa listened to all of it with the patient attention of someone who has been hunted across two hundred miles of coastline and has developed opinions about excuses.

Then she said, quietly, "They forged your name onto a contract once. Or your number. Whatever you were before you were a number." And she let that sit in the room the way cold sits in a stone house. "Seems fair you should get to hold the pen this time."

I didn't sleep that night. Instead I lay there doing arithmetic, which is what clerks do instead of praying. And somewhere around the fourth hour I realized the arithmetic had changed sides without asking my permission. For nine years the sum had been simple: survive until twenty-one, keep head down, buy the rest of my life back hour by hour. Now the sum had a remainder, and the remainder was a woman sleeping ten feet away whose lungs were still rattling from seawater, and a drawer of names in a vault that might include my own.

So. Wax. Candle stub. Mother's knife. Three in the morning.

* * *

The first attempt was a catastrophe, and I record it here honestly because if history ever happens to me—if I ever become one of those names people argue about in taverns—I refuse to be remembered as competent on the first try.

I melted the wax too far. This is apparently a cardinal sin; sealing wax isn't tallow, it has a temper, and when you abuse it, it goes thin and glassy and spits little angry bubbles at you. I poured a puddle the size of a saucer onto a broken roof slate, watched it spread into something no self-respecting factor would ever sanction, and then realized I had no way to make the impression at all. No die. That had been the plan's single largest hole, and I had walked right up to it in the dark and fallen in.

What I had instead was the knife and a scrap of whalebone, and an idea so stupid it could only have been born at three in the morning: carve the sigil backwards into something hard, and press that into the wax. Mirror-writing for seals. I'd seen apprentices do it with initials on windowpanes. Surely a ship and two anchors couldn't be harder than letters.

Surely, says the clerk, who has never carved anything but his own initials into a beam he hoped nobody would ever inspect.

The knife took the bone grudgingly. Whalebone is dense stuff, and my mother's knife is made for prying, not paring, and twice I slipped and opened the heel of my thumb, and both times I thought—not for the first time, but with new weight—that my hands were supposed to be good at one thing only: holding a pen at a regulation angle, producing numbers in a regulation hand, for a regulation lifetime. My body had been indentured along with the rest of me. Using it for anything else felt like speaking out of turn.

The scar from the pearl beds caught the candlelight while I worked. There's a habit divers' children have, learned before we learn our letters: you keep your forearms turned in. Palms down, sleeves long, wrists angled toward your body, always. The Company taught us that in the cradle, or its agents did—the ones who signed us. Smooth-armed children read as clerks' brats. Scarred children read as property. It was the first forgery I ever practiced, actually, now that I think of it. Nine years of turning my wrists inward every time I reached for a ledger. I'd been counterfeiting a whole self since I was twelve. The wax was just the honest version.

* * *

By the second night I had a mirror-image ship that looked less like a ship than like a beetle that had lost an argument with a boot, and two anchors that resembled nothing so much as fishhooks dreaming of greatness. But the wax was behaving better. I'd learned its moods: melt it slow, off the direct flame, until it went glossy like a wet chestnut; pour in one motion; wait for the skin to form; then press—firm, straight down, no wobble—and lift clean.

Press. Lift.

Beetle. Fishhooks. Boot-wrecked beetle.

"Terrible," said Dagny's voice behind me, and I nearly swallowed the knife.

She'd come up the ladder without a sound, which for a woman of fifty with knees like ship's tackle should be impossible, and stood wrapped in her shawl looking down at my slate of failures with the expression she usually reserved for knots tied by landlubbers.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." She crouched, took the whalebone blank out of my hand without asking—it is astonishing how casually people take things out of my hand, even now, even when my hand is full of felony—and turned it in the candlelight. Her thumbnail found the anchor. "This one's not bad. Ship's wrong. You've got the masts too tall. Look at the company flag on the customs house—ship rides low, masts squat, like it's tired. Everything about their seal is tired. It's the most honest thing about them."

"You've studied it?"

"I've mended sails under it for thirty years." She handed the blank back. "And my brother used to forge harbor writs before they took his license for it. Different crime, same craft. He always said the secret isn't the cutting." She tapped my slate, where the best impression sat surrounded by its failed siblings like ducklings. "It's the pour. Anyone can cut. The pour tells whether you've got nerve."

"The pour."

"Wax waits for no coward," Dagny said. "Pour slow and it sets before you're ready. Pour fast and it runs past your lines. You have to pour like you've already decided." She looked at me then, really looked, the way she almost never does, and said, "Have you decided, boy?"

And because it was three in the morning and the truth keeps different hours, I heard myself say: "I don't know what I'm deciding. That's the terrible part. I keep waiting to feel certain, and certainty doesn't come, and meanwhile I'm carving anchors into whalebone like a man possessed."

"Hm." She resettled her shawl. "When I buried my brother's license—what was left of it, after the Company burned it in front of him—I wasn't certain either. Certainty's for magistrates and factors. Everyone else just decides once and then keeps deciding, over and over, every morning." She rose, knees cracking like rigging in a squall. "Carve the masts shorter. And for the love of the deep, stop bleeding on the evidence."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about committing your first crime: it feels exactly like doing your job.

That's the horror of it, or maybe the comfort. The same posture—bent over a desk, candle low, breath held. The same tools—blade, wax, steady hands. The same absorption, the same narrowing of the world down to the space between blade-tip and line. I have spent nine years learning to lose myself in copying, to become a machine for transcription, and it turns out that machine makes no moral distinction between input. Give it a manifest, it copies. Give it a sigil, it carves. The guilt arrives late, standing off to the side like a supervisor who can't decide whether to reprimand you or take notes.

On the fourth night I got the pour right.

I remember it precisely: the wax going glossy, the single motion, the skin forming like the surface of the harbor at slack tide, and then the bone blank coming down—firm, straight, no wobble—and the small sound it made, a sound like a held breath being let out. I counted five heartbeats, because I had watched Factor Vessel count five heartbeats a hundred times without knowing why, and lifted.

A ship rode in the wax. Squat-masted, tired, low in the hull. Two anchors crossed beneath it. It was not perfect—the border of the impression was rougher than the factor's, and one anchor fluke had a hairline flaw where my knife had skated—but it was unmistakably, damningly, the seal of the Halvard Trading Company. I had made the Company say a thing it never said. I had, using nothing but wax and patience and a dead diver's knife, spoken in the voice of the institution that owns me.

I sat back on my heels and laughed, one syllable, before clapping a hand over my mouth. Above me the beams held their silence. Below me the house slept, all of them—Sefa with her bruised lungs, Osk with her yellowed knuckles, the orphans sprawled like spilled cargo—and none of them knew that the boy in the loft had just crossed a line that does not appear on any map and cannot be recrossed.

That's the thing they don't put in the pamphlets about crime. It isn't the fear of getting caught. I know fear of getting caught; I've carried it since childhood like a second knife, and it weighs nothing anymore, we're old companions. What frightened me, alone in the loft with a passable counterfeit cooling on the slate, was the thrill. Because underneath the terror, singing in my blood like the first cold shock of a dive, was joy. Pure, disgraceful joy. For nine years I had arranged my entire existence around one principle: *nothing that happens in Vessing should happen because of Lior Nystrom.* Be the tide's afterthought. Be the margin note. And now I had picked up a blade and written myself into the story—invisible ink no longer—and some traitorous part of me had gone *finally,* with the same relief as a held breath released underwater.

History happens to other people. That was my creed. Except creeds, it turns out, are also just things that can be forged.

* * *

I should have stopped at one good impression. A sensible clerk practices until passable and stops, banks the candle, files the evidence of his madness under wastage. But the pour had gone well, and Dagny's voice was in my ear—*pour like you've already decided*—and so I did what obsessives and clerks do: I sought perfection in repetition. Six more impressions before dawn. Then I did something worse.

I practiced the hand.

Not mine—mine is regulation, drilled into me by four years of the Company's writing-master, a hand like fence pickets, legible and anonymous and dead. No. I practiced the factor's endorsement script, the looping certification he scrawls beneath every seal: *Examined and approved, H. Vessel, Factor.* I had copied that endorsement hundreds of times in the course of honest work, and I had thought nothing of it, and now every one of those hundred repetitions stood up in my memory like witnesses offering testimony against me. The V with its flat bottom, like a boat run aground. The way his F crosses the crossbar twice, an old habit, a stutter of ink. The little hook he puts on the final r, as though the word were trying to escape him.

I filled a slate with endorsements until my wrist ached, then wiped them away with a rag, then wrote them again. *Examined and approved.* By whom? By a boy the Company bought at eleven years old and never bothered to look at. Approved, H. Vessel, Factor—who had perhaps, if the letters spoke true, signed his approval onto contracts shipping children overseas like cured fish. Somewhere in a vault sat ledgers with names in them, and one of those names might be mine, entered in a hand very much like the one I was teaching my wrist to perform.

Somewhere near dawn I stopped, because my hands had begun to shake, and not from fatigue. It's a strange vertigo, holding a stranger's signature in your muscles. It's a stranger's soul in your tendons. I looked at my mother's knife lying on the slate, whalebone dust in its edge, and thought: *she used this to feed us. To pry loose the one thing the sea would give a poor woman. And now her son uses it to pry loose something else.* I don't know if that would have pleased her. She died before I could ask, which is the arrangement mothers have with the sea.

* * *

Sefa found the evidence two mornings later, because of course she did. I came up to the loft with breakfast and found her holding the best impression to the window light, turning it, her brows drawn together in the expression of a woman reading a letter she doesn't like.

"This is good," she said.

"It's adequate."

"Lior." She used my actual name, which she does rarely and always like a weapon laid flat on a table between us. "This would fool a gate guard at arm's length. Maybe closer." She set it down carefully, as if it might go off. "How?"

"Dagny's brother, apparently, was a forger. She's been giving notes." I hesitated. "The pour is everything, it turns out."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, more the place a smile had been recently and might return to. "My father used to say something like that. About signatures." She went quiet, the way she does whenever the word *father* slips out, and I pretended to be very interested in the breakfast, and the moment passed over both of us like weather.

"There's a problem," she said finally.

"Only one?"

"When you use this—and you will use it, don't make that face, you didn't spend four nights carving anchors into a soup bone for the artistic fulfillment—the guard at the Tide Gate will look at your paper and then he will look at *you*." She let that land. "Papers get a clerk through gates. Faces get remembered. The moment that seal touches wax in anger, you stop being furniture, Lior. You become a person the Company describes to its hunters. Height, hands, habits." She tapped her own temple. "I can recite my own description from memory. Fourteen coastal towns' worth of hunters, and they all carry the same page. You do not want to hear your name read aloud by a man deciding whether to earn his bonus."

I looked at the seal on the table. Tired ship, crossed anchors, the Company's whole weight balanced on a disc of carved bone and a puddle of wax. Nine years of invisibility, and I had built myself a key out of the lock's own face.

"I've been described before," I said, which was a lie, or mostly one. "In the contract. Height, distinguishing marks, value per annum."

"That's not the same."

"No," I agreed. "It's worse. In the contract I was worth something."

She laughed then—short, startled, pained—and it occurred to me that we were both orphans doing arithmetic with the only currency we had, which is each other. Downstairs, Osk's kettle began its morning argument with the stove. Dagny's saw-snore broke off mid-board. The Pearlhouse woke around us, plank by plank, and I slid the forged seal into the false bottom of my ration tin, next to the half-stick of stolen wax and my mother's knife, the beginnings of a criminal's inventory, and went down to breakfast like a clerk with nothing in his pockets but lint.

Except that afternoon, when I passed the customs house on my way to the counting-house, I did something I had never once done in nine years of studied irrelevance. I stopped. I stood in the street, in plain view, and stared up at the Company flag—ship riding low, masts squat, tired, honest—and I memorized it openly, deliberately, letting my face do what faces do.

And across the square, framed in the counting-house window with a column of figures in his hand, Corvin Hale watched me do it, and did not look away.
