## 10. Dagny's Nets

There are three ways to mend a net, and I learned all of them before I was ten: quick, honest, and invisible. The quick way gets you back to fishing by dawn. The honest way holds for a season. The invisible way—knots tucked so the patch reads like water, so no inspector's eye catches on it—that's the way my mother taught me last, because she said it was the only one that mattered in Vessing.

I was thinking about that on the morning after we voted to hide Sefa, working a herring net with a tear in it you could have driven a skiff through, and I realized I'd been mending my whole life the invisible way. My knots. My name. My brother.

The Pearlhouse smelled of kelp smoke and porridge that morning, the way it always does before the fog burns off. Sefa was asleep in the loft where we'd stowed her, sleeping like something that had learned to sleep fast and wake faster. Lior was at the long table doing whatever it is clerks do when they're not being watched—I'd caught them the night before copying something out of the oilskin packet onto scrap paper, hand moving quick as a crab, pretending very hard they weren't doing it. Mama Osk was arguing with the stove. The stove was winning, as usual.

And I sat there with my net across my knees and thought: *well, girl, you voted to hide her, which means you voted for what comes next, and what comes next is the part nobody votes for.*

Because hiding a woman is easy. Hiding is just knots and dark corners and looking bored. What isn't easy is moving her—or the letters she carried—out of Vessing, past three checkpoints, into the hands of a magistrate who may or may not be anything better than the men hunting her. And there is exactly one person in this harbor who knows how things move out of Vessing without the Halvard Company's blessing, and that person has spent eleven years pretending she doesn't.

That person is me. Which is inconvenient, because I've built a whole respectable life on top of the other one, and respectable lives are like nets—you don't notice how much they hold until you start pulling threads.

* * *

Let me tell you about my brother, since apparently I've decided to tell everyone everything now, like a woman standing on a dock shouting her sins into the fog.

Torvald Brand was the best boat-hand in Vessing. Not the strongest, not the boldest—the *best*, which is different. He could read weather the way Lior reads ledgers, all at once and all the way down, and he could bring a loaded ketch through the Tide Gate in a crosswind with his pipe still lit. When the company started licensing harbor traffic—every hull counted, every cargo weighed, every run stamped and taxed—Torvald got license number forty-one, and he wore the tin badge of it like other men wear wedding rings.

For two years he played it straight. Then came the winter our father's boat went down off the Shallows with a company inspector's "all clear" stamped on a hull that any child could see was rotten. No compensation. No inquiry. A clerk named Fenwick sent us a letter saying the loss was "regrettable but non-actionable," and I have hated tidy handwriting ever since.

So Torvald started carrying things that weren't on manifests. Nothing dramatic—not guns, not fugitives, though God knows he'd have taken them. Tea without tax. Wool without stamp. Medicine up-coast for villages the company's pricing had gutted. He called it "correcting the arithmetic," and he'd laugh when he said it, that laugh of his that made even Mama Osk smile, and she doesn't smile for anyone.

He ran clean for four years. Then a man he'd trusted—a man whose wedding he'd stood at—sold the route to the company for thirty crowns and a supervisor's post. They didn't hang Torvald. Hanging's for crimes against people. What he did was a crime against *paper*, and paper crimes get punished slower and worse. They revoked license forty-one in front of the whole harbor office, made him stand there while a clerk crossed out his name—they let the clerk do the crossing-out, that was the cruelty of it, some boy younger than Lior with orders and no choice—and then they blacklisted him from every licensed berth on the coast. Eleven years of skill, struck through with a line of ink.

He went north after that. Works the free ports past Hessel now, comes south maybe twice a year, never the same berth twice. We write in cipher that wouldn't fool a clever child but fools company inspectors fine, which tells you something about company inspectors. Last letter said the northern crews were talking about Vessing again. About the ledgers. About rumors that the dead founder wasn't dead.

When Sefa washed up with those letters, half the harbor heard whispers inside two days, because harbors are made of whispers. But only I knew that Torvald had already written me, six weeks before Sefa ever tangled under those pilings: *If word from the south ever proves true, little sister, there are men here who would carry it to the circuit judges themselves. Ask and the route opens.*

I hadn't asked. I'd read the letter four times and put it in the loose brick behind the stove, and told myself asking was a door you couldn't shut again.

* * *

I found Lior at midday on the low pier behind the Pearlhouse, mending a rowboat's paint with the concentration of someone trying very hard to be furniture. That's the thing about that clerk—nine years in the company's house and they've turned invisibility into a craft, near as good as my knots. But I've spent my life reading small movements; it's how you survive a harbor. And I'd watched Lior watch Sefa, and watched them flinch when boots hit the deck overhead, and I knew that flinch. It's the flinch of someone deciding, daily, hourly, whether to be brave yet.

"Your stitching's wrong," I said, sitting down beside them without asking. The rowboat rocked. "You're pulling too tight. Paint's not thread, clerk. It needs give."

"I'll remember," Lior said, in the flat company voice, and then—because the pier was empty and the fog was thick and even brave people can be honest in fog—"I won't remember. I don't know the first thing about boats."

"No," I agreed. "You know about numbers instead. Worse luck for you."

They almost smiled. Almost. Nine years of practice keeping the face still.

"Sefa needs to move," I said. "Or the letters do. Sooner or later—and knowing the company, sooner—the hunters widen their search past the docks. Mama Osk can hide her in the loft for a week, maybe two, but a week isn't a plan. It's a delay wearing a plan's coat."

Lior set down the brush. Carefully. Everything they do is careful, like the world once taught them carelessness costs blood. "Do you have one? A plan?"

"I have a brother."

"That's not a plan."

"It's better. Plans break. Family owes." I looked out at the water, gray over gray, gulls hanging motionless like thoughts nobody finished. "Torvald runs the northern routes. Free ports past Hessel, contacts at every unlicensed jetty from here to the capital's back stairs. If there's a way to move a packet—or a person—past three checkpoints without the company smelling it, he knows it, or knows the man who does."

Lior went very still. I could see the arithmetic running behind their eyes—it's an eerie thing, watching a clerk think; you can nearly hear the abacus. "The cost," they said finally. "There's always a cost."

"There is." I pulled a strand of net-cord from my pocket and worried it between my fingers, because my hands need work when my mouth does hard labor. "Smugglers don't take coin from strangers. Coin can be traced; trust can't. Torvald will move this freight because it's me asking. Which means if it goes wrong—if anyone follows the route back—it finds my name, and then his. His license is already ash. Another strike and he's not blacklisted, he's *hunted*, same as Sefa."

"You'd risk that."

I laughed. It came out rougher than I meant. "Clerk, let me tell you what I risk every day already. I mend nets for the company's licensed fleet—did you know that? Half the boats in this harbor fish under company license, pay company tithe, sell at company price to company warehouses that log them down at half worth and resell up-coast at triple. My neighbor Brenna lost her berth when the company bought the herring grounds and 'consolidated.' Old Piet dives oysters on a permit that costs more each year than he earns, because his grandfather dove it and nobody told him he could stop. There are children on these docks who'll be signed into indenture at eight because their parents owed the company store at winter and the store keeps its own arithmetic. You think Sefa's war is hers alone?" I shook my head. "The harbor's been bleeding for years, quiet, one nick at a time. Nobody bled enough at once to make a scar anyone could point to. Those letters might be the scar."

Lior was staring at me. Not the polite clerk-stare. Something rawer underneath, like a person hearing their own private grief read aloud in a crowd.

"The double entry," they said quietly. "The pearls logged twice."

"Mm. Heard about that." I hadn't, not directly—but harbors are made of whispers, and I'd heard *something* watching Lior watch columns. "Say it plainly, clerk. What did you find?"

And here's the thing I'll say about Lior Nystrom, whatever else gets written about them after: they decided. Right there on the pier, in the space of one breath, I watched them decide—watched the invisible knots come undone, one by one, like a net finally allowed to show its mending. They told me about the column. The shipment recorded twice. The quiet instruction drilled into every company clerk: *see nothing, say nothing, reconcile nothing.* And they told me, voice steady as a lead-line, why they'd copied pages out of Sefa's packet in secret—because if the letters were lost, someone should carry the pattern of them in memory, in their own hand, somewhere the company couldn't burn.

"Well," I said, when they finished. "You're not much of an invisible clerk after all."

"Neither are you much of a respectable net-mender."

"No," I said. "We're a harbor of liars with honest faces. Always have been. It's how we survived the company's honesty." I stood, brushed off my skirts, and made the decision official the only way decisions get made in Vessing—out loud, in fog, past the point of taking back. "Tonight I write north. Torvald will answer inside a fortnight with a route and a price. Until then, nobody moves and nobody talks. Not to Mama Osk, not to me, not to your own reflection."

"And the cost?" Lior said. "You never gave me the full cost."

I looked at them for a long moment. Fog in the rigging. Gulls arguing over something dead.

"The cost is that we stop being able to lose," I said. "You, me, Torvald, Sefa, Osk, every soul who touches this. Once the letters start moving, we're not people anymore—we're evidence. And evidence doesn't get to retire."

* * *

I wrote the letter that night by stove-light, in our childish cipher, while Mama Osk pretended not to watch and Sefa slept badly overhead, muttering names in a language I didn't know. I wrote: *Word proved true. Sister asks. Route opens.* And then, because I'm my mother's daughter and my mother taught me to finish things, I added one line plain, no cipher, where any inspector who intercepted it would read it and understand nothing but would be wrong:

*This one matters. This one's for Papa's boat.*

Mama Osk took it from me without a word, read it twice, and folded it into her apron with hands like knotted oak. "My sister-in-law's cousin runs the pilot boat to Hessel," she said. "Leaves Thursday, tide before dawn. Your letter travels free and unseen, or my name's not Osk." She looked at me over the stove that had finally surrendered. "Eleven years you kept that brother locked in your chest, girl. What changed?"

I thought about lying to her. She'd have known; she always knows; it's her superpower along with winning arguments against stoves.

"Papa's boat sank with a company stamp on the survey," I said. "Torvald lost his name to a clerk's pen. And now there's a woman upstairs with a dead man's letters, and the company wants her found before anyone reads them." I shrugged, and it felt like setting down something heavy I'd forgotten I was carrying. "I'm tired of mending invisible, Mama. I want one knot in my life tied honest."

She nodded slowly, the way she does, like the nod has to travel a long way to get to her head. "Honest knots hold worst," she said. "In nets and otherwise. My mother told me the same."

"What held for her, then?"

Osk smiled—one of the rare ones, the ones she pays for later with silence. "Nothing," she said. "That's how I knew she was right about everything else."

Upstairs, Sefa cried out in her sleep—a short, bitten-off sound, the kind you learn to swallow young—and I heard Lior's bare feet on the ladder going up to sit with her, quick and quiet, before I'd even finished turning around.

*Thursday, then,* I thought. *Tide before dawn.*

And that was the plan, such as it was: letter north, route back, smugglers' arithmetic, three checkpoints between us and a magistrate. Simple. Harbors teach you there's no such thing as simple, but hope is a tide and it doesn't ask permission.

It was two days later, Wednesday, gray and mean, that I noticed the man.

He came down the low road past the Pearlhouse in a company coat cut for a bigger man, walking slow, the way company men walk when they're counting windows. Not enforcers—enforcers come loud. This was worse. This was somebody *thinking*. He stopped at the fence, looked at the roofline, looked at the loft window where the shutter hung a finger's width open, and wrote something in a little book.

Then he looked directly at me, standing in the yard with a net over my arm, and touched his hat brim like a gentleman, and walked on toward the counting-house.

I stood in the yard with my heart going like a drum-boat in fog and made myself breathe. Company men touch their hats all the time. Company men count windows all the time. But my hands knew what my head was denying: that shutter had been open a finger's width since Tuesday, and Tuesday was the day I'd propped it to air the mildew out of Sefa's blankets, because hiding a living woman means feeding her and airing her, and living leaves traces everywhere.

I went inside, found Lior bent over the wash basin, and said the words I'd sworn two days ago I wouldn't need until Thursday:

"Clerk. Change of schedule. Someone's been counting our windows—and if I had to bet this harbor, I'd say we've got less time than anyone thinks."
