## 12. Letters from the Exile

There is an art to reading a letter aloud, and my father taught it to me without ever meaning to. He was a man who wrote the way other men prayed—carefully, obsessively, with the sense that someone was listening who might punish a sloppy sentence. When I was small, he would read his own correspondence back to himself in the counting-house after hours, and I would sit under the desk and steal dried plums from his satchel and learn, plum by plum, that words said out loud carry a different weight than words left on paper. Paper can be denied. A voice, once heard, belongs to the room.

Which is why, on the fourth night after Mama Osk's vote, I took the oilskin packet to the center of the Pearlhouse kitchen instead of reading it privately to Lior in the loft where we slept.

"Sit down," I told them. "All of you. I'm going to read you a dead man's letters."

Mama Osk kept ladling soup, because Mama Osk believed—correctly—that no crisis had yet been invented that could not be met with soup in hand. Dagny set down her netting needles. The divers came in from the cold with their hair still dripping and sat on the floor like children at a fire, because they were children, mostly, and they knew a story when one walked into their house half-drowned.

Lior stood by the door. Of course Lior stood by the door. In the days I'd known them, I had never once seen Lior sit anywhere without calculating the distance to an exit first. It was, I would come to understand, not cowardice but arithmetic—the kind of sum a diver's child learns before learning letters.

"You don't have to do this," Lior said quietly.

"Yes, I do." I unwrapped the oilskin. "Paper can be burned. What I'm about to say can't be."

* * *

I should explain what I carried, so you understand what they heard.

Seven letters, written over three years in exile, in my father's hand—a hand I could pick out of a thousand, all sharp ascenders and ink-saved economy, the writing of a man raised on rationed paper. Each letter addressed to no one, which was his way of addressing everyone: *To whoever finds this, if anyone does.* He had given them to me in installments, sealed, with instructions I only partly understood then. Take them to Vessing. Wait for the tide of things to turn. If I drowned, truly drowned, deliver them to the magistrate's spring circuit and let the numbers speak.

He had not drowned. But he had been declared drowned, which in the eyes of the Halvard Trading Company amounted to the same thing, and there are men in this world who will spend money to make a declaration true retroactively.

The first letter began the way he began everything: with an apology for the length of the document.

*"Forgive the tedium of accounts,"* I read, *"but tedium is where the company buries its crimes. No one audits a boring thing."*

Then he got to it. The Halvard Trading Company held indentures—hundreds of them, dock children and diver children bound until twenty-one, their contracts held as security against debts inherited from parents who had signed them in desperate seasons. This much was legal, or legal enough; the coast had always run on such arrangements. What my father found, in the deep columns of the company's overseas books, was that the contracts were being sold. Not assigned—sold, outright and permanently, to buyers in the southern ports and beyond: pearl fleets, salt works, houses whose names appeared nowhere in any honest registry. The children did not go. The children stayed in Vessing and worked their years. But the paper—the paper traveled, and changed hands, and multiplied in value, and somewhere across the water there were men collecting interest, year after year, on the lives of children they had never seen.

And when those children turned twenty-one and asked for their freedom papers, the company shrugged and produced the record showing their contract belonged to some house in Karst or Callowmere, and the debt began again, or the freedom simply never came, and no one—not the child, not the parents, often not even the local factor—knew why.

"It's not the work that's the crime," I read, my father's careful voice coming out of my mouth in a room smelling of fish stew. "The coast has always put its children to work, and shame on us all for it, but there is work and there is theft. This is theft. They have taken the one thing these children own outright—their own futures—and sold it out from under them while they slept in company bunks."

Dagny's needles had gone still. One of the young divers, Pell, was staring at the table as though the wood grain might explain something.

"The factor here—Grell, in those days still under-factor—proposed it to me as innovation," I read. "He called it 'liquidity.' He said the contracts were capital lying idle. I told him the only thing idle in Vessing was his conscience, and within the month I was a drowning victim."

* * *

I paused there, because the next part was mine as much as his, and my voice wanted to fail me, and I am vain enough about my voice to deny it the satisfaction.

My father had faked his death badly at first—a boat lost in open water, no body, an insult to the intelligence of anyone who had ever lived beside a sea. So he'd done it again, better: a body purchased from a pauper's ward, a grieving "daughter" produced for the record—I was nineteen and furious about my role in that ceremony and have since apologized to myself at length—and a quiet exit southward while the company rang bells for a founder it had already begun robbing. For seven years he had lived in rooms rented under false names, moving whenever the company's correspondence moved, writing his letters, building his case column by column, waiting for a magistrate's circuit strong enough—and far enough from Grell's purse—to hear him.

"He trusted the law more than he trusted people," I said, folding the third letter. "That was always his flaw. He thought if the numbers were right, the world would have to agree with them."

"And you don't?" Mama Osk asked. She had finally sat down, which I suspected was significant.

"I trust people to listen," I said. "It's the agreeing I've stopped counting on."

Lior hadn't moved from the door. But Lior's face had gone through several changes since I began—wry attention, then stillness, and now something I couldn't name, a look like a person doing sums and arriving somewhere terrible.

* * *

I read the fifth letter last of the night, because I had saved it, though I told myself I hadn't.

*"They keep two sets of books,"* my father wrote. *"Every counting-house keeps two; that is the trade's oldest joke. But Halvard keeps three. The second ledger holds the truth of the port—the real shipments, the real prices, the pearls recorded twice and paid for once. That ledger sits in the Company House vault and comes out twice a year for the capital audit. The third—"*

Here his handwriting changed. Slower. As though he had to press each word into the page individually.

*"The third ledger is the one I cannot forgive. It records the sales themselves. Every contract sold overseas, listed by name. Not by number—Grell's cleverness was to keep names, because names raise the price. Buyers pay more for a child with a history, a mother, a birth season. It reads like a parish register of everything we have broken."*

I looked up.

"In the packet," I said, "there is a partial copy. He could never take the whole book. But he copied pages. Names, dates, buyers. Enough to hang every man who touched it, if it reaches the circuit."

The kitchen was very quiet. Out on the water, the tide was turning—you could hear it change under the pilings if you'd grown up here, a long slow exhale beneath the house.

And then Lior spoke, from the doorway, in a voice I hadn't heard from them before. Flat. Careful. Each word placed like a foot on wet rock.

"Read the names."

"Sefa—" Dagny started.

"Read the names," Lior said again, and crossed the room, and took the copied pages out of my hands before I could decide whether to allow it.

* * *

I watched Lior read. I want to be honest about what that was like, because honesty is the whole point of the exercise and I have lied enough for one lifetime on my father's behalf.

Lior Nystrom read those pages the way a diver works a bottom—methodically, breath held, eyes moving in steady sweeps, refusing to come up early. The fire popped. Pell whispered something to the diver beside them and was hushed. And Lior's finger went down the column of names, and slowed, and stopped.

I saw the stopping. From where I sat I saw the exact moment the finger quit moving, and I knew—I knew before Lior said anything—what kind of name stops a finger like that.

"Nystrom," Lior said. "It's here. Lior Nystrom. Indenture number 4-1-1-9. Sold." A pause like a held breath underwater. "Sold to the Callowmere Salt Works. Six years ago."

The arithmetic arrived in the room before the feeling did. I could see it land on everyone at different speeds. Dagny got there fast—she swore, one short word, and put her hand flat on the table. Mama Osk closed her eyes. Pell and the others just looked, the way children look at weather they don't yet have words for.

But Lior kept reading. That was the thing about Lior I was beginning to understand and could not yet forgive: they read to the bottom of the page, and the next page, running their finger past their own name like a diver passing a wreck they'll come back for, cataloguing. How many others. Who else in this room. Who else on this street.

"Twenty-three," Lior said finally, setting the pages down with a care that frightened me more than throwing them would have. "Twenty-three names from Vessing on these pages alone. Half of them I know. Two of them are eating your soup right now."

Pell made a small sound.

"That's why the contract's strange," Lior went on, and now the voice was doing something complicated, staying level through sheer force of clerkly habit. "Everyone always said it. The Nystrom indenture doesn't read like the others. Different terms. A renewal clause nobody could explain. My mother signed a seven-year bond and I've served nine and the company keeps telling me the paperwork is 'in resolution.'" A laugh escaped them, awful and dry. "It's not in resolution. It's been earning interest in Callowmere for six years. I've been working off a debt to someone I've never met."

"Lior." I stood up. "Lior, sit down—"

"Don't tell me to sit." Not angry, exactly. Something past anger, colder and cleaner. "You knew. You knew what was in these letters before you read them tonight."

"I knew what my father found. I didn't know your name was in it. I haven't read every page—there wasn't time, there's never been time—"

"But you guessed," Lior said. "On the pilings, the first night. You asked me how old I was. You asked when my indenture ended. I thought you were making conversation." Another terrible little laugh. "Nobody makes conversation with me. I'm not conversationable. I'm furniture that writes in a fine hand."

"I was trying to find out if you'd been sold," I said, because it was true and because lying to Lior Nystrom had begun to seem like a physiologically impossible act. "Because if you had been—if any of you had—it meant the pages in this packet weren't just evidence. They were proof that matched living people. Names with faces. Do you understand what that's worth in front of a magistrate? A ledger proves theft. A ledger plus a person standing in the room saying *that's me* proves it so hard it can't be buried."

"So I'm useful," Lior said.

"You're—" I stopped. Took a breath. "Yes. Fine. Yes, you are useful, and I am sorry the truth of your life arrived packaged in my father's handwriting, and no, I did not plan it, and yes, I would have told you the moment I knew, and I didn't know, and you may hate me for an hour if it helps."

Lior looked at me for a long moment. Then, incredibly, they sat down—the first time I had ever seen them sit anywhere in that room without checking the window first—and picked up the pages again.

"An hour," they said. "Starting now."

* * *

We worked until the candle guttered, because grief in the Pearlhouse apparently converted directly into labor.

Lior cross-referenced. It was astonishing to watch—the clerk taking over completely, the fear folded up and put away somewhere it could be retrieved later, the training running like an engine. Twenty-three names became a list; the list became a map of the harbor streets; the map became, in Lior's dry voice, "a witness pool, if any of them can be persuaded to stand up in public, which historically in Vessing the answer is no."

"People said nothing for nine years," Dagny pointed out. "Why would they say something now?"

"Because now there's a book," Lior said. "People won't testify against a rumor. They'll testify against paper. Paper means it might actually fall on somebody." They rubbed their eyes. "Also because half of them will want their contracts voided more than they want to stay safe, and the other half have mothers like mine had, and my mother would have marched into that square with a boat hook."

"Your mother dove," Mama Osk said quietly, from her corner. It wasn't quite a question.

"My mother dove," Lior said, and something passed between them that I was not invited into, and did not ask about. Later. There was a *later* in that look, and I have learned to respect a later.

Near the end, when the list was done and the copies were squared and rewrapped in the oilskin, Lior held the packet a moment before handing it back.

"Your father," they said. "When he found his own name on the sale lists—he didn't, I assume. Founders don't sell themselves."

"No," I said. "He found something worse. He found that his signature was on the authorizations. Forged, but expertly—Grell had a man for it. As far as the capital books show, Aldric Vane invented the scheme himself. That's the second reason he had to die. The crime needed a dead architect, and he was available."

Lior absorbed that. Then, in the flattest voice I'd yet heard from them:

"So the company didn't just kill him. They made him the confession."

"And he's spent seven years writing the correction," I said. "Column by column. From rooms with bad roofs, eating food I wouldn't serve to a gull."

"And you're the correction's courier."

"I'm his daughter," I said, "who happens to be a courier, and I have spent a long time telling myself those are different things." I tied the oilskin closed. "Ask me again sometime whether I believe that."

Lior almost smiled. Almost. It was, I suspect, the hour of hatred expiring exactly on schedule.

"There's a problem," Lior said instead. "A procedural one. Now that I know what I'm carrying and why."

"Name it."

"The magistrate's circuit sits in spring. Spring is months away. These letters describe ledgers that move twice a year for audit—and the autumn audit is soon. If Grell sends the books to the capital before the circuit arrives, we're carrying proof of a crime whose evidence has emigrated." They finally, finally let the exhaustion show. "We don't have until spring, Sefa. We might not have until the month's end. Whatever we're going to do with this, the route, the checkpoints, all of it—we need to start now. Tonight, if the tide allows. Which it doesn't. But it never does, so."

I looked around the kitchen—at Dagny already reaching for her needles and her temper, at Mama Osk rising with the unhurried gravity of a woman about to organize a conspiracy between courses, at the divers pretending not to listen and listening with their whole bodies, at Lior Nystrom holding a dead man's letters like something that had teeth.

"Well then," I said. "Somebody fetch a chart. We're going to need a route past three checkpoints, and I don't imagine the company will be stamping our papers."
