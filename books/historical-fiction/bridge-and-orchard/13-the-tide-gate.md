## 13. The Tide Gate

There is a particular smell to a lie that has been sewn by hand: tallow, salt, and thread waxed with fear. I know this now because I spent four hours the night before the Tide Gate stitching myself into a criminal.

Not literally. Literally would have been simpler.

The packet—Vane's letters, seven years of a dead man's handwriting—lay flat as a communion wafer between two layers of canvas, sewn into the belly of a repaired jibsail that belonged, on paper, to the *Kestrel's Mercy*, a coastal trader with a hold full of dried cod and a captain who owed Dagny more favors than he could count without weeping. The sail was legitimate. The mending was legitimate. The clerk carrying it across the Tide Gate was the only forgery in the whole arrangement, and forgeries of people are the hardest kind, because you have to bring the original with you.

"Say it back," Dagny said, working her needle through the last inch of seam. Her hands did not tremble. She had been mending nets since before I was born, and a needle in Dagny's grip was less a tool than an extension of her opinion.

"The *Kestrel's Mercy* came in Tuesday with a split jib," I recited. "Company regulation requires damaged sail to be carried to the sail-loft at Millward's for inspection before repair. Repaired sail returning to a vessel must pass the Tide Gate with a chit signed by the loft and countersigned by a company clerk. I am that clerk. Number 4-4-1-7."

"And why does a clerk carry a sail himself instead of sending a porter?"

I hesitated half a breath too long, and Dagny's eyes came up like gulls spotting offal.

"Lior."

"Because," I said, "the loft was short-handed and the *Mercy* sails on tomorrow's tide, and clerks do small humiliations all day long. It's true. That's the beauty of it. It's *almost* true."

"Almost true is what the gallows is made of." She bit the thread. "But it'll hold, if your hands don't."

My hands. Right. Let me tell you something about my hands: they had spent nine years learning to be invisible. They copied manifests in a hand so neutral that Hale once told me—I thought it was a compliment at the time—that reading my ledgers was like listening to rain. My hands knew how to hold a pen so that nothing of me leaked onto the page. They did not know how to carry a dead man's letters through a checkpoint while my heart attempted to exit through my throat.

Sefa watched from the loft's dark corner, hood up, one shoulder still stiff from the sea. She had wanted to come to the Gate herself, which was the one thing we had all agreed she could not do, because the company had paid good money to watch for her face, and Sefa's face was the sort that got itself remembered. She'd argued anyway. Sefa argued the way water argues with stone: patiently, continuously, with total confidence in the outcome.

"If it goes wrong," she said now, for the fourth time, "you drop the sail. You curse the loft, you demand a porter, you make yourself a nuisance. Nuisances get waved through. Nobody searches a man who's complaining about his back."

"And if they search him anyway?"

"Then you run," Dagny said flatly, "and you don't stop running until you're under the pilings, and you don't come up for anything. Not for me, not for her. Sail can be remade. Letters can't. And clerks—" she looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, "—clerks are rarer than they think they are."

That night I slept three hours, which I counted a triumph.

* * *

The Tide Gate is not a gate in any sense a farmer would understand. It is a gap in the harbor chain where the channel narrows between the old customs wall and the pilings of the fish-market, and everything that moves by water—or pretends to—must pass its single stone arch. Above the arch sits a guardhouse the color of old teeth. Below it, twice a day, the tide runs hard enough to fold a swimmer in half. The company chose it for a checkpoint for the same reason it chooses everything: because the geography had already done the cruel part, and the company hates paying for cruelty it can get at cost.

I came down Harbor Street at the gray hour, when the lamps are lit but ashamed of it, carrying the rolled sail over my shoulder like a corpse that had agreed to cooperate. The chit was in my breast pocket. The packet was in the sail. The lie was in my mouth, rehearsed until it had worn smooth, like a stone in a boy's pocket.

The guard at the arch was not one of the big ones. He was a thin man with a beard trimmed with more care than his wages should have allowed, and he was eating a boiled egg, and he looked at me the way guards look at clerks—which is to say, past me, at nothing, with the mild contempt of a man whose job consists entirely of other people's paperwork.

"Chit," he said, around egg.

I gave him the chit. My hand did not shake. I want that recorded somewhere. Somewhere in the great ledger of my life there ought to be a column marked *did not shake*, and this entry in it.

He read it slowly. Boiled eggs are slow work; everything around them becomes slow too. Behind me the tide sucked at the arch, and ahead of me the *Kestrel's Mercy* lay at anchor in the outer roads, and somewhere in the crowd behind—a crowd I did not look at, because looking is a form of confessing—Dagny was mending a net thirty feet from the wall, and if things went wrong she would see them go wrong and there was nothing on this earth she could do about it, which is the arithmetic of every plan worth the name.

"Sail inspection," the guard said. "For the *Mercy*."

"Yes."

"Where's the loft mark?"

"On the head of the sail. Third reef band, port side." This was true. Dagny had put it there with a copying brush and the loft's own stamp, borrowed through channels I had declined to ask about. Truth again, laid like ballast.

He grunted and unrolled six inches of the sail across his table, and my heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as a diver's turn, and then he found the mark, and he grunted again, and he began to roll it back—

—and stopped.

Now. Here is the moment I will remember when I am old, if I am permitted to be old, which given the week I was having seemed optimistic. His thumb found the place. Not the place—the *kind* of place. A patch. A mended spot, canvas doubled and stitched, four inches wide, exactly the width of an oilskin packet folded flat, exactly where a sensible woman who loved me would have told me to hide something, except Dagny hadn't hidden it there because Dagny understood what I was still learning: that guards find what cleverness puts in front of them, and miss what boredom hides.

She had put the patch amid a dozen others. A split jib gets patched in ten places. She had scattered repairs across the whole body of the sail like a woman salting a field, so that no single stitch meant anything. But the guard's thumb had stopped on one of them anyway—not suspiciously, just the idle thumb of a bored man reading cloth the way I read columns—and he pressed it once, twice, testing the seam, and said:

"This recent?"

"Tuesday's work," I said. My voice came out level. I have no idea how. Somewhere inside me a boy who had been trained since childhood to be unremarkable took over the interview, the way a helmsman takes over from a drowning sailor. "Loft said the canvas was rotten along the foot. They doubled the whole run."

"Mm." He pressed the seam again. Under his thumb, separated from his fingernail by two layers of canvas and one of oilskin and perhaps half an inch of history, lay Aldric Vane's handwriting, and the names of sold children, and the reason a founder of the Halvard Trading Company had chosen to be dead. "Loft usually sends these down on the porter's barrow."

"The *Mercy* sails on the morning tide," I said. "Captain Voss wouldn't wait for the barrow. He doesn't wait for anything. He weighs anchor if you're mid-sentence."

This was true. It was so true that the guard snorted, because everyone in Vessing knew Captain Voss, and knowing Captain Voss was itself a kind of alibi. Truth, I was discovering, works like current: you don't fight it, you trim your sails to it.

The guard rolled the sail closed. Tied it. Handed it back.

And then, because the world is not built for the comfort of conspirators, he said: "You're Nystrom. The company clerk. Pearl-diver's kid."

I want to explain what happened to me then. Nothing happened. Externally. Internally, every plan I had ever made caught fire simultaneously. Because being known is the one thing I had spent nine years avoiding. I was the boy nobody remembered, the number nobody read, the ledger-line nobody audited twice—and here was a guard at the Tide Gate saying my name as if names were free.

"You went to the charity school," he went on, wiping his fingers on his coat. "Winter term. My sister sat behind you. Said you never talked."

"No," I agreed. "Never talked."

"Huh." He looked at me a moment longer, and in that moment I understood that casual recognition is more dangerous than suspicion, because suspicion has procedure, and procedure can be survived, but recognition has only memory, and memory can go anywhere. "Well. Mind the tide. It turns in twenty minutes and it won't wait for clerks."

"It never does," I said, and walked through the arch.

I did not look back. Looking back is for people in stories. I walked with the sail on my shoulder and my pulse hammering so loud I was sure it would leave marks on the canvas, out the far side of the gate, down the wet steps to the waiting skiff, where Dagny's nephew held the painter and pretended, with great professionalism, not to exist.

It was only when we were out past the second piling, the Gate shrinking behind us in the mist, that I discovered my hands were shaking. Nine years of stillness, undone in ninety seconds of aftermath. The body keeps its own ledgers, and it settles accounts later, when it's safe.

We came alongside the *Kestrel's Mercy* and handed the sail up to the bosun, who whistled it aloft with the rest of the working canvas, and I stood in the bilgewater-smelling bottom of a skiff and watched Aldric Vane's testimony rise up the mast of a cod boat, headed for the open sea, the coast road, the tollhouse, the Company House, the magistrate.

"That's the easy one done," said Dagny's nephew.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me sideways, ragged, halfway to a sob. The easy one. The Tide Gate—a bored guard, an egg, a name from a winter term nine years ago, a thumb on a seam. If that was easy, I shuddered to think what vocabulary the Salt Road tollhouse would require.

* * *

I came back through the fish-market at dusk, empty-shouldered, light as a liar with a clean chit. The sail had gone aloft; the letters were at sea; the first checkpoint was a story I would tell someday, if I lived to collect stories. I bought a hot eel pie with actual money, from an actual vendor, as an actual free-ish man walking home from an actual day's treason, and I ate it standing at the rail above the tideline, and it was the best meal of my life, which says something grim about my life.

Sefa found me there. She had a genius for appearing at my elbow exactly when my guard was lowest, which I suspected was not coincidence but craft. She looked out at the darkening water, at the lamp of the *Mercy* far out in the roads, and didn't ask. That was her way: she never asked what could be seen.

"Well?" she said finally.

"Gone through. Barely." I wiped my fingers. "A guard knew my name. Knew my school. Knew I never talked."

"He wasn't wrong."

"I'm aware." I looked at her. In the lamplight her face had that quality the sea has at slack tide—all its violence paused, held, aimed somewhere. "It frightened me more than a search would have. Being visible."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she said, in a voice unlike her usual one—lower, older, like something she kept wrapped: "Lior. There's something you should know before the tollhouse. About why I'm really doing this. About who Vane is to me."

The tide turned below us with a sound like a door unlocking.

"I saw the way you look at the letters," she said. "Like they're evidence. Paper to be moved from one column to another. And they are. But they're also—" she stopped, jaw working, and for the first time since I'd dragged her half-drowned from under the pilings, Sefa looked afraid, not of hunters or checkpoints, but of a sentence she was about to finish.

"Aldric Vane isn't just the man who wrote those letters," she said. "He's my father. And I'm not going to stop at the magistrate."

Below us, the sea kept moving, indifferent as a ledger, and I understood that the second checkpoint was going to be the least of what the Salt Road asked of me.
