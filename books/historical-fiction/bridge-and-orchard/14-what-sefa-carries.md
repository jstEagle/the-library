## 14. What Sefa Carries

There is a particular kind of courage required to confess something to a person who has already risked their neck for you, and it is not the kind people sing songs about. The songs are all about leaping in front of blades. Nobody writes ballads about sitting on a salt-damp crate in the back of a net loft at midnight and saying the true thing out loud for the first time.

I'd been carrying it for seven years. It had gotten heavier, not lighter, the way secrets do—like a stone you keep in your pocket so long your body grows around it, and then taking it out feels like losing a bone.

Lior sat across from me with a lamp turned low between us, mending a tear in the hem of my coat because apparently that was what Lior did instead of sleeping. Neat little stitches. Hands that had never once been noticed by anyone important, which was the whole point of them.

"You're staring at me," Lior said, without looking up. "People stare at me all day at the counting-house. I come here to be stared at less."

"I need to tell you something."

"That's ominous." They bit the thread. "Say it before I invent six worse possibilities. I'm very good at that. It's a clerk's disease."

I had rehearsed this. On the boat south, in the water, under the pilings while my fingers went numb, I had rehearsed it—clean versions, brave versions, versions where I said it lightly and laughed. None of those came out. What came out was:

"Aldric Vane is my father."

The needle stopped. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the tide was doing its patient work against the harbor stones, and I thought absurdly that it sounded like an audience waiting.

"Lior," I said, when they didn't speak. "Say something."

"You've been calling him *Vane*." Their voice was flat, careful—the voice I'd learned they used when something mattered too much to let any color into it. "For four days. You've said *the founder*, *the old man*, *Vane*. You read his letters to us like a herald reading a proclamation."

"Because if I called him what he is, I wouldn't have been able to read them at all." I heard how it sounded even as I said it, and hated it, and kept going, because the worst part of a confession is that once it starts it doesn't care how it makes you look. "You don't understand what those letters cost me. Every one of them is in his hand. He does the little hook on the descenders—you've seen it, you recognized it from the old documents—and every time I see that hook I'm nine years old again watching him write across the bottom of my schoolwork. *Good reasoning, Sefa. Watch your margins.*" My laugh came out wrong. "He corrected my margins. The man who built half of Vessing corrected a child's margins, and now he's alive, and the whole world thinks he's at the bottom of the sea, and I've spent seven years being the only person who knew and couldn't tell anyone."

Lior set down the coat. Slowly, the way they did everything—like a person who had learned that sudden movements got noticed, and being noticed got you hurt.

"The letters say he's alive," Lior said. "They say where he can be reached. You told us the courier line goes through the fish-factor at Corrin's Reach."

"Yes."

"And you mean to go there. Not to the magistrate's circuit. To him."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway. "Yes."

* * *

Here is what I had not said, in the Pearlhouse supper debates, in the planning by lamplight, in all the careful talk of checkpoints and seals and papers: that the plan we had agreed on was mine only halfway. Everyone had voted to get the evidence to the magistrate. That was the mission. That was the thing Lior had stitched and forged and bled for. And I had nodded along, because it was true, because the ledger and the letters needed to land somewhere with the power to break Halvard Trading open like a lobster.

But underneath it, quiet as silt, I had always intended something else. Get the packet close enough to the circuit to be safe, and then peel away north to Corrin's Reach, and then to wherever my father's next hiding place was. Deliver the daughter along with the letters. Let the magistrate do justice without me; let me have the one thing justice couldn't give me.

I had told myself it wasn't a betrayal. I'd gotten very good at telling myself things.

"You knew," I said now, "that I wanted to find him. I said it the first night. I said *if he's alive, someone should reach him.*"

"You said it like a courier's duty," Lior said. "Not like a daughter's."

"Maybe I didn't know the difference myself."

"No," Lior said, and there was no cruelty in it, only that terrible clerical precision, "you knew. You just filed it under the other heading so you wouldn't have to look at it. I do that too. I know exactly how it's done."

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about Lior Nystrom by then, four days out from dragging me bleeding into the Pearlhouse: almost nothing true, and everything important.

I knew they were nineteen, indentured since childhood, bound to the company until twenty-one like a shipment with a delivery date. I knew they hid a scar from pearl diving under long sleeves even indoors, and that Mama Osk knew why, and that Lior flinched whenever anyone asked about their mother. I knew they could copy any hand they'd seen twice, which was either a gift or a curse depending on which side of the forgery you stood. I knew they moved through rooms like a draft—present, unnoticed, gone.

And I had assumed—I see it now, the arrogance of it—that a person like that, a person who had made themselves invisible to survive, would want nothing except to survive. That the mission for them was the ledger, clean and impersonal, an act of arithmetic rather than love. That they were helping me out of decency and danger-sharing and would be glad to be rid of both me and my complications once the circuit sat.

I hadn't understood yet that invisible people keep the deepest accounts. That a clerk who watches everything and says nothing is not empty inside; they are a ledger themselves, every grievance entered fair and neat, compounding interest for years.

So when Lior looked up at me across the lamp and said, "No," I genuinely didn't understand at first.

"No?"

"I mean no, you can't peel off. Or—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes, and for a moment looked about twelve years old, and exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "I mean I don't know. Damn it, Sefa. Do you know what happens if you leave the route? If you go north after him?"

"Nothing happens. The packet still goes to the magistrate—"

"*We* go to the magistrate. You're not a parcel I can post. If you vanish at the Salt Road, Hale looks like a fool, Dagny's contacts burn, and everyone who hid you answers questions with their bones. And if you're taken—" Their voice cracked and they hated it, I watched them hate it. "If you're taken, Grell doesn't need the ledger anymore. He needs *you*. Vane's daughter, alive, in company custody. Do you know what that's worth to him? It's worth the magistrate ignoring every letter in that oilskin packet. Because a witness can be discredited, Sefa, but a blood relation they can parade in front of the circuit—weeping, recanting, *confessing to manipulation by foreign agents*, whatever script Grell writes—that kills the evidence better than fire ever could."

I hadn't thought of that. I want to be honest, since honesty was supposedly the business of the evening: I had thought about my father's face, and about Corrin's Reach, and about the fish-factor's back room where the courier line ran, and I had not once thought about what my capture would do to the case. To the others. To Lior.

"You'd be handing him the whole war," Lior said, quieter now. "Your father wrote those letters knowing you might die bringing them. Read the last page again. He doesn't ask you to come find him. He tells you not to."

"He tells me not to because he thinks I'll be safer unknown. But I'm known now, aren't I? The hunters know my face. The tollhouse keeper knows my face. The safety he arranged for stopped existing the moment I washed under these docks."

"Yes," Lior said. "And that's the cruelest thing about it. His protection failed, and now you want to spend what's left of it on reaching him instead of on finishing what he started. And I understand it. God help me, I understand wanting—" They stopped.

"Wanting what?" I asked, though I thought I knew.

Lior picked the coat back up. Put three more stitches in it, tight and even, buying time the way careful people do. Then:

"My mother dove the oyster beds for the company. She died diving them. I've spent nine years not asking questions I was entitled to ask, because asking questions gets indentured clerks sold overseas or drowned. I have her pearl. That's all. One pearl, in a cloth, in a box under a floorboard, and I have never once held it and thought *I will find where she comes from.* Because finding costs. Finding is a luxury for people whose lives belong to them." They looked up. "Yours belongs to you now, or nearly. Mine doesn't. Not till the contracts burn. So yes—part of this is jealousy, if you want the whole confession on the table. You have a father alive to reach, and I'd trade every tooth in my head for that. But I wouldn't trade the mission for it. Because the mission is the thing that frees everyone standing in this harbor, including me, including *you*—and your father, if it lands. Don't you see? Getting the evidence to the circuit *is* reaching him. It's the only road to him that doesn't end with both of you destroyed."

* * *

We sat with that a while. The tide turned below the loft; you could hear the change, the way the water's voice dropped half a tone, like a crowd settling.

I was angry, is the honest record. Angry at Lior for being right in that bloodless, itemized way, angry at my father for writing *stay hidden* instead of *come to me*, angry at myself for four days of small dishonesties that suddenly looked enormous. I had let everyone believe my stake was the same as theirs. It wasn't. It had never been.

"Do you know what I carried the night I shipped out of Corrin's Reach?" I said finally. "Everyone assumes it's the letters. The letters are almost beside the point—he wrote copies, he hid copies, paper is cheap. What I carry that matters is this." I pulled the cord at my throat. Two things hung there always: the courier's token, a lead fish stamped with the fish-factor's mark, and beneath it, wrapped in waxed silk no bigger than a thumbnail, a pearl. Small, gray-rose, imperfect. "His first dive-gift to my mother, before the money, before the company, before any of it. She wore it until the day she went into hiding, and then she gave it to me and said *keep this where the letters can't go.* Because letters can be forged. A pearl like this one—there's not another in the world with its flaws. When he sees it, he'll know I'm alive and free, not a lure, not bait, not some orphan Grell dressed up in my story." My hand had closed around it without my permission; it always did that. "That's what I carry, Lior. Not paper. Proof of *us*. And you're asking me to mail it to a magistrate."

"No," Lior said softly. "I'm asking you to keep it in your fist a few weeks longer. There's a difference."

"The difference being?"

"That after the circuit sits, after the ledger is read and Grell chokes on it—after that, going to him isn't desertion. It's just a daughter traveling north. Nobody hunting. Nothing left to steal from you by taking you." They set the finished coat in my lap, stitches neat as a signature. "Right now, going to him is a knife at the throat of everything he sacrificed seven years to build. Afterward, it's the reward. Same road, Sefa. Different timing. Timing is most of what survival is—I learned that from watching the tide."

I wanted to argue. Some stubborn diver's part of me wanted to stand up and declare that I answered to no clerk, that my father was mine and my choice was mine, and hang the consequences.

But I kept seeing it the way Lior had drawn it: my own face over a courtroom bench, drugged or beaten or coached, recanting while Grell smiled, and my father—somewhere north, alive, hiding—learning of it from a broadsheet. Learning his daughter had been the weapon that killed his life's work. There were fates worse than distance. Distance at least left a person whole.

"Swear something to me," I said at last.

"If I can."

"When this is done—when the circuit sits and the evidence lands—you walk with me as far as Corrin's Reach. Not to meet him. Just to the courier door. You carry me there the way you carried me out of the water. And then you come home to whatever home turns out to be."

Lior considered it the way they considered everything: seriously, as if words were clauses and clauses bound. "Agreed. And swear something back. If it goes wrong—if we're taken, if it falls apart—you give the pearl to the water. Not to Grell's men. To the sea. Promise me he never learns of you through anything but good news."

I laughed, and it hurt, and it was the first laugh in four days that wasn't a performance. "You want me to throw my father's pearl into the harbor."

"I want Grell holding nothing but salt water. Yes."

"Then sworn." I tucked it back under my collar, where it lay against my heart like a small cold moon. "Though I warn you, clerk, I dive well. It would take Grell's men a long time to find anything I've hidden below the waterline."

Something crossed Lior's face then—not a smile, exactly. More like a door opening a crack onto a room full of light they weren't sure they were allowed to enter. "My mother used to say that," they said. "Almost word for word."

And that was when we both heard the knock—not on the Pearlhouse door, but on the loft's street shutter, three slow raps and two fast, Dagny's signal and then one rap too many.

Lior was on their feet with the lamp snuffed before I'd finished standing. In the dark, their hand found my arm, steady as a cleat.

"That's the extra rap," they whispered. "That means trouble."

Outside, boots on the cobbles. Many boots, moving in the unhurried way of men paid by the night rather than the hour.
