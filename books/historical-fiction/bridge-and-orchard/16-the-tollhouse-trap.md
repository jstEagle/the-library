## 16. The Tollhouse Trap

The Salt Road climbed out of Vessing like a rope thrown to a drowning country, and we climbed it in the gray hour when the tide goes out and takes everyone's courage with it.

Sefa walked three paces ahead, hood up, hands buried in her sleeves. We had argued about that—her walking ahead, apart, as if distance could make her someone else's problem. I'd lost the argument the way I lost most arguments: by being right and having no authority whatsoever.

"The keeper's name is Brast," she said, not turning. "Old soldier. One leg from a pike at Karren's Ford, so he'll be seated. Seated men don't chase you; they watch you."

"And paid men?" I asked.

"Paid men count twice."

That was the whole of our intelligence on the second checkpoint, gathered from Dagny before the Pearlhouse burned and from smugglers since, and it amounted to: an old man, a stool, a ledger of faces, and coin from the Halvard Trading Company to fill it. They weren't watching for me. Nobody had ever watched for me; invisibility was the one skill my indenture had taught me well. They were watching for a woman with sea-colored eyes and a courier's walk, carrying papers worth more than the tollhouse itself.

Which is why Sefa carried nothing, and I carried everything, and the everything was sewn into the lining of a fishmonger's coat that smelled like it had already died once and was working on a second time.

"You could smile," Sefa said. "You're going to a tollhouse, not a funeral."

"I've been to funerals with better company." I shifted the coat's weight on my shoulders. "Besides, smiling is for people whose faces aren't on file."

"Your face isn't on file anywhere, clerk. That's your one talent."

It stung because it was true, and because nine years ago I would have taken it as a compliment. A diver's child survives by being unremarkable—I had built a life on that stone, laid it brick by brick, and now here I was hauling it all toward a tollhouse to be spent in a single morning.

The road bent inland through gorse and wind-flattened grass. Below us, Vessing lay gray and small, its harbor a dull coin of water. Somewhere down there, Hale was supposed to be finding his own way to the capital road, separately, drawing no attention. That had been the plan. Plans, I have since learned, are what you make so the world has something to improve upon.

* * *

The tollhouse sat where the road narrowed between two stone markers, a squat building with a chain across the lane and a brazier smoking beside it. The keeper was exactly as advertised: broad, gray, settled onto a stool with his bad leg propped on a crate, a mug of something steaming in his fist. He looked at us the way old soldiers look at everything—the way you look at weather, cataloguing it without caring about it.

Then he looked a second time. At Sefa.

I felt the change the way you feel a current shift under calm water. His hand didn't move. His face didn't move. But something behind his eyes stood up and started counting.

"Road tax," he said. "Two coppers each, names in the book."

"We're not travelers," Sefa said easily. "We're bound for the oyster farms past Torrel's field. Day labor."

"Day labor pays a copper a day and you're spending two on a road tax." He smiled without warmth. "Names in the book anyway. Law's the law even for folk who lie badly about oysters."

Sefa gave a name—Marta, of nowhere in particular. The keeper wrote it slowly, enjoying his own slowness. Then he looked at me.

"Lior Fen," I said. It wasn't my name. It also wasn't not my name; I had been collecting aliases the way other children collected shells. "Clerk, out to audit the oyster beds. Company business."

Something flickered. Company business was either a door or a wall, depending on which side of it a man stood, and this man had been paid by the company, which meant it was probably a wall with a trapdoor in it.

"Company business," he repeated. He set down his pen. "Funny thing. I got word last week. Description of a young person, slight, dark-haired, likely traveling with a woman. Foreign-looking. Says they might claim company business to get through." He picked up his mug, drank, watched us over the rim. "Description came with money in it. Enough money that a one-legged man thinks about retiring warm."

The brazier popped. Somewhere behind the tollhouse a goat complained about existence.

"You understand my difficulty," Brast went on, conversational, terrible. "Because if I let you pass and you're who the letter says, I've lost my retirement. And if I hold you and you're not, I've insulted company business, which is worse. So what I need—" he leaned forward on his crate, "—is for one of you to be exactly who the letter describes. For tidiness."

Sefa's hand drifted toward her sleeve, where there was nothing but her own fingers and a knife. I did the fastest arithmetic of my life and found no solution in any column. Run? He had eyes and a horn by the brazier; I could see the horn now that I knew to look. Fight? Against a pike veteran with a knife in a fish coat. Bribe? With what—my mother's pearl had gone to the smugglers weeks ago, along with every coin I'd ever skimmed from manifest margins.

So I did the only thing left. I kept talking, because talking is what clerks do while they wait for the numbers to change.

"Read me the description," I said.

Brast blinked. "What?"

"The letter. Read me the description. Exactly. If you're going to hang someone on it, read it aloud. Company letters are precise. If the description says five foot four and green eyes and I'm five six with brown ones, then you owe me an apology and I'll want it in writing, because I'm a clerk, and clerks collect things in writing."

It was a stupid gambit. It was the kind of thing that works in stories and gets you beaten in ditches. But it cost him something—because the truth was the letter hadn't been precise. Paid informants never are. I watched him weigh whether the woman beside me matched "foreign-looking," and watched him decide she did, and watched him reach for the horn—

And then the current changed.

A horse came up the road at a canter, mud to the knee, ridden by a heavyset man in a company auditor's traveling coat that had seen better decades. Corvin Hale swung down stiffly, hitched the horse to the marker stone, and approached the tollhouse with the air of a man arriving late to a meeting he intended to make everyone else regret.

"Keeper," he said. "Hale. Regional audit, capital office. I'll need your toll records for the quarter, your guest register, and use of your table until evening."

Brast's stool creaked as he straightened. "Regional audit? There's no circuit scheduled—"

"There's always a circuit scheduled. You just haven't been told about it, which is itself an irregularity I'll note." Hale produced a leather folio, stamped with the company seal, and opened it on the tollhouse table with the crisp violence of a man who has spent thirty years making lesser men flinch at paperwork. "Names. Every traveler through since the first of the month. You keep a register?"

"I—" Brast glanced at his ledger of faces. "I record names and descriptions. As required."

"As required, yes. And yet the requirement specifies *daily submission of duplicates* to the factor's office, and I find no duplicates." Hale let the silence do some work. "Which means either you're keeping records you fail to submit, or you're keeping records you submit selectively. Both interest me. Only one of them interests the factor, and only one of those ends with you keeping your post."

I understood then, with a cold sliding feeling, what was happening. Hale was lying—or half-lying, which is the same thing done carefully—and he was doing it on our behalf, in front of a witness, with his own seal showing. The moment Brast reported this morning's events to the factor's office—and he would report, because paid men report—someone was going to compare notes. An auditor who appeared uninvited, asked the wrong perfect questions, and let two suspicious travelers walk through his inspection.

Hale was burning himself alive in front of us, and he was doing it briskly, like a man who found the fire inefficient and had notes on how to improve it.

"Now," Hale said, without looking at us, "these two. Oyster beds at Torrel's field?"

"Claimed so," Brast said carefully.

"Hm. Torrel's contracts lapsed last spring; the beds are worked direct from the harbor co-op now, under license fourteen-C. I have the schedule here." He turned pages he could not possibly have needed to turn. "There's no audit due at Torrel's. Which means you two are misrouted, and the misrouting originated in the harbor office, which means somebody signed a chit they shouldn't have, which means—" he snapped the folio shut, "—I'm going to need to speak with you properly after all. Not here. Up the road, where there's a table that doesn't smell of goat. Keeper, the records. Now."

He said it so that *up the road* sounded like custody, and Brast heard custody, and something in the old soldier's arithmetic quietly resolved. Two travelers under escort by a company auditor: whatever happened next belonged to Hale's paperwork, not to Brast's letter. The cleanest answer to a riddle is to give it to someone else.

"Go," Brast said, jerking his chin at the chain. He was already reaching to lift it, already composing his report, already deciding exactly how much of this morning he would admit to. "Move along. The auditor will deal with you."

"He will," Hale said grimly, gathering his folio. "At length."

We passed the chain. I did not run. Running confirms things. We walked up the Salt Road at the pace of the genuinely doomed, and when the tollhouse dropped behind the rise, Sefa let out a breath she'd been holding since the bridge.

"He bluffed," she said. "With his real seal. On the open road."

"Yes."

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"With Hale it's generally both, itemized." I looked back. At the top of the rise, small against the sky, Hale stood by his horse, watching us go, making no move to follow. He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. More like a man closing a ledger.

Then he turned and rode back down toward the tollhouse, toward the keeper's questions, toward the report that would reach the factor's office by nightfall with his name in it.

* * *

We walked until noon before either of us said what both of us were thinking.

"He gave them the thread," Sefa said finally. "One pull and his whole coat comes apart. They'll check his credentials, his movements, why a suspended auditor from the capital rode the Salt Road the same morning two strangers cleared the toll."

"They'll do worse than check." I kept my eyes on the ruts in the road. "Grell doesn't investigate people like Hale. He uses them until they're empty, then files them. Hale walked into that tollhouse knowing exactly what he was spending."

"His post."

"His post, his pension, his rooms, his name in the company rolls back to the day he signed on." I kicked a stone off the roadbed with more force than the stone deserved. "Everything except his life, which he's leaving as the balance owing."

Sefa was quiet for a while. The gorse hissed in the wind. Ahead, the road ran silver-wet from last night's rain toward hills we couldn't name yet.

"Why?" she said at last. "Men like him don't do that. He audited the child contracts, Lior. He saw the sales and he signed off. Why spend everything now?"

I thought about the counting-house at night, the double ledger locked in the vault, the neat columns where children's names had been converted to cargo. I thought about a man who had looked away once and had apparently been looking ever since, thirty years of looking, waiting for the column of numbers that would let him stop.

"Debt," I said. "He's paying one. Some people do their arithmetic in coin. Hale does his in consequences."

We made the shepherd's track by dusk and took shelter in a bothy that smelled of sheep and old smoke, and I should have slept, because the next days would need all of me. Instead I lay awake doing sums I couldn't finish.

Because Brast would write his report tonight. And in it would be a description of a slight, dark-haired traveler claiming company business, and a foreign woman, and an auditor whose questions fit together too neatly. Grell would read it over his morning wine, and Grell was many things—cruel, patient, careful—but he was never slow. He would look at Hale's name and he would see what I saw on the road: that the auditor had chosen a side in front of a witness, and that witnesses can be bought but choices cannot be unmade.

By tomorrow, Hale wouldn't just be suspected. He'd be watched. Followed. Stripped of whatever shreds of access he still carried, his seal voided, his lodgings searched. Everything he'd used to save us at the tollhouse would be taken from him within the day—and everything he still *knew* would make him dangerous, and dangerous men in Vessing had a way of becoming missing men.

And if they moved on him fast enough, they'd move on the ledgers too. The originals, the true books, the ones locked in the Company House vault—Grell would never leave evidence sitting in a port where an auditor had just gone crooked. He'd crate them for the capital before the week was out, before the magistrate's circuit could ever sit in Vessing, and every name in those columns would sail away beyond anyone's reach.

I sat up in the dark, and Sefa's voice came out of the blackness, dry as driftwood.

"You've thought of it too."

"The ledgers," I said. "They'll move them. Soon. Maybe days."

"Then we're out of time."

Out on the headland, the sea worked at the rocks in the dark, patient as a clerk copying the same line forever. Somewhere down that dark coast, a man who had sold his silence thirty years ago had just spent his last quiet night buying it back—and had no idea yet how little time the company would give him to enjoy the purchase.

We had to warn him. We had to get to the ledgers before the crates did. And somewhere between those two necessities, I knew, one of us was going to run out of road.

It was just beginning to rain when I heard hooves on the track below the bothy—too fast for a farmer, too careful for a patrol—and Sefa's hand closed on my arm in the dark.
