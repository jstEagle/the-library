## 17. Burned

They took my office on a Tuesday, which I thought was needlessly theatrical of them. Companies that forge ledgers and sell children prefer to do their cruelties on Fridays—let the weekend soften the memory. Tuesday meant someone in the counting-house wanted me to sit with it a full three days before the harbor's rhythm gave them anything else to think about.

Factor Grell did not deliver the news himself. He sent a boy with a printed notice and instructions to wait while I read it, which was its own kind of message: *we know you'll want an audience for your humiliation, so here is a child to witness it.*

The notice was brief, as these things are. Corvin Hale, formerly auditor of the Vessing branch of the Halvard Trading Company, was relieved of his position pending "irregularities of conduct unbecoming an officer of the firm." No mention of what the irregularities were. No mention of the tollhouse on the Salt Road, or the two travelers who had passed through it under my seal on the worst-bluffed evening of my life.

They didn't know about the tollhouse. Not specifically. If they had known *specifically*, I would not be writing this. I'd be in the company jail beside Dagny Skarre, or floating in the tidal basin with my pockets full of ballast, which is how this company handles men who become inconvenient. I have audited enough of their accidents to know the arithmetic of them.

What they knew was vaguer and therefore worse: that their disgraced auditor's loyalties had developed a lean. Grell's notice was a warning shot across a bow he couldn't yet identify. Strip the man, watch the man, and wait to see what he does when he has nothing left to lose. It is a sound strategy. I would have recommended it myself, back when anyone asked my recommendations.

So: no office. No ledger access. No credentials, though I still had the physical credential token in my coat—the brass tally stamped with my auditor's mark, the one Lior Nystrom would need if the third checkpoint was going to be anything other than a slaughter. I was supposed to surrender it with the office. I told the boy I'd misplaced it. The boy wrote down "misplaced" in his own little ledger, and I remember thinking that the company had finally found a use for bookkeeping I could respect.

* * *

The first day of my unemployment, I did what any sensible ruined man does: I sat in my rented room above the ropewalk and drank half a bottle of plum brandy that I could no longer afford, and I took stock.

Here is the inventory. One room. One coat with a stolen brass token sewn into the lining—I had done the sewing myself at midnight, badly, with the kind of stitches that would embarrass Dagny Skarre into silence. One working knowledge of every fraudulent practice the Halvard Trading Company has employed in the nine years since I stopped looking at the numbers honestly. And one debt, thirty years old and compounding, that I had been trying to pay off ever since a young auditor named Corvin Hale looked at a column of children's contract sales, understood exactly what he was seeing, and signed his name beneath it anyway.

I want to be precise about that moment, because everyone in this story will eventually ask themselves the same question I did: *what would you have done?*

What I did was tell myself the contracts were probably kinder than they looked on paper. What I did was remind myself I had a mother with a bad hip and no one else to keep her. What I did was initial the page, collect a commendation, and go home and drink until the arithmetic stopped moving. And then I did it again the next year, and the year after that, until the practice of not-seeing became so smooth I genuinely believed I was still an honest man who happened to work among lies, rather than a liar with excellent penmanship.

Nine years ago Aldric Vane looked at those same numbers and chose differently. They drowned him for it—or said they did—and I helped certify the death record. My signature is on that too. I have counted.

That is the thing about being an auditor that no one tells you: you cannot stop counting. Every kindness omitted, every page initialed, every year of silence—it all sits in columns whether you look at them or not. The totals accrue interest.

* * *

On the second day, they started watching me properly, and I confess I almost enjoyed it.

There were two of them, rotating out of three shifts, which told me Grell considered me a moderate threat rather than an urgent one. The first was a broad, patient man called Rennick whom I remembered from the enforcement floor—he once carried a Pearlhouse woman out of her own doorway by the collar, and I had signed the expense report for the door repairs. The second was younger, nervous, kept checking a folded paper of instructions like a cook consulting a recipe. Between them, they made a comedy of subtlety: Rennick took up a post at the Salt Lamb tavern across from the ropewalk and proceeded to drink ale at a rate that suggested the company was either paying him by the pint or testing his kidneys. The young one walked a circuit past my window every twenty minutes, regular as tide-tables.

I waved at him once. He did not wave back, but he blushed, and I filed that away. Nervous men blush; loyal men don't waste the blood. A man who blushes at surveillance can be turned, given cause. I noted him down in the ledger I now kept in my head, because I had lost the right to keep one anywhere else.

Being watched changes how you move. I could no longer walk to the counting-house district and linger, could not visit the records annex where the original manifests were kept, could not so much as buy ink without someone noting it. Every errand was a sentence in a report that went to Grell each evening. I spent that second day performing the decline of a ruined man for an audience of two: I bought fish I didn't eat, sat on the seawall for two hours staring at nothing, and visited the Salt Lamb long enough for Rennick to relax visibly over his ninth pint.

But ruin buys a strange clarity. With no position to protect, I found my mind running freely along lines it had been forbidden for years—and the line it kept returning to was this:

The magistrate's spring circuit would sit in Vessing within the month. Everyone in the company's upper floors knew it; the circuit's schedule arrived by packet boat and was posted in the market square. A magistrate sitting in your town means questions asked under oath. And Grell was many things—cruel, vain, clever in the narrow way of men who have never been contradicted—but he was not stupid. He knew what a real audit would find, because he had built the fraud it would find.

Which meant the original ledgers—the true ones, the ones with the child-contract sales recorded in the honest hand of whoever had done that grim bookkeeping—could not be in Vessing when the circuit arrived. They would be moved to the capital, into the company's central archive, where magistrates needed ministerial writs to touch a single page and where pages had been known to vanish between writs. I had watched documents disappear into that archive for fifteen years. It is not a library. It is a mouth.

I did not know the date of the transfer. But I knew the mechanics, because transfers were once my responsibility to audit: such shipments go out under heavy guard on the fastest vessel available, with papers describing them as something dull. Specie, usually. Or surveying instruments. And they go out quietly, at night, on a tide that doesn't advertise itself.

If the ledger left Vessing before the circuit sat, everything—the letters, Sefa's testimony, whatever Lior Nystrom was building toward with my stolen credentials and their improbably steady hands—would collapse into theater. The court would hear accusations against paper that no longer existed. Grell would express sorrow. The archive would swallow the truth whole, and I would spend my remaining years knowing I had watched it happen through a tavern window while drinking ale bought with company coin.

* * *

The third day, I began planning how to warn them, which is when I discovered the true purpose of surveillance: not to catch you doing something, but to make doing anything so complicated that you decide against it.

I could hardly stroll to the Pearlhouse. The house had burned four nights ago—Grell's answer to a question nobody had asked him aloud—and the fire had drawn the watchers' attention to that quarter of the harbor like flies to a wound. Anyone approaching the ruins, or the smugglers' cellars that honeycombed that shore, would be noted thrice over. Dagny was in the company jail. Mama Osk had gone to ground somewhere with the younger children. Lior Nystrom and Sefa were ghosts, and finding a ghost while two men catalogued your boots for posterity was a problem worthy of a better mathematician than me.

And even if I reached them—what then? A conversation under observation, a note passed, a whisper in a fish market? Grell's people would see the contact itself. The warning would cost them their safety and me my neck, and possibly accomplish nothing but confirming to Grell that his disgraced auditor and his missing clerk were in league—which, to be fair, we were.

No. It had to be paper. Paper could be dropped, planted, disguised, denied. Paper was my native tongue. And so I sat in my room above the ropewalk with the brandy untouched—day three, and I was already a better man than day one—and composed a letter in the only code I trusted: accounting.

Anyone can crack a cipher given time and reason to try. But an ordinary business letter, filled with figures and trade jargon, reads as noise to an enforcer and as music to a clerk. I wrote to a fictitious creditor about a fictitious dispute over shipment valuations, and I buried the substance in the numbers, using conventions that any Halvard-trained bookkeeper would parse without effort. The convention for *reversal* was a figure entered in red parentheses. The convention for *transfer imminent* was a dated bill of lading reference. The convention for *night tide*—well, that one I invented, but I invented it in the shape of a hundred real entries Lior Nystrom had copied a thousand times: a manifest notation for oysters, entered at an hour oysters are never shipped. A clerk who notices a column that doesn't reconcile—and I had watched this one notice exactly that—notices an oyster shipment logged at midnight.

The substance, once decoded, read simply:

*Original ledgers depart for capital archive by sea, night tide, within the fortnight. Guarded as specie. After departure, nothing in Vessing will matter. Move before the ship moves.*

I could not say which night. That was the flaw in the warning, and flaws in warnings get people killed. But a deadline shaped like *soon* is better than a surprise.

Delivery was the remaining problem, and I solved it the way all my best work has been solved: by exploiting someone else's routine.

The young watcher—the blusher—walked his circuit past my window every twenty minutes, and every fourth circuit he paused at the chandlery on the corner, where the chandler's daughter kept a stall of secondhand books and paper. I had observed this pause four times before I understood it, and twice more before I confirmed it: he was courting her, clumsily, under cover of duty. Surveillance as courtship. The company pays these boys nothing, and love finds a way, as it has always done, to make idiots of security arrangements.

The chandler's daughter also sold her paper to clerks. Including, twice in the past week, a slight figure with diver's shoulders and ink-stained fingers who bought quills and paid in small coin and otherwise resembled a ghost—because Lior Nystrom, whatever fire they were hiding from, still needed to write, and still needed paper, and the corner chandlery was neutral ground that no watcher would think to watch, being watched already by an idiot in company livery.

So on the morning of the fourth day, I wrote my false creditor's letter, sealed it plainly, addressed it to a name that meant nothing, and left it—through a small bribe I could ill afford—with the chandler's daughter, to be tucked into the paper packet of the next ink-buying clerk who matched a description I gave with care: *young, quiet, salt-scarred hands, looks at everything and is looked at by no one.*

Then I went out and sat on the seawall in plain view of both my watchers, and let them record a ruined man feeding gulls, while inside my coat the brass credential token pressed against my ribs like a second heart, and inside the chandlery a letter waited to travel farther than I could.

It was, I reflected, the most honest act of accounting I had performed in thirty years: a debit taken against my own remaining balance, entered at last in the correct column.

I only hoped the clerk would read it in time. And that the reader would forgive the sender for the thirty years it took him to send anything at all.
