## 18. Pearlhouse in Ashes

The fire came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Disasters ought to have the decency to arrive on significant days—storm feasts, market days, the anniversary of your indenture signing. Tuesdays are for mending nets and arguing about herring prices. Instead, at roughly the hour when Mama Osk would have been ladling out the barley and Dagny would have been telling the children that no, the tide would not wait for their chores, someone put a lamp to the Pearlhouse and let it eat.

I wasn't there. That's the part I keep turning over, the way you turn a stone in your pocket until it goes smooth. I was three streets away, sitting in the back of the sail-loft where we'd moved Sefa two nights earlier, copying out Vane's letters in my own hand so that if the originals were taken, the words would survive somewhere. It was careful work. It was important work. And it meant that when the sky over the harbor turned the color of a bruise going rotten, I was holding a quill instead of a bucket.

I smelled it before I understood it. Smoke on the wind has a grammar to it, and anyone raised on the docks knows the difference between a chimney's polite cough and a building screaming. This was the second kind. I was out the loft door before my ink had time to dry.

* * *

You hear people describe fires afterward and they always talk about the flames, as if fire were the interesting part. Fire is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what people do. I ran down Harrow Lane and the whole street was already out—net-menders in their nightclothes, the fishwives with their hair loose, old Piet standing in his doorway in his union shirt with an axe he clearly had no plan for—and every one of them was looking up at the Pearlhouse like a congregation watching something they were not permitted to interrupt.

The roof was gone first. Roofs always go first; they're just timber and tar, all appetite and no memory. But the walls held for a while, and through the black-rimmed windows I could see the interior of everything I knew—the great room where we'd voted to shelter Sefa, the shelf by the door where the children kept their found things (gull skulls, a brass button, half a china doll), the narrow stair where I had once hidden for two hours from a company tally-man while Mama Osk told him, with magnificent boredom, that she hadn't seen a Nystrom child in weeks.

That was nine years ago. He'd believed her because Osk does not lie; she simply arranges the truth like a fishmonger arranges a display, best face outward.

Now the best face of the Pearlhouse was on fire.

"Dagny." Someone said her name near me, or I said it, or the night said it. Then I saw her.

She was on the steps of the burning house—not inside it, thank every salt god, not inside—but she was being held there, arms pinned by two men wearing company gray with the sleeves rolled, and a third man stood in front of her holding up a coil of slow-match like a preacher with a relic. I didn't know his face. Grell's men don't wear badges; they wear competence, which is worse, because competence can be hired anywhere.

"—found these in her loft," the man with the match was saying, in the flat reciting voice of someone who has said the sentence several times already and expects the crowd to be bored of it too. "Wicking material. Lamp oil residue on her tools. A woman with debts and grudges against the company, alone in a house full of children—" he paused, and I will give him this, he had a talent for the pause, "—well. You can draw your own conclusions."

Dagny was shouting. Not pleading—shouting. From thirty feet I couldn't make out the words but I knew their shape, because I'd heard her use them on deckhands who fouled her lines: short, structural, load-bearing profanity. Her hands were bound in front of her with her own net-needle still tucked in her belt, which even now strikes me as the single most Dagny detail of the entire catastrophe. They arrested her with her own needle in her belt and she hadn't bitten anyone yet. Restraint of the highest order.

The crowd did nothing. I want to be fair to the crowd, since I was part of it and doing nothing is easier to describe than to forgive. There were four company men and perhaps sixty of us, and the arithmetic of the harbor is old arithmetic: sixty people who owe rent to the company do not start a riot over one woman, however much they love her. The men in gray knew that sum better than we did. It's why they'd chosen Tuesday.

They marched her past us toward the Company House. As she passed me—I was at the edge, invisible, invisible, the oldest trick I own—she turned her head and looked directly at me, and her mouth kept its shouting shape for exactly one word longer than the shouting required.

*Barrel.*

Then she grinned, this wild white grin under the light of her own home burning, and was gone between the gray shoulders.

She meant the fish barrel. She meant: *the packet is safe, you idiot, stop staring, move.*

I moved.

* * *

Here is what I did in the following hour, and I record it without pride because there wasn't any in it, only motion: I went to the barrel line behind the smokehouse where we'd cached the oilskin packet inside a keg of brine-cured herring, and I confirmed the seal was unbroken, and then I took it out entirely and strapped it against my ribs beneath my coat, because a hiding place known to a prisoner in custody is not a hiding place, it is a countdown. Then I went back to the fire.

By then the Pearlhouse was mostly light and noise. The walls had begun to go, and the heat pushed the crowd back another ten feet, and everyone stood in that ring of upturned faces that fire demands. Mama Osk was there. I found her because I heard her before I saw her—she was counting children.

"Fourteen," she was saying, walking the line of small damp bodies wrapped in borrowed blankets, tapping each head like she was tallying crates. "Fifteen. Sixteen. Brekka, where's Toma—Toma. Seventeen. All of them, all of them out, all—" and then her voice did something I had never heard it do in nine years of knowing her. It cracked. Just once, just a seam opening, and then closed again like a practiced hand smoothing a ledger page. "All accounted for."

"Mama Osk." I came beside her. Up close I could see her hands were burned—not badly, the palms pink and blistered across the heel, the burns of someone who had reached into a doorway anyway. "Who got them out?"

"The eldest girls. They'd gone to the loft for the winter quilts because the night turned cold, if you can believe it, cold, in all that—" She stopped. She looked at the house. "I was at the bathhouse. I left at moonrise. By the time I got back the stairwell was gone."

"You weren't here."

"None of us were meant to be here," she said, and there was the terrible clarity in it, arriving in her voice the way bad news arrives by boat. "It went up fast, Lior. Too fast for a lamp knocked over. Tar and straw don't catch like that unless they're helped, and they were helped by someone who knew the house was full at supper and empty at moonrise. Someone watched us long enough to know our hours." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes in the firelight were very calm, which from Mama Osk is the loudest alarm there is. "Which means they'll watch the ashes next, to see who grieves and how loudly. So we will grieve quietly, do you understand me? Quietly."

I understood her. I also could not obey her, quite, because standing in that ring of light I finally let myself feel the specific weight of what was gone. Not the building—buildings are honest about being replaceable—but the contents. The table scarred by twenty years of knives and homework. Osk's recipe box. The children's shelf of found things, the gull skull and the brass button and the half a china doll, melted now into a single sad artifact of nothing. Nine years I had walked into that house and been nobody, gratefully nobody, and the house had held my nobody-ness the way water holds a hull, and now it was making that sound—that wet, collapsing sound buildings make when they give up—and I understood that I had been angry for a long time without having anywhere to put it.

Now the anger had an address. It was written in charcoal across the harbor sky.

"They took Dagny," I said.

"I saw."

"It's a scapegoat. They'll try her for arson and hang her and call the matter concluded, and the magistrate will get a tidy report and no letters."

"Yes," said Mama Osk. "So." She looked at the crowd, at the fishwives and net-menders and the dockmen who had stood in silence while Dagny was walked away, and something passed over her face like weather. When she spoke again her voice carried just far enough and no farther, the voice of a woman who had spent forty years making announcements over tide-noise and knew its exact range. "The Pearlhouse has lost its roof. Those with beds, take a child. Those with floors, take two. We scatter tonight and we gather tomorrow at the smoke-house yard at dusk, and anyone who has ever owed that woman a mended net will know what to bring."

Nobody cheered. Cheer would have been wrong. But I watched the faces around that ring of firelight change, all of them, the same shift happening sixty times at once—the shift from *witnesses* to *something else*—and I remember thinking, with a clarity that frightened me: Grell has made a serious error. He thinks he burned a building. He has actually issued a summons.

* * *

We scattered, as ordered. The children went out in twos and threes into lofts and cellars and the warm fish-smelling kitchens of women who had known some of them since birth. I took two—Brekka and little Toma, who fell asleep against my shoulder on the stairs of the sail-loft before I'd even set him down, the way children do, carrying their terror only while awake and setting it down like a pack at bedtime. Sefa sat with me on the floor afterward. The loft owner, a widow named Herrit who owed Dagny more mended nets than God, asked no questions, which is its own kind of courage.

"She'll be in the company jail," Sefa said quietly. "Not the magistrate's lockup. They'll keep her where Grell can talk to her."

"Grell doesn't talk. He waits."

"Then he'll wait until circuit season and hang her for the fire, and every witness will swear to whatever they were paid to swear, and it will be clean." Sefa's jaw was set. In the lamplight she looked older than her years, and hard, and I remembered she had crossed half the sea carrying a dead man's letters sewn into her coat, so hardness was not new to her. "Lior. They did this because of us. Because of what we're carrying."

"I know."

"No—you know it the way you know arithmetic. I need you to *know* it." She took my wrist. Her hand was shaking slightly, and I noticed it because she was letting me notice it, which for Sefa was nearly a confession. "A woman is in a cell because I swam into your harbor. If I walk to the magistrates' office tomorrow and give myself up—"

"—then they take the letters, burn them, hang her anyway, hang you, and find us within the week, and the children lose the Pearlhouse twice." I said it flatly, because I had run the sums already, in the dark, while Toma slept on my shoulder. "You asked me once why I stayed invisible for nine years. Because survival is arithmetic, Sefa. Well. Now the arithmetic says we finish this. Fast. Before her trial, before they move anything, before Grell gets his tidy report."

Sefa studied me for a long moment. "Listen to you."

"Don't," I said, and meant it, because if she pointed at what I was becoming I might look at it directly and be terrified, and terror was a luxury the schedule could not afford.

* * *

The smoke-house yard at dusk the next day held more people than the Pearlhouse ever had. That's the sentence I want entered into the record, because it surprised me and it should surprise you. Forty-some souls, quiet, arranged loosely around the big curing sheds—fishwives, divers, two of the pilots off the bar-boats, old Piet with his axe replaced by a coil of rope, which showed growth. Word travels fast in a port when the message is simple enough: *they burned the house and took Dagny.* Ports keep accounts too.

Mama Osk stood on a crate and did not raise her voice, because she never needed to.

"You all saw what happened," she said. "I won't repeat the company's story, because it's a lie and lies don't improve with retelling. What I'll say is this. The Pearlhouse isn't a building. Never was. A building is where a family keeps its things. The family is what stands in this yard." She let that sit. Somewhere out on the flats a gull complained, absurdly, like a heckler. "Now. Some of you are thinking: this is not my fight, I have rents, I have licenses, I have children. All true. So here is what the fight costs, and you may pay or not pay, and no one in this yard will think less of you." She held up one burned hand, palm out, so they could see the blisters. "Tomorrow and every day after, someone walks past the Company House and notes who goes in and out, and what carts leave, and at what hour, and brings it to me. No violence. No names spoken aloud in the street. Only eyes and memory. The company has taken a woman who mended half this harbor's nets for free, and it has told us, in tar and straw, exactly how much our safety is worth to it. I suggest we begin keeping our own ledgers."

And the yard—quietly, without a cheer, without a fist raised, which is the Vessing way—agreed. Hands went up around the circle, one after another, unhurried, like divers signaling from below. Piet nodded. The fishwives nodded. A boy of maybe twelve, one of Dagny's orphan crew, raised his hand so high and so solemn that something in my chest tore along an old seam.

I stood at the edge of the yard with the packet against my ribs and understood what we had become. Fugitives run. We had stopped running somewhere between the fire and this crate, and what remained was slower and colder and much, much worse news for Factor Grell: a network. Small, poor, furious, and patient as tide.

Afterward, Osk drew me aside behind the curing shed. Up close she looked hollowed, the way a hull looks after a storm—still sound, but visibly worked upon.

"You have the letters safe?"

"Closer than safe. On my body."

"Good." She hesitated, and Mama Osk hesitating is like the sea pausing mid-wave; it means the wave coming next is large. "There's more, child, and I'll say it plain because plain is all I've got. The man who took Dagny—the one with the slow-match—he wore no badge, but I knew his voice. He was one of the pair who searched our house last month and turned over every barrel." She looked at me steadily. "He described the barrels to his men. He counted them. Lior—if they counted the barrels during the search, and they count them again when they inventory the ashes—"

"—they'll know one keg of cured herring walked out of a burned yard," I finished. My stomach dropped through the planks of the world. "They'll know the evidence was here. Under their noses. In the fire."

"And they'll ask whose errand removed it," said Osk, "and they will come asking, and asking is the gentle version." She put her good hand on my shoulder, briefly, hard. "You have until their next inventory. Days, maybe. Whatever you and that girl mean to do with those papers—do it before they count."

Behind us, out in the yard, the boy with the solemn raised hand was teaching the smaller children to memorize cart schedules like a skipping rhyme. Above the harbor, where the Pearlhouse had stood, smoke still rose thin and straight from the ruins, a single gray thread stitching the charred ground to the sky.

Days, then. I pressed my hand flat against my ribs, against the oilskin, against the handwriting of a dead man, and started walking toward the one person in Vessing who knew exactly when the company counted its barrels—and who had, as of yesterday, every reason in the world to hate them as much as I did.

It was time to visit Corvin Hale.
