## 19. Bargaining with Smugglers

There is a particular arithmetic to owning almost nothing. I had done the sums many times over nine years of indenture, because counting was my trade and despair, like everything else in Vessing, eventually becomes a ledger.

My possessions, in full: one company coat with someone else's number stitched at the collar (mine, technically, but it read like an accusation). One diver's knife, inherited. One hair comb missing three teeth. And a pearl — small as a winter moon, grey-white, slightly uneven, worth less than a fine one and more than everything else I'd ever touched.

My mother's pearl. The last thing her hands gave to mine before the sea took her back for its own records.

I had sworn at twelve years old that I would die before parting with it, which shows you how much a twelve-year-old knows about dying. At nineteen, standing in the reeking dark behind Brann's Salt House with Sefa's wound going hot under its bandage and the Pearlhouse still smoking on the headland like a snuffed candle, I discovered that swearing is cheap and pearls have a price, and only one of those things buys passage out of a hunted harbor.

* * *

The smugglers worked out of the salt cellar beneath Brann's, down a stair so narrow I had to turn my shoulders sideways, into air that tasted of brine and tallow and men who had stopped caring what they smelled like sometime around their second shipwreck. Dagny's letter of introduction — scratched on the back of a net-mending pattern, folded twice — got me past the boy at the door. The letter said, in effect: *the bearer is desperate and honest about it, which is rarer than honesty alone.*

Three men sat around a barrel that had been repurposed as a table. Cards, coins, a jug. The man in the middle was old in the way driftwood is old — silvered, split, still stubbornly afloat. His hands told the whole story before he opened his mouth: rope scars across both palms, fingers gone crooked from a line that had taken them where it wanted to go. They called him Skarn, which is not so much a name as a diagnosis.

"You're the clerk," he said. Not a question. "Dagny's letter says you want two things I can sell: quiet water for your sick friend, and a boat when the tide's right." He turned a card over, looked at it with contempt, discarded it. "And I know what you've got to trade, or you wouldn't be here. Nobody comes to me with empty pockets unless they're stupid, and Dagny doesn't waste letters on stupid."

"I haven't said what I'm trading."

"You're wearing a coat with a company number on it and shoes patched with sailcloth. Whatever's in your pockets, it isn't coin." He finally looked up. His eyes were the pale washed-out blue of very deep water. "So it's the pearl. Every diver's whelp in this port has one tucked away like a heartbeat they keep outside their chest. Don't look surprised. Your mother had hers too."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. "You knew my mother?"

"Everyone who worked the beds twenty years ago knew Maren Nystrom. Best breath in the fleet. Could stay down long enough to make the oysters nervous." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "Sit down, clerk. Let's do this properly, since you came all this way down my stairs."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, how the bargaining went, because later — after everything — people asked me if it hurt, and I always said no, and that was true by then, but it hadn't been true that night.

Skarn named a number first, because smugglers are not sentimental. Passage for two — Sefa and myself — on a boat leaving within the week, plus hiding in the salt cellars until then, plus food, plus a name kept out of certain mouths. It was a fair price for a person with nothing. It would have bought my mother's pearl and left him change.

"Too much," I said, because apparently I had learned something in nine years of copying other people's numbers: never accept the first figure, even when your heart is already out of your chest and on the table. "It's a small pearl. Grey. Off-round."

"Off-round pearls hold light better than round ones. Ask any jeweler." He poured something from the jug into a tin cup and pushed it toward me. I didn't drink it. Drinking things in cellars is how stories end early. "And passage is dear right now, clerk. The company's watching every hull that leaves this harbor. My boys are risking forfeiture, maybe worse, moving anyone with a story attached. Your friend has a very large story attached. You've seen to that yourself, I hear."

"We've seen to it together," I said. "Which means the company's watching you whether you carry us or not. If they find her in this town and shake her hard enough, she'll tell them whose cellar she slept in. I'd think a man in your trade prefers his guests unfindable."

The card player to Skarn's left — young, with a burn scar down one cheek — laughed once, sharply, and then pretended he hadn't.

Skarn studied me for a long moment. There was a quality to the study I recognized, because auditors did it too, and factors, and every adult who ever looked at an indentured child and tried to work out what we were worth. But there was something else underneath it, and I couldn't place it until he spoke again.

"Maren's whelp," he said, half to himself. "She sat where you're sitting once. Different cellar, same smell. She traded too."

"My mother wasn't a smuggler."

"No. She was a diver with a sick friend and no coin, same as tonight. Traded her knife for medicine." He nodded at my belt, at the worn handle of the blade that had been in my family longer than the company had been in Vessing. "Got it back later. Borrowed it, she said. Never returned it properly." A pause, and then, dry as old salt: "You could say I owe the Nystroms a knife's worth of goodwill. Consider tonight's discount the interest."

I don't know what my face did. I had trained it for nine years to do nothing, and it obeyed, mostly, but something must have moved behind it, because Skarn's expression softened by perhaps a degree — the smallest thaw ever recorded in the harbor — and he dealt the next card face-down instead of up.

"The pearl for everything you asked," he said. "Passage, hiding, silence. One condition."

"Name it."

"When it's done — when your friend is safe and the magistrate's had his say, however it goes — you come back here and tell me how it ended. Not why. I don't need why. Just how." He gathered the cards slowly, arthritic fingers failing twice. "A man in my trade hears a hundred endings a year and almost none of them are true. I'd like one true one, from someone who was in the middle of it."

"That's all? That's the condition?"

"That's all. It costs you nothing but a walk down my stairs and some minutes of your life." He smiled, and it rearranged his whole ruined face into something almost kind. "Though fair warning: the way you people go about things, it may cost you considerably more than that."

* * *

Here is what I did not say in the cellar, because saying it aloud would have made it real, and I needed another hour of unreality to get through the transaction:

I had carried that pearl against my skin since I was seven. Not in a pocket. Pockets get searched; indentured children learn this before they learn their letters. I wore it sewn into my waistband, against the scar on my hip — the diver's mark, the rope-burn scar from the harness, the one I spent nine years keeping hidden under company linen. The pearl rode just above it, warm from my body, a small hard fact of my mother pressing against the small soft fact of everything the sea and the company had done to us between them.

When I finally took it out, in the yellow light of Skarn's lamp, my hands were steady. That surprised me. I had expected them to shake. Instead there was this strange calm, the calm of a clerk balancing a column that has finally, at long last, come out even.

The pearl sat in my palm looking exactly as it always had: imperfect, grey-tinged, catching the lamplight the way off-round pearls do — not a single bright point but a whole soft smear of it, light spread wide, like something refusing to be reduced to one shine.

"It's small," I said again, uselessly.

"It's a Nystrom pearl," Skarn said. "It'll do."

He held out his hand — the crooked one, the rope-scarred one — and I set it in his palm, and there was a moment, just a breath of a moment, where I almost closed my fingers back around it. Nine years of invisibility, nine years of holding still, nine years of telling myself that survival meant keeping everything that mattered hidden and untouched, and here was the whole philosophy of my life sitting in a smuggler's hand, being weighed by a man who knew my mother's name.

"For what it's worth," Skarn said quietly, closing his fist, "she'd have made this trade in a heartbeat. For a stranger, let alone a friend. Divers understand the sea keeps what it keeps. We just borrow things for a while." He turned away, and the transaction was over, the way most important things are over — without ceremony, without thunder, just a fist closing and a life changing weight inside it.

* * *

Sefa was waiting in the loft above the cooperage two streets over, where the smugglers had moved her at dusk. She was propped against grain sacks with her shoulder wound dressed in salt-packed linen, pale as the underside of a fish, and sharpening Vane's letters' oilskin wrapping into strips with more concentration than the task required. When I climbed through the hatch she looked up, and I watched her do the reading — Sefa read people the way I read ledgers, fast and completely — and saw her arrive at the answer before I opened my mouth.

"You traded it," she said. "The pearl."

"There was no other currency in circulation."

"Lior." She said my name — my actual name, which only the Pearlhouse people used now, which still landed strangely every time, like a shoe that fits better than the ones you'd been wearing. "That was your mother's."

"Yes." I sat down on a sack across from her. Somewhere below us a cooper was working late, the tap-tap-tap of hoops driven home, a sound like a patient clock. "And my mother spent her whole life in the water taking risks for other people's profit. I imagine she'd find it fitting that her pearl went the same way. At least this time the buyer is honest about what he's stealing."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. This is accounting. I'm very good at accounting." My voice did something on the last word that I hadn't authorized — cracked, just slightly, along a seam I hadn't known was load-bearing — and Sefa put down the oilskin strips and crossed the space between us, wound and all, and did the thing that Mama Osk used to do, which was simply to sit beside someone and be solid. No speech. No comfort worth the name. Just weight, and warmth, and presence, offered without invoice.

After a while I said: "He knew her. Skarn. She saved his life once, or he saved hers, or they borrowed a knife from each other in a storm — the details were vague, deliberately, the way old sailors keep them. He discounted the passage." I laughed, or produced the sound that stands in for laughing when your throat is busy with other business. "Do you understand what that means? Even my grief gets a discount in this town. Even my losses have a line item."

"That's not loss," Sefa said. "What you did tonight. Listen to me, because I've thought about this more than you'd credit, lying flat on my back in salt cellars." She took my hand — the one that had held the pearl, the palm still remembering its shape. "A pearl sits in the dark doing nothing forever, or it moves. It buys a boat. It buys a hiding place. It buys your friend enough days to reach a magistrate who can burn down everything that killed her." Her grip tightened, careful of nothing now, careful of everything. "Your mother's pearl is going to help bring down the company that drowned her, and that's not spending, Lior. That's the best investment anyone in this harbor has made in twenty years."

"And when it's over," I said, "he wants to hear how it ended. That's all Skarn wants. One true ending."

"Then we'll give him one." She said it with such simple ferocity that for a moment I believed the whole plan — the vault, the ledger, the circuit, the dawn — was already finished history, already written in some book neither of us had read yet.

Then the hatch scraped.

* * *

It was the burned boy from the cellar, breathless from stairs, and the news came out of him in the wrong order, the way bad news does: "Company riders on the Salt Road — Hale's been stripped, they say, walked out of the counting-house with his coat over his arm — and Skarn says move you tonight, both of you, because—" he gulped air, "—because there's a rumor going round the taverns. Paid rumor, company money. Says a girl matching Sefa's description will try for the magistrate's circuit, and there's gold for whoever brings her in *before* the circuit sits."

Before. Not after. Which meant Grell understood the calendar the same way we did: whatever was going to happen had to happen soon, and everything — the hiding places, the passages, the ten days we thought we had — had just gotten smaller.

Sefa was already on her feet, grey-faced, jaw set, one hand pressed to the salt-packed linen at her shoulder. I stood beside her, lighter by one pearl, heavier by everything else, and realized I was reaching for my knife out of pure inherited habit.

"Tonight, then," I said. "Tell Skarn we're coming down."

Because the pearl was gone, and the boat was real, and somewhere inland a magistrate was rolling toward us at the speed of paperwork — and in Vessing, that night, that was the only currency left that anyone honored.
