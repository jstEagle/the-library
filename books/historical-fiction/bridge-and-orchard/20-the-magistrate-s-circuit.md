## 20. The Magistrate's Circuit

The news came wrapped in a fish delivery, which struck me as fitting. In my experience, everything worth having in Vessing arrived smelling of brine and slightly bruised.

I was mending nets with Dagny's girls in the back room of the smugglers' cellar—they insisted on calling it "the parlor," with the deadpan pride of people who had never owned anything so grand as a chair that matched another chair—when the knock came. Three short, one long. Tam at the door, a boy of maybe fourteen with the solemn face of an altar server and the hands of a fish thief, holding up a crate of herring like an offering.

"From Brannic," he said. "Off the *Kestrel*."

Brannic was Dagny's smuggler contact, the one with the fast boat and the slow smile, and he had been set to watch the harbor office for exactly this kind of word. I put down the net I wasn't really mending and pried open the crate while Tam watched with the particular hunger of a boy who wanted to know things and knew better than to ask.

Under the herring, on a scrap of oilcloth written in charcoal: *Circuit spring route confirmed. Vane—Vessing sits 14th. Ten days. Departs for season after. No return till autumn.*

I read it three times. The words didn't change. Ten days.

"Well," I said, to no one in particular, "that's that, then."

Tam leaned over the crate, sniffed the herring, and said, "Bad catch?"

"The worst," I told him. "Have a fish."

He took two and left. Smart boy. He'd go far, provided nobody hanged him first.

*

Here is what ten days means when you are trying to topple a trading company that has ruled a port for thirty years: it means nothing is enough time. It means every hour you sleep is an hour someone else spends burning evidence, or moving it, or finding you. It means the careful plan you built over weeks—the slow patient architecture of forged papers and borrowed credentials—suddenly has to stand up and run.

Lior took it better than I expected. They read Brannic's note twice, set it down, and said, "Ten days. That's actually generous. I assumed we'd have six."

"You assumed wrong?"

"I assume wrong constantly. It's how I've survived. If you expect disaster, ordinary bad luck feels like a gift." They said it in their flat clerk's voice, the one they used for reading manifests aloud, but their hands were doing arithmetic against their thigh—finger after finger folding down. I'd learned to watch Lior's hands. Their mouth lied all day long, professionally, beautifully. Their hands couldn't.

"We need the vault by the twelfth," they said. "At the latest. The magistrate needs the ledger in hand before they sit, not during—if it lands mid-session, Grell's advocates will bury it in procedure until the circuit leaves. We hand it over the night before, through Hale, to the magistrate's own clerks. That part's arranged?"

"That part's arranged," I said. It was the only part that was, and even it rested on Hale, who currently couldn't walk down his own street without being followed by two men who pretended to be examining the architecture. Vessing's architecture did not deserve the attention. It was mostly rot held upright by habit.

"And the papers?"

"I'm working on them."

"Your seal work is improving," Lior said, which from Lior was roughly a sonnet. "You got the anchor flukes right this time. Last batch looked like a squid having opinions."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and Lior almost smiled—that narrow, startled thing they did, like a person catching sunlight in a jar and not knowing where to put it. Nine years in that counting-house had taught them to keep their face as smooth as a ledger page. I was learning to read the margins anyway.

But the laugh died quick, because the arithmetic didn't care about our feelings. Ten days. The vault. A factor named Grell who had already burned down the Pearlhouse and arrested Dagny, and who would not hesitate at anything gentler than fire if he guessed what we carried. Somewhere out there were my father's letters and a woman the company still believed had drowned conveniently in the harbor, and here I sat in a cellar smelling of herring and lamp oil, forging the seals of men who would see me hanged if they ever learned my face.

My father. Seven years I'd thought he was at the bottom of the sea, and now he was alive and exiled and writing letters in his terrible leftward slant of a hand, and I was going to reach him if it killed me, or more likely if it killed everyone around me first. Some inheritance you don't get to refuse. You just carry it.

*

Mama Osk called the council that evening. Council might be generous—it was whoever could fit into the cellar's back room without knocking over the brandy, which meant nine of us and a cat who refused to acknowledge any authority, including ours.

Osk sat at the head because Osk always sat at the head; age confers a rank that no charter can revoke. Her ribs had been broken in the Pearlhouse beating three weeks ago and she still breathed like each inhale was a negotiation she intended to win. When she spoke, even the cat listened.

"Fourteen days," she said. "Then the circuit goes, and we wait until autumn for justice. By autumn, Grell will have buried the second ledger under six feet of new paperwork and half of us under six feet of harbor mud. So. We do it now, or we don't do it."

"We don't even know if Dagny's out," said Petra, one of the older divers, arms crossed. "Jailbreak's not exactly in our skill set. Our skill set is fish."

"Dagny's working on her own exit," Osk said. "She has a friend inside."

"A turnkey."

"Even jails are made of people, girl. Remember that. Every wall you'll ever face is made of people, and people have sisters."

Petra subsided. I thought about that line for a long time afterward. *Every wall is made of people.* It's the truest thing anyone said to me in Vessing, and Osk delivered it like a complaint about the weather.

Lior laid out the plan the way they laid out everything—flatly, precisely, in the order that made terror feel merely administrative. Hale's stolen credentials. A summons letter in the factor's own style, forged by a clerk whose forgery of official hands had become, in three short weeks, genuinely unsettling to behold. Night entry during the guard rotation Dagny had mapped from her cell. The second ledger, out of the vault and into waxed canvas, and across the harbor to the magistrate's clerks before dawn on the thirteenth.

"It works," Petra said grudgingly, "if nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing has gone wrong yet," Lior said.

Everyone looked at them. Even the cat.

"All right," Lior amended. "One thing went wrong. Several things. But statistically—"

"Lior," Osk said gently, "you're an optimist. It's unbecoming in a professional. Mind it doesn't get you killed."

That got a real laugh out of the room—the first since the Pearlhouse burned. It's a strange alchemy, grief and laughter in the same small space. They don't cancel out. They compound.

*

Afterward, Lior and I stayed behind to wash the cups, because in the Pearlhouse's scattered exile you cleaned if you wanted to feel useful, and everyone wanted to feel useful desperately.

"You should leave," Lior said, not looking up from the washing. "Before the fourteenth. There'll be a boat with Brannic's run on the eleventh. You could be gone before any of it happens. The letters prove enough with your testimony—we don't need you standing in Vessing when it lands."

"No," I said.

"They'll hunt you regardless. You're the courier. You're the loose thread."

"I'm also the only one Grell can't intimidate with paperwork, because I don't exist in any of it. No contract, no record, no number." I handed them a cup to dry. "You spent nine years learning to be invisible. I spent seven learning there's no safety in it worth the price. My father tried disappearing and lost everything including his name. I'm done paying that toll."

Lior dried the cup very thoroughly. "I hate when you make sense. It's disorienting. Like weather changing indoors."

"Also," I said, "when the magistrate sits, I'm going to stand up in that square and say my name. Sefa Vane. Out loud. In front of everyone."

The cup stopped moving.

"You don't have to do that."

"Yes, I do. The letters alone are paper from a stranger. The moment I name myself, they're a dead man's testimony delivered by his living daughter, and every dockworker in that square knows what it costs me to say it. Grell can forge columns. He can't forge me." I took the next cup. "Besides, if I'm going to be hanged for something, I'd rather it be my own name than a false one. You taught me that, actually. Chapter twenty-seven of your ongoing memoir, *Forged Papers, True Names*. Very moving. I wept."

"I don't write memoirs. I write requisition forms."

"Requisition forms with feelings. I've seen them."

Lior gave up on the cup and looked at me straight, and for once their eyes and their mouth agreed with each other. "Ten days," they said. "Whatever happens—after the fourteenth—I want you to know that before you washed up under those pilings, I thought my whole life was going to be copying other people's numbers until someone else's death released me. Now I'm going to die of something interesting instead. It's an improvement. I want that noted in the record."

"Noted," I said. "Filed. Cross-referenced."

We finished the cups. Outside, above the cellar stairs, the harbor lay dark under a thin rain, and somewhere beyond it a magistrate was riding toward us along the salt road, carrying with them the whole machinery of law, indifferent and slow and coming anyway. Ten days.

Then Tam came tumbling down the stairs with wet hair and wide eyes, and the look on his face retired every joke I had.

"There's a man asking at the fish market," he said. "Asking after a girl with river-country speech. He's showing a drawing of your face."

Lior and I looked at each other over the clean cups.

"How good a drawing?" I asked.

Tam's answer was to hold up his fingers an inch apart, and then, apologetically, widen them.
