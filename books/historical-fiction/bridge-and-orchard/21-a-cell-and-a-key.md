## 21. A Cell and a Key

The company jail smelled of wet rope and old decisions, and I had made enough of both to know the difference.

They put me in the second cell from the end—the good one, as jails went, because it had a window the width of a loaf of bread that let in a sliver of harbor. I could see the tide come and go twice a day, which was more than most prisoners got and less than any fisherman needed. Somewhere beyond that window was the Pearlhouse, burned to its ribs. Somewhere beyond *that* was Lior, carrying God knew what through God knew where, and every hour I spent counting knots in the ceiling plank was an hour they were out there without me.

I had been arrested for arson I hadn't committed, by men who knew I hadn't committed it, on the orders of a factor who needed a body to hang the fire on before anyone asked whose lantern had been seen near the counting-house that night. That was the company way: never lie when a scapegoat will do the work cheaper.

Three days in, I had learned the rhythms of the place. The morning slop bucket came at six bells. The bread came at noon, if the baker liked the turnkey. The screaming came whenever Factor Grell needed reminding that his jail still functioned, which lately seemed often. A man who has to keep proving he's feared is a man who knows fear is leaking out of him somewhere.

I was mending this observation into something useful when the turnkey came down with my evening water.

He wasn't the usual one. The usual one was a slab of gristle named Polt who whistled company hymns off-key and kicked cell doors for the pleasure of hearing them complain. This one was younger—twenty, maybe twenty-two—with a jaw like he'd been carved by someone who ran out of patience halfway through, and hands that moved like he'd grown up around lines and rigging. He set the water down inside the door instead of shoving it through, which in my experience marked a man as either new or kind, and in jails those are usually the same thing briefly.

"You're Dagny Sundvik," he said. Not a question.

"I'm whoever's on the charge sheet," I said. "That's how these places work."

"The net-mender." He said it strange, like he was checking the word against something. "East pier. Blue dory."

"Also the arsonist, apparently. Talented woman."

Something crossed his face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would live if he could afford one. He glanced up the stairwell, listened, and then did the thing that changed everything: he sat down on the corridor floor across from my bars, at an angle, casual as a man resting his feet, so anyone coming down the stairs would see a turnkey taking his ease and not a turnkey talking to the prisoner Grell wanted hanged.

"My name's Joren," he said, quiet. "My sister was Maren."

"Was."

"Sold. Seven years back. Company contract, overseas labor clause, the works. She was twelve." He turned the ring of keys over in his hands, not looking at them, looking at the wall. "She wrote twice. Then nothing."

I knew that story. Every soul on the Vessing docks knew some version of that story; we traded them like coins worn smooth. But I'd never heard it from inside the walls, from a man wearing the company's keys on his own belt.

"That's a hard thing to carry while you unlock their doors," I said.

"It's a hard thing to carry anywhere." He looked at me then, direct, and I saw the thing underneath the tiredness. Not grief exactly. Grief that had been fed until it grew teeth. "I've read your file."

"Flattering."

"It says you mended nets for the fleet for eleven years. It says your brother held a salvage license until the company revoked it for 'irregularities' that don't appear in any other record. It says half the divers on the east pier bring you their gear because they trust your knots over the rigger's." He paused. "It says you're dangerous."

"Files are honest in their way."

"Maren would be twenty now." His voice didn't waver, which is how I knew how much it cost him. "If she's alive. And I've spent three years working here telling myself that someday there'd be a way to find out, and every day I unlocked doors for people being processed out and never once—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Then they burn down a house full of orphans' beds and blame the net-mender, and I have to stand in this corridor and pretend that's justice."

"So you're saying you have a conscience problem."

"I'm saying," Joren said, "that I heard the night sergeant talking. There's a magistrate's circuit coming. Ten days, maybe less. And Grell's been sending messages to the capital fast enough to wear out two riders."

That landed somewhere below my ribs. Ten days. If Lior's whole mad scheme hung on catching that circuit—and it did; I'd memorized the route with the rest of them around Mama Osk's table, back when the table still existed—then ten days was everything.

"What kind of messages?" I asked.

"Ones a turnkey isn't meant to read. Ones I read anyway." He leaned closer to the bars, close enough that I could smell lamp oil on him, close enough that I dropped my voice to match his without either of us agreeing to. "There's a transfer order drafted. Original ledgers, sealed crates, armed escort, road to the capital. To arrive *after* the circuit leaves Vessing. Grell means the magistrate to sit here, hear nothing, sign nothing, and go home believing the harbor is clean."

"And the witnesses?"

"There's always room in a jail," Joren said, "for people who talk too much."

* * *

He came back the next night. And the next. That alone should have told me what he was, because no man risks his neck three nights running for a stranger unless the stranger has stopped being a stranger in some way that frightens him.

On the fourth night he brought bread that wasn't jail bread—still warm, real crust, the kind of small smuggling that gets a man flogged—and sat in his spot against the corridor wall, and told me about Maren properly. How she used to dive the shallows before she was old enough to hold her breath long, coming up with shells she'd trade to Lior-sized children for candied ginger. How she'd cried when the company clerk came with the papers, not because she understood, but because her mother understood. How the clerk had smiled while he took her hand to press her mark to the page.

"They always smile," I said. "It's taught. First lesson at clerk school: the families take their cue from your face. Frown and they panic. Smile and they'll walk up the gangplank thanking you."

"You sound like you know clerk school."

"I know a clerk." I thought of Lior then, the way everyone who loved them eventually did—involuntarily, mid-sentence, like a stone in the shoe. "Youngster came up from a diver's family, indentured at nine years old. Learned early that surviving means being furniture. Spent nine years perfecting it. Now—" I laughed, and it came out rougher than I intended. "Now they're forging seals and running letters for a dead man, and doing it better than people who've lied professionally all their lives."

"A dead man."

"Founder. Vane. Officially drowned, actually exiled, definitely writing letters that would gut this company from gullet to tail." I watched his face do the arithmetic. Everyone on the docks remembered Aldric Vane; the older divers still swore by his harbor rules. "You can call me a liar. Most people would."

Joren was quiet a long moment. Water slapped the pilings somewhere below us; the whole jail sat on stilts of its own bad history, close enough to the tide that you could feel it breathe.

"I processed a shipment record eight months ago," he said finally. "Incoming contracts, reassignment of indenture. Forty-one names, transferred to buyers in the southern colonies. I copied the manifest because it was my job. I still remember three of the names." He said them slowly, like setting down heavy things in order. "One of them might have been Maren's. The age was right. The origin port was right. I've been unable to sleep since."

"Because it might be her."

"Because I signed the receipt." His knuckles whitened around the key ring. "I helped file her away and I didn't even know it. That's what this place does. It makes you complicit in your own family and calls it wages."

There it was. I'd been waiting for it—the crack in him where the plan could get in. Every good knot starts with the right opening in the rope. You don't force it. You find the place already wanting to give.

"Joren," I said, "what happens to me in ten days?"

"Circuit sits. You're arraigned for the fire. There are two witnesses lined up, both company men, both lying. You'll be convicted. Given Grell's mood, I'd guess transportation rather than hanging—you're useful and the optics of hanging a woman are poor—but transportation is just hanging slowly, overseas, same as Maren."

"And what happens to the evidence against the company?"

"Grell burns it or ships it. Either way it never reaches the square."

"Right." I stood up, and my knees cracked like ship's timbers, and I decided I was too old for this work and would do it anyway. "So here's my proposition, and understand I've had four days of bad bread and worse thinking to build it, so poke holes in it freely.

"The magistrate sits in ten days. Grell plans to move the ledgers after, because moving them before invites questions from the escort captain—questions like *why*. Which means for ten days, everything Grell needs to bury forty-one children is sitting in one vault in the Company House, guarded by men on a rotation that a turnkey reads off a board every morning." I gripped the bars. "You know the rotation. I know the people outside who are coming for that ledger whether Grell likes it or not. And between us we know the names on the manifests—which means we know the names of every child this company sold, including maybe your Maren and maybe half the Pearlhouse besides."

"They'll never get into the vault," Joren said. But he said it like a man testing a knot, not cutting it.

"They don't need to get in. They need it to come *out*, once, at the right hour, into the right hands, in front of a magistrate who can't unhear it afterward. And the person who walks it out needs papers, a reason, and a door left unlatched." I let that sit. "You've got keys, Joren. You've had them on your belt for three years, and every night you've gone home knowing what they unlock and done nothing. This is the last stretch of nights where you'll ever have to do nothing again."

He stared at me. Above us, boots crossed the floor—two men, unhurried, passing—and we both went silent and still, and I watched a young man make himself invisible with the same skill a diver's child learns, and thought: *there's more dock in you than uniform.*

When the boots faded, he spoke.

"If I'm caught, they won't hang me. They'll ask who's outside waiting, and they'll keep asking until I tell them, and I don't know yet whether I'm strong enough not to."

"Then don't be strong," I said. "Be smart. Don't know the names. Know the meeting place and the hour and nothing else. A man who can only point to a cellar can't betray a network."

"And you? They'll ask you the same."

"They already did, day two. Polt asked, and Grell's man asked nicer, and I gave them the same answer both times: I mend nets and mind my business." I grinned, and it felt good, the first real grin since the fire. "Here's the beautiful thing about being arrested for a crime I didn't commit, Joren. Everything I say is a liar's noise. They can't believe me when I tell the truth and can't disbelieve me when I lie. I am, professionally speaking, worthless to interrogate."

He almost laughed. It escaped him as a breath, quickly strangled, and he pressed the back of his hand to his mouth like pushing it back in.

"The rotation changes at shift change," he said at last. "Dawn and dusk bell. There's a quarter-hour gap when the vault corridor is one man, and that man is usually eating." He looked down at his keys, and I watched three years of helplessness rearrange themselves into purpose, slow as a tide turning. "If your friends outside have someone who can pass for company staff..."

"They've got someone who can pass for anything. That's the trouble with clerks. Nobody looks at furniture."

* * *

On the sixth night, he brought more than bread.

He brought a scrap of charcoal and the back of a duty roster, and he crouched in the corridor drawing the vault corridor from memory—the turns, the door, the sightline from the sergeant's desk—while I memorized with my eyes shut, the way I'd learned charts as a girl because paper was dear and memory was free. Twice he stopped, frowned, corrected himself. A man who checks his own work twice is a man worth trusting with yours.

Then he did the other thing. He reached into his shirt and drew out a small oilskin fold, and opened it on his knee, and there was Maren. A diving shell on a cord, a lock of hair going brown-paper brittle, and a letter with a southern colony's postmark, salt-stained, read soft at the folds.

"She could write," he said. "Taught herself off cargo manifests. Look—" His finger touched the line, careful, the way you touch a net you're afraid of tearing further. "*Dear Jor. The water here is warm and wrong. I miss cold tides. Tell the pier I'll come back rich.*"

He folded it away before I could see whether his hands shook.

"Forty-one names," I said quietly. "Maybe hers among them. Maybe alive. The southern routes run through three ports, and smugglers know them all, and I happen to know some smugglers who owe the Pearlhouse favors and a few who owe it apologies. When this is done—when the ledger lands—those names become evidence, and evidence becomes search writs, and search writs become ships boarded. She could be found, Joren. Not promised. But possible. I deal in possible. It's the only cargo that keeps."

He nodded, throat working. Then he stood, brushed off his trousers, became a turnkey again—all posture, flat face, ring of iron at his hip—and paused at the bottom of the stairs.

"Dagny. Your friends outside. Whatever they're planning for that vault—it has to happen before the circuit sits, doesn't it? Because once the magistrate hears nothing and leaves..."

"Once the circuit leaves, the company owns the law in this harbor for another year, and we'll all be dead or transported or worse, forgotten." I settled back against the wall. "So yes. Before. Which means whatever message you carry out, carry it tonight."

"I know," he said. "I've known since the first night. I was just waiting until I was sure of you."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sure of everyone in this building except myself," Joren said, "and I've decided that's enough."

He climbed the stairs, and I heard the outer door, and then it was me and the tide and the loaf-slit window showing a slice of black harbor with one lantern moving across it—a boat, small, heading out past the breakwater toward the headland where a burned house stood and a scattered family gathered itself back together somewhere in the dark.

Ten days. A quarter-hour gap at shift change. One man eating.

I lay back and closed my eyes and started tying knots in my head—the complicated ones, the ones that hold under load—and somewhere before dawn I heard footsteps come down the corridor again, pause at my bars, and leave a folded scrap of paper where the water bowl usually sat.

I waited until the steps receded. Then I opened it.

Not Joren's hand. Rounder, hurried, written by someone using a knee for a desk:

*Dagny — Hale says he can get them into the vault, but only if he lives long enough to testify. They're watching him now. Tell the turnkey the guard rotation is wrong — Grell changed it yesterday. Trust the new schedule, not the old one. And if you get the chance: ask Joren about the third name on the manifest. L.*

I read it three times by the light of the guard-lamp bleeding under the door, and then I ate it, fiber by bitter fiber, because that's what you do with a message that matters.

The third name. Lior had remembered it too.

Hope, it turns out, comes in through the smallest windows. You just have to be sitting facing the bars when it does.
