## 24. Osk's Story

The Pearlhouse burned down to its bones on a Tuesday, and by Thursday we were already arguing about who would cook.

That is the way of things when you have fed divers, net-menders, orphans, smugglers' cousins, and one runaway courier for thirty years: grief does not get its own chair at the table. It sits where it can. It eats what's left. And the rest of us keep the fire going because if we stop, everything else stops too.

We had taken refuge in the back room of Sela's chandlery—a low-ceilinged place that smelled of tar and old rope and, faintly, of Sela's endless resentment at having her storeroom borrowed. The Pearlhouse survivors were folded into every corner like laundry nobody wanted to fold properly. Dagny was gone—arrested, blamed for a fire she'd have had to be an idiot and an arsonist to set, and Dagny has never been either. The children slept in a heap near the stove the way they always did, a pile of elbows and salt-stiff hair, breathing in shifts like a single animal.

And Lior Nystrom sat apart from all of it, at the end of the workbench, turning their mother's pearl over in their fingers—or the space where it used to be, I should say, because the pearl had been traded away by then, upriver to the smugglers for passage and silence. They kept reaching for it anyway. That's the thing about hands. They remember what they held long after the holding is done.

I watched them do it three times before I decided.

Now, I am not a woman given to deciding quickly. Ask anyone. I have sat on the question of whether to replace the Pearlhouse roof since the year of the great hail, and the roof made the decision for me last week when it came through the ceiling in flames. But some decisions you make slowly, and some make themselves, and this one had been making itself for twenty-odd years, sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook.

Lior's past was not what Lior thought it was. And I was the only one left alive who knew.

* * *

You should understand something about me before I tell it. I was a diver before I was anything. Before I was Mama Osk, before I was a keeper of a dockside house full of strays, before my knees gave out and my hearing went soft in the left ear, I was a girl with a weighted stone and a rope and a pair of lungs that could outlast most boys' pride. I pulled oysters off the bottom of Vessing harbor from the time I was eight until the time I was twenty-six, and I can still hold my breath long enough to embarrass a fisherman, which I occasionally do, for morale.

And in those years—the good years, before the company bought up the beds and turned free divers into licensed serfs—I dove beside a woman named Maren Nystrom.

Lior knows the name the way you know a name on a paper. It's theirs, it's attached to them, it comes up at awkward moments. What they do not know—and could not know, because everyone who might have told them is dead except me—is that Maren Nystrom was the finest diver this harbor ever produced, and that she died for reasons written down in no ledger, and that her child was marked by the Halvard Trading Company before that child could walk.

So there I was, watching Lior's empty hand close around nothing, and I thought: enough. Twenty years of keeping my mouth shut to keep myself safe, and look where safety got us. A burned house. A jailed friend. A packet of letters from a drowned man who isn't drowned. Safety, it turns out, was just the fire arriving slowly.

"Boy," I said—because I call Lior "boy" even though I've never been sure Lior is one or the other, and Lior stopped correcting me years ago, which tells you something about how tired they are—"come here. Bring me that jug, and sit down. I have a story, and you're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

Lior came and sat. Of course they did. Lior has spent nine years learning to obey without seeming to obey, which is a skill I neither admire nor blame, but it means that when I ask, they come, and only afterward do their eyes go around the room counting exits. Old habit. Diver's child. Always knowing where the surface is.

"The jug," I said.

They poured. I drank. The chandlery's stock is rough stuff but it serves.

"You know your mother dove," I said. "Everyone's told you that much, I expect. Or nobody's told you anything and you worked it out yourself, which is more likely, knowing how this town handles the dead. Talks about the weather, goes quiet about the graves."

Lior said nothing. Just that careful stillness they do. Nine years as a clerk taught them to wait for information rather than chase it. I find it infuriating in a child and useful in an ally, and Lior is somehow both.

"I dove with her," I said. "Maren Nystrom and Osk Brandt, side by side, six days a week, for nine years. From the time I was fourteen. She was older than me by two years and better than me by ten, and I say that as someone who does not hand out compliments unless there's drink involved, and there is, so." I raised the cup. "Your mother was the best I ever saw. She could read the tide the way your company clerks read a manifest—like it was telling her something it wasn't telling anyone else. She knew where the oysters would bed before the oysters did."

Something moved behind Lior's face. I watched it move. I let it.

"She found pearls," I went on, "more than her share and more than anyone's share, and the company noticed. That's the beginning of the whole rotten story, boy, so lean in. The company noticed, and the company did what the company does. It didn't buy her catch. It bought *her*. A salvage contract—exclusive rights to her findings, in exchange for a wage that looked fair on paper and was theft in practice, and I'll thank you not to correct me on the accounting, because I've seen your face over ledgers and I know you already believe me."

"They wrote her findings at half value," Lior said quietly. "I've seen the contracts. I copy them now. That's the standard clause."

"And you've never once wondered why you can see the theft plainly, at your age, with your training, and your mother signed anyway?"

Lior's jaw tightened. "She had to eat."

"She had more than eating to think about," I said. "She had you."

I let that sit a moment. Outside, the harbor slapped against the pilings the way it always does, patient and indifferent, like a creditor.

"Maren was pregnant with you when she signed," I said. "And here is the part nobody living knows but me. Your father—" I waved a hand. "A sailor, a foreigner, gone or dead, she never said and I never pressed; a diver learns not to press. The point is, she was carrying you alone, and diving alone, and the company knew it. And the company's man in those days—old Factor Ellery, Grell's predecessor, may he rot wherever he's rotted—came to her with an offer. Not the salvage contract. Something else. Something she threw in his face so hard he bled on the floor of the Pearlhouse, which was a different building then but the same idea."

"What offer?" Lior asked. Their voice had gone very flat. Clerks' flatness. I recognized it and hated using it on them.

"Indenture," I said. "*Yours.* Ellery wanted your contract drawn before you were born. He wanted the child of the best diver in Vessing bound to the company from birth to twenty-one—not for your labor, boy, don't flatter yourself, a clerk's wages are nothing to a company that steals whole harvests. He wanted you bound because binding you bound *her*. Think about it the way you think about numbers, since that's how you think. A free diver with a bonded child can run. Can take the boat, take the skills, take the pearls, disappear up the coast where the company's writ thins out. But a diver whose child belongs to the company? That diver stays. That diver dives. That diver hands over whatever the scale says her catch weighs and smiles about it, because the price of complaint is written in a crib somewhere with the company's seal on it."

* * *

Lior didn't speak for a long while. One of the orphan children shifted in the pile by the stove and settled again, and the fire popped, and the harbor went on slapping the pilings like it had all the time in the world, which, to be fair, it does.

"That's why the contract is strange," Lior said finally. Slowly. Like working out a sum aloud. "I always thought—it's a clerical oddity. My indenture names a guardian-of-record instead of a guardian-of-person. It cites a maritime statute instead of the standard apprenticeship act. It was drawn *before my birth date*, which I checked four times because documents aren't supposed to do that." Their eyes came up to mine, and there was the diver under the clerk, the one I'd been waiting twenty years to see. "It's strange because it was never meant to be an indenture at all. It's collateral. I'm collateral."

"Now you're doing arithmetic," I said. "Good. You're quicker than Hale, and I'll deny saying that."

"Why didn't she run?" Lior asked, and there it was—not anger, worse, the genuine bafflement of someone who has survived by running inside themselves and cannot imagine why anyone wouldn't. "If I was the chain, why stay chained? Take me and go. Disappear. People did it."

"People tried," I said. "The company caught most of them. And Maren—" I stopped. This was the hard part, the part I'd carried alone since the night it happened, and carrying it alone had felt for years like loyalty and lately felt like cowardice, and I suppose that's how most cowardice works: it waits for the right moment to rebrand itself. "Maren stayed because Ellery promised her the indenture would convert. Serve quiet, dive honest, and when the child turned twelve the company would sell her the contract back at cost. Paper for coin. Clean."

"And they didn't."

"They never intended to. It was bait on a hook, and she took it because mothers bite hooks, boy, it's practically the definition. And when you turned eleven and she went to Ellery with the coin saved—and she had it, every copper, I saw the jar—Ellery laughed at her. Told her the contract had been *transferred*. Sold upstream. Filed into a block of indentures bundled for overseas sale along with a dozen other children's papers, sold to buyers in the southern colonies who would never set eyes on Vessing harbor." I watched Lior's face go white in the firelight. "Yes. You've heard that story recently, haven't you. From a certain packet of letters."

"Vane's letters," Lior whispered. "He found the sales. The child-contract blocks. He listed—" Their breath caught. "Sefa read the lists. Names and dates of transfer. I looked at them and felt sick and I didn't—I didn't look for my own name, because I was afraid it would be there."

"It's there," I said gently. "I'd stake the Pearlhouse on it. Which is ash, so easy stakes these days."

* * *

Here is the rest, and I will tell it quickly because my throat doesn't like the shape of it.

Three weeks after Ellery laughed at her, Maren Nystrom went down on the eastern beds in ordinary water, ordinary weather, and did not come up. Her line-mate swore she was fine at the bottom—waved, even, that little two-finger wave divers give when they mean *all's well, stop staring at me*. Then she didn't surface. Then the line went slack in a way lines don't go slack unless they're cut.

The company ruled it misadventure. Cramp, maybe. A bad breath-hold. The sea takes whom the sea takes, said the coroner's report, which was drafted by a company clerk, and I have lived long enough to know that the sea gets blamed for a great many things it didn't do.

But I dove those beds, and I knew them like my own kitchen, and I knew there was a piling stump off the eastern point—an old mooring, rotted to a jagged knee—that Maren had navigated around ten thousand times blind. And when they dragged her up, her rope was cut clean, not frayed, and clean-cut rope at forty feet means a knife, and knives don't swim.

I went to Ellery with it. I was twenty-three and stupid and grieving, and I stood in his office and told him what I thought, and he listened very politely, and then he explained to me—with real warmth, almost kindness—that the orphaned child of a dead diver would be placed in company care, and that company care could be a ward-house in the capital, far from salt water and everyone who loved her, or it could be a place here in Vessing among friends. He said *friends* the way you'd spit.

So I took the second thing. I opened what became the Pearlhouse—a rented room, then a wharf-shack, then the house itself—and I made sure the company's collateral grew up fed and dry and invisible, within reach of people who remembered her mother. I couldn't undo the contract. I couldn't prove the murder. But I could stand at a doorway for eighteen years and make sure the door opened both ways.

Nobody ever told Lior any of this. It was the condition, implied. Friends keep quiet; wards get kept.

"You *knew* me," Lior said at last, and their voice cracked down the middle like green wood. "All those years. Suppers. Mending. You never—you let me think I was nobody's. That nobody had ever—"

"You weren't nobody's," I said, sharper than I meant to, because the guilt in it stung coming up. "You were *ours*. Every bowl of soup in that house was a message, even if the words never got said. But yes. I let you think the silence was the whole truth, and that was a coward's charity, and I'll answer for it. Not tonight, though. Tonight I need you to hear the ending, because the ending matters."

"There's an ending?"

"There's a reason I'm telling you now," I said. "Think, boy. Why does a company keep a falsified indenture alive for twenty-one years? Why does Grell's crowd burn a boarding house and arrest Dagny instead of just seizing the letters? Because paper is the whole game. The letters prove Vane lived and objected. The second ledger proves what was done. But your contract—" I leaned forward and put my hand flat on the workbench between us, the way I used to steady crates in a swell. "Your contract, with its impossible dates and its wrong statutes and its guardian-of-record who doesn't exist, is a signature on everything. It's the child-trade, documented, in company hand, with a living witness attached. You're not just carrying evidence anymore, Lior Nystrom. As far as the company is concerned, you *are* evidence. And evidence that walks and talks and reads ledgers is evidence they will drown, and call it misadventure, and write it up in a tidy column."

* * *

Lior sat very still. I have seen divers come up from deep water with that same stillness—too much world arriving too fast, the body refusing to panic because panic spends air.

"My mother," they said finally. "They killed her. For the pearls. And then they kept me like—like a lien against her ghost. And now they'll kill me for the same numbers wearing different ink."

"They'll try," I said. "But here's the difference between you and her, and I want you to hear it plain. Maren dove alone against them, and the water was hers but the paper was theirs, and paper won. You've got what she never had. You've got the letters. You've got Hale, guilty as sin and ready to bleed for it. You've got Dagny working the inside of their own jail, God help us all. You've got Sefa, who carries a name worth more than any seal you can forge. And you've got me, old and slow and owed twenty years of silence, which I intend to start paying back immediately."

"How?" Lior asked.

"By being exactly what I've always been," I said. "An old woman nobody watches, standing in a doorway. Only the doorway's changed."

It was then that the knock came at the chandlery's back door—three quick, two slow, Dagny's smuggler-sign though Dagny herself was locked in a cell across the harbor—and my heart went up into my throat, because that signal meant one thing: word from inside, urgent, and passed by someone who shouldn't have it.

One of the orphans opened the slot. A scrap of paper came through, greasy and folded small.

I read it. Then I read it again, because the first reading refused to make sense, and then I handed it to Lior and watched their face while they understood what I'd understood.

The note said: *Company made contact with the Nystrom clerk today. Offered full pardon and freedom papers in exchange for the woman Sefa. Clerk has three days to answer.*

Lior looked up at me. And I saw the diver's stillness crack, just once, like ice under a boot.

"Osk," they said, "they didn't summon me. Nobody delivered an offer. How did Dagny's turnkey know what the company offered me—before I've even been asked?"

And that, boy and girls, is when the fire in my chest went from grief to something with teeth. Because there were only two answers to that question, and one of them meant the company was playing games with our heads, and the other meant somebody close to us was already talking.

Neither answer was one I liked. Both meant we had less time than anybody thought.
