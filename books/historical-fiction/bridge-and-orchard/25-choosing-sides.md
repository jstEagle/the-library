## 25. Choosing Sides

The summons came the way dangerous things always come in Vessing: politely, on good paper, delivered by someone whose boots cost more than my year of wages.

I was mending a column of freight figures in the smugglers' cellar—Dagny's brother Rurik kept his accounts in a hand that could only be described as hostile—when one of Osk's orphans, a girl named Vess with salt-scoured braids and the unbothered expression of a gull, slipped down the stairs and held out a folded paper.

"A man in gray gave me a coin to bring this to the clerk," she said. "I told him I didn't know any clerk. He gave me two coins."

"Smart girl."

"I know." She waited until I took it, then went back up two steps and paused. "He had company ink on his cuffs. The purple kind."

Of course he did. The Halvard Trading Company spared no expense except where it counted.

The paper inside was heavy stock, cream-colored, watermarked—the kind of paper that expected to be obeyed. Three sentences, in a secretary's careful hand:

*The Factor Grell requests the pleasure of Lior Nystrom's company at the tide pools below the west seawall, this evening at slack water. Come alone. Matters of mutual interest will be discussed.*

No signature. No seal. That was the message itself: *we don't need to prove anything to you.* A man who signs his letters is asking for something. A man who doesn't is telling you.

Mutual interest. In my experience—and nine years as a company clerk had given me a very thorough education in the subject—when a trading concern speaks of mutual interest, the interest is never mutual. One party has something the other wants, and the conversation consists of discovering which of you it is.

I knew. I'd known since the oyster beds, since the ledger sat sealed in wax beneath the waterline like a second heart beating under the harbor. They wanted Sefa. And they thought they could buy her with me.

I read the note twice more, then held it over the cellar's single lamp and watched the cream paper curl and blacken. Vess watched too, with great professional interest.

"Should I tell anyone?" she asked.

"Yes," I said. "Tell everyone. Tell them a clerk burned a letter and looked frightened doing it."

"Were you frightened?"

"That depends entirely on how the evening goes."

* * *

Slack water comes twice a day, that hour when the sea can't decide between coming and going, when the whole harbor holds its breath and even the gulls seem embarrassed by the silence. It's a diver's hour. My mother used to say the sea gives you nothing at slack tide—not current, not help, not excuse. Whatever you do in that stillness, you do alone.

Someone at the company had chosen the time deliberately. Or perhaps they simply knew the harbor well enough that the symbolism arrived without being invited. Either way, I walked out along the west seawall as the light went bronze and long, and found Factor Grell standing at the edge of the tide pools like a man inspecting property he intended to repossess.

I had seen him perhaps six times in nine years, always at a distance, always surrounded. Up close he was smaller than I expected, and neater—a compact man in a charcoal coat with not a hair out of place, the kind of face you forget while you're looking directly at it. He did not turn when my footsteps sounded on the stone. Why would he? He already knew I'd come. Men like Grell are never surprised by compliance; they budget for it.

"Clerk Nystrom," he said, to the water. "You'll forgive the unconventional venue. Walls have ears, and mine have developed an inconvenient tendency toward honesty lately."

"That must be frustrating."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that I'd said a thing, filed under *later*. "Your mother dove these pools," he said. "Maren Nystrom. Third-best pearl-taker on the coast, by the old assessments. You have her hands. Diver's hands. I noticed them the first week you were in the counting-house—you hold your pen like a knife."

"You've been watching me for nine years?"

"I've been watching everyone for nine years. It's the job. Most people are worth exactly the attention they pay themselves." He turned then and looked at me properly, and I understood why the company sent him instead of enforcers. The enforcers were fists. This man was arithmetic. "You are not most people, Clerk Nystrom. Which is why we're having this conversation instead of a different one."

"And what would the different one sound like?"

Grell crouched beside a tide pool, examined an anemone retreating into itself, and said, in the tone of a man reading shipping rates: "It would sound like the Pearlhouse in ashes, and Dagny Skarn on a transport south, and Corvin Hale drowned quietly off the pilings with his ledgers sewn into his coat. It would sound like a young woman named Sefa taken at the border with no papers and no name, and no record anywhere that she ever existed. It would be a very short conversation. Nobody would enjoy it. Least of all me—I dislike waste."

The sea breathed. In, out. Slack water, holding its breath with me.

"But," Grell continued, straightening, brushing nothing from his immaculate sleeve, "I am authorized to offer an alternative. Full pardon. Every irregularity forgiven—the forged seals, the stolen credentials, the theft of company property. Your indenture dissolved today, not in three years. Freedom papers signed before witnesses. Passage anywhere you like, and a letter of credit sufficient to start a life somewhere the name Nystrom means nothing at all." He paused, letting the size of it settle. "In exchange for the courier. Delivered to the Company House within three days, alive and unharmed. She is not to be hurt, Clerk. I want to be clear about that. This isn't vengeance. It's bookkeeping."

"Bookkeeping," I repeated.

"She knows things that will destabilize a charter that employs four hundred people in this town alone. Dockworkers. Chandlers. Families." He said it gently, the way you'd explain tides to a child. "You've seen what happens when the company feels threatened. The fire was regrettable. I would prefer there not be a second one. Help me prevent it."

For a moment—one long, shameful moment—I let myself look at it. The whole shining thing. Freedom papers. My contract, that strip of paper that had been nailed across my life since I was twelve, gone up in smoke legally, officially, with witnesses. Three years returned to me. Three years of not being a number. A letter of credit. A door opening onto a life where I could stand in a room and be seen, could sign my own name because I wanted to and not because history demanded it.

Three years. And all it cost was handing over a woman who had nearly drowned carrying her father's letters, to men who had already burned down the home that sheltered her.

"You're weighing it," Grell observed. There was no contempt in his voice. That was the worst part. He said it the way you'd note the weather.

"Anyone would weigh it," I said.

"Yes. That's rather the point. The offer exists precisely so that refusing it becomes your choice, made freely, with full knowledge. It makes everything afterward cleaner." He checked a pocket watch—gold, unhurried. "You have until the third day. If the courier reaches the Company House, none of the other things need happen. If she doesn't—" he gestured at the horizon, at the whole grey machinery of consequences waiting out there beyond the seawall, "—then I proceed with the shorter conversation. Good evening, Clerk Nystrom. Give my regards to the tide pools. They remember your mother better than you do, I suspect."

He walked away along the seawall toward the Company House lights, neat as a closing column, and left me alone with the water.

* * *

I didn't go back right away. I climbed down to the tide pools instead, because my legs decided before I did, and sat on the wet stone where the sea couldn't quite reach.

My mother's place, apparently. I don't remember her here—I was seven when she died, and memory at seven is mostly weather—but I knew how to sit at a tideline the way divers sit, knees up, spine loose, breathing with the water. Some inheritance skips the mind and lands in the body. The company took her contract, then took her life in installments, and finally took me, and the only thing they never managed to seize was the way I sit on stones.

Let's be honest, since nobody else is here to audit me. I weighed it. Properly. Not the theatrical weighing of a hero in a ballad, but the real, low, animal arithmetic of a survivor. Nine years I had survived by being unremarkable. Nine years of lowered eyes and copied columns and a scar kept hidden under my sleeve because a diver's scar invites questions and questions invite attention. Survival wasn't my philosophy; it was my craft. I was good at it. And here was the company—my company, in every way that mattered for a decade—offering to make survival permanent. Signed. Witnessed. Free.

And against it: Sefa, who slept badly and woke apologizing for it. Mama Osk's split lip healing crooked. Dagny in a cell for the crime of knowing her brother. The Pearlhouse gutted, the smell of burnt net and charred beam still living in my clothes. Hale, walking into custody with nothing but arithmetic in his mouth.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a stone under silt: the double entry. Column forty-one, page ninety, the pearls recorded twice. The first crack. Before Sefa, before the letters, before any of it, there had been one afternoon when I saw a lie in the numbers and felt—God help me—*curious*. Curiosity over obedience. Everything since had just been that crack widening.

If I handed Sefa over, what would I actually be buying? Three years of my life back, to spend in a town where the Pearlhouse was ash and my friends were scattered or chained or dead. Freedom papers issued by the same hand that wrote fraudulent contracts for children—including, if Vane's letters were true, possibly my own. What kind of freedom is it that's sold to you in exchange for a friend? It's not freedom. It's just a longer leash, and I'd been leashed long enough to know the difference by feel.

There was something else, too. Something I kept turning over like a pearl with a flaw in it.

Grell had said my mother's name. Maren Nystrom. He'd known her assessments, her hands, her tide pools. Osk had told me the indenture was written strange—that the company had marked me early, watched me, kept me close. Nine years of being *unremarkable*, and it turns out I had been remarkable all along, carefully filed under unremarkable by men who knew exactly what I was. My invisibility hadn't been my achievement. It had been their design.

That changes a person. You spend nine years believing you survived through skill, and then a neat man in a charcoal coat informs you that your survival was a line item, budgeted and approved, and that the offer to end it is also a line item. Nothing about me had ever been hidden from them. So what, exactly, was left to protect by hiding further?

The tide began to move. Barely—a shiver along the surface, the first slow lean of the ebb. Slack water breaking. My mother used to say the sea gives you nothing at slack tide, whatever you do, you do alone. Fine. Then let the record show—since records matter, since this entire war is a war of records—that Lior Nystrom, contract number 1147, sat alone at the west seawall at slack water and did the sums.

Here is what I wrote in the ledger of myself:

They offered me everything I ever wanted, in exchange for the only things I ever chose.

Every good thing in my life for nine years had been assigned to me—a bed, a desk, a number, a sentence. Sefa I chose. The Pearlhouse I chose. Osk's terrible tea and Dagny's profanity and the orphans' conspiracy of coins—all chosen, all paid for out of the tiny secret income of a life the company thought it owned. Handing Sefa over wouldn't buy back my freedom. It would sell the last piece of me that was free already. You don't trade the coin you actually have for counterfeit.

And there was Sefa herself to think of—not as cargo, but as a person with her own terrible arithmetic. She wasn't running to save the town. She was running to reach her father, a man the world insisted was dead, and she carried letters in his handwriting the way other people carry relics. If I gave her to Grell, she wouldn't just lose her freedom. She'd lose the ending of her story—the reunion, the restoration, the father. I knew what it was to have your story interrupted. Mine had been interrupted at twelve years old by a contract with strange clauses. I would not do that to another orphan of another storm.

Besides—and here the wry voice, the clerk's voice, the voice that had gotten me through nine years of tedium with my soul more or less intact—besides, practically speaking, the woman had survived shipwreck, hunters, fire, and my cooking. It seemed rude to undo all that progress.

I laughed, alone, at the tideline. It came out wrong, half sob, but it came out.

Then I stood up, brushed off my trousers, and did the thing you do at the end of an entry. I closed the column. Totaled it. Refused.

Not dramatically. No speech, no raised fist, no vow to the sea. Just a clerk finishing a calculation and finding the balance didn't clear. "No," I said to the water, quietly, the way you'd decline a shipment—*return to sender, terms unacceptable.* The sea took it the way the sea takes everything, without comment, and kept moving, and so did I.

But I knew what it meant, even saying it softly. Grell had designed the offer precisely so refusal would be my choice, freely made. He wanted my refusal on record—not written anywhere, of course, but *known*, the way the company knows things—so that everything after would be clean. Clean fire. Clean arrests. Clean drowning off the pilings. By saying no, I had signed something. I had sealed my own fate with the same deliberateness they'd use to break it.

Three days. That was the clock now. And behind me, up the hill, the Company House windows burned steady and patient, and somewhere inside them Grell was already entering tomorrow's transactions.

I put my hand in my pocket and found the small hard shape of my mother's pearl—or the empty spot where it had been, traded weeks ago to the smugglers, and my fingers remembered its weight anyway. Some prices you keep paying long after the coin is gone. I turned toward the dark harbor, toward the oyster beds where the second ledger slept under wax and water, toward the cellar where my family was counting contraband by lamplight and waiting to hear what the company had offered.

They'd want to know. They'd insist on voting, probably, because that's what the Pearlhouse did—even scattered, even in ashes, they voted. And I'd tell them the truth: that I'd been offered everything, and that I'd said no, and that the no was going to cost us all.

Then I saw the light.

A lantern, low near the fish-market tunnels, swinging in the old signal rhythm—two quick, one slow, two quick. Dagny's rhythm. The turnkey's rhythm. *Urgent. Come now.*

Three days, Grell had given me. The lantern said the company had decided not to wait.
