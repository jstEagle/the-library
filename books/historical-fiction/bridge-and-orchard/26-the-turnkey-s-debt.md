## 26. The Turnkey's Debt

There is a particular smell to a company jail cell, and it is not despair, whatever the poets would have you believe. Despair has no odor. What you smell in here is old brine, wet stone, and the sour sweat of men who have been paid too little to care about their work. I know because I have had six days to catalog it, and a net-mender with nothing to mend will catalog anything.

My name is Dagny Skalle. I mend nets. I am also, as of last week, an arsonist of record, which is a lie so lazy even the company should be embarrassed by it. The Pearlhouse burned; someone had to hang for it; and I was the one they could catch without exerting themselves. Factor Grell's men did not even run after me. They walked. Like men collecting a delivery.

I have spent my six days doing what any sensible prisoner does: sleeping, counting the stones — two hundred and eleven, if you include the cracked one by the bucket that is really half a stone wearing a crown of moss — and befriending my turnkey.

The befriending was not sentiment. Well. It began as strategy and turned into something else, the way most true things do.

His name is Joren Vask. He is perhaps twenty-four, broad through the shoulders in the way of a man who grew up hauling crates, and he cannot look at me without his ears going red. On my second day he brought me water and I said thank you, and the poor boy flinched like I'd thrown it back at him. On my third day I asked how his sister was keeping, because Mama Osk's network of gossip reaches even through company walls, and I had heard — from the fishwives, who hear everything — that a Vask girl had come back from overseas service three years ago with salt-scars on her lungs and a silence on her that nobody could lift.

That question changed the weather in this cell.

He stood there holding the water jug and did not move for so long I thought he'd forgotten where doors work. Then he said, "She doesn't talk about it," and I said, "No. They never do," and I told him about my brother Torvald, who lost his boat license to the company on a technicality written by a clerk who probably got a commendation for it, and how Torvald went quiet too, in a different way, the way of a man who keeps rowing toward a shore that isn't there anymore.

I didn't tell him anything useful. That's important. Whatever else happens, I gave him nothing but grief, freely shared, which is the only currency in this harbor that the company hasn't figured out how to tax.

* * *

On the sixth day, shift change comes late because the morning turnkey — a fat man named Brakk who breathes like a bellows with opinions — has taken sick, or claims to have, and Joren is pulling a double.

He comes down the stairs with his lamp held low, the way he always does, so the light doesn't wake the drunks in the holding cells. He stops at my bars. His face is doing something complicated.

"You're being moved," he says. "Tomorrow. To the capital."

The capital. I have heard what happens to prisoners on the way to the capital. The sea between here and there is deep, and Grell writes the manifests, and drowned prisoners require no wagons, no guards, no awkward questions at circuit court. I put my hands flat on my knees so they don't see them shake.

"When tomorrow?"

"Dawn." He looks up the stairs. Looks back down. His jaw works like he's chewing on something that bites back. "Dagny."

"Aye."

"I need to tell you about my sister."

And then he tells me, in a voice kept low and level by main force, what happened to Petra Vask. How she signed an indenture at fourteen — a diver's contract, deep-water clause — and how the contract she signed and the contract the company filed were not the same document. How she found that out when she tried to buy her freedom at nineteen and the paper in the vault said twenty-five years, said a debt she'd never incurred, said she'd received wages that went into a factor's pocket. How she wrote letters of protest that were answered with copies of her own signature beside terms she never agreed to. How she came home at twenty-one, freed at last by a clerical "error" that arrived suspiciously soon after she stopped being profitable, and how she sat in her mother's kitchen for a month before she said one word out loud, and the word was *liars*.

"They sold children," Joren says. "I've started wondering how many. I've started wondering whether anyone will ever be made to answer for it."

"Someone's trying," I say. "Right now. Tonight, probably, someone's trying."

He crouches then, so his eyes are level with mine through the bars, and I see that the red-eared boy with the careful lamp is gone, or rather that he was always a covering over something harder, the way calm water covers current.

"The vault rotation changes at the end of the month," he says. "But I hear the guards talk. Two men on the strong-room door until the middle watch. Then one, and the other walks the upper floors. The relief is always late. Always by a quarter hour, sometimes twenty minutes. They gamble dice in the stairwell before they come down, and Grell docks their pay for it and lets them keep gambling because a man in debt obeys."

I commit it to memory the way I commit knot patterns: two on, then one, the gap at the changing of the watch. A quarter hour. Twenty minutes if the dice run long.

"Why are you telling me this?" I ask, though I already know, and I want to hear him say it, because some things a person needs to have said aloud in their own voice to know they're the kind of person who says them.

"Because Petra can't say it," he says. "So I'll say it for her."

Then he does his keys, finds the one that doesn't belong to any lock on this block — I notice these things, I've had six days and two hundred and twelve stones — and opens my door.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about escaping a jail: it is mostly walking. All that drama, all those iron bars and locked doors, and in the end the great obstacle is that your legs have been folded on a stone shelf for six days and do not wish to participate. I came out of that cell moving like a crab off a hot rock, and Joren had the grace not to laugh, though his mouth fought him.

He gives me a fish-oil cloak that smells like the inside of a barrel and a lantern with the wick turned so low it's more rumor than light.

"Tunnels under the fish market," he says. "The old drainage ways. The company sealed the grates years ago, but the smugglers unseal them every season because the company forgets everything it isn't currently stealing." He almost smiles at that, then doesn't. "Third grate past the smokehouse. There's a wedge cut into the frame — you'll feel it. Follow the water downhill. It lets out behind the tannery, and I'd recommend you don't stop to admire the view, because the tannery view is why nobody lingers there."

"Joren." I take his wrist. His pulse is going like a gull's heart. "They'll know. When they find the cell empty, they'll know it was you."

"Not if the cell wasn't escaped." He produces, from under the cloak, a set of spare prisoner's slops, gray and foul, and a coil of rope. "You're going to leave your slops folded on the shelf, and I'm going to leave a length of rope knotted to the bar grille, and Brakk — who is genuinely sick, I made sure of it — will swear tomorrow morning that he saw a rope on the bars during evening rounds and assumed the night man had checked it. Climbed out the high vent. Very daring. No help from anyone."

"That's a stupid plan."

"It's a plan that keeps my neck on my shoulders, which is my primary criterion for plans."

"It's a stupid plan," I repeat, "and I'm grateful for every stupid inch of it." I don't let go of his wrist yet. "When the magistrate sits — and he's coming, within days — there'll be a reckoning. Names read out. Debts answered. When that happens, Petra gets to stand up and be counted. You make sure she knows that. Her signature on nothing she agreed to ever again."

His ears go red. Even in the near-dark, I can tell. Some men blush with their whole lives.

"Go," he says. "Downhill. Don't drown. It would embarrass us both."

* * *

The vent is exactly as stupid as promised — narrow, rusted, and above my head, which Joren solves by dragging a crate over and looking elsewhere while I climb it in a manner no lady net-mender should describe in company. Six days of stone shelves have left me the flexibility of a mast, but fear is an excellent liniment. I get my shoulders through, then my hips, then I am in the crawl between the jail roof and the warehouse eaves, breathing fish-smell and freedom, both of which are overrated but welcome.

From there it's drop and roll onto the packing-yard mud, then the long hunched walk between stacks of barrels that smell of the Pearlhouse's funeral — brine-cured herring, hundreds of them, and somewhere among those barrels, once, an oilskin packet hid from searching hands. The world turns on stranger hinges than we give it credit for.

The fish market at midnight is a city of bones. Guts and scales swept into heaps, gulls arguing in their sleep on the ridgepoles, tables bleached pale by decades of fish laid out like a congregation waiting for a sermon that never comes. And beneath it, behind the smokehouse, the third grate, with its wedge cut into the frame — Joren's information good to the last detail.

The grate swings on a hinge someone maintains with love. Below it, iron rungs, and below those, the tunnels.

I have mended nets in the dark by feel for thirty years. I know what it is to trust your hands when your eyes are useless. But the tunnel tests even that. The water runs ankle-deep and cold as a creditor's greeting, the ceiling sweats, and every few yards there's a junction where the black goes three directions at once. Downhill, Joren said, and I put my hand to the water and follow the pull of it, and I think — this is what it is to be a diver's child, this is what Lior knows in the marrow — that the sea gets into everyone eventually, even underground, even in a jailbreak, calling the water home.

I count my paces because counting is prayer for practical people. At four hundred, the tunnel forks around a fallen beam, and wedged in the fork, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, is a stub of candle and a striker, left by whoever maintains that loving hinge. Smugglers. Dagny, I tell myself, you are being smuggled. At your age. With your knees. There are worse fates.

I don't light the candle. Light travels in tunnels like gossip in a small town. I save it, and I keep walking, and somewhere in the dark I start talking to Torvald, the way I do when the work is long. Telling him about the vault. Two on the door till middle watch. One after. The relief late by a quarter hour, twenty if the dice run kind. You'd have liked this, brother, I tell him. All your grievances grown up at last into something with teeth.

At seven hundred paces the air changes — tannin and lime and the particular rotten-egg frankness of the tannery — and ahead, a coin of gray light. The outlet. Beyond it, the town, the scattered Pearlhouse, Lior and Sefa and Mama Osk and Hale, and a plan with a hole in it shaped like a guard rotation that I am about to fill.

* * *

Coming up out of the ground behind the tannery is like being born ugly: necessary, undignified, effective. I surface into weeds and old lime pits, take my bearings by the harbor bell buoy — still there, still tolling its idiot note — and cut uphill along the drainage ditch with my borrowed cloak pulled tight.

The smugglers' cellar takes some finding in the dark, but my feet remember what my eyes can't: the leaning chimney, the gate with the sheep-knot tied in its latch-string, the cellar steps that descend into warmth and lamplight and the smell of people I would tear the company apart with my bare hands to protect.

Mama Osk sees me first. She is mending — of course she is mending, the woman would mend in a house fire — and her needle stops mid-stitch, and her face does not perform surprise because Osk considers performance a form of theft.

"Well," she says. "The jail's standards have slipped."

"They were always generous," I say. "They let me go."

Chairs scrape. Lior is on their feet before I'm through the doorway, eyes wide, and Sefa beside them with a knife already palmed and already lowered once she sees my face. Corvin Hale rises more slowly, the way men rise when they understand the weight of news before they've heard it. Young Pip, one of the orphan divers, just stares and stares and then says, "You smell like the underneath of the fish market," which is the most accurate report anyone will give me all night.

"Two things," I say, sitting down on the offered crate because my knees have submitted their resignation and I am accepting it with dignity. "First: I'm out, they think I climbed out the vent alone, and the turnkey who opened my door has a sister whose contract was rewritten behind her, and he'd like the world to answer for it. Write his name down. Joren Vask. He's one of ours now, whether he likes it or not."

Lior is already scratching on scrap paper. Good clerk. Terrible criminal. Best ally I've ever had.

"Second thing." I lean forward, and the room leans with me, the whole ragged family of it, drawn in like a net closing. "The Company House vault. Two guards on the strong-room door until the middle watch. Then one, while the other walks the upper floors. The relief comes late — a quarter hour, sometimes twenty minutes, because they shoot dice in the stairwell first. That's the gap. That's when the second ledger can come out of that vault, and not one minute either side of it."

Silence, but the working kind. I watch Lior's lips move, filing numbers. I watch Hale's eyes go distant, running the arithmetic of risk the way he runs all arithmetic — reluctantly, honestly, and all the way to the end. I watch Sefa look at Lior, and I watch Lior look back, and I think: there it is. Whatever those two are to each other, the next few days will name it.

"One more thing," I add, because debts should be paid in full. "Joren says the company means to move the ledgers to the capital. He didn't have a date. But Grell doesn't wait well, and the magistrate's coming, and every hour we spend being careful is an hour Grell spends being quick."

Mama Osk ties off her stitch, snaps the thread with her teeth, and folds her mending with the finality of a woman closing a ledger of her own.

"Then we'd best stop being careful," she says, "and start being ready."

Above us, through the floorboards, the harbor bell buoys ring on in the dark — and somewhere across the sleeping town, in a vault with one tired guard and dice rattling in a stairwell, a book full of stolen names waits for someone brave enough and foolish enough to come collect it.

I put my hand flat on the table, palm down, the way I set my hand to a net before the haul.

"So," I say. "Tell me the plan."
