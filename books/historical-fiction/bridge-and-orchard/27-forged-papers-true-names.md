## 27. Forged Papers, True Names

The smugglers' cellar smelled of lamp oil, brine, and nine years of other people's secrets, and I had come to add mine to the collection.

Not mine, precisely. A clerk's number—C-4471, Halvard Trading Company, indentured until majority—which was what I had been instead of a name for most of my remembered life. But we would get to that. Everything in this cellar, lately, got to that.

I had commandeered the dry corner near the coal chute, where Dagny's brother Renn kept his "accounts," which was a word he used loosely and I used like a knife. Three crates upended for a desk. A stub of tallow candle. The company's transit papers spread flat under my palms, genuine ones, lifted from a drunk factor's assistant two nights back, and beside them my working materials: scrap vellum cut from an old manifest's backing, a pen knife worn to a sliver, a stick of sealing wax the color of dried blood, and a pearl diver's knife that had once belonged to my mother.

It was the knife that made the seal possible. That was the joke nobody but me would ever hear: the company's great security, the sigil of the Halvard Trading Company pressed into every document that moved through Vessing like water through gills, defeated by a diving blade and a patient hand. Grell could keep his guards and his checkpoints and his hunters on the Salt Road. He could not keep out the fact that I had been copying official documents since I was twelve years old, and that a copyist who pays attention learns everything.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about forgery: it is mostly bookkeeping.

Anyone can carve a fake seal. What defeats a checkpoint guard is not the seal itself but the paper aging correctly, the ink matching the batch, the entry numbers falling into sequence with the real registers, the date written in a hand that slants the way tired clerks slant at the end of a long shift rather than the way forgers slant, which is too careful. Guards do not examine seals. They examine boredom. They look for the document that is trying too hard, the one without coffee stains, without the small honest errors of haste. A perfect forgery is the loudest thing in the world. A *believable* one has to be imperfect in all the right places.

So I began, as I begin everything, with the ledgers.

Renn thought I was mad when I asked him to describe, in detail, every cargo that had passed the Tide Gate in the last six weeks. He sat on an overturned barrel with his arms crossed—a big man gone soft in the middle, hands like knotted rope—and recited from memory while I built a false register in my head: which shipments had legitimate transit papers already issued, which numbers were used, which gaps existed in the sequence. Because that was the trick. You didn't invent a document from nothing. You found the place where the real paperwork had a hole, and you grew your forgery in it like a pearl around a grit of truth.

"There," I said, tapping the mental ledger. "Shipment 8841. Oyster shell, bound for the kilns inland. Papers were requested and never collected—the factor's assistant drowned, did you know that? Fell off the pier sober, which around here is practically suicide."

"Tragic," Renn said, in a voice suggesting he'd helped.

"That means there is a requisition logged, a fee paid, and no papers issued. If anyone checks—and they won't, because checking means work—the record will show the papers were lost in transit. Which they technically were." I looked up. "I'm going to lose them in transit again. Into our pockets."

Dagny, mending a net by candlelight even down here in a cellar where no net would ever be needed, snorted. "You've been spending too much time with Hale. That was almost legal reasoning."

"Hale would have made it boring. That's how you know he's reformed."

But my hands weren't steady when I said it, and Dagny saw. Dagny always saw. She put down the net and came over and stood behind my shoulder the way she used to stand behind the orphans learning their knots, and she said nothing at all, which was her version of a benediction.

Because here was the thing none of them had said aloud: if this went wrong—if a guard looked twice at shipment 8841, if Grell's description of me had reached the tollhouse keepers, if anything in the whole fragile architecture of lies collapsed—we wouldn't all be punished equally. Sefa would hang for certain. Hale was already a dead man walking. And I would go back to being C-4471, except companies don't keep clerks who forge documents; they keep examples of them, hung at the Tide Gate where the tide can lift their coat tails twice a day as a lesson to the others.

And now the company knew about Dagny. Knew about Mama Osk. The Pearlhouse had burned, and the people who had voted to shelter a half-drowned stranger had paid for that vote in smoke and bruises, and here I was asking them to stake the remainder on my handwriting.

"You're doing the thing," Dagny said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you apologize with your face and don't use words. Your mother did it too. It's very annoying."

I hadn't known that. One more thing Osk and her stories kept handing me, pieces of a woman I'd built out of salt water and guesswork. I bent over the vellum.

* * *

The seal took four attempts.

The first was too clean—I pressed the heated knife spine into the wax with a diver's patience and produced a Halvard crest so crisp it looked minted, and I scraped it off the practice board immediately because no seal in Vessing had ever looked like that. Real seals are applied by tired men in a hurry, over ink that isn't fully dry, at angles that drift. The second attempt I roughed up deliberately, and it looked damaged instead of human. The third cracked.

The fourth, I closed my eyes first.

This is going to sound like superstition and I don't care. I closed my eyes and I thought about the counting-house on a Friday evening, about old Master Pellin with his ink-stained cuffs pressing the great brass matrix into wax while complaining about his knees, about the exact weary twist of his wrist on the downstroke, the little drag at the finish where he was already reaching for the next document. I had watched that wrist for nine years. Nine years of making myself unremarkable, of standing in corners and being furniture—and furniture sees everything. The company taught me invisibility so thoroughly that they never noticed they'd also taught me observation. Every survival skill they gave me, they gave me by accident.

I pressed. Twisted. Dragged.

When I opened my eyes, the wax held a Halvard crest that had been placed there by a man with sore knees on a Friday evening, and I felt sick, and I felt magnificent, and both feelings were completely sincere.

"Sefa," I called toward the dark end of the cellar. "Come look at your own doom."

She came out of the shadows the way she always did, quiet, watchful, one hand near the knife she carried the way I carried guilt—always, everywhere, touching it to be sure. She was healing slow. The wound on her shoulder from the harbor chase had scabbed and split twice, and she refused to favor it, and every time she forgot and reached with that arm I saw her flinch and pretend she hadn't. We had stopped commenting on each other's pretenses somewhere around the oyster beds. It was a kind of courtesy.

She leaned over the wax. Her face did something complicated.

"It's wrong," she said.

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the mud, all the way to the harbor. "*Wrong*—"

"The crest. Look." She pointed with a fingertip, not touching. "The anchor in the center. On the real seal, the shank crosses the ring at the third link of the chain. Yours crosses at the fourth."

I stared at it. Four links. I counted them three times. Four links, and my entire body went cold, because I had copied that crest hundreds of times in the margins of manifests, and I had copied it *wrong* hundreds of times, because the original documents came to the copying room already sealed and I'd been reproducing the image from memory of other copies—

"No," I said slowly. "No, wait. Wait." I grabbed her wrist, gentler than my urgency deserved. "Sefa. The copying room. Clerks copy the crest from *other copies*. If one early copyist made the same error—" I was already digging through Renn's crate of salvaged papers, and there, a month-old delivery note, genuine seal, and I held it to the candle with my forged one beside it, and there they both were: third link, fourth link. Two generations of the same mistake.

Sefa started laughing. It turned into coughing, because her ribs were still bad, and she sat down hard on a crate still laughing in a wheezing, painful, delighted way, and I realized I was laughing too, half-hysterical, both of us bent over a forged seal in a smuggler's cellar like a pair of drunks.

"The company's own security mark," she managed, "is a copy of a copy of a mistake—"

"Aldric Vane should see it," I said, and then heard myself say it, and the laughter died in the air between us.

That was still a tender subject. She meant to reach her father after the magistrate—after the circuit, after the reading, after whatever was left of any of us. I meant the evidence to land and nothing more; I had told her once that my mission was paper, not people, and she had looked at me with something like pity and said, *Lior. Paper is made of people.* I hadn't forgiven her for being right.

"He'd appreciate it," she said quietly. "He loved finding errors in things. Said an error was just the truth leaving fingerprints."

"Your father sounds exhausting."

"He is." She said *is*, firmly, present tense, and I let it stand.

* * *

Then there was only the name left to write.

The transit papers required, in the final box, the name of the authorized bearer. Every document I had signed in nine years had been signed C-4471. Contracts, receipts, manifests, the receipt for my own mother's burial plot—company number, company number, company number, a little grid of characters where a person should be. I had written it so often I could do it in the dark, and I had done it in the dark, in the counting-house, nights when the lamps guttered and my hand moved on its own while my mind floated somewhere above the harbor.

I dipped the pen.

And stopped.

Because here was the trap I hadn't anticipated, the one no one warns you about: I could forge Aldric Vane's seal. I could forge Master Pellin's weary wrist. I could counterfeit the entire apparatus of the Halvard Trading Company, because I had spent nine years studying it the way a prisoner studies walls. But when I went to write my own name, my hand didn't know what it looked like.

Lior Nystrom. Two words. I had spoken them maybe a dozen times in nine years, mostly to myself, quietly, the way you touch a scar. My mother's surname. Osk had said it to me a week ago across a burnt ruin of a kitchen—*your mother dove beside me, Lior Nystrom's daughter, and the company wrote your indenture strange on purpose*—and hearing it in someone else's mouth had been like surfacing too fast.

But speaking a name and signing it are different animals. A signature is a confession of the hand. And my hand had only ever confessed to being property.

"What's wrong?" Sefa asked.

"I don't know what my handwriting looks like."

She considered that with the seriousness she gave everything. "Write it anyway. However it comes out. That's the point of a name—it's yours before you're good at it."

Which was either very wise or complete nonsense, and I didn't have time to determine which, because dawn was coming and the papers had to reach the Tollhouse contact by the morning skiff, and so I wrote:

*Lior Nystrom.*

It came out crooked. The capital N leaned like a man against a wind, the y descended too far, the whole signature slanted the wrong direction from every document I'd ever produced, because those had all been written in the trained company crouch, upright, obedient, identical to ten thousand other clerks' hands. This was none of those things. This was the handwriting of somebody who had never been taught how to be themselves and was doing it anyway, badly, in ink.

It was the worst forgery in the cellar and the only true thing on the page.

Sefa read it upside down, as couriers do. "There," she said softly. "Now the deception is complete. Everything false on this paper exists to carry one true line across the Salt Road." She tapped the crooked signature. "Grell can check every seal, every register, every number. He'll find us flawless everywhere except here. And here—" her finger rested on my name "—here he'll find nothing to compare it against, because no one ever wrote it before."

* * *

Renn took the papers at first light, wrapped in oilskin inside a hollow bollard of dried cod, because smugglers believe sincerely that no inspector in history has willingly opened dried cod, and so far the evidence supports them.

I stood at the cellar door and watched him go up the steps into the grey morning, and I thought about how strange it was that everything—Pearlhouse, ledger, magistrate, my mother's pearl lying in some smuggler's strongbox as payment—now depended on a clerk's ability to lie beautifully and one true crooked word.

Mama Osk found me there. She didn't say anything for a while. She was carrying a kettle that had survived the fire, dented, blackened, still in service, which seemed right.

"You signed it," she said.

"You've seen it?"

"Dagny showed me. She was proud. Don't tell her I said so." She poured tea into a tin cup I hadn't noticed her holding and pushed it into my hands. "Your mother signed her diver's registry the same way. Crooked N. Leaned into the wind."

I drank the tea so I wouldn't have to answer, which she knew, which was why she'd given it to me.

Above, in the street, the town was waking. Somewhere past the rooftops, the magistrate's tents were rising in the market square; somewhere inland, Hale was deciding whether to live long enough to testify; somewhere under the harbor, in wax and darkness, a stolen ledger waited for slack tide and my lungs. Ten days had become four. Four would become one.

And then, from the top of the stairs, Renn's voice came back down—too casual, which from Renn meant trouble:

"Lior. Better get up here. There's a notice nailed to the harbor post. New one, this morning's ink." A pause, the particular pause of a man reading something twice. "They've announced a reward for information regarding a runaway clerk. And they've printed a name."

The cup was warm in my hands. Nine years, and they had finally, accidentally, given me the one thing the company never had.

They knew my name.

"They've spelled it wrong," Renn called down. "Nystrom with two s's. Somebody in that counting-house has worse handwriting than you."
