## 28. Night Before

The smugglers' cellar smelled of tar, damp stone, and forty years of men deciding not to ask questions. It was, I decided, the most honest room in Vessing. Every other room in this town pretended to be something it wasn't—a counting-house pretending to be arithmetic, a company house pretending to be a home. This cellar made no such claims. It was a hole under a fish-smoker's shed where contraband went to wait out the law, and it welcomed us with the indifference of a place that had seen worse liars than a courier, a clerk, an auditor, and a net-mender with a fresh escape bruise on her wrist.

Dagny had lit three lamps, which was two more than prudence allowed. When I raised an eyebrow at her, she shrugged.

"If they're coming for us tomorrow anyway," she said, "I'd rather see their faces."

Mama Osk sat nearest the stove with her hands wrapped around a mug of barley tea she hadn't drunk. Her ribs were still strapped from the beating she'd taken three weeks ago, and she moved the way old divers do when the cold has gotten into them—slowly, deliberately, as if the air itself were thick water. She had insisted on coming. Nobody had argued. You do not argue with Mama Osk about where she will and will not be; you simply adjust your plans to include her, the way sailors adjust for tide.

Lior arrived last, carrying a canvas satchel against their chest like a child. They paused in the doorway to check the street behind them—old habit, though the habit was only weeks old—and I watched the lamplight catch the scar at their collarbone, the diver's scar they used to hide under high-buttoned collars back when I first met them dragging me out of the harbor. They didn't hide it anymore. Small things, by now, counted as victories.

"Well," Lior said, setting the satchel on the barrel that served as our table. "Everyone who's about to commit crimes against the Halvard Trading Company is here."

"We prefer *irregulars*," Dagny said.

"We prefer *family*," Osk said, without looking up from her tea, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has said the true thing.

Hale cleared his throat. He had claimed the corner farthest from the stairs, some auditor's instinct about sight lines and exits, and he'd brought his testimony written out fair-copy in a hand so neat it looked printed. He kept touching the pages. I had begun to understand it was not nervousness. It was penance. The man touched his own words the way other men touch relics.

"Order of operations," Hale said, because he was the sort of man who believed that if you arranged a thing correctly enough, the world would consent to behave. "At dawn the circuit sits in the market square. Sefa speaks first—"

"I know," I said.

"—then Lior produces the ledger—"

"I *know*, Corvin."

He blinked at me. I realized my voice had come out sharper than I'd intended, and that the sharpness was fear wearing my mouth like a mask. I apologized with a shrug, which he accepted with a nod, and we resumed being conspirators instead of frightened people pretending at procedure.

The truth was I had not slept properly in six nights, and the truth beneath that truth was that tomorrow I intended to stand in front of a magistrate and say my father's name aloud for the first time since I was eleven years old. Aldric Vane. Drowned man. Exile. Fraud, according to the record; ghost, according to the town; father, according to a packet of letters sewn into oilskin that had crossed half a coastline pressed against my ribs. Tomorrow the letters would stop being evidence and start being what they had always been: a daughter carrying her father's voice into a room full of strangers and asking them to believe she knew it when she heard it.

Lior caught my eye across the cellar. They always knew. It was frankly irritating, how well they'd learned to read me, considering we'd met while I was unconscious and leaking seawater onto their floor.

"All right," Lior said gently. "One last time. The seals."

They opened the satchel. Inside lay everything we were: Vane's letters in their oilskin, the forged transit papers with their wax seals still faintly warm-looking, the second ledger wrapped in sailcloth and smelling of brine and oyster beds, and Hale's stolen credentials with the ink altered in four careful places. Lior lifted one of the forged seals to the lamp and turned it, and the light moved across the impression of the company's bridge-and-pearl device, and I saw them do the thing they did—the small exhale through the nose, the flicker of pride immediately drowned in terror, the clerk's soul briefly visible in a face that had learned invisibility as a survival skill.

"Dagny's knife-work or mine?" Dagny asked.

"The third one's yours." Lior passed it over. "You cut deeper than I do. Seals are supposed to look *stamped*, not carved."

"That's what my brother said about fish prices," Dagny said, examining the wax. "Everything worth forging looks honest until you press hard."

Her brother. I had heard the story by now, twice around supper fires and once in the dark of a storm watch: how Rennik Dagny had held a salvage license the company wanted, how the license had developed irregularities, how Rennik had developed debts, and how the whole family had developed a grudge with the longevity of oak. Dagny mended nets by day and ran whatever the harbor needed run by night, and she had never once in my hearing called it smuggling. She called it *the family trade*. Grudges, in Vessing, were inheritable property, and she carried hers openly the way other women wore lockets.

Osk finally drank her tea. Then she said, in the voice she used for things that were not negotiable:

"Before we rehearse again. Each of us says what they're afraid to lose. All of it, out loud, tonight, so it doesn't come up tomorrow shaking."

"That's not standard preparation," Hale began.

"Corvin." She looked at him over the rim of the mug. "You've been preparing papers for seven years. Papers don't walk into a square. People do. People shake. We pour the shaking out now."

And that—that was Mama Osk, who had taken a beating meant for questions and answered none of them, prescribing confession like a remedy, because she had spent thirty years running a dockside house where drowning children came to remember they could breathe.

Nobody argued. That was the thing about the Pearlhouse, even scattered, even burned: when Osk asked, you gave.

* * *

She started with herself, because she would never ask what she wouldn't do first.

"I'm afraid to lose the house again," she said. "It burned once on my account already. Bricks can be rebuilt—I know that better than anyone, I've done it—but the *records* of who slept there, the marks on the doorframe where the little ones got measured every solstice, those don't come back. Tomorrow, if it goes wrong, they won't just take us. They'll take the proof that any of those children were ever loved under that roof." She set the mug down with great care. "There. Poured."

Dagny went next, turning the forged seal in her fingers like she was checking a knot.

"My brother's out past the Salt Road with the inland runners," she said. "If Grell needs hostages, Rennik's the obvious one. I'm afraid I'll hear his name read out in some company jail log and know it was the price of my choices." A pause. "Also I'm afraid of heights and tomorrow involves standing taller than I'd like in front of mounted men. But that's secondary."

Hale took longer. He smoothed his testimony pages twice before he spoke, and when he did, his voice had the flatness of a man reading numbers he wished were different.

"I audited the child-contract transfers nine years ago," he said. "I saw the entries. I told myself they were reassignments, administrative relabeling. I signed off. My fear—" He stopped. Started again. "My fear is that it's too late for me to lose anything, because I lost it already, the day I looked away, and everything since has been interest accruing on that debt. And underneath that—" the flatness cracked, just slightly— "underneath that, I'm afraid that if this fails, no one will ever know I tried. That I'll die in the ledger of my own life as a line item marked *paid in full* when the account never balanced."

The cellar was very quiet. Even the tar smell seemed to hold still.

Lior spoke last of the adults, and they did it the way they did everything—not dramatically, but precisely, with the economy of someone who had learned that attention was expensive and spent it carefully.

"For nine years my entire strategy was to not exist," they said. "Don't reconcile the column. Don't ask about the scar. Don't be worth noticing. It worked. It kept me alive and it cost me—" they gestured vaguely at the satchel, at the forged papers bearing their own name signed in their own hand for the first time— "everything else. So tomorrow I'm afraid of the opposite of what you'd expect. I'm not afraid of losing my safety. I'm afraid of losing *this*"—they touched the satchel— "before it lands. Because for the first time in my life I want something loudly enough that wanting it feels dangerous."

Then they looked at me, and everyone looked at me, and I understood it was my turn whether I wanted it or not.

What was I afraid to lose?

I could have said the letters. True, but easy—I'd been afraid of losing those since the day hunters first put my description on a tollhouse wall. I could have said my father, whom I'd already lost twice, once to the sea and once to the lie. Also true. Also a dodge.

"Myself," I said finally, and heard how it sounded, and let it sound that way. "Tomorrow I say my real name in public for the first time since I was a child. Sefa Vane. Not the courier. Not the woman who washed up under the pilings. If I say it and the court doesn't believe me—if they rule him a fraud and me a liar—then that name goes back in the water and I don't get to take it out again. I've been nobody for eleven years, and nobody is *safe*, and I am terrified down to the bone of standing up and being somebody and having the world say *no, you aren't.*"

I expected comfort. What I got was better: Osk reached over and gripped my wrist, diver to diver, the grip you use underwater to say *I have you* without words.

"You were somebody," she said, "the night you nearly drowned getting those letters to us. The world just hasn't caught up yet."

* * *

We rehearsed after that, because grief poured out leaves room for procedure, which I suspect was Osk's design all along.

Hale walked us through his testimony: the double entry, the vault ledger, the transfer authorizations for indentured contracts routed through the Meridian office under labels like *special labor placement*. He recited figures the way other men swear oaths, and I watched Lior check each one against memory, nodding, their lips moving faintly—they had memorized the columns too, all of them, in case the physical ledgers were seized and only heads remained. The company had taught them numbers as chains. Somewhere along the way the chains had become weapons. There was probably a sermon in that, if either of us were the sermonizing kind.

Dagny laid out the morning's logistics on the barrel top with pebbles and a fish bone: the market square, the magistrate's platform, the company guards' rotation, the two alleys, the route to the harbor if everything failed at once. She placed the fish bone at the oyster beds.

"If it goes wrong," she said, "this is where we scatter to. Sefa takes the water lane north. Lior—"

"Lior stays with the ledger," Lior and I said together, and then glared at each other, because we'd had this argument four times and neither of us had won it, which meant both of us had.

"The ledger goes in the water if it must," Lior said. "Waxed. Weighted. I marked the spot. The sea kept our family's secrets before; it can keep ours."

"*Our* family," I said, before I could stop myself.

Lior looked at me. Something crossed their face—quick, unguarded, gone—and they said, "Well. You did bleed on my floor. Around here that's practically adoption papers."

Dagny barked a laugh. Even Osk smiled into her tea. And I felt it again, that vertiginous thing I'd been feeling for weeks now: the strange weight of belonging to people, which is heavier than belonging to nothing, and worth it, and terrifying, exactly like everything else I'd chosen lately.

We ate smoked fish and hard bread. Dagny told a long, filthy story about a customs officer and a crate of what he thought was brandy. Hale laughed twice—actually laughed, a rusty sound like a gate opening after years—and looked startled at himself both times. Osk measured the children of the operation with her eyes, meaning me and Lior, and found us, if not well, then fed.

Near midnight, the others began to settle. Dagny took first watch at the stair. Hale fell asleep sitting up, his testimony pages still in his hand, and Dagny draped a tarpaulin over him with a gentleness she'd deny under oath.

That left Lior and me awake by the stove, and Lior doing the thing with the seals again, practicing the press-and-turn one last time on scrap wax, over and over, perfect each time, still repeating.

"They'll work," I said quietly.

"Wax doesn't worry me." They set down the seal. In the low light their face was all planes and shadows and that stubborn, quiet fire I'd first mistaken for meekness. "It's the part after. Everyone keeps talking about tomorrow like it ends something. The reading. The verdict. Like the truth lands and the story closes." They turned the scrap of wax over in their fingers. "But stories don't close. The names in that ledger—those kids are scattered across three coastlines. Verdicts don't fetch anybody home. Somebody has to go find them. After."

I understood then what they weren't quite saying, and it landed in my chest like a dropped anchor.

"You're thinking about going," I said. "After. To find them."

"Somebody who knows the ledgers should." A pause. The wax turned over again. "And somebody should help you find your father. If you'll have the company."

I thought about correcting *company* to *family*, and decided some words shouldn't be spent cheaply at midnight. Instead I leaned my shoulder against theirs, and we sat like that, two orphans of different storms watching the stove breathe, and I thought: whatever happens in that square, I have already gotten back more than the letters. I have gotten *this*.

I slept maybe two hours, dreamless, the letters under my head like a pillow stuffed with my own heart.

* * *

Dagny woke us before the gulls did, which is to say, in darkness, which is to say, wrong.

She stood at the foot of the stair with the lamp turned low, and her face told me before her mouth did. When her mouth did, it was worse than her face.

"Turnkey's boy just came through the fish-market tunnel," she said. "Grell moved in the night. The magistrate's platform is being torn down—'structural concerns,' officially. The circuit's been invited to sit at the Company House instead." She swallowed. "Company grounds. Company guards. Company walls. He wants the court inside the vault, so to speak."

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Osk rose from her cot, slow as deep water, and began strapping her ribs with the unhurried competence of a woman dressing for ordinary work, and said:

"Then we'd best not be late."
