## 29. Checkpoint Three: The Company House

There is a particular way a clerk walks when they've been summoned. Not hurried—hurrying invites questions—but not strolling either, because nobody strolls toward the counting-house unless they're rich enough to be insane. It's a walk I've spent nine years perfecting: shoulders level, eyes forward but not fixed, hands carrying something official-looking. Tonight the something official-looking is a leather folio with Hale's stolen credentials inside and my heart hammering against it like a fish in a bucket.

I'd practiced the walk on the way from the smugglers' cellar, past the shuttered chandlery, through the fog that Vessing lays down over its streets this time of year like a mother pulling blankets over a child's head. The fog was on our side. Most of Vessing was on our side, if it only knew it. The trouble was that the one thing not on our side was the building I was walking toward, and it happened to be the biggest thing in town.

The Company House had been built by men who wanted the harbor to know who owned it. Three stories of grey stone, pillars out front carved to look like they were holding up the sky, windows tall enough to dive through if you were foolish enough. As a child I'd stood across the street once—I was maybe eight, still diving, still free in the way that meant poor instead of bound—and watched Aldric Vane come down those steps into his carriage. He'd looked at the crowd of dockworkers the way a man looks at weather he's decided not to think about.

Seven years dead, officially. Seven years writing letters from wherever he'd gone to be dead in peace.

*Just a clerk*, I told myself, adjusting the folio under my arm. *Summoned for the quarter-close audit preparation. You've done this walk a hundred times.*

Except I hadn't. That was the problem. In nine years of indenture, I had never once entered the Company House after dark. Daytime, yes—clerks are furniture during the day, moved from office to office as needed, noticed exactly as much as furniture is noticed. But night changed the arithmetic. Night meant guards who didn't expect me, a factor whose habits I could only guess at, and a vault that opened for exactly two sets of papers: the factor's own, and the credentials of a senior auditor summoned from another port.

Hale's credentials said the second thing. Whether Vessing believed the first thing was about to be decided in the next quarter hour, and no amount of practice walks would change it.

* *

The side door was where Dagny's turnkey friend said it would be—the clerks' entrance, off the alley where the oyster carts unloaded at dawn. A single lantern burned above it. I stopped in the shadow of the cart-ramp and did what I'd rehearsed until Sefa threatened to smother me with a net: I became the number again.

Not Lior Nystrom, diver's child, orphan, person with a scar on the palm from a shell edge and opinions and a mother's pearl traded away three nights ago to men who smelled of tar. Clerk 4471, Halvard Trading Company, indentured, summoned. The number was safe. The number had walked these streets without being seen for nine years. I put the number on like a coat and stepped into the lantern light.

The door guard was young, which was either very good or very bad. Young guards either follow procedure to the letter or ignore it entirely, and you don't know which until it's too late.

"Papers."

I handed him the summons first—forged by my own hand, seals pressed from wax and a diver's knife, the finest work I've ever done and will never admit to—and kept Hale's credential folio tucked under my arm, because auditors' credentials are shown inside, not outside. Procedure. I knew their procedure better than they did; I'd copied it often enough.

He read the summons slowly. I counted my breaths the way Mama Osk taught the little ones to count waves—*in while it rises, hold while it curls, out while it breaks*. Somewhere behind that grey stone façade was a vault, and in the vault was a ledger with names in it. Children's names. Possibly mine.

"Quarter-close," the guard said, as if reminding himself. "You're late."

"The tide delayed the ferry from Saltrow." There was no ferry from Saltrow. There was barely a Saltrow. But he wouldn't know that, and I delivered the lie with the flat boredom of a man who has been inconvenienced by tides all his professional life, which, in a sense, I have been.

"Counting-room's on the second floor. Factor Grell's still in the building, so mind your step."

My lungs performed the wave-counting exercise with great discipline while my mouth said, "Thank you."

Grell. Still in the building. This was not in the plan. The plan, drafted in a cellar that smelled of brine and smugglers, had assumed the factor would be home drinking the good brandy that appeared on no manifest anywhere, because I had personally checked every manifest for six years. The plan had not accounted for Grell working late, seven days before a magistrate arrived who might ask inconvenient questions.

Then again—maybe that was exactly why he was working late. A man doesn't burn the midnight oil over honest books.

"Right," I said, and went in before my face could betray anything, because faces are for people and I was currently a number.

* *

Inside, the Company House at night smelled different than by day. By day it's ink, wax, cold tea, the sourness of men who eat lunch at their desks. By night it was lamp oil and stone and something underneath—salt, maybe, or old money rotting politely. My footsteps on the stairs sounded enormous. I made them smaller.

Second floor. The counting-room doors stood open, lamplight spilling out across the corridor in long gold bars. I flattened against the wall and listened: the scratch of a pen. One pen. One man.

The vault was supposed to be beyond the counting-room—a door of banded iron set into the stone, older than the rest of the fittings, because the company had built its offices around the thing it actually valued. Everything else was furniture. The vault was the company.

I waited through four more scratches of the pen, then risked a look.

Factor Grell sat at the head of the long table with his back half-turned to me, a heavyset silhouette bent over documents, rings on his fingers catching the lamplight. On the table around him: papers. Many papers. And beside them, an iron-bound strongbox, lid open, empty.

An empty strongbox and a man moving papers at midnight, seven days before the circuit sits.

I didn't need Hale's arithmetic to do that sum. They were preparing to move things. Or burn them. Hale's coded warning had said the ledgers would go to the capital before the magistrate arrived—*move the originals before anyone can demand them*. Grell wasn't working late. Grell was packing evidence for shipment.

Which meant the second ledger might not even be in the vault anymore.

My stomach dropped through all nine years of careful invisibility and kept going. But there was nothing to do but go forward, because going back meant everything—Osk's bruises, Dagny's cell, the Pearlhouse in ashes, Sefa bleeding on a rooftop—meant all of it had bought nothing.

So: the plan, amended. If the vault was empty, I'd find out what was in the strongbox instead. A clerk carries things. Nobody questions a clerk leaving with a folio, because clerks are always carrying folios, in and out, day after day, invisible as furniture. The whole architecture of my life had accidentally prepared me for theft. There's a joke in that, and someday, if we survive, someone might tell it.

I took a breath, put on the bored expression, and walked into the counting-room.

* *

"Factor Grell." I pitched my voice at the exact register of a man announcing a minor inconvenience. "Clerk 4471, summoned for the quarter-close preparation. Apologies for the hour—tide delayed the Saltrow ferry."

Grell didn't look up right away. That scratch of the pen finished whatever figure it was making, deliberately, the way a man finishes a sentence before answering a question he doesn't like. Then he raised his head, and I got the full weight of his attention, and understood why people brought him things he asked for.

He had the kind of face that had been chosen rather than grown—broad, smooth, unremarkable except for the eyes, which did all the work of noticing so the rest of him could afford to look idle. Rings on every finger but the writing hand. I catalogued them automatically, the way I catalogued everything: garnet, garnet, signet, pearl.

A pearl. Of course. Probably fished out of some diver's catch for a tenth of its worth, worn as decoration by the man whose ledgers recorded it twice.

"Clerk." His voice was mild. Mild as a knife laid flat on a table. "From which office?"

"Reconciliation, sir. Third floor annex." Also fictional. Also plausible; the annex existed, though its duties were vague even to the people who worked in it, which I know because I once spent three weeks copying its correspondence and concluded that its primary function was generating paper to justify its ink budget.

"And you were summoned by?"

"Senior Auditor Hale's office. His credentials—" I lifted the folio a precise two inches, no more. Auditors' credentials are shown when requested, not offered. Offering is eagerness. Eagerness is memorable. "—are to be verified at the vault, per procedure."

Something moved behind the idle face. Not alarm—interest. The interest of a man turning over a card to see whether it helps him or ruins him.

"Hale." He said the name the way you'd spit out a bone. "His office sends a clerk at this hour."

"The quarter-close waits for no one, sir." I let a fraction of weary solidarity into my voice—the universal bond of underlings ground beneath procedure. "You'll forgive me, I've copies to make before dawn regardless."

He studied me. I studied the middle distance over his shoulder, where a framed charter hung, and thought about nothing visible at all.

"Open the vault, then," Grell said, rising, pocketing his pen with terrible casualness. "I'll accompany you. The auditor's credentials should be witnessed."

Every plan has a moment where it stops being a plan and starts being a fall you haven't hit the bottom of yet. This was mine. Grell accompanying me meant Grell watching Hale's credentials—which were stolen, which were proof of everything, which would hang us all if he read them closely. Hale had warned me: *the factor knows my face and knows I'm watched. Those papers are only good at arm's length.* Arm's length. Grell intended to be considerably closer than that.

"Yes, sir," said the number, and followed him to the iron door.

* *

The vault door was banded iron with a lock mechanism I'd only ever seen described in the copying-room: dual keyhole, dual paper slot. Credentials in the slot, key in the hole, and the tumblers read both. Hale's key hung warm against my chest where I'd sewn it into my coat lining—he'd pressed it into my hand in the cellar and said, with the ghost of his auditor's dryness, "Nine years I kept this and never once opened that door. Make it count."

I fed the credential folio into the slot.

Grell came to stand at my shoulder. Close. Far too close. Close enough to read the embossed name on the folio's face if the lamplight caught it right.

I turned the key with my body angled between him and the slot, and talked.

"Third floor annex runs the reconciliation copies, sir, but the originals cross-check goes through the senior auditors—" words, just words, procedural sludge, the kind of talk that makes eyes glaze, and I kept the cadence flat and endless, a clerk's lullaby, while the tumblers clicked somewhere inside the iron like a slow clock. "—and the quarter-close requires countersignature from a credential-holder of standing, which is why the summons specifies verification at the vault rather than the counting-room floor, per the amendment of—"

"You talk a great deal for a clerk," Grell observed.

"My apologies, sir." Beat. "It's the hour."

The last tumbler fell. The door shifted, heavy as a coffin lid, and swung inward onto darkness that breathed out cold and dust and the smell of old paper—the smell of the company's actual soul.

Grell reached past me and lit the vault lamp himself. And there it was, in the widening circle of light: shelves of document boxes, deed chests, contract drawers with brass labels. *Indentures — N–R*. My number lived in one of those drawers. Maybe my name did too.

And on the second shelf, alone where a whole row should have stood, one black leather volume. Spine cracked, corners worn soft. A ledger.

The second ledger. Not packed in the strongbox after all—or already retrieved from it, set aside, waiting to travel. Either way: here. Within reach. While the factor stood an arm's length away watching me with those chosen eyes.

"Your credentials, clerk," Grell said quietly, and held out his ringed hand.

* *

Time does strange things in vaults. I handed him the folio because refusing was impossible, and in the seconds it took to cross his fingers over the leather, I made the only decision available: the ledger couldn't come out openly now, under his lamp, under his eyes. It had to come out hidden. And I knew—God help me, I knew from experience—exactly one place in this building designed to hide paper from a searching hand.

While Grell opened the folio and began, slowly, to read, I crossed to the shelf as if confirming inventory, pulled the black ledger, and held it against my chest beneath my folded arms, spine out, the way clerks carry volumes. Then I turned to the drawer labeled *Indentures — N–R* and slid it open with my thumb, as though checking it against some imaginary list.

Papers. Dozens of contracts, each one a child reduced to terms. I found the N's. *Nystrom*. My mother's name, then mine below it in the childish scrawl of a clerk who'd been copying faster than he was thinking. Terms I'd never been allowed to see: transfer clauses, overseas assignments, a sale price.

I'd expected it to feel like ice. It felt like confirmation. Osk had tried to tell me—*your contract was written strangely, child, written by someone who wanted you findable.* Vane. Vane had marked my papers before he vanished. The dead man had been looking out for me for seven years from inside his exile, and I was standing in his company's vault holding his evidence against my chest.

"Well," Grell said behind me, and the temperature of his voice had dropped ten degrees. "These credentials are remarkably current for a man under suspension."

I did not turn around. Turning around is for guilty people. I drew the Nystrom contract out of the drawer—it slid in behind the ledger, thin paper against leather, invisible—and closed the drawer with a click, and faced him with the blank patience of the thoroughly bored.

"Suspension pending review, sir. The quarter-close proceeds regardless. If the credential fails verification, I'll simply wait for a countersignatory in the morning." I let weariness do the work. "I'd be obliged if you mentioned to whoever reviews this that the delay cost the annex a full night's copies."

He looked at me for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us a floorboard settled. In his hand, Hale's folio hung open, and I watched his eyes move across it and knew he recognized the hand that filled it, knew he was doing his own arithmetic—*Hale, the turncoat, the watched man, whose credentials open my vault at midnight on the eve of the circuit*—and coming to sums that should have ended me.

But here is the thing about Factor Grell, the thing nine years of watching company men had taught me: he was a man who preferred problems solved quietly. A clerk caught thieving makes noise—inquiries, witnesses, a scene seven days before the magistrate sat. A clerk dismissed for irregularities makes no noise at all.

"You're the Nystrom clerk," he said suddenly. "Pearlhouse. Nine-year indenture."

"Yes, sir."

"I've been meaning to make you an offer."

The ledger's spine pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Behind my back, folded in my coat lining, Hale's key lay against Hale's truth. In the folio in his hand lay the proof of everything, and in my arms, disguised as a tired clerk's burden, lay the proof of everything else.

"Have you, sir," I said.

He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who believes the room is already his, and he said, "Walk with me, clerk. Upstairs. We'll discuss your future"—his eyes flicked, once, to the empty shelf space where the ledger should have stood, and back to my face, mild as a knife laid flat—"and the whereabouts of a certain volume."
