## 30. Running the Harbor

### Lior

Nine years of being invisible ended in the time it took Factor Grell to say my name.

"Lior Nystrom." He said it like he was tasting a spoiled oyster. "Clerk, third grade. Contract number—" and then he recited it, the number I had answered to since I was twelve, the number that had replaced my name on every document I'd ever touched. He'd had it memorized. That was the worst part. Somewhere in that grey counting-house heart of his, someone had taught him to say my number the way other men say a prayer.

I stood in the vault doorway with the second ledger under my coat and Hale's credentials crumpling in my fist, and I did the arithmetic clerks do: exits, witnesses, seconds.

Three exits. One locked. Two guards between me and the stairs who were still deciding whether to be shocked or obedient. And Grell, standing at his desk with a cup of tea going cold beside him, because even men who order beatings drink tea while they wait.

"You were not summoned to that vault," Grell said.

"No, factor," I said. "I wasn't."

And then I threw the tea into his face.

I want it recorded—I want it read aloud at some future inquiry, column by column—that the tea was cold anyway, and that throwing it was the single most satisfying act of my entire indenture. Then I went past him through the window. Not the door. The window. It opened onto the loading gallery, and I had known about that window for nine years the way I knew everything about that building: quietly, uselessly, until tonight.

Behind me, Grell was shouting a word that meant *run them down*, and Vessing's rooftops opened up beneath my feet like a second city, one I'd only ever seen from below.

* * *

### Sefa

I was on the pilings with the tide coming in when I heard the bell.

Company House bells don't ring for fires at this hour—they ring for losses. I knew that sound from another port, another life, back when Papa's letters came folded inside fish barrels and I was small enough to hide in a net loft. A loss bell means someone took something the company loved. I put my hand flat on the wet wood and thought: *Lior.*

Then I ran.

You learn things growing up around couriers. You learn that crowds move like water and you should move like a fish—through them, never against. You learn that the first alley is the one they watch. You learn that if you're going to bleed tonight, do it after.

The market square was waking up angry. Word had gone out ahead of me somehow—Vessing is a town that runs on gossip the way ships run on ballast—and there were dockworkers on their thresholds with lamps, looking toward the Company House the way you look toward thunder. I pushed through them, and hands steadied me instead of stopping me. Remember that. When people ask later how we got away, tell them this: the town itself leaned.

I found Lior three streets over, halfway down a drainpipe that had opinions about holding their weight, ledger-shaped lump under a soaked coat, hair full of tile grit.

"Give it to me," I said.

"No," said Lior, from the pipe.

"Lior. If they catch you with it, they kill you. They'll kill me too, but they have to catch me first, and I'm faster."

"I've seen you eat," Lior said. "You are not faster."

Which was unfair, and also true, which is the worst kind of thing anyone can say to you. But there was no time to argue properly—the boots behind us had stopped being one set of boots—and so I did what couriers do. I lied.

"They know your number," I said. "Grell said it out loud. Your face, your contract, your name—they're all written down in that building now. The packet has to change hands or neither of us makes the harbor."

Something moved behind Lior's eyes. I watched nine years of being nobody fight one night of being somebody, and I watched somebody win, which I confess surprised me less than it should have.

Lior pressed the packet into my hands—the letters, oilskin-wrapped, salt-stained, my father's whole furious heart—and kept the ledger. Split the evidence. It's the oldest rule there is, and we broke it together in an alley, dividing truth like stolen bread.

"Salt Road tollhouse," Lior said. "If we're split by dawn, the packet goes where we planned."

"If we're split by dawn," I said, "you go to Mama Osk and you *stay* there."

We didn't shake on it. People who shake on things believe they'll see each other at dawn. We just ran.

* * *

### Lior

Here is something no one tells you about rooftops in a port town: they are designed by hatred. Every builder slopes them differently, every gap between buildings is measured by a man who died rich before your grandmother was born, and the tiles are glazed slick with salt spray so that running across them feels less like athletics and more like an insult you haven't yet identified.

But I grew up diving. Before Vessing, before the company, before the number—there was a woman who carried me into cold water before I could walk and taught my lungs to be patient. My body remembered things my mind had spent nine years filing away. When my foot skidded on a glazed ridge, my weight shifted before fear arrived. When the gap between the chandlery and the sail-loft came up too fast, I jumped it the way you dive off a piling—committing early, tucking late, trusting the water. Except there was no water. There was a stack of rope. It caught me hard enough to rearrange my opinion of rope.

I lay there one whole breath, listening to the bell, to boots on the street below, to the harbor breathing its old salt breath two streets away.

*Almost there,* I told myself. This is a lie runners tell themselves. There is no *almost there*. There is only the next roof.

They had cut off the direct way down—of course they had; Grell was a clerk once too, and clerks think in choke points. So I went the wrong way instead, out over the fish-market, along the spine of the smokehouse where the roof was warm and greasy under my palms, and dropped into the narrow world between buildings that only children and smugglers know. Nine years of invisibility had made me a scholar of Vessing's gaps. I fit myself through the space behind the cooper's yard, past the drainage channel Dagny's brother had widened with a chisel sometime before I was born, and came out among the pilings underneath the long pier, where the tide slapped the barnacles and the dark smelled like everything I'd been trying not to remember.

The oyster beds were out past the point. The plan was the beds. The sea keeps secrets; it had kept my mother's.

I was forty feet from open water when the lantern swung down the pier ladder and a man said my contract number again, patient as arithmetic.

There were two of them. One above with the lantern, one below, between me and the tideline, and both of them had the particular unhurried calm of men being paid by the hour rather than by results.

"Clerk Nystrom," said the one below. "Factor wants the book. Doesn't especially want you. Walk up the ladder and this stays boring."

Here is what I had: a ledger, a knife I'd practiced forging seals with, and a body that remembered cold water. Here is what I did not have: a miracle.

What happened next happened fast, and I only understood it afterward, the way you understand weather.

* * *

### Sefa

I'd circled wide—rooftop to rooftop to the warehouse gable over the pier—and so I saw it laid out below me like a diagram: Lior cornered under the pilings, two company men, lantern light making a yellow cage of the space between the beams.

Papa wrote once that courage is mostly bad planning done quickly. He was being funny. He was also right.

I went off the gable onto the warehouse awning—it took most of my momentum and gave most of it back as pain—and from the awning to the pier ladder, and I hit the lower man from above and behind the way a sack of ship's nails hits a deck. He went down. His lantern went out. The other man shouted and grabbed for me, and I felt the knife before I understood the knife, a line of cold fire across my side that turned hot almost instantly.

Fine. Hot is information. Cold would have been worse.

"Run," I told Lior, and I meant it as a suggestion but it came out sounding like an inheritance, like something a father says. "RUN."

Lior ran. I heard boots on pilings, then splash, then nothing, and that nothing was the best sound of my life, because Lior swims like the diver's child they are and those men did not.

Then I dealt with the second man, briefly, using a technique I can only describe as biting.

Afterward there was a stretch of pier, and my back against a bollard, and my hand pressed to my side coming away darker than I'd prefer. The wound was long, not deep—a knife dragged rather than driven—but long wounds leak with ambition. I wrapped it with sash, tied it one-handed the way couriers tie knots, and laughed at nothing, because adrenaline makes idiots of us all.

The packet was still inside my shirt. Papa's letters, riding over my heartbeat like a stowaway.

Somewhere out in the dark water, Lior was swimming for the oyster beds with half the truth of Vessing sealed against their ribs. Somewhere inland, Hale was either hiding, bleeding, or doing arithmetic at someone. Somewhere across the harbor, the Pearlhouse survivors were keeping watch from the burnt shell of home.

And somewhere close—too close, I realized when I heard the whistles coordinate, two notes, repeated, moving along the waterfront in a pattern that meant *she's here, close the circle*—the company was learning from its mistakes.

They weren't hunting the packet anymore. They were hunting me. Sefa, the courier. The daughter.

They knew. Grell had read enough of the letters, or heard enough from seven years of hunters, to know exactly whose blood I carried, and he wanted it stopped at the source.

I got up off the bollard. The harbor tilted politely and settled. Right, I told my legs. Once more into the pilings.

* * *

### Lior

The water took me the way it always had—not gently, but faithfully. Cold closed over my head and the world simplified: swim, breathe, swim. I surfaced behind the breakwater, filled my lungs, and looked back at the pier I'd left.

I saw the lantern die. I saw a figure drop from the warehouse awning like something thrown, and I saw the flash of the knife, and I heard—carried flat across the water the way sound travels at night—one voice saying *run*.

Sefa.

Everything in me said turn back. Everything trained into me by nine years of survival said: *you have the ledger, the ledger is the mission, a courier knows her trade.* And all of that was true, and none of it mattered, because I had already learned the lesson of this whole rotten year: safety purchased with silence is just debt. I'd spent my childhood paying it. I was done.

I swam two strokes toward shore before my brain caught up with my body and pointed out that swimming to a pier guarded by company men carrying a company ledger was not rescue, it was donation. So I did the harder thing. I trod water in the dark and I trusted her, the way she'd trusted me with the letters, and I hated every second of it, and I stayed.

From the breakwater I watched her come out along the pilings, moving wrong—favoring her left side, one arm clamped to her ribs—and I watched her lose the pursuit twice, once by doubling back through the fish racks and once by simply vanishing, which is apparently a skill couriers get along with the lying. She reappeared at the far end of the tar sheds, where the seawall steps run down to low-tide mud, and she looked out across the black water, and I did the one thing I could do.

I whistled.

Divers' whistle, mother's whistle—the three-note rise that says *here, safe, come.* It carries over water better than any shout, and it means nothing to anyone who never dove the beds.

Her head came up. Even at eighty yards I felt her find me.

She came down the seawall steps and into the shallows and I swam to meet her, and the moment the water reached her waist she stopped pretending to be fine and let me take some of her weight. Her blood spread in the current in slow ribbons, and the moon came out from behind cloud just enough to show me her face—grey, set, furious at her own body.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"You told me to run."

"I'm allowed to be wrong." She hissed when the cold found the cut. "The beds?"

"The beds. The wax holds. Can you dive?"

"I can float," she said, "with supervision."

Between the two of us we managed it—her arm over my shoulders, the water doing half the work, the tide slack and kind for once. Halfway out past the point, she stopped me, treading water with her good side, and pulled the oilskin packet from inside her shirt.

"Take them," she said.

"You gave me the ledger—"

"And now you have both, which makes you worth more alive than any two of us, which is a horrible thing to make a person, so take the letters and stop arguing with a bleeding woman."

I took them. The packet changed hands for the second time that night, warm from her skin, and I held Papa Vane's whole furious heart against my chest next to half the truth, and I understood suddenly what Mama Osk had tried to tell me weeks ago: that some inheritances come as paper, and some come as scars, and the lucky get both.

"There," Sefa said softly, and nodded toward the shore.

Lanterns. Four of them, maybe five, strung along the tideline in a slow, deliberate sweep, dragging the harbor the way Grell's men drag for drowned cargo. Between us and the oyster beds. Between us and anywhere.

"Tell me you have another idea," Sefa whispered.

As it happened, I did. It was a bad one. It involved the tide, the wax, and a promise I'd made myself at my mother's grave-marker-that-wasn't. But the lanterns were closing, and the water was rising, and dawn was coming whether the magistrate sat or not.

"Hold your breath," I told Sefa, "and trust the divers."

Out beyond the point, where the beds lie dark and secret, something answered my earlier whistle—three notes, rising, young voices carrying over the water.

The children of the Pearlhouse were already coming for us.
