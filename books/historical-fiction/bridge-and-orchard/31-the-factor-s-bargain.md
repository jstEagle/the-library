## 31. The Factor's Bargain

There is a particular pleasure in being the most reasonable man in a room, and I have made a career of it. Anyone can burn down a warehouse. It takes talent to stand in the ashes with a clean collar and explain to the magistrate's clerk that the fire was, on balance, an act of municipal improvement. Fear is cheap, gentlemen. Plausibility is where the money is.

I was thinking about this — not idly, but the way a man thinks about a tool he may shortly need — while my clerks below me tallied the week's manifests in the counting-house, their pens scratching like rats in a wall. From my window above the Tide Gate I could watch the harbor do what harbors do: lie beautifully. Gulls wheeling, sails fattening, brine-cured fish going out and silver coming in, and not one soul on those docks aware that the entire edifice rested on a column of figures I had personally adjusted eleven years ago.

That is the trade, you understand. Everyone imagines the Halvard Trading Company deals in pearls and salt cod. We deal in confidence. The pearls are merely the receipt.

And someone had been spending our confidence rather freely of late.

* * *

The trouble had a name, though I did not yet know it. What I knew was this: seven days since the Pearlhouse burned, six since Dagny Skarre went into the company jail as an arsonist she most certainly was not, and in that time the harbor had developed a silence of a very specific grain. I have lived in Vessing thirty years. There is the ordinary silence of a working port at dawn, which is simply noise resting. And there is the other kind — the silence of people who have decided, collectively and without consultation, to stop telling you things.

Fishwives who used to bicker at my clerks now counted change without a word. The turnkey reported that his prisoners had stopped singing. Even the tide seemed to come in quieter, though I grant that last part may have been sentiment.

Silence, in my experience, precedes two things: obedience or arithmetic. People go quiet when they've been cowed, or when they're counting what they're owed.

Vessing was counting.

* * *

"Factor." My under-clerk Pell stood in the doorway wearing the expression he reserved for news that would require me to be charming. Pell is a thin man assembled entirely from apology, but he is useful, because bad news tells him things it wouldn't dare tell me. "The searcher's report. On the woman Sefa."

"Sit," I said, and he didn't, because sitting implies the conversation might run long, and Pell's whole art was keeping conversations short enough to survive.

"She's gone to ground. The smugglers' quarter won't hold her long — they're frightened. But here's the thing." He wet his lips. "The fish-barrels. The ones searched at the Pearlhouse. Brine-cured herring, three barrels flagged for the smell."

"The smell?"

"Brine over old oil. Someone packed something in fish oil to mask it before the search, then moved it after. The barrels were resealed with company wax, factor. Somebody's clerk did that. Somebody who knows our seals."

I set down my pen, which is how I indicate to subordinates that a matter has graduated from tedious to interesting. "A clerk," I said. "In this office."

"There are forty-one clerks."

"And one of them can pour wax." Forty-one clerks, and I could already feel the list narrowing itself. Clerks who know seals are clerks who copy them; clerks who copy them are clerks who've looked too closely at documents; clerks who look too closely are the reason God invented shallow graves, though I'd never say so aloud in an office with windows. "Which clerks requested leave in the past fortnight?"

"One."

The name he gave me meant nothing to me, which surprised me, because I make a point of knowing every indentured body in this house the way a jeweler knows his stones. Lior Nystrom. I dredged for the face and came up with almost nothing — a small clerk, quiet, nine years on the rolls, copies manifests, scarred hands from some childhood in the water. Unremarkable. And then, being the man I am, I heard my own thought and turned it over, because *unremarkable* is precisely what a person chooses to be when they have something to hide. Nobody arrives at invisible by accident. Invisible is a career, like anything else.

"Pell," I said pleasantly, "bring me the Nystrom file."

* * *

Here is what the file told me, between its lines, where files keep their true contents: Nystrom's mother had been a diver, dead of the bends or the company or both, depending on who wrote the entry. Her child indentured at twelve — young, even for us — and the contract drawn with an unusual clause, one I remembered now only because the paper remembered it for me: assignment rights held by the Company House, transferable overseas. A clause we had used perhaps sixty times in a decade, always on the children of divers, always quietly. Contracts are like that clause, if you follow me. Small, legal, and load-bearing.

Sixty children. I did not let myself think of them as children, because I am fifty-three years old and have found that thinking of people as categories keeps the soup down, as it were. Sixty *transferable assets*. One of whom was now, apparently, pouring company wax onto smugglers' barrels.

I sat back and did what I do best, which is arithmetic.

The letters existed. I had known since the tollhouse fiasco — Hale, that sentimental ruin, bluffing a paid keeper with credentials I'd signed off on myself three years prior; there is a special sting in being betrayed by paperwork bearing your own signature. Letters, carried by a woman pulled half-drowned from the harbor, letters in the hand of Aldric Vane, founder, benefactor, officially a stain on the seabed these seven years. If those letters reached a magistrate, the death record fell, and if the death record fell, then the succession of the company fell, and if the succession fell, then every transaction ratified under the executors' seal — including certain sales, including certain ledgers, including eleven years of my own careful signatures — came open like a gutted fish.

So: the letters. Could I burn them? In theory. In practice, couriers who fear for their lives make copies, and this courier had crossed half a coastline with them, which argued for redundancy. Burn the letters and I'd light a signal fire instead. Every sailor knows you don't chase smoke to its source if the source might be holding the other end of a fuse.

Could I kill her? Again, in theory. In practice, she had already become a story. The Pearlhouse burning had seen to that — I confess the fire was intended as pressure, not scripture, but fires are illiterate and write what they like. Half the harbor believed the company had torched a house full of orphans. The other half knew it had. You cannot drown a woman in a harbor full of people who have already decided she's a martyr. Martyrs float.

Which left the third door, the one I was trying very hard not to open, because once opened, doors of that kind swing both ways.

* * *

The magistrate's spring circuit would sit in Vessing. This I learned from the assize notice that arrived with the coastal packet, all flourishes and wax, informing the town with bureaucratic tenderness that His Honor would hear petitions, settle disputes, and review charter compliance — *compliance*, mind, the word set in the same ink as everything else but somehow reading louder.

Pell laid the notice on my desk as though it were a dead bird.

"They'll bury it," I said, mostly to him, mostly to hear whether I believed it. "That's what circuits are for. A charter inquiry takes eighteen months of depositions. By midsummer everyone will have forgotten why they were angry, the letters will sit in an evidence trunk in the capital, and the second ledger will sit in our vault, and I will still have my position, my pension, and my digestion."

"Then we're done?" Pell asked, with the naked hope of a man who wants to go back to copying things.

"We're never done," I said. "We are merely, at present, ahead."

But that evening I walked the harbor alone, as I do — a factor should be seen walking his port like a landlord walks his fields; it costs nothing and buys the illusion of stewardship — and I looked at the water and did honest arithmetic against my will.

Eighteen months of procedure requires witnesses to survive eighteen months of procedure. That was the flaw in the beautiful plan. Procedure doesn't need the letters buried; it needs the *people* scattered, intimidated, bought, or gone. And the people were not going gently. A clerk forging seals. An auditor turned traitor out of what — conscience? At his age? The vanity of it. A net-mender in my jail who had said exactly four words in interrogation, all of them insubordinate. A dead man's daughter, if the rumors from the smugglers' cellar were worth anything, and rumor usually is.

And beneath all of it, the ledger itself. The second ledger, the true one, sleeping in my vault behind a door that had opened for exactly three people in seven years. As long as that book stayed put, the letters were just an old man's handwriting. But I had read the searcher's reports. I knew they knew the ledger existed. Knew it was here. Which meant that sooner or later — and with this breed it is always sooner — someone was going to come for my vault.

I stood at the harbor rail in the failing light and admitted the thing I had been circling for two days.

Burning the evidence would fail. Procedural burial would fail, because procedure needs time and time needed witnesses, and the witnesses had gotten better at surviving than I was at removing them. Every option I respected — the clean options, the deniable options — lost.

Which left the ugly one. Kill the witnesses quietly. Not the courier, not the clerk, not the famous names — kill the *story*. Kill the connective tissue. A drowned auditor before he can testify. A jailed net-mender who dies of gaol fever, terribly common, dreadfully sad. A clerk who goes out on the night tide and doesn't come back — divers' children drown, it's practically a local custom. Scatter the rest. No trial, no testimony, no eighteen months of anything. A circuit that sits and finds nothing to sit upon.

Quietly, mind. That was the craft of it. Quietly meant no hunters in Pearlhouse colors, no torches, none of the crude theatre that had cost me the harbor's fear and gained me its hatred. Hatred I could work with; hatred plus a martyr I could not.

* * *

I want to be clear about something, because whoever writes whatever gets written about me deserves an honest subject: I am not a monster, and the distinction matters. Monsters enjoy their work. I merely prefer it to the alternative, the way a man prefers amputation to gangrene and does not pretend the knife is kind. Eleven years ago, when the double entries began, I told myself what every man in my chair tells himself: that the company feeds four hundred families in this port, that a wound in the ledger is smaller than a wound in the town, that someone must hold the knife and better a steady hand than a trembling one. Whether any of that was true I genuinely cannot say anymore. What I can say is that I have slept tolerably well for eleven years, and I intend to sleep tolerably well for the remainder, however many nights that turns out to be.

The next morning I summoned three men to my private room above the counting-house. I won't dress them up; they were hunters, the company's, kept off the rolls and off the books, paid through a shell chandler so that no line item anywhere reads what it actually is. Their leader was called Merrick, a former wrecker from the north cliffs with a face like a dropped plate and the useful quality of having no imagination whatsoever. Men without imagination don't hesitate, don't repent, don't write confessions. They are the bluntest instruments in the drawer, and therefore the safest.

"Gentlemen," I said, pouring wine I had no intention of drinking. "The circuit sits in ten days."

Merrick nodded. His men nodded, because he did.

"I want no fires," I said. "No knives in market squares. Nothing that makes a story. Do you understand the difference between killing a man and killing a rumor? A man is easy. A rumor fights back."

They understood the way oxen understand mathematics — sufficiently, at feeding time.

"Auditor Hale first. He's watched, he's predictable, and he's proud, which means he'll walk home the same way every night. Gaol fever for the Skarre woman — the turnkey will be compensated; her cell will simply become unwholesome. The courier when she surfaces; the smugglers will sell her location within a week, they always do, fright makes them patriotic. And the clerk—" I paused, because saying the name aloud felt oddly like pulling a thread. "Nystrom. Find where the clerk sleeps. Don't touch the clerk yet. Watch first. A clerk who forges seals has contacts, and contacts lead to the letters, and the letters lead—" I caught myself elaborating and stopped. "The letters lead nowhere, once everyone who could authenticate them is unavailable. That's all this is. Authentication. We are not murderers, gentlemen. We are editors."

Merrick took his purse and his instructions and went, and I stood a long moment at the window watching the harbor glitter, doing the thing I do when a job is finished, which is to refuse to think about it further. Refusal is a discipline. I recommend it.

* * *

It was Pell, of course, who ruined the afternoon, because ruin is his vocation.

"Factor — the vault log."

I read it. Once a day I read it, and for seven years the log has shown me the same dull litany: my key, the head guard's countersignature, dust. Today's line showed my key. Yesterday's line also showed my key. Both entries in my own hand, because I keep the log myself, and both genuine, because I had gone in yesterday to check the ledger and again this morning, sleep having developed opinions of its own.

"What of it?" I said, and then saw it — the way you see a wrong note in a familiar hymn. Between yesterday's entry and this morning's, the page had been turned. The binding string retied. Left-handed knot. I tie right-handed, as does the head guard, as does every soul in this building except—

Except a clerk. A small, quiet, unremarkable clerk with diver's scars, who had requested leave, who could pour wax, who tied knots the way dockside children do, left over right, mirror-fashion, taught by net-menders and mothers hauling nets.

"Nystrom," I said, tasting the name properly this time. Nine years invisible. Nine years of copying manifests with perfect, insulting accuracy. And somewhere in those nine years, the invisible clerk had looked up from the page and *read* it.

The ledger. Was it still in the vault? I went down myself, right then, past the startled guards, key in the lock, hands steady enough, thank the gods, for the work of turning it. The strongbox stood where it has always stood. The lock showed no scratching. I lifted the lid.

Empty.

Not disturbed, not rifled — empty, the way a thing is emptied by someone who has practiced the lifting of it, cloth laid beneath, weight replaced with wrapped ballast so the box would not rattle if jostled. A professional theft, except that no professional alive ties a left-handed knot in a company counting-house. Only someone raised on the docks ties like that. Only someone raised beside nets and oyster lines and mothers who mended rigging with their teeth.

The clerk hadn't just found the ledger. The clerk had *carried it out of my vault*, past my guards, through my halls, wearing my own invisibility like a borrowed coat — and I realized, standing there with the empty box in my hands, that the coat had never been mine. I had merely worn it longer.

Eleven years of careful signatures, and undone in one night by a clerk whose file I couldn't previously be bothered to memorize.

I closed the strongbox gently, which surprised me. Then I went upstairs, told Pell to fetch Merrick back before the man had finished his breakfast, and gave the order I had hoped all morning I wouldn't need to give.

"Not the auditor," I said. "Not the jail. The clerk. The Nystrom clerk, and the courier, tonight, wherever they surface — and Merrick, listen to me. They will run for the water. Divers' children always run for the water. Post your men on the oyster beds."

He went. I remained at my window, watching the tide turn, doing the only arithmetic left to me and finding, for the first time in eleven years, that it would not resolve. Somewhere under that gray water was a ledger with sixty names in it, and one of those names, I now recalled, reading the file again in memory — one of the transferred contracts had borne the surname Nystrom.

The sea keeps our secrets, they say in Vessing. It had kept mine for a decade.

It was about to start giving them back.
