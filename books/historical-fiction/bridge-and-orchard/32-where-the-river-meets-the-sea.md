## 32. Where the River Meets the Sea

The oyster beds off Sallow Point don't appear on any company chart, which is precisely why I chose them.

Charts cost money to draw and more to correct, and the Halvard Trading Company has never spent a copper on anything it couldn't resell. The beds sit where the Vessing River gives up pretending to be a river and becomes the sea instead—a gray seam in the water that only a diver can find by taste. Fresh above, salt below, and between them a layer so cold it makes your teeth ache. My mother found it once, when I was small enough to still be learning, and made me hold my breath and slide down through it.

*Do you feel that?* she'd signed, her hands pale as moonlight in the murk. *That's where two worlds argue. Nothing that hides there is ever found.*

She'd been talking about pearls. She might as well have been talking about me.

* * *

Getting out of the harbor with the ledger had been the ugly part. There's no graceful way for a clerk to flee their own counting-house, and I had abandoned all pretense of grace somewhere around the moment I'd vaulted a desk—an act my knees would be invoicing me for later. The packet of letters rode against my chest; the second ledger rode inside its oilskin wrapping under my arm like an infant I had stolen, which in a sense I had. Somewhere behind me, Grell's hunters were learning what I already knew: that a person who has spent nine years being invisible is very difficult to see when they decide not to be.

I went to ground the way I'd been taught before I could write. Not in the Pearlhouse—that was ash, and Dagny was in a cell, and Mama Osk's ribs were still knitted wrong from the enforcers' boots. Not with the smugglers, whose cellar held Sefa and whose loyalty, though real, had a price tag sewn into its hem. I went where nobody goes willingly after dark, where the mud sucks at your shins and the mosquitoes conduct their own inquiry into the affairs of men.

Sallow Point. The oyster beds.

* * *

Here is what inheritance looks like when you get down close to it: a woman's hands, scarred in patterns the company never recorded because divers were entered in the ledgers as equipment. *Two sets diving irons. One Nystrom, female, durable.* Durable. That was the word they used for my mother, as if she were a hammer. She died at twenty-nine in water she knew better than anyone alive, and the company wrote *loss of gear* in the margin and docked her final wage for the iron she didn't bring up.

I have hated them a long time for that. But standing on the mudflat with the tide going out and the ledger heavy against my ribs, I understood something I hadn't before. The sea had kept my mother's secrets. Every pearl she found and didn't report—there were some; there are always some; divers are human before they are durable—went into hiding places like these. Wax-sealed, weighted, tucked into the seams of the world where the river argues with the sea. That's how she paid for my indenture bond to be written strangely. That's why Osk knew. That's why the company marked me early, and why Grell wants me quiet now.

My whole life has been conducted in the margins of their arithmetic. Well. Tonight the arithmetic comes home to roost, or rather to submerge.

* * *

Preparing the ledger took an hour by candle-stub, crouched on an overturned skiff half-buried in the mud. I won't pretend my hands were steady. This was the thing entire—the proof, the names, the double columns that turned children into cargo—and I was about to entrust it to seawater, which is famous for taking things and not giving them back.

But wax is older than paper, and my mother taught me this too, though she didn't know she was teaching it. Melt the tallow slow. Seal every seam twice. Weight it with a diver's stone—not an iron; I wasn't giving the company even the poetry of returning one of theirs. Then carry it down through the cold seam at slack tide, and set it where the beds grow thick, among ten thousand oysters who spend their whole patient lives doing one thing: taking what the water brings them and making it into something worth keeping.

It seemed fitting. Everything in Vessing that was ever precious got that way by swallowing grit.

* * *

The water at night is a different country. By day the harbor is work—ropes and hulls and the flat commerce of survival. At night it is the old place, the first place, black-green and breathing, and when I slid in with the ledger lashed to my chest I felt nine years of clerks' ink dissolve off me like it had never been. My body remembered what my mind had been forced to forget. The kick from the hip, not the knee. Breathe through the nose. Let the cold come, and then let it pass through you, because you cannot fight the sea, only negotiate with it.

Down through the warm layer. Down through the argument. The cold seam closed over me like a door, and my ears ached, and my scars—my own, the ones I've hidden under company linen since I was twelve—throbbed with something that was not quite pain. Recognition, maybe. My mother dove here. Her mother, if she had one, dove somewhere like it. The sea keeps our family's secrets in its ledger, column upon column, and it has never once falsified an entry.

I wedged the stone into the bed's foundation, among shells older than the company charter, and worked the wax seal one last time to be sure. Then I did something I cannot fully account for. I put my palm flat against it, the way you'd press a hand to a grave-marker or a sleeping child's back, and I stayed until my lungs began their complaint.

*Hold on,* I thought, not to the ledger exactly, and not to my mother exactly, but to whatever stands between the two. *Hold on until morning. We have an appointment with the truth.*

Then up, through the arguing waters, into air that tasted of salt and low tide and the particular freedom of a man with nothing left to carry.

* * *

I did not go back to shore right away.

There's a hummock above the high-water line at Sallow Point, a wind-bent thorn tree, a view across the whole gray reach of the harbor with its lamps strung along the pilings like a constellation someone gave up on halfway. I sat against the trunk, wringing brine from my sleeves, and watched the water that now held everything I had.

And I thought about my mother's hands again. Isn't it strange, the things grief chooses to keep? Not her face—I'm losing that, I know I am, the details going soft at the edges like wet paper. But her hands I have perfectly. The way they moved when she signed. *Pearl,* with one finger drawn down the opposite palm, quick, secret. *Dive,* both hands cutting flat and downward. And once, only once, when the fever was on her and she knew what was coming before any of us would say it: *Yours,* pressed against my sternum, hard enough to leave a ghost.

I was twelve. I took the word literally, the way children do. Yours. Meaning what? A pearl? A debt? Now, sitting in the dark with a stolen ledger buried beneath ten thousand bivalves, I finally understood she'd meant the whole inheritance—the diving, the hiding, the knowing when to be silent and when silence becomes complicity. She'd handed me the sea itself, folded small enough to fit under my ribs, and told me to keep it until I needed it.

I needed it tonight.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight I heard the boats.

Not smugglers—I know the rhythm of smugglers' oars the way other people know hymns, quick and muffled, apologetic. These were ship's boats, heavy, pulled in long strokes, and there were lanterns in them, which meant men who wanted to be seen by each other and didn't care who else noticed. Company men. Drag lines over the sides, sweeping the harbor channels in slow, methodical passes.

They were looking for me. Or for the ledger. Same thing, in their arithmetic—one entry to be erased.

I should have been afraid, and I suppose at the bottom of me I was, a cold little fish of fear swimming loops around the larger resolve. But mostly what I felt was contempt, and I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of everything we've bled for. They were dragging the water for a book while the book lay under their boats the whole time, wrapped in wax, guarded by oysters. It seemed like a parable about the company entire. Search everywhere except where the poor things live, and you'll never find what you're looking for.

One boat came closer to Sallow Point than the rest. A lantern swung, and light swept the mudflat, the thorn tree, and—for one long breath—me.

I didn't move. Nine years of practice: be furniture, be shadow, be driftwood. The light passed over. The boat moved on, and I sat there with my heart slamming and thought, wryly, that invisibility had finally become useful instead of merely survivable. The trick, I realized, was never to stop being invisible. It was to choose the moment to be seen. Tomorrow, in the market square, with the whole circuit watching—then I would be seen so thoroughly that no one in Vessing would ever unsee it.

But that was tomorrow's work. Tonight I was driftwood. Tonight I belonged to the tide.

* * *

Near dawn the cold got into me properly, the kind that settles in the joints and files its grievances. I climbed and shook myself and stood a while looking down at the water, which had gone silver-gray and innocent, keeping its counsel as it always had.

"Keep it safe," I said aloud, feeling foolish, and then, because foolishness is free and I'd earned it: "You kept hers."

The tide was turning. Slack water, the brief still hour when the river and the sea stop arguing and agree, just for a moment, to hold everything in place. My mother had timed her dives by it. I would time mine by it too, when I came back down through the cold seam with the whole harbor watching for me and the magistrate's court waiting on the far side of my breath.

Behind me, inland, a bell began to ring—the market bell, far too early, ringing wrong. Not the measured peal of trade but a rapid, urgent clatter, alarm-pattern, the kind used for fire, for riot, for a prisoner escaped or a man taken.

Someone was running the story ahead of me. And I had a terrible, cold-stomach certainty, sitting there with salt drying white on my forearms, that whichever way that bell was telling it—they'd caught Hale, or Sefa, or both—it would be read in the square within the hour, and dawn was coming whether the evidence surfaced or not.
