## 33. The Turncoat's Choice

There is an arithmetic to surrender, and I had worked it out twice before I ever set my boots on the Salt Road.

The first time was in the counting-house, thirty years ago, when I learned that a man who balances columns for a living will eventually have to choose which side of the ledger he stands on. The second time was last night, in the smugglers' cellar, when Lior Nystrom told me where they were going and I understood — with the particular clarity that comes only to men who have spent their lives being wrong about important things — that someone would have to be the stone thrown into the other side of the scale.

Grell's hunters were sweeping the harbor. Three parties, working outward from the burned Pearlhouse in a fan, the way a man searches a room he knows contains a mouse. They had dogs, or so Dagny's turnkey friend reported, though in my experience company men claim dogs the way they claim dividends: it sounds better in the telling. What they certainly had was patience, and orders, and the one thing I could not allow them to have: proximity to the oyster beds where a clerk with salt-scoured hands was currently sealing a stolen ledger beneath the waterline of Vessing harbor.

So I did what any auditor worth his ink would do. I made myself the largest number in the equation.

I put on my good coat — the grey one, the one I'd been arrested in once already, which seemed fitting — and I walked out the north end of the Salt Road at dusk, alone, whistling. Not a tune, precisely. I've never had the ear for tunes. A sequence. Numbers set to rhythm, the way I keep them in my head: seven, eleven, nineteen, the primes, the honest numbers that can't be divided by anything but themselves and one.

I have often thought there is something admirable in a prime number. It refuses to be broken into parts. More than I can say for most men, and considerably more than I could say for myself until quite recently.

They took me within the hour. Of course they did. I had not exactly made it difficult.

* * *

The room they put me in was above the fish-market, which I found either a deliberate cruelty or a complete failure of imagination on Grell's part. The smell of brine and old herring came up through the floorboards like a memory with teeth. There was a table, two chairs, and a lantern turned down low enough that whoever sat opposite me would be a shadow, which is the oldest trick in interrogation and the first one I ever learned from the other side of the table.

I counted the floorboard gaps instead. Forty-one. Prime.

"You understand," said the shadow, and I recognized the voice — Vessel, one of Grell's factor-clerks, a man I had once caught falsifying grain weights and, in my former life, chosen to report rather than blackmail, which tells you how long ago my stupidity dates from — "that Mr. Grell has been very generous regarding your situation."

"My situation," I said, "is a chair."

"Your situation is a rope." He let that sit in the dark between us. "You were seen leaving the city alone. You were carrying nothing. And yet here you are, delivered to us like a parcel. Either you are the stupidest fugitive on this coast, Mr. Hale—"

"I am," I agreed. "It's well documented. Ask the board of directors. They kept minutes."

"—or you wanted to be caught." The shadow leaned forward. Lantern light found half his face, the half I hadn't ruined with a report nine years ago. "Which means whatever you're protecting is somewhere you're very keen for us not to look."

I said nothing. This is a technique, too — silence — though in truth I wasn't employing it strategically. I was doing sums. Somewhere out beyond the market, past the Tide Gate, in the cold green dark of the oyster beds, a ledger was resting under a fathom of water sealed in wax, and every minute Vessel spent in this room with me was a minute his search parties spent dragging empty nets through the wrong part of the harbor.

I have never in my life been more useful than I was being at that moment, sitting completely still.

"Where is the girl?" Vessel asked.

"Which girl? You'll need to be specific. I've failed a great many people of both sexes."

"Sefa."

"Ah." I folded my hands, which is something I do when I'm about to lie; I've never managed to stop the habit, so instead I've learned to make it work for me by folding them when I intend to tell the truth as well, until the gesture means nothing at all. "The courier. I don't know where she is. This is accurate, incidentally — you may check my inflection against previous testimony. When I don't know a thing, I say so. It's a professional deformity."

"Then tell us about the ledger."

And there it was. The real question, arriving late, the way real questions do. Grell didn't want Sefa; Sefa was a symptom. Grell wanted the second ledger, the true one, the one with the actual weights and the actual prices and — most damning of all, in a column I had verified with my own eyes eleven years ago and then, God forgive me, signed off on — the names.

"Which ledger?" I asked.

"Do not toy with me."

"It's a legitimate question. Your employer keeps two. If you're asking about the one he shows the magistrate's clerks, I can summarize it: the Halvard Trading Company is modestly profitable, pays its duties, and has never sold an indentured child overseas in its life. If you're asking about the other one—" I spread my hands, still folded, meaning nothing. "That one I could talk about for hours."

Vessel stood up. I heard the chair scrape and then I felt his fist, which was brief and professional and landed where fists are supposed to land, and I tasted blood and thought, distantly, *seventeen teeth remaining if that loosens the molar, which it has, prime again.*

"The numbers," Vessel said, straightening his sleeve. "Mr. Grell wants to know what you gave them. What they have. What we're looking for."

"They have the numbers," I said, and smiled with my split lip, and watched him understand that I was going to be no use whatsoever except as a wall for him to beat his hands against while the tide did its slow, faithful work a mile away.

* * *

Interrogation, I have come to learn, is mostly scheduling. They question you in sessions because men break in sessions the way ledgers break under audit — not all at once but line by line, fatigue compounding like interest. So they left me chained to a ring in the floorboards and came back at intervals, different men, same questions, and between sessions I did what I have always done when the world became unbearable.

I counted.

I counted the forty-one gaps in the floorboards and the sixty-two knots in the ceiling planks and the intervals of the watchman's boots overhead, which came every three hundred heartbeats, give or take, which meant the watch changed on the half-hour and the man walking it favored his left knee. I built the whole building in my head, load-bearing walls and all, the way I had once rebuilt the Halvard accounts from fragmentary manifests during the Vane inquiry, back when rebuilding the accounts was my sin instead of my penance.

And in the spaces between the counts, I talked.

That surprised them, I think. Men expect silence from the defiant and screams from the frightened, and I gave them neither. I gave them arithmetic.

"You want to know what the ledger proves?" I said to the third one, a broad silent man whose technique consisted entirely of waiting. "I'll tell you. In the spring of the ninth year of the charter, four hundred and twelve barrels of pearl-shell were entered at the Vessing weigh-house. Two hundred and eighty-three were real. Do you know what the other hundred and twenty-nine barrels contained, by weight and displacement?"

Silence. Good. Silence meant listening.

"Nothing. Empty barrels, ballasted with wet sand. The difference between the recorded tonnage and the actual tonnage was used to offset the cost of transport for a different cargo entirely — human cargo. Contracts. Children's indentures, bought at Vessing for the standard penalty rate and sold at the eastern ports for eleven times the price. Eleven times. I can show you the multiplier. It's in the margin of page forty, in Aldric Vane's own hand, because he wrote it there before he understood what it meant, and then he wrote *God forgive us* underneath it, and then he disappeared, and everyone in this city has spent seven years calling him drowned."

The broad man shifted. Just slightly. But I had spent thirty years reading faces across audit tables, and I knew what that shift meant. It meant *page forty* had landed somewhere below his professional indifference and lodged there.

"There were sixty-one children," I continued, because I had decided, somewhere in the dark with my wrist chafing against iron, that if these were to be my last days I would spend them the way I had wasted my whole life — being precise. "Sixty-one. I know the number because I audited the transfers. I saw each name. I looked away from each name. Sixty-one is a prime, incidentally. It cannot be divided. Neither, it turns out, can I — not anymore, not by Grell, not by the board, not by the memory of my own signature at the bottom of those pages."

They came back four more times. I gave them barrel weights. I gave them the exchange rates at the eastern ports, quarter by quarter, across seven years. I gave them the compound interest on stolen childhoods, calculated annually, and I recited it without notes, and somewhere around the fourth session the broad man stopped meeting my eyes, and by the fifth he had begun, very quietly, to ask questions that weren't on Grell's list. *How old was the youngest.* Nine years old. *Were any of them returned.* Two. Both dead of the crossing. *Who signed.*

"Grell signed," I said. "And Vane refused to sign, and refusing to sign is why the harbor calls him dead. And I signed, which is why I am sitting in a fish cellar telling you the truth, which is the first time this company has gotten the truth out of me at a fair price."

* * *

On what I judged to be the second night — the watchman's limp had grown worse, which happens over double shifts — Vessel came down himself. No shadow games this time; he brought the lantern close and sat across from me with his elbows on his knees, and I saw that he had not slept either, and that his hands, which had hit me once with such confidence, now wouldn't stay still in his lap.

"Grell's moving the original ledgers to the capital," he said. "Day after tomorrow. On the *Merrow's Purse*. If the evidence isn't produced before then, it's gone. Buried in procedure. He'll let the circuit sit, let it hear everything, and there'll be nothing to hear it *with*. You should know that your sacrifice—" he said the word like it tasted of herring— "has purchased nothing."

"Thank you," I said. "That's genuinely useful information, and I want you to know I recognize the enormous professional risk involved in bringing it to me."

"Don't be clever with me, Hale."

"I'm not being clever. I'm being grateful. It feels very similar, I admit."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man who has been doing sums of his own: "My sister went east on a contract. Twenty years ago. She'd be thirty-four now. I checked the transfer lists after your first session." His jaw worked. "Her name's there. Page fifty-one. You recited the page numbers."

"I recite everything," I said gently. "It's a curse."

"Is she alive?"

I looked at him, this man who had beaten me and threatened me and was now asking me the only question in the world, and I wished with a sudden physical ache that I had a kinder profession than honesty.

"The lists record sale," I said. "They do not record death. That absence — the missing death column, on page after page after page — was the thing Aldric Vane couldn't stomach. He called it *the company's cruelest arithmetic*: they sold the children and then declined, even on paper, to imagine them dying." I held his eyes. "So I can't tell you she's alive. But no one has written her dead either, and until the ledger says otherwise, she is a live entry. An open account. I would advise you, as your former auditor, to keep it open."

Vessel put his face in his hands. I let him. Some calculations a man must finish alone.

When he stood, he said, without turning around: "The oyster beds. That's where the clerk went. That's what you're covering."

And I confess my heart performed an unscheduled adjustment, because a wrong answer there drowns everything — the letters, the ledger, the girl, the whole fragile architecture we had raised out of wax and stubbornness.

"Search wherever you like," I said. "But consider the arithmetic. If I knew where the ledger was, Grell would already have it, because I break easily — ask anyone. The fact that you're asking me means your master suspects, and suspicion is not knowledge, and knowledge is the only thing that saves your position now. Drag the beds if you must. Drag the whole harbor. But drag it *loudly*, in daylight, where the whole waterfront can watch the Halvard Trading Company publicly searching for its own sins." I smiled through the blood in my mouth. "Or sit here and wait, knowing that if the circuit hears the truth first, page fifty-one gets read aloud in the market square, with your sister's name in it, read as a *victim*, not a crime scene."

He left without another word. The lantern went with him. I sat in the dark among my forty-one gaps and sixty-two knots and thought: *either I just saved them or I just buried them, and the tide will tell me which, and I have never in my life hated not knowing a figure so much.*

* * *

They came for me at dawn, but not to the gallows and not to another session. Vessel himself unlocked the chain, and behind him stood two men I didn't know, wearing the grey of the magistrate's provosts, and Vessel's face was the face of a man stepping off a ledge he has been inspecting for years.

"Corvin Hale," he said formally, as though reading from a document, and perhaps he was; perhaps he'd written it himself in the night. "You are remanded to the custody of the circuit magistrate on charges of obstruction, fraud, and conspiracy, to be held for hearing." Then, lower, in a voice that belonged to a boy from page fifty-one's family and not to the Halvard Trading Company at all: "Grell ordered your death this morning. Quietly, he said. In the phrase of the trade." He rubbed his wrist where, I noticed now, the company seal had been pried off his signet. "I find I've developed objections to quiet orders. I'm told that runs in the harbor."

They walked me out through the fish-market, into the grey beginning of morning, and the smell that had been my prison was suddenly only the smell of Vessing, which is to say the smell of home, which I had never once let myself call that. Down at the water, the tide was turning. Slack water, the divers call it — the held breath between ebb and flood, when anything sunk can be lifted and anything hidden can surface.

And across the square, gathering in ones and twos with the slow inevitability of weather, the dockworkers were already assembling for the circuit's first sitting, and above their heads, being passed hand to hand like a child passed through a crowd, I saw an oilskin packet, and I heard Lior Nystrom's voice — thin, terrified, and absolutely steady — begin to read.

Numbers, of course. It began with numbers.

Mine had done their work. Now it was theirs.
