## 34. The Circuit Sits

The magistrate's court convened in the market square because Vessing had no courthouse worth the name and the company had never seen fit to build one. Justice, in a tidal port, was a traveling thing—like the fish, like the weather, like every hope anyone here had ever entertained.

I stood at the edge of the square at dawn and watched them set it up: a platform of rough planks, a canopy of striped canvas gone gray with salt, a clerk's table with two chairs, one of which would hold a man with the power to unmake everything my father had spent seven years dying to say. It looked absurdly small. Seven years of exile, three checkpoints, a burned Pearlhouse, Dagny in a cell, Hale under guard, Lior somewhere beneath the harbor with a ledger sealed in wax—and all of it funneling down toward forty feet of splintered lumber and a fat man's chair.

There is a particular comedy in watching history prepare its seating arrangements.

The magistrate himself did not appear until mid-morning, which gave the crowd time to assemble, and the crowd assembled the way crowds do in Vessing: gradually, then all at once, then pretending they'd been there all along. Dockworkers who should have been on shift. Net-menders with their hands still smelling of tar. The diver children, scrubbed and solemn, arranged near the front by some unspoken understanding that if anything went wrong, they were the ones who could vanish fastest and swim farthest.

Mama Osk stood among them with a bruise going yellow along her jaw from the search, arms crossed, radiating the patience of a woman who had outlived three company factors already and fully intended to outlive this one too.

"You're staring," she said, without looking at me. She had a genius for materializing beside me exactly when I least wanted company, which I had come to understand was a form of love.

"I'm assessing," I said.

"You're staring. Your father stares the same way. Stared." She corrected herself with a gentleness that was worse than bluntness. "Same jaw when he's working something out."

"He isn't my father," I said, automatically, the lie so worn now it barely qualified as speech. "Not publicly."

"No," Mama Osk said. "Publicly you're nobody at all. A courier with no papers and no name that anyone can prove. Which brings me to what everyone's been too polite to ask: what happens when the magistrate asks *you* who you are?"

* * *

Here is what we had, in total, after everything:

Eleven letters in Aldric Vane's hand. Beautiful hand—I could say that with an auditor's objectivity, having watched Lior examine them against old manifests for the hundredth time. The looping *V*, the peculiar way he crossed his sevens, the ink stains where he wrote fast when frightened. Lior could swear to the handwriting. Corvin Hale could swear to it. But handwriting proves a man lived and wrote; it does not prove what he wrote was true.

And what he had written was this: that the Halvard Trading Company, seven years ago, began selling indentured contracts of port children to overseas buyers—sugar colonies, pearl fisheries, places where a nine-year-old with salt-lung was worth more than she was in Vessing. That when Vane discovered it and threatened to go to the magistrate himself, the company's board arranged his drowning. That he had spent seven years in exile assembling proof, and that the proof—the second ledger, the true ledger—was locked in the Company House vault.

That last part was the problem. The letters named the vault. They described the fraud in detail. But a dead man's accusations, however beautifully penned, are just grief with good penmanship until something corroborates them. Hale could testify—he had audited the sales, had looked away, was prepared to say so aloud, which took a kind of courage I hadn't expected to find in him. But Hale was also a disgraced auditor with every reason to fabricate a grievance, and Grell's advocates would peel him apart by noon. And Hale was currently being watched, possibly detained; we didn't know. He hadn't come to the cellar. His coded warning had been the last anyone heard.

And the ledger itself—Lior had gone into the water for it two nights ago, into the oyster beds while Grell's men dragged the harbor, and had surfaced surrounded by Pearlhouse divers forming a shield of small brown bodies around them. Afterward Lior had handed the packet off and vanished into the smugglers' warren before the hunters regrouped, and I hadn't seen them since. No word. That was the plan—no word, no connection, nothing for Grell's people to follow—but plans are cold comfort at four in the morning when the person you've dragged across two provinces is somewhere you can't see and men are dragging the harbor for paper.

So: letters, a possibly-jailed auditor, and a ledger whose existence we could assert but not produce. Not yet. Lior needed one more tide, and tides keep their own schedule.

"Magistrate doesn't have to hear us at all," Dagny's brother Rurik had pointed out in the cellar, the night before. He was a broad, bitter man with a smuggler's talent for cataloguing every way a thing could fail. "Circuit sits, hears the routine disputes, moves on. We're not on any docket. You walk up there and start talking about drowned founders and sold children, and Grell's advocate will have you declared mad or malicious before you finish your second sentence. Disorderly petitioning. Contempt. There are cells in this town, girl. You've seen the inside of the system already."

He wasn't wrong. That was the whole shape of our problem. We had truth, but truth needs a door, and doors need someone to open them, and magistrates are men who open doors on schedules.

Unless someone gives them a reason.

* * *

The circuit opened with property disputes.

I want to record, for posterity, that the great reckoning of the Halvard Trading Company—the moment toward which seven years of my father's exile and one very long winter of all our lives had bent—was preceded by ninety minutes of adjudicating a fence dispute between two herring fishermen, followed by a complaint about a goat. The magistrate, a heavy man named Ondrell with a face like a dropped pie, handled the goat with more visible passion than he would later show for the fate of children. Grell sat at the company's table in a dove-gray coat, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man watching weather he had already ordered.

I stood thirty feet away in a fish-seller's apron I had borrowed, holding a basket I had filled mostly with rocks, and tried to think.

Mama Osk's question kept circling: *what happens when he asks who you are?*

Because here was the door, if there was one. A petitioner. Courts hear petitions—it's how they justify themselves, how the empire pretends its justice reaches the waterline. If someone stood up during open petitions and made an accusation of fraud serious enough, public enough, that dismissing it outright would be its own scandal—then maybe, maybe, the door creaked.

But petitions come from persons. And I was not, on any paper that existed, a person with standing. I was Sefa of nowhere, no surname on record, arrived in Vessing half-drowned. My claim rested entirely on being Aldric Vane's daughter—a fact attested by no document, no parish roll, no witness the court would recognize. My mother had died on the mainland when I was six. Vane had kept us secret deliberately, because a founder with a bastard daughter by a mainland woman had enemies enough without giving them leverage. He had hidden us the way you hide anything precious: well. So well that now I couldn't prove I existed.

If I stood up and said *I am Sefa Vane*, Grell's advocate would smile the way lawyers smile, and ask for evidence, and produce instead a genealogy of nothing. An orphan claiming a dead man's name to slander his company—why, it was almost poetic. Almost sympathetic. Almost dismissible.

And worse: if I claimed him and failed publicly, I wouldn't just fail myself. I'd poison the well. Every letter, every scrap of testimony afterward would be footnoted with *advanced by the same discredited woman*. I could spend the whole truth in one sentence and buy nothing with it.

Rurik had said, in the cellar, "Let Hale do it. Let the auditor carry it. He's got standing, or near enough. You stay invisible."

Invisible. The word landed in me strangely, because it wasn't my word—it was Lior's whole doctrine, the survival creed of a diver's child, and I had watched Lior break it piece by piece over a winter until forging papers under their own true name. I had lectured Lior once, on the Salt Road, in a shepherd's bothy with rain hammering the turf roof: *they can't hang what they can't find.* And Lior had said—not angrily, which made it worse—*and what's left of you when nothing can find you?*

I looked at the crowd. At Mama Osk's crossed arms. At the diver children, who had been sold or nearly sold, whose names were in a ledger in my basket under the fish and the rocks, if Lior had gotten it out. At Dagny's empty place—she was still in the company jail, or newly escaped through the fish-market tunnels depending on which rumor you trusted, and God, I hoped it was the tunnels.

They were betting on me. Not on the letters. On me.

* * *

Petitions were called at noon. The herald—a bored company man with a scroll—announced that the circuit would hear grievances against parties present, properly filed, in order of receipt.

Properly filed. In order of receipt. Our petition, of course, had never been filed, because filing it would have told Grell everything a week early. The system was a lock, and Grell held the key ring, and the magistrate was a man who respected locks.

Grell rose during a lull, all courtesy, and addressed the bench. "Your Honor, if the court will permit a remark. Vessing has lately been troubled by rumors—seditions, frankly—circulated by unknown persons. The company welcomes inquiry. We welcome it eagerly." He smiled at the crowd like a man welcoming rain onto land he'd already flooded. "We ask only that accusers stand forward under their own names, so that the company may answer charges rather than ghosts."

It was masterful. I'll give him that. He was daring us into the open, making anonymity itself look like guilt, pre-poisoning whatever came next. And the magistrate nodded along, because order and procedure are comfortable things, and Grell was offering him a tidy afternoon.

Under my own names. Singular name. One name, actually, since Sefa was only half of it.

My hands were shaking. I noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to my mother, who had also had clever shaking hands when the tax men came. Seven years I had carried those letters folded against my ribs, through three provinces, past two checkpoints and a tollhouse trap, shot at once in the shoulder, burned out of a bed in the night. Seven years my father had been dead to the world, and alive to me, writing to a daughter he called *my gull* because I always came back to him over water. *Keep low, little gull. Fly low until the season turns.*

The season had turned.

I thought about what my father would actually want, which is a question I had avoided for seven years because the answer kept changing. When I was twenty and furious, I thought he'd want vengeance shouted. At twenty-three, hunted, I decided he'd want me alive and silent forever. Now, standing in fish-seller's wool with rocks in my basket, I thought: he'd want it done right. He was a ledger man. He believed numbers spoken aloud in daylight could outweigh any darkness—that was naive, and it was beautiful, and it was why they'd had to kill him, and it was the only inheritance that mattered.

Hale, if he could even attend, might break under cross-examination. Lior might be caught in the harbor before slack tide. The letters might be ruled forgeries by lunch. Everything might fail, and probably would, and my father had taught me one other thing about ledgers: you make your entry anyway, in your own hand, because the record is the thing. Someone has to be the person who stood up.

"Grell wants names," Mama Osk said quietly at my elbow. I never heard her come up on me. "Well. Names cost nothing to say. It's the saying them that costs everything else." She looked at me then, directly, her eyes wet and absolutely steady. "Your father stood up once, in the counting-house, in front of the whole board. Alone. I wasn't there—but Dagny's brother heard tell of it for years. One man, saying *this is wrong*, knowing what it would cost. Whatever else you decide, girl—you come from people who stand up."

The herald called for further petitions. The pause stretched. Somewhere in the crowd a gull screamed, absurdly on cue.

I set down the basket. Took the apron off, folded it, laid it on the basket—some habits of tidiness run deeper than fear. Walked the thirty feet to the platform through a crowd that parted slowly, the way water parts, closing behind me.

"Your Honor." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I have a grievance against the Halvard Trading Company. It is not properly filed. It cannot be, because the company has spent seven years ensuring that no one who could file it survived to do so."

Ondrell frowned. Grell's head turned with the smooth inevitability of a lighthouse. The advocate was already rising, already smiling.

"The company's founder, Aldric Vane," I said, "did not drown seven years ago. He fled for his life, because he discovered this company sells indentured children overseas and falsifies its ledgers to hide it. I know because I carried his letters. I know—" and here my voice finally shook, and I let it, because some truths deserve to arrive trembling— "because he was my father. My name is Sefa Vane. And I am asking this court to hear us before the circuit sails."

The square did not gasp. This was Vessing; Vessing had heard better lies about smaller things. But it went very quiet, and then—from the back, spreading forward like fire finding dry grass—the sound of dockworkers standing up. One, then a rank of them, then all of them, and the net-menders, and Mama Osk's divers, small and straight-backed, a wall of people who owed the company nothing but debts.

Grell leaned toward his advocate and said something I couldn't hear. But I saw his face, and for the first time in seven years of hunting me, Factor Grell looked uncertain—and I understood, with a lurch, that uncertainty in a man like Grell was more dangerous than confidence. Confident men make plans. Uncertain ones reach for matches.

The magistrate looked at me for a long moment, then at the standing crowd, then at the harbor, where the circuit ship rode at anchor with its departure tide due at dawn.

"The court will sit again at first light," Ondrell said heavily, "to hear evidence in the matter of the Halvard Trading Company. Petitioner—" his eyes settled on me, tired and appraising, a man calculating which way the wind of empire blew and disliking both options— "you will appear. With your proofs."

First light. Slack tide was an hour before first light. If Lior was coming out of the water with the ledger, it would be in the last dark hour of the morning—and between Lior and this platform stood Grell's hunters, who now knew exactly where and when the endgame would be played.

I walked back through the crowd on legs that felt borrowed, and Mama Osk caught my arm, and whispered the only thing left to say:

"Now we pray the sea gives your friend back."
