## 35. Testimony

There is a particular smell to a crowd that has decided not to move. I learned it young—my father used to say you could smell a mutiny three streets off, salt and sweat and stubbornness—and I smelled it in the Vessing market square before the magistrate's clerks had even finished setting up their benches.

They had come for the circuit court the way gulls come for a fish cart: officially to observe, actually to take. Dockworkers with rope-burned hands shoved deep in their pockets. Oyster-wives with baskets they had no intention of selling today. Divers who should have been out on the beds at morning tide, standing shoulder to shoulder in their shore clothes, dripping nothing and daring anyone to ask why. And at the edges, like a hem sewn around the whole garment, the scattered survivors of the Pearlhouse fire, soot still faintly ghosted in the creases of their necks.

I stood among them trying very hard not to look like the reason all of them were there.

This is harder than it sounds. When you are carrying a secret, you develop a theory that your body will betray you—that your guilt will radiate outward like heat off a stove and every guard within ten feet will feel it. Nine months as Aldric Vane's courier taught me the opposite lesson: nobody looks at a person who looks like they belong. Belonging is the best disguise ever invented. That morning, belonging was easy. I wore a borrowed shawl of Mama Osk's, I carried a basket of mending, and I stood next to Corvin Hale—a disgraced auditor with a face like an unpaid bill—which made me, by comparison, invisible as fog.

"You're doing the breathing wrong," Hale murmured without moving his lips. He had a gift for that; he spoke the way ledgers were written, everything compressed into the smallest possible space.

"I'm breathing fine."

"You're breathing like a woman about to do something memorable. Stop it."

"I *am* about to do something memorable. That's rather the point."

"Then breathe after." His eyes tracked the company men at the square's corners—the factor's guards, six of them, wearing the Halvard colors like they'd been ironed into the cloth. "Once you speak, they can't simply disappear you in front of a circuit magistrate. Before you speak, they absolutely can. The interval between those two conditions is where you live or die. I'd treat it with respect."

There was something almost tender in the way he said it, the way a man might describe interest rates to a child. I had spent weeks distrusting Hale on principle—he was a company man, and company men, in my experience, came in two varieties: those who had robbed you and those who were planning to. But he had let himself be stripped of his position for us, had sent warnings through fish barrels and smugglers' cellars, and this morning he stood in the cold with his collar frayed and his credentials gone, betting the last coin of his reputation on my name.

My name. There was the joke of it, the great cosmic comedy that had kept me awake half the night laughing at nothing in Vane's cellar behind the smugglers' storehouse. My whole life I had carried names the way other people carry contraband—Sefa, which was true but incomplete; the courier-girl, which was useful; no-name-at-all, which was safest. My father had taught me that. *Aldric Vane*, founder of the Halvard Trading Company, had spent years teaching his daughter that the most valuable thing she owned was her own erasure. He faked his death so well that even the sea believed it. And now the entire strategy of my life—hide, run, deliver the letters, stay small—had to be burned down in front of several hundred strangers and a magistrate, because paper alone wasn't enough.

The letters proved a dead man's hand. Only a living witness could prove the man.

* * *

The circuit sat in the open air because Vessing had no hall big enough and the magistrate, a dry stick of a woman named Circuit Justice Ambreze, preferred daylight and witnesses who could be seen flinching. She occupied a raised bench beneath an awning, flanked by two clerks and a clerk-of-records whose expression suggested he had been promised a quiet posting seven years ago and was still waiting for it to begin.

Company business came first, of course. It always did. The morning was consumed with cargo disputes, a wharfage fine, a salvage claim—all of it read out in the flat cadence of matters already decided. The crowd waited. The crowd was very good at waiting; dock people are paid by the hour to wait.

Then the herald called the docket, and the factor's man stepped forward—Grell's under-clerk, a soft-bellied creature named Pemmett whom I had seen once before through the slats of a fence, carrying orders I was never meant to overhear. He presented a petition: that the circuit decline jurisdiction over certain commercial matters in Vessing port, such matters being properly the province of the Halvard Company's own tribunal, per charter, per custom, per a great sheaf of documents that looked expensive and meant nothing.

Ambreze listened the way stones listen. Then she said, "The circuit sits where the crown sends it. Petition denied. Next."

Pemmett tried again, more softly, in the tone of a man mentioning that the weather might turn dangerous. Ambreze said, "Next," again, and this time it had teeth in it.

And that was my entrance cue, arranged the night before over candle stubs and cold fish: when the docket cleared, before the magistrate could adjourn for the midday tide, I would stand up and speak. Simple. A sentence, maybe two. Hale had drilled me on it. *Do not make a speech. Speeches are for after you have protection. Make a claim. Claims force procedure, and procedure is the only god these people fear.*

I stood up.

Here is what nobody tells you about courage: it feels exactly like nausea. My legs worked, which seemed like a betrayal—I had half hoped they'd refuse, giving me an honorable excuse—but they carried me forward through the crowd, which parted, and then, I noticed with a strange distant wonder, did not part entirely. They closed behind me. Dockworkers shifted left and right into the gaps I passed through, and by the time I reached the open ground before the bench there was no clear line from me to the crowd, no lane a guard could walk down quickly. I hadn't asked them for that. Dagny must have arranged it from her side, or maybe nobody arranged it at all and it was simply what the Pearlhouse knew how to do: build a wall out of bodies and call it sheltering.

"Name," said the clerk-of-records, quill ready.

I looked at him. I looked at Ambreze, who had the patience of driftwood and the attention of a hawk, and I understood that whatever happened next would happen only once.

"My name," I said, and heard my voice come out steadier than I had any right to expect, "is Sefa Vane. My father was Aldric Vane, founder of the Halvard Trading Company, recorded drowned seven years ago in the wreck of the *Merrow's Gift*."

The square went quiet in the peculiar way of harbors—not silent, because the gulls and the rigging never stop, but hushed underneath, the sound dropping away like the tide going out. Somewhere behind me a basket hit the cobbles.

"He did not drown," I said. "He is alive. He went into hiding seven years ago because he discovered that the company he built was falsifying its ledgers—that it was recording shipments twice, understating profits, and, in its overseas offices, selling the indenture contracts of children, some of them as young as five, to buyers in ports where no one checks whether a child lives out their term. When he objected, the men who run the company now arranged his death. He chose instead to arrange theirs, slowly, with paper. I know this because I was his courier, and because he is my father, and because I have spent nine months running from the company's hunters, who have known all along that the drowned man was writing letters."

Pemmett was already moving, one hand raised, mouth opening around the word *slander* like a man reaching for a familiar tool, and Hale stepped out of the crowd beside me—out of nowhere, out of nowhere is precisely where we'd planned for him to step from—and said, in his flattest auditor's voice, "If the court pleases, the undersigned is prepared to authenticate the hand. Corvin Hale, formerly senior auditor of the Halvard Trading Company, eastern district. I reconciled accounts against Vane's correspondence for eleven years. I will certify samples under oath."

"And I," said another voice, and I nearly lost my composure entirely, because it was Mama Osk, planted at the front of the crowd with her arms folded across a chest like a figurehead, "kept his books' receipts at the Pearlhouse twenty years back, and I'll swear to the hand too, if swearing's wanted, though what I mostly want sworn is what happened to my house."

What happened next was the thing I will remember until I die, and I mean that literally—I have decided it is the memory I will keep when all the others wear out. The crowd did not cheer. Crowds in stories cheer. This crowd did something better and more frightening: it moved. One pace, forward, all together, the way water moves, so that the empty ground around me filled with oyster-wives and net-menders and divers and children standing on crates to see, until there was no separating the witness from the witnesses, no line where I ended and Vessing began. Nobody shouted. Nobody had to. It was arithmetic, plain as one of Hale's columns: they could arrest one woman. They could not arrest four hundred people who had all, just now, become witnesses to a name.

Pemmett saw it too. I watched him do the same sum and arrive at the same total, watched the calculation die behind his eyes. He turned and pushed through his own guards toward the Company House, fast, a man going to report that the weather had turned.

* * *

Ambreze let the noise—or the absence of noise—run its course. Then she leaned forward on the bench, and up close her face was all angles and appraisal, a woman who had spent thirty years deciding which parts of the empire were lying to her.

"Sefa Vane," she said. "You understand what you've claimed."

"Yes, Your Honor."

"You've claimed that a man legally dead is alive. You've accused sitting charter-holders of the Halvard Company of fraud, of trafficking in indenture contracts, and—" her voice didn't change at all, which somehow made it worse, "—of attempted murder. Do you have evidence?"

"I have letters in my father's hand, authenticated by two witnesses. I have an auditor prepared to testify to systematic falsification. I have—" and here I paused, because the next part was the lie we had agreed on, the necessary one, the truth bent just enough to hold shape. "I have word that the original second ledger exists, in the company's keeping, and can be produced."

It was not strictly false. The ledger existed; Lior had seen it, through a vault door, at terrible cost. Whether it *could be produced* depended on a girl with forged papers and a diver's lungs, and on a tide table, and on Grell's men not dragging the harbor quite early enough. But magistrates deal in the difference between *exists* and *can be produced* the way sharks deal in the difference between blood and water, so I let the ambiguity sit there and look confident.

Pemmett returned—faster than I'd expected, which meant Grell had been expecting this day and had instructions pre-written—and rose to protest. "Your Honor, this court is being asked to entertain the fantasies of a runaway and a dismissed employee. The company's records are open to any proper inspection—"

"They are," Ambreze agreed mildly. "Produce the second ledger for inspection."

A pause. A beautiful pause, four seconds long, worth more to me than the whole rest of the morning.

"The court misunderstands," Pemmett said. "There is no second ledger."

"Then the claim is easily disproved and your principals are safe," said Ambreze. "Odd that you find that reassuring and not insulting." She turned back to me, and I saw her make her decision the way sailors make decisions about weather: not hopefully, not kindly, just correctly. "Vessing is the last sitting of the season. The circuit departs when its business concludes. Fraud upon a chartered company, trafficking in contracts, and the status of a presumed-dead founder are crown matters and I'll hear them. I will not hear them on rumor." She glanced at the light, gauged the hours remaining. "Evidence begins at dawn tomorrow. First sitting. If your letters, your witnesses, and your ledger appear, they'll be heard. If they don't—" she looked at me with something that was not quite sympathy and not quite warning, "—the claims die with the light, and I'd think carefully, Miss Vane, about what survives a night in this harbor."

She struck the flat of her hand on the bench, and it was done.

* * *

Afterward the crowd broke apart the way crowds do, reluctantly, in eddies, everyone wanting to touch my sleeve or press something into my hands—an apple, a twist of tobacco, a dried fig that had clearly survived better intentions. Dagny reached me first, out of custody barely two days and wearing her freedom like an ill-fitting coat, and gripped my shoulders hard enough to bruise.

"Dawn," she said. "That's nothing. That's one tide."

"That's one night," I corrected. "One night in which Grell knows exactly what has to disappear before sunrise."

"Then we'd best go collect it before he finishes his supper." She grinned, and there was iron under the grin; jail had either broken Dagny or tempered her, and I saw now which it had been. "Lior's already gone to the beds, hasn't she?"

"She went at slack water." Which meant Lior was, at this moment, somewhere under the gray skin of the harbor with a wax-sealed ledger strapped beneath a fake oyster bed while Grell's boats dragged the water above her. I had sent her with a blessing and a lie—told her I'd wait at the seawall, knowing I'd be here, knowing one of us had to be in the square at dawn regardless of what the tide gave back. That is the arithmetic of conspiracies: someone always has to be the one who waits, and someone always has to be the one who doesn't.

Hale appeared at my elbow, pale but composed, rebuttoning his coat as though he'd merely concluded routine business. "You did well," he said. "Excessively well. You've forced procedure, and procedure is armor. But understand what you bought." He nodded toward the Company House, where lamplight was already blooming in windows that should have been dark. "You told Grell the deadline. Now he has one too. Everything he owns depends on that ledger never reaching this square, and he has one night, full authority, and no further reason to be careful."

"Then we'd best hope," I said, watching the harbor lights come on one by one across the black water, "that the sea is still on our side."

Out past the breakwater, where the channel markers bobbed, a boat was putting out quietly with no lantern lit.
