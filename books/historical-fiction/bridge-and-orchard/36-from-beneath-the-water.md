## 36. From Beneath the Water

Slack tide comes at the hour when the harbor can't decide what it wants to be. The water goes glassy and thoughtful, the current forgets its arguments, and for perhaps forty minutes the sea holds its breath. My mother used to say those minutes were the sea being fair — the only time in a diver's life when nothing was trying to kill you out of simple indifference.

She also died diving, so I have always taken her opinions on fairness with a grain of salt. Several grains. A whole shaker.

I stood at the tideline below the ruined Pearlhouse with the waxed bundle under my arm and watched the water do its undecided shimmering, and I thought: *well. Here is where she'd tell me I was an idiot.* Because I was about to walk into that fair, breath-held water carrying the only true account of nine years of stolen children, while Grell's men — who had been dragging the harbor since midnight with grappling hooks and lanterns and the patience of men paid by the day — were somewhere behind the headland, coming back around on their third pass.

The bundle was a brick of oilcloth and sealing wax, roughly the size of a prayer book and considerably less comforting. Inside it: the second ledger, pried from the Company House vault two nights ago at the cost of my pulse, Hale's freedom, and a quantity of my dignity I don't care to itemize. I had sealed it myself, kneeling in Dagny's brother's cellar, pouring wax over the seams until my fingers smelled like a tallow chandler's regret. The sea had kept my family's secrets before. It seemed only polite to trust it with this one.

"You're doing the thing," said a voice behind me. "The standing-on-the-shore-looking-doomed thing."

Pell was eleven, orphaned twice over — once by fever, once by the fire — and had appointed himself my conscience sometime around the burning of the Pearlhouse, which was a promotion nobody voted on. He was wrapped in one of Osk's old shawls and he was holding, inexplicably, a boiled egg.

"Go home," I said.

"Home burned down." He said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather, and something in my chest did its familiar fold. "I live in a cellar now. With smugglers. It's very educational."

"Pell—"

"Mama Osk says if you drown, I'm to have your boots." He handed me the egg. "So eat something first. Boots stretch."

I took the egg because arguing with children raised on dockside arithmetic is a losing proposition, and because my hands were shaking and holding something helped. I ate it in four bites, standing there like a gull, and Pell watched the horizon with the seriousness of a harbormaster.

"They came around the point ten minutes ago," he said. "Three boats. Lanterns on short poles, low to the water so they don't show against the sky. They're dragging the channel now. By slack tide they'll be over the oyster beds."

Of course they would be. Grell wasn't stupid; that was his most annoying quality. He knew I hadn't left Vessing — the roads were watched, the Tide Gate was watching harder — and he knew I hadn't gone inland, because Sefa had been seen in the fish market yesterday with her bandaged shoulder and her terrible poker face, which meant the packet was still close. Where does a diver's child hide a thing? Not in a wall. Not in a floor. We hide things the way our mothers taught us: under the water, where company men won't follow, because company men sink.

"He'll find it," I said. "If he drags long enough."

"He'll find a bed of oysters," Pell said. "And about thirty other things. Old Kessin lost an anchor chain there in the spring. There's a cannon ball somebody swears is from the war but is probably just a rock." He shrugged. "The harbor's full of everything. That's the point of a harbor."

* * *

Getting into cold water at gray dawn is a negotiation your body loses every time. The first shock is a fist to the ribs; the second is a long argument between your lungs and your sense of self-preservation; and then there's the third stage, the one divers know and landfolk never learn to love, where the cold stops being an enemy and becomes simply a fact, like gravity, like hunger, like the company.

I went in off the rocks north of the beds, low and quiet, wading until the water took my weight, then swimming flat and slow with the bundle slung across my chest in a net harness Dagny had knotted for me before they took her — God keep her, God rot the jail walls around her — out of good tarred twine that wouldn't shine or snag. The scar on my ribs ached in the cold, the way it always did. Mother-of-pearl scar, Mama Osk called it once, seeing it in lamplight. Shiny where the world bit you.

Down I went.

The oyster beds are a drowned country. You feel them before you see them — the bottom changes texture, shell and shell-hash and the particular silt that oysters make by the simple industry of living — and then the light comes down gray-green through the water and you see the beds themselves, long pale ridges of them, like a field under snow. My mother worked these beds for six years. She could hold her breath longer than anyone in Vessing and she taught me that the trick isn't the lungs at all. The trick is being willing to be, for three minutes, a thing that doesn't breathe.

I found the spot by counting strokes. Forty-two from the split rock, down along the second ridge, where the bed drops into a scoured hollow that the current keeps clean. Three nights ago, in the dark, I had wedged the ledger-wax into that hollow under a slab of stone, and I had left a marker only a diver would read: a broken oyster shell set upright, hinge-down, like a tiny door.

The door was still shut. The slab was still seated. Nobody had found it.

For one whole moment, floating above that hollow with my hair streaming and my lungs beginning their complaint, I felt something so foreign I nearly laughed into the water, and it took me a moment to name it. Relief. Actual relief. Nine years of keeping my head down and here, finally, was proof that invisibility worked —

The lantern light swept across the surface above me.

I went flat against the bottom before I thought about it, which is diver instinct, older than thinking. Through perhaps a fathom and a half of green water I saw it: a broad yellow smear sliding over the beds, the reflection of a lantern hung low on a pole, exactly as Pell had described. Beneath it, faintly, the sound of the boat — oar-locks muffled with rag, which told me everything about the sort of work Grell's men expected to be doing. Honest fishermen don't muffle their oars.

Then came the grapnel. I heard it hit the water with a slap, heard the chain run out, and then I felt it: the heavy iron drag of a three-fluked hook scraping along the bottom toward me, plowing a furrow through the oyster hash, clumsy as a drunk in a chapel.

There is a specific quality to fear underwater. On land, fear makes you fast. Underwater, fear spends your air, and air is the only currency you carry. So I made myself do the slow thing: I slid my hands under the slab, worried the wax bundle free — it came heavier than I remembered, as though the ledger had been eating stones down there — hugged it to my chest, and began to move along the hollow, away from the furrow the grapnel was plowing. Kick, glide. Kick, glide. Don't fight the water. Be the water. Be a thing the water barely notices, the way my mother was, the way I spent nine years being on dry land—

The grapnel caught the slab. I heard the iron bite stone with a sound that travels through water into your teeth, and then the chain went taut and the slab tore up out of the hollow behind me like a grave opening in reverse, and the whole bed erupted in silt.

They'd found *something*. Not the right something, not yet — what came up on their hook was a forty-pound slab of harbor stone, and I'd have given a great deal to watch the face of the man who hauled it aboard — but they knew the beds were worth dragging now. They'd come back. They'd come back with more boats, and finer hooks, and all the time in the world, because Grell pays by the day and I pay in lungs.

My chest was burning. The surface was a smear of lantern-yellow directly ahead, right where the boat sat. Between me and open water lay forty feet of lit-up shallows and three men with a hook.

Kick, glide. Kick, glide. The bundle pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. My vision had begun to gather its dark edges in — that beautiful, treacherous softness at the corners of the world, the sea offering you a bargain it has offered every diver since the first one: *stop struggling, and it stops hurting.*

That's when the water filled with people.

* * *

I saw them as shapes first — four, five, six slim shadows dropping off the hulls of the drag-boats in perfect silence, no splash, no fuss, entering the water the way Pearlhouse children entered it, which is to say the way you enter your own house. Children of the beds. Kids I'd copied manifests beside, fed at supper, taught their letters on the backs of waybills. Joss, who was fourteen and could out-hold any grown man in the harbor. The Tam twins, twelve and merciless. Little Wren, who was ten and weighed less than my bundle.

None of them looked at me. That was the discipline of it, the thing that made my throat close up worse than the drowning did: they surfaced *around* me, spread loose across forty feet of water between my position and the lantern-light, and began — with the artless perfection of genuine accident — to be children in the water at dawn.

They splashed. They shrieked. Joss bellowed something about who'd pinched whom; the Tams commenced a wrestling match of tremendous violence and zero progress; Wren floated on her back directly beneath the drag-boat's bow and sang, at the top of a voice far too large for her body, one of Osk's net-mending songs, the ones with the filthy verses that everyone pretends not to know.

"Oi!" A man's voice, furious, from above. "Clear off! Company water! Clear off, you little guttersharks!"

"We're *swimming!*" Joss shouted back, aggrieved, treading water precisely over the furrow the grapnel had cut, churning it to foam with his kicks. "It's a free harbor!"

"It's *company* harbor, you—"

"It's the *sea*, you great barnacle, did you buy the *sea*?"

I have never loved anyone more than I loved that insolent boy in that moment, and I told him so later, and he said, "You can buy me an eel pie and we'll call it even."

Underwater, I moved. Kick, glide, kick, glide, low and flat beneath the churn and noise, under the shadow of their splashing legs, past the stern of the drag-boat so close I could have touched the rudder — and the men above couldn't put a hook down, couldn't net, couldn't do anything except shout, because to grab at one of those children in front of the others, in view of the shore, at dawn, with the whole harbor waking — well. Grell's method was quiet. Six splashing children at first light was the loudest thing in Vessing.

Wren's song followed me out. I could hear it through the water, warped and huge, some nonsense about a fisherman's wife and a minister's hat, and I thought, absurdly, of my mother, of every diver who had ever sung in the water to prove to the sea she wasn't afraid, and I kicked, and glided, and came up gasping behind the breakwater with the ledger still against my heart.

Behind me, the argument escalated magnificently. Someone threatened someone's mother. A boot was thrown — I heard it hit water. And then, on some signal I never saw, the children dove as one, six clean silent dives, vanishing like the tide going out, leaving three furious company men in a boat surrounded by nothing but their own bad luck and one floating boiled-egg shell I'd been too greedy to finish ashore.

* * *

Dagny's brother met me at the smokehouse slip with a blanket and no questions, which is the highest form of hospitality the coast offers. I sat on the boards dripping, ledger in my lap, and shook — not from cold, or not only from cold. From the arithmetic of it. Six children had put themselves between me and the company's iron, and the company's iron does not distinguish. I had spent nine years learning to be unremarkable so that no harm would ever come looking for me, and here I sat, extremely remarkable, having just been defended by people with milk teeth.

"You're grinning," Rye observed, handing me tea that tasted like smoked fish because everything in the smokehouse did.

"I'm having a nervous event."

"That's what grinning's for." He squinted at the bundle. "Is that it, then? The whole thing?"

"The whole thing." Nine years of double entries. Names. Every child whose contract traveled east in one column and vanished in another. My own number was in there somewhere; I hadn't let myself look yet. Some pages you read only once, in front of witnesses, where you can't burn them.

Rye nodded slowly. "Magistrate sits at dawn. Market square. Sefa's already gone to the square — half the harbor's gone. Fishermen are leaving their boats tied up, that's how you know it's real." He looked out at the brightening water, where three company boats were still circling the oyster beds, hauling up rocks in their hooks, growing smaller and more ridiculous as the light rose. "Grell knows too. He's got men all round the square. Whole square's going to look innocent and be armed, both sides."

"Both sides?"

"Lior." Rye gave me the gentle, pitying look dockfolk reserve for clerks. "Every man jack in that square has lost someone to a ledger. You think they're staying home for the reading?"

I got dressed. Wet shirt, wet everything; there wasn't time and I didn't care. I wrapped the ledger in the blanket and held it against my chest the way you carry a sleeping child, and I started up the path from the smokehouse toward the town, and the light was coming up gold over the harbor, and the bell in the market tower began to ring — not the alarm, not the tide bell, but the old summons bell, the one they say hasn't sounded for a sitting of the circuit in twenty years. Somebody had climbed the tower. Somebody had decided this morning deserved ringing.

Halfway up the hill I met Pell running down it, breathless, eggless, incandescent.

"They're starting!" he shouted. "Sefa's on the steps! And Lior—" He grabbed my sleeve, and his face did something complicated, joy and terror sharing the same small real estate. "Grell just walked into the square with forty men. He's *coming to the hearing.*"

Of course he was. I shouldered past him uphill, ledger against my heart, wet boots hammering cobbles, toward the square where a magistrate, a dead man's daughter, and every name in my arms were waiting for the tide to turn.

Slack water was over.
