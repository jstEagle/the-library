## 37. The Reading of the Ledger

I have stood in the market square of Vessing perhaps ten thousand times. I know its cobblestones the way I know the scar on my palm—every uneven flag, every place the tide-salt has eaten a groove into the mortar between the stalls. For nine years I moved through that square like smoke, head down, ledger under my arm, invisible as a clerk is meant to be.

So it was a strange and terrible thing to stand in it at dawn and be *looked at*.

They had come in the dark. That was Dagny's doing; she said if you want a crowd that holds, you feed it first, and so the Pearlhouse survivors—what was left of us—had set out bread and smoked fish and hot barley tea along the well, the way you'd lay out a funeral feast. And that's what it felt like. A funeral. Or possibly a wedding. With Grell involved, there was no telling which until someone got stabbed.

The magistrate's platform had been raised against the customs house steps, and Magistrate Ellery Voss herself sat behind it, a small dry woman with spectacles and the patient expression of someone who has spent thirty years listening to men lie about grain weights. Sefa stood beside her, having given her name to the circuit the evening before—*Aldric Vane's daughter*, spoken aloud in the market square where anyone could hear it—and the town had heard it, and the town had come.

Divers stood at the front with their knives still on their belts, because some arguments are best conducted close at hand. Net-menders. Oyster-men. The turnkey whose sister went overseas in a company hold, holding his hat like he wasn't sure he was allowed to be off duty. Mama Osk sat on a bench near the front with her ribs strapped, refusing all assistance with the ferocity of a woman who has decided exactly how much weakness she will permit herself in public, and it is none.

And me. Lior Nystrom, indenture number 4471, standing in the middle of everything, holding a book.

Not just any book. The second ledger, pulled from the Company House vault at slack tide while men dragged the harbor for it, sealed in wax and hidden beneath the waterline among my mother's oysters, retrieved by lamplight while children of the Pearlhouse surfaced around me like a ring of shields. It smelled of brine and tallow. The cover was warped from the sea, which felt correct. Everything the company built, the sea should get to warp eventually.

"Read," said Magistrate Voss, without ceremony.

So I opened it, and I read.

* * *

You should understand something about ledgers: they are not neutral. People say *it's just numbers*, as though numbers were weather, as though arithmetic fell from the sky and nobody chose it. But every column is a choice made by a hand, and every hand belongs to a person who could have written differently and did not.

I read the shipment columns first, because that was the order of the book. Pearls sold twice—once honestly, once into accounts that did not exist, the difference siphoned into a name called the Meridian Consortium, which turned out, when Hale took the stand, to be a partnership of four men, three of whom sat on the company board. Hale testified the way he does everything: precisely, joylessly, like a man doing long division in a burning building.

"I audited these entries six years ago," he said. "I found the discrepancy. I reported it to Factor Grell." A pause, the length of a man deciding to stop lying. "I then amended my report to remove the discrepancy. In exchange, my position was confirmed. I have since counted, conservatively, eleven thousand crowns diverted through falsified double-entry. My signature appears on documents attesting that I found nothing. I am here to attest now that I found everything."

He looked smaller when he stepped down. Not lighter. There's a difference. Confession takes weight off and puts weight back on in a different shape, and you carry it anyway.

Then came the part we had all come for, whether we knew it or not.

The contract register. Page after page of names—not numbers, *names*, because whoever kept this ledger had been careless in the specific way that cruel people are careless, writing down actual children for actual buyers in actual ports across the water. Torren Beck, age eight, sold to the herring fleets. Mirela Oskarsdatter, age ten. A boy listed only as "the diver's kid, N—" which stopped my breath in my throat for three full lines because I thought—I genuinely thought—

It wasn't me. My contract had been altered, not sold; Mama Osk told me that in the oyster beds two nights ago, how the Nystrom indenture was written strange on purpose, marked early, held back from the overseas lists by hands she wouldn't name yet. But reading that entry, seeing the *N-* trailing off into ink, I understood for the first time how close the margin had been. How thin the paper wall between my life and Torren Beck's.

I read the names aloud. All of them. Forty-one children over nine years.

The square did not riot. I had expected, somehow, rioting—noise, fury, something operatic. Instead there was silence, the heavy kind, and somewhere in it a woman began quietly to weep, and I recognized her, distantly, as the mother of one of the names. And then an old oyster-man said, in a voice like gravel settling, "Say that one again," and I read his granddaughter's name again, slowly, and he nodded as though receiving goods he had paid for long ago.

That was worse than rioting. That will stay with me longer than any of it.

Grell stood at the edge of the platform the whole time. I'll grant him this: he didn't look away. He watched the reading the way a man watches a flood come up his street—assessing, calculating, waiting to see if it would crest the sill or stop short. And when it crested—when Voss ordered the register entered into the circuit record and the clerk-of-court began transcribing names into the official rolls of the crown—Grell stopped calculating and started acting.

* * *

I saw him signal. Everyone saw him signal, afterward; but at the time it was nothing, a factor touching his collar, crossing the platform, murmuring to the six company guards posted at the customs house steps. Our arrangement with the magistrate had included those guards—the company's own men, assigned to keep order, a concession Grell had made because refusing would have looked like guilt. Six men in company gray, standing between the court and the crowd.

Grell spoke to their sergeant. Low. Then he straightened his coat and walked to the center of the platform, and in a voice trained by twenty years of dictating letters, he said:

"This proceeding is irregular. The document before the court is stolen property, obtained by trespass and theft, and I invoke the company's charter right to recover its effects. Sergeant—secure the ledger and detain the thieves."

And the sergeant—a big weathered man named Brakke, whom I had passed a thousand times at the Tide Gate and never once looked in the eye—did not move.

"Grell," Brakke said, and the whole square heard a company guard address the factor by bare surname, "read me the fourth name on that page."

I want it noted for history that I hesitated, because I thought it was a trick, and then I read: "*Mirela Oskarsdatter, age ten, sold to the southern fisheries, autumn contract.*"

"My sister," said the guard next to Brakke. Not loudly. Just saying it, like setting something down that he'd carried too long.

Another guard, younger, near the end of the line: "Torren Beck. He was in my mother's Sunday class."

Nobody shouted. That's the thing I keep returning to, the thing I'll tell Sefa years later when she asks me what it was like: it was quiet. Six men in company gray turned—slowly, without drama, the way a tide turns—to face inward, toward the platform, and Grell stood alone in front of his own court with his own guards at his back looking at him the way you look at a ledger entry that doesn't reconcile.

"Arrest them," Grell said. "That is an order."

"Sergeant Brakke," said Magistrate Voss, taking off her spectacles with the air of a woman who has waited thirty years to do precisely this, "the court thanks you for your restraint. Factor Grell, you are detained pending inquiry into conspiracy, fraud, trafficking of persons under indenture, and obstruction of a crown circuit. Do not make it necessary for me to ask again."

Brakke took Grell's arm. Gently, even—almost courteously—which somehow made it more final. Grell looked out at the square then, past all of us, at the harbor, and for one moment his face did something I didn't expect. Not rage. Arithmetic. You could see him running the sums: guards lost, charter suspended coming, testimony stacking up column after column, and the total coming due. A man discovering that he had been, all along, merely an entry in someone else's larger book.

He did not struggle. He went down the customs house steps between two of his own former guards, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts, without malice and without mercy, and I watched until he was gone because some things you owe your eyes.

* * *

Afterward, the square did what squares do: it exhaled, and then it became itself again. Tea went around. Someone started crying and laughing at once. Dagny appeared at my elbow with bread she had almost certainly not paid for and pressed it into my hands like a medal.

"You read good," she said. "For a clerk."

"For a clerk," I agreed.

Hale came and stood beside me, looking at the warped, salt-stained book in my lap as though it were a relation he had complicated feelings about. Which, in fairness, it was.

"Eleven thousand crowns," he said softly. "Forty-one children. And the whole time it was sitting in a vault forty feet from my office." He shook his head. "I audited that room, Nystrom. I inventoried the shelves."

"You looked away," I said. Not cruelly. Just checking the arithmetic.

"I looked away," he said. "And then a clerk with an oilskin packet looked back." He glanced at me sideways. "Your mother dove the east beds, yes? Before the reclamation?"

The question caught me off-guard. "How did you know that?"

"I've been reading the old manifests since they suspended me," he said. "There's a lot I haven't finished reading." He said it lightly, but Hale never says anything without a column of intent behind it, and I filed that away—one more number that didn't reconcile yet, waiting for its entry.

Sefa found me as the crowd thinned. She looked hollowed-out and lit-from-within at the same time, the way I imagine a candle feels. Her father's name was in the record now—restored, contested by no one, entered in the roll beside the words *wrongfully declared dead*. Whatever came next, whatever the inquiry dug up about where Aldric Vane had gone to ground across the water, she had done the thing she came ashore to do.

"He'd have liked the reading," she said. "The aloudness of it. He always said paper only tells the truth once somebody reads it out where the guilty can hear."

"Your father sounds like a man who talked a lot for someone who wrote letters instead."

She laughed—cracked in the middle, real. Then she sobered, looking down at the ledger. "Voss wants it copied before it goes to the capital. Three copies. She asked who could be trusted with the work." A pause, and a small wicked tilt to her mouth. "I may have mentioned I knew an excellent clerk."

"I'm not a clerk anymore," I said. "My contract burns tomorrow."

"No," Sefa said. "Tomorrow your contract burns. Today you're the only person in Vessing who can copy a ledger without dropping a line." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Come on. The magistrate's clerk-of-court has terrible handwriting and I promised Voss you'd fix at least one thing in this town before spring."

But we didn't get three steps before a runner from the jail pushed through the thinning crowd, cap in hand, breathing hard, and found Magistrate Voss on the platform.

"Your honor," he said, loud enough that half the square stopped turning away. "Factor Grell's chamber pot was found empty this morning. His cell door was locked from outside. There's a window in the cells that opens over the fish-market tunnels, and the turnkey swears it was barred." He swallowed. "He's gone. Grell's gone into the harbor quarter, and he took the original first ledger with him."

The square went very quiet for the second time that morning.

Beside me, Dagny cracked her knuckles and looked at the mouth of the fish-market tunnels, dark and waiting below the square like a held breath.

"Well," she said. "He can't swim forever."
