## 38. Aftermath of Truth

I have spent thirty-one years of my life believing that arithmetic was the closest thing to honesty the world offered. Numbers do not flatter. Numbers do not weep or perjure themselves. A column either reconciles or it does not, and I built a career on standing at the right side of that question—or telling myself I did, which amounts to nearly the same thing in the trade of ledgers.

So it is a particular kind of humiliation, on the morning after the reading, to find myself weeping over a column.

The market square still smells of smoke and fish brine and the tallow of too many candles burned overnight. The magistrate's clerks have been at the second ledger since before dawn, and they work the way I used to work—two readers, one calling figures, one confirming against the letters and the first ledger, a third recording everything in fair hand. Column by column. Name by name.

I sit where they have told me to sit, at a folding table near the dais, because I am still technically under guard until Magistrate Erren says otherwise, and I listen to names I audited seven years ago read aloud into the cold spring air.

"Contract 4471, sold to the Meridian Combine, Port Andel. Age eleven. Female."

A woman in the crowd makes a sound like a gull being stepped on, and then she is quiet again, because everyone here has learned this week how to be quiet while waiting.

I know that contract. Of course I know it. I reconciled it. That was my job, the year I turned twenty-six: to take the ugly truth of what the Halvard Trading Company did with children's papers and make it *balance*. Not to hide it—the hiding was done above my head, by men whose names were never in the books they signed. My job was smaller and therefore more damning. I made the sums agree. I found the offsetting entries. I chose the round numbers that would pass an inspection, because a forger's great mistake is always precision, and I was precise.

I told myself then what every auditor tells himself: *I am not the one who sells the children. I am the one who writes down what was already sold.* As if the pen were innocent. As if ink had never drowned anyone.

The clerk's voice drones on. "Contract 4489, sold to the Coriander House, Sarneth. Age nine. Male."

Somewhere in that crowd is a mother, or there was one, once. Somewhere in Sarneth, if he survived the oyster beds there—and the oyster beds always take their tithe—is a boy of sixteen who does not know his own surname was struck from a company roll in exchange for ninety marks and a falsified entry.

I did that math. I did it well. I want someone to understand that I did it *well*, because that is the whole obscenity of it: competence. The company did not need monsters. It needed men like me, who took pride in clean work.

* * *

They bring Grell down from the Company House at midmorning, and the square goes still in the way water goes still before it decides whether to be a wave.

Factor Grell has been Factor Grell for as long as anyone in Vessing can remember, which is to say he has been the company's face, its voice, its signature on every indenture and every denial. He is not a large man. He has never needed to be; the charter behind him was large enough for both of us. He walks between the magistrate's marshals with his hands bound in front of him—not dragged, not shoved, though God knows the crowd would permit it—and he holds his head at the angle of a man reviewing a shipment manifest. Inspecting. Unimpressed.

His eyes find me at my table. Of course they do. We have been circling each other for months, Grell and I, two men who both understood that paper is the only weapon worth fearing, and I watch him perform the calculation he cannot help performing: *Hale testified. Hale broke.* His mouth does something that is almost a smile, and I realize with a lurch that it is not contempt. It is acknowledgment. He would have done the same, in my place, if it had served him. He simply cannot conceive that it didn't.

He stops in front of me. The marshals let him, because even chained he is only a soft man in a good coat, and because they are curious.

"Auditor," he says.

"Factor."

"You always double-checked your carry digits." It is not a question, and it is not quite an insult. It is the nearest thing to respect Grell knows how to render. "I used to tell the board you were the most honest man in Vessing."

"And you were right," I say. "That was the problem. You needed me dishonest for six more years."

Something moves behind his eyes—irritation, maybe, or the first cold draft of comprehension—but the marshals move him along before he can answer, and the crowd parts around him the way it parted for him for twenty years, out of habit, and then closes behind him like water, and he is only a man in chains going where chains go.

The charter suspension is posted on the counting-house door within the hour. I read it twice, though I could have recited it: *the charter of the Halvard Trading Company is suspended in all its operations pending full inquiry; all contracts, indentures, and obligations issued under said charter are hereby stayed; the seal of the circuit supersedes the company seal in all matters.* Below it, in smaller script, the amendment I came downstairs early to see copied:

*Further, the record of death for Aldric Edran Vane, entered in the seventh year prior, is amended: the said Aldric Vane lives, the said death was procured by fraud, and the said Vane's name is restored to the rolls of the company he founded.*

Restored to the rolls. Seven years of tide over a man's grave that was never a grave, and now ink un-drowns him. I confess I felt something close to vertigo reading it, standing in the counting-house doorway in the gray light, because this is the thing about ledgers that no auditor admits: entries feel permanent. Amending one feels like breaking the world's spine. And yet here was the proof that it can be done—that a lie written well enough to survive seven years can be unwritten in a morning, provided enough people are willing to stand in a square and be counted.

Vane himself was not there, of course. Still in exile, still across the water, somewhere north where even Sefa's letters grew thin. His daughter stood in for him yesterday, and I watched her do it—a slight woman with salt-scoured hands, saying her father's name into a silence that had been paid for with a Pearlhouse burned to its foundations and a smuggler's cellar full of frightened people and, if I am honest, six years of my own cowardice compounded like interest.

She named herself. In front of the circuit, in front of Grell's guards, in front of half the harbor. *I am Sefa Vane, and Aldric Vane is my father.* I have heard men testify to murders with less fear in their voices.

* * *

In the afternoon they take my testimony again, formally, and this time it is not arithmetic.

Magistrate Erren is a dry woman with ink-stained cuffs and the patience of a person who has spent thirty years listening to liars, and she does not thank me when I finish. She does not condemn me either. She asks the question I have been dreading, and she asks it without cruelty, which makes it worse.

"Auditor Hale, you state that you identified the child-contract sales as irregular in the seventh year prior to today. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And you did not report them."

"I did not."

"Why?"

There it is. The whole of me, asked in one syllable. I could give her the true answer, which is that I wanted to keep my post, my rooms, my reputation, my small clean life of reconciled columns. Or I could dress it up, and I have dressed things up before—it is half my trade.

I gave her the truth.

"Because reporting them would have cost me everything I had," I said, "and I valued what I had more than I valued the children. That is the whole of it. There is no finer version."

The square was quiet. Somewhere in it, I knew, Lior Nystrom was listening—young Nystrom, who lost nine years to a fraudulent indenture and their mother to the sea and who had every right in the world to spit at me—and when I finished I made myself look in that direction, because looking away seemed like one more small dishonesty, and I have resolved, somewhat late, to stop practicing those.

Nystrom did not spit. They looked at me with an expression I am still trying to file anywhere in my system of categories, and after a moment I understood it was neither forgiveness nor its opposite. It was assessment. The look of someone deciding what I am worth going forward, which is, I suppose, the only look that matters now. The past has been read aloud. The past is settled arithmetic. What remains open is the running balance.

Erren struck my testimony formally, ordered it entered, and then said the sentence I will carry longer than any fine:

"The inquiry notes that Auditor Hale's evidence was material and timely. The inquiry also notes that Auditor Hale knew, and did not speak, for six years. Both facts are true. Both are entered."

Both are entered. That is all I ever wanted from the ledgers, and it is the cruelest thing anyone has ever done to me.

* * *

Evening comes down over Vessing the way it always has, indifferent and gray-gold, and I walk the harbor because my rooms above the counting-house are no longer mine—the counting-house is sealed, everything under writ, my own files impounded, which means that for the first time in thirty-one years I own nothing but my handwriting, and I find, to my considerable surprise, that I am not distressed.

The Pearlhouse ruin still smolders faintly at the dock end, a thin thread of smoke rising like something reluctant to leave. They have begun to clear it—I can see figures moving among the blackened joists, hauling charred timber, and among them, unless my eyes deceive me, young Nystrom, sleeves rolled, doing the work of four people out of what appears to be pure refusal to grieve slowly.

Dagny is out too—released yesterday when the turnkey's nerve finally broke and the jail simply emptied of people who should never have been in it. I saw her briefly at the reading: a broad weathered woman with a net-mender's hands and a brother's grievance, and she looked at me the way half the harbor looks at me now, which is to say, measuring the exact distance between *useful witness* and *coward who got paid for six years*. Fair. Entirely fair.

But it is Mama Osk I find at the tideline, sitting on an overturned dory with a shawl around her shoulders, watching the water do what water does.

I almost walk past. I have been walking past Osk for twenty years; she runs the Pearlhouse the way I run a ledger, everything entered, everything remembered, and we have exchanged perhaps forty words in two decades, all of them about fish prices. But something in the arithmetic of the day compels me, and I stop.

"Mama Osk."

"Auditor." She does not smile. She pats the dory beside her anyway, which is more than I deserve, and I sit, and for a while we watch the tide come in over the flats where the divers work.

"They'll rebuild," she says eventually. "Pearlhouse. Same footings. Wood is wood."

"Yes."

"You'll stay in Vessing, then."

It is not a question either, and I consider lying to her, and then I consider that I have spent a fortune of lies in my life and am bankrupt. "I don't know where else I'd go. I don't know that I deserve to stay."

"Deserving." She says it the way you'd spit out a bad mussel. "The sea doesn't ask if you deserve to come back in. It just takes you or it doesn't." She turns and looks at me properly then, and her eyes are old diver's eyes, and they have seen drowned things and brought them up anyway. "You knew. Six years. You're going to carry that, good. Carry it. But you got on that stand and you said it plain, and plain is rare, and the harbor remembers plain." She looks back at the water. "The new inquiry will need an honest auditor. Someone who knows exactly how the rot was done, from the inside of the sums. Someone who won't pretend it was other men's hands."

"You're proposing I apply for a position."

"I'm proposing you stop drowning yourself in the shallows and get to work." She stands, joints cracking like rigging, and gathers her shawl. "Come by the Pearlhouse tomorrow. We'll need numbers for the rebuilding, and I'm not having some charter-man cheat me on lumber. You'll do. Probably."

She walks off toward the smoke and the working figures, and I remain on the overturned dory like a man who has just been sentenced and pardoned in the same breath, which, I suppose, is precisely what has occurred.

* * *

Night, now. The lamps of Vessing prickle along the waterfront, and the magistrate's clerks have gone to their cots, and the second ledger lies locked in a strongbox under triple watch, and somewhere across the water a dead-alive man waits for news that his name exists again.

Victory tastes strange. I keep reaching for the word and finding only textures: it tastes like the chalk dust of erased figures, like brine, like the specific metallic tang of a pen held too long. It tastes like a column that balances perfectly and tells you nothing about what was bought to make it balance.

We won. Say it plainly, since plain is the currency now: we won. The charter is suspended. Grell went to chains. The children's names are in the record where names belong. And yet I keep thinking of Contract 4471, age eleven, female, Port Andel, and I would give every entry I ever made to know if she is alive tonight, and if she knows, and if knowing would come to her as rescue or merely as weather.

Tomorrow the Pearlhouse numbers. Tomorrow the slow, unglamorous labor of making what was broken reconcile—which is, I begin to suspect, the only honest prayer I have ever known how to say.

But tonight there is one more piece of business. A runner found me at the tideline an hour ago—a scrap of a boy from the circuit's own staff, breathless, hand-picked, which means the message matters. The note is brief, in Erren's cramped hand:

*Aldric Vane sails for Vessing. Arrives within the fortnight, under the circuit's protection. The daughter is not yet informed. She will want to meet him at the Tide Gate, and the Tide Gate will want witnesses. Be one of them, Auditor. You of all people should see a name come home.*

I fold the note and put it inside my coat, over the heart, where I used to keep the key to the second ledger, and I stand a long time looking at the black water that kept all our secrets and has finally, grudgingly, given most of them up.

A fortnight. A father. A daughter who does not yet know.

Well then. It seems the account is not yet closed, and I—late, disgraced, and for the first time in thirty-one years honest—find that I should very much like to see how this one balances.
