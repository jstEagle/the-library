## 39. Contracts Burned

The magistrate's clerk read my name wrong.

Not the company number—I'd burned that bridge myself, or rather forged my way across it—but my actual name, the one I'd signed on the transit papers like a confession: *Lior Nystrom*. He pronounced it with a soft *o*, as if it were a foreign coin he wasn't sure was gold. Nine years in the Halvard Company's counting-house and no one had ever said my name aloud in that building. I'd have laughed if my stomach weren't somewhere around my knees.

"Step forward," the magistrate said.

The market square had been scrubbed by rain two days prior, but Vessing never stays clean for long. Fish scales glittered in the cobbles like a spill of coins no one would stoop for. The scaffold that had held the reading of the ledger now held a table, and on the table lay a chest, and in the chest—stacked like kindling, which was precisely what they were about to become—the contracts.

I found them easily. Mine was thinner than most, one page folded twice, the ink faded to the brown of old seawater. The Nystrom indenture. Written when I was eleven years old and still smelled of brine. Signed by a company factor whose name I'd copied a hundred times since without once thinking of him as a man. There was a clause in it I'd never been allowed to read. Mama Osk had told me why it was written so strangely: the company had marked me early, set me aside like a pearl of uncertain luster, priced for a market across the sea.

The magistrate read the proclamation in a voice built for it. "All indentures executed under the Halvard Trading Company within the period under inquiry are hereby declared void, having been found fraudulent in instrument, in consideration, and in intent. The persons bound thereby are free."

*Free.* A word like a door left open in winter. Everyone in the square heard it differently. The diver children from the Pearlhouse heard it as permission to stop flinching. Dagny, standing with her arms crossed and her wrists still ringed with the bruises of the company jail, heard it as an apology that would never be spoken properly. Hale heard it, I think, as arithmetic finally balancing—he stood at the edge of the crowd with his ledger-keeper's posture, and when the word landed he closed his eyes for exactly one breath.

I heard it as a sound I didn't recognize.

"Bring the brazier," the magistrate said.

They'd set it up at the foot of the scaffold: an iron basin on legs, coals already breathing orange. A company clerk—not mine anymore, one of the magistrate's own men—began feeding contracts into it one at a time, calling names as they burned. It was meant to be ceremony. It was, for most of the square. People cheered each name like a race won. An old net-mender wept openly. Someone started singing a hauling song and got three verses in before the magistrate's frown killed it.

Then my name was called—pronounced wrong again—and I stepped forward, and the clerk held out my contract to me instead of the fire.

"You should do it yourself," he said. "Magistrate's orders. The ones who were bound longest."

The paper was lighter than I remembered. Of course it was; paper is paper. But my hands knew its weight anyway, the way a diver's hands know a stone they've pried from the seabed a hundred times. I could feel through the fold where my mark was pressed—a child's careful signature, because even at eleven I'd understood that neatness was survival. Be unremarkable. Be legible. Be worth keeping.

I held it over the coals and did not let go.

That's the part no one warns you about. I'd imagined this moment for weeks—in the oyster beds, in the smugglers' cellar, in Sefa's borrowed room at the rebuilt half of the Pearlhouse—and in every imagining my hands opened. In every imagining there was a lightness, a great exhale, some bird leaving my chest. That is what freedom is supposed to feel like. That is what the songs say.

What actually happened was vertigo.

The corner caught. Flame walked up the fold toward my name, unhurried, thorough, and I felt the ground tilt. Not grief—my mother had been dead nine years and the contract had never been her, whatever it claimed to own of her daughter. Not relief either. It was the sensation of a hand letting go of a rope I'd been gripping so long my fingers had grown around it. Nine years of knowing exactly what I was: a number, a clerk, an indentured girl until age twenty-one, small and necessary and safe. The contract had been a cage, yes. But a cage has bars, and bars tell you where the edges are.

Now there were no edges.

"For the first time," I said, to no one, "a whole unwritten life."

Dagny appeared at my shoulder, smelling of tar and the jail's particular stink of wet stone. "You look like you're going to be sick," she observed.

"I might be."

"Good," she said. "Means it's real." She watched the last corner curl to ash. "I burned my brother's license papers after the company took the boat. Stood on the headland and fed them to a lantern one piece at a time. Felt nothing for three days. Then I woke up one morning and thought, *I could go anywhere*, and cried into the bread dough until noon." She shrugged. "Freedom arrives late in Vessing. Weather coming in."

Mama Osk found us there, as she finds everyone eventually, the way water finds low ground. She took my chin in her hand—the same grip she used on orphaned divers caught stealing biscuits—and studied my face.

"The vertigo passes," she said. "What stays is harder. You wake up one day and no one owns your hours, and you have to decide what they're for. Most people never have to decide anything that large. You'll muddle it. We all do. Then you'll muddle it better."

"And if I muddle it worse?"

"Then you'll muddle it among people who'll help you sweep up," she said. "That's the whole secret. Nobody gets through an unwritten life alone. That's not the tragedy, child. That's the design."

* * *

The square emptied slowly, the way harbors empty at ebb—first the curious, then the hungry, then the last few stragglers who couldn't bear to leave a thing before it was finished. By late afternoon only the ash remained, a fine grey drift around the brazier's legs, already working itself into the cobbles' cracks. Ash from forty-some contracts. Ash from mine. I crouched and touched it, because I am apparently the sort of person now who needs to touch things to believe them, and it was only ash, gritty and ordinary, already indistinguishable from every other fire's leavings.

Sefa found me like that. She'd gotten good at finding me; I'd stopped asking how.

"You didn't cheer," she said, sitting down beside me on the cold steps of the fish-market awning.

"Did you?"

"No." She pulled her coat tighter—she still dressed like a woman expecting to run, though the hunters were gone, Grell in chains, the whole rotten apparatus suspended pending inquiry. "I keep waiting to feel finished. My father's name is restored to the record. The ledger was read aloud. Every column, every name." She looked out at the harbor, where the light was doing that Vessing thing, silver and merciless. "And I woke this morning and the first thing I thought was: *he's still out there.* Not the factor. Him. Aldric Vane. Alive, somewhere past the Salt Road, writing letters no one delivers fast enough." She smiled ruefully. "You spend seven years being a courier for a ghost, you don't stop being a courier just because the ghost got a pardon."

"When do you leave?"

She glanced at me. "Spring," she said. "When the roads dry. I told you."

"You told Mama Osk. You've been telling everyone but me."

"Because you're the one who'll take it hardest," she said, with the terrible directness she reserved for people she loved. "You finally have a family that stayed, and I'm the one who leaves. You'll stand on the pilings and watch my boat go and feel the rope-hands close again."

I wanted to argue. I was, in fact, assembling quite a good argument, with citations, in the manner of a clerk. But she was right, and we both knew it, and nine years of hiding had taught me exactly one useful skill: recognizing a true ledger entry when I saw one, however much it hurt to reconcile.

"I'll survive it," I said.

"I know," she said. "That's what frightens me about you. You'd survive anything. I'd like to see you do something more ambitious than survive, someday." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Plant something. That's my recommendation. Survivors dig holes. Planters do too, but theirs are hopeful."

We sat until the tide turned. Neither of us said anything else, which is its own kind of conversation, and possibly the truest kind we had.

* * *

Hale came to the counting-house on my last scheduled day—which is to say, my last day ever, since there would be no schedule now unless I wrote it myself. He stood in the doorway of the clerks' hall where I'd spent nine years making myself invisible among forty desks, and he looked around at it with the expression of a man revisiting a cell he'd shared with someone.

"They're offering me the auditor's post," he said. "Port authority this time. Independent charter, magistrate's seal. Honest books, if such a thing exists in Vessing."

"Will you take it?"

"I spent eleven years looking away from columns that didn't reconcile," he said. "I figure I owe the numbers some attention." He set a document on the desk in front of me. "And this is for you."

It was my employment record—or what remained of it. Nine years of copied manifests, reconciliations, flawless penmanship, all of it annotated in the margin in Hale's precise hand: *Recommended for full clerk's certification. Exceptional accuracy. Unremarkable presentation, which is itself remarkable.* He'd signed and dated it.

"It's not a pardon," he said. "It's a reference. First honest document the company ever produced about you, and it took a scandal to get it written. Still." He almost smiled. Hale's smiles are structural events, rare as earthquakes and roughly as warm. "You were the best clerk I never officially noticed."

"That's the nicest insult anyone's given me."

"I mean it as arithmetic," he said. "You added up. When it mattered, you carried the sum." He paused at the door, awkward, a man who had confessed perjury and conspiracy in the market square but couldn't manage a farewell. "Osk says you're staying in Vessing."

"I am."

"May asked why. Everyone asks why. Freed people leave port towns—they go inland, where the air doesn't smell of fish and old debts."

"What did you tell them?"

"That you'd have to answer it yourself, eventually." He nodded once, auditor to clerk, and left.

I sat at my old desk—it was just a desk now, I could sit anywhere, that was the entire unbearable point—and answered the question the way I answer everything: on paper.

*I stay because I know where everything is buried here,* I wrote. *The good and the bad both. My mother is in the water off the headland. Osk's kitchen is four streets from the harbor. Dagny mends nets twenty paces from where her brother's boat used to moor. Sefa will leave in spring, and someone should be here when the letters come. I spent nine years being unremarkable in this town. It seems wrong to make it remarkable by leaving.*

I read it back and found it mostly true, which for a first draft of a life is as much as anyone can hope.

* * *

On the last evening of winter, I went out to the headland.

The Pearlhouse was rebuilt enough to live in—the eastern wall still raw new timber, pale as a scar, but the kitchen stood, and Osk's stove, and the long table where the vote had been taken to shelter a half-drowned stranger. The diver children were asleep in rows. Dagny was teaching the eldest two a card game that was mostly cheating. Ordinary sounds. The sounds I had chosen.

I climbed the path above the house with a shovel over my shoulder and a sack of saplings I'd traded for—apple stock, from an inland grower, absurd this close to salt spray, which is precisely why I wanted it. The ground was cold but workable. I dug the first hole where the soil went dark, sheltered from the wind, and set the roots in, and packed the earth down with the flat of the blade.

Plant something, Sefa had said. Survivors dig holes; planters do too, but theirs are hopeful.

By full dark I had six trees in the ground and dirt under nails I'd kept ink-stained for nine years. From the ridge I could see the whole harbor: the Tide Gate, the counting-house windows, the pilings where I'd dragged a drowning woman out of the sea and ruined my own careful invisibility forever. Lanterns moved along the quay. Somewhere below, the tide was turning, patient as arithmetic, carrying in whatever the sea had decided to give back next.

I put my hand flat on the cold earth over the first sapling's roots. The vertigo was still there, faintly—the endless open feeling of a life with no bars. But standing on the headland, with my family's lights below me and spring coming, I found it had changed shape. It felt less like falling.

It felt like standing somewhere high enough to see everything I might build.

I was still standing there when I heard footsteps on the path behind me—light, deliberate, familiar—and turned to find Dagny climbing toward me with a lantern and an expression I'd learned to distrust.

"You'd better come down," she said. "There's a rider at the gate. Says she's come ahead of the spring post. Says—" she paused, enjoying the suspense the way only Dagny could, "—says she carries letters. From the Salt Road. And she's asking for you by name."
