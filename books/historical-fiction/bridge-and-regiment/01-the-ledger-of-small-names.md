## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The schoolhouse had been a place for sums once. Felix Lockridge could still read the ghost of them on the blackboard behind the stove—*7 × 8 = 56*, chalked by some teacher's patient hand and never quite wiped away—and he found, in the worst hours of the night, that the arithmetic comforted him. Fifty-six. A clean number. Numbers in this room had stopped being clean months ago.

He had converted the building himself, more or less, back when "more or less" still described anything in his life. The desks had gone out to the yard to make way for cots; the maps of rivers and mountain ranges had come down off the walls, though someone had left up the map of the great river system, perhaps out of respect, since it was the river that brought him everything now—supplies when he was lucky, wounded men when he was not, and lately both at once, tangled together in the same gray cargo.

Felix was not a doctor. He wanted that understood, and he understood it better than anyone. He had been a pharmacist's apprentice in a city far from here, then briefly, absurdly, an accountant for a shipping firm whose ledgers balanced so beautifully he had once caught himself humming while closing out a quarter. Then the war came up the valley like weather, and a man with steady hands and a head for figures turned out to be precisely what a field infirmary required. The army did not ask whether his heart was in it. It asked whether his hands were.

They were. That was the whole of his qualification, and most nights it was enough.

"Mr. Lockridge." The voice came from cot eleven, thin and apologetic, the voice of a boy who had been raised never to trouble anyone. "Mr. Lockridge, I don't feel entirely—"

Felix was already moving. He knew the sound. The basin got there just in time, which was the small victory of the evening, and afterward he wiped Tam Weller's mouth with a cloth dampened in rainwater and eased him back against the pillow.

"There," Felix said. "Entirely done, I should think."

"Sorry, sir."

"You've apologized four times today for being ill. I'm considering charging you a fee."

Tam smiled, which cost him something—a fever of 103 makes even a smile expensive—and closed his eyes. Seventeen years old. Conscripted out of a farm two valleys east with his older brother's boots on his feet because the boots were army property now and the dead do not requisition. His brother had fallen at a place whose name Felix had learned and then tried, without success, to unlearn.

Felix made his rounds. Thirty-one cots, thirty-one breathing sounds, each one catalogued in his head the way he had once catalogued freight tonnage: regular, irregular, labored, absent. Absent was the category you listened hardest for, because absence could be corrected if caught early, and could not be corrected at all if caught late.

At cot nineteen, young Pim Sorel was asleep with his fist curled around a button. Not a uniform button—a coat button, horn-colored, civilian, the kind that came off a good winter coat belonging to somebody's father. Pim had arrived clutching it three weeks ago and had not let go since, even under chloroform, even in sleep. Felix had never asked. Some accounts you did not open.

At cot twenty-four, nothing listened back.

Felix stood there for a long moment. Corporal Anders had been sinking for two days, and Felix had done everything the manual suggested and several things it didn't, and around two o'clock the corporal's breathing had gone from *labored* to *quiet*, and quiet, in this room, was its own kind of alarm. Felix took his wrist. The pulse was a rumor. Then it wasn't even that.

He drew the sheet up over the face, and around the room, without waking fully, the other men stirred—the animal knowledge that moves through a ward when death passes through it, like wind over barley.

* * *

The officers' report would say *one (1) death, pyrexia, natural causes incidental to service.* Felix knew this because he had seen the reports. They came down weekly from headquarters in neat columns, and they bore about as much resemblance to the room he stood in as a map bears to mud.

It had started innocently, the way most compromises do—which is to say, it had started with a sense of being helpful. In the first month, an adjutant had come through collecting casualty figures and had asked Felix, briskly, how many deaths. Felix had said four. The adjutant had written two. Felix had opened his mouth, and then had looked at the adjutant's face—young, tired, already fluent in the arithmetic of empire—and had closed it again. Two, said the paper. Four, said the ground outside the window where Felix buried them.

That night, unable to sleep, he had taken one of the infirmary's blank exercise books—schoolhouse stock, *Composition Book No. 3* stamped on the cover in faded blue—and had written, in his careful shipping-clerk hand:

*Corporal J. Anders, 24th Cons. Regt., d. 14 December, fever of the lungs. From Marlowe's Crossing. Mother living, address unknown to me.*

And beneath it:

*Private E. Dunnock, d. 2 November. Drowned carrying ammunition across the ford at Tetchy Bend. Not listed in any regiment. Listed nowhere.*

He had not known why he was doing it. Or rather, he had told himself a reason at the time—that it was for the records, that someday someone official would want accurate figures—but reasons told to oneself at two in the morning are like alibis given to no one. The truth was simpler and stranger: it seemed unbearable that these men existed only in the memory of a pharmacist's apprentice who had never commanded anything larger than a stockroom. If the army would not count them, then someone would count them, and Felix supposed that someone was going to be him.

He called it his ledger, privately, with a small irony he permitted himself. Ledger of Small Names, he thought of it as—not because the names were small but because the war considered them so. A farm boy here. A drowned porter there. An old woman in the eastern quarter whose death from want had been attributed, in the district summary, to *seasonal attrition*, a term Felix had needed to read three times before he understood it meant simply that winter had eaten her and no one had minded.

By lamplight now, he added Anders. He wrote small and close, because the book had to last, and because writing small felt appropriate—as if loud handwriting might somehow be disrespectful to the contents.

"What's that you're always scratching in?"

Felix looked up. Sister Bramble—she was not technically a sister of anything, but she had run the town's laundry for forty years and had appointed herself to nursing with the serene inevitability of a force of nature—stood at his elbow with a cup of chicory coffee. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a dock crane, and afraid of nothing except, in her own words, *being useless*, which she considered a fate worse than shells.

"Notes," Felix said. "Temperatures. Treatments."

"Hm." She looked at the book with the frank appraisal of a woman who had sorted honest linen from smuggled linen for four decades. "Temperatures don't usually get their mothers' names wrote next to them."

"This one does. Anders. His mother will want to know where he lies, someday, when someone tells her."

Sister Bramble was quiet for a moment, which from her was practically a ceremony. Then she handed him the coffee. "Your hand's steadier than your face, Mr. Lockridge. That'll serve you. But mind you keep both out of the wrong drawing rooms." She shuffled off toward cot seven, where Private Halloran was inventing new obscenities for his bad leg, and Felix sat with his chicory cooling and understood that he had just been given advice, or a warning, or possibly a blessing, and that with Sister Bramble all three arrived in the same envelope.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, the river fog pressing at the schoolhouse windows like a customer at a closed shop. Felix washed in a bucket, put on the apron he had owned since his pharmacy days—stained past any hope, soft as an old letter—and began the day's inventory, because inventory was prayer to him, or as near to prayer as he reliably got.

Quinine: enough for nine days, ten if he stretched it, and stretching it killed people, so: nine days. Carbolic: adequate. Bandages: adequate if nobody bled too generously, which nobody ever managed. Morphine: he kept this figure in his head alone, and lied about it to everyone including himself.

The supply forms went to the army quartermaster, and the army quartermaster sent back roughly half of what was asked, in roughly the wrong proportions, with roughly the tone of a man doing you a favor. Felix filled out the forms anyway, in his beautiful clerk's hand, because beautiful handwriting was one of maybe three powers he possessed, and he deployed all three daily.

It was mid-morning when the new convoy came up from the landing—wagons first, then infantry, and Felix went to the window because something about the marching sounded different. Sharper. Newer.

The uniforms were new. That was what it was. Gray wool with hardly a crease worn into it yet, the kind of gray that hadn't seen rain or blood or a laundry line, and the men inside them marched like men who had not yet learned that wars are mostly waiting and lice. Fresh troops. Felix watched them pass the schoolyard fence, young faces above new collars, and felt the particular chill that comes not from fear exactly but from recognition—like watching a cart loaded with glass take a corner too fast.

"Who's that lot, then?" muttered Halloran from his cot, craning.

"Replacements," said Pim Sorel quietly, and the word moved down the row of cots like a draft, and every man in the room understood it in his own private way. Replacements meant the fighting had gone somewhere and taken a toll. Replacements meant the arithmetic upstream had changed.

An officer reined up at the gate—a captain by his cuffs, polished to a shine that suggested either vanity or recent promotion—and called across the yard in a pleasant, carrying voice that the school district's buildings were hereby subject to survey for billeting purposes, that cooperation was appreciated and expected, and that the army thanked the townsfolk in advance for their contribution to the war effort.

Contribution, Felix thought. As if the town were making a donation. As if Mrs. Halloway down on Chandler Street, whose kitchen had fed half the neighborhood's children through two hard winters, were about to be asked for her house the way one asks a neighbor for an egg.

He didn't know yet. He would know soon. But the words *billeting purposes* sat in his stomach like swallowed river stones, and he turned from the window and went back to work, because there were thirty cots and thirty-one was the number of men he intended to keep alive through whatever was coming up the road in those brand-new gray coats.

That evening, after the lamps were trimmed and most of the ward slept, Felix opened the ledger to its current page and sat looking at it longer than usual. It had grown past what he'd imagined. Forty-three entries now, in ink that varied from brown-black to fresh blue depending on what the war had left him to write with. Forty-three people the official record had misplaced, misspelled, halved, rounded down, or erased entirely. He had told himself from the beginning that he was nobody—that history happened in headquarters and capitals, among men whose signatures moved armies, and that his own business was smaller: fevers, dressings, bedpans, the modest machinery of keeping particular bodies breathing. History happened elsewhere. He was only a nurse.

But he had noticed something lately, and the noticing troubled him the way a splinter troubles a finger—not painfully, exactly, but always present. The ledger was becoming an argument. Every name he entered was a small contradiction of the official reports, and contradictions accumulate, and somewhere in the future—some inquiry, some reckoning he could not picture—these pages might be the only place certain truths survived. He was not comfortable with that. Comfortable was not a word that applied. What he was, he suspected, was complicit in something, though he couldn't decide whether the crime was the recording or the long silence that had preceded it.

He wrote the day's entry—no deaths today, a mercy, the first in a week—then closed the book and slid it back under the loose floorboard behind the stove, where the heat kept the damp out and where no one had reason to look.

Outside, down by the landing, the new-gray soldiers were pitching tents in the fog, and their lanterns burned in a tidy row, and their voices carried across the water—young voices, joking about food, about letters from home, about a girl one of them claimed to have left behind in a town whose name the wind took before Felix could catch it.

He banked the stove and lay down on his pallet between cots fourteen and fifteen, where he slept every night so he could hear both ends of the ward, and he thought, as he fell toward sleep, that the town had felt quieter lately than a town should feel, and that quiet in wartime was rarely peace. It was more often held breath.

Three days later, the breath was let out. Three days later, Mrs. Halloway was standing in her own doorway in the snow while strangers carried her furniture into the street, and Felix Lockridge, watching from the infirmary steps with a bandage roll forgotten in his hands, understood that the ledger was about to need a great many more pages—and that the war had finally decided to notice the town, and him, and everything in it.
