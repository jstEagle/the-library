## 2. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which felt somehow rude of them. Sunday would have had a certain theater to it—banners, trumpets, the whole grim pageant—but Tuesday was market day, and so the gray column marched into Thornhollow past stalls of winter cabbage and a man arguing bitterly about the price of eels, and history arrived not with thunder but with the squeak of an ill-oiled supply cart.

Felix Lockridge was elbow-deep in the infirmary's second-best basin, lancing a boil on Private Wrenn's neck, when he heard the boots. Many boots. He counted by habit—he counted everything now—and made it perhaps two hundred men passing along the high street, their new uniforms still holding the sharp crease of the packing crate.

"Gray," said Private Wrenn, craning his head toward the window. "I thought ours were gray."

"Ours are *our* gray," Felix said. "Theirs is a different gray entirely. Very important distinction. Hold still."

"You could cut a man's throat with that distinction."

"I could cut yours if you don't stop wriggling." He pressed the lance home, Wrenn yelped with theatrical offense, and outside the window the empire walked past in its unfamiliar cloth.

Later, Felix stood on the infirmary steps and watched them work, and what struck him most was how *orderly* they were. He had expected the usual chaos of occupation—the looting, the drunkenness, the sullen vandalism that followed armies like gulls follow a fishing boat. Instead these men moved through Thornhollow like clerks through a ledger. A lieutenant with a clipboard stood at the well and read street names aloud while a sergeant chalked numbers on doors: one house in three marked for billeting, the telegraph office circled twice, the schoolhouse—his schoolhouse, converted already to its infirmary purpose—noted and left alone.

"They're polite," said Nurse Okafor beside him, in the tone of a woman who did not trust politeness any further than she could throw it.

"Politeness is cheaper than discipline," Felix said. "It costs nothing to be courteous until the day it costs everything."

"That's very cynical, Mr. Lockridge."

"That's very Tuesday, Miss Okafor."

She snorted and went back inside, and Felix stayed on the steps a moment longer, watching a young soldier—barely older than the farm boys who came through his ward—carefully straighten a bicycle that a comrade had knocked against a fence. The boy set the bicycle precisely upright, adjusted the handlebars, patted the seat once as though apologizing to it, and hurried on after his column.

Felix found himself absurdly comforted by this, and then immediately suspicious of his own comfort. That was the trouble with small kindnesses from large machines: they taught you to like the machine.

* * *

By evening the town had been rearranged. The Hartwell family of Mill Lane slept in their own attic while strangers occupied their bedrooms; the baker's front room held four signallers who rose before dawn and filled the street with the smell of army coffee, which even the baker admitted was better than his own, though he said it in the voice of a man confessing to a crime.

Felix made his rounds with extra tallow candles and found Mrs. Halloway sitting in the infirmary kitchen, her hands folded on the table like birds that had forgotten how to fly.

"They've taken my house," she said, without preamble, because Mrs. Halloway had never in forty years used a preamble in her life. "All of it. A Captain Mowbray. He has my good chairs, Felix. The ones with the needlework seats my mother did before I was born. He put his *boots* on the table. I saw him do it through my own window, standing across the street like a beggar at my own glass."

"Mrs. Halloway—"

"And he apologized. That's the part I can't get past. He took my kitchen and apologized for it, and told me the requisition was temporary and necessary, and then he asked me where we kept the good tea." Her hands tightened on each other. "I told him. God help me, I told him it was in the blue tin above the stove. Forty years of being mistress of that house and I couldn't stop myself being useful to him for five seconds."

Felix sat down across from her. There was no right thing to say here, so he poured her the weak chicory that passed for coffee in the infirmary, and she drank it because it was warm and something to do.

"Where are you staying?" he asked.

"The Tanners have my sister's room for a fortnight. After that—" She waved a hand at the arithmetic of charity, which always ran out faster than the kindness feeding it. "After that I'll manage. I've managed worse. The year my Thomas died I managed, didn't I?"

"You did."

"Well then." She straightened her spine, and for a moment she was the woman who had run the best kitchen in Thornhollow and fed half the town's funerals from it. "Well then, there's no call to make a fuss. You'll tell me if you need help with the washing. Don't spare me because I'm old, Felix Lockridge, I'll know, and I won't forgive it."

He promised. She gathered her shawl and went out into the dark toward the Tanners', pausing at the door to look back once at the infirmary kitchen—the stove, the long table, the window over the sink—with an expression he couldn't name and never forgot.

When she had gone, Felix opened his ledger.

He kept it in the bottom drawer under the surgical ledgers, a plain notebook bought for sixpence in a better year, and in it he wrote things that no official report considered worth writing. He told himself it was only a habit, only a nurse's memory-keeping, the way other men whittled sticks or hummed. He was not making a record. Records were dangerous things, built to be found. This was just... remembering. Remembering hurt nobody.

*Halloway, Marta,* he wrote. *Of Bridge Street. House requisitioned 14th December. Kept her mother's chairs.*

Then he sat looking at the entry, and it looked back at him, and he understood—for the first time, truly understood—that it wasn't nothing. It was a small thing, but it was not nothing. Somewhere in the war office there would be a form recording the requisition of one dwelling, Bridge Street, owner compensated per schedule. The form would not mention the boots on the table or the blue tin or the way her hands folded up like birds. The form would not remember her at all, except as a line in a column.

And he had decided, without ever deciding it, that someone should.

"Habit," he said aloud to the empty kitchen, testing the word. It sounded thinner than it had last week.

* * *

The telegraph office conversion took two days and was, in its way, the most fascinating thing Felix had seen since the war started—which was itself an uncomfortable realization, because fascination was not the emotion one ought to feel about the machinery of occupation. But he passed the office each morning on his rounds, and each morning he slowed, the way one slows past an open window where music is playing.

They ran new wire down the poles in neat hanks. They installed a second key at the far bench and soundproofed the operating room with quilts requisitioned—of course—from Mrs. Halloway's neighbor. And they kept the town's own operator on, which surprised him: Miss Quill, the thin, quick-fingered woman who had sent every birth announcement and death notice in Thornhollow for six years, now sat at her same stool sending the army's traffic instead, her face composed into perfect professional blankness.

He stopped in once, officially, to confirm the infirmary's wire connection for medical supplies. The soldier at the door checked his papers with great courtesy and let him through, and there was Ada Quill at her key, tapping away, not looking up.

"—confirming stores for the infirmary," Felix said. "Bandages, quinine, carbolic. The requisition form is countersigned but the wire confirmation hasn't come."

"It'll come," she said, still sending. Her rhythm was remarkable—even, unbroken, like rain on a roof. Then, without pausing her hand or turning her head: "You write a very distinctive hand, Mr. Lockridge."

"I beg your pardon?"

"On the forms. The supply forms. Your handwriting. Loops on the descenders, like a schoolmaster's. I process forty forms a week and I know every hand in this town." Now she glanced at him, briefly, gray eyes taking his measure. "Yours is the only one that looks like it's apologizing for asking."

Felix laughed before he could stop himself. "Does it?"

"Everything you submit looks like it regrets the inconvenience. Quinine, regretfully requested. Bandages, sorry to trouble you." Her hand kept moving through all of it, steady as a clock. "The army likes your forms. They say you're honest. No padding, no skim. In my experience that's either a saint or a man who hasn't yet learned what forms are for."

"And which do you think I am?"

The key stopped. The silence in the office was sudden and total, the way silence falls in rooms full of machinery, and she turned fully around on her stool and looked at him properly for the first time.

"I think," she said, "that honest men are having a very difficult December. And I think you'd best have your wire confirmation by Thursday." She turned back to her key. "Good afternoon, Mr. Lockridge."

Walking home through the first snow of the season, Felix turned the exchange over like a coin he suspected of being foreign currency. She knew things, that woman. She sat all day in the middle of the army's nervous system, and every impulse the occupation felt anywhere in the province passed through her fingers first. And she had chosen, out of all the dull administrative truths available to her, to tell him that honesty was becoming dangerous.

Either it was a warning, or it was a test, and he could not determine which, and the uncertainty kept him company the whole way down Bridge Street—past the Halloway house, where lamplight burned behind curtains that were no longer hers to draw.

* * *

December tightened. The infirmary filled and emptied and filled again—measles from the garrison now, the army's own children brought low and treated by Felix with exactly the same care he'd give any patient, because a fever does not check your papers before it moves in. The soldiers noticed. Word traveled, as word does, and soon men in gray called him *sir* without irony and left contraband oranges on his windowsill, and Felix found himself disliked less and trusted more by an army he wanted neither to like nor to trust.

That was the trap, he began to see. Not cruelty. Kindness. An occupation that apologized and brought oranges and straightened fallen bicycles was far harder to resist than one that burned and looted, because it recruited your decency against you. Every time he mended a gray-uniformed boy he felt the thin wire of it pull tighter: *you are useful, you are harmless, you are invisible, and invisibility is safety.*

On the nineteenth, Mrs. Halloway came back to the infirmary to help with the washing as promised, and found Captain Mowbray's batman in her former garden cutting down the apple tree.

"For the sight lines," the batman explained, cheerful and apologetic, saw in hand. "Officer's window. Security requirement, ma'am. There's compensation paperwork."

Mrs. Halloway stood in the snow and watched the tree that had fed her family for sixty years come down branch by branch, and she did not weep and did not shout, which was worse. She stood very straight, and when it was done she picked up one length of cut applewood, polished pale and fragrant in the cold, and carried it home under her arm like a relic.

Felix heard about it at dusk from Nurse Okafor, and went to his drawer, and opened the ledger, and found the entry he had written five days before.

*Halloway, Marta. Of Bridge Street. Kept her mother's chairs.*

His pen hovered. There was nothing accurate to add that wouldn't turn the ledger into evidence—*apple tree felled for the sight line of Captain Mowbray's window* was not a nurse's memory-keeping; it was testimony, and testimony was a thing men were shot for. He could close the drawer. He could leave the record as it was, small and safe and insufficient. Nobody would ever know. Invisibility was safety. History happened elsewhere, in capitals, in ministries, in reports written by men whose boots had never touched a kitchen table.

But the tree had been sixty years in the ground, and the report would say *one apple tree removed, compensation per schedule*, and somewhere between those two sentences—the tree and the sentence—a woman had gone home carrying a piece of wood in the snow, and no form in the empire was going to hold that.

His hand was shaking slightly when he wrote:

*19th December. The apple tree taken also. She carried a branch home.*

He blotted it, closed the book, pushed the drawer shut, and stood a moment in the darkening infirmary listening to the wind off the river and the distant clatter of the telegraph office, where Miss Quill's key was talking to headquarters in its even, unbroken rain.

Invisible men survive, he told himself. Invisible men keep their heads down and their hands clean and their names out of reports.

Then why, he asked the darkness, am I keeping names in?

* * *

Three days before Christmas, the dispatches began arriving at the telegraph office that nobody in Thornhollow was meant to see—sealed pouches, armed couriers, a new gravity settling over the gray uniforms like weather coming in. Felix noticed it the way a doctor notices a change in a pulse: nothing you could write on a chart, everything you could feel in your fingertips.

And on Christmas Eve, as he crossed the square with a lantern, he saw Ada Quill step out of the office into the falling snow, alone, and stand there a long moment with her face turned up to the dark, her hands—those quick, tireless, even hands—pressed flat against her sides as if to stop them from saying anything more.

Whatever was moving down that wire, it was bigger than bandages and quinine.

Felix turned up his collar and went home, and did not sleep well, and wrote nothing in any ledger at all.
