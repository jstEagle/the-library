## 3. The Operator

Ada Quill had learned to read lies the way other people read weather: by the pressure behind them.

The dispatches came through the army wire at all hours now—*engagement concluded, enemy routed, casualties light*—and she transcribed them in her neat operator's hand without a flicker, because a flicker was the sort of thing Sergeant Dunn's clerks had been posted to watch for. But she could feel the falsehood the way you feel a storm coming through a bad tooth. Casualty figures halved before they ever reached the wire. Engagements invented whole out of a colonel's need for a victory to staple to his report. One dispatch last Tuesday described a brilliant skirmish at a ford she happened to know was dry in August and frozen solid now, which meant that either the army had fought a battle on ice skates or somebody's ink had gotten ahead of somebody's cavalry.

She tapped it all through anyway, letter by letter, her wrist moving with the easy rhythm of nine years at the key. The lie went down the line clean as scripture. That was the trick of it: a lie transmitted properly sounds exactly like the truth. The wire didn't care. The wire never cared about anything.

"Quill." The duty lieutenant leaned over her partition, smelling of wet wool and self-importance. "This one goes priority. Colonel's own report."

"Yes, sir."

He lingered, watching her copy it out, and she understood the lingering. They'd posted him there to watch her, and he watched her the way a man watches a clock he suspects of running slow. She gave him nothing but the soft clatter of the key and a face like a closed ledger.

When he'd gone she sat back and flexed her fingers. Outside the frosted window, the river town went about its business under new gray uniforms—the requisitioned warehouses, the soldiers billeted three to a parlor, Mrs. Halloway's kitchen now an officers' mess where Mrs. Halloway herself was permitted to cook. Ada had watched the eviction from this same window two weeks ago, the old woman carrying out a single chair while a corporal inventoried her teacups. Ada had transcribed the requisition order herself that morning. Her own wrist had signed that woman out of her own life.

She kept a list of those too. Not on paper—paper got carted away now; the army was consolidating records, whatever that euphemism meant—but in her head, filed under the only cipher no signals officer could intercept. *Mrs. Halloway, evicted. Mr. Prewitt, ferryman, questioned twice. Boy of maybe twelve, arrested at the mill, no charge given.* It wasn't much of a ledger. It had no columns. But it was hers, and it was honest, and honesty these days had to be kept somewhere the sun couldn't get at it.

* * *

The supply forms were what first drew her attention to Felix Lockridge.

They came across her desk every third day—requisitions from the infirmary in the converted schoolhouse, bandages, quinine, lamp oil—and they were, absurdly, beautifully written. Most requisitions came in the scrawl of men who'd learned to write only what the army required of them: numbers, initials, a signature like a bird's footprint. Lockridge's forms looked like invitations. His hand was small and upright and patient, each figure placed as though it had been considered individually and found worthy. Even his crossings-out were tidy, a single line through the error and the correction set beside it, initialed.

Ada had spent nine years reading handwriting, because handwriting was how people lied when they thought the wire couldn't hear them. She knew what a nervous hand looked like. She knew what a guilty hand looked like. Lockridge's hand was neither. It was the hand of a man who believed that if you wrote things down carefully enough, carefully might count for something.

It was such a naive idea that it stopped her heart for half a second.

She began, without quite deciding to, keeping his forms to the bottom of the stack so she could look at them last, the way you save the best sweet from the box. On the fourth form there was an anomaly. A request for forty dressings, where the infirmary held—by the army's own returns, which she also processed—perhaps thirty wounded. Forty dressings for thirty men. And in the margin, in that careful hand, a small notation: *incl. porters.* As though porters needed dressings too. As though there were porters who ought to be counted among the wounded and weren't, anywhere official.

Ada sat with that for a long while, the office clattering around her, the stove ticking, Lieutenant What-Was-His-Name two partitions over pretending to inventory rifles.

Porters. She remembered the dispatch from October, the one the wire had carried at midnight: *civilian laborer drowned during crossing, regrettable, no further action.* No name given. The army did not consider porters worth naming, any more than it considered telegraph operators worth consulting. But Felix Lockridge had written *incl. porters* in his tidy margin, which meant that somewhere in that schoolhouse infirmary, a man who told everyone he was only a nurse was keeping count of the uncounted.

She knew the type. She was the type. Two people fluent in official falsehood can always smell each other across a room, or across a requisition form. It is not friendship. It is more like recognition—the wary, narrowed-eye recognition of two card sharps at the same table, each certain the other is holding something worth stealing.

She decided to meet him. Carefully. The way you approach any wild thing whose trust matters more than your curiosity.

* * *

The pretext was easy: a discrepancy in the quinine returns. Such discrepancies were common and dull, which made them perfect. She waited until the duty shift changed, wrapped her coat around her, and walked the six blocks to the schoolhouse through snow that squeaked like new leather.

The schoolhouse still smelled of chalk under the carbolic. Desks had been pushed against the walls and beds set between them, and the beds were full—boys, most of them, conscripts with winter in their lungs, coughing in a ragged chorus that no amount of official optimism could halve. Ada signed the visitor book with a fictional name out of habit and asked for Mr. Lockridge.

He came out of the ward wiping his hands, and up close he was younger than his forms suggested and more tired—a long-boned man with ink stains at his cuffs that matched hers, and the particular stillness of someone who has trained himself not to react.

"Miss Quill? Telegraph office?"

"The same." She produced the requisition forms. "Your quinine doesn't reconcile with our returns. I'm told to find out why. Nobody tells me why anything else happens, so I thought I'd take this one personally."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would be kept if anyone were storing such things. "That's very thorough of you."

"I'm a very thorough liar," Ada said pleasantly. "It's my trade. I transmit reports of battles that didn't happen in places that are frozen solid. So when something crosses my desk that reconciles honestly, I notice. It's rare. Like a comet."

His face didn't change, exactly. But his hands, wrapping around each other, changed—knuckles whitening, then deliberately loosening. He glanced toward the ward door.

"Perhaps we shouldn't discuss the army's honesty in the corridor," he said quietly.

"Perhaps we shouldn't discuss it anywhere," Ada said. "That's rather the point. I only came to say—" and here she paused, because she had rehearsed a dozen versions of this sentence on the walk over and none of them survived contact with his tired eyes. In the end she said simply: "*Incl. porters.* I noticed. That's all. I noticed."

For a moment neither of them breathed. Somewhere in the ward a boy called out for his mother in a dialect the army hadn't bothered to learn, and Lockridge's shoulder turned toward the sound and back, an entire moral crisis conducted in the space of a shrug.

"You transcribe everything," he said at last. It wasn't an accusation. It was arithmetic.

"Everything they give me."

"And you remember it."

"Some of it," Ada said. "The parts with weight. Names mostly, when there are names, which there usually aren't." She looked at him steadily. "You keep accounts, Mr. Lockridge. I can tell. Your hand is too careful. Careless people don't initial their corrections."

He laughed then—one breath of it, startled out of him, quickly smothered. "I've been told my hand is old-fashioned."

"Your hand is a confession waiting to be read. Which is either very brave or very foolish, and I haven't decided which." She tucked the forms under her arm. "I'll mark the quinine discrepancy resolved. Clerical error. We're drowning in those lately; one more won't be noticed."

"Miss Quill." He said it as she turned away, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard from anyone in months—not since before the gray uniforms came. It took her a moment to identify it. Trust. Tentative, unproven, probably stupid, but trust.

"Don't thank me," she said over her shoulder. "Thanking people is how they get arrested."

* * *

They developed a system that was not a system, because systems left patterns and patterns got people shot. Every few days the quinine wouldn't reconcile, or the lamp oil, or the stretcher count, and one of them would walk through the snow to the other's post, and they would talk about clerical errors for four minutes in voices pitched for privacy, and in those four minutes exchange more truth than the entire army wire carried in a week.

She learned that he had been a medical student once, two years short of finishing, and spoke of it without self-pity, the way you speak of a leg lost to a train. She learned that he wrote letters home for the boys who couldn't, and read them aloud to the feverish, and had somehow memorized the family details of forty strangers because he refused to let them be *conscript, unknown* on any document he controlled. She even learned his humor, which was dry and arrived sideways, like weather from an unexpected quarter.

"They've billeted a captain in the vestry," he told her one afternoon, sorting dressings while she ostensibly audited his stores. "He prays loudly. Out loud, I mean. At volume. I don't think God has a choice about hearing him."

"And you?"

"I pray quietly. Mostly that the captain will be transferred."

"To where?"

"Somewhere with fewer witnesses. It's the witnesses that make a war expensive." He folded a dressing into a precise square. "Paperwork, Miss Quill, is the only weapon both sides respect."

"Is that what yours is? Paperwork?"

He was quiet a moment. Through the wall came the coughing chorus, and beneath it, faintly, the bell of the requisitioned church marking the hour as though time itself had been conscripted.

"It's a ledger," he said finally. "Private. Names. The ones the reports leave out." He said it the way a man says a thing he has decided to stop being afraid of saying. "A farm boy from the valley. A porter who drowned. Mrs. Halloway's kitchen. Small names. I tell myself it's nothing—that history happens elsewhere, in capitals, to important people—but I write them down anyway, because nobody else does, and if nobody writes them down..."

"Then they didn't happen," Ada finished. "Yes. I know how the arithmetic works. I do the same sums in my head every night." She hesitated, and then, because his honesty deserved better than her caution: "My brother was a soldier. There's a version of his story on the wire. It's short. Desertion, trial, execution. Nine minutes, the trial. I timed it by the church bell." She smoothed her glove flat on the table. "The true version is longer. Nobody transmits the long version anymore."

Felix looked at her, and she watched him decide not to offer comfort, because they were past comfort and into the territory where comfort would have been an insult—two professionals acknowledging each other's fluency in the same terrible language.

"What's your name?" he asked. "Your brother's."

"Jonas. Jonas Quill."

He nodded slowly, and she understood that Jonas Quill had just been added to a ledger, and that this small act—this scrupulous, stubborn, almost certainly futile act of writing a name where the army wanted no name written—was the most decent thing that had happened to her family in four months.

"Careful, Mr. Lockridge," she said, and her voice was lighter than she felt, because lightness was how you carried heavy things. "Names accumulate. Ledgers grow. And this town is filling up with gentlemen whose whole profession is making sure the books balance."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I watch the traffic, Felix—I may call you Felix, we're co-conspirators now, it's practically official—and the traffic is getting heavier. Requisitions for record crates. Transport orders. They're consolidating the town's papers, whatever that means, and I'll tell you what it means: it means someone upstairs wants the past packed in boxes and hauled off where it can't testify." She stood, buttoning her coat. "Whatever your ledger is, it's about to become the only honest account in this town. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Frightened?" he offered.

"Important," Ada said. "Which is worse."

At the door she paused. Down the corridor, orderlies were moving fast—too fast for routine—with stretcher canvas flapping, and the boyish voice of a runner calling for Mr. Lockridge, urgent now, the words tumbling: survivors in, wounded from the bridge engagement, Marrow Bridge, dozens of them, and an officer already on his way to supervise the admissions.

Felix was already reaching for his apron. Their eyes met over the noise.

"Marrow Bridge," Ada repeated softly. She had transcribed three dispatches about Marrow Bridge that month, and every one of them had lied to her teeth. "How many survivors?"

"That's just it, miss," the runner said, gray-faced. "Eleven walking wounded and one who won't stop talking. Won't stop talking, sir says, and sir wants him made comfortable."

*Made comfortable.* In nine years of military language, Ada had never heard a phrase that meant *silence* so politely.

Felix went in to his patients. Ada stood a moment in the doorway of the schoolhouse, snow gathering on her shoulders, listening to the distant click of her own telegraph office like a heart beating in another room—and understood, with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading what lay underneath the message, that whatever came through that door tonight would need writing down, and that the writing down was going to cost them both far more than quinine discrepancies ever had.

She pulled her collar up and walked back into the snow to work.
