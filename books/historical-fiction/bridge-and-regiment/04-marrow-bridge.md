## 4. Marrow Bridge

The fever had broken in Private Wilcox sometime during the night, which meant that by morning he was well enough to complain about the porridge, and Felix counted this among the week's genuine victories. Complaint about porridge was, in his experience, the surest sign of a man who intended to live.

"Salt," Wilcox said, pushing the bowl an inch away as though it had insulted his mother. "That's all I'm asking. One pinch of salt."

"You'll have salt when the supply wagon remembers we exist," Felix said. "Until then, you'll have porridge that tastes of honest rainwater, and you'll be grateful for it."

"I'm grateful for nothing that tastes like this."

"There's gratitude," Felix said, taking the bowl, "and there's appetite. You've confused them. It happens to convalescents. Eat it anyway."

It was the kind of exchange he had come to treasure more than he could reasonably explain — small, useless, entirely human. The schoolhouse infirmary held thirty-one cots now where once it had held desks, and the slate boards still bore the ghost of a geography lesson no one had bothered to wash away. Rivers and mountain ranges in white chalk, faint as old scars, above men who would never see most of them. Felix liked to think the lesson was still being absorbed somehow: *here is the world, gentlemen; here are its borders; here is how little they matter from a cot.*

He told himself, most mornings, that this was his whole occupation. Bodies. Fevers. Bowels and bandages. That history was being made somewhere else — at headquarters, across the river, in rooms where men used words like *position* and *initiative* — and that his job was simply to stand at the far end of it and catch what fell.

He kept the ledger because it helped him believe that.

It was a plain thing, bound in gray cloth, the sort of book a grocer might use for flour accounts. He kept it beneath the false bottom of the instrument cabinet, along with his private store of laudanum and one decent bottle of brandy, and every few evenings he entered into it the names that did not appear anywhere else. Not out of rebellion — he was quite clear with himself on this point, nightly and unconvincingly — but out of tidiness. A nurse should know whom he had failed. That was all. A private arithmetic of grief, kept for no one.

The official casualty lists told a different story, or rather told no story at all. They recorded men lost *in action*, which had a clean sound to it, like coins counted into a palm. They did not record Corporal Nye's porter, whose name Felix had never learned, drowned three weeks ago carrying ammunition across the ford because the army had requisitioned every boat. They did not record the farm boy — Tomas Ellery, seventeen, conscripted off his own father's land — who had died of a wound Felix might have treated properly if the surgical thread hadn't run out. In the officers' reports, these men were weather. In Felix's ledger, they were names, written in his careful hand with whatever details he could gather, because a name alone seemed to him insufficient shelter.

He was adding the evening's entry — Mrs. Adele Halloway, of Mill Lane, evicted from her own kitchen on the twelfth; her jams confiscated as *provisions* — when the wagons came.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them: not the rhythm of a supply column but the arrhythmic clatter of wheels carrying weight badly, the sound of a cart whose springs had given up its secrets. Then voices in the yard, and Sergeant Mole's head around the infirmary door, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he'd rather have left on the step.

"Doctor says you're to have these," Mole said. "Says you're not to ask where they've been."

"I never ask where anything's been. It's why we get on."

But when Felix went out into the blue end of the afternoon, he understood immediately that something had changed shape. There were eleven men laid in the two wagons, and they were not the usual cargo of a skirmish. Skirmish wounded came in angry or stoic or unconscious, and their wounds had the grammar of battle about them — front, shoulder, thigh, the honest geometry of men facing each other. These men lay curled. Several had wounds in their backs. Two had their hands wrapped, and when Felix peeled back the first dressing to look, he found the palms shot through, neat holes straight through both hands, as though someone had held them flat and fired through the stack.

He did not let his face do anything. This was the first discipline of his trade, learned early and practiced often: the face stays level while everything behind it falls down stairs.

"Bring them in," he said. "Gently, if gently's available. If not, gently anyway."

They worked until lamplight. Felix moved from cot to cot doing what could be done, which was less than he wanted and more than he'd expected, and gradually the picture assembled itself the way it always did — not from any single account but from fragments, overheard between morphine and sleep, repeated with variations that only confirmed each other.

There had been an action at Marrow Bridge. That much the official version would say, eventually, in some form. A column of irregulars, perhaps; a crossing contested. But the men in the cots had not been the ones fighting. Most of them were villagers taken for questioning, and a handful were soldiers of the garrison company who had refused an order, or hesitated at one, or simply been present when the wrong moment arrived. The surrendered villagers had been lined up on the bridge itself — the bridge, everyone said the bridge, as though the location mattered, as though naming the place were a kind of penance — and shot.

"They had their hands up," said Private Ansel Crane, who was twenty and had a bullet graze along his ribs and could not stop talking now that he had started, the words coming out of him like water finding a crack. "I want to be clear about that, sir. I want somebody to write it down. Their hands were up. Old Mr. Fetlock — he kept bees, he supplied the garrison honey, the officers *knew* him — he had his hands up and he was saying something, I couldn't hear what, maybe praying, maybe just talking, old fellows talk — and the lieutenant gave the word and they just —"

Crane stopped. His hands were shaking, and Felix took them, unwrapped them, dressed them again, giving the boy's mouth time to decide whether to continue.

"We tried to stop it," Crane said finally, quieter. "Some of us. That's why—" He gestured vaguely at his own rib, at the ward full of curled bodies. "That's why we're here instead of there. They turned it on us too. But we didn't fire first, sir. I need you to know we didn't fire first. Will somebody write that down?"

"Somebody will write it down," Felix said, which was a kindness, and possibly a lie, and he hated himself for not knowing which.

* * *

By midnight the ward had settled into the heavy breathing and occasional cry of real exhaustion, and Felix sat at the schoolmaster's desk with his ledger open and a candle burning low, and his hands would not cooperate.

This was new. He had written dead men's names before — the drowned porter, Tomas Ellery, a dozen others — and his hand had stayed steady, because steadiness was part of the ritual, part of the small dignity he could offer. Tonight the pen hovered and trembled like something nervous perched on his fingers, and he had to press his wrist flat against the desk to make it behave.

Eleven names. He gathered them from Crane and from a sergeant named Boil who drifted in and out of consciousness with perfect courtesy, apologizing each time, and from a boy called Pimm who was fourteen and had hidden under the bridge and seen everything and would carry it his whole life, though he didn't know that yet.

*Josiah Fetlock,* Felix wrote. *Beekeeper. Hands raised.*

*Margaret Fetlock.* His wife. She had gone onto the bridge after him, apparently, shouting, and they had shot her too, and Felix had to stop writing for a moment and look at the chalk rivers on the wall above the slate trays, the patient white tributaries of a world that presumably still contained places where things like this didn't happen, though he was losing confidence in the map.

*Marta Voss, miller's daughter.* (No relation, he would learn later, to any journalist; the coincidence would strike him years afterward.) *Daniel Oakes, wheelwright. Peter Oakes, his son, fifteen.* Father and son together on the line, the witnesses agreed; whether the boy had held his father's hand at the end no one could tell Felix, and he found he needed desperately to know, and then found he needed even more desperately not to.

Three garrison soldiers as well: Lance-Corporal Hearn, Private Duval, Private Ansel Crane himself, who Felix believed would live and wrote down anyway, because the ledger was not only for the dead anymore. It was for the men the reports would call *casualties of friendly confusion*, or wouldn't mention, or would list among *irregulars engaged*. Eleven names. His handwriting, when he finished, looked like it had been done by a stranger with palsy.

He blotted the page, closed the book, and sat listening to the ward breathe.

Then the door opened without anyone knocking, which in an infirmary is a declaration of rank.

* * *

Lieutenant Harrow was young, correct, and carried a notebook of his own, which Felix noted with the dull professional interest of a man recognizing a rival instrument. Harrow had come twice before to take testimony — always at night, always with the same air of a bank examiner visiting a poor relation.

"The Marrow Bridge cases," Harrow said. "Which of them can talk?"

"One or two, in spells. Why?"

"Inquiry procedure. Statements to be taken down verbatim." He said it fluently, the way one recites something memorized, and Felix watched him and thought: *he has already taken them down. Someone has already decided what they say.*

"They're heavily medicated."

"Then I'll take what I can." Harrow moved down the ward with his lamp, pausing at Crane's cot. The boy was asleep, thank God, his bandaged hands lying outside the blanket like something set out to dry. Harrow looked at him for a long moment, wrote a single line, and moved on. At Pimm's cot he stopped longer, and Felix saw the boy's eyes open — children wake at the wrong moments, it is their gift — and saw Pimm look up at the lieutenant's uniform and close his eyes again, quickly, the way you shut a door on something.

In the corridor, Harrow paused by the desk where the day's treatment records lay. He leafed through them without asking, which was also a declaration of rank.

"Your notes are thorough," he said.

"It's a habit."

"Mm." He closed the book. "Regarding tonight's admissions. The men will have spoken. Feverish men say a great many things." He smiled, and it was not an unpleasant smile, which made it worse. "Whatever they said, it isn't evidence. And whatever you may have written down—" a glance at the desk, light and precise as a blade laid flat— "*isn't recorded*. Do you understand me? Nothing from this ward regarding Marrow Bridge exists. Officially, it hasn't happened. That is not a request; it is a description of reality as it will appear in the record."

"And the men themselves?"

"Will be transferred when fit. Somewhere restful." Harrow settled his cap. "You've done good work here, Lockridge. The colonel notices thoroughness. Try to be thorough about the right things."

His boots went down the corridor, unhurried, a man with paperwork ahead of him and nothing behind.

Felix stood in the doorway watching the ward — the breathing, the candle shadows, Pimm lying rigidly awake and pretending otherwise — and understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a level, what he was actually holding. Not a nurse's tidy account. Not private grief-keeping. Evidence. The only eleven names outside Harrow's sanitized notebook and Ashworth's clean war that would ever attach themselves to that bridge.

Horror, he had always assumed, would arrive with trumpets, with spectacle, with some terrible noon. Instead it had arrived as paperwork. As a young lieutenant saying *isn't recorded* in the tone of a man confirming a dinner engagement.

Felix went back to the desk, opened the ledger, and beneath the eleven names he added a single line in his smallest, steadiest hand:

*Taken down contrary to orders, this 14th of December. Witnessed by no one. Therefore witnessed by me.*

He blotted it, put the book away beneath the false bottom of the instrument cabinet, and was reaching for the brandy he no longer tasted when Sergeant Mole appeared again, stamping snow from his boots, wearing an expression Felix had not seen on him before — not the usual long-suffering good humor but something guarded, almost apologetic.

"Beg pardon, sir. You're to know the quartermaster's been round to the school storeroom. And the parish office. And the merchant premises on Front Street." Mole shifted, uncomfortable. "Requisitioning all ledgers and registers, sir. Everything with writing in it. Says they're wanted for transport to headquarters tomorrow morning."

Felix heard himself say, lightly, "Whatever for? Does headquarters suspect the church register of desertion?"

"That's not for me to say, sir." Mole lingered, and for a moment his eyes went to the instrument cabinet, then away, very fast, a glance so quick it might have been nothing at all. "Only — my missus keeps accounts too, sir. Sugar, thread, what's owed and what's forgiven. She says a household that loses its books loses its memory." He straightened. "Thought you'd want to know early. Good night, sir."

His boots faded down the corridor. Felix sat quite still in the candlelight, listening to the ward breathe, and began — methodically, the nurse in him taking inventory before the fugitive had even woken — to calculate how much space there was inside a crate of surgical dressings.
