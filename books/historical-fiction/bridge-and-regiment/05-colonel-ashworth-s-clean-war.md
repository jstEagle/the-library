## 5. Colonel Ashworth's Clean War

Colonel Reginald Ashworth rose at five each morning, winter included, because he believed a man who could not govern his own hours had no business governing other men. He lit the stove in his quarters himself—the requisitioned parlor of a mill owner whose name he had never bothered to learn—and made tea that was too weak because the good leaves went to the hospital, and he drank it standing at the window while the river town assembled itself below him in the gray light.

It was not a beautiful town. It had never pretended to be one. It was a hinge between two banks, a place of rope and timber and barges, and its people spoke a dialect the headquarters clerks found amusing. Ashworth did not find it amusing. He found it *instructive*. Places like this were what the empire was actually made of—not the marble fronts in the capital, but hinges. Crossings. The unglamorous machinery of connection. Whoever held the crossings held the war, and whoever held the war's story held everything after it.

He was fifty-four years old, straight-backed, silver at the temples in a way that subalterns privately described as *conveniently handsome*, and he had spent thirty-one years learning that battles were won by soldiers but wars were won by paperwork. This conviction had earned him a reputation for coldness that he considered unfair. He was not cold. He was *tidy*, and there is nothing the untidy resent so much as tidiness.

This morning he sat down at the mill owner's desk—a heavy thing of dark oak, better furniture than he'd have chosen for himself—and began the report of Marrow Bridge.

* * *

The pen moved steadily. Ashworth wrote in a small, exact hand, the hand of a man who had filled out ten thousand returns and never once needed the wastebasket.

*On the morning of the 19th, elements of the 2nd Battalion engaged armed irregulars at the Marrow Bridge crossing. The position was carried after ninety minutes' action. Enemy losses are estimated at forty. Our own: three wounded, none fatally.*

There. That much was true, if you held the words up to the light at the correct angle. There *had* been irregulars at the bridge—eleven men with old hunting rifles, which was fewer than forty, but estimates were estimates, and the word gave the sentence room to breathe. There *had* been an action. Three of his men had been wounded, one of them by his own sergeant's nervous horse, though that detail would not assist the reader.

The difficulty was the villagers.

Ashworth set down his pen and looked at the river. He had read the survivors' statements—or rather, he had been present while a captain summarized them to him, which was not quite the same thing, and he had let it not be the same thing, and he knew he had let it, and this knowledge sat in him like a swallowed stone.

Surrendered men shot at the waterline. A porter drowned in the shallows with his hands above his head. Boys, according to the summary. Farm boys and a ferryman and a schoolteacher who had run out to stand between the lines and been gathered up by the same volley as the rest. Eleven civilians. The number kept arriving in his head wearing different clothes, as numbers do when one is trying not to admit them: eleven names on a list, eleven widows, eleven empty chairs at eleven tables where someone would go on setting places out of habit for years.

And here was the thing—the thing that his critics, had they known it, would never have understood:

He did not want the number to be false. He wanted it to be *irrelevant*.

That was the distinction he would have explained, patiently, over brandy, to anyone willing to listen. A lie corrodes. A lie requires maintenance; a lie breeds more lies like flies on meat, until the whole archive stinks of them and some clerk in some future decade pulls the wrong thread and the empire unravels in public. But a truth, properly framed? A truth survives every audit. *Engagement with armed irregulars; regrettable incidental casualties among persons whose status could not be verified.* That sentence would hold. That sentence had steel bones. Because God help him, it was even substantially true—they had been at the bridge, some of them had possibly carried rifles at some point, no one had stopped to verify anything, and verification was, after all, the whole art of war.

He took up the pen again.

*A subsequent search of the crossing confirmed the irregular force had been drawing support from the adjacent settlement. In the confusion of the action, and given the impossibility of distinguishing combatant from non-combatant under fire, casualties among the civilian population are assessed at zero.*

Zero. He looked at the word a long moment. It was a clean little number, round as a coin, and it would circulate through the empire buying exactly what coins buy. Somewhere in the capital a minister would read it over breakfast and feel the pleasant warmth of a well-run war. Somewhere in thirty years a historian would copy it into a footnote. Zero was the most powerful number in the imperial arithmetic. It required no graves, no pensions, no inquiries, no apologies. It weighed nothing and carried everything.

He was protecting them, you know. That was the part no one ever credited. He was protecting the empire from itself—from its own conscience, which was a tender organ, badly housed, prone to inflammation. An empire that paused to mourn every accident of its expansion would simply stop expanding, and then some coarser power would come and do the expanding *without* the pauses, and the towns along this river would learn what an untidy conqueror looked like. Ashworth had seen what untidy conquerors looked like. He had photographs in a drawer he did not open. His war would be a clean war or it would be nothing, and cleanliness, he had found, was mostly a matter of filing.

He signed the report. Then, because a signature is only ink until it is enforced, he rang for his adjutant.

* * *

Captain Mowbray arrived at six sharp, buttoning his tunic, young enough that his mustache still looked aspirational. Ashworth liked Mowbray. The boy was honest, which was a liability, and intelligent enough to conceal it, which was a career.

"Sir."

"Mowbray. Sit." Ashworth pushed the teapot toward him out of habit, remembered it was empty, and had the grace to look faintly apologetic. "I want the town records consolidated. Everything."

"Everything, sir?"

"Parish registers. Merchant ledgers. The school's roll books. Ferry manifests, tax rolls, coroner's returns if there are any—there will be, this town drowns a man every other summer and likes a paper trail about it. Cart it all to the warehouse by Thursday. I want an inventory, and I want the inventory kept *here*, not at the warehouse." He tapped the signed report. "Headquarters has requested documentation relevant to the engagement at Marrow Bridge. I intend to send them a curated selection."

Mowbray's face did the small complicated thing it did when he was following instructions and disapproving of them in the same breath. Ashworth found it almost endearing. "Curated, sir?"

"You're a literate man, Captain, so I'll say this once and we'll both pretend it was unnecessary." Ashworth stood and went to the window. Below, the town was waking: smoke from chimneys, the bell of the ferry landing, a file of his gray-coated soldiers crossing the square with the self-importance of men carrying something official. "A war is not decided by what happened. It is decided by what can be *proven*, twenty years from now, by people who hate us. Every document in this town is either evidence or ammunition, and I will not leave ammunition lying about for the next occupant of my desk. So. Consolidate. Inventory. And anything that contradicts the enclosed report goes into the second crate."

"And the second crate, sir?"

"Is incinerated." He said it the way another man might have said *is recycled*, mildly, almost housekeeping-adjacent. "The baptismal registers stay—they're sentimental value only and the Church makes a fuss. School records stay; children are neutral. Merchant ledgers stay unless they mention army requisitions, which they all do, so—" a thin smile, "—we'll call those a judgment for the inventory clerk's discretion, and we'll choose a discreet clerk."

"Yes, sir." Mowbray hesitated at the door, which he did when his honesty had backed him into a corner. "The hospital, sir. The infirmary keeps its own notes. Treatment records, that sort of thing. There's a nurse—Lockridge—writes a very fair hand. Half my supply forms come through him."

"Then his forms are in order and he may keep his treatment notes," Ashworth said. "I've no quarrel with a man who writes neatly. Fever charts and dosages are of interest to no one but fevers." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes—the flicker of a mind that checked its own ledgers nightly. "Though it's worth remembering, Mowbray, that the dangerous documents are never the ones that look dangerous. They're the ones that look *humble*. A general's memoirs lie loudly enough to be caught. It's the quiet lists that hang men."

"I'll bear it in mind, sir."

"Do. And Mowbray—" Ashworth softened it with the ghost of warmth he reserved for subordinates he intended to keep, "—you think I'm burning history. I know. You have an honest face and it announces your thoughts like a telegraph. So let me tell you what I'm actually doing, so you can sleep."

Mowbray turned around fully.

"In twenty years, there will be an inquiry. There is always an inquiry; it is the empire's way of digesting. And when it comes, there will be a room of tired men with folders, and the question before that room will be whether Colonel Ashworth conducted a lawful action at Marrow Bridge. Now—" he held up the report, "—if that room is handed eleven contradictory affidavits, a parish register with fresh gaps, and a coroner's list of bodies pulled from the shallows, they will build a case, because that is what tired men with folders are *for*. But if that room is handed one clear, consistent, professionally drafted report—" he set it down gently, "—they will conclude the matter and go to lunch. I am not destroying the truth, Captain. I am deciding which truth is robust enough to survive. The alternative truth is fragile. It would break us all."

He believed it. That was the terrible economy of the man—he wasted nothing, least of all conviction. He had constructed a moral architecture in which every act of erasure was a load-bearing act of protection, and he lived inside it comfortably, the way a man lives in a well-built house without ever going down to look at the foundations.

Mowbray saluted and left, and Ashworth returned to his window, and watched the soldiers begin their rounds of the town below.

* * *

At nine he inspected the telegraph office, now flying the army pennant, and found it running to his satisfaction. He always visited the wire first among his inspections; the telegraph was the empire's nervous system, and he trusted it more than he trusted most of its limbs. The operator on duty—a local woman, Quill, competent, incurious-looking, the ideal temperament—transcribed dispatches with quick, correct fingers while he read over the traffic log.

Casualty figures halved per his standing instruction. An engagement invented out of two pot-shots at a fence post, rendered in proper operational language. All flowing southward in his name, tidy as stitching.

"You find the work satisfactory, Miss Quill?" he asked, in the courteous tone he used with civilians, which cost him nothing and purchased a great deal.

"Satisfactory, sir," she said, eyes on the key. "Very regular hours."

He appreciated that answer. Regular hours. A woman who understood that civilization *was* regular hours, that the alternative was the bridge at Marrow and men making decisions at speed in the smoke. He complimented her transmission quality, told her the office would receive an extra ration of coal, and left feeling—if the word may be used of a man so disciplined—*cheerful*.

The morning continued in this vein. At the warehouse he watched the first crates of town records arrive and personally checked the inventory against Mowbray's manifest, finding two discrepancies and enjoying finding them, because a colonel who catches his own errors commits fewer. He lunched on soup. He dictated letters. At three o'clock he reviewed the list of civilians displaced by billeting—Mrs. Halloway's kitchen among them—and approved compensation at one-third the market rate, on the grounds that full rates would establish precedent, and precedents, in wartime, were simply lies with tenure.

It was, by any measure, a good day. The report was drafted. The record was coming in like harvest. By Thursday the town's memory would sit in one building under one lock, and whatever left that building would be the past, officially.

Only once, late in the afternoon, did the stone in his chest shift.

He was back at the warehouse, alone, doing what he privately called *the audit of conscience*—walking the crates, reading spines. Parish registers. Ferry books. And in the third crate, a stack of hospital forms, fever charts in a small, precise, unfamiliar hand—neat columns of temperatures, dosages, pulse rates. Humble documents. Exactly the kind he had assured Mowbray were of interest to no one but fevers.

He flipped through them. Nothing. Names, dates, numbers. He was closing the crate when his eye caught the last form in the stack, half-tucked beneath the others: a supply requisition in the same neat hand, listing dressings issued, and beside one entry, in pencil so faint it was nearly an apology, a note in the margin. Not a comment on dressings. A date. The 19th. And a small careful figure, repeated twice, as if the writer had wanted to be certain of it himself.

Eleven. Eleven.

Ashworth stood very still in the cold warehouse, listening to the river move under the ice, and felt the peculiar sensation of a tidy man realizing that somewhere in his beautifully filed world, a page had been left loose.

He did not suspect conspiracy. He did not yet suspect anything so dramatic. He suspected arithmetic—that somewhere in this town, a man with good handwriting was keeping a private count that differed from the official one by exactly the margin the empire could least afford.

He closed the crate, locked the warehouse door behind him, and went to find Captain Mowbray, walking briskly, a man with a small discrepancy to resolve before it became a large one.

"Captain," he said, when he found him. "Tell me about the nurse. Lockridge."
