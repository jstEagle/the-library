## 6. What the Army Takes

They came for the town's memory on a Tuesday morning, which Felix thought was characteristic of the army. Sundays were for church and sentiment; Tuesdays were for paperwork.

He heard the wagon before he saw it — the particular complaint of an overloaded cart on frozen ruts, drawn by a horse that had opinions about hills. Then came Corporal Fenwick, who was nineteen, freckled, and earnest in the way of young men who have been given a list and told that the list is important.

"Doctor Lockridge," Fenwick said, touching his cap. Felix had given up correcting the title weeks ago; it seemed to comfort the boy, and comfort was in short supply. "Colonel's orders. All civic records to be consolidated for transport to headquarters. Parish registers, merchant accounts, school documents. Anything of official bearing."

"Official bearing," Felix repeated. "In a schoolhouse?"

"The roll books, sir. Attendance records." Fenwick consulted his list with the gravity of a man defusing something. "Also the church marriage register, the burial register, and — this one I don't understand, sir, but it's written down twice so I imagine somebody wants it — the minutes of the Fishing and Angling Society."

"The trout records," Felix said solemnly. "Naturally. If the enemy got hold of those, the war would be over by Thursday."

Fenwick grinned before he could stop himself, then remembered he was confiscating things and arranged his face into administrative sorrow.

* * *

Felix followed the corporal out into the street because a part of him — the nurse in him, or perhaps something older and less flattering — needed to watch. You learned things at bedsides that you couldn't learn anywhere else: how people carried pain, how they carried fear, and above all how they carried loss. He wanted to see what losing your records looked like on a face.

It looked like Mrs. Halloway standing on her own doorstep with her arms folded, watching two soldiers carry out the great ledger in which her late husband had recorded twenty-six years of grain sales, debts, favors, feuds, and the exact weight of every sack of flour that had ever crossed his counter.

"Careful with that," she said, in the voice of a woman addressing fools. "That book is worth more than every one of you put together, and I include the horse."

"Yes, ma'am," said the soldier carrying it, who was visibly afraid of her.

"You'll want the third volume as well," Mrs. Halloway continued, inexorable. "Bottom shelf, behind the brandy. My husband hid it there from the tax man, God rest him, on the principle that what the government doesn't know can't grieve his widow. If you're taking everything, take everything."

Felix caught her eye across the street. She did not smile. But something passed between them — the same look she'd given him the day the soldiers evicted her from her kitchen and she'd walked out with her head high and her recipe box under her arm, daring anyone to call it defeat.

*The ledger*, he thought, and his stomach turned over with a slow, cold deliberateness, like a boat coming loose from its mooring. *They're not archiving. They're amputating.*

A town without its records was a town that couldn't prove it had existed. Marriages unprovable. Burials deniable. And somewhere in those parish registers, he knew, were entries that would corroborate what the survivors of Marrow Bridge had babbled to him in their fever — dates, names, families who had lost sons who officially had never been born.

* * *

By noon the schoolhouse infirmary had acquired a strange holiday atmosphere, if one celebrated holidays by having strangers rummage through your belongings while your patients tried very hard to be furniture.

Father Anselm had come down from the church to argue about his registers and stayed to help, mostly by standing in doorways and being inconveniently holy. The soldiers were careful around priests; Ashworth's clean new army was scrupulous about appearances, and there was nothing worse for appearances than a clergyman watching you steal.

"The baptismal register is a sacramental record, Corporal," Father Anselm was saying for the fourth time, with the patience of a man who has explained grace to skeptics for forty years. "It belongs to the Church."

"And the Colonel says everything belongs to the Crown now, Father, so I suppose you'll have to take that up through channels."

"I shall. My channels are somewhat higher than yours, but I'm told they accept correspondence."

Felix left them to it and went back down the ward. Twelve men today, four of them surgical, the rest convalescing in various states of complaint. Private Willets, whose fever had finally broken, was conducting a whispered commerce in contraband biscuits with the boy in the next cot. Sergeant Marris lay staring at the ceiling with the fixed attention of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"They're taking the burial register," Marris said, without turning his head. It wasn't a question; he'd watched the wagon through the window all morning.

"They're consolidating records," Felix said carefully. "For safekeeping."

Marris made a sound that in a healthier man would have been a laugh. "I've been soldiering eighteen years, Doctor. I know what safekeeping looks like. Safekeeping has locks on it and a receipt. That—" he nodded toward the window, where a soldier was stacking ledgers like firewood, "—that's tidying up."

Felix didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wasn't dangerous, and Marris knew it too, which was why he said these things to the ceiling rather than to Felix's face. A kindness, of its grim kind.

* * *

The trouble started — well, no. The trouble had started long ago; what started on Tuesday afternoon was Felix's understanding of it.

He was in the supply room, cataloguing dressings, when he heard Fenwick outside say the words *"the doctor's papers"* and felt the whole world tilt a degree on its axis, the way a floor does just before you realize the ice won't hold.

"—medical records stay, obviously," Fenwick was telling the sergeant with him. "But any private notes, journals, that sort of thing. Orders say anything of documentary value."

"Documentary value," the sergeant said. "In a hospital. What do they think he's been writing, poetry?"

Felix stood very still among the crates, listening to his own heart conduct its business, and understood with perfect clarity two things at once.

The first was that his ledger — the small black book in which, over these winter months, he had written the names the reports omitted — was sitting in the bottom drawer of his desk not ten feet from where they stood.

The second was that he had never truly believed anyone would come for it. He had told himself, in the comfortable grammar of the timid, that it was only a private account, only a nurse's habit, only a way of keeping his own conscience off the books of heaven. Nobody arrests a man for writing names in a notebook. History happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals.

Except history, it turned out, was happening in a requisitioned river town in the form of a sergeant asking whether the doctor kept journals, and the answer was yes, and the journal contained eleven names from Marrow Bridge and a drowned porter and Mrs. Halloway's eviction and forty-some entries of small, stubborn, unforgivable truth.

"Felix Lockridge," he murmured to the dressings, "you are a fool of the first water," and even his inner voice sounded like Mrs. Halloway saying it.

* * *

He moved fast once he'd stopped being still.

The desk drawer was out of the question now — drawers get opened, that is their entire vocation. Under the mattress: no. Floorboard: the first place any searcher with sense looks, and these men had lists. Stove: burned, and burning it himself was unthinkable, though he noticed, with a cold little shock, that some tidy corner of his mind had already priced the option.

In the end it was the simplest hiding place, which is to say the one nobody wants to search: the crate of surgical dressings, packed for the field hospital's next movement, sitting in the corner under a tarpaulin. Felix prised up the top layer of neatly rolled bandages — each one folded by Mrs. Halloway, who had declared that if she was to be a refugee she would at least be a useful one — laid the ledger flat against the crate's floor, buried it again, and pressed the lid down with hands that would not entirely stop shaking.

There. Done. Invisible.

And then, kneeling there in the lamplight with his palms flat on the lid, he felt the second understanding arrive, the one that follows the first the way thunder follows the flash, and it arrived with the gentle, terrible precision of all true realizations:

Hiding it was worse than keeping it.

If the ledger sat openly in his desk, it was eccentric — a doctor's notes, dull as ditchwater, a curiosity. But a hidden ledger, found hidden, concealed beneath bandages like contraband, was evidence. Concealment was confession. Every inch of care he took now was a thread in the rope they might hang him with, and he had just, voluntarily, with his own hands, tied the first knot.

He knelt there a moment longer, feeling the fear move through him — not the hot spike of sudden danger but the deep, steady ache of a tooth that means to come out eventually — and waited to see what the fear would turn into.

Because it turned into something. That was the surprise. He had expected fear to end in paralysis or flight; instead it settled in his chest like a coal and began, quietly, to generate heat. *They are afraid of a black notebook,* he thought. *An entire army, with artillery, is afraid of a schoolmaster's pocketbook full of dead farm boys' names.* Somewhere in that absurdity was a fact worth attending to: you do not confiscate a nation's registers unless names frighten you. You do not burn accounts unless accounts testify.

*Well then,* Felix thought, rising stiffly to his feet and smoothing the tarpaulin. *Let us make sure something testifies.*

* * *

That evening the wagon pulled out loaded with the town's past: parish registers and merchant ledgers, the school roll books, the minutes of the Fishing and Angling Society (largest recorded trout: nine pounds, taken by Aldous Prentice in 1887, witnessed by God and three skeptical Methodists), and Mrs. Halloway's third volume, smuggled out from behind the brandy with her blessing and, she assured the soldiers, her curse.

Felix stood in the doorway of the infirmary and watched it go, and beside him Father Anselm stood too, hands tucked in his sleeves.

"They missed the fishing society's second minute book," the priest observed mildly.

"There's a second?"

"In the vestry. Prentice kept two — one for the committee, one for the truth. The committee version flattered him." Father Anselm's eyes crinkled. "I mention it only as an illustration, my son, of an old pastoral observation."

"Which is?"

"That whenever powerful men collect all the records, honest men should immediately start keeping their own."

Felix looked at him. The old man's face was perfectly innocent, which from Father Anselm was the most suspicious expression available.

"Father—"

"I saw nothing," the priest said serenely. "I hear a great deal in my confessional and repeat none of it, and I extend the same professional courtesy to infirmaries. But I will say this much aloud, since it is merely theology: a lie requires maintenance. Truth only requires a keeper." He patted Felix's shoulder with a hand like a sparrow landing. "Goodnight, Doctor. Mind how you keep."

He shuffled off into the snow, and Felix stood alone in the doorway, aware that he had either just been warned, blessed, or deputized — possibly all three.

Behind him, down the ward, Sergeant Marris had turned his head at last and was looking at Felix with eyes that had done their arithmetic. He didn't say anything. He just looked, for a long moment, and then gave the smallest nod, one soldier to another, and turned back to the ceiling.

Felix went inside and bolted the door — a gesture, he knew; bolts stop weather, not armies — and sat down at his desk, and drew toward him a fresh sheet of paper and the stub of a pencil.

If the ledger in the dressing crate was discovered, it must be discoverable as nothing. Which meant there had to be a nothing for it to hide inside: a set of medical notes, dull as ditchwater, that would satisfy a searcher and explain the book's existence beyond argument.

He began to write. *Case notes, January. Private Willets, fever, lanced, improving. Temperature chart attached.* And beneath it, in the same hand, the same ink, the same easy slant — because a forger varies his letters, but a tired doctor does not — the eleven names of Marrow Bridge, copied out one more time, each spelled with the care owed to a man's whole estate.

His hand was steady now. That was the strangest part. The fear was still there, but it had changed employers; it no longer worked for the army, whispering *hide, shrink, survive*. It worked for the ledger, whispering *careful, precise, exact* — the same voice that had taught him to tie a ligature, the voice that said a mistake here costs a life, so do not make mistakes.

Outside, the sentries called the hour. Somewhere across the dark river, where the telegraph office hummed its army-approved lies into the wire, Ada Quill was presumably sending casualty figures that had been halved by someone's pencil. He thought of her steady hands on the key and wondered — idly, professionally, the way you wonder about a colleague — whether she also kept accounts of her own.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would find out. Tomorrow he would begin inventing plausible reasons to send supply forms to the telegraph office, and study what came back.

Tonight there was only the lamp, the pencil, the names, and the cold — and underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the fear in his chest resolving, note by note, into resolve.

He wrote until midnight.
