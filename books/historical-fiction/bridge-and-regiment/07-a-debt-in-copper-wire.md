## 7. A Debt in Copper Wire

Ada Quill had a theory about lies: they weighed something. Every false dispatch she tapped out across the wire added an ounce to her chest, and by December she was carrying enough of them to sink a flatboat.

She thought about weight a great deal, because her whole trade was weight. A message was measured in words; a word in currents; a current in the small muscular dance of her wrist against the brass key. The army's lies were heavier than most traffic—clumsy things, badly balanced, like furniture made by someone who had never sat down. *Engagement concluded successfully. Casualties negligible.* Negligible. She'd begun keeping a private count of the word's appearances, the way other women counted days until a birthday.

The telegraph office had been requisitioned in November, and now smelled of wet wool and gun oil where it used to smell of paraffin and Post Office tea. The army had installed two sergeants and a signals lieutenant who believed, with the serene confidence of the very stupid, that Ada couldn't read what she transcribed. She could read it faster than they could dictate it. That was the whole joke of her profession, and she had been laughing at it alone for weeks.

Tonight the office was quiet after ten. The lieutenant had gone to his billet with a candle stub and a paperback romance he thought nobody knew about (she knew; she filed everything). One sergeant snored gently over a stove that gave more smoke than heat. Ada stayed late on the pretense of clearing a backlog, which was real, and on the hope that a particular caller would come, which she refused to admit even to herself.

He came.

Felix Lockridge knocked on the doorframe the way a man knocks when he has been told twice already that knocking is unnecessary—a small apologetic rap, as if the wood might be resting. He carried a canvas satchel and that careful expression she'd first noticed on supply forms, the handwriting so neat it looked embarrassed to be seen.

"Miss Quill. The infirmary's out of carbolic. I'm told requisitions go through here."

"Requisitions go through here," Ada agreed, "in the sense that they come here to die."

He almost smiled. He was a man who kept smiles in reserve, like coins in a boot. "May I?"

"There's a chair that doesn't wobble and a chair that does. Take your pick and live with your consequences."

He took the wobbly one. Of course he did.

She pulled the requisition ledger toward herself and watched him write while she pretended to sort tape. There it was again—that hand. Not beautiful, exactly. *Honest* was the strange word that came to mind. Every letter the same weight, no flourishes, nothing hidden. She had spent nine years reading hands across a wire, and this one bothered her the way a wrong note bothers a fiddler: not because it was bad, but because it meant something.

"Mr. Lockridge," she said, before she could decide not to, "why does a nurse keep accounts?"

His pen stopped. Just for a beat—the length of a heartbeat, the length of a dot on the wire—and then continued. But she had heard the hesitation, and he knew she'd heard it, and there was no longer any use pretending either of them was slow.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." She drew a form toward her and began ticking boxes that needed no ticking. "I transcribe maybe forty dispatches a day for the Colonel's people. Do you know what I've learned? Nobody writes neatly unless they're afraid of being misread later. Sloppy men trust memory. Careful men—" she glanced up—"careful men are building something for whoever comes after."

The stove popped. The sergeant snored on, faithful as a metronome.

"You're imagining things," Felix said mildly.

"Constantly. It's my best quality." She set down the pencil. "Last Tuesday a requisition came through for forty stretcher canvases. Your hand. And on the same day, a burial detail request for—shall I say it quietly?—for *stores*, which is what they call men now, apparently, when the numbers get embarrassing. Forty and thirty-one don't have anything to do with each other, except they do."

For a long moment he looked at her, and she watched him perform the arithmetic every careful person in an occupied town performed: *what does she know, who does she tell, what does it cost me.* She let him look. She had nothing better to hide than he did, and both of them were beginning to suspect it.

"If I said yes," he said finally, "you'd be in a great deal of danger just by knowing."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me anyway."

"I'm telling you anyway," Ada said, "because I've been on the other side of this conversation, and it's colder there."

* *

Here is what she did not say yet, what she had said aloud to no living soul since September, and what rose in her now the way water rises in a locked well:

Her brother Tom had joined the regiment at seventeen because the recruiting sergeant bought him a meat pie and told him the war would be over by spring. It was not over by spring. It was not over by the following spring, or the one after, and somewhere in the middle of it Tom Quill stood up from a flooded trench outside a nameless village and walked. Not toward the enemy. Just *away*, three hundred yards through the mud, in the general direction of home, until a corporal who wanted an easy commendation brought him back.

The trial lasted nine minutes.

Ada knew it lasted nine minutes because she had asked, afterward, four separate times, and pieced together the timetable from four separate liars whose lies didn't quite agree. Nine minutes: long enough for the charges to be read, short enough that the officers' luncheon went uneaten only slightly. There had been no defense counsel worth the name. There had been no witnesses called, though eleven men in Tom's company would have testified that he was seventeen and shell-shocked and had walked in his sleep before, once, on the march north. None of them were asked.

What there *had* been was paperwork. A summary of evidence written before the trial. A verdict written before the verdict. And afterward, the official version, transmitted along the wire in her own hand because she happened to be on duty when it came through, and no one had thought to spare her:

*Private T. Quill, found guilty of desertion in the face of the enemy. Sentence duly carried out. Discipline maintained.*

Discipline maintained. Four words. She had sent them without her hands shaking, which was the part that haunted her—not the sending, but that she was trained so thoroughly the sending didn't even register. Her body had learned to transmit anything. It was only later, at night, in her lodgings, that the weight arrived.

And every dispatch since—the halved casualty figures, the invented victories, the word *negligible* marching across the wire like a file of soldiers—every one of those she had sent with the same steady wrist, and each one was a small stone laid on Tom's grave by her own hand. They had shot her brother to make a point about discipline, and she had spent every day since proving the point for them, free of charge.

That was why she cooperated. That was the whole ugly sum of it. Not fear—fear was there, but it was housekeeping, not architecture. She cooperated because a girl who had failed to save one soldier with her name on him had somehow gotten the idea that obedience might be retroactively redeemed. It could not. She had checked. Thoroughly.

But there was another thing she had checked, too, in the small hours, alone with the office key and the lamp turned low. She had checked what else a wire could carry.

* *

"The requisition," Felix said at last, "isn't really why I came."

"No," Ada agreed. "Though the carbolic is real. You can take some."

He opened the satchel instead, hesitated, and then—she saw him decide, saw the decision travel across his face like weather—drew out a book. Not a grand book. An account book, stationer's stock, the kind a grocer uses, its cover gone soft at the corners from handling.

"This is yours," she said. Not a question.

"It's nobody's," he said. "Officially." He set it on the desk between them but did not open it, which she understood to be a courtesy, or a test, or both. "There are names in it. Names the reports leave out. A farm boy from Kessel who died of fever under my hands in October. A porter drowned off the coal barge. Mrs. Halloway, who was put out of her own kitchen. Since Marrow Bridge—" His voice did something very small and controlled. "Since Marrow Bridge there are more."

"How many?"

"Sixty-three."

Ada looked at the grocer's book and felt the well-water rise to the top. Sixty-three names, kept in a nurse's private ledger, against an army that owned every press, every post, every wire in the province. Sixty-three names that existed nowhere else—which meant they lived in one binding of soft cardboard, in a schoolhouse infirmary, guarded by a man who apologized to doorframes.

"They'll burn the town records eventually," she said. "You know that. Ashworth's been consolidating everything for 'transport to headquarters' for a week. Parish registers, roll books, merchant ledgers. When headquarters says *transport*, I transcribe *incinerate*—the cipher's not subtle." She touched the ledger's cover, lightly, the way she'd touch a key. "Whatever's in here dies with this room unless it leaves the town."

"Which is impossible," Felix said. "Three checkpoints. Bray at the bridge searches everything down to the seams of your boots. I've considered hiding it, and hiding it only works until the first time someone shakes a crate."

"Hiding it is stupid," Ada said. "Forgive me. It's worse than stupid—it's half a plan wearing a whole plan's coat." She stood, crossed to the machine, and laid her palm flat on the frame, warm from the day's work. "But you're a medical man. Tell me what happens to a thing that can be in many places at once."

Felix frowned at her, and she watched the idea arrive in stages, the way dawn arrives on water.

"You want to send it."

"I want to *send* it," she said. "Name by name, date by date, dressed up as ordinary traffic. Weather reports to the garrison at Fordham. Freight manifests for the commissary. Routine confirmations, duplicate copies, all the boring paper the army drowns in. Nobody reads boring paper, Mr. Lockridge—they merely *file* it. And meanwhile, on a spool of tape in a signals shed in Fordham, or wherever the duplicates land, sits every name your book holds. Unburnable. Uncrateable. In fifty places at once." She smiled, and found she meant it, which startled her. "You can't hide the truth. You can only multiply it."

He was quiet a long moment. The sergeant snored; the stove smoked; the wire hummed its faint constant note, the sound Ada privately thought of as the world holding its breath.

"It would take days," Felix said.

"Ten, twelve. Sixty-three names done properly, with dates and circumstances, buried deep enough that even a clever man sees only freight."

"And if you're caught—if anyone notices the traffic doubled—"

"Noticed," Ada repeated, pleasantly. "You say that as though being noticed is the worst thing that can happen to a person. I've been unnoticed for two years, Mr. Lockridge. It's considerably worse." Then, because the moment demanded honesty and honesty was a currency she rarely got to spend: "My brother was executed in September. Desertion. The trial lasted nine minutes—I've timed it out from the paperwork, backwards and forwards, and it comes to nine. The official version went over this wire, and I sent it, because it was my shift and my wrist is obedient. I have sent their version of everything since." She looked at her own hand, lying on the brass key, steady as ever. Steady always. "I am owed a debt, and the interest is compounding daily. You have sixty-three names in a grocer's book. I have a machine that can make them immortal. Between us we are nearly one honest person."

Felix Lockridge looked at her then the way she imagined he looked at feverish men—clearly, without flinching, with the specific attention of someone counting what could still be saved. Whatever he found in her face seemed to satisfy him, because he slid the ledger across the desk and opened it, and there they were, all of them: the small names, the erased ones, written in that careful hand that feared being misread.

"Where do we start?" he said.

"We start," Ada said, pulling fresh tape toward her, "with the farm boy from Kessel, because he's first in your book and I like a man who keeps order. We work nights, after ten, when the lieutenant is asleep with his romance novel and Sergeant Hobbs is dreaming of his farm. You'll stand watch and invent plausible traffic to fill the gaps—cargo weights, grain prices, anything dull. I encode. By New Year's Eve every name will be in fifty places, and Colonel Ashworth can burn whatever he likes and accomplish nothing."

Felix turned the pages slowly. "Why New Year's Eve?"

"Because it's the deadline that makes the work possible," she said. "Deadlines are mercy, dressed as pressure. Also because Voss prints on the first tide of January."

His head came up sharply. "You know about Voss."

"I know about everyone," Ada said. "It's my best quality, and my worst." She reached for the key, and beneath her fingers the circuit closed with its small familiar click, and for the first time in two years the sound felt less like a stone laid on a grave and more like a bell rung before a door that was about to open. "Now. Name one. Out loud. Let's begin."

Felix bent over the ledger, and read the first name into the lamplight—and neither of them saw, in the dark yard outside, a figure pause by the window a moment too long, note the hour, and walk on toward the barracks with a new entry already forming in his meticulous mind.
