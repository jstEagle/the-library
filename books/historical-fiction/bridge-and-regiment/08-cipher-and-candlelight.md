## 8. Cipher and Candlelight

### Ada

The telegraph office at night was a different animal from the telegraph office by day. By day it belonged to the army—boots on the floorboards, the smell of pipe tobacco and wet wool, a signals lieutenant who hummed hymns off-key while he dictated lies. By night it belonged to Ada, to the stove that ticked as it cooled, and to the wire itself, which sang its small dry songs whether anyone listened or not.

Felix Lockridge came on the first night at half past eleven, letting himself in with the knock they'd agreed upon: two, pause, one. Like a man asking permission rather than demanding entry. She had decided already that she liked this about him.

"You brought them," she said.

He set his ledger on the desk between the candle and the key. It was a plain thing, oilcloth-bound, swollen with slips of paper tucked into its seams like feathers into a mattress. He handled it the way other men handled infants or explosives.

"Forty-one names," he said. "As of Tuesday. There's a porter who drowned off the coal barge that I haven't confirmed yet. I don't write them until I'm sure."

"Sure of what?"

"Sure they're dead." He sat down across from her, and up close she could see he hadn't slept properly in weeks; there was a bruised look under his eyes that no amount of careful washing removed. "A name in here is an accusation, Miss Quill. I want it to be a true one."

*Ada,* she almost said. *Call me Ada.* She didn't. Not yet. There were orders of intimacy, and names came after conspiracy, not before.

She opened her cipher book—the army's own, issued with her commissioning papers, which struck her now as the single funniest thing the war office had ever done—and smoothed a fresh sheet of flimsy paper.

"Right," she said. "Here's how we hang ourselves. Weather reports go out at midnight and four. Nobody reads them, because nobody cares whether it rained on the marsh. Freight manifests go out when the quartermaster sleeps, which is always, because Corporal Pell drinks. We put your names in as tonnage."

"As tonnage," Felix repeated.

"Twelve hundredweight of barley means twelve men. Barley is infantry. Oats are wounded. 'Damaged in transit'—" she paused, and found her voice had gone quieter than she intended, "—is dead."

Felix looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then he said, "And the names themselves? The actual names?"

"Initials and a number. F.L., forty-one, if you like. Anyone reading it cold sees a clerk's shorthand." She looked up at him. "It isn't elegant, Mr. Lockridge. It isn't even safe. But it goes out tonight, and it keeps going out every night until it's all across the river, and no colonel can burn what's already flown."

He turned back the oilcloth cover. In the candlelight his handwriting appeared—small, upright, patient, each letter formed as though the man it named deserved better than haste. She watched him copy the first entry onto her flimsy sheet, and she thought: *he writes them like prayers too.* She knew that style of devotion. She had practiced it herself, once, writing letters to a brother who had been dead for a year before anyone told her.

At midnight she sent the first report. Rain on the marsh, she tapped. Wind southeast. Twelve hundredweight barley, damaged in transit: three. Her fist was steady. Felix stood watch at the shuttered window, counting soldiers past in the snow, and neither of them spoke until it was done.

"Well," he said at last, into the silence where the wire's echo still hung. "That's Elias Halloway on his way to the river."

"That's twelve hundredweight of barley on its way," Ada corrected gently. "Elias went when you wrote him down."

* * *

### Felix

On the second night, Felix learned that Ada Quill swore like a stevedore when the key stuck, and apologized to nobody for it, including the machine.

"It does this," she announced, thumping the brass frame with the heel of her hand, "every time the temperature drops below the point of Christian behavior. You have to coax it. It's a vain old thing."

"Like most army property," Felix said.

"Like most men," said Ada, and coaxed it, and it worked.

They had settled into a rhythm that surprised him with its ease. She sat at the key; he sat at the desk with the ledger open and a second sheet for inventing plausible traffic—a fictitious shipment of harness fittings here, a routine query about fodder there—to pad the wire so that the real cargo wouldn't stand out like a black thread on white cloth. The padding, he discovered, required a strange kind of imagination. He had never supposed that lying well was a craft, but it was: every invented crate needed a weight, a destination, a reason.

"Pell will notice if we ship oats to a depot that has no horses," Ada said, leaning over his shoulder. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and machine oil. "Give it to the ferry landing. They'll sign for anything."

"The ferry landing. Right." He wrote it. "You know, I spent six years believing I wasn't suited to deception. I'm beginning to think I merely lacked instruction."

"You're a quick study." She took the sheet, checked his figures against her cipher book, tapping each one with a pencil stub. "There. And there. And—no, wait, that's my error, not yours. Twelve hundredweight, not thirteen. Congratulations, Mr. Lockridge, you've just murdered a man with arithmetic."

"I'll try not to make a habit of it."

At some point past two in the morning she produced a tin of cigarettes from her coat pocket, and they smoked by the stove with the shades drawn, passing it back and forth because she had only the one and he had nothing to contribute but his silence, which she seemed to accept as fair payment.

"Why did you learn cipher?" he asked. "Properly, I mean. Most operators just follow the book."

"My brother taught me. Before the army took him." She said it evenly, looking at the ember, and Felix understood that the door behind those words was locked and that tonight was not the night she meant to open it. "He said the book cipher was designed by idiots to be used by idiots, and that any idiot willing to sit down and think for a week could beat it. He was right. It took me eight days."

"He sounds like someone worth knowing."

"He was." A drag on the cigarette. The ember brightened and dimmed, like something deciding. "He was shot in April. For desertion. The trial took nine minutes; I've done the arithmetic since, and nine minutes works out to about forty seconds per man accused that morning. So you'll forgive me if I don't weep over the army's barley."

Felix found that his throat had closed. He offered the only thing he had.

"In the ledger," he said, "there's a boy called Tomas Quill. Sixteen. Conscripted off the road near Ferrow in October."

Ada went very still.

"Ferrow," she repeated.

"Quill isn't so rare a name. It may be nobody."

"It may be somebody." She stood, crossed to the desk, and turned the pages with a care that undid him more than any weeping would have. When she found the entry she read it twice, and then she closed the ledger and rested both hands flat upon it, the way one rests hands on the lid of a coffin at a burial, and she said nothing for a long time.

When she finally spoke, her voice was perfectly level, which frightened him more than tears would have.

"Tomorrow night we send the rest," she said. "All of them. Every name you've got."

* * *

### Ada

The third night, the snow came down thick enough to muffle the sentries' boots, which made the whole enterprise feel oddly cozy, like smuggling contraband during a church service.

By then they had a system and even, God help them, jokes. Felix had taken to composing absurd freight—"sixty stone of pickled herring, condition poor, consignee unknown"—solely to make her break concentration, and she had taken to punishing him with extra manifest entries whenever he succeeded. The ledger emptied by inches. Forty-one names became twenty-eight became nineteen, each one riding out of the town disguised as agricultural produce, bound for a fishing village where a disgraced correspondent sat waiting for exactly this kind of dirty weather.

"Voss," Felix said, watching her send. "Tell me about him again."

"Drunken, disgraced, unemployable, and honest," Ada said. "The last quality is the expensive one. Every respectable man in the profession sold his byline years ago; Voss's crime was refusing. They took everything else instead." The key clattered under her fingers, unhurried. "He'll print it. That's the whole of his character, really—he prints things. It's ruined him twice and it will ruin him a third time and he'll die pleased."

"And us? Are we ruined too, do you think?"

She glanced back at him. He was sitting in the chair by the stove with the ledger open on his knees, half-smiling, candlelight on the tired planes of his face, and she felt the question land somewhere beneath her ribs where questions had no business landing.

"Probably," she said. "Try to look surprised when it happens."

Around three o'clock, between the last barley and the final damaged-in-transit, they ran out of cigarettes and names at nearly the same moment, and fell into one of the silences that had become, over three nights, their native language. It was not an awkward silence. It was the kind two people earn.

"You know what I keep thinking," Felix said eventually. "That I told myself for months I was only a nurse. That history happened elsewhere, in the headquarters and the reports, and I just mopped up after it." He shook his head slowly. "I was never only anything. None of us were. We were all keeping accounts—we simply kept different ones."

"Yours balance," Ada said.

"So does yours. Nine minutes for a trial." He met her eyes across the candle flame. "Somebody should pay that debt, Ada."

*Ada.* She noticed it only now—that he had crossed the line she'd drawn without ever seeming to step over it, the way snow crosses a sill. She found she did not mind at all.

"Somebody will," she said.

She was reaching for the key to send the final confirmation—a routine acknowledgment, harmless, the last stitch in three nights of seamwork—when Felix raised a hand.

"Wait."

He was looking at the window. Through the gap in the shutters, snow was falling, and beyond the snow, at the corner of the street, a lantern stood motionless where no sentry had any reason to stand motionless. It had been there, Felix mouthed, since before midnight.

Ada blew out the candle. In the sudden dark the office filled with the sound of their breathing and the small ticking of the cooling stove, and outside, unhurried, deliberate, a man's boots began to crunch through the snow toward the telegraph office door.
