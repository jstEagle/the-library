## 9. Sergeant Bray Counts Heads

Sergeant Daniel Bray had been counting things since he was seven years old, when his father put him on a stool behind the family's greengrocer stall in Chedley and told him that a man who could count faster than he could talk would never go hungry. It was good advice, and Bray had followed it faithfully for thirty-one years, which was precisely twenty-nine years longer than his father had managed before the drink caught up with the arithmetic.

He counted the crates of the morning convoy: fourteen, two short of the manifest. He counted the mules: nine, one of them lame in the near hind and pretending not to be. He counted the soldiers escorting them: six, of whom four were awake, and of those four only two were worth counting at all. And then, because it was habit and habit was the spine that held him upright through these gray winter mornings at the ferry landing checkpoint, he counted the gulls wheeling over the river — eleven — and decided they looked better fed than the conscripts.

"You're doing the birds again, Sarge," said Private Oakes, who had served under him long enough to read his lips moving.

"Eleven," Bray said. "Same as yesterday. Gulls don't lie, Oakes. They've got no reason to."

"Soldiers have got no reason to either, Sarge."

Bray looked at him. Oakes was twenty and still believed things like that, and Bray hadn't the heart to correct him before breakfast. He'd learned in nineteen years of service — India, the Cape, and now this frozen river town whose name he still stumbled over — that everybody had a reason to lie, and the better the cause, the better the lying. The army lied about rations. Officers lied about engagements. He himself lied to his wife in letters, cheerfully and constantly, about how warm his boots were and how well the food kept. Lying wasn't a sin in Bray's book. It was a currency. What mattered was watching the exchange rates.

And lately, the exchange rates at the telegraph office had gone strange.

* * *

The trouble began, if you could call it trouble — and Bray did, quietly, professionally, the way other men called trouble by its first name — three weeks after the occupation. The town had been taken without a shot, which Bray considered the finest kind of taking, and the army had moved into the schoolhouse, the church vestry, and the telegraph office on the square like a family moving into furniture that didn't belong to them. Bray drew checkpoint duty at the ferry landing, which was the sort of posting you got when you were too good to promote and too honest to trust, and he accepted it as he accepted everything, by counting it: forty paces of plank road, one barrier arm, one stove that heated exactly half a hut, and one view of brown water that made him homesick for a country he'd left at eighteen.

Every day, traffic came down the wire from the town to headquarters across the river. Every day, the signals clerk at the relay copied the daily summary for the checkpoint file, because regulations said he must, and because nobody had ever thought to say otherwise. Bray read the summaries over his tea. Not out of ambition — God knew he'd turned down the exam twice, once on principle and once because the examination room in the fort had smelled of wet wool — but out of habit. A man counts what passes in front of him. That was all.

For the first fortnight, the summaries ran eleven, twelve items a day. Weather. Ration returns. Patrol reports so dull Bray used them to settle himself to sleep, which he suspected was their true purpose. Then, around the third week, twelve became fifteen. Fifteen became nineteen. By Christmas week there were days with twenty-four separate transmissions logged out of that little office, in a town of barely four hundred souls, most of whom were now either evicted or employed at fixing the army's boots.

"Oakes," Bray said one evening, laying the summaries side by side on the crate they used for a table, "what do you see?"

Oakes leaned in, hopeful, the way a dog hopes the pointing finger means food and not the window. "Paper, Sarge?"

"Look again."

Oakes looked again, honestly, which was the best thing about him. "More paper?"

"That's right." Bray tapped the column of figures. "More paper. Twice as much wire out of a town this size as there was before we came. Now think about that. We brought the town order, didn't we? Order means less to report, not more. When I was a boy, my mum sent me to the neighbor's to say her sister had arrived safe. One message. If I'd gone round every hour to say she was still there, still there, still there, my mum would've wanted to know what I was doing at the neighbor's house."

Oakes considered this. "Maybe the weather's worse, Sarge."

"The weather," Bray said, "is uniformly terrible. It doesn't need describing twenty-four times a day." He squared the pages against the crate edge, a small satisfying motion. "Something's riding on that wire that isn't in the summaries. And somebody's dressed it up as freight and fog."

He didn't say anything more dramatic than that, because drama was for men with fewer responsibilities. But he took an empty ration ledger from the bottom of his kit — the kind with the oilcloth cover that survived everything but direct fire — and ruled himself some columns with a carpenter's pencil, because if you couldn't count your way to an answer, you could at least count your way to a better question.

Column one: date. Column two: transmissions logged. Column three: anomalies. He wasn't sure yet what qualified as an anomaly, which struck him as the sort of problem that would solve itself if he simply wrote things down and let the numbers do the talking. Numbers always talked eventually. Sometimes they said things you didn't want to hear, but they never said things that weren't true, and Bray had built his whole life on that distinction.

* * *

The anomalies began introducing themselves politely.

On the twelfth of December, a freight manifest crossed the checkpoint for medical stores that listed, among the usual splints and carbolic, *four hundredweight of candlewick*. Bray stood in the road holding the manifest and turned this over. Candlewick. Four hundredweight. For a hospital. He'd seen military hospitals from Delhi to Durban, and not one of them, in his professional recollection, had ever wicked a candle. Hospitals had lamplighters and paraffin and, latterly, electric wires in the officers' wards. Candlewick was what you ordered when you needed wicks — and wicks, in turn, were what you burned things slowly with, or measured things out in, or — he stopped himself there, because he was constructing a conspiracy out of string, and a man who saw conspiracies in string ended up writing angry letters to newspapers.

Still. Into column three it went.

On the fifteenth, a weather report went through at an hour when weather reports didn't go through — 0300, when the only weather worth reporting was the weather inside a man's coat. On the seventeenth, two identical manifests crossed within four hours, differing only in the initials of the signing officer, one *F.L.* and one *F.L.*, in handwriting so careful it looked printed, the kind of hand that belonged to a man who wrote things out twice to be sure. On the nineteenth, the tally in his ledger read *transmissions: 26*, and Bray sat back on his stool and whistled through the gap in his teeth.

Twenty-six dispatches out of a requisitioned river town with one telegraph key, one operator, and nothing happening that the official reports didn't already describe in terms so bland they could be read aloud at dinner parties.

He began, in the evenings, to walk past the telegraph office on his way to nowhere in particular. This required no deception; sergeants were allowed to walk, and walking was free, and the square lay between his hut and the canteen, or would have if he'd bothered going to the canteen, which he didn't, because canteen conversation consisted of men telling each other how much worse things had been in their last posting, a contest nobody won. So he walked, hands clasped behind him, breath steaming, and he noted things.

He noted that the operator was a woman — Miss Quill, the townsfolk called her, though the soldiers mostly didn't call her anything, because there was something about her that discouraged calling-out, a stillness like deep water. She worked long hours, longer than any schedule demanded. She worked them alone, which was irregular; army procedure wanted a second signatory on night traffic, and there was none. He noted that when dispatch riders came to carry the tape across the river, she handed it over sealed and counted the spools herself before releasing them, and that her fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle, and that she never once looked at the checkpoint sergeant walking past the window, which was itself interesting, because everyone else looked. People look at power. It's a reflex, like flinching. Not looking at it — that takes practice, and practice takes motive.

He noted, too, the nurse. Lockridge, the infirmary man, tall and stooped and quiet as a held breath, who came down to the landing every few days with supply forms for medical stores crossing to the forward posts. His forms were always in order. Always. Perfectly, immaculately in order, in that same printed-looking hand, every i dotted like a man setting type. And Bray had processed perhaps ten thousand forms in his career, and he could tell you that perfectly ordered forms were like perfectly calm seas: pleasant, and a warning. The sea is calmest over the deepest water.

"Morning, Sergeant," Lockridge said, one bright freezing Tuesday, offering up his papers at the barrier.

"Sergeant," Bray said, and looked at the form, and looked at Lockridge, and felt the peculiar pleasure of two pieces of a puzzle touching edges without yet fitting. "Cold enough for you?"

"It suits the work," Lockridge said, which was the answer of a man who'd rehearsed being asked about the weather, and why would anyone rehearse that? Nobody rehearsed the weather. You rehearsed the questions that mattered.

"Mm," said Bray, and stamped the form, because the form was genuine, and the stores were genuine, and a man couldn't detain cargo on account of neat handwriting, though God knows he'd have liked to. He watched Lockridge walk back up the hill toward the schoolhouse with his coat collar up, unhurried, and he thought: *there's a man carrying something heavier than what's in his bag.* Then he thought: *or he's a fussy clerk and you're an old fool making work for yourself.*

He went back to his ledger that night and added a fourth column. He titled it, after some deliberation, *weight*, because he couldn't think of a better word for the quality certain men and certain manifests had of mattering more than their size suggested. Against Lockridge he wrote *3*. Against Miss Quill, *4*. Against the candlewick, *2*. Against his own growing certainty, he wrote nothing, because certainty was exactly what he didn't have yet, and he was too old a hand to write down guesses. Guesses were for officers. Sergeants dealt in facts, and facts were like potatoes: you dug them one at a time, and you washed off the dirt before you served them.

* * *

It was Oakes, of all people, who handed him the next potato, entirely by accident, on the day after Christmas.

"Found this blowing about the yard, Sarge," Oakes said, bringing in a scrap of paper tape, the punched kind, torn off short. "Telegraph leavings. Thought you'd want the stove to have it."

Bray took it, meaning to feed it straight to the fire, and then — because a man counts what passes in front of him, even scraps — he held it to the lamp first. Punch-tape. Standard five-hole code, the kind every signalman learned before they taught him anything else. He'd picked up a smattering of it years ago in India, from a signals corporal who traded lessons for cigarettes, and his fingers remembered more than his head did. He laid the tape flat and read the hole-patterns the way his father had read the price of cabbage: quickly, greedily, without understanding until afterward.

Most of it was routine — he could pick out the shapes for common letters, fragments, *the... and... ing* — freight language, manifest language. But there was a run in the middle where the pattern shifted, groups of holes in a rhythm that didn't parse as English, regular as fence-posts, deliberate. Cipher. Encoded text dressed inside ordinary traffic, riding along in the body of a message like stowaways under tarpaulin.

He sat with it a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the river ground its ice against the pilings, patient as everything else in this war.

Now, the proper thing — the regulation thing, the thing that would appear in his own report if he did it — was to carry the scrap to Captain Hessler at signals intelligence and say, *found this, sir,* and let the machinery grind. And he would do that. Of course he would. In the morning.

But first, he took out his oilcloth ledger and wrote, beneath the running tally, a line of plain fact:

*Dec 26. Cipher present in freight traffic. Duration unknown. Volume unknown.*

And then, because honesty was the whole point of the exercise, he added:

*Unknown how long I've been standing next to it without seeing.*

That was the part that kept him awake that night, staring at the hut ceiling while Oakes slept the blameless sleep of the young. Not the cipher — cipher was a tool, and tools told you what a thing was doing, if not why. The volume. If the traffic had doubled since the occupation, and the doubling had started three weeks ago, and he'd only noticed it last week — then the pattern had been talking for some time before he'd learned its language. Whatever was being smuggled down that wire, some of it had already crossed the river. Some of it was already out of anyone's reach, including his.

And there was a further thought underneath that one, a cold one, the kind that came to him rarely and always at night: *suppose the smuggling isn't the crime.* Suppose the official version was. Bray had served long enough to know that the difference between the dispatches and the truth was usually a rounding error, but errors of that size — doubled traffic, hidden cipher, a nurse with rehearsed answers and an operator who wouldn't meet his eye — errors that size meant somebody upstream was doing arithmetic of their own. Making numbers come out the way they wanted. Halving things, maybe. Erasing things.

He thought about the wounded who'd come through the landing from Marrow Bridge direction, and how the official engagement report had described a clean action against armed irregulars, and how one of the stretcher cases, feverish, had gripped Oakes's sleeve and babbled something about *they'd put down their guns, they'd put them down,* before the morphine took him. Oakes hadn't reported it. Oakes was twenty and believed soldiers had no reason to lie, and the babbling of a dying man didn't fit that belief, so Oakes had quietly set it aside, the way you set aside a coin of the wrong weight.

But Bray had picked it up. Bray picked up everything. It was his trade, his inheritance, his single talent: heads counted, crates counted, words counted, and now, apparently, lies counted too.

In the morning, he took the scrap of tape across the yard to Captain Hessler, and reported it cleanly, and watched Hessler initial the receipt with the bored efficiency of a man who received forty scraps a week. And then Bray walked back to his checkpoint, and sat down at his crate, and opened his oilcloth ledger, and began — carefully, methodically, in columns that would have made his father proud — to build a list of every person and every document that had crossed his barrier since the eleventh of December, weighted by his own instinct, annotated in a shorthand only he could read.

It was, he reflected, not unlike keeping books for a greengrocer. Somewhere in the columns, the sums refused to balance. His job was to find the item that didn't exist on any official manifest and yet kept appearing in the totals.

He had narrowed it, by the new year, to three names, one woman, and four hundredweight of candlewick.

And on the last morning of the old century, with frost ferns climbing the inside of the hut windows and the river running gray under a sky like scoured tin, the orderly came down from the schoolhouse with a movement order: a medical convoy, stretcher cases and stores, crossing the river on the afternoon tide. Escort duty assigned to the checkpoint. Signed in a hand so careful it looked printed.

*F.L.*

Bray read it twice, folded it, and put it in the breast pocket where he kept the things he intended to think about. Then he took out his pencil and licked the tip, and beside the name *Lockridge* in column three, in the space reserved for weight, he crossed out the *3*.

He wrote *5*.

"Afternoon's going to be busy, Oakes," he said. "Fetch the barrier grease. And wake up properly. Today we count everything."
