## 10. The First Checkpoint

The order came at dawn, written in the round, self-satisfied hand of Captain Merrow: *Corporal-nurse Lockridge to escort medical consignment across the river, first light, four stretchers, six crates. Sergeant Bray's checkpoint to be accorded full cooperation.*

*Full cooperation.* In the army's vocabulary that meant standing very still while other men handled your belongings as though looking for something they intended to find.

Felix read the order twice by candlelight, then folded it into his pocket and sat for a long moment on the edge of his cot, listening to the infirmary breathe. Forty men sleeping, coughing, murmuring into fever dreams. Somewhere among them was Private Oakes, who had asked him last night whether his mother would be told he'd lost two fingers or three, and Felix had said two, because a man could hold on to hope better than arithmetic.

And beneath all of it — under the cots and the bandages and the smell of carbolic and the whole shivering apparatus of mercy — lay a ledger with forty-one names sewn into stretcher canvas number three.

He had done the sewing himself, badly. Ada had offered to do it, her hands being quicker and cleaner, but he had refused, and not out of chivalry either, though she'd raised an eyebrow that suggested she suspected chivalry. He'd refused because if it went wrong he wanted the guilt to sit where it belonged. That was the arithmetic he'd made peace with: *my hands, my thread, my neck.* It felt noble right up until the moment you noticed that nobility is mostly fear wearing a better coat.

The stitching ran along the underside of the canvas frame, hidden by a fold, disguised as repair work. To any reasonable eye it looked exactly like what it pretended to be — a field medic patching his own equipment, which every medic did constantly, because the army issued stretchers made of optimism and burlap.

To any unreasonable eye — to the eye of a sergeant who counted things — it might look like a man smuggling paper.

He dressed quietly. He checked the canvas twice more, running his thumb along the seam like a man feeling for his own pulse. Then he went to wake the orderlies and begin the terrible ordinary business of loading a convoy.

* * *

The river town had learned to be quiet in the way of animals. Smoke rose from chimneys straight and thin, apologetic. Mrs. Halloway stood at her window as the wagons passed, and Felix tipped his hat to her, and she did not wave back, and he understood that too was a kind of message — one that said, *I know what you're doing, and I know it isn't enough.* She was in his ledger now. Her name sat between a drowned porter and a farm boy called Tewson, and there were days when Felix thought the ledger should simply have a page headed HALLOWAY and nothing else, because how many names did you need before the page became the story?

Private Wren drove the lead wagon, a boy of nineteen who narrated everything he saw in a low constant murmur, like a man reading scripture to himself on a long walk. "Gate's open. Frost on the axle. Dog again. Same dog." The dog in question trotted alongside them for half a mile, gray and dignified, and Wren addressed it once directly: "Morning."

Behind the wagons came the four stretchers, lashed upright to a handcart, canvas number third from the left. Felix walked beside it. If anyone had asked why he kept touching it he would have said *checking the lashings*, and it would even have been true, in the way that most lies are true wearing someone else's coat.

The dying soldier came last. Corporal Hale, twenty-six, a hemorrhage deep in the belly that the regimental surgeon had pronounced "a matter of hours" with the brisk confidence of a man reading tomorrow's weather. They had strapped Hale to a litter between two barrels of dressing lint, and he drifted in and out, sometimes asking for water, sometimes apologizing — actually apologizing — for the inconvenience.

"You're no inconvenience, Corporal," Felix told him.

"Feel like one," Hale said. "Bloody embarrassing. Everyone so busy." And then, after a silence: "Is it Tuesday?"

"It's whatever day gets you across the river fastest," Felix said, which was neither honest nor dishonest but was, he hoped, warm.

They reached the bridge checkpoint at nine. The frost had hardened into little white teeth along the planks, and Sergeant Bray stood at the far end of them like a man whom winter had personally commissioned.

Bray was not large. He was not loud. He was, Felix had decided during their previous encounters, the most dangerous kind of soldier: a patient one. He had small eyes set close together and the habit of counting without appearing to count — wagons, crates, heads, breaths. When Felix approached with the papers held out, Bray took them and read them at leisure, as though they were correspondence from an old friend.

"Medical consignment," Bray said.

"That's what it says."

"It usually does." He folded the order with great care, creasing each fold precisely, and put it away. "Six crates."

"Six."

"Four stretchers."

"Four."

"Funny thing," Bray said pleasantly, "last convoy was five crates. Month before, seven. Your department runs light some months and heavy others. Almost as if somebody over there can't add." He smiled. It was not a smile that invited company.

"The army's figures are the army's figures, Sergeant."

"They generally are," Bray agreed. "That's what makes them interesting." He turned and raised his voice just enough. "Right! Everything off. Everything open. You know the drill, gentlemen — the drill knows you."

* * *

The search took an hour and eleven minutes, and Felix counted every one of them.

Bray did not rush. Rushing, Felix understood, was for men who wanted to get somewhere. Bray wanted to *be* somewhere — here, at the middle of this bridge, in the cold, exercising the small enormous kingdom of a checkpoint. He opened the first crate himself, lifted out the lint rolls, sniffed one, set it aside. An orderly opened the second. A lance-corporal with red ears opened the third and fourth while Bray supervised, offering corrections.

"Careful with that. That's chloroform. You spill it, you'll be the most popular man in the garrison for ten minutes and unconscious for two hours."

Red Ears grinned. Even terror, Felix noted, has a comedy circuit.

Wagon by wagon, crate by crate. Felix stood beside stretcher three and performed boredom. Boredom, he had decided, was the uniform you wore over fear; he only hoped it fit. He examined the sky. He examined the river. He examined a knot in the bridge rail with the absorption of a naturalist discovering a new species. Behind him, Private Wren murmured, "Barrels next. He'll tap the barrels. He always taps the barrels," and indeed Bray tapped the barrels, and listened to each one as though the lint might answer questions.

Then Bray came to the stretchers.

He started at number one. Ran his hand under the canvas, pressed the frame, turned it over. Number two, same. He worked slowly, humming something — Felix recognized it distantly as a hymn, which struck him as theologically ambitious for a checkpoint — and moved toward number three, and Felix's heart began doing work that no medical consignment required.

There was time, in those few seconds, for the mind to travel. Felix thought of Ada's hands — quick, certain, black-stained fingers that spoke fluently to machines — and how she'd said, the night they'd finished copying the names into cipher, *"Paper burns. Wire doesn't. Remember that."* He thought of the eleven names from Marrow Bridge, how his handwriting had shaken so badly that young Tewson's *T* looked like a gallows. He thought, absurdly, of Mrs. Halloway's kitchen, the copper pots she'd been permitted to take and the ones she hadn't, and how the army had inventoried both.

Sergeant Bray's hand found the fold.

He pressed it. Frowned slightly — a small frown, economical, the frown of a man whose trade was patterns and who had just met one that didn't match. His fingers came back along the seam. Pressed again. There was, Felix knew, a thickness there. Paper wrapped in oilcloth. Not much of one. Enough.

"Hm," said Bray.

It was the smallest possible syllable, and it contained a trial, a verdict, and a wall against which Felix had already measured his own height.

At which point Corporal Hale began to die loudly.

It started as a groan from the litter by the barrels, then a wet, urgent sound, and then the orderly shouting — "Sir! Sergeant! He's coming apart, sir, the packing's gone through—" and there was blood, quite a lot of it, spreading through the blanket with shocking speed, and Hale's eyes were open and fixed on the sky as if reading dispatches there.

Bray straightened. For one second — Felix would replay that second for the rest of his life — the sergeant looked at the fold under the canvas, and then at the bleeding man, and the arithmetic behind his small eyes was perfectly visible: *one mystery, one emergency, one sergeant, two hands.*

"Your man's leaking out," Bray said.

"I can see that."

"Well then." He stepped back from stretcher three and gestured at the bridge with something almost like courtesy. "Get him across. Move the rest after. I'll want a word about your canvas when you're done — this one." He patted stretcher three. "This one stays on my side till we've talked."

"Yes, Sergeant," Felix said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he was a bell being struck.

They got Hale across the bridge at a run, the handcart rattling, Wren narrating in a strangled whisper — "frost, ice, don't slip, God, don't slip" — and Felix holding the dying man's hand without remembering reaching for it. On the far bank the relief orderly took over, and there was a cottage requisitioned as a receiving station, and a surgeon, and the machinery of trying. Felix stood in the doorway while they worked, breathing the smell of blood and lamp oil, and found that his shame arrived before his relief did, which seemed significant.

Because here was the truth he had to carry across the bridge along with everything else: he had used a dying man as a screen. Hale's hemorrhage had been, functionally, a diversion — the universe's cruelest sleight of hand, performed on his behalf. He had passed by inches, and the inches had been bought with another man's blood, and somewhere behind him on that bridge sat stretcher three, in the custody of Sergeant Bray, containing forty-one names that would hang him if the seam were ever opened.

Hale lived until evening. The surgeon said the hemorrhage had been beyond saving from the start; nothing Felix did or failed to do would have changed the ledger there. But knowing a thing and forgiving yourself for it are different countries, and Felix couldn't cross between them. He wrote down Hale's name that night — date, rank, wound, hour — and underneath it, in smaller script, he added: *He bought me the bridge. I owe him more than ink.*

* * *

The word came back two days later, delivered by a supply driver with a taste for gossip: the convoy's rear elements had eventually crossed, all six crates accounted for, all four stretchers — including number three — released after inspection.

"Inspection?" Felix asked, keeping his voice level around the cold weight in his chest.

"Bray went over that canvas like his mother made it." The driver grinned. "Found a loose rivet on stretcher two and confiscated it. Then he stood there a good ten minutes with your number three, feeling along the seams. Then he let it go."

"Let it go."

"Said, 'Tell Lockridge his stitching's a disgrace.'" The driver laughed. "Cheeky beggar. Like a tailor inspecting a bishop."

Felix smiled, thanked him, and walked back to the infirmary with his skin crawling pleasantly the entire way. Because there were two readings of that story, and he was fluent in both. In the first, Bray had searched and found nothing, because the oilcloth packet rode the fold in a way that felt like padding, and padding was everywhere in army equipment. In the second — the one that kept Felix awake — Bray had found *something*, had weighed it in his careful fingers, had understood precisely what it was and what it meant, and had let it cross anyway, and filed it.

*Said patterns were his trade,* Felix thought, lying in the dark. *And a pattern needs more than one point before it's a line.*

Either way, the ledger was across the river now. Either way, Sergeant Bray had touched the truth and handed it back, and Felix could not decide whether he'd been spared, marked, or recruited — and suspected, with a nurse's instinct for symptoms, that the worst possibility was all three.

He was still turning it over when a runner appeared at the infirmary door, stamping snow off his boots, holding out a folded paper with a familiar round-handed signature.

Captain Merrow's compliments. Nurse Lockridge was requested to present himself at headquarters Friday evening. Dinner dress optional.

Colonel Ashworth looked forward to making his acquaintance.

Felix read it twice. Across the room, a kettle sang; someone moaned in fever; the infirmary went on breathing. He folded the invitation into his breast pocket, where it sat over his heart like a stone.

*Dinner,* he thought, *with the man who is burying a massacre.* He looked down at his hands — the same hands that had sewn a ledger into canvas, that had held Corporal Hale's while the life ran out of them — and wondered which of them would still be free by Sunday.

Friday, then. Headquarters. He would bring his best manners and his worst intentions, and pray the two stayed sorted.

Across the river, in a fishing hut he could not yet see, a disgraced correspondent waited for names. Behind him, in a telegraph office, Ada Quill was feeding them into the wire one cipher at a time. And somewhere between the three of them and the gallows stood Sergeant Bray, humming a hymn, counting.
