## 11. The Man Across the River

Ada Quill had a theory about lies: they were like cheap buttons. They held fine at a distance, but press them and they popped, one by one, until the whole coat hung open in the cold.

The army's dispatches were sewn entirely of cheap buttons.

She sat in the telegraph office at half past six in the morning, before the duty signallers arrived, transcribing from the night's tray the message she was not supposed to find surprising and did anyway. *Engagement north of Marrow Bridge concluded successfully. Enemy irregulars dispersed. Casualties: eleven wounded, none serious.* She had copied the same formula so many times now that her hand produced it the way a music box produces its tune — faithfully, mechanically, without any reference to anything true.

*None serious.* She thought of the stretchers she'd seen carried past the schoolhouse window two nights ago, and the smell that had followed them down the lane, and the boy on the last stretcher who had been making a sound like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

Ada took out her own pencil — small, silver-topped, a gift from her brother Tom back when gifts between them were still ordinary things — and wrote in the margin of her workbook, in a shorthand only she could read: *eleven "none serious" = 40+ real. Ask F.*

Then she blotted it with her sleeve and put the pencil away, and felt the small steady pleasure she always felt now when the ledger came to mind. Felix Lockridge's ledger. Forty-one names as of Tuesday night. Forty-one people the war office had decided, for reasons of tidiness, had never existed.

Well. Ada had been erased once herself, in a manner of speaking. She knew how it was done, and she knew it could be undone.

She fed the official lie into the wire to headquarters, dot and dash, each false figure tapped out clean as a hymn, and thought: *you have no idea what else these fingers can carry.*

* * *

The question of Elias Voss had been nagging her for three days, the way a loose thread nags — you can leave it alone for an hour, but your thumb keeps finding it.

Felix had raised him first, one evening in the infirmary storeroom, in the low voice he used now for everything that mattered, which was more or less everything.

"Voss," he'd said, turning his teacup in circles without drinking from it. "Elias Voss. Do you know him?"

"Know *of*," Ada said. "Every operator knows of Voss. He's the reason we now require journalists to apply in triplicate." She smiled despite herself. "He was at Kessel's Landing three years ago. Wrote that the punitive expedition had burned the granaries with the harvest still inside, and the war office said he was a drunk and a fabulist, and then six months later their own commission quietly confirmed every word. They ruined him anyway. Took his accreditation, his lodgings, his credit at the hotel. There's a certain thoroughness about them."

"And now?"

"Now he lives—" she lowered her voice even though they were alone among crates of bandages, "—or camped, rather, somewhere on the far shore. Fishing village. Greyhaven, they say, or the hamlet past it. He writes for a little press in the capital that nobody respectable reads. Yet."

Felix had looked at her for a long moment. "Nobody respectable reads it *yet*. You say that like a promise."

"I say it like arithmetic," Ada said. "Lies are expensive to maintain, Felix. Truth is cheap. It just costs you everything up front."

So it fell to her to make contact. Of course it did. The wire was hers; the cipher was hers; the risk was hers to spend, and she had already spent so much of herself on the army's version of events that spending a little on her own seemed almost restful.

There was a procedure for this sort of thing, invented by Ada Quill over several sleepless nights and refined through practice: the request for contact would be buried inside routine traffic. A query about freight rates. A weather advisory for coastal shipping. Something so dull that a signals officer reading over the tape would slide past it like a man walking past a shop window he has no interest in.

She spent Wednesday afternoon composing it. The trick — and she was rather proud of this — was to make the cipher do double duty. On the surface, a standard commercial code group about herring catches. Underneath, in the second layer of the key she and Felix had worked out by candlelight, a plain instruction: *VOSS GREYHAVEN STOP HAVE GOODS FOR SALE AS DISCUSSED STOP REPLY THROUGH USUAL CHANNELS STOP Q.*

*Goods for sale.* Let the interceptors chew on that. Let them imagine contraband tobacco, smuggled brandy, army blankets sold out the back of a wagon. Greed made men lazy; suspicion of greed made them lazier still, because everyone knew what smugglers were and no one imagined smugglers carrying forty-one names.

She sent it Thursday morning, wedged between a requisition confirmation for boot polish and a routine tide table, her fist — the individual rhythm of her tapping, as personal as handwriting — kept deliberately flat and unremarkable. Then she went and stood outside in the frost for ten minutes, breathing and looking at the river, because even a woman fluent in falsehood needed occasionally to stand somewhere honest.

* * *

Friday brought the reply, and the reply was wrong.

Not wrong in content — there was nothing yet in content, only a routine acknowledgment of the freight query, correct in every particular. It was wrong in *texture*. Ada read the tape twice, then a third time with her finger under the groups, and frowned at it the way one frowns at a familiar room where a chair has been moved two inches.

Her own acknowledgment, the one she'd sent Thursday, had come back through the relay with something added. Not much. Three extra spaces in the padding before the final group. Nobody but an operator would ever notice. Nobody but an operator who had spent nine years listening to the army breathe would ever care.

But it meant the message had been *handled*. Taken off the line, examined, re-sent. And whoever handled it had padded the gap clumsily — or, worse, deliberately, leaving a mark the way a gamekeeper leaves a slashed branch across a trail: *passed here.*

Ada sat very still in the booth, hands in her lap, the morning light coming gray through the frosted window, and let the arithmetic finish itself.

Somebody was watching the coastal freight bands. Somebody had pulled Thursday's tape, spread it open, and dug down into the first layer of her cipher — maybe further. Maybe all the way to *goods for sale*, and wondered what goods, whose sale, and why a telegraph operator in a requisitioned river town cared about herring quotas addressed to nobody in particular.

Or maybe — she allowed herself this, briefly, the way one allows oneself a single sweet with dinner — maybe it was nothing. Relays were sloppy. Splices ate padding. Winter frayed wire and tempers alike.

She tested the theory against her own professional pride and found it wanting. The relay operators she knew were sloppy, yes, but sloppiness scattered; it didn't gather itself neatly into three spaces before a final group. That wasn't accident. That was somebody filing a note in a cabinet marked *Quill, A. — pending.*

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty booth, in the brisk tone she used on nervous conscripts handed a telegram from home, "isn't that interesting."

It was not interesting. It was alarming. But her mother had taught her that a woman who says *alarming* aloud gives the alarm furniture and rent, whereas a woman who says *interesting* keeps the upper hand, and Ada had found this nonsense useful often enough to keep paying for it.

* * *

She went to see Felix at noon, under cover of returning a borrowed hot-water pan, which gave her legitimate business in the infirmary and a legitimate reason to be carrying something warm.

The schoolhouse smelled of carbolic and wet wool. Conscripts coughed in rows. Felix was at the far end changing a dressing, sleeves rolled, forearms thin and steady, talking to the boy in a low murmur about — she caught fragments — a dog named Admiral and whether Admiral could be trusted with a whole ham. The boy was laughing, which cost him something, and paying for it gladly.

Ada waited, and watched him work, and felt the familiar tightening in her chest that she refused, on principle, to examine too closely. There was no time in her life currently budgeted for tenderness. Tenderness was a luxury item, like sugar. And yet.

He finished, washed his hands, and came over. "Pan," he said. "Good. I've been lying awake wondering if you'd forgotten my pan."

"You've been lying awake wondering about the herring catch of the northeastern fleet."

His face changed — not dramatically; Felix's face never did anything dramatically, it moved like weather, gradual and total. "Sit down," he said.

They sat on two upturned crates behind the coal store, out of sight of the windows, which had become their unofficial conference room and which Ada privately called the Foreign Office.

"The reply came back handled," she said. "Padded by a stranger. Someone's opened my traffic, Felix, and someone left a calling card doing it. Deliberately."

"Could it be routine?"

"Routine inspection doesn't re-seal. Routine inspection either passes things along or pulls the sender in for questioning. This is neither. This is *filing*." She wrapped her hands around nothing, missing a cigarette she'd given up for economy. "Whoever it is wants to watch where the thread leads before pulling it. Which means we have days, perhaps. Perhaps hours."

"Did Voss answer?"

"Not yet. And now I'm frightened of the answer arriving, and frightened of it not arriving. If he replies through the usual channels, the watcher sees the reply. If he doesn't reply, we've lost our only—"

"Fishing line," Felix said. "You've lost the fishing line, and you're standing on the dock telling me the fish are circling the hook."

Ada laughed, short and surprised. "That's exactly it, yes. Aren't you comforting."

"I'm a nurse," Felix said. "I comfort professionally. But I'll tell you what I actually think, which is less comforting." He rubbed his jaw. "If they're watching you, they may not be watching only you. Bray counts heads. Ashworth collects paper. Somewhere in between there's a signals officer with tidy habits, and tidy men don't pad gaps out of curiosity. They pad gaps out of *instruction*. Which means orders have already been given. We're not being suspected, Ada. We're being *awaited*."

The word landed in the frost between them and stayed there, steaming.

"So we accelerate," Ada said.

"Do we? Half the names are sent. Half aren't. If we rush the rest—"

"If we wait, there will be no rest to send." She heard her own voice go hard and flat, Tom's voice, the Quill stubbornness that had gotten her brother shot at dawn and would presumably get her something similar eventually. "Felix, I have spent years sending this army's version of the truth, and I will tell you the thing I know about versions: the version that exists is the one that wins. Whatever hasn't crossed that river by the time they come for us might as well never have been written. So we send it. Tonight. All of it."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, faintly, through the wall, the boy with the dog named Admiral coughed twice and settled.

"All right," Felix said quietly. "Tonight. God help us, tonight."

"That's the spirit," said Ada. "I'll bring cigarettes. Bring the names."

* * *

She went back to the booth and did the day's honest dishonest work: casualty figures halved, engagements invented, the whole liturgy of the cheap buttons. And while her fist tapped out the army's afternoon prayers, some quieter part of her mind stood apart, as it had learned to do, and took its own inventory.

Because there was one more thing, and she hadn't told Felix, and the reason she hadn't told Felix was that telling him would make it real.

The padding anomaly hadn't appeared on just one message. It had appeared on two. The second instance was this morning, on a tape she had checked purely from habit — and the padding had been followed by something else, something so small she'd nearly missed it: a correction to the duty roster posted outside the signals office. Effective Saturday, the coastal bands — *her* coastal bands, the quiet commercial frequencies where nothing ever happened except weather and herring and, now, one careful woman's entire conscience — would be monitored at all hours by a Lieutenant Harrow, recently attached from headquarters.

Harrow. Ada didn't know him, but she knew the type the way a pianist knows a keyboard: young, exact, ambitious, the sort of man who found in other people's secrets the career that talent had denied him. Ashworth's kind of instrument. A man sent not to listen but to *catch*.

Saturday. Two days. Possibly fewer, if Harrow was keen, and men like Harrow were always keen; keenness was the whole of their equipment.

Ada finished the last dispatch, signed the log with her neat, unremarkable signature, and looked out the frosted window at the river — low and gray and patient, carrying ice toward the sea, minding its own business the way rivers had managed to do for rather longer than empires.

Somewhere down that river, past the checkpoints she couldn't count and the nets she could feel closing, a disgraced journalist sat in a freezing hut with a printing press and nobody respectable reading him. *Yet,* she reminded herself. Arithmetic was on their side. Truth was cheap.

It just cost everything up front, and the bill, she now understood, was coming due Saturday morning.

Ada wound the clock, squared the tapes, and began, very carefully and from memory, drafting in her head the most important weather report of her life.
