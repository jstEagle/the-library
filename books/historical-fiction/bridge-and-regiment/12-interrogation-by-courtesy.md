## 12. Interrogation by Courtesy

Colonel Edmund Ashworth believed in the civilizing power of a good dinner. He had said so at mess nights in three provinces, and no one had ever contradicted him, which he took as confirmation. A man might lie across a desk. A man might lie under a lamp in an interrogation room that smelled of kerosene and fear. But seat him at a properly laid table, pour him a decent claret, set before him something involving butter, and his defenses dissolved like sugar in warm water. He would tell you things he did not know he knew. He would agree with positions he had not held an hour before.

It was not cruelty. Ashworth wished that understood—he wished it written on his headstone if it came to that: *He was not cruel; he was thorough.* The difference mattered. Cruelty was appetite, and appetite was disorder. Thoroughness was love of country wearing its best coat.

So on the fourth evening after Marrow Bridge, he ordered his batman to lay the good cloth in the requisitioned parlor of the customs house—the one with the water-stained portrait of some local worthy, now turned politely to the wall—and sent a corporal down to the converted schoolhouse with a verbal invitation.

"A dinner invitation," the corporal reported back, somewhat bewildered. "The nurse asked whether he should bring his own spoon."

Ashworth smiled at that. He liked this Lockridge. That was precisely the problem.

* * *

He had been studying the man for two weeks the way he studied all useful instruments: patiently, from angles they could not see. Felix Lockridge, late of some provincial hospital whose name Ashworth's adjutant had mislaid, volunteer nurse, uncomplaining, competent. The men trusted him. The wounded asked for him by name, which in Ashworth's experience meant he was either a saint or a smuggler, and saints were rarer.

More to the point: Lockridge kept notes. Ashworth had seen them himself, once, by accident—or rather, he had arranged to see them, which was how he preferred his accidents. Supply forms filled out in a small, precise hand, quantities exact to the half-unit, dates correct even where correctness was inconvenient. Nobody wrote numbers that honest without writing other numbers somewhere else. An honest man's arithmetic always left fingerprints.

And then there was Marrow Bridge. Eleven wounded brought in off the flats, eleven sets of testimony, and Sergeant Bray's report—which Bray, bless his literal heart, had filed through channels without first filing it through discretion—stating that the schoolhouse nurse had spent a full night taking down what the delirious men said. Taking it down *somewhere*. The official transcript contained nothing. The officer responsible had followed orders exactly. Orders said the testimony was not to be recorded, and it was not recorded, and there the matter ended, officially.

Officially was where Ashworth lived. But he had learned long ago that unofficially was where wars kept their teeth, and a man who walked around with his mouth full of teeth that belonged to someone else's war was either a fool or a fuse.

Tonight he intended to find out which. And if the man was merely a fool—well. Fools could be promoted into harmlessness.

* * *

Lockridge arrived at seven o'clock precisely, washed, combed, and wearing his one decent jacket over a shirt that had been mended with visible competence and invisible vanity. He stood in the doorway a moment, taking in the table: white cloth, silver recovered from the Halloway house, two candles burning with the extravagance of occupation.

"You'll forgive the setting," Ashworth said, extending his hand. "We are parasites here, but we needn't be untidy ones."

"Colonel." Lockridge shook the hand. His grip was dry and firm and gave away nothing, which Ashworth noted with professional appreciation. "I wasn't sure I owned anything appropriate for dinner with the army."

"Nobody does. That's rather the point of armies—we dress everyone eventually." Ashworth gestured him to a chair. "Wine?"

"Thank you."

"Red or red? I'm afraid the enemy has monopolized the white."

Lockridge's mouth twitched. Good. A man who laughed at bad jokes was halfway to laughing at worse ones.

The soup was a credit to the cook Ashworth had inherited along with the customs house—a Frenchman, or a man claiming to be French, which came to the same flavor. They spoke of nothing for ten minutes: the weather, the roads, the shocking price of coal. Ashworth let him settle. You could not carve a joint that had just come off the fire; you let it rest, and it yielded.

"I'll confess," Ashworth said at last, refilling the wineglass, "that I've wanted this conversation for some time. I find I'm starved for intelligent company. My officers are brave, my clerks are obedient, and between those two virtues I could go a month without hearing an original sentence."

"That seems an occupational hazard of command."

"It is the disease of command, Mr. Lockridge. Everyone tells you what you wish to hear until you forget what you wish. Do you know what I miss most?" He leaned back. "Being contradicted."

"I could arrange that," Lockridge said, "if you'd like to hear what the men think of the requisitioned beds."

Ah. There it was, quick as a fish rising. Not aggressive—almost playful—but a hook nonetheless, testing whether contradiction was truly welcome or only advertised.

"And what do they think?"

"They think," said Lockridge, calmly eating his fish, "that sleeping in their own blankets would improve morale more than sleeping in Mrs. Halloway's sheets."

"Mrs. Halloway is comfortably resettled."

"She's in her sister's pantry, sir, with four children."

"With four children," Ashworth agreed pleasantly, "which is why she required a whole house, and we require her house less than she does. These are the arithmetics of war, Mr. Lockridge. Unpleasant sums that must nevertheless be done correctly." He smiled. "You strike me as a man who respects arithmetic."

The pause was small. A civilian would have missed it entirely. Lockridge cut his fish, laid down his knife, and said, "I respect accurate records, Colonel. They're different animals."

"Were they ever the same animal?"

"Not in my lifetime."

Ashworth laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and raised his glass. "To accurate records, then. May they rest in peace."

Lockridge touched his glass to the Colonel's, and drank, and Ashworth watched his throat move and thought: *There. He heard me.*

Good. It was better when they heard you early. The alternative—letting a man believe the knives were hidden—was a cruelty Ashworth declined to practice.

* * *

The main course arrived, something done with the last of the town's ducks, and Ashworth dismissed the orderly and poured the port himself. There was a particular intimacy in pouring a man's wine with your own hands. It said: *I do not need servants to destroy you.* It also said: *I am choosing to be kind,* and men were grateful for kindness the way drowning men were grateful for driftwood, without examining its provenance.

"Do you know," Ashworth said, settling back, "what I admire about your profession? Nursing, I mean."

"I can't imagine. Most officers admire it the way they admire rain."

"Then most officers are fools, which we've established." He turned the glass by its stem. "Nursing is the discipline of seeing things clearly and acting anyway. A doctor may console himself with theory. A surgeon with technique. But a nurse stands in the mess itself, hour after hour, and does what can be done. It requires—" he searched for the word, knowing he already had it, "—a tolerance for unpleasant truths. Most men haven't got it. Most men will look anywhere else."

"You flatter us to soften us up, Colonel. I've seen it done to horses."

Ashworth grinned. "Is it working?"

"No."

"Honest. Good. Then let me be honest in return." He set down the glass. "You keep private notes, Mr. Lockridge."

The candle flames did not move. The clock on the mantel ticked twice. Ashworth counted both ticks the way he counted everything, and watched Lockridge finish chewing, swallow, dab his mouth with the napkin, and fold his hands.

"Yes," Lockridge said.

Not *what notes*. Not *who told you*. Just yes. Ashworth felt the rare pleasure of a hypothesis confirmed by a braver answer than expected.

"Supply tallies," he said. "Rations, dressings, laudanum. The ordinary bookkeeping of mercy."

"Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" Ashworth repeated, tasting the word. "You see, this is where our friendship is tested, because mostly is not a word I can carry upstairs. Mostly is the sort of word that grows." He rose, crossed to the sideboard, and came back with a folder—not thick, but fat enough—and laid it beside Lockridge's plate as though it were a condiment. "May I?"

Lockridge nodded once.

Ashworth opened the folder and read, unhurried, letting each item land separately. "Sergeant Bray reports anomalous record-keeping at the schoolhouse infirmary. Corporal Dunn, convalescent, mentions in a letter to his mother—intercepted, purely routine—that Nurse Lockridge writes in a black ledger every night, 'even the names of the dead ones, especially them.' Signals section flags telegraph traffic from this station running at nearly double the pre-occupation volume, much of it weather and freight, weather and freight, weather and freight, in a winter too cold to freight anything." He closed the folder. "None of this is evidence. Evidence requires a thing. Notes require paper, and paper can be found. I have not looked for yours, Mr. Lockridge, because I am hoping very much that I will not have to."

"And if you found it," Lockridge said quietly, "what would it contain that the army hasn't already decided about itself?"

"There it is," Ashworth said, almost with relief. "There's the sentence I invited you to dinner to hear." He sat down again and leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment let the courtesy drop from his voice the way a man loosens his collar at home. "Let me tell you about myself, since you've earned it. I have served the Empire for thirty-one years. I have burned villages—two, and I remember the names of both. I have signed orders I would not read aloud to my mother. And I will tell you what I learned at the cost of those years: the Empire does not die from what it does. Empires do terrible things for centuries and stand. The Empire dies from what it *admits*. One honest ledger, published at the wrong moment, in the wrong mood, and a hundred years of careful work comes apart in a season. Not because the truth is powerful—it isn't, it's weak, it's the weakest thing there is, it cannot hold a hilltop for a single afternoon—but because belief is powerful, and truth in the wrong hands kills belief." He straightened. "I am not protecting crimes, Mr. Lockridge. I am protecting the structure that will, in time, correct them. Slowly. Badly. But *in time*, which is the only direction history moves for institutions."

"That's a beautiful speech," Lockridge said. "You should send it to the families at Marrow Bridge."

The silence that followed had weight and temperature. Ashworth let it sit, because he deserved it, and because watching a man pay for his courage in real time told him more than any dossier.

"I don't expect you to agree," he said finally, mildly. "I expect you to be intelligent. Those are different requirements." He reached into his breast pocket and drew out a single folded sheet and laid it beside the folder. "Captaincy. Medical staff, attached to headquarters. A bed that doesn't leak, a salary with a comma in it, and the authority to run your infirmaries properly instead of fighting quartermasters for lint. Your record supports it—I have made sure of that. All it requires is what any captain provides." He tapped the paper once. "Loyalty of the ordinary kind. The kind that knows which reports matter and which rumors don't. Sign nothing tonight. Think. You have until the New Year."

Lockridge looked at the commission for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentle, which Ashworth had not anticipated and which unsettled him more than defiance would have.

"Colonel, may I ask you something sincere?"

"You may."

"When you were a young subaltern, was there a ledger you didn't sign? Someone offered you this same chair, the same wine, the same arithmetic—and you signed. Is that where the two villages come from?"

Ashworth held his gaze. Thirty-one years of holding his gaze held now.

"The ducks were excellent," he said. "Give my compliments to nobody; the cook and I have an arrangement."

Lockridge rose, buttoned his jacket, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth, sir—I don't think you're protecting the Empire. I think the Empire stopped needing protection years ago, and started needing witnesses, and you volunteered for the wrong job."

He left. He actually bowed slightly as he went, the courteous wretch.

* * *

Alone, Ashworth sat for a while with the untouched commission and the two candles, and permitted himself the luxury of doubt, briefly, the way other men permitted themselves brandy.

The boy was guilty. Obviously, certainly guilty—not perhaps of treason, but of the seed of it, which is the belief that one's private conscience outranks one's public oath. Guilt of that kind could not be arrested; it had to be *outgrown*, starved, made ridiculous. Promotion would have helped with that. Kindness would have helped. He had offered both, generously, and been refused with such politeness that refusal felt like a slap delivered in white gloves.

Very well. Courtesy had failed. It usually did, in the end, which was why he kept it for first meetings rather than last ones.

He rang for the adjutant, who appeared with the speed of a man who had been listening at doors out of pure professional habit.

"Two memoranda," Ashworth said. "First: the infirmary's typhus protocols are inadequate. Have the medical officer certify it. Stores that can't be disinfected will be burned—crates, bedding, canvas, everything, effective tomorrow morning." The adjutant wrote. "Second: transfer Operator Quill's duties to signals section at headquarters. Effective tonight. She's been at that key too long; fatigue produces errors in traffic, and I want the wire clean going into the New Year."

The adjutant hesitated. "Both, sir?"

"Both," said Ashworth, and picked up his fork, and finished the duck alone, thoroughly, and without pleasure.
