## 13. Burn Notice

The order arrived the way most disasters did in Wexley's Ford that winter: on paper, stamped twice, and carried by a boy too young to shave.

Felix was in the schoolhouse infirmary changing a dressing on Private Hollis's knee — an unglamorous wound, a bayonet graze picked up falling off a wagon while drunk, which Hollis insisted on describing to anyone who would listen as "action at the ford" — when Corporal Pell poked his head through the door with a sheet of foolscap and an expression of a man delivering news he hadn't quite read yet.

"Orders, sir. From headquarters. For the medical officer."

"I'm not the medical officer," Felix said. "I'm the only person here who can tell a fever from a chill."

"That's what it says. More or less." Pell handed it over.

Felix wiped his hands, took the paper, and read it standing very still, which was how he had learned to read bad things. *By direction of Colonel E. Ashworth, commanding: owing to confirmed cases of typhus within the occupied township, all stores held at the infirmary — dressings, bedding, linens, crates, and miscellaneous materiel — are to be burned by noon tomorrow as a sanitary precaution. Replacement stores will follow from the divisional depot.*

"Materiel," Felix repeated. "Miscellaneous materiel."

"That's you told," said Hollis cheerfully from his cot. "You're miscellaneous, sir."

"Hush your leg and your mouth together, Private." Felix folded the order along its crease, then again, then again, until it was a small hard square he could have used to drive a nail. Typhus. There had been two cases in November, both recovered, neither typhus. It was not a precaution. It was an eraser, drawn neatly across the last room in town where anything honest might be lying around.

The crate. The crate of surgical dressings where he had hidden his ledger three weeks ago, back when hiding had seemed like cleverness instead of what it actually was — a way of signing his own confession and leaving it in plain view.

"Corporal Pell," Felix said, keeping his voice at the pleasant register he'd practiced in front of mirrors without meaning to, "when is this to happen?"

"Noon tomorrow, sir. Sergeant Bray's detail handles the burning. He's coming round this evening to make an inventory first." Pell hesitated. "He counts everything, that one. Counts twice."

"Yes," Felix said. "I've noticed."

* * *

The trouble with having done the clever thing three weeks ago was that cleverness had a shelf life, like milk. Felix spent the afternoon walking past the storeroom in a manner he hoped looked casual and suspected looked like a man orbiting a gallows. The crate sat on its shelf between the chloroform tins and a barrel of salt pork, stencilled SURGICAL DRESSINGS, GRADE II, exactly as anonymous as he had intended it to be. His ledger lay inside, wrapped in a dressing gown that had belonged to the schoolmaster, beneath forty bandages rolled by Mrs. Halloway before the army put her out of her own kitchen.

Two hundred and thirty-one names. Every one of them written in his own hand, dated, sourced, witnessed where witness was possible. Eleven from Marrow Bridge. The drowned porter whose body had come downriver tangled in fence posts. The farm boys conscripted off the north road and dead of measles inside a fortnight because the army's surgeon had been reassigned to staff work. Mrs. Halloway, who was not dead but erased, which in the official arithmetic amounted to the same thing.

If the ledger burned, half of those names existed nowhere on earth except Ada Quill's transmitted cipher — and Ada had told him, the last time they'd spoken over the supply forms, that she had gotten through only the letters A through M of his alphabetical arrangement before the wire traffic changed pattern and she'd had to stop.

Half. He had let himself think of the transmitted half as the safe half, the seed safely banked, and the physical ledger as merely the working copy. Now the working copy was scheduled for immolation at noon, and the safe half ended somewhere around the letter M, which meant that every man named Niles, Novak, Ostrander, Pell — no, not Pell, Pell was alive and annoying him — every soul after M lived now only in Felix's memory and in a stretcher canvas already sewn and gone across the river.

His memory. Good God. He was a man who could not remember where he'd left his spectacles, and he had made himself the sole archive of two hundred lives.

* * *

Sergeant Bray arrived at six o'clock precisely, carrying a lantern, a notebook, and the unhurried air of a man who had never once been surprised by anything and regarded surprise as a personal failing in others.

"Evening, Mr. Lockridge."

"Sergeant."

"Inventory first, burn at noon. Army likes its fires documented." Bray set the lantern on the schoolmaster's desk, which still bore, scratched into the wood, some child's attempt at a ship. He opened his notebook, licked his thumb, and began.

Felix stood by and watched the counting with his heart performing small unscheduled maneuvers behind his ribs. Bray counted the way other men prayed — thoroughly, and with total absorption. Tins of chloroform: fourteen. Bandages, rolled: he counted them in fives without touching them, which was somehow worse than touching. Salt pork: one barrel, weight estimated. And then he came to the crate.

"Surgical dressings, Grade Two," Bray read off the stencil. He lifted the lid.

Forty bandages. A dressing gown, gray, worn at the elbows. Bray held the gown up by its shoulders, considering it as though it might have opinions worth interrogating.

"Schoolmaster's," Felix said. His voice came out level, which astonished him. "Confiscated linen. I use it for packing wounds."

Bray turned the gown over once, slowly. Under the lantern light his face was a country with no visible borders — nothing readable there, neither suspicion nor its absence. Felix understood, in the way one understands a thing while looking directly away from it, that Bray had probably already noticed the telegraph office's traffic had doubled since the occupation; that a man who counted bandages in fives without touching them was a man who counted anomalies for a living; and that he was, at this moment, being weighed on scales whose markings only the sergeant could see.

"Linen's linen," Bray said at last, and wrote something down. "Crate burns with the rest?"

"The order says all materiel."

"Materiel." Bray closed the lid with a softness that frightened Felix more than any slam could have. "Funny word, materiel. Covers a lot of sins." He gathered his lantern and notebook. "Noon tomorrow, Mr. Lockridge. You'll want to remove anything personal beforehand. Papers, notes. Men in my line find papers interesting, and I'd hate for you to be interesting."

Was it a warning? Felix turned it over all evening, sitting by the stove in his narrow room above the infirmary, turning it like the folded order, smaller and harder each time. Either Bray was a decent man signaling him across a gap, or Bray was a patient man setting bait. With Bray, the two possibilities were not even strangers to one another. They might have been brothers.

Either way, the ledger could not be in that crate at noon.

* * *

The problem was removing it. Stores were to remain untouched until the burning — Pell mentioned, helpfully, that the detail would post a guard from dawn specifically so nobody "helped themselves." Of course they would. Ashworth ran a clean war; clean wars counted their spoils before they torched them. Felix lay awake doing arithmetic: the guard posted at dawn, the fire at noon, and himself, in between, expected to be nowhere near the storeroom except perhaps under escort.

Which left tonight. Which left the window above the woodshed, a drop of eleven feet onto frozen ground, and a moon like an informer's lantern.

He went down at half past one, in stockinged feet, coat over nightshirt, feeling simultaneously ridiculous and doomed, which he was learning were nearly the same sensation. The lock on the storeroom was a schoolroom lock, installed circa the reign of some forgettable king, and it surrendered to a butter knife in nine seconds. Inside, the lantern-less dark smelled of carbolic and pork. He found the crate by memory, lifted the lid, unwrapped the dressing gown, and took out the ledger — his ledger, his two hundred and thirty-one names, warm somehow, or he imagined it warm, the way a stone is warm if you've been carrying it long enough.

And then he did the thing he should have done three weeks ago and hated himself with a clean, cold efficiency while he did it. He did not hide it again. Hiding had been tried; hiding was milk; hiding soured. Instead he took the ledger back to his room and, by the light of a candle shielded with his hat, began to tear it apart.

Not destroy — disperse. Pages folded into the lining of his greatcoat, flat against his back where a man carries nothing suspicious. Names copied onto the backs of prescription slips, in a hand deliberately altered to look like prescribing. Eleven Marrow Bridge names written twice and sewn — clumsily, painfully, pricking his thumb a dozen times — into the hem of a spare shirt. By four in the morning the ledger was no longer a book. It was a hundred scraps wearing disguises, distributed through his belongings like smugglers through a crowd.

It occurred to him, folding the final page, that this was what his life had become: a man dismantling truth into pieces small enough to swallow. He thought of Ada saying, in her dry way, *You know what we are now? We're stationery, Felix. Stationery that walks.* He had laughed then. He did not laugh now. He blew out the candle and slept two hours with the torn cover of the ledger under his pillow like a brick, dreaming of fires he failed to reach in time.

* * *

At half past ten the next morning, Ada came to the infirmary with a requisition chit for lint — the army consumed lint the way a stove consumes coal — and found Felix wrapping bandages with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"You look terrible," she said, warmly.

"Thank you. I've been sewing."

"At your age. Whatever will the matrons say." She took up a pair of scissors and began cutting lint beside him, their hands moving in the easy division of labor they'd fallen into without ever discussing it. After a moment, quietly, eyes on her work: "Well?"

"It's out of the crate. It's everywhere now. My coat, my shirts, my boots. If they search me thoroughly I'm guilty in installments."

"If they search you thoroughly we're both guilty. That was always true." She cut a strip of lint, precise as a metronome. "What matters is the wire half. Listen to me. What I sent — A through M — it's in Voss's hands by now, or nearly. That half is safe whether we live or die. You built the alphabet, Felix. I finished half of it."

"Half," Felix said. The word came out of him with more weight than he intended, and Ada's scissors stopped.

"How many after M?"

"Ninety-eight. Ninety-eight names that exist in two places now. In my head, and in a stretcher canvas crossing the river with an escort convoy that may or may not have reached Voss." He set down his bandage. "Ada. If the canvas was searched—"

"It wasn't. Elias would have signaled."

"And if his signal was intercepted? Signals get intercepted. Yours do. It's practically your profession."

Ada was quiet. Outside, someone was leading horses across the frozen yard, hooves ringing on the stones like a clock that had given up telling time and settled for marking it. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its dryness entirely.

"Felix. I need to say the ugly arithmetic, because you won't." She turned to face him. "Ninety-eight names. Half our ledger. And the army is about to burn the room where the evidence of everything else lived, and call it medicine. If you're taken, those ninety-eight names die with you — not the people, they're already dead, the *names*, the proof, the only thing left of them the world gets to keep. Do you understand what you're carrying? You're not a courier anymore. You're the last copy of a country that's being deleted."

"Thank you," Felix said. "I was sleeping so well before."

"I'm serious."

"So am I. Terribly seriously, constantly. It's my defining feature." But the joke came out cracked down the middle, and Ada heard it. He looked at his hands — a nurse's hands, he had always thought, hands that tended rather than testified — and found they didn't feel like his anymore. They felt like instruments someone else had repurposed.

"Here's what keeps circling me," he said. "Three weeks ago I hid the ledger and called it caution. If I'd sent it all then — if I'd trusted you with all of it — we wouldn't be sitting here dividing two hundred and thirty-one souls into the saved half and the carried half. I was careful, and carelessness in slow motion is all carefulness turns out to be."

Ada reached over and took one of those unfamiliar hands, briefly, firmly, the way one takes a pulse.

"Then we finish the job," she said. "A through M went by wire. The rest goes by boot leather, or boat, or whatever the river gives us. And Felix — it goes soon. Ashworth doesn't burn storerooms and stop at storerooms. First the records, then the stores, then the witnesses. We're on the same list. We're just filed later."

* * *

At noon they burned the stores.

They did it in the yard behind the infirmary, and it drew half the town, the way fires do, everyone standing in the snow with the particular silence of people watching their own possessions go up in someone else's smoke. Mrs. Halloway stood at the front with her arms folded, watching army blankets — blankets that had been hers, in a kitchen that had been hers — curl and blacken, her face doing nothing at all, which was louder than weeping. Bray's detail worked methodically, feeding the flames, and Bray himself stood apart with his notebook, checking items against inventory as they burned, ticking off the world.

Felix watched the crate go on the pyre — SURGICAL DRESSINGS, GRADE II, lid open and empty now, officially innocent — and felt nothing so simple as grief. The empty box burned fine. The full one had walked out of the fire's way at one in the morning, dressed in disguise, and was currently scattered through a tired man's clothing like confetti from a funeral.

Ashworth had not come to watch, of course. Colonels signed; sergeants burned. But Felix imagined him anyway, in his warmed office, drafting the next report — *stores destroyed, sanitation maintained, zero irregularities* — and understood with sudden, total clarity what he was looking at. Not a precaution. A rehearsal. Today a crate. Tomorrow the parish registers' leftovers, the school rolls' remnants, the testimony of eleven men about a bridge. Then the men themselves, transferred, dispersed, dead of natural causes. Then the town's memory, which was old women and a telegraph girl and a nurse with paper sewn into his coat hem.

*Zero civilian casualties.* The phrase had seemed monstrous when he first read it in a captured dispatch. Now it seemed simply accurate — a description of the intention, not a denial of the fact.

Pell appeared at his elbow, warming his hands at arm's length from the blaze. "Shame about the stores," he said. "Good bandages. I rolled some of those myself."

"They'll issue replacements," Felix said.

"Oh, aye. Replacements." Pell spat thoughtfully into the snow. "Begging pardon, sir, but I've been in this army long enough to know what replacements look like. Usually arrive after the war. Or the inquiry." He said the word *inquiry* the way one sets a match to fuse and steps back, and wandered off whistling, leaving Felix staring after him and adding, reluctantly, another name to the growing private census of men in gray uniforms who might not be enemies.

The fire burned down through the afternoon. By dusk it was a red eye sinking into ash, and the snow around it was trampled gray, and Sergeant Bray closed his notebook with a snap that carried across the yard, and looked — once, briefly, unreadably — at Felix Lockridge, standing in the crowd with his pockets full of forbidden history.

That night, a runner brought a note to the infirmary. Not stamped twice this time. Not official at all. Ada's hand, in pencil, in the terse telegraphist's shorthand they'd improvised:

*CONVOY ARRIVED. CANVAS SAFE. VOSS HAS ALL NAMES THRU R. ONLY S-Z REMAIN WITH YOU. COME TO BOATHOUSE TOMORROW NIGHT. WE FINISH IT.*

Felix read it twice, fed it to the stove, and watched it curl. Through R. Only S through Z remained — twenty-six names, the tail of the alphabet, the last of the dead — existing nowhere on earth but in his memory and his coat lining.

Tomorrow night, the boathouse. Tomorrow night, they finished it.

He sat down to mend a shirt hem that needed no mending, so that his hands would have something honest to do, and tried not to notice that in the morning, when he checked the yard, Corporal Dunn was on sentry duty by the woodshed — and that Dunn, who owed Felix his life, watched him back a moment too long.
