## 14. The Second Checkpoint

Ada Quill had always believed that fear was a thing with a shape, and that if you could name its shape you could hold it at arm's length. Fear of the dark was a closet door. Fear of drowning was the green lip of water above your head. But fear of carrying forty-one names across a frozen river in a basket of telegraph spools turned out to have no shape at all. It was simply everywhere, like cold.

She had spent two evenings winding the punch-tapes herself, working by candlelight with the shutters drawn, feeding strips of oiled paper through a borrowed perforator one careful stroke at a time. To any inspector they were repair spools—blank tape wound tight on wooden bobbins, the sort every signal section burned through by the yard and hoarded like hens' eggs. Only the tapes were not blank. Under the top layer of genuine blanks she had wound the encoded names: dates, initials, coordinates of the graves behind Marrow Bridge, each name reduced to holes in paper no wider than a fingernail. A message that looked like weather until you had the key, and the key existed only in her head, where Sergeant Bray's clever fingers could never dig it out.

"You'll want to look bored," Felix had told her the night before he went into the marsh, holding the door as though the wind might carry off his courage with it. He was pale and unshaven and had the look of a man who had stopped sleeping out of respect for other people's nerves. "Bored is best. Soldiers never search a bored woman thoroughly. They search frightened women and pretty ones."

"And which am I to be?"

"Neither," Felix said. "You're to be tedious. Tediousness is armor, Ada. I've worn it for years."

She smiled for him then, because he needed her to, and because it cost nothing, and because she suspected she would not be able to smile for some days afterward.

* * *

Sergeant William Bray stood at the ferry landing with his hands clasped behind his back and his breath rising in slow white columns, watching the ice floes come down the river like a procession of broken plates. He had been counting things since dawn: eleven floes of dangerous size, four carts on the north road, two crows, one ferryman who chewed tobacco too fast to be an honest man.

Bray kept tallies the way other men kept God. It was not ambition, whatever Lieutenant Purcell believed when Bray's anomaly reports came back annotated in red ink with the word *noted* — which Bray had learned, after twenty years of service, meant *ignored*. It was simply that the world told the truth in numbers even when it lied in words, and Bray trusted numbers the way a drowning man trusts timber.

And the numbers had been wrong for weeks now. Telegraph traffic from this miserable river town had doubled, then nearly tripled, and the surplus traffic was all freight manifests and weather reports, which was precisely what a clever person would use if a clever person wished to move something invisible through a busy wire. Weather reports. He had transcribed ten thousand of them in his career and he had yet to meet anyone who cared about the weather more than once a day, briefly, while looking at the sky.

Then there was the matter of the medical convoy that had crossed at the bridge checkpoint three days early for its scheduled escort, carrying one stretcher more than its manifest allowed, attended by an infirmary nurse named Lockridge whose supply forms were written in handwriting so beautiful it ought to be illegal. And now this: a woman from the signals section requesting passage on the morning ferry, alone, carrying a hamper of repair spools under a requisition order signed with a staff officer's number that Bray did not recognize — because he had memorized every staff officer's number in this district, the way a mother memorizes her children's coughs.

"Miss Quill," he said, as she came down the icy planking with her hamper on her hip. "Morning crossing isn't until nine."

"The wire said eight-thirty," Ada said pleasantly. "Repair priority. There's a fault line past the mill and the section wants spools up before the frost gets into the junction boxes."

"The wire said eight-thirty," Bray agreed. "I read the wire. I read everything on the wire." He let that sit a moment, the way he'd let a stone sit on a scale. "That's a deal of spools for one fault line."

"Fault lines eat spools," Ada said. "Like officers eat breakfast."

One of the two soldiers flanking the landing made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in its crib. Bray did not turn around. He liked her answer. That was exactly why he didn't trust it.

"Set the hamper down, Miss Quill."

* * *

Ada set the hamper down on the frost-silvered boards and thought, absurdly, of her brother Tom teaching her to skip stones on this same river before the army taught him to run and then taught him to die for it. Nine minutes, his trial had lasted. She had counted the seconds of her own life ever since, dividing them into before and after, and she had discovered that terror compressed time beautifully: she could feel each heartbeat individually now, like coins dropped one at a time into a tin.

Bray lifted the lid of the hamper. His hands were large, cracked at the knuckles, unhurried. He took out a bobbin and held it to the gray light, turning it slowly.

"Repair spools," he said.

"Remarkable deduction, Sergeant."

"Blank tape?"

"Wound blank, yes." Her voice stayed level. Somewhere beneath her ribs an animal was screaming and throwing itself against a locked door; she had put it there years ago, when she signed her first falsified casualty return, and she knew how to keep it quiet. "The perforator chews the ends, so we strip the first arm's length and wind fresh over it. If you want to test one, the reading head's in the ferry office, but I'd have to witness it — section property, there's a form—"

"There's always a form," Bray murmured. He unwound a foot of tape from the bobbin, held it up, squinted along its length. Blank. He wound it back, chose another bobbin. Unwound. Blank. His eyes never left her face while his hands worked, which was the trick of it, and she met his gaze with the flat patience of a woman who had better things to do, because Felix was right — tedium was armor, and she intended to be the most boring creature ever to stand on a ferry landing.

Third bobbin. Fourth. The animal under her ribs threw itself harder.

"You know what I do, Miss Quill?" Bray said, still winding. "I count heads. Convoys in, convoys out. Spools ordered, spools used. Coal delivered against coal burned. It's dull work." He set the fourth bobbin down with exaggerated care, as though it were an egg he suspected of being rotten. "Dull work suits me. Because here's what dull work shows: your section has sent double traffic since the occupation, and near all of it is freight and weather, and nobody in this army cares about the weather. So either somebody up the line is very interested in barometric pressure—" he straightened, and for the first time he smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, it was the smile of a man laying his last card face-up "—or those spools aren't as blank as they look, and I'm holding the whole war in my hands."

The wind came off the river and found the gaps in her collar. Ada heard herself say, calmly, "May I see the requisition order you're objecting to?"

"I'm not objecting to an order. I'm objecting to you."

"Then let's resolve it properly." She drew the folded papers from inside her coat — the forged order, the one Felix had produced at a cost she only partly knew, written in a hand he had practiced until it fooled a clerk who had known the real signatory for a decade — and she held it out, and she watched this suspicious, patient, dangerous man take it, and she thought: *Tom, this is for you, this is for nine minutes of justice, don't let my hands shake.*

* * *

Bray read the order aloud, because reading aloud was how he caught lies. Words behaved differently in the mouth. On paper a forgery could sit quietly and pass; spoken in the cold air, in your own voice, its seams showed.

"'By direction of the Chief Signals Officer, Third Section,'" he read. "'Operator A. Quill is authorized transport of signal stores across the river, priority one, effective immediately. Signed'—" he paused "—'Deputy Director Halloran, staff number 4-1-1-9.'"

"That's the order," Ada said.

"Mm." He read it again, silently, lips moving. The signature was good. The stamp was very good — he tilted it toward the light and the embossing caught the sun, and he thought, not for the first time, that whoever had made this stamp had done it with love. "Halloran," he said. "There's no Deputy Director Halloran."

"There will be by Thursday," Ada said. "New appointment. Announced in the dispatches of the eleventh. You'd know that if you read the wire."

He did know it. That was the trouble. He had read it, filed it, and marked it *unverified*, because the announcement had come through at an odd hour with an odd routing, one more small wrongness in a season of them. He looked at the woman standing on his landing with the wind pulling at her coat and the patience of a saint on her face, and he understood, with the certainty he usually reserved for arithmetic, that she was lying to him, that the hamper at her feet contained something worth hanging for, and that she would stand there all day, courteous and tedious, until he either searched every spool down to the last inch of tape or let her onto the ferry.

He could search them. He thought about searching them. Forty-one bobbins — he'd counted them while her back was turned, of course he'd counted them — and each one wound with perhaps two hundred yards of tape, and the reading head in the ferry office was a temperamental antique that ate tape as readily as it read it, and if he ran forty-one spools through it he would be at this landing until spring, and if the order was genuine — *if* — then by evening he would be explaining to a genuine deputy director why he had delayed priority-one signal stores during a fault-line repair in freezing weather, and Lieutenant Purcell's red ink would be the least of his troubles.

And there was the other thing, the thing he had been carrying for two weeks like a stone in his boot. The intercepted traffic he'd copied out in his private book. The casualty figures that did not agree with the casualty figures. The engagement at Marrow Bridge that appeared in the official record as a skirmish and appeared in the wire as nothing at all, as silence, as a hole shaped like a village. Bray had gone into the army believing that the paperwork and the truth were the same document. He was fifty-one years old, and he had learned lately that they were different documents, and that his whole trade had been keeping the books for men who wrote fiction.

"Miss Quill," he said slowly, "do you know what happens to me if you're carrying what I think you're carrying?"

"No," Ada said. "But I know what happened to my brother, and it took nine minutes, and I've decided I can survive anything longer than that."

For a moment neither of them moved. Downriver, an ice floe the size of a barn struck the pilings with a sound like distant artillery, and both of them glanced at it, and in that shared glance something passed between them that neither would ever quite name — the recognition of two people doing sums the rest of the world had refused to check.

Bray folded the order along its existing creases, handed it back, and stepped aside from the gangplank.

"Ferry leaves at nine," he said. "Not eight-thirty. Whatever the wire says." Then, as she gathered the hamper's handles, he added, quietly, without looking at her: "Tell whoever winds your spools that the third bobbin was wound too loose. Left-handed tension. A signals man taught me that trick years ago, and I've never once needed it until today."

Ada went very still. Then she picked up the hamper, climbed the gangplank, and did not look back, because looking back was for people who could afford it.

* * *

Bray watched the ferry pull out into the current, its smoke dragging flat against the low sky, and waited until it reached midstream. Then he opened his tally book, wet the pencil stub, and wrote, in his small exact hand:

*0857. One operator, one hamper, forty-one spools. Order genuine or forged — undetermined. Let pass.*

He hesitated, pencil hovering. Below it he wrote:

*If forged, I am now an accessory. Note: the river is frozen in six places between here and the fishing villages. Note: I know six places.*

He closed the book and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept everything that mattered, and he turned back to counting the ice.

* * *

On the far bank, Ada stepped off into snow up to her shins and walked the field path toward the tree line, the hamper light against her hip now, forty-one names lighter than air. Halfway across the field she permitted herself one breath that shook, just one, spent and done.

Then she saw, at the edge of the trees by the boathouse, a figure in a greatcoat raising a hand to her — Corporal Dunn, whom Felix had pulled back from death with his own hands, waving her in, saying he had word of Felix, saying *come quick, miss, he's waiting for you.*

And because kindness had not yet taught her suspicion, Ada turned toward the boathouse.
