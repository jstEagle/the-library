## 15. Betrayal at the Boathouse

The winter had settled over the town like a creditor who intended to stay. Ice sheathed the eaves of the infirmary in long yellow fangs, and the marsh beyond the boathouse road had gone stiff and pale, a drowned field turned to porcelain. Felix Lockridge walked it every morning now, ostensibly to check on the two convalescent cases billeted in the ferryman's cottage, actually because walking kept him from counting the hours until Ada's next shift ended.

He was learning the particular arithmetic of conspiracy. Every secret had a cost. Every cost had to be carried by someone, and the someone was never himself for long.

Corporal Thomas Dunn was one of those costs.

Felix had saved him in October — not dramatically, nothing worth a medal. A shell splinter had opened Dunn's side like an envelope, and Felix had spent four hours with forceps and catgut and a candle guttering down to its brass dish while the boy — he was hardly more, nineteen perhaps, with a cowlick that stood up no matter how the army cropped his hair — had gripped the table edge and sworn cheerfully at God. When it was done, Dunn had caught Felix's sleeve with a blood-slick hand and said, "You're the first man in this army who's touched me without wanting something out of me."

Felix had written *Dunn, T., corporal, mending* in the ledger that night, and felt briefly, foolishly good about the war.

Now Dunn stood at the end of the boathouse slip in his greatcoat and a wool cap pulled low, and there was something in the way he shifted his weight from foot to foot that made Felix's stomach tighten before a single word was spoken. He had seen men stand like that before. It was the stance of a man carrying news that does not belong to him, the way you carry a bucket of water across a slick floor — carefully, miserably, knowing you'll spill some of yourself along the way.

"Corporal." Felix nodded, keeping his voice easy. "You're meant to be indoors. The cold's no friend to that wound."

"No, sir." Dunn did not smile. Dunn always smiled. That was the second wrong thing.

Behind them, the boathouse creaked. The army had requisitioned it as a store for pontoon timbers, but half of it still smelled of tar and fish, and the ferry landing stretched out beside it, iced ropes hanging slack. Ada would cross this landing twice a day. Ada was crossing it now, in fact, coming up the far path from the telegraph office with her collar turned up and a canvas satchel — repair spools, three of them, each holding more names than any spool of copper had ever held before — swinging at her hip.

She was fifty yards away. Forty-five. Felix did the arithmetic again, faster this time.

"I've put in for my discharge papers," Dunn said. "Medical."

"You've earned them."

"They won't sign them." He swallowed. His throat worked like a boy swallowing medicine. "They say I'm fit for garrison duty. My mother's in Hallowmere, sir. You know what happened to Hallowmere. The colonel's moved everyone out of the river villages ahead of the spring offensive, and her papers—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, the way men do when they are about to confess something they have already done rather than something they intend to do. "Lieutenant Sorrel says if I tell him what I know, my mother gets on the first evacuation barge. A warm cabin. Priority listing."

The ice on the river sang — a long groaning note, like a bowed instrument, the sound it made when the current beneath shifted.

"What do you know, Tom?" Felix asked gently. Because gentleness was all that was left in his kit, and because he already knew the answer, and wanted — absurdly, uselessly — to give the boy one more minute of not having said it.

Dunn looked at the ground. "I help carry the dispatch boxes from the office to headquarters. I see the traffic logs. Three nights running there's been double the weather traffic, and freight manifests going out at hours when no freight moves, and I saw the cipher key change without a signal order authorizing it, which only happens when—" He stopped. "And I've seen the operator. Miss Quill. And I've seen you standing watch outside the office window at midnight like a man waiting for a tram that doesn't run at midnight."

Thirty yards. Ada's boots crunched the frozen mud of the landing. She was humming, very quietly, something tuneless and content — Felix knew that hum; it meant a transmission had gone clean, no garbles, the whole ledger's second half flowing down the wire toward Voss like water finding the sea.

"Dunn," Felix said. "Look at me."

The boy looked at him. His eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself.

"They have my mother," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'd sooner cut my own— I know what you did for me, I know it, and I'll burn for it, but she's seventy-one years old and she can't survive another winter in a village the army means to empty, and Sorrel showed me the papers all filled out except for one signature, and that signature is mine to earn, and God forgive me, sir, God forgive me—"

Two soldiers stepped out from behind the pontoon stacks.

They had been there the whole time. Of course they had. Felix understood it all in one cold flash — Sorrel had not needed Dunn's information so much as his testimony, a witness with a clean record and a grateful heart, the sort of witness who played well at a tribunal. The boy had been traded too. Everyone in this war was both currency and buyer.

"Miss Quill," Lieutenant Sorrel said pleasantly, stepping onto the landing, his breath streaming. "Would you open that satchel for me?"

Twenty yards. Fifteen.

Ada stopped. Felix watched her take in the scene the way she took in a jammed relay — fast, total, unsentimental. Her eyes went to Felix first. Not accusingly. That was somehow worse. She looked at him the way you look at a door that has closed, noting only that it has closed.

"Gentlemen," she said, and her voice was steady as a telegraph key. "If you're interested in repair spools, I'm afraid your war is in worse trouble than you think. Half the line between here and the ferry is held together with string and prayer."

"Open it, please."

She set the satchel down on the ice-glazed boards. And here — Felix would replay this moment for the rest of his life, in exile, in lamplight, in dreams — here she did something extraordinary. She opened the satchel slowly, complaining about the cold, about the army, about the state of army spools generally, in a tone of such genuine grievance that the soldiers leaned in slightly, disarmed by the sheer domesticity of it, and her eyes flicked up once to Felix and cut left. Marsh. Left. Now.

Then she pulled a spool free and let it drop.

It hit the frozen boards and unspooled, clattering, thirty feet of punch-tape skittering across the ice like a startled ribbon, and every man on that landing watched it go, because a rolling thing commands the eye, and Ada Quill understood as well as anyone alive that the best hiding place in the world is a distraction.

Felix ran.

Not toward the road — the road had soldiers on it, and roads remember footsteps. He went left, off the landing's edge, down the bank where the reeds stood dead and rattling, and the crust of ice took his weight for exactly three strides before it didn't, and then he was into the marsh proper, shin-deep, then knee-deep in freezing black water that soaked him to the groin and stole the breath from his lungs. Behind him: shouting. Sorrel's voice cracking into its true register, all pleasantness gone. The slap of boots on boards.

The marsh was a maze Felix had walked a dozen mornings, and every morning he had been grateful for its tedium; now he blessed it. There was no path, only remembered ground — a fallen willow here, a hummock of peat there, the drowned fence posts of a farm nobody admitted had existed. He ran bent double through the reeds, lungs burning, hands so cold they felt like borrowed gloves, and the shouts spread out behind him in the wrong configuration, chasing along the bank instead of cutting the shallows, because soldiers from dry climates did not believe ice-water marsh could be crossed, and Felix Lockridge, who had spent a lifetime believing himself incapable of anything, was crossing it.

That was when the shot came.

It cracked flat across the marsh, and something smacked the reeds six feet to his left, spraying gray pith, and Felix threw himself down into the freezing soup and lay there among the dead rushes with his heart slamming, and heard, faintly, over the wind—

"—hold your fire! HOLD your fire, you idiot, the colonel wants them ALIVE—"

And beneath that command, another voice. Younger. Hoarse. Screaming upward into the sky as though it could reach Felix through the reeds and the water and the whole gray machinery of the empire:

"THE LEFT CHANNEL! STAY IN THE LEFT CHANNEL! THE RIGHT ONE'S DREDGED TOO DEEP!"

Silence. Then boots receding, fast, toward the landing, and Sorrel bellowing about insubordination, and then nothing but wind and the tick of ice forming.

Felix lay in the marsh and understood.

Dunn had hesitated. That was the truth of it, plain as a ledger line. In the moment between the order given and the order obeyed, Corporal Thomas Dunn — nineteen years old, gut scarred by shrapnel, mother in a condemned village — had screamed him a path to safety. It might cost the boy a flogging. It might cost him worse. He had paid it anyway, out of four hours of catgut and candlelight, out of the small terrible treasury of gratitude, and Felix realized he was weeping, or the marsh water was simply running down his face from every direction, and there was no telling the difference and no time to try.

*Survival bought with silence,* he thought. *But what do you call survival bought with someone else's blood? What column does that go under?*

He got up and ran the left channel, stumbling, floundering, praying to a God he had addressed perhaps six times in his life, and the marsh received him the way it received everything — without comment.

* * *

By dusk he had come out the far side of the fen and doubled back along the hedgerows to the ruined smokehouse behind the Halloway place, which had become, in the strange economy of occupation, the safest room in town precisely because everyone assumed it was searched weekly. It was searched monthly. Ada had taught him that difference; she had a spy's eye for bureaucratic laziness.

He lit no lamp. He sat in the dark among the old smoke hooks, soaked to the bone, teeth chattering in a rhythm that matched some distant telegraph key, and he made himself think.

They had Ada. They had the spool she'd dropped — though Felix would have wagered everything he owned that the dropped spool was freight schedules, genuine ones, dull as porridge; Ada did not carry evidence she wasn't prepared to lose. They had Dunn's testimony, coerced and probably already recanted in part, because Dunn would recant, Dunn would swallow any punishment to keep his mother's papers safe, and Sorrel knew it, and would squeeze him slowly.

What they did not have was Felix. Not yet. But they would have his description by nightfall, and the town gates would close, and Ashworth — courteous, patient Ashworth, who invited men to dinner before he ruined them — would understand at last that the quiet nurse with the careful handwriting had never been quiet at all.

In the morning, if Ada held, they would have nothing but a dropped spool and a deserter's trail leading into the marsh. If Ada broke —

She wouldn't break. But she might perform breaking, which was nearly as dangerous, feeding them just enough confession to seem cooperative while the false cipher key she'd planted garbled their intercepts into nonsense. Either way, she was alone in a cell, and he was here, and the last names — the eleven from Marrow Bridge, the ones that existed nowhere but his memory and a scrap of paper in his boot — were his alone to carry now.

Felix took out the scrap. In the blue dark he could not read it, so he recited instead, the way he imagined she was reciting in her cell, name after name after name, farm boys and porters and a miller's wife and eleven souls from a bridge whose name had stopped meaning anything honest months ago.

When he finished, the moon had cleared the smokehouse roof, and down by the river he could hear the garrison settling, and somewhere — faint, almost lost under the wind — a single bell began tolling the hour, and then, oddly, kept tolling past it.

Nine. Ten. Eleven strokes, and still ringing.

Felix stood up, every joint announcing itself. That was no clock. That was the church bell being rung by hand, fast and ragged, the way a town rang news. Alarm, or celebration, or summons — and with New Year's Eve two days off, and Ashworth's fortifications going up along the Marrow Bridge road like a fist closing, it could not be good news.

He pulled his collar up, tucked the names back against his ankle, and slipped out into the dark to find out what the bells were saying — and whether, somewhere between here and the bridge, there was still one road left open that led across the river.
