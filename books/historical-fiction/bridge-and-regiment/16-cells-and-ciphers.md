## 16. Cells and Ciphers

They took her boots at the door of the cell, which struck Ada as an odd first cruelty. Not her hands, which were the instruments of everything she'd done—not her eyes, which had read and copied and transmitted more truth in three nights than the army's entire signal section had managed in a winter—but her boots. As if the body committed crimes and the feet merely accompanied it.

"Regulations," said the private who held the sack that now contained her boots, her laces, her hairpins, and her little brass pencil. "Nothing you can tie, cut, or write with."

"You've left me my thoughts," Ada said.

The private looked at her with something that might have been pity, or might have been indigestion; in a young face the two are difficult to distinguish. Then he shut the door, and the bolt went home with the sound of a full stop.

The cell had been a coal cellar once, back when the telegraph office belonged to the railway and not to Colonel Ashworth's ambitions. Ada knew this because she had helped string the wire that converted it. There was a poetry in that, she supposed—the sort she did not have leisure to appreciate. The walls sweated cold. A stool stood in the middle like an accusation. Somewhere above her, muffled by floorboards, the army's traffic clicked on without her: routine, orderly, half of it lies, all of it now in the hands of men who believed a cipher was a lock and locks kept things *safe*.

Ada sat down on the stool and began, methodically, to memorize the room. This was a habit from her trade. An operator who knows her room can work blind—can send and receive by touch alone, in darkness, in panic. She counted the stones. She mapped the pipe that ran up the corner, the bracket where the coal chute had been sealed, the draught that came from somewhere under the floor and smelled of the river. She put the room away inside herself, the way she had put away the names.

Then she waited, because waiting was also part of the trade. Messages do not arrive when you are ready for them. They arrive when the line is open.

* * *

The line opened at what her body said was mid-afternoon, though the cellar had no opinion on the matter.

Colonel Ashworth came down himself. She heard him before she saw him: the measured tread, the pause at the threshold—he was a man who paused at thresholds the way other men paused before mirrors—and then he was filling the doorway with his gray bulk, holding a lantern that he did not need because someone had already brought a lamp, but which he carried anyway, perhaps as punctuation.

"Miss Quill." He set the lantern on the floor, precisely centered between them, as though they were about to play a game across it. "I apologize for the accommodations. I understand they were once used for coal."

"They still smell ambitious," Ada said.

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile; more the memory of one, filed away with other documents no longer current. He gestured, and a chair was brought for him by the silent private. He sat. He arranged his hands on his knees. He looked, Ada thought, like a bishop preparing to discuss parish accounts: patient, reasonable, quite certain that God was on his side and would not be needing to be consulted personally.

"You have been with the occupation since the second week," Ashworth said. "You are competent—I will go further; you are the best operator I have inherited in twenty years of service. Your error rate is negligible. Your fist"—he meant her sending style, the signature rhythm every operator's hand leaves in the wire—"is clean enough that I could pick you out of a hundred."

"You flatter," said Ada. "Or you've been reading my traffic logs, which is less romantic."

"I have been reading your traffic volumes." He let that sit. Outside, faintly, a bugle called the hour, and got it slightly wrong. "In the week before your arrest, outgoing traffic from this station doubled. Weather reports tripled. Freight manifests appeared for consignments no quartermaster will admit to. And every one of them, Miss Quill, was flawless. No operator doubles her output flawlessly out of boredom. Boredom makes mistakes. Purpose does not."

Ada regarded him. It was oddly restful, being interrogated by a man who had done his arithmetic. It saved her the labor of pretending there was nothing to explain.

"Purpose," she agreed. "Yes. I've always been a purposeful woman. My mother despaired of it."

"What was the purpose?"

"To serve His Majesty's signals to the best of my considerable ability."

The colonel nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something. From his coat he drew a folded paper—not her confession; nothing so crude—a telegram, and unfolded it with the care of a man handling a moth wing.

"Your brother," he said.

The cellar grew no colder. It had been cold all along; it simply became the only fact in the room.

"Corporal Thomas Quill," Ashworth read. "Second Regiment of Foot. Tried at Veldenhof, August last. Desertion in the face of the enemy. Sentence executed the same day." He refolded the paper. "I didn't order it. I wasn't there. I want you to understand that I say these next words as a soldier and not as a lawyer: the trial was fast because the army was frightened, and frightened institutions do sloppy arithmetic. If it is any comfort—and I am told it is not—it should not have gone the way it went."

Ada found that her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles, and that she had no memory of folding them. Nine minutes. She had calculated the trial's length once, from the date on the notification to the time of the execution listed two lines below, and nine minutes was the figure her mind kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Nine minutes. She had taken longer deciding whether to have tea or coffee that same morning. She could not remember which she had chosen.

"What do you want, Colonel?" Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"The key," Ashworth said. "Whatever cipher you used for the false traffic. The book, the rotation, the offset—whatever system you and Lockridge built between you. Give it to me, and here is what happens." He counted it off on thick fingers, unhurried, a man reading terms of trade. "Your cooperation is noted in the record. You are remanded for the duration under protective custody rather than charged. And when this war finds its way home—as they all do—you stand in the column marked *loyal*, not the one marked *traitor*. Your mother reads one word in the papers instead of two."

"My mother," Ada said, "reads nothing. She died believing Tommy died a hero, because that is what the notification said, and I am the person who taught her to believe what the wire says."

Ashworth absorbed this without visible effect. That was the terrible thing about him, she was learning: not cruelty—cruelty would at least have been *warm*—but efficiency. He was a man who believed the empire was a great house whose foundations had cracked, and that his whole duty lay in repainting the walls before anyone looked down.

"Miss Quill," he said gently, "you are a practical woman. You have spent your life moving other people's words. You know better than most that words are cheap. One key, given freely, buys a lifetime of standing on the right side of the record. Whereas silence—" he spread his hands, regretful, a merchant displaying empty shelves—"silence buys a cell, a trial, and a paragraph in a report that no one will ever contest, because reports, once filed, become true. You of all people know how that works."

*I know exactly how it works*, Ada thought. *That is why you should be more afraid of me than you are.*

But she only looked at him for a long moment, letting the silence do its own transmitting, and then she sighed—the sigh of a tired professional conceding to management.

"The key," she said. "All right. All right. But you'll want it accurate, and you'll want it from me and no one else, because if you take a wrong digit off to headquarters you'll garble six weeks of intercepts and hang the wrong men. Do you have writing materials? Or has regulation confiscated the alphabet entire?"

* * *

Here is what Ada Quill knew, and what Colonel Ashworth did not:

A cipher is not a lock. A lock keeps things out. A cipher is more like a lens—if you grind it wrong, everything seen through it looks true and means nothing. She had learned this at sixteen, from a drunk old line-operator named Petch who had once, in the border troubles of her girlhood, built a false book purely to feed to a captured courier. *Never give them a broken key*, Petch had said, wheezing with laughter into his tea. *A broken key they'll spot in a day. Give them a beautiful key. Give them a key that works perfectly—on a different book.*

So she gave Ashworth beauty.

She dictated, and the private wrote, and she corrected the private twice with genuine irritation—"No, *shift four* on odd days, are you transcribing or composing poetry?"—until the system lay on the paper in immaculate rows: a rotating offset against a standard freight codebook, keyed to the calendar, elegant as clockwork. It would decode her transmitted traffic flawlessly. Every weather report, every manifest, every innocent line would resolve, under her key, into plausible military nonsense—supply dumps that didn't exist, columns marching toward towns that had burned in autumn, casualty figures that contradicted the ones Ashworth himself had written. And because it resolved into *something*, because it worked, he would trust it. He would build his picture of Felix's network through her lens, and every intercept his signals officer pulled from the air for the next month would arrive pre-garbled, aimed like a thrown rug at the feet of his own certainty.

She even wept a little while she did it. This surprised her; she had thought herself past such currency. But the tears were not for Ashworth, and they were not performance either—or not only. They were for Tommy, who had faced his nine minutes alone, and for the fact that the only monument she could raise to him was a lie constructed so carefully it would take an empire a season to fall over. Grief, she discovered, transmits at any distance, over any medium, and cannot be intercepted.

Ashworth watched her cry and misread it entirely. She saw him misread it—saw the small settling of his shoulders, the satisfaction of a man watching a debt settle. He believed she was breaking. He believed the tears were the sound of a lock opening.

"Thank you," he said, rising, gathering the pages with the reverence due to intelligence. "You have chosen wisely, Miss Quill. When this is over, there will be a place for you. The army forgives competence; it cannot run without it."

"I know," Ada said, wiping her face with the heel of her hand. "That is what frightens me about it."

He paused at the door—his threshold pause—and turned halfway. "Lockridge," he said. "Where is he?"

"Gone, I imagine," Ada said. "He was always going somewhere quietly. It was his single virtue."

"And the ledger?"

"I never saw a ledger, Colonel. I saw supply forms in a very careful hand. You'll find no crime in careful handwriting."

He looked at her a moment longer—that flat, weighing look, a man checking his figures against a sum that refused to come out—and then he went, taking his lantern, taking her beautiful false key, taking the light.

* * *

The dark came down again, and Ada sat with it.

This was the part no one trained you for: not the interrogation, which after all was only another form of traffic—send, receive, confirm—but the hours after, when the line was closed and there was nothing to work and nothing to watch except the inside of your own skull. She had always pitied people who feared the dark. Darkness was only a poor connection. What frightened her was bandwidth: the fear that her memory, given silence, would start dropping packets. That the names would thin. That somewhere around the fourth night she would reach for a name and find the wire dead.

So she did what she had done every night since Marrow Bridge, since the night Felix Lockridge had stood in the infirmary doorway with shaking hands and a shaking book and asked her, in a voice stripped down to the studs, whether a man could mail a conscience.

She began at the beginning.

"Aldous Petrie," she said to the dark, barely aloud, her lips doing most of the work. "Farmer's son. Seventeen. Fever, Tuesday."

The names came forward in their order, the order Felix had written them and she had copied into cipher and then, when the cipher felt too fragile to hold them, into the plain memory behind her eyes. She had memorized them the way other women memorized hymns—by repetition, by rhythm, by attaching each name to something solid. Aldous Petrie she had tied to the smell of carbolic. Marta Osei, the drowned porter's wife, she had tied to the sound of the river under ice. The eleven from Marrow Bridge she kept last and longest, each one spoken twice, because those were the names the official record said had never existed, and a name spoken twice is harder to unmake.

It was not heroism. She wanted that understood, if not by history then at least by herself, sitting on a stolen stool in a coal cellar in her stockinged feet. Heroism was charge and flash and the banner in the smoke. This was slower and meaner: endurance, which is heroism's homely sister, the one who does the washing. Resistance, for Ada Quill, was the refusal to forget in a room designed to make forgetting comfortable. It was reciting the roll call of the erased to sweating stone, night after night, like prayers to a god who had so far declined to answer but might simply be on another frequency.

"Marta Osei," she whispered. "Say it again. Marta Osei."

Above her, the army's traffic clicked and chattered, carrying her false key out into the winter air, garbling the colonel's world one flawless transmission at a time. She listened to it the way a sailor listens to rigging. And beneath the click of the wire—fainter, farther, so faint she might have invented it, and would keep inventing it every night after—she heard something else. Boots on frost. A door somewhere in the town opening and shutting softly. The sound, unmistakable to a woman who had spent her life listening for signals, of one man moving through the dark toward the river, carrying nothing but the last of the names and whatever courage he had failed to leave behind.

Hold on, Felix Lockridge, she thought, settling back against the cold stone, closing her eyes, beginning again at the top of the roll. Hold on, and cross. The third checkpoint is Bray's, and Bray counts heads—

—and tomorrow, at Marrow Bridge, he would count yours.
