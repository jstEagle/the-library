## 17. The Third Checkpoint

The last day of the century arrived wearing gray bunting and gun oil.

Felix saw it from the ridge above the river road, where he had spent the night pretending to be a man sleeping upright against a fence post. Below him, Marrow Bridge lay strung across the ice like a bone someone had tried to set badly—stone piers, timber deck, and now sandbag emplacements at both ends, searchlight rigs lashed to the old toll posts, and soldiers in new gray greatcoats stamping their feet against a cold that had opinions of its own. Bunting had been hung along the railings for New Year's Eve, red and white streamers gone stiff as sheet iron. Someone at headquarters had decided that a fortified checkpoint ought to look festive. The result was a gallows dressed for a wedding.

He counted sentries twice and got eleven both times, which he found vaguely insulting. Eleven was not a number that inspired confidence in an army's arithmetic. Then again, eleven had been the number of names at Marrow Bridge, once—the first eleven—and so perhaps it was simply the river's favorite figure, come round again like a debt collector.

His boot itched. He resisted scratching it, because inside the boot, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged beneath his heel like a corn of the most dangerous variety, was a scrap of paper bearing thirty-one names. The rest were carried in the only vault no sergeant had ever successfully searched: the damp, overworked archive of Felix Lockridge's skull. Forty-three more names there, recited nightly like a pharmacist going over stock, each one attached to a face, a village, a small particular detail that made it impossible to forget. Tomas Verek, who could whistle through his fingers loud enough to call a dog home across two fields. Anselm Petty, drowned carrying ammunition he'd been promised would count as time served.

"You'll do," Felix told himself, in the tone he used on feverish men who wanted to get up and be heroes. "You'll lie down and do."

Then he went down the ridge to join the column of wounded.

* * *

The evacuation convoy assembled in the yard of the burned tannery, and it was, in its way, the most honest assembly Felix had seen in this war: forty men who had been shot, frozen, or rotted, being moved because somebody at headquarters had finally noticed that dead soldiers made worse paperwork than dying ones. Orderlies in blood-spotted aprons loaded stretchers onto wagons. A chaplain stood by the gate murmuring blessings at a speed suggesting he had a train to catch.

Felix wore an orderly's coat three sizes too large and a face he hoped read as *tired, unremarkable, and none of your business*. He had learned that expression years ago, back when he believed it was a philosophy rather than a disguise.

"Hallo," said a voice from the second wagon. "You're the nurse."

Corporal Dunn looked up at him from under a blanket, one arm splinted, his color better than Felix expected and his eyes considerably worse. Felix's stomach performed a slow, professional maneuver.

"I'm an orderly today," Felix said.

"Today you're whatever you say you are." Dunn glanced left and right, then leaned close enough that Felix could smell the carbolic on him. "They're saying the telegraph girl escaped custody. Saying nobody knows how. Saying there's a man they want, a nurse, and that he's heading for the bridge tonight."

"Rumors travel faster than wagons," Felix said. "It's why the army keeps trying to shoot them."

Dunn held his gaze a moment too long. Then he lay back. "Mind your heel, sir," he said quietly. "The ice on the far span is bad. I crossed it drunk in November and it sang at me the whole way."

And that was all. Felix walked on, heart hammering a rhythm entirely unsuited to an orderly's dignity, and understood two things at once: that Dunn knew exactly what was in his boot, and that Dunn had chosen to tell him about the *ice*. Whatever else the corporal had traded away at the boathouse—and Felix had not yet let himself think too hard about that—he was paying something back now, in the only currency a splinted man in a guarded convoy possessed.

Gentle humor, Felix reflected, climbing onto the tailboard of the third wagon, was a luxury. But gratitude delivered sideways, disguised as advice about footwork—that appeared to be this country's native tongue.

* * *

The column moved out at half past four, in the blue hour when the snow turned the color of a bruise and the cold stopped bothering to be subtle. The wagons creaked. The mules expressed themselves at length on the subject of winter. Somewhere ahead, across the darkening river, church bells in the fishing villages began ringing the hour, then kept ringing—peals rolling up and down the valley, because tomorrow was not merely a new day but a new year and, the papers insisted, a new age entire. The men in the wagons listened with the particular stillness of the wounded hearing a world that intended to go on without asking their permission.

"New century," muttered a private with a bandaged jaw, slurring cheerfully through gauze. "They say there won't be no more wars in the new century. That's what my mum wrote me. Scientific progress, she says."

"Your mum sounds like a sensible woman," Felix said.

"She is. Wrong, but sensible."

The bridge grew. That was the thing about checkpoints—you watched them for an hour and they never got closer, and then suddenly they were enormous and full of rifles. The sandbag emplacement at the near end resolved into individual bags, individual men, and then into one man stepping into the road with a lantern raised, wearing a sergeant's stripes and the expression of an accountant who has finally, after months of patient work, found the discrepancy.

Sergeant Bray.

Felix had rehearsed this moment in every configuration his imagination permitted, and his imagination, given a career in triage, was thorough. But rehearsal dissolved the instant the lantern light hit his face. There is no acting the part of a bored orderly when your pulse is audible to yourself at six feet.

"Wagons halt," Bray called, and the column halted, and the cold poured in around them like water finding its level.

Bray worked the convoy the way he did everything: slowly, thoroughly, and with visible pleasure in his own competence. He went wagon by wagon. He counted heads aloud, and Felix heard in his counting the same cadence Ada had described—the tally-keeper's music, anomalies his trade. He lifted blankets, checked requisition chits, interrogated the driver about a discrepancy of one between the manifest and the mules. ("One mule, Driver. Either your manifest lies or your wagon is haunted." "Could be both, Sarge." "It usually is.")

Then it was the third wagon. Then it was Felix.

"Lift your feet," Bray said. "Both of them. One at a time."

The lantern came down. The light found Felix's boots—borrowed, scuffed, wrong by a size—and stayed there. Felix thought, absurdly, of Mrs. Halloway standing in her own kitchen while strangers inventoried her pots, and understood for the first time in his comfortable life what it meant to have your fate rest in the hands of a man following rules he did not write.

"You're Lockridge," Bray said. Not a question. He said it the way he might have said *you're the discrepancy*.

The bells rang on across the water. Somewhere behind them, up the valley, a single artillery shell went off—somebody's idea of a New Year's salute—and the sound rolled over the bridge like a stone over a wall.

"The name's on a list," Bray continued, conversationally, crouching so the lantern lit them both like a pair of men examining a horse. "Described as 'the nurse with the private notes.' There's a reward attached. There's also, I notice, an order dated the twenty-ninth instructing all personnel at this crossing to detain anyone matching certain descriptions—" he produced a paper from his coat, unfolded it with ceremony, "—and here's the interesting part, Nurse. This order is countersigned with a cipher key we've been using since October. Only, we haven't. Been using it since October. Because the key we've been using since October isn't this key. Which means either headquarters has changed keys without telling anyone, which happens, or somebody very clever fed us a false one, which also happens." He folded the paper away. "I've spent four months counting things that don't add up at this crossing. Casualty figures halved before transmission. Engagements reported where my own patrols report none. And now cipher keys that garble our own intercepts." He straightened. "Do you know what I concluded, in the end? That the arithmetic was rotten at the top. Not crooked—I've known crooked sergeants, decent men mostly. Rotten. The kind of sums a man does when he wants a particular answer before he starts."

Felix said nothing. In his experience, the innocent and the guilty both fared better by silence; it was the terrified who talked, and terror was, after all, manageable. He managed it.

"Eleven men shot at Marrow Bridge after they surrendered," Bray said, quietly now. "I've read the intercepts. Real ones, before the wire went sour. I counted those names myself, off a signal that was supposed to have been destroyed." He looked down the bridge, toward the far shore where the bells were ringing in a century that believed itself improved. "My whole trade is patterns, Nurse. And the pattern says the men who gave that order are on the side of the wire I'm standing on. Which presents a sergeant with a professional problem."

"Which is?" Felix asked, because the silence had become unbearable.

"Whether the uniform makes the side, or the side makes the uniform." Bray took off one glove, flexed his bare hand as if testing it for some future use, then put the glove back on. "I've been asking it since November. Tonight I'm done asking."

He stepped back from the wagon, turned to his sentries, and pitched his voice to carry with parade-ground perfection.

"Convoy's clean! Manifests match, heads match, mules match—which surprises everyone. Pass them over!" Then, lower, to Felix alone, as the drivers shook up the reins: "Third span. Mind the ice—it sings. Walk in the wheel ruts and don't stop for anything, including regret."

" Sergeant," Felix managed, "your post—"

"My post," said Bray, picking up the lantern and turning back toward his sentries, "is wherever the truth is being counted. It seems I've been standing in the wrong place to do it." He paused, and added, with the ghost of the checkpoint's old relish for power, now aimed elsewhere: "Report me as a deserter, if it helps your newspaper man fill a column. Sergeant W. Bray, late of the crossing at Marrow Bridge, departed his post at the turn of the year. Make sure he spells it properly. I've a mother reads everything."

* * *

The wagons rolled onto the bridge and the planks boomed under them like a slow drum. Felix did not ride; he walked, as ordered, in the wheel ruts of the third wagon, close enough to put a hand on Dunn's stretcher rail without appearing to.

At the third span the ice beneath did sing—a high, thin whine, like a wet finger on glass, like something vast being asked to hold just a little longer than it liked. The men fell quiet. Even the mules stopped complaining. The bells on the far bank reached a crescendo, and Felix realized with a jolt that it was nearly midnight; that below his boots was the water that had taken Anselm Petty; that behind him a sergeant was unbuckling his own career with the calm of a man doing sums that finally balanced; and that in his boot, under his heel, thirty-one names rode across the singing ice toward morning.

The far sentries waved the convoy through without challenge—they had already received Bray's hail—and the wagons crunched off the stones onto the frozen mud of the landing road, and the column dispersed into the dark toward the lamp-lit huts of the fishing village.

"There," said Dunn from his wagon, as an orderly wheeled him toward the aid post. "Told you about the ice, didn't I. We're square now, sir." His grin was crooked and young. "Mostly square. Don't make me a saint in your book. Make me honest. It's rarer."

Felix watched him go. Then he checked the direction of the wind, pulled his collar up, and set off along the shore path toward a hut where, if Ada's last message had gotten through, a disgraced correspondent named Elias Voss was sitting beside a crate of type with a lamp turned low and a press that had been oiled and waiting for three days.

The bells struck twelve as he walked. The century died quietly, in snow, mid-peal, and the new one came in making the same sound, which Felix found either hopeful or damning and hadn't time to decide which.

In the sixth hut from the seawall, a lamp was burning. Through the frost-furred window he could see a broad-shouldered silhouette bent over something small and metallic, setting letters into a composing stick one careful piece at a time, and beneath the door, in lamplight, the words were already legible upside down:

MARROW BRIDGE — THE TRUE LIST —

Felix knocked. The door opened on a chain, a pair of sharp eyes, and the smell of ink.

"You'd be Lockridge," said the man across the river. "Ada said you'd come tonight. She also said—" the chain rattled free, the door swung wide, "—you'd arrive with half a list in your head and the other half in your boot, and that I was to feed you before you dictated a word, because a man can't testify to a massacre on an empty stomach." He stepped aside. "She wasn't specific about the menu. Come in before the cold does. We've got until dawn, and the tide, and God help us, enough type."

Felix stepped over the threshold, out of the century that was finished and into the one that would have to answer for it. Behind him, faint across the water, he could hear the searchlights sweeping the empty bridge—and, farther off, hoofbeats on the frozen road. A rider, coming fast, from the direction of headquarters.

Voss heard it too. His eyes narrowed over the lamp.

"Well," he said. "Let's see what the old century wants to say for itself."
