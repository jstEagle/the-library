## 18. Midnight Deadline

The hut smelled of fish, lamp oil, and forty years of a man's solitude, and Felix thought he had never in his life been anywhere so much like a church.

Elias Voss had spread the last clean sheets of newsprint across the table like an altar cloth and was now cleaning his type with a toothbrush, humming something that might once have been a hymn before the sea got into it. He was shorter than his reputation, rounder, with ink permanently lodged under his fingernails in patterns that looked from a distance like old bruises. When Felix had stumbled into the fishing village at dusk, half-frozen and unable to feel three of his fingers, Voss had looked him over, looked at the scrap of canvas protruding from his coat, and said only, "You'll be Lockridge. You're late for the century."

"I came as fast as the checkpoints allowed."

"Everyone says that." Voss had handed him a bowl of fish stew that was mostly pepper and salt holding hands over a few potatoes. "Nobody ever arrives slowly for history. They just arrive late and blame the roads."

Now the hut's single window showed nothing but darkness and the occasional white flutter of snow, and outside, the village slept the way soldiers sleep before an assault — lightly, guiltily, one ear open. Somewhere down the shore a dog was barking at the year 1899 for dying.

"The names," Voss said, setting his type case on the table between them like a deck of cards before a fortune-teller. "We go in order?"

"In order. The order matters. It's how I learned them."

"Then dictate slowly, spell anything odd, and if your voice cracks, keep going anyway. Cracking voices print beautifully." He licked his thumb and sorted a row of letters with the tenderness other men reserved for photographs of their children. "I've waited eleven years to set honest type. My hands may shake. Ignore them."

Felix took out his ledger.

It had crossed the river sewn into stretcher canvas, smuggled past Sergeant Bray by inches and by hemorrhage, and now it lay open on a fisherman's table looking smaller than it had any right to. Forty-one pages. Two hundred and six names. He knew them all now — not read them, *knew* them, the way you know the route to your own front door — because between the infirmary and the second checkpoint he had spent every sleepless hour reciting them like a rosary against forgetting.

"The first name," Felix said, "is Halloway. Margaret Halloway. Evicted the fourteenth of December, died of pneumonia in the church vestry on the twentieth. The army report lists her as 'displaced, outcome unknown.'"

Voss's fingers moved. *H-a-l-l-o-w-a-y.* "Outcome known," he murmured. "That's the whole newspaper, isn't it. Two words."

"That's the whole war."

* * *

They worked while the century ran out its sand.

The hut's stove gave grudging heat and less light, so Voss hung the lamp directly over the type case and they bent into its circle together, two heads close as conspirators — which, Felix supposed, was now a matter of legal record. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. Inside, the only sounds were the small iron music of letters being set, Felix's voice, and occasionally Voss swearing with great fluency and no heat at a missing ligature or a worn *e.*

"Quill," Felix dictated. "Tomas Quill. Telegrapher's apprentice, executed the ninth of August. Trial duration, nine minutes. The dispatch said 'desertion, sentence carried out promptly.' Nine minutes, Mr. Voss. They tried a boy in less time than it takes to boil an egg."

Voss stopped setting type. He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on.

"I printed his name once already," he said quietly. "August the twelfth. Half a column, buried on page four next to a shipping notice. The War Office denied it, my editor apologized, and I have been fishing ever since." He resumed sorting letters. "Tonight we do it properly. Page one. No apology."

"There are eleven men from Marrow Bridge who surrendered and were shot where they knelt. I have all their names. An officer ordered their testimony, quote, 'not recorded.'"

"Not recorded," Voss repeated, and there was something terrible in how gently he said it. He began setting the line. "You know what I love about type, Lockridge? It can't be promoted, pardoned, or reassigned. Once it's locked in the chase, it says what it says forever. A colonel can burn a parish register. He cannot burn a galley proof." He glanced up, lamplight catching the wet in his eyes or perhaps only the oil. "Well. He can try. He has tried. That's rather the point of me."

Felix turned the page. His hand left a damp print on the margin, and he found he didn't want to smooth it away. Let the record be damp. Let it show a human hand had been here.

"Dunn," he said, and stopped.

"Corporal Dunn?" Voss asked. "Is he on your list?"

"He is. Fever, wounds, recovered under my care. And three days ago he sold me to Ashworth's men for his mother's evacuation papers." Felix heard his own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when he dressed a wound that was worse than the patient believed. "Ada is in a cell because of him. I got through the marsh because he hesitated one second longer than he needed to. I don't know which of those facts belongs in print."

"Both," Voss said. "Print both. That's what makes it true instead of a pamphlet. A pamphlet has heroes. A newspaper has people." He worked a moment, then added, "Spell his mother's name too, if you have it. Women like her never get into anyone's ledger."

Felix did have it. Of course he had it. He wrote it into the account, and the act of writing loosened something in his chest that had been clenched since the boathouse — not forgiveness, nothing so tidy, but the recognition that Dunn's betrayal and Dunn's hesitation were the same wound seen from two sides, and Felix had survived both of them, and survival was not the same thing as innocence. It never had been. He had told himself for years that he was only a nurse, that history happened elsewhere, in offices and on bridges, to other people. The ledger in his hands was the confession that this had always been a lie told politely, in a low voice, over feverish men.

"Next name," Voss prompted.

He gave it. Then another. Then another. The pile of set type grew; Voss began locking lines into the forme with wedges and a mallet, tapping with the absorbed contentment of a man building a wall that would outlive everyone who hated it.

* * *

Near eleven o'clock the bells started.

They came from across the water first — the garrison chapel at the fort, ringing the old year down with official enthusiasm — and then the village answered, St. Brendan's cracked bell heaving itself into motion like an old man rising from a chair he'd sworn never to leave again. Between them, out on the dark water, boats began sounding horns. Someone on the far shore fired off a volley, celebration or provocation, impossible to say; along this border the two had shared a grammar for years.

"Nineteen hundred," Voss said, checking his pocket watch without breaking stride at the type case. "A whole new century arriving, and it finds us working. Good. Let it start as it means to go on."

Felix laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that surprised them both. "You're enjoying yourself."

"I am a disgraced journalist setting forbidden truth by fish-oil lamplight in a hut that smells of cod, while artillery-grade fireworks go off over a frozen river." Voss tightened a wedge with the mallet. "Lockridge, this is the finest New Year's Eve of my entire life, and I once spent one in Lisbon with a countess."

"What happened in Lisbon?"

"She stole my notes and married a consul. Which taught me the most valuable lesson of my career: paper is only safe once it's *many.* One copy is a secret. A thousand copies is a fact." He nodded at the press against the far wall — a flatbed older than the war, older probably than the century, iron bones gleaming with fresh grease he'd clearly spent the afternoon applying. "By dawn we will be a fact."

Felix turned to the final page. The last names. The ones that existed nowhere now but in his head and in the scrap in his boot — the crate burned, the infirmary stores gone up in Ashworth's tidy typhus fire, Ada's transmitted half-copy incomplete. He had walked across Marrow Bridge itself carrying these, past Bray's lifted lantern, past the moment when a man trained to count everything had chosen, silently, to miscount one.

"Ready," he said.

"Ready."

He gave the last eleven. The bridge dead. Slowly, distinctly, while the bells clashed and the horns moaned and somewhere a fiddler struck up something reckless. When he finished, neither of them spoke for a moment. The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. The new century stood in the doorway, dripping, waiting to see what kind of house it had entered.

"Well," Voss said at last, blinking hard and blaming the lamp smoke, "that's the lot. Now comes the part where we find out if my hands remember their trade." He lifted the heavy forme, fitted it to the bed of the press, and cranked the rollers down over it. Ink went onto type with a sigh. "Hand me those sheets. And stand clear of the flywheel unless you'd like your obituary set in advance."

* * *

The first pull was bad. The second was worse. The third printed a page that made them both go quiet.

Felix held it to the lamp. Black on white, square and unarguable: the headline Voss had composed without consulting anyone —

**THE NAMES AT MARROW BRIDGE: AN ACCOUNT THE WAR OFFICE DOES NOT KEEP**

— and beneath it, column after column, the list. Not commentary. Not accusation. Just names, dates, causes, and the plain sentence Felix had given him for the masthead note: *Compiled by one who treated the wounded and recorded what the reports omitted.*

"It looks," Felix said slowly, "like a telephone directory written by God."

"It looks like journalism," Voss corrected, pulling the lever. "Same thing, fewer angels." The press clanked, breathed, delivered. Clanked, breathed, delivered. Sheets accumulated on the table, then in stacks on the floor, then everywhere, the hut filling with paper the way a tide fills a channel. Voss worked the press like a pump organ at a revival, sweating in the cold, shouting encouragement to the machine. "There she goes! Fifty years old and faster than the entire War Office! Mind the stacks — mind them, man, that's the evidence!"

Outside, the village fireworks redoubled, and mixed among the crackers Felix heard, unmistakably now, rifle volleys from the garrison — the army celebrating the century too, spending its ammunition on joy for once, or on warning, depending on the hour and the listener. The bells would not stop. St. Brendan's bell had gotten its second wind and seemed determined to ring until physically prevented.

At midnight precisely, Voss pulled the sheet he declared the First True Copy of the Twentieth Century and pinned it to the door facing inward, "so the future knows what it's walking into."

And that was when the horse arrived.

They heard it first — hooves crunching the frozen shingle, unhurried, official — then a fist on the door, twice, courteously. Felix blew out the lamp in the same instant Voss slid the press's handle free to use as a club, and for one heartbeat the two of them stood in the dark among a thousand copies of the truth like burglars in a bank of honesty.

"If it's soldiers," Voss whispered, "the window. You go first. I'm too round for glory."

The fist came again. "Mister Voss." A young voice, cold, winded, trying to sound formal through a scarf. "Rider from Colonel Ashworth's headquarters. I've a letter for the gentleman from the infirmary. Felix Lockridge."

Felix looked at the dark shape of Voss. Voss shrugged elaborately enough to be visible in starlight. Felix relit the lamp, opened the door a hand's width, and took the offered envelope — good paper, wax seal, the colonel's crest stamped neat as a signature on a confession.

The rider, a boy of perhaps eighteen with frost in his eyebrows, peered past Felix into the lamplit hut, saw the mountains of newsprint, understood exactly what he was looking at, and visibly decided not to have seen it. "I'm to wait for an answer," he said. "There's a stove at the end of the lane."

"There is," Felix agreed, and shut the door.

He broke the seal. Voss crowded in, still holding the press handle, reading over his shoulder. The colonel's hand was beautiful — upright, disciplined, each letter drilled.

*Mister Lockridge. In view of your services to the medical establishment of this command, and in the spirit of the new century, a full and unconditional pardon is available to you, contingent upon a single formality: a signed statement acknowledging that certain private notes in your possession contain unintentional inaccuracies, and their surrender to this headquarters. You would return to your duties with honor intact. The alternative is arrest upon your return, a matter I would regret, as I regret all waste. Kindly send your answer by the bearer. — Col. G. R. Ashworth.*

"'Unconditional,'" Voss said, "except for the condition."

"And what a gentle word he's found for it. *Inaccuracies.*" Felix read it again. Margaret Halloway's kitchen. Tomas Quill's nine minutes. Eleven men shot kneeling at the water's edge, their testimony *not recorded.* Ada in her cell, feeding the interrogator a cipher key that would garble his own intercepts like a prayer going astray. Bray at the third checkpoint, lifting his lantern and choosing blindness — deserting, in his way, for the sake of the truth, trading his career for eleven names.

Freedom for silence. It was, Felix had to admit, generously priced. He could go back to nursing. He could grow old telling himself again that history happened elsewhere. All it cost was two hundred and six people.

"You don't need me for this," Felix said.

"No," Voss said. "But I'll watch anyway. I've waited eleven years for this particular pleasure and I intend to enjoy every second."

Felix took the pen Voss kept behind his ear, and the back of one of the spoiled first pulls, and wrote a single line. His hand did not shake. After the week he'd had, it was the least he could ask of it.

*History happened here.*

He folded it, sealed it with candle wax because he had nothing better, and opened the door. The rider's horse steamed at the end of the lane like a kettle. Felix walked the answer down through the falling snow, past villagers beginning to stumble out of doors with lamps, drawn by rumors of printing, and put the note into the boy's mittened hand.

"He'll ask what it says," Felix said. "Tell him exactly."

The boy looked at the paper, then at Felix, then — briefly, fatally, in the manner of the very young deciding something that will cost him later — nodded once and mounted.

Felix stood in the lane and watched the rider shrink down the coast road toward the fort, carrying the refusal into the teeth of the garrison, and felt the strangest thing rise in his chest: not triumph, nothing so clean. Just lightness. The ledger was finished. Whatever the century did with it now was no longer his to carry alone.

Behind him, the press was already clanking again, and Voss was shouting about dawn tides, and the first gray hint of the year 1900 was considering the horizon.

Then hoofbeats turned — fast now, urgent — and the rider came pounding back through the snow, waving his hat, and shouted something across the water-dark that Felix couldn't yet hear, but that made the dogs start up all down the shoreline at once.
