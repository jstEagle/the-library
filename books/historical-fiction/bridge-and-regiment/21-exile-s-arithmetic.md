## 21. Exile's Arithmetic

The harbor at Vlissingen smelled of herring and tar and the particular cold that comes off water in January, a cold that seemed to have opinions about you personally. Felix Lockridge stood at the foot of the gangplank with one canvas bag, one coat that had been mended twice by strangers, and no country that would have him.

"You're dawdling," said Elias Voss behind him. "Dawdlers miss boats, and men who miss boats become philosophers. I've seen it happen."

"I'm saying goodbye to a town."

"It's not even our town."

"No," Felix agreed. "That's rather the point."

The fishing village across the river from the war had become, in the six weeks since the *Marrow Bridge Dispatch* went out with the dawn tide, something between a refuge and a circus. Journalists arrived first, then the inquiry commissioners, then the commissioners' clerks, then the clerks' wives, who found the fish unpalatable and said so in letters that were later quoted in the papers for reasons no one could explain. And through it all the fishing went on, because herring did not care about empires.

The ship was the *Meridian*, bound for Cape Town by way of ports Felix would need an atlas to name. Voss had secured them passage by the simple method of writing a piece about the captain, which was so flattering that the man had wept into his beard and offered them the good cabins.

Felix looked back once at the river, gray under a low sky, at the bridge on the far side where he'd walked past Sergeant Bray's raised lamp while the whole checkpoint held its breath. Then he climbed.

* * *

The arithmetic began, as most of his accounting did, at sea.

He was three days out before he admitted to himself what he was doing: standing at the stern rail each evening, tallying. It was an old habit with a new ledger now. The first ledger—the small brown book with the names of the erased—had crossed the ocean inside Voss's typewriter case and then into evidence, photographed page by page by men whose expressions never changed but whose hands slowed when they turned certain leaves. It would come home eventually, the lawyers said. Evidence did that. Men did not.

So Felix started a second book, bought from the ship's purser for an outrageous sum, and on its first page he wrote nothing at all for a long time. He just held it and thought about what a ledger was for.

"You look like a man doing sums," Voss said, appearing beside him with two cups of coffee that tasted like boiled rope. The ship's cook made coffee the way artillery made craters: with conviction and no refinement whatsoever.

"I am."

"Bad news?"

"Dunn," Felix said.

Voss nodded slowly and drank his terrible coffee. He knew the story; everyone who mattered knew the story. Corporal Dunn, whom Felix had pulled back from fever twice, whose bandages Felix had changed with his own hands, had gone to the army with everything he knew about the telegraph conspiracy. In exchange: evacuation papers for his mother. A fair trade, if you believed survival was a currency. Dunn had hesitated one second—one—when they came to take Ada at the boathouse, and that single second of conscience was the only reason Felix wasn't in a cell in Ashworth's headquarters, or in a ditch outside it.

"He'll live with it," Voss said. "That's the sentence. Not the papers for his mother—he earned those, probably. Living with it. That's what you gave him, along with the fever cures."

"That's a generous reading."

"I'm a journalist. Generosity is the one luxury my trade permits." He squinted out at the water. "Besides, you've got it backward, as usual. You keep counting what it cost. Try counting what it bought."

* * *

What it bought, so far as Felix could determine from the newspapers that reached the ship at the second port:

An inquiry reopened, with terms broadened to include "the conduct of officers at and subsequent to the engagement of 14 December." Colonel Ashworth removed from command pending findings—a word, *pending*, that carried a man's whole career inside it like a seed. Eleven names read aloud in a chamber full of men who had previously declined to know them. Mrs. Halloway restored to her kitchen, which she reported was exactly as the army had left it, meaning ruined, meaning she was cleaning it herself and would thank the government not to help.

And Ada Quill still in custody, or so the last reliable report said, though the same report said charges were "under review," and Felix had learned what that phrase meant in the mouths of men losing an argument. Under review meant they were looking for a door.

"She got herself out of worse," Voss said, when Felix mentioned it for the fourth time that week. "She fed Ashworth a cipher key that garbled his own intercepts for a fortnight. She recited your dead men's names to her cell wall until she had them perfect. A woman like that doesn't stay caught. Caught is a temporary condition for her, like weather."

"You sound certain."

"I am certain. I interviewed her for eleven hours over two days and she didn't answer a single question directly and I came away knowing everything." He tapped ash from his cigarette over the rail. "You don't leave a person like that behind, Lockridge. You just leave before she does. There's a difference, and she'd be the first to tell you."

Felix wrote that down. Not in the new ledger—in his head, which was where he kept the things too true for paper.

* * *

Bray was harder.

Sergeant Bray had let him pass at Marrow Bridge on New Year's Eve, had looked at Felix with the flat eyes of a man who has counted heads for a living and finally counted himself, and had then done the arithmetic and chosen. He'd deserted his post. The newspapers used softer language—*resigned under a cloud*, which was how empires said *he told the truth*—but Felix knew what it cost a man like Bray to walk away from a uniform. Bray had been nothing but the uniform for thirty years. Patterns were his trade, and the last pattern he'd recognized was his own side's, and it had broken something in him that would not be mended by any inquiry.

The last anyone had heard, he'd taken work as a railway clerk in some provincial junction, keeping timetables instead of tallies. Felix hoped the trains ran on time under him. He suspected they ran early.

In the new ledger, on the third evening out, Felix finally wrote the first entry. His pen hovered a long time over the opening line. The old ledger had been a record of the dead, names rescued from reports that preferred round numbers. This one would be different. He printed carefully, in the handwriting Ada had once noticed on supply forms and correctly diagnosed as a man keeping accounts of his own:

*Dunn, Cpl. — betrayed me. Fed his mother for it. Saved my life by one second of hesitation. Entered under both columns.*

Then, beneath it:

*Bray, Sgt. — lost everything he had, which was the uniform. Gave me the road. I owe him a debt he will refuse to acknowledge, which is the hardest kind.*

"What are you writing?" Voss asked, dropping into the deck chair opposite with the air of a man settling in for entertainment.

"An inventory."

"Of?"

"The living."

Voss considered this for a moment, then took out his own notebook, which was fat with clippings. "Mind if I steal the idea? My paper wants a book. Everyone wants a book about the massacre. Nobody wants a book about the people who ended up paying for telling the truth about it. That's the better book."

"Take it," Felix said. "I'll want royalties in cigarettes."

"Done." Voss lit one and passed it over, and for a while they smoked in companionable silence while the ship climbed the long swells and the southern stars came out, unfamiliar and orderly, like an army that had never lied to anyone.

* * *

They fell into a routine, which Felix distrusted and depended on equally. Mornings, Voss drafted chapters and swore at sailors with great warmth. Afternoons, Felix worked in the ship's infirmary—the captain having discovered his nursing background and requisitioned him within a day, because a colonel's habits die hard even in kinder men. The crew's ailments turned out to be gloriously ordinary: a boil, a sprained wrist, seasickness in fourteen varieties. No fevers that hid massacres. No paperwork at all.

He hadn't realized how much of his life had been paperwork until there was none. Just a man, a bucket of clean water, a sailor grateful for ointment. History, it turned out, happened elsewhere after all—but only because other people were carrying it, and carrying it well.

Evenings he wrote in the new ledger. It grew slowly, because gratitude required more words than grief. Grief fit neatly into a name and a date; the army had taught him that much. Gratitude sprawled. For Ada he filled two pages and crossed out half of them, trying to find a sentence equal to a woman who had transmitted the truth in weather reports and freight manifests, three nights running, by candlelight, with her brother's execution sitting in her chest like swallowed lead—and who had joked about the quality of army cigarettes the entire time.

*Quill, Ada,* he finally wrote. *Left behind. Not abandoned—there is a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life learning it. Every name she sent is a brick in a building Ashworth can't tear down. If this ledger ever reaches her, the first page says what I couldn't say at the ferry landing: that I counted the cost of survival all wrong, and she corrected my figures.*

He read it back and decided it was terrible and true, which was the best he could manage, and kept it.

* * *

At the fourth port a letter found them, forwarded through three hands and smelling faintly of fish, which Voss declared was the authentic scent of journalism.

It was from Mrs. Halloway.

*Mr. Lockridge,* it began, *I do not know where ships go so I am sending this everywhere until one of them answers. They have let the Quill woman out. Released without trial, which the newspaper calls a scandal and I call Tuesday. She is staying above the bakery and repairing the telegraph office, which the army left in a state I would not describe to a Christian. She asked me to tell you two things. First, that the wire works again, and that she sends on the open band every Friday evening, plain speech, no cipher, and that you should listen if ships can listen.*

*Second, she said to tell you the names are safe. All of them. She memorized the rest from you without your knowing it, watching you write, which she says is a professional habit and not eavesdropping, though I have met her and I doubt it.*

*The town remembers you kindly, mostly. Some remember you as the man who brought all this down on us, but those are mostly the ones who profited from the old arrangement, and their opinion was always for sale anyway.*

*Come home when you can. Or don't, and be well somewhere else, which is also a kind of coming home.*

*Respectfully, E. Halloway.*

Felix read it four times. On the fourth reading he laughed, unexpectedly, and had to sit down on a bollard.

"She's free," he said.

"So I gather from your face, which is doing something embarrassing."

"She's free, and she's broadcasting the names every Friday, uncoded, to whoever has ears." He shook his head. "They cut her wire once. They'll cut it again. She'll repair it again. She's going to make them exhaust an empire's supply of wire cutters, one Friday at a time."

Voss grinned around his cigarette. "And you're smiling because—"

"Because I spent a year thinking I was the only honest account in existence." Felix folded the letter with exaggerated care, as though it might bruise. "Turns out I was never the ledger. I was just the pen. She's the ledger. She always was."

* * *

That night, at the stern rail, Felix opened the new ledger to its first page and added one more line, the last of the crossing, written large enough that Voss could read it upside down and did:

*Lockridge, F. — nurse, smuggler, exile. Survived by trading silence for action at the worst possible exchange rate. Debt outstanding to: one operator, one sergeant, one traitor with a good heart, one widow who writes letters that smell of herring. Intends to repay in the only currency accepted: memory, kept accurately, against all instructions.*

"You know," Voss said mildly, "that page will end up in an archive someday. Some clerk will catalog it. 'Sentiments of an exile, circa 1900.'"

"Good," Felix said. "Let him. It'll be the only honest thing in the drawer."

The ship ran on southward through the dark, and the century behind them grew smaller with every mile—not gone, never gone, but carried now in more hands than the army could count, in cells and cellars and bakery rooms and Friday broadcasts, in ledgers kept by the stubborn. Felix watched the wake unspool behind them, pale and straight as a written line.

Then the lookout called down from the mast that there was a light on the horizon, another ship, heading the same way, and Voss lowered his voice and said, "Lockridge. There's something you should hear before we make port. About what Ada sent on the last Friday before we sailed—and about who else heard it."

Felix closed the ledger and turned. "Tell me."

"Not here," Voss said, glancing up at the mast, at the light, at the whole listening dark. "And you'll want to be sitting down."
