## 22. Bridge and Regiment

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday in March, which Ada Quill considered fitting, because Tuesdays had always been the day the world decided to complicate itself.

She found it leaning against the door of her cottage that morning—a brown-paper bundle tied with twine, addressed in a hand she would have known anywhere, even after nine years, even blindfolded, even underwater. Felix Lockridge wrote the letter *g* with a small impatient loop, as though the letter had personally offended him and he was pinning it down.

Ada stood in her doorway a long moment, the parcel in her hands, weighing it. Not heavy. That was the first surprise. She had expected—she didn't know what. Something with heft. Ledgers, in her experience, were heavy things. They carried weight the way anchors did.

This one felt like bread.

"You going to stand there all morning, or are you going to open it?" called Mrs. Tansy from next door, who was ninety-one and considered every event within fifty yards of her kitchen window to be a public performance requiring an audience.

"It's private," Ada called back.

"Private things are always the most interesting," said Mrs. Tansy, unrepentant, and went back to beating a rug with the violence of a woman who had outlived three husbands and intended to outlive the rug too.

Ada took the parcel inside.

Her cottage sat at the edge of town now—not the river town of the war, but a smaller place two valleys over, where she'd gone after the amnesty to be nobody again. She worked the telegraph line for the railway, a quiet post, mostly freight schedules and the occasional apology for a delayed milk train. The army was done with her. The army, as far as she knew, was done pretending it had ever known her.

She made tea first. Some parcels demanded ceremony.

Then she cut the twine with the little knife she kept in the sugar tin—because where else does one keep knives—and unfolded the brown paper, and there it was.

A ledger. Navy blue cloth, corners worn soft, thicker than she remembered his old one being. On the cover, in gold leaf that had already begun to flake, someone had stamped two words:

*Bridge and Regiment.*

Her hands were steady. Nine years ago they would not have been. She noticed this about herself the way one notices a scar healing—that it no longer hurts when the weather changes.

She opened it.

The first page bore a single name, written large and centered, with the careful looped *g* and the impatient crossbar on the *t*:

**Ada Quill — who taught the wire to speak the truth.**

Below it, in smaller script: *"The living who helped. Volume One."*

Ada set down her tea because her hands had reconsidered their steadiness, and read on.

Page after page. Names she knew and names she didn't. Mrs. Halloway, who had fed wounded men from her own evicted kitchen and never once asked who they were fighting for. Sergeant Bray, listed with the annotation *"let me pass at Marrow Bridge; deserted rather than arrest a nurse; last seen heading south with a mule named after a colonel."* Ada laughed aloud at that—one short, startled bark—which sent Mrs. Tansy's curtains twitching across the way.

Corporal Dunn was there too. Ada's smile faded reading it. *"Betrayed us for his mother's life,"* Felix had written. *"I would have done the same. I have written his name here because history keeps enough lists of villains, and I am tired of them."*

That was Felix all over. He could never let a sin sit still without trying to find the fear underneath it.

Near the middle of the ledger she found her brother.

Thomas Quill. Executed for desertion, after a trial lasting nine minutes—she had counted them, all those years ago, sitting outside the tent with her hands over her ears while the verdict was read in a voice like a man ordering soup. The army had called him a coward. Their mother had died calling him that too, because the official version had arrived first and grief is lazy; it takes whatever story is handed to it.

Felix had written: *"Thomas Quill. Deserted after being ordered to fire on civilians. Refused twice. The second refusal cost him his life and saved, by my count, four people in the village below the ridge. A coward does not refuse twice."*

Ada pressed the page flat with both palms and sat very still for a while.

Outside, Mrs. Tansy beat the rug within an inch of its life.

* * *

Beneath the ledger, folded into the brown paper, was a newspaper clipping. She recognized the masthead immediately—the same press that had clanked through the midnight of the old century in Elias Voss's freezing fishing hut, printing truth faster than the war office could print denials. The clipping was newer than that. Much newer. Dated six weeks past.

**MARROW BRIDGE INQUIRY REOPENED**, the headline ran. **NEW EVIDENCE FORCES REEXAMINATION OF 1899 ACTION.**

Ada read it three times.

The inquiry had been closed years ago—shut with great ceremony and greater speed once it became clear that the officers testifying had memorized identical answers to non-identical questions. Ashworth himself had appeared in full dress, gray at the temples, magnificent, and declared that the action at Marrow Bridge had been "a regrettable necessity conducted with honor," which was, Ada reflected, the sort of sentence that required two lies working in shifts.

But inquiries, unlike soldiers, did not always stay dead. Someone had found records. Dispatch intercepts—the weather reports and freight manifests, decoded at last by a signals clerk with more patience than ambition—who discovered that between November and December of 1899, army traffic had doubled for no military reason whatsoever, and that buried in the noise, name by name by name, marched the dead of Marrow Bridge.

Someone had found Ada's ciphers. And someone, somewhere in a warm office, had spent months doing what she had done by candlelight: counting.

The article ended with a line she read aloud to the empty room, just to hear how it sounded in the air:

*"Among the evidence submitted is a privately compiled ledger of civilian deaths, authenticated by multiple witnesses, whose compiler remains abroad but has indicated through counsel his willingness to testify 'if the inquiry can guarantee I will not be pardoned into silence.'"*

He was coming back. Or offering to. Felix Lockridge, exile, was standing at the edge of home the way he used to stand at checkpoints—with everything hidden on his person and his heart going like a telegraph key.

Ada looked at the ledger, then at the clipping, then at her cold tea.

"Well," she said to nobody. "Damn."

Mrs. Tansy's curtain twitched again. Ada drew hers.

* * *

She walked to the bridge at dusk.

It took some doing, these days—her knees had opinions about hills, formed sometime around her fiftieth birthday and expressed freely ever since—but the path down to the water was gentle, and she gave herself leave to be slow. Slow was a luxury. During the war she had been fast at everything: fast at sending, fast at lying, fast at deciding whose message went first. Speed had nearly killed her. Slowness felt like victory.

The bridge was new. Of course it was new; the army had blown the old one in the last retreat, as though destroying a bridge could destroy what happened on it—as though timber and iron had been the guilty parties. The replacement span had been raised five years ago by engineers who did not know its history and townspeople who very much did. It was wider than the original, and plainer, and painted an institutional green that nobody had asked for.

It was still the most important bridge in the world.

The river ran beneath it, darkening with evening, carrying the last light downstream in long copper ribbons. Ada stopped at the midpoint, where the rail met the water view, and put both hands on the cold metal.

She had not come here in nine years. She had told herself this was because of distance—two valleys, bad roads, a railway line that served milk trains with more enthusiasm than punctuality. But the truth was simpler and she finally had the age to admit simple truths: she had been afraid the river wouldn't remember.

Rivers forget. That is their profession.

So she did what she had practiced alone in her cell all those winters ago, when the names had been the only property the army couldn't confiscate. She recited them aloud.

"Halloway, Margaret. Evicted from her own kitchen. Died in the spring, of the cold they gave her."

The water moved. Whether it listened was between the water and God, but Ada had spent her whole career believing that messages mattered even if no one answered.

"Porter, unnamed. Drowned carrying army stores, unrecorded by the quartermaster, recorded by me."

Wind off the water. Her voice, low and level, in the cadence of transmission—she couldn't help it; forty years at the key had given her a rhythm, each word spaced clean, no slurring of the signal.

"Verek, Josef. Farmer's son. Seventeen."

One by one. The eleven from Marrow Bridge, whom Felix had written with shaking hands while an officer ordered their testimony destroyed. The others from the winter lists, the small names, the erased ones. She spoke them the way you feed a fire that refuses to go out—steadily, without flourish, because flourish is for people who don't believe the fuel will catch.

Halfway down the list she heard footsteps on the planks behind her and paused only long enough to identify them as unhurried, singular, and pausing at a respectful distance—the footsteps of a person waiting, not following. She finished the name she was on—"Ashdown, Peter. Blacksmith. Shot with his tools beside him"—and turned.

A man stood ten feet away, hat in his hands, gray through the temples, thinner than the photograph in her memory but unmistakable. He wrote the letter *g* with an impatient loop; he had crossed oceans and refused pardons; and he was standing on the rebuilt bridge at dusk looking at her as if nine years were a misprint he'd only just caught.

"You're heavier than your parcel," Ada said.

"I've been told I'm dense," said Felix Lockridge. His voice cracked on the second word, which pleased her enormously.

They stood there in the failing light, two conspirators of the old century, while the river went about its forgetting below them.

"How long have you been standing there listening?" she asked.

"Since Halloway." He came forward and put his hand on the rail beside hers, so their shadows lay together on the planks. "You got the list right. All of them. I checked against the ledger on the train."

"I memorized them like prayers," she said. "You know that. I told you once, through a cell wall."

"You told me through a lawyer, actually."

"The lawyer was a wall with better vocabulary."

Felix laughed—a real laugh, rusty, the laugh of a man who had spent nine years among strangers learning to keep it holstered. Then he grew quiet, and Ada watched him look down at the water with the particular expression she remembered from infirmary nights: humility wearing guilt's old coat.

"I used to tell myself I was only a nurse," he said. "That history happened elsewhere, to other people, and my job was to mop up after it. Then I started writing names down, and I told myself that was enough—just record, don't act. You were the one who acted. You put the names on the wire. I only put them on paper."

"And I only put them on wire," Ada said. "Bray only let them pass. Voss only printed them. Dunn only betrayed them, which counts as helping, if you squint and hold the paper sideways." She nudged him with her elbow. "Nobody carries the whole bridge, Felix. That's why it takes a regiment."

"A regiment," he repeated softly. He looked up the length of the span—at the institutional green paint, the new iron, the far bank where the road climbed toward town. "Is that why you came? To say it properly? To the water?"

"I came to say it properly," Ada said. "And to find out whether rivers remember." She nodded at the current, running dark and patient toward the sea. "They don't, as it turns out. So we'd best keep doing it ourselves."

* * *

They walked back up the hill together as the lamps came on, slowly, in no hurry, arguing pleasantly about whether Voss had gotten fat or merely prosperous—he claimed both, apparently, in his letters, with the pride of a man who had finally learned to eat regularly.

At her gate, Felix hesitated. Ada watched him wrestle with something, the way he used to wrestle with supply forms, patiently and slightly resentfully.

"The inquiry," he said at last. "They've summoned witnesses for June. Voss. Me. And—" he glanced at her, carefully— "the operator. The one who transmitted the names. If she can be located."

Ada looked at her cottage. At the garden she'd planted. At Mrs. Tansy's window, glowing nosily across the lane. Two valleys of quiet. A milk train every Tuesday.

Nine minutes, they had given Thomas. Nine minutes to decide a man's entire meaning.

Ada straightened her shoulders.

"Tell the inquiry," she said, "that the operator can be located. Tell her she'll need a new dress, and that she intends to read the names into the record herself—all of them, aloud, slowly, in transmission rhythm, and that anyone who tries to hurry her will answer to her knees."

Felix smiled, wrote nothing down—it was the sort of thing he trusted memory to hold—and offered his arm.

"Then we'd best catch the morning train," he said. "History's happening elsewhere again."

"Not this time," said Ada, and took it.

* * *

On the first page of a brand-new ledger, in a hand with an impatient loop on the *g*, a second entry waited beneath her name, its ink barely dry:

**Sergeant Bray — whereabouts unknown. Last heard heading south. If found, tell him the bridge held.**
