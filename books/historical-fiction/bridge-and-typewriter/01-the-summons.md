## 1. The Summons

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days nothing ever happened at the workshop of Herr Albrecht Fenn.

Oscar Ishikawa had catalogued this phenomenon over three years of apprenticeship. Mondays brought the week's deliveries and therefore a modest bustle. Wednesdays brought Herr Fenn's moods, which were their own kind of weather. Thursdays brought customers, or at least the occasional widow with a mantel clock that struck thirteen times at midnight and frightened her cat. Fridays brought wages and the small ceremony of sweeping the floor one final time before the Sabbath. But Tuesdays — Tuesdays were the day the workshop seemed to hold its breath, as though even time itself found the place dull.

So when the postman's knock came that particular Tuesday in November of 1911 — a double knock, urgent, the kind usually reserved for telegrams and deaths — Oscar set down his tweezers with the care of a man setting down something explosive.

Herr Fenn did not look up from the carriage clock he was regulating. He was seventy-one years old, built like a fireplug, and possessed of the conviction that any interruption shorter than a house fire was an insult to the workbench. "If it's the man about the cuckoo again," he said to the movement, "tell him the bird is not broken. The bird has never been broken. The *man* is broken."

"It's not the cuckoo man," Oscar said. "It's for you. It looks official."

"Everything looks official. That is how they get you." But he wiped his hands on his apron — a ritual Oscar had seen perhaps four times in three years, each preceding an event of genuine consequence — and took the letter.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, stamped with a seal Oscar didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a line, like an eye half-closed. The postmark read VERLOW'S LANDING. Oscar had never heard of Verlow's Landing. He suspected most people hadn't, which was possibly the point of it.

Herr Fenn read the letter twice. His eyebrows performed a small migration — first up, then together, then into the settled configuration that meant *work*.

"The observatory at Verlow's Landing," he said. "Their master clock is failing. Regulator of some consequence, apparently — observatory grade, keeps the town's time, all the rest of it." He sniffed. "They want me."

"You?" Oscar said, and then, quickly, because Herr Fenn's head turned with the slow menace of a lighthouse beam, "I mean — naturally they want you. Who else would they want?"

"No one," said Herr Fenn, "which is why I am not going."

Oscar waited. With Herr Fenn, waiting was not merely polite; it was structural. Sentences arrived the way trains arrived on rural lines — infrequently, but always carrying something.

"I am seventy-one years old," the old man said at last. "I am not riding four days through November to stand on a hill in the wind arguing with a pendulum. Pendulums argue. It is what they do. You will go."

Oscar's heart performed a maneuver that no escapement could have measured — a leap, a stall, a catch, a resumption at twice its former rate. He kept his face still. Three years of apprenticeship had taught him that too. "Go," he said carefully, "as in—"

"As in take the train tomorrow, assess the mechanism, and repair it if it is within your competence. If it is not within your competence, you will wire me, and I will laugh, and then I will tell you what to do anyway." He handed over the letter. "Read it yourself. Then pack."

* * *

Oscar read it eleven times that evening, sitting on his narrow bed in the room above the workshop with the gas turned low to save coin.

*To the Master Horologist Fenn,* it began, which alone would have made the old man insufferable for a month. *The master regulator of the Verlow Observatory has developed irregularities we are unable to diagnose locally. We are given to understand your establishment maintains the highest standards. Time here is not a trivial matter; the whole town sets itself by our clock, and lately it has been lying to us.*

*Lately it has been lying to us.* Oscar turned that phrase over. Clocks did not lie. That was rather the entire theology of clocks. A clock might be wrong, but it was wrong honestly — it erred by mechanism, not by intent. A clock that lied was a clock that had been made to lie, and who on earth would make a town's master clock lie?

The letter was signed *Ada Verlow, Keeper.* Beneath the signature, in different ink and a smaller hand, someone had added: *Please come soon. — J.*

He liked that. He couldn't have said why. Perhaps because it was honest where the letter was formal — the formal part asking for a craftsman, the small part simply asking for help. There was a whole philosophy of horology in that distinction, Oscar thought, though he was wise enough to know nobody wanted to hear it.

He packed by lamplight: two shirts, the good coat, his tool roll wrapped in oilcloth, a tin of his mother's tea leaves that he had been rationing since her death two winters past and would probably still be rationing at forty. At the bottom of the bag, after a moment's debate with himself, went the notebook — the private one, not the workshop ledger — in which he wrote down things he noticed. Most of the entries were technical. Some were not. On the last used page, dated the previous spring, stood a sentence he had written after closing the shop one evening and standing in the empty street feeling the particular loneliness of a young man whose entire world fit inside four walls:

*I would like, someday, to be someone things happen to.*

It sounded childish written down. He almost tore the page out. He did not tear the page out. Instead he closed the notebook on it, the way one closes a door on a room one intends to return to.

* * *

In the morning he presented himself to Herr Fenn for the traditional pre-departure address, which Oscar had observed delivered to two previous apprentices before their first solo commissions. Both apprentices had returned changed men, according to workshop legend — one had come back engaged, the other had come back with a mustache. Oscar hoped for something in between.

"Sit," said Herr Fenn, which meant stand near the bench and don't touch anything.

The old man selected a loupe from the drawer, examined it, put it back, and folded his hands. "You are going to a strange town," he began, "to fix a great clock. Here is what you must remember. One: the customer is always wrong about what is broken. Two: the clock is never wrong about what is broken. Three—" he raised a finger, and his voice dropped into the register he reserved for scripture, "—when you open the back of any clock, you are opening the back of whoever built it, whoever wound it, and whoever neglected it. A mechanism is a confession. Read it kindly, but read all of it."

"Yes, Master."

"Do not call me Master out there. Out there you are the horologist. Say it until you believe it." He scowled, which was his version of tenderness. "You have good hands, Ishikawa. Better than mine were at your age, and mine were very good indeed. What you do not have yet is patience for people, only patience for gears. Gears forgive. People do not. Try to remember which species you belong to."

"That's generous, coming from you."

"I am aware. Do not grow accustomed to it." He turned back to his bench, which was dismissal, but then, as Oscar reached the door, added without turning around: "The tea. Take less of it than you think you need and more than you can afford. And boy —" a pause long enough that Oscar actually stopped — "come back. Or don't. But send word either way. I am too old to wonder."

Oscar stood in the doorway with his bag on his shoulder and understood that he had just been told, in the only dialect Herr Fenn spoke fluently, that he would be missed. It filled him with a warmth so sudden and inconvenient that he had to pretend to check the door latch.

"I'll wire you from Verlow's Landing," he said.

"Wire me the diagnosis, not the scenery. I don't pay you to have impressions."

But as Oscar stepped out into the cold bright morning, he could have sworn — though he would never testify to it — that the old man was smiling at the carriage clock.

* * *

The train left at noon from the eastbound platform, and Oscar spent the ride doing what he always did on trains, which was listening to the rhythm of the wheels and trying to identify their tempo. This train ran slightly fast, he decided — a 4/4 with the last beat clipped, probably worn driving boxes — and he amused himself for the first hour composing, mentally, the letter of complaint he would never send to the railway company.

The landscape unspooled: factory smoke giving way to bare fields, fields giving way to the gray-brown stubble of late autumn, and always, far off, the dark line of hills where the map promised rivers. Other passengers came and went. A salesman of agricultural implements tried to sell him a newfangled seed drill and accepted defeat gracefully. An old woman knitted something the color of bruises and told him, unprompted, that he had honest hands, "like a workman in a painting," which he decided to receive as a compliment.

Somewhere past the second change of trains, as dusk came down blue and early, a fellow passenger — a commercial traveler with a sample case full of buttons — asked where he was bound.

"Verlow's Landing," Oscar said.

The button man laughed. "Nobody goes to Verlow's Landing. You *end up* there. Like a coin behind a sofa cushion." He chewed his cigar thoughtfully. "River town. Used to be mills. Mills mostly dead now. They've got an observatory up on the hill, though — funny thing, an observatory in a mill town. Old Verlow money. Built it back in the seventies so the town could say it had the truest time in the county." He winked. "Truest time! As if there's such a thing. Time's whatever the railroad says it is now, friend. Standardized. They're running lines through every valley in the state, and where the rails go, the timetables follow, and where the timetables go—" he tapped his temple— "everything else follows too. Mark my words. In ten years there'll be one time in America and it'll live in Chicago."

Oscar smiled politely and said something noncommittal, but the phrase snagged in him like a thread on a nail. *Time's whatever the railroad says it is.* It was meant as a joke about progress. It sat in his chest like a warning.

He got off at the junction well after dark, in a wind that smelled of iron and coming snow, and found the coach that would carry him the last leg — a single horse-drawn conveyance, more lantern than vehicle, driven by a man wrapped in enough scarves to constitute a minor wardrobe.

"Verlow's Landing?" Oscar asked.

"Everyone on this coach is going to Verlow's Landing," the driver said, "because everyone on this coach is me and you." He looked Oscar over — the bag, the tool roll, the coat a size too optimistic for the weather. "Clock fella?"

"How did you know?"

"Letter come through the office Tuesday. Whole town knows the clock's sick. Whole town's been arguing about what time it *really* is since September." He clucked the horse forward. "Climb aboard, clock fella. Bridge run takes an hour if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

The driver glanced up at a sky the color of wet slate, where the first few flakes were already coming down, fat and unhurried, like guests arriving early to a party.

"Then," he said cheerfully, "you'll get to know us better than you planned."

The coach rolled out onto the iron bridge just as the snow thickened, and below them, black and immense, the river moved under its first skin of ice — patient, unhurried, keeping its own time.

Oscar pressed his face to the window and felt the notebook in his bag against his hip, and the sentence inside it, and the strange lightness of a young man leaving everything familiar behind in pursuit of a broken clock in a town he couldn't find on a map.

*Someone things happen to,* he thought. *Well. Here I am.*

He did not yet know that the bridge behind them would close by morning.
