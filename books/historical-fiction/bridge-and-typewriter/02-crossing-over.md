## 2. Crossing Over

The iron bridge sang.

That was Oscar's first thought, anyway — that the sound was a kind of singing, a low harmonic hum that rose up through the coach's wheels and into the floor and the bench and finally into his own ribs, as if the whole structure were one enormous tuning fork struck by the winter wind. He had spent his life around mechanisms small enough to hold in two hands, and here was a mechanism the size of a cathedral, all trusses and rivets and girders receding into the snow-gloom behind them, still humming its single long note as though it had been ringing since the day it was built and simply hadn't been allowed to stop.

He pressed his gloved palm flat against the window glass, as though he might feel the vibration through it.

"You'll want to close your mouth before something flies in," said the driver's voice from somewhere ahead, muffled by canvas and weather. "River's not going anywhere."

The river disagreed. Below, through the lattice of the bridge work, Oscar could see it moving — a vast dark muscle sliding under its new skin of ice, breaking out in slow black seams where the current ran too fast to freeze. The ice on the shallows near the banks looked solid enough, gray-white and confident, but out in the middle the river kept its own counsel, rolling and folding beneath a thin ceiling of frost-fog that hung over it like breath held too long.

Snow came down in earnest now, fat flakes that hit the window and stayed, so that Oscar watched the town through a hole that kept shrinking. He wiped it with his sleeve and it shrank again.

Verlow's Landing assembled itself out of the murk piece by piece: first a water tower with a list to it, like a man leaning to hear better; then rooftops, ranked along a riverside street; then the mills — three of them, maybe four, their long faces turned toward the water, every window dark, every wheel dry. A millrace ran beside the nearest building, choked with slush. A sign hung from a bracket over what might have been a mill office, swinging gently, and Oscar could read only the word *EST.* before the angle changed and the rest went with it.

*Shuttered,* he thought. *All of it shuttered.* And yet the houses along the street showed lamplight in their windows, and there was smoke from chimneys, and a boy led a horse across the road ahead of the coach with the bored confidence of someone who did this every evening of his life. So the town lived. It just didn't live in the buildings by the river anymore. Whatever Verlow's Landing did now, it did farther up the slope, in smaller rooms, with fewer windows facing the water.

And above everything, on its hill at the north end of town, stood the observatory.

Even half-dissolved in snow it was unmistakable — a squat stone drum with a dome on top, the dome's shutter open a few inches like a squinting eye. A clock tower rose from one side of it, slender and stubborn, and Oscar caught himself leaning forward in his seat, doing the thing he always did when he saw a clock tower, which was to try to guess its movement from the shape of its case and the length of its pendulum housing. Four-second beat, probably. Maybe five. Big enough to matter. Big enough to have brought him four hundred miles through increasingly bad weather.

*Someone things happen to,* he reminded himself. He had written the sentence in his notebook on the second day of travel and had read it perhaps forty times since, which he felt was reasonable for a sentence carrying the entire weight of his future.

The girl across the coach had noticed him looking.

She was perhaps sixteen, wrapped in a coat that had been good once and would be good again if the family fortunes turned, with a knit cap pulled low and a book shut on her thumb to keep her place. She had boarded two stops back without introduction, sat opposite him without ceremony, and read her book through the whole of the last leg with the fierce concentration of someone guarding territory. Now she had abandoned the book to watch him watch her town.

"You're the clock man," she said.

It wasn't a question, which surprised him until he reasoned it out. "Word travels."

"Word doesn't travel. Mrs. Petch telegraphs everything." She said this with an air of mild apology for the human race. "You're for the observatory. The master clock's gone strange."

"Has it?"

"It strikes eleven at eleven past," she said. "Everyone says so. Everyone's said so since October." She pronounced *everyone* as though it were a legal entity she personally found tiresome.

"That's very useful," Oscar said. "Eleven minutes, or eleven minutes fast or slow?"

"How would I know? I don't carry a watch that agrees with it." She considered him a moment longer, the way one might consider a coin of uncertain mint. Then, apparently deciding he'd passed some private inspection, she leaned forward and lowered her voice, though there was no one else in the coach but a sleeping farmer under a tarpaulin of newspapers. "I'm Juniper Kessler. My aunt keeps the archive. Ada Verlow. You'll be staying above us — don't look surprised, Mrs. Petch arranged it before you'd finished eating breakfast, wherever you were when you ate breakfast."

"Oscar Ishikawa," he said. "Pleased to—"

"Juniper," she repeated, in the tone of one marking an item off a list, and returned to her book.

Oscar settled back. The bridge hummed. Snow accumulated on the sill outside the glass. He began composing, silently, the opening of a letter he would never send to his master — *the journey is uneventful except for the bridge, which sings* — and got as far as admiring his own phrasing when Juniper spoke again without looking up.

"Don't ask about the fire."

"The fire?"

"The year the records burned. Don't ask anyone about it." She turned a page with unnecessary force. "Not my aunt, not the hotel people, nobody. Especially not old Mr. Coyle at the feed store. If you mention it he'll tell you everything he thinks he remembers and none of it will be true, and then he'll follow you around for a week."

"I wasn't aware there had been a fire," Oscar said carefully.

"There was a fire." Her eyes flicked up, sharp and gray and older than sixteen. "Everybody knows there was a fire. What they don't agree on is anything after that."

The coach reached the far end of the bridge, and the singing stopped all at once, the way a held note stops, leaving a silence with a shape to it. Wheels met packed earth. The town slid closer.

Oscar looked at her. She looked back with the frank patience of someone waiting to see whether the stranger was going to be sensible or another problem.

"What year?" he asked.

"Eighty-six," she said. "The grant office. Everything in it." And then, before he could arrange the next question into words: "That's the part you don't ask about. Not the fire. What came after."

* * *

The coach set him down at the foot of Bridge Street, under a lamp that had just decided to be lit, and drove on toward whatever barns coaches drive toward, its lamps dwindling to two orange sparks in the falling snow. Juniper had leapt down before the wheels stopped, called something over her shoulder — "Supper's at seven; Aunt Ada eats punctually and so do you now" — and vanished up a side lane with her book clamped under her arm.

So Oscar stood alone in Verlow's Landing for the first time, holding his bag, while the snow came down thick and soft and erased the street behind him almost as quickly as the coach had crossed it.

He was not, he reflected, unused to being alone in new places. He had been apprenticed at thirteen and sent to install regulators in banks and courthouses up and down the line since seventeen. But those had always been working visits with return tickets in his pocket, and the towns had all been towns of the ordinary kind, where the clocks were wrong because nobody cared and the people were friendly because strangers were rare and pleasant. This felt different. This felt like arriving somewhere that had been listening for footsteps.

Bridge Street climbed away from the river in a gentle grade, lined with the fronts of businesses whose signs he read one by one as he passed: a general store, glowing and busy; a barber's, dark; the feed store, dark, with a dog asleep on the porch who opened one eye, assessed him, and closed it again with prejudice. Above the store roofs, the hill rose, and the observatory stood against a sky gone the color of pewter, its clock face faintly luminous.

He checked it out of pure habit, tilting his head back in the snow.

His own watch said six minutes to six. The tower said nine past.

*Eleven minutes,* he thought. *Fast, then.* And felt, in the same instant, the peculiar thrill he had never learned to be embarrassed by — the thrill of a mechanism misbehaving in public, of a secret told by hands and gears to everyone who would look up. Somewhere inside that stone tower a train of wheels was lying to an entire town, steadily, four hundred times a minute, and no one had been able to catch it in the act.

Well. He had come four hundred miles to catch it.

The general store door opened as he passed, spilling warmth and lamplight, and a broad woman in an apron leaned out and called, "You'll be the clock fellow! Mrs. Petch says the archive house is the third past the church!" and withdrew again before he could answer, taking most of the warmth with her. A moment later the door reopened. "And there's bread if you're quick!"

He was not quick; he was staring up at the tower again, doing sums. Eleven minutes was too much for wear alone. Wear stole seconds; it did not steal a full quarter hour over months. Something had slipped. Something had been altered, or something had failed in a way that failed *forward*, and either possibility meant the fault was structural, interesting, possibly unique —

"You're letting all the heat out standing there," said the store woman, who had reappeared with a heel of bread wrapped in paper. She put it in his hand the way one feeds a stray cat. "Go on. Third past the church. Blue door."

The church was easy to find; it was the only building on the hill with a steeple competing honestly with the observatory. The third house past it was narrow and tall and painted a blue that had probably been cheerful twenty years ago, with lamplight in the downstairs windows and a sign beside the door, hand-lettered and dignified: **VERLOW ARCHIVE — RECORDS, DEEDS & CORRESPONDENCE, KEPT & COPIED.**

Beneath the lettering, in smaller script, as though added later by a different and more cautious hand: *Inquiries by appointment.*

Oscar knocked.

The door was opened by a woman of perhaps fifty-five, small and straight-backed, with white hair pinned up and the particular calm of someone who has answered many doors and expected this one. Her eyes went to his bag, then his face, then his bag again.

"Mr. Ishikawa," she said. "You're early. The coach isn't due until the half hour."

"The bridge sings," Oscar said, which was not an explanation, and seemed to satisfy her anyway.

"Ada Verlow," she said, shaking his hand with a grip that noted, filed, and approved the calluses. "Come in before you turn into an ornament. Juniper! Your boarder arrived by flying bridge, apparently."

From upstairs came a thump, a pause, and a voice: "Did he ask about the fire?"

"Juniper."

"I'm *ascertaining.*"

"He has been in town nine minutes," Ada Verlow said to the stairwell, in the tone of a woman who has been saying similar things for years, and turned to Oscar with an apologetic half-smile. "My niece anticipates conversations. Pay her no mind. Supper is at seven; your room is at the top of the stairs; the observatory is yours from eight tomorrow morning, and the keeper's key—" She produced it from her apron pocket, a long iron thing worn smooth, and hesitated for exactly one heartbeat before handing it over. "The keeper's key opens the clock room and the stairs. There is a drawer in the cabinet under the east window that does not concern you. Anything else in that room concerns you entirely, and I am told you are the best apprentice Halloran ever turned out, which he denies loudly and confirms in every other sentence."

Oscar took the key. It was heavier than it needed to be, and cold as the river. "And the drawer?"

"Doesn't concern you," said Ada Verlow pleasantly, and showed him the stairs.

The room was narrow and clean, with a bed, a washstand, a window, and a chair, and from the window — when he set his bag down and leaned to the glass — he could see the whole of the town laid out below in lamplight and falling snow, the dead mills along the shore, the black seam of the unfrozen river, and the observatory on its hill with the tower clock burning pale above it all.

Nine past, it still said. Sixteen past, said his watch, when he checked.

Somewhere down there, he thought, was a town that had lost a building full of records to a fire, and gained eleven minutes it hadn't asked for, and arranged both facts — he was somehow sure of it, sure the way you are sure of a note sung slightly flat — with great care.

*Don't ask about the year the records burned.*

He took out his notebook, sat on the bed, and wrote:

*Verlow's Landing. Clock runs 11 min fast since Oct. Too much for wear — alteration or failure. J.K.: fire in '86, grant office, "what came after" is the part no one asks about. Drawer under east window. Ask nothing.*

He looked at the last three words for a long moment. Then, feeling the small hot pleasure of a young man committing his first act of quiet rebellion, he added:

*(But find out.)*

Below, the river moved under its ice, patient and unhurried. On the hill, the observatory clock prepared to strike the hour — eleven strokes, though the hour was only ten — and Oscar Ishikawa, stranger in a town with a buried year, fell asleep with the light still on and did not hear a single one of them.
