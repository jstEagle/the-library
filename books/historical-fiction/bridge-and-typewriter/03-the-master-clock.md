## 3. The Master Clock

The observatory door was oak, and the knocker on it was brass shaped like a comet, and Oscar stood before both for a full minute rehearsing his introduction.

*Ishikawa. Apprentice, Halloran & Sons, Clockmakers. Sent regarding the master clock.* He practiced it in the voice he used for adults — level, unhurried, a voice that belonged to someone with more years and fewer ink stains on his cuffs. Then he knocked, and the sound went up through the tower like a stone dropped down a well, and before the echo finished, the door opened.

The woman who answered was perhaps fifty-five, tall in the way of people who have spent decades looking upward, with a spine that curved very slightly back rather than forward. Her hair was silver at the temples and pinned with what Oscar recognized, after a startled second, as a broken pocket-watch case — the works removed, the engraved cover worn as ornament. Her eyes were gray and direct and did not so much look at him as take an inventory.

"You're the boy," she said.

"Oscar Ishikawa. Apprentice, Halloran and—"

"I know who you are. You're the only person who's come up that road since Tuesday who wasn't a crow." She stepped back to let him in. "Ada Verlow. I keep this place. Come in before the wind does."

The entry hall smelled of lamp oil and cold stone and, beneath it, something green — dried herbs hanging from a beam, which struck Oscar as an odd thing to find in an observatory until he decided everything about Verlow's Landing would be odd and he might as well begin collecting examples.

She led him up a spiral stair so narrow his toolbag scraped the wall on both sides, and she talked as she climbed, in the manner of someone who had been alone with her own voice for a long time and had made peace with it.

"The clock stopped Tuesday night. Nine minutes past eleven, by my watch, though I suppose we can't agree on whose watch is right anymore, can we, or you'd be out of work." A dry laugh, more breath than sound. "It slowed for three days first. Gaining, then losing, then a sort of hesitation every seventh swing, like an old man crossing a street. I wound it properly. I always wind it properly. Whatever's wrong is inside."

"Then it's likely the escapement," Oscar said, "or the suspension."

She paused on a step and looked back at him, one eyebrow raised. "You diagnose already? Most of your trade wants to charge me for the diagnosis."

"My master says a clock tells you what's wrong if you're quiet long enough to hear it."

"Hm." The eyebrow came down. Something in her face shifted, a fraction of warmth entering like light under a door. "Your master sounds like a man I'd have liked, once. Well. Here."

* * *

They emerged into the clock room, and Oscar forgot every rehearsal, every introduction, every careful adult thing he had prepared.

He had seen master clocks before. He had cleaned them, oiled them, stood on ladders beside their cases with a rag and a tin of grease while Mr. Halloran senior did the actual work. He understood, in the abstract, what a master clock was: the heart of a building, the standard against which every lesser timepiece was set.

But he had never stood inside one.

The room itself was the case. The mechanism rose through the center of the tower like a skeleton of brass and iron three stories tall — the great driving weight hanging in its shaft below, chains thick as his wrist running up past galleries and catwalks to the drum, the pendulum sweeping above him in slow, patient arcs, its rod longer than any he had ever seen, its bob a disc of polished iron wider than his arms could span. Light came down through the lantern windows at the top of the tower, lay across the works in long winter slants, and turned the whole assembly into something between machinery and cathedral.

"Oh," Oscar said. It came out without permission.

"Yes," Ada Verlow said. "That's generally the response."

He walked to the base of the frame without deciding to. His hand rose toward the movement and stopped an inch short — the discipline beaten into him across four years: *never touch what you haven't been given permission to touch* — and Ada saw the restraint and, apparently, approved of it.

"Go ahead. She hasn't been handled kindly lately, but she doesn't bite. Unlike some towns I could mention."

Oscar touched the frame. Cold iron, trembling faintly with the pendulum's rhythm even now, even stopped — no, not stopped; he looked up sharply. The pendulum hung still, but the whole structure held a tension, like an orchestra between movements. He put his ear against the frame post, closed his eyes, and listened.

"What do you hear?" Ada asked, softer now.

"Heat," Oscar said. "The metal's still contracting from when it ran. And—" he listened harder— "the weight cable. It shifted when it stopped. It's off the drum true by maybe a hair. Someone let it run down fast instead of stopping it square."

"I stopped it," Ada said. There was something defensive in it. "I pulled the maintaining power and let the train run out. That's how you stop a clock."

"That's how you stop a small clock," Oscar said, and then heard himself, and winced. "I mean — begging pardon, ma'am. On a movement this size you want to pin the train first, hold the wheels, then ease the weight. Otherwise the last wheel takes the shock, and—" he pointed up, tracing the line of the going train— "whatever failed, failing under load leaves worse marks than failing quiet. It'll tell us a lie about how it died."

Ada was silent for a moment. When he glanced at her, she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read — surprise, yes, but underneath it something older and wearier, as if she had expected to be disappointed and was recalculating.

"Well," she said finally. "Halloran sent a boy who corrects me within five minutes of arriving. Either he's a fool or he trusts you more than he lets on."

"It's mostly that I don't stop talking," Oscar said. "My master says it'll be the death of me."

"Let's hope he's wrong twice in one week." She gestured up the catwalk. "Come. If you're going to accuse my stopping technique, you can at least see the escapement while you do it."

* * *

The catwalk circled the movement at the second level, iron plate bolted to the tower walls, and it flexed just enough underfoot that Oscar felt the building breathing. At the top of the frame, housed in its own glass-fronted cabinet against drafts, sat the escapement — the deadbeat, the anchor and escape wheel, the part of any clock where time was actually cut into pieces, tooth by tooth, swing by swing.

Even stopped, it was beautiful. The escape wheel's teeth were hand-finished, each one chamfered by someone who had cared, the wheel's spokes tapered like feathers. The pallets of the anchor gleamed where they had been polished — recently, Oscar thought, or at least maintained with attention. This was not a neglected clock. Someone had loved it for a long time.

"She was built in 'sixty-one," Ada said, standing beside him with her hands folded. "By Voss and Kettering, of Basel. There were two of them made to this pattern. Ours and one in a courthouse in Sacramento that burned. So this is the only one, now. Which makes every tooth irreplaceable, Mr. Ishikawa, in case that affects your approach."

"It does," Oscar said honestly. He crouched at the cabinet, eye-level with the anchor, and looked without touching for a long while. "When did it start hesitating? Exactly."

"Three days before it stopped. Every seventh swing, a catch. Like a hitch in a heartbeat."

"Seventh." He nodded slowly. "That's a wheel count. Something with a period of seven teeth engagement — a damaged tooth repeating on cycle. Or a bent pivot arbor letting the anchor drop late once per revolution." He reached into his bag for his loupe, screwed it into his eye, and leaned close to the escape wheel.

And stopped.

There was something on the wheel hub. Not damage — an addition. A small cam, a shaped brass lobe, fixed to the arbor behind the wheel where no functional part belonged, connected by a slender linkage rod to a sliding weight on the pendulum rod above. It was old work, older than Oscar, done well, done seamlessly — done to look original. But it wasn't in any Voss and Kettering pattern he had studied. He had studied all of them. He had copied plates from their catalogs onto scraps of paper and kept the scraps in his coat lining like other boys kept pictures of actresses.

"Ma'am," he said carefully. "What's this?"

For a moment — just a moment, less than a swing of the pendulum could measure — Ada Verlow went entirely still. Not the stillness of someone thinking. The stillness of someone deciding.

"That?" Her voice was easy. Too easy. "Old regulation work. Some keeper fifty years ago with ideas about temperature compensation. Half these towers are full of somebody's clever nonsense. It's disconnected. Leave it be."

Oscar looked at it again. Disconnected work does not have polished bearing points. Disconnected work does not show wear on the linkage pin — fresh, bright wear, wear from motion, recent motion.

But he was sixteen, alone, three days' travel from anyone who owed him anything, and the woman owned the roof over his head until the bridge reopened. He said, "Yes, ma'am," and moved his loupe to the escape teeth.

* * *

The fault, when he found it, was almost disappointingly ordinary: the seventh tooth of the escape wheel had a hairline crack at its root, invisible until tilted to the lamplight, letting the tooth flex minutely under the pallet's drop. Flex, release, snap back — the hitch. It would have to come out, which meant the wheel would have to come out, which meant the whole train above the great wheel would need supporting, pinning, easing. Three days' work at least, delicate work, the kind of work he had only ever done under supervision.

"I'll need to strip the going train to third wheel," he told Ada, straightening. "I can make a new tooth — the wheel has to go to a workshop for that normally, but I can silver-solder and file it here, if there's heat and good light. It won't be pretty. It'll be right, but it won't be pretty."

"Pretty is for parlors," Ada said. "Right is for clocks. You'll have the workshop below — the old grant office room. Stove, bench, north light."

Something flickered in Oscar at the word *grant*, but before he could ask, Ada had turned away toward the stair, and then turned back, as if remembering an obligation.

"There's one more thing. In the base of the case, there's a drawer. It holds the maintenance ledgers — the clock's own records. Windings, regulations, corrections, keeper's notes going back to 'sixty-one. You may need them, to know what's been done to the mechanism over the years. But—" and here her voice acquired a precise, deliberate edge, like a blade being set down on a table rather than used— "there are other books in that drawer that are not the clock's business, and they are not your concern either. You'll take out what you need for the mechanism and leave the rest exactly where it lies. Do you understand me, Mr. Ishikawa?"

It was the most words she had aimed directly at him since the door. Her hands, folded neatly at her waist, were perfectly still — too perfectly, the stillness of hands being prevented from doing something.

"Yes, ma'am," Oscar said. "Not my concern."

"Good." The edge vanished; the reasonable woman returned, as quickly as a cloud crossing off the sun. "Lunch is at one. Juniper will ring the bell and you'll come down whether you've found God or the fault. Which floor is the workshop on—"

"The grant office," Oscar said. "You said."

"So I did." She started down the stairs, then paused. "Mr. Ishikawa. Do you read much?"

The question arrived sideways, from nowhere, and Oscar blinked. "Some. Novels, mostly. Anything with a shipwreck in it."

"Hm. Novels." She said the word the way another woman might say *weeds*. "Well. Ships sink in archives too. Mind the seventh tooth."

And she was gone, her footsteps winding away down the spiral, leaving Oscar alone on the catwalk with the greatest clock he had ever seen and the growing, itching certainty that he had just been handed two problems instead of one.

* * *

He worked until the light failed.

The first day's labor was preparation: rigging the supporting pins, tying off the train wheels with soft cord, easing the tension out of the great weight cable inch by inch with a Spanish windlass he improvised from the tower's own spare block. It was the kind of work he loved without reservation — work where the stakes were visible and the rules were absolute and the material answered effort with honesty. Iron does not flatter. Brass does not lie. After four years of being the smallest, youngest, most-overheard person in the Halloran workshop, there was a mercy in materials that never pretended.

He found the maintenance ledgers in the base drawer, exactly as promised, and took out only the thin cloth-bound log marked for the mechanism, leaving the stack of leather volumes behind it untouched, exactly where they lay. Mostly. He noted, in passing, purely as a professional observation, that the leather volumes were of different ages, and that the newest of them had a date range stamped on its spine that overlapped strangely with the older ones, as if two books were trying to tell the same years different stories. He noted it and filed it away in the same inner pocket where he kept things he wasn't supposed to notice, which was, by now, quite a full pocket.

The maintenance log itself was a revelation. Page after page of keepers' hands — a fine copperplate from the sixties, a cramped pencil scrawl from the seventies, and then, from 1881 onward, a hand Oscar recognized without ever having met its owner: upright, disciplined, faintly ornamental. Ada Verlow had kept this clock for forty years. Forty years of daily entries, windings and cleanings, and Oscar read them the way other people read letters from home.

Then he reached the 1890s, and the entries changed.

Not in handwriting — hers still, steady as ever. In content. Where the earlier decades recorded regulation in seconds, the later pages recorded it in minutes. *Regulated +4 min, seasonal. Restored −4 min, seasonal.* Over and over, spring and autumn, year after year, like a tide chart of small dishonesties. And beside several of the autumn adjustments, in the margin, in the same upright hand but fainter, pressed lighter, as if written while holding one's breath: *+4.*

Four minutes. Seasonally. For thirty years.

Oscar sat back on his stool and looked up at the dark shape of the escapement overhead, and the little brass cam on its arbor, and he thought about what a cam on an escape wheel arbor could do. He thought about what an observatory clock was *for* in a river town — that it set the time for the courthouse, the mills, the ferry schedules, the railroad telegraph offices, the notary stamps, the ledger clerks. He thought about timestamps. About documents dated and timed against the town's one official standard. About what four minutes could buy, or bury, if you knew which season to shift them into.

Then he thought about a locked drawer, and a woman's too-still hands, and *not your concern,* and he realized that Ada Verlow had not been protecting the clock from him.

She had been protecting him from knowing what the clock had been made to do — or protecting herself from hearing it said aloud.

The stove in the corner ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, in the house attached to the archive, a bell rang — one o'clock, or near it, though whose one o'clock, Oscar now wondered, and by what authority. He closed the maintenance log, put it back precisely where it lived, and sat a moment longer in the cooling workshop with his hands around a cold cup of tea.

Outside, the snow was coming down thicker, fat flakes spiraling past the north window. Downriver, faint in the white murk, he could just make out the iron span of the bridge, and the small figures — two of them, unfamiliar, in city coats — standing at its closed gate, talking to the ferryman, gesturing at the ice.

They were pointing at the town. They were pointing, as far as he could tell through the falling snow, at the archive.

Oscar drained the tea, picked up his tools, and went down to lunch wondering, with a sensation he could not yet name, whether he had arrived to fix the clock — or whether the clock had been waiting all these years for someone to fix what it had been forced to record.
