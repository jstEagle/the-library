## 4. Bridge Closed

The storm announced itself the way a dishonest witness does — quietly at first, then all at once and with great conviction.

Oscar had been at the observatory since morning, elbow-deep in the master clock's movement, when the light in the tower windows changed. It went from white to gray to a sort of bruised pewter, and Ada Verlow paused on the stairs with a lamp she no longer needed and said, "You'll want to see this," in the tone of someone inviting a stranger to watch a door close.

He climbed to the observation deck with his coat half-on and looked down at the valley, and there it was: the river gone the color of old solder, the iron bridge spanning it in one long dark line — and beyond the bridge, coming up the valley like something being unrolled, the snow.

Not falling so much as arriving. A wall of it, soft-edged but absolute, swallowing the far hills first, then the mills, then the near shore.

"The telegraph office sent a boy up an hour ago," Ada said beside him, her voice raised against a wind that had begun to lean on them. "Northern Pacific is pulling their maintenance crews off the bridge. They'll shut the gates before nightfall."

"Shut them how?"

"Iron gates, both ends. Padlocked. It isn't the snow, Mr. Ishikawa — snow, we'd shrug at. It's the ice. When the river starts to jam, the current pushes the floes under the spans, and if a pier takes a bad strike with a train on the bridge..." She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. The gesture she made instead — a small, flat motion of her hand, like something being leveled — was eloquent enough.

"But I came across yesterday."

"You did. And yesterday the bridge was open." She said it without apology, which he would come to learn was simply how Ada Verlow said everything. "The ferry might still get you across before it ices solid, if you run."

He ran. He was twenty-three years old, and he ran down the observatory hill with his toolbag banging against his knee, past shuttered mill windows already going white, down to the landing where the ferry sat low and grumbling in the current with its rail slicked black and the pilot shouting that he was casting off, casting off *now*, and Oscar reached the end of the gangway just as two deckhands hauled it in.

He stood on the bank and watched the ferry wallow out into the gray water, and the pilot did not look back at him, and after a while neither did Oscar, because looking back felt like agreeing to be humiliated twice by the same view.

The first flakes caught in his eyelashes.

"Well," he said, to nobody, to the river, to the whole unreasonable arrangement of the world. "Well."

* *

By full dark the wind had organized itself into something with opinions, and Verlow's Landing had withdrawn indoors the way a turtle does, completely and without discussion. Oscar walked back up from the waterfront through snow that fell sideways, past the general store (dark), the hotel (dark), the saloon (not dark, but its door opened as he passed to emit warmth, piano, and three faces that examined him with frank curiosity before deciding he wasn't worth letting the cold in for).

The archive building sat where the town began its climb toward the observatory hill — a long, low stone structure with deep sills and a slate roof and, above the door, carved letters he could only feel rather than read in the failing light: VERLOW LANDING FREE ARCHIVE & RECORDS, 1887.

Ada answered his knock already dressed for sympathy: shawl, lamp, and an expression suggesting she had been expecting him since noon.

"The ferry?"

"Gone. Without me. Rather decisively, in fact."

"And the bridge?"

"Gated, according to a boy I met running the other direction." He pushed wet hair off his forehead. "I'm stranded, Mrs. Verlow. Until thaw, possibly longer. I can't even fetch my trunk from the hotel until this lets up, and my tools are—" he lifted the bag, which had somehow remained at his side through all of it, the way tools do when nothing else will stay put, "—these are what I have."

Ada considered him for a moment. Snow accumulated on both their shoulders in the doorway, and somewhere behind her, deeper in the building, a clock ticked — not the master clock, something smaller, domestic, insistent.

"You'll take the room upstairs," she said. "It's a storeroom, technically, but it has a bed, a stove, and a window, which is more than most storerooms can claim. Board's included. In exchange you'll keep your boots by the door, because Juniper just mopped, and you'll try not to look tragic at meals. Tragedy is contagious in a small house."

"I— are you certain? I can pay—"

"You can pay when the thaw comes and the bridge opens and you've finished the clock," she said, already turning inside. "Come in out of the weather, Mr. Ishikawa. You're letting the century in."

* *

The room was exactly as promised: bed, stove, window, and shelves of boxed records along one wall, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, patient as sleeping livestock. Someone had left a washbasin and a candle stub and, on the pillow, a folded blanket that smelled faintly of cedar. Oscar hung his soaked coat by the stove, watched steam lift off it like a departing spirit, and let himself, finally and fully, feel the shape of what had happened to him.

Trapped.

He had come to make things happen — to be, for once, a person things happened *to* — and the world had obliged with a enthusiasm he now regretted requesting. He was locked on the wrong side of a river in a town whose mills were dead, whose hotel was dark, whose one open door had looked at him and closed again, lodged above a building full of papers nobody wanted him reading, employed to fix a clock that a woman wouldn't let him fully see. His master would laugh. His master would say, *This is what happens when you pray for an interesting life, boy; interesting is not the same as good.*

And yet.

He lay on the bed after a supper of stew and bread taken at a table set for three — the third chair occupied by a girl who had looked up from her book, assessed him in a single sweep, and returned to her pages with the finality of a judge dismissing a case. Juniper, Ada had named her. My niece. She had said perhaps nine words total, four of which were "pass the salt, please," and all nine of which had been delivered with an exactness that suggested the other words existed and were being held in reserve.

Now, in his storeroom, Oscar listened to the storm work at the shutters and found, to his mild irritation, that his frustration kept curdling into something else. Something less respectable.

Curiosity.

Because here was a thing he had noticed and couldn't unnotice: the ledgers in the drawer Ada kept locked were not the only records in this building. There were hundreds of boxes on these very shelves. An entire town's paper memory, stacked around his bed. And the town had been strange about its own memory from the moment he arrived. The girl on the coach — *don't ask about the year the records burned*, she'd said, and hadn't said it like gossip; she'd said it like a warning about a hole in the ice. Ada's hands on the drawer key. The way conversation stopped, politely, whenever the eighteen-eighties came within hailing distance, the way a household stops talking when someone walks past a door they think is shut.

He got up, lit the candle stub, and regarded the nearest box. Brown paper. String. No label facing outward, though he could see pencil marks on the underside where it faced the shelf, as though whoever had packaged these had preferred the information to face inward. Toward the wall. Away from casual eyes.

He did not touch it. He was a guest, and a clockmaker, and a coward in roughly equal measure at present. But he looked. Looking cost nothing.

Behind him, the floorboard creaked.

Juniper stood in his doorway in a dressing gown and bare feet, arms crossed, holding a second candle, and she looked at him looking at the box with an expression of profound and weary disappointment, as though he were the fourth or fifth apprentice clockmaker to stand in that exact spot having that exact thought, and she had been assigned to intercept each of them personally.

"They're just land records," she said. "Deeds, surveys, tax rolls, meeting minutes. Forty years of men arguing about fence lines. It's the most boring collection in the state, and I've cataloged all of it, so I'm qualified to say so."

"I wasn't—"

"You were thinking about it very loudly." She crossed the room, took the box off the shelf, turned it so the pencil label faced outward, and put it back — a correction performed with the brisk economy of someone righting a picture frame. "If you're going to stare at things in this house, stare at the clock. At least that's honest about wanting attention." She turned for the door, then paused. "Breakfast is at seven. Aunt Ada makes eggs like she's punishing them. Eat them anyway. It's polite."

And she was gone down the hall, candlelight swaying along the wall after her, leaving Oscar alone with a corrected box, a howling storm, and the distinct impression that he had just been measured, found curious, filed, and shelved.

* *

Morning arrived without changing anything except the depth of the snow, which now stood level with the archive's lower windowsills, and the temperature, which had dropped into the range where breath crackled. The telegraph boy trudged up through drifts to deliver official word: bridge closed until further notice, ferry iced in at its moorings, no traffic before thaw, duration of thaw unknown, signed by someone at Northern Pacific whose signature had the smug loopiness of a man paid per word.

Oscar read it over Ada's shoulder at the breakfast table and felt the frustration return, hot and useless.

"Three weeks," he said. "Perhaps four. The master clock needs parts I don't have. I'll have to make them, and I can't make them and carry coal and chop wood and—"

"You'll have time," Ada said placidly, dealing out eggs like cards. "Time is the one thing this winter is handing out free."

"Time is exactly what I *don't*—" He stopped, because Juniper had looked up from her book again, and there was something in the look — not mockery exactly, though it had mockery in its ancestry — that made him reconsider his volume. "What?"

"Nothing," she said. "It's only that you talk about the clock the way some men talk about a woman who's left them."

"*Juniper.*" From Ada, mildly.

"It's a professional observation." She turned a page. "You said yourself it was altered. You said the escapement teeth were wrong for the plate design, wrong by decades, wrong like someone's repaired a wound with the wrong bone. You muttered it into your coffee for ten minutes yesterday. If I were trapped in a town until thaw with one mystery available to me—" she looked up, and this time there was nothing amused in it at all, "—I know which one I'd choose."

The kitchen went quiet enough that Oscar could hear the small clock ticking in the hall, and beneath it, faintly, through walls and floors and half a hillside, the memory-weight of the great clock in the tower, still running, still keeping whatever time it had been taught.

Ada buttered her toast with great composure. "After breakfast," she said, to neither of them particularly, "Mr. Ishikawa should continue his repairs. The tower is cold work. Juniper, take him up the thermos."

"That's your job."

"My knees have opinions about hills in January."

Juniper held her aunt's gaze for a moment — a silent negotiation conducted entirely in eyebrows, the terms of which Oscar could not follow — and then subsided, marking her page with a ribbon.

"Fine," she said. "Thermos at nine. If you're late I leave without it and you can drink snow."

* *

At five minutes to nine, Oscar stood in the archive's entry hall with his toolbag and watched Juniper come down the stairs wearing a coat that had clearly been cut for someone six inches taller, a scarf wound to the nose, and boots she stamped into with the resentment of the just-ordered-around.

She thrust the thermos at him like a summons.

As they stepped out into the blue-white morning — the storm spent, the air scrubbed clean and ringing cold, the observatory dome above them catching sun like a struck match — she stopped on the packed snow of the path and looked back down at the town. The river ran below, black between banks of ice, and the bridge stood gated at both ends, a dark spine with the light locked out.

"Do you know what's under the archive?" she asked suddenly, not looking at him.

"Cellar?"

"Cellar." A pause. "Flood line from '89 is painted on the cellar wall. Six feet. Everything below that mark in '89 went down the river — grant office, courthouse annex, half of Mill Street, and my uncle." Another pause, shorter, harder. "So when people in this town get quiet about the eighties, Mr. Ishikawa, it isn't boredom. It's buoyancy. Everything anyone still owns is floating on top of that year, and everyone knows what happens to towns that rock the boat."

She started up the hill. After three steps she added, over her shoulder, with terrible casualness:

"The ledgers in the drawer aren't the ones with the problem, by the way. In case you were still wondering. It's the ones that *aren't* in any drawer that you should wonder about. Thermos is yours till noon. Don't lose the cup."

Oscar stood a moment longer in the snow, the cold biting his ears, the great clock waiting above with its altered heart, and understood with sudden clarity that the girl had just handed him something — not trust, not yet, but maybe the key-shaped shadow of it — and that the winter ahead had just become a very different kind of repair job entirely.

He picked up his tools and followed her up the hill.
