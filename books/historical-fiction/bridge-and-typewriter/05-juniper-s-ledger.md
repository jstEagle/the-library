## 5. Juniper's Ledger

Juniper Verlow had a system, and the system was this: everything in its place, every place in a ledger, and every ledger in her own two hands.

She rose before dawn because that was when the archive belonged to her alone — before Aunt Ada came down to make coffee and worry, before the clockmaker upstairs began his thumping about like a man who had never learned that floors were meant to be walked on softly. The stove in the back room took twenty minutes to coax into warmth, and she spent them sharpening six pencils to needles-fine points and laying out her catalog cards in a fan across the worktable, white as a hand of cards she intended to win with.

The archive of Verlow's Landing occupied three rooms of what had once been the grant office's overflow annex, which was a polite way of saying the building where documents went when nobody important wanted to think about them. There were deed books with spines gone soft as bread. There were tax rolls in boxes labeled by men who had apparently believed the word MISC. constituted a filing philosophy. There was a drawer containing nothing but forty years of receipts for lamp oil, which Juniper had cataloged anyway, out of spite, on the principle that if she had to suffer, the record would too.

"Sub-collection four," she said to the empty room, writing as she spoke, because saying things aloud kept her honest. "Correspondence, 1879 to 1891. Condition: poor. Organization: none. Custodian's note — " she paused, considered, wrote it anyway — "whoever sorted this should be tried."

The clockmaker's shoes sat by the door where he'd left them last night, wet through from his walk down to see the ice. She'd have moved them, except that moving them felt like an admission that she'd noticed him enough to be annoyed. Oscar Ishikawa. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three. Hands like a surgeon's and the manner of a man apologizing for occupying space. He had arrived three days ago to fix the observatory clock, and now the storm had shut the bridge and he was living in the spare room above the archive, eating their bread, winding his pocket watch at all hours like it owed him money, and looking at everything — the shelves, the ledgers, Ada, *her* — with those dark, careful eyes that missed nothing.

That was the problem. She had watched people look at archives before. Scholars looked hungrily; clerks looked dully; children looked bored. This boy looked like a man reading a room for exits and load-bearing walls. And yesterday she had caught him, twice, watching the locked drawer in Ada's office with an expression that could only be called professional interest.

Nobody came to Verlow's Landing in January by accident, and nobody looked at that drawer like that without wanting something.

Juniper pulled sub-collection four toward her and got to work.

* * *

The trouble with the archive — the trouble Juniper never said aloud, not even to herself in so many words, though she had said it to herself in every other kind of word for eleven years — was that it was lying by omission.

Oh, the surface of it told fine stories. Here were the incorporation papers of the Landing Milling Company, 1881, signatures crisp and confident. Here were the land grants dividing the river bottomlands among the town's founding families: Halloway, Brandt, Okafor, Petrosian, Verlow. Here was the whole tidy genealogy of how Verlow's Landing came to be, arranged in folders and tied with government twine, ready for any historian or railroad man who cared to read it.

But Juniper had been cataloging since she was thirteen, and cataloging teaches you a specific and incurable disease: you begin to notice what is *missing*. A grant book referenced in three separate letters but absent from the shelf. Deed numbers that skipped, like a song with holes in the verse. The year 1884, which appeared in correspondence as a busy, quarrelsome, litigious year, and appeared in the official record as almost nothing at all — as if the town had spent twelve months holding its breath.

And there were the signatures.

She had noticed it first two winters ago, working through the grant series with cold fingers and a suspicion she hadn't yet given shape. Elias Halloway, the millwright, signed his name with a flat-topped H and a tail on the y that hooked backward, like a fisherman's lure. She knew this because his signature appeared dozens of times across thirty years of documents — on mill leases, on freight claims, on a petition about bridge tolls that Juniper privately considered the finest piece of civic grumbling ever committed to paper.

The signature on the 1884 water-rights assignment was not that signature. It was close. It was very close — the same general architecture, the same confident slant. But the H was rounded, and the y-hook curled inward instead of back, and whoever had written it had pressed hard enough to emboss the page, the way a person does when they are concentrating on forming someone else's name correctly.

Juniper had laid the two pages side by side on the worktable that winter and looked at them for a long time, and then she had put them back exactly where they belonged, and she had not taken them out again until now.

Because here was the other thing about systems, the part she kept in the drawer under her heart: if the record was wrong, and she said so loudly, then either people believed her — which meant the record was wrong, which meant everything built on it was wrong, which meant the town itself rested on sand — or they did not believe her, and then she was just the strange Verlow girl with her catalog cards and her accusations. Eleven years ago the records had burned, or been said to have burned, and the town had made its choice about what happened next, and the choice had been silence. You did not break a silence that big alone. You broke it carefully, or you drowned in it.

Her father — Uncle Theo, dead these nine years — had chosen loudly. She remembered only fragments: his voice through the office door, low and furious; Ada crying in the kitchen and pretending not to be; the morning the house went quiet in the particular way houses go quiet around a grief. Afterward, Ada had turned careful as frost. *We keep the records,* was all she would say, when Juniper pushed. *Keeping is enough.*

It wasn't. Juniper had known it wasn't since she was fifteen. But knowing what to do instead required something she didn't have yet: proof that could survive being disbelieved. Proof with teeth.

So she cataloged. And she waited. And she hid things.

* * *

By ten o'clock the stove had won its war against the cold, and Juniper had the 1884 folder open on the worktable under the good lamp, the forged water-rights assignment beside the petition with Halloway's true hand, comparing them for perhaps the fortieth time in her life and finding, for the fortieth time, exactly the lie she had found before. It did not wear out. That was the awful thing about lies done well: they held up. They waited patiently, year after year, in acid-free folders, for somebody to come along and believe them.

Somebody like a pair of railroad historians, she thought, and her stomach tightened. The telegram had come through on the second day of the storm — the line still worked, the bridge did not — announcing that Messrs. Marsh and Quill of the Northern Pacific Historical Bureau would arrive "at earliest practicable date" to conduct a preservation survey of regional records. Preservation survey. As if the railroad had ever preserved anything it couldn't put its name on. She had watched Ada read that telegram twice, fold it once, and set it under the sugar bowl, and neither of them had spoken of it, which was how the women of this house discussed important things: by elaborate mutual pretense that they weren't.

The stairs creaked. Juniper closed the folder with one practiced motion and had her pencil moving on a card about lamp oil before the door finished opening.

Ada came in with the coffee pot and two cups, and behind Ada, inevitably, came the clockmaker, hovering in the doorway like weather deciding whether to commit.

"You're up early," Ada said.

"I'm always up early. You're up late." Juniper sipped the coffee Ada poured and studied the clockmaker over the rim. He had the decency to look embarrassed about existing, which she grudgingly noted most people managed worse. "Did you sleep? The pipes banged half the night."

"That was me," he admitted. "Hot water bottle. The pipes object."

"The pipes object because you treat this house like a machine shop." She let that sit, then, because fairness was a habit she practiced even when it inconvenienced her: "There's a rug in the hall closet. Put it under your feet and the pipes will forgive you by Thursday."

He thanked her with such sincerity that she felt briefly criminal, which was another thing about Oscar Ishikawa: he made rudeness feel like vandalism. It was infuriating. She had built her whole manner on the assumption that people met sharpness with sharpness, and he kept meeting it with gratitude, like a man catching knives by the handle.

"The observatory clock," he said to Ada, hesitant. "I've been thinking about the third-season irregularity. I'd like to go back up today, if the roads allow. I want to look at the escapement again."

Something crossed Ada's face — quick, gone, but Juniper saw it, because seeing things was her trade. Fear, or the memory-shaped outline of fear.

"Whenever you like," Ada said lightly. "Mind the hill, it ices over."

He nodded and withdrew, and his footsteps went up the stairs, and even those were apologetic.

"He watches that clock like it owes him a confession," Juniper said.

"He watches it like a craftsman." Ada gathered the cups. "There's a difference."

"There isn't, always." Juniper turned back to her table before her aunt could examine her face. Ada left, and the door closed, and the room settled back into its furnace hum and paper smell, and Juniper sat for a full minute doing nothing at all, which for her was a great deal.

Then she opened the folder again.

* * *

Here is what Juniper Verlow decided between eleven o'clock and noon, alone in the back room of the annex, with the winter light coming thin through the frosted glass:

She decided that the clockmaker was either an enemy or a lever, and that she could not tell which, and that the difference might be discoverable.

Enemies of this family came in good coats and spoke of preservation. They did not arrive alone in January with one valise and a bag of clock tools and eat bread and dripping in the kitchen like they'd never seen bread before. Enemies did not spend an entire dinner conversation explaining, with visible delight, why the great clocks of Europe drifted apart from one another like siblings raised in different countries, while Juniper pretended not to find it interesting. Enemies did not say, as he had said last night on the landing, unprompted: *Your aunt keeps the most orderly archive I've ever seen. Somebody must have spent years on it.* And looked, when he said it, not at Ada, but at Juniper — as if he understood whose years those were.

Or maybe that was exactly what a clever spy would do. She allowed the possibility. She simply didn't believe it, and believing things against evidence was a vice she refused to cultivate.

A lever, then. If the clock had been altered — and she had heard him muttering about seasonal drift, about cams, about *someone changed this on purpose*, words that had stopped her cold in the hallway outside the observatory two days ago — then the clock and the ledgers told the same story from opposite ends. The timestamps and the signatures. Two halves of one forgery. She had spent eleven years holding one half alone, unable to prove anything, because a mismatched signature proves nothing but doubt, and doubt dies quietly in committee rooms.

But a master clock that was built to lie — that was different. That was proof with a heartbeat.

Which meant that if she was going to do the frightening thing, she would need him. And if she needed him, she needed to know whether he was brave. Not clever — she suspected clever. Brave. Because whatever had frightened her aunt in the years before Juniper's memory, it had teeth, and it had recently sent telegrams.

So: test him. Small, controlled, deniable.

Juniper took out a fresh card and wrote, in her smallest hand: *Water rights, 1884 — see also maintenance log, obs. clock, 1886–87 (cross-ref pending).* Then she slid the forged assignment out of its sleeve, and beneath it in the folder, exactly where she had found it three days ago and replaced it, lay the loose sheet she had set there herself — the photostat her father had made in the last months of his life, off the true county ledger, before the county vault changed its visiting hours and its story. Elias Halloway's real signature on the real assignment of 1884, which granted the water rights to no railroad at all.

She had shown it to no one. Not Ada. Certainly not the clockmaker.

She weighed it in her hands a moment — one page, light as a moth, heavy as the river in spring — and then she folded it into her coat pocket, because decisions made alone are the only ones you can't unmake by committee, and she had just made hers.

If Oscar Ishikawa went up the hill today and found what she thought he'd find in that escapement, then tonight she would leave the photostat somewhere he couldn't help but see it, and watch his face when he did. If he reached for it hungrily — enemy. If he looked at it the way he looked at broken mechanisms, turning it over in his mind, asking how it worked and who had broken it —

Lever. Ally. Whatever word existed for the strangers fate shoves into your house during a storm.

Footsteps on the stairs again — heavier this time, and pausing at each creak as though apologizing to it individually. Juniper tucked the photostat deeper into her pocket, squared her catalog cards, and arranged her face into its usual expression of mild contempt for the general category of visitors.

Oscar Ishikawa stood in the doorway in his good coat, watch chain glinting, looking like a man walking into a headwind. "I'm going up to the observatory," he said. "I wondered — that is, Ada mentioned the road ices near the bend, and I thought perhaps you'd know the safe footing. If you're not busy."

Juniper looked at him for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere under the floor, the pipes groaned like a ship.

"As it happens," she said, reaching for her coat, "I'm very busy. So you'll have to talk while we walk."

He blinked. "Talk about what?"

"Clocks," said Juniper Verlow, pulling on her gloves. "Start with the escapement. And don't leave anything out — I'll know."
