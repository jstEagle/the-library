## 6. Teeth of the Escapement

The third morning of my confinement at Verlow's Landing began with Juniper hammering on my door as though the house were on fire and I was somehow to blame for it.

"Ada says you're to come up directly. The clock's striking wrong again."

"Wrong how?" I asked, fumbling with my collar.

"Wrong in a way that annoys her. Which is every way, but this one especially. It lost four seconds overnight." She said it with the flat disgust of someone reporting a mouse in the flour bin. "Four seconds, Ishikawa. In this town that's practically a scandal."

"In most towns it would be considered rather good, actually."

"This isn't most towns." And she was gone down the stairs before I could ask what she meant, which had become, I noticed, her preferred method of conversation: deliver the mystery, withhold the explanation, depart.

I dressed quickly and followed her up through the archive's back stair — the one Ada used, I suspected, precisely because it did not pass through the public reading room — and out into the gray morning. Snow still came down, thinner now, the storm spending its last temper. The observatory stood against the white sky like a chess piece left standing after everyone else has gone home.

* * *

Inside, Ada Verlow was already waiting beneath the great clock, arms folded, watching the pendulum swing with an expression I can only describe as personal betrayal.

"There you are," she said. "It lost time."

"So I'm told. Forcefully."

"It never loses time when it's lying," she said, and then pressed her lips together as though she'd said too much, which she clearly had, and which I filed away with the growing stack of things the people of Verlow's Landing said and then visibly regretted.

"I'll have a look at the escapement," I said, deciding to be merciful. My master always said a good repairman knows when not to ask questions, mostly because the answers make the customer cry.

The master clock was a magnificent thing, and I want to be clear that my professional admiration was entirely untainted by anything that happened later that morning. It was built — so the plate inside the case told me — in 1874 by the Fenwick works of London, a nine-foot regulator with a deadbeat escapement and a mercury-compensated pendulum, the kind of instrument an observatory uses to define noon for an entire valley. When I first opened its case three days earlier I had felt something close to reverence. Clocks like this are cathedrals. You do not walk into a cathedral whistling.

I laid out my tools on the felt cloth — each piece in its place, a habit beaten into me by Master Aldrich along with several other habits I've since decided were less virtues than scars — and opened the movement's rear access panel.

The escapement is the heart of any clock, if the heart were also the conscience. Every tick is a small decision: release, catch, release, catch. Get it right and the clock keeps honest time through heat and cold and neglect. Get it wrong — or get it *interfered with* — and the clock becomes a liar with excellent posture.

I worked upward from the pallets, checking oil, wear, seating. All sound. I checked the suspension. Sound. I checked the crutch, the beat, the rate screw. All of it sound, all of it beautifully made, and none of it explaining a loss of four seconds, which was far too small to be dramatic and far too large to ignore.

Which meant the answer wasn't in what should be there. It was in what shouldn't be.

* * *

I found it an hour later, and I want to tell it slowly, because I found it slowly, and because the slowness matters to what came after.

Between the escape wheel arbor and the minute hand's motion work, mounted on a small brass bracket bolted — *bolted*, not pinned, bolted, like something someone wanted secure — there was a cam. A shaped disc of brass, perhaps two inches across, riding on a secondary arbor geared off the hour wheel. As the clock ran, a light spring follower traced the cam's profile, and at the profile's high points, the follower pressed against a lever that bore, very gently, on the pendulum rod itself.

A touch of friction. A whisper of drag. Enough to steal — I estimated, doing the arithmetic twice because I didn't believe it the first time — nearly three minutes over the course of a day.

I sat back on my stool and looked at it for a long while.

Here is the thing about cams: they don't happen by accident. Nothing in a clock happens by accident; a clock is the least accidental object humans make. This cam had been *designed*. Its profile was smooth where it needed to rise gradually and steep where it wanted the change sudden. Someone had sat somewhere warm with graver and file and thought carefully about exactly how much dishonesty the mechanism could carry without anyone noticing.

And more: the cam was not original. I knew Fenwick regulators the way a priest knows scripture, from Master Aldrich's obsession with them, and this was no Fenwick part. The brass was a different color, a slightly redder alloy. The bolt heads were machine-threaded — American threads, Whitworth-adjacent, nothing a London workshop of 1874 would have used. The bracket screw holes had bright fresh metal around them where they'd been drilled into castings that had never been intended to hold them.

Someone had altered this clock years after it was built. Deliberately. Skillfully. And with a patience I found genuinely unsettling once I understood the shape of it.

Because here was the elegant, horrible part. The cam was geared to the hour wheel, which meant it turned once every twelve hours. But it was also phased — set at a particular position relative to the hands — and its profile was not uniform. There was a long low stretch where the follower touched nothing and the clock ran perfectly true. Then, over some hours, the profile rose, the follower pressed, the pendulum dragged, and the clock gained minutes. Then it fell away again, and the clock quietly returned to honesty, the accumulated error vanishing into the correction winding.

The clock lied only during certain seasons. Only during certain weeks, I'd wager, if you mapped the profile precisely. It would run fast through whatever window mattered — a land auction, say, or the filing of claims, or the recording of a deed at the county office two valleys over, where the official timestamp came, as official timestamps did in towns like this, from the local observatory's master clock.

Then it would return to perfect truth, and anyone who checked afterward would find the clock keeping immaculate time, and would conclude, reasonably, that it had always done so.

* * *

"You've gone pale," Ada said behind me. "Or gray. Clockmaker's gray. Is that a professional condition?"

I hadn't heard her come in. I didn't turn around right away, because turning around would make it real, and I wanted another moment of it being a puzzle, a charming mechanical curiosity, a story I'd tell Master Aldrich someday about the strange little cam in the Verlow's Landing regulator.

Instead I heard myself say, "Madam Verlow. When was this clock last fully serviced?"

"Serviced? I wind it daily. It was cleaned — oh, before my husband—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "It was serviced before my husband bought the place. Ninety-one, perhaps. Why?"

"May I ask you to look at something without telling me what I think it is?"

She came around the open case door and looked where my lamp pointed. I watched her face. I had learned in three days that Ada Verlow governed her face the way her clock governed its escapement — everything released in measured, deliberate increments. What escaped her now was not much. A slow blink. Her hand rising to the case's edge and gripping it until the knuckles whitened.

"That wasn't there," she said. "That bracket. That isn't— Elias kept this clock like a religion. He'd have known within a day."

"When did he die?"

"Eighty-nine." Her voice had gone flat. "In the flood. You know that. Everyone knows that."

"The flood took the grant office. The records burned — drowned, I'm sorry, I'm mixing the town's stories—"

"Drowned and burned," Ada said. "The river does both. It's thorough."

I looked back at the cam. At its careful, patient profile. At the seasons it chose to lie.

"Madam Verlow," I said, and my mouth was dry, "do you know what this mechanism does?"

"I can see what it does. It presses the pendulum." She said it the way a person identifies a weapon they've been shown. Then, softer, almost to herself: "Elias used to check the clock against his star transits obsessively. Every clear night. He kept saying the clock and the sky disagreed, just barely, just sometimes, and he couldn't find why. He wrote to the county about it. He wrote to the railroad." Something moved through her then, something old and cold. "Six months later he was rowing across the millrace in the dark with a satchel of documents, and the river—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. I am an apprentice clockmaker; I understand incomplete mechanisms. You can infer the rest of a train from the wheels that remain.

"He thought the clock was wrong," I said gently. "He spent years thinking his clock was sick. But the clock was never sick, Madam Verlow. The clock was *told* to lie, and it has been obeying instructions faithfully ever since. That's the cruelty of it. It's a superb instrument. It does exactly what it was modified to do."

"Modified by whom?"

"I don't know. But the workmanship is fine, and the timing is deliberate, and—" I took a breath. "Whoever did it understood that nobody suspects a clock. We check ledgers. We check signatures. We check witnesses and seals and wax. Nobody checks whether time itself arrived on schedule."

Ada sat down slowly on the stool I'd abandoned. She stared into the case for a long time, and I had the sense that thirty years were rearranging themselves behind her eyes — every small oddity, every dismissed discrepancy, every night her husband had come to bed frowning over star tables that refused to agree with brass and steel.

"The timestamps," she said at last. "The grant filings. The claim registrations. Everything recorded in this valley between—" She stopped. "Juniper," she said, suddenly, sharply, standing. "Where's Juniper?"

"At the archive, cataloging. She's been at it since breakfast, muttering about mislabeled boxes—"

Ada was already moving for the door. She paused with her hand on it and looked back at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, Ada Verlow looked her whole age and then some, and younger at once, the way people do when something long frozen begins, painfully, to thaw.

"Oscar." She had never used my name before. "Don't touch the cam. Don't remove it. Don't let it be seen that we know."

"Because whoever put it there—"

"Is probably dead," she said. "But whoever paid for it is probably alive, and comfortable, and has recently employed two polite young men who keep asking to inventory our records. So." She straightened her collar, and the governor resumed its measured releases. "We know nothing yet. We suspect everything. And you will please finish repairing my clock, Mr. Ishikawa, exactly as thoroughly as you were going to anyway — because in a few days, I think, we are going to need it to keep absolutely honest time, and I intend for it to do so in front of witnesses."

* * *

She left me alone with the mechanism, which I suspect was a test, or a trust, or both.

I did not touch the cam. I did measure it — with my finest calipers, quickly, and then again more slowly, committing the profile to memory the way my master taught me to memorize wheel counts: not as numbers but as a shape, a feel under the thumb, a thing you could reproduce blind. I sketched it afterward in the margin of my rate log, in the coded shorthand clockmakers use for profiles, which reads to the untrained eye like nothing more interesting than a laundry list. If I'm honest — and I had begun, that morning, to care rather intensely about honesty — I sketched it partly for the truth and partly because drawing it made my hands stop shaking.

Then I cleaned the train, dressed the pallets, corrected the beat, and closed the case. By afternoon the great clock was ticking with the deep, level heartbeat of a Fenwick at its best, losing nothing, gaining nothing, telling the truth except for those patient seasonal lies coiled in its brass.

I stood listening to it and thought about the girl on the coach, on my first morning here, warning me not to ask about the year the records burned. I had assumed she meant shame. Towns guard their shames jealously. But standing under the clock I understood she might have meant something closer to fear — the specific fear of a town that has lived its whole life inside a lie so old it has become furniture, wallpaper, the shape of the light through the windows.

I thought about Juniper hiding pages in the archive. About Ada's shaking hands when the railroad men visited. About a husband rowing into the dark with a satchel.

And I thought — with the peculiar clarity that arrives sometimes in cold rooms beside large machines — that I had come to Verlow's Landing to fix a clock, and that the clock, as it turned out, was the smallest of the broken things.

That evening Juniper knocked on my door again. Not hammering this time. One soft knock, almost apologetic, which frightened me considerably more than the hammering would have.

"Ishikawa," she said through the wood, in a voice stripped of all its usual armor. "You'd better come downstairs. Aunt Ada's opened the locked drawer."
