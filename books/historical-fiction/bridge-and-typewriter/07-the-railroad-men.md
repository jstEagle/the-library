## 7. The Railroad Men

The knock came at half past nine on Thursday morning, and it was the wrong kind of knock.

I have spent my life around mechanisms, and I will maintain until I am dust that everything that ticks has a signature. A clockmaker's knock is quick and apologetic — three raps and a step back. Juniper's knock is a drumroll, performed with the flat of her hand because she cannot be bothered to make a fist. Ada Verlow doesn't knock at all; she simply appears in doorways like weather.

This knock was two slow blows, evenly spaced, delivered by someone who expected the door to open because he had paid for the privilege of doors.

I was elbow-deep in the strike train, and Juniper was up a ladder with a duster she was using more as a weapon than a tool, so it fell to Ada to answer. I heard the bolt slide. I heard the wind come in off the river, carrying snow with it. And then I heard a voice like warm oil poured over cold stones.

"Madam Verlow? Mr. Ellery Marsh, Northern Pacific Historical Bureau. This is my colleague, Mr. Ash. We telegraphed ahead."

"You telegraphed the depot," said Ada. "The depot didn't tell me."

"The wires are unreliable in storm," said the first voice, unbothered. "May we come in out of it?"

There is a particular quality to a man who says *may we come in out of it* when he means *we are coming in*. I set down my pliers. I did not know yet why my spine had gone stiff; I only knew that some animal part of me had heard something my ears hadn't.

* * *

They were an ill-matched pair standing in the archive's entry hall, shedding snow onto Juniper's clean floor.

Ellery Marsh was somewhere past sixty, tall and folded like a folding rule, with a scholar's stoop and a pair of spectacles he polished constantly, as though the world were slightly smudged and only he knew it. His coat was good wool gone shiny at the cuffs — a coat bought once, well, and worn ever since. Everything about him suggested a man who had loved something honestly once and now handled its corpse for wages.

The younger one — Ash — was another creature entirely. Thirty perhaps, with the smooth pink hands of a man who had never held anything heavier than a pen, and a smile that arrived a half-second before any reason for it. He carried a leather portfolio under one arm the way other men carry a Bible or a pistol, which is to say: like a thing whose purpose is belief.

"Mr. Ishikawa," Ash said, before anyone had introduced us. "The clockmaker from the city. We make it our business to know who's working in towns on our lines."

"Our lines," I repeated.

"The railroad's lines," he said, smiling wider. "Same thing, out here, soon enough."

Juniper came down her ladder with the unhurried dignity of a queen descending to receive ambassadors, looked Ash over from boots to collar, and said, "You track mud everywhere, I'd thank you to stand on the mat. Both of you. The whole town, if possible."

Marsh had the grace to look abashed. Ash laughed as though she'd said something delightful.

Ada, meanwhile, had not moved from where the welcome had deposited her — one hand on the newel post, spine straight as a pendulum rod. And it was there, watching her, that I saw what the animal part of me had been trying to tell me all along.

Her hands were shaking.

Not much. Not enough that Marsh or Ash would notice, because they were looking at her face, which was composed into perfect courtesy. But I have spent ten thousand hours watching hands, because hands tell you what a mechanism is doing regardless of what the dial claims. Ada Verlow's hand gripped that newel post the way a man grips a rail in high water.

She was afraid. Of two polite men with a portfolio and a bad coat.

* * *

They wanted, they said, to help.

"The bureau," Marsh explained, once we had been installed in the archive's reading room — Ada in her chair behind the desk, the rest of us distributed like chess pieces, Ash having selected the seat nearest the ledger shelves without appearing to select it at all — "is charged with preserving the documentary heritage of the territories the line serves. Towns like Verlow's Landing hold records of enormous historical value. Grant ledgers, mill accounts, correspondence. Paper, madam, does not last. Fire, damp, mice, neglect." He spread his long fingers. "We catalog, we copy, and we ensure that what matters is not lost."

"How generous," said Ada.

"We've completed work in Harwick and Dunmore," Ash offered. "Lovely towns. Very cooperative."

Something passed across the room then, quick as a draft. Juniper, by the window, went very still. I did not know why, but I filed it, the way I file an odd sound in a movement — to be examined later, under better light.

"And your method?" Ada asked. Her voice was level. Her hands, folded on the desk, were still.

"Inventory first," said Marsh. "We begin Monday. A simple accounting — box numbers, contents, condition. Nothing leaves this building without your written consent and a receipt countersigned by yourself." He produced, from inside his coat, a printed card and laid it on her desk with two fingers, gently, the way one lays a flower on a grave. "Those are our credentials. You'll find references from three county clerks."

Ada looked at the card for a long moment. Then she said, "You may stay at the hotel on Front Street. Supper at the Hartwell house if Mrs. Hartwell takes you in, which she may, because she'll take in anyone who compliments her preserves. The archive is open to researchers Tuesdays and Saturdays. Ten to four."

A pause, small as the space between tick and tock.

"That won't suit," said Ash, pleasantly. "We're on a schedule. The thaw closes the roads and opens the bridge, and we're expected in Corvane by the first of March. We'd hoped to begin tomorrow."

"The schedule is yours," said Ada. "The archive is mine."

And there it was — the first true thing anyone had said aloud since the knock. Ash's smile held. It was a remarkable performance; I will give him that. But I saw his thumb press white against the edge of his portfolio, and I understood that pleasantness, for this man, was not a temperament. It was a tool. He kept it in his hand the way I keep a screwdriver.

Marsh touched Ash's sleeve — the smallest gesture, a watchmaker's adjustment — and rose.

"Monday, then," he said, and bowed over Ada's hand without taking it. "Thank you for your hospitality, Madam Verlow. Your husband was, I believe, also a great friend to this town's history."

The silence that followed had weight. I felt it press on my ears like a change of altitude.

"You knew my husband," Ada said.

"I corresponded with him," said Marsh. "Years ago. On matters of local record." He put his spectacles back on, and behind the glass his eyes were old and tired and entirely unreadable. "Good day to you all."

* * *

The door closed. The bolt slid. Juniper exhaled through her nose like a horse refusing a fence.

"Well," she said. "They can stay. I've poisoned better men than those."

"Juniper," said Ada.

"It's a joke, Aunt Ada. Mostly." But her eyes weren't joking; they were on the window, tracking two sets of footprints down toward Front Street. "Harwick and Dunmore. 'Very cooperative.' Do you know what happened to Harwick's grant records?"

"They were preserved," Ada said quietly.

"They were *removed*. Every original page, crated and shipped east for 'conservation' in the spring of '09, and what came back were certified copies — copies that happen to show every water right in the valley running clean to the railroad. Old Man Petrie fought it for two years and lost, because the originals, per the county clerk, no longer exist." She turned from the window. "That's what preservation looks like when the railroad does it, Aunt Ada. They don't burn books anymore. They just borrow them forever."

I thought of the locked drawer upstairs. I thought of the way Ada had said *not your concern*, and how the words had sounded less like secrecy than like a bandage over something that had never been allowed to close.

"Mrs. Verlow," I said — and then found I didn't know what came after it. I am a man who can take apart a chiming movement blindfolded and put it back together in the dark, and I could not finish a sentence.

But Ada saved me the trouble, or perhaps simply ran out of strength for pretense. She looked down at her own hands on the desk, and I watched her look at them, and I watched them shake, and she let them.

"He wrote to them," she said. "My husband. For six years he wrote to their bureau, and to the county, and to the land office, asking questions about the grants along this stretch of river. Polite letters. Scholarly letters. He was a gentle man; he believed paper would protect him, because paper had always protected him." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "In November of '93 he took the ferry to Corvane with a satchel of documents he intended to deposit with the county court. There was ice on the river. They told me it happens."

Nobody said anything. The stove ticked. Even the stove, it seemed, kept honest time.

"And now," Ada went on, in a voice like a woman reading an inventory, "they send a courteous old man and a smiling young one, ahead of the thaw, to inventory what remains. And I shall serve them tea, and I shall be gracious, and I shall count the days until the bridge opens, and I shall be glad when they are gone." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Because I have been afraid for twenty years, Mr. Ishikawa, and fear is a habit like any other. One keeps it wound."

Juniper crossed the room and put her hand over her aunt's, and for once said nothing at all, which from Juniper is roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

It was left to me, God help us, to say the practical thing.

"Then we should decide," I said, "whether the habit gets broken before Monday."

* * *

Upstairs afterward, alone with the great clock, I found my thoughts would not settle to the escapement.

Here is what I knew, assembled like a train laid out on a bench: a cam cut decades after the clock was built, altering its rate in specific seasons. Timestamps minutes fast, in seasons that mattered. A town whose records burned in one convenient year. A railroad hiring historians the way other institutions hire lawyers. An archive full of paper that two professionals wished very much to inventory. And a keeper who had spent twenty years winding her fear daily rather than look at what it guarded.

I worked the escape wheel free and held it to the lamplight, and I confess I spoke to it, as I sometimes do, because brass listens better than people.

"Someone bent you to lie," I told it. "Small lies. Minute-sized lies, season after season, until nobody alive remembered what truth sounded like on the hour." I turned the wheel. "And now they've come to preserve the lying, nicely, in acid-free folders."

The wheel caught the light and said nothing, being brass.

But beneath the anger — and it was anger, which surprised me; I had not known I was capable of it on another person's behalf — there was something else, quieter and more troublesome. Twice in my life things had happened to me: once when my mother put me on a boat, and once when a letter arrived summoning me here. I had come to Verlow's Landing wanting the world to take an interest in me, and it had obliged with a vengeance, and now I stood in its machinery holding a piece of evidence in my bare hands, wondering whether I was a clockmaker or a witness.

A witness, I decided, could still be a clockmaker. But a clockmaker who had seen the cam and said nothing would never again be able to look at a dial — any dial — without tasting ash.

Below me, faintly, I heard Juniper laugh at something Ada had said, and the sound came up through the floorboards like warmth through a wall.

* * *

They came back Friday.

Not to the archive — to the observatory. I was at the top of the tower with the dome cracked open to air the works when I heard boot leather on the iron stair, and Ash emerged into the instrument room rubbing his hands and remarking on the view, the remarkable view, you could see the whole river from up here, couldn't you, and the bridge, and wasn't the bridge a marvel of engineering for its decade.

"It is," I said. "So is the clock. Would you like to see it?"

I do not know why I said that. Perhaps because a man with a tool respects another man with tools; perhaps simply to watch him flinch. He didn't flinch. He looked at the great case, and the pendulum, and the dial, with the fond appraisal of a man pricing furniture, and said, "Magnificent. They say it's kept this town's time for fifty years. Court filings, mill shifts, land registrations — all of it stamped against this clock, I shouldn't wonder."

"All of it," I agreed.

"Fifty years of timestamps." He smiled at me, that half-second-early smile. "Makes a fellow think. All those official moments, hanging off one pendulum. Change the pendulum—" he caught himself, lightly, artfully, "—well. You couldn't, of course. But a poet might dream about it."

"A poet," I said, "would need to get past the escapement."

"Would he." Something moved behind his eyes, brief and cold, a fish turning under ice. "You're a serious young man, Mr. Ishikawa. I like that. The bureau likes serious young men. There are positions, after this — Corvane, maybe even the head office in Saint Paul. A man could do worse than making himself useful to people who remember their friends."

"I'll keep it in mind," I said.

He left then, boots ringing down the iron stair, and I stood in the cold of the instrument room listening to the master clock tick out its altered, faithful, wounded time, and I understood two things with complete clarity.

The first was that they suspected nothing yet — but Ash had just told me exactly where to look, which meant he already knew where looking might lead, which meant someone, somewhere, had never entirely stopped worrying about this clock.

The second was that Ada's shaking hands had not been fear of two historians.

They had been fear of what the historians had come for — and of how nearly, in twenty years of careful silence, she had let them come too late.

That night I sat at my bench past midnight, and below me the archive light burned equally late, and once — I am nearly sure of it — I heard Juniper's typewriter going, quick and defiant, like rain beginning.
