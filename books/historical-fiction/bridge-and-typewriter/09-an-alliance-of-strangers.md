## 9. An Alliance of Strangers

I did not sleep well in the room above the archive. The wind worried the shutters like a bill collector, and somewhere below me the house ticked — not the easy tick of a domestic clock but something arrhythmic, irregular, as though the building itself had a heart murmur. I lay awake cataloguing the sound until nearly two o'clock before I understood what it was: Ada Verlow, downstairs, walking from one end of the archive to the other and back again. Not sleeping either, then.

The cam sat on my nightstand where I had left it, wrapped in a chamois cloth like something sick that needed keeping warm. Every few minutes I reached over and touched it through the fabric, the way you'd check a child's forehead for fever. It was only a piece of brass, three inches across, cut with a single eccentric lobe. Any clockmaker would have called it elegant work. That was precisely the problem. Nobody adds an elegant cam to an honest clock.

By morning I had rehearsed perhaps forty ways of saying what needed saying, and discarded all of them, because every version began with *Mrs. Verlow, your clock has been lying to this town for forty years,* and there is no gentle way to say such a thing to a woman who keeps her dead husband's ledgers under lock.

She was in the kitchen when I came down, hands around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, watching the window where the snow came down in that flat sideways way of a storm that means to stay. Juniper was at the table with her elbows planted, guarding a plate of toast the way generals guard territory. Neither of them spoke. The silence had the specific texture of a household that had been silent for years and gotten comfortable with it.

"Mrs. Verlow," I said, and set the chamois bundle on the table between the toast and the teapot.

Ada looked at it the way you look at a letter you've been dreading. "You found something."

"I found something." I unwrapped the cloth. The cam caught the gray morning light and threw it back, bright as a coin. "It was fitted into the season-change work sometime after the original build. I'd guess the eighties, from the tooling marks. When it's engaged, the strike train runs four minutes eleven seconds fast through the winter months."

"Four minutes," Juniper said slowly, "eleven seconds."

"That's the figure, yes."

"And why would anyone want a clock to run fast in winter?" Ada asked. Her voice was perfectly level. Her hands, wrapped around the cooling cup, were not. I watched her knuckles whiten and thought of the drawer upstairs, locked, the ledgers inside she had told me were not my concern.

"Because," I said, "an observatory clock sets the official time for everything in its jurisdiction. Deeds filed at the county office. Bills of sale. Notices posted. If a man wanted to prove he had recorded a claim before someone else — if he wanted to backdate by a hair, or forward-date, or make his paper arrive at a moment when the offices were closed and nobody could contest it — he wouldn't need to forge a date. He'd only need to move the clock."

The stove ticked. The snow ticked against the glass. Juniper had stopped chewing.

Ada looked at the cam for a long time. Then she stood, walked to the window, and stood with her back to us, which I took at first for dismissal. But when she turned around, her eyes were wet and furious, and fury, I have since learned, is Mrs. Verlow's preferred armor against grief.

"My husband used to say the clock never lied," she said. "He said it out loud, at dinner, to visitors, to the railroad men who came sniffing in 'ninety-three. *The clock never lies.* He believed it right up until—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Right up until the river."

"The flood of 'eighty-nine," I said quietly. "Juniper mentioned it. Only briefly."

"There is no briefly about it." She came back to the table and sat down across from me, spine straight as a plumb line. "The grant office stood at the foot of the bridge. In March of 'eighty-nine the ice went out early and took half the waterfront with it, grant office included. Every original land record for Verlow's Landing went into the river. What survived was copies. And copies, Mr. Ishikawa, are made by whoever holds the pen."

Juniper pushed her chair back with a scrape. "Aunt Ada."

"Hush."

"You're going to tell him."

"I am going to tell him," Ada said, "because he has just laid a forged cam on my breakfast table, and because he cannot leave until the thaw regardless, and because—" her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word— "because I am tired, Juniper. I have been tired for twenty-two years."

* * *

What she told me then took most of the morning, and I will not reproduce all of it here, because some of it belongs to her and not to my chapter of the story. But the shape of it was this:

In 1887, the Northern Pacific had wanted the river frontage. The mills owned it. The millwright Elias Halloway held title to the deepest-water parcel, and three other families held the rest in parcels so tangled by inheritance that any honest surveyor would have needed a month to unpick them. Then the paperwork began arriving at the county seat with dates on it that didn't quite agree with itself — claims filed at hours when the recorder's office was shuttered, notices posted with timestamps certified by the observatory clock. And the observatory clock, said everyone in town, never lied.

"Somebody bought the clockkeeper," I said.

"Nobody bought him." Ada's mouth went thin. "The keeper before my husband. A man named Corliss. He was paid, or he was frightened, or both — he left town in 'ninety-one and no one ever traced him. My husband took the position after, and found the alteration within a year. He spent nine years assembling proof. Correspondence, maintenance logs, affidavits from men who had watched the timestamps shift. He was building a case to take to the state land office in the spring of 'eighty-nine."

"The spring of the flood."

"He went down to the grant office that morning to retrieve the last of the documents himself. The ones too important to trust to anyone else." Ada picked up the cam, turned it over once, and set it down with exaggerated care, the way you handle a thing when you badly want to throw it. "They never recovered him. They recovered his coat."

The stove ticked. Juniper had gone very still, and I realized she had heard some version of this before — enough to know its outlines, not enough to be unmoved by the whole of it.

"After that," Ada went on, "I locked the drawer. His papers stayed where he'd left them. I told myself it was grief. Then I told myself it was prudence. Then I told myself the truth, which is that I was afraid, and I let the town believe the victors' story because believing it was easier than fighting it, and now the railroad is sending historians to write the story into permanence, and I find that being tired is finally worse than being afraid." She lifted her chin. "So. You are a stranger, Mr. Ishikawa, and a boy besides, and I owe you nothing, least of all my husband's grave. But I am given to understand that you know clocks. Do you know clocks?"

"Ma'am," I said, "clocks are the only thing I do know."

"Then open the drawer with me."

* * *

The archive occupied the long upper floor of the house, and the master clock's little brother — the regulator that had once controlled the observatory's secondary dials — stood at its head like a sentry. Beneath it, in a cabinet bolted to the wall, was the drawer I had been politely pretending not to notice since my second day under their roof. Ada produced a key from the watch chain at her waist. It had been worn smooth; I suspected she carried it the way other widows carry wedding rings.

Inside were nine ledgers, a tin box of correspondence, and — this was the thing that stopped me — a maintenance log bound in green cloth, kept in the observatory's own hand. Not the county records. Not affidavits. The clock's own diary.

"What's this?" I asked, though my fingers already knew.

"Every servicing, every adjustment, every part replaced, from 1861 onward," Ada said. "My husband's hand from 'eighty-eight. Corliss's hand before that. And in Corliss's hand—" she opened it to a page marked with a slip of ribbon— "this."

Corliss's script was cramped and slantwise, the writing of a man working quickly or guiltily. *Fitted new cam to season-change assembly per instruction of Mr. R. Trentham, agent, N.P.R.R. Clock to gain 4 min 11 sec during months of December through March. Client requests discretion. No entry to public log.*

"No entry to public log," Juniper read over my shoulder, and laughed — a short, bitter sound entirely unlike her usual needle. "Well. At least he wrote it down somewhere. Criminals are their own worst archivists."

"He wrote it down," I said slowly, turning pages, "and look — here, 1891, your husband's hand. *Cam removed, inspected, retained as evidence. Replacement escapement fitted true.* He took it out. He fixed the clock. He kept the cam." I looked up. "This is the same cam. The one from the observatory."

"It was in the drawer when I locked it," Ada said. "And it was in the mechanism when you arrived. Which means someone refitted it, sometime in the last year, without entering the log." Her eyes met mine, and understanding passed between us like current through wire. "The railroad knows the thaw will bring scrutiny. They're preparing the ground. If the clock runs false again, then any document we produce can be dismissed as timestamped by an unreliable instrument."

"They're not just rewriting history," Juniper said. "They're pre-discrediting anyone who might contradict it."

"Which brings us," Ada said, "to the question of what we do about it. Before you answer, understand what you'd be joining. Mr. Marsh and his colleague have the full weight of the Northern Pacific behind them. My husband fought them with nine years of careful work and the river swallowed him along with it. I will not pretend the odds have improved."

"Aunt Ada." Juniper's voice had lost its edge entirely. "You're asking him. You're actually going to fight."

"I am actually going to fight," said Ada, "if these two will stand with me. I'm done standing alone. It's a poor formation. One person is easily drowned."

I looked down at the green ledger, at Corliss's confession in cramped ink, at Elias Halloway's name in the margin of a page I hadn't yet read. I thought about my master back home, who had called this job small work, and about the girl on the coach who told me not to ask about the year the records burned. Everyone in this town had been telling me not to ask questions since the moment I crossed the bridge. It occurred to me, standing in that archive with the snow coming down sideways, that I had crossed the bridge precisely hoping things would happen to me. Well. Things had happened. This was one of them.

"Here's the difficulty," I said. "The cam proves the clock was altered. The log proves who altered it. But neither proves what the *true* time was — and the true time is what makes the ledgers lie. If we can show the clock ran fast in the winters of '87 and '88, then every certified timestamp from those winters moves, and the dates on the railroad's claims collapse into impossibility."

"Can you show it?" Ada asked.

"With this?" I tapped the green ledger. "Yes. A clock is a machine, ma'am — it obeys laws. If the cam was in place, the error is calculable to the second. We reconstruct the falsified intervals mathematically and match them against the filing dates in the grant ledgers. Where they intersect, we have proof no historian can wave away, because it isn't testimony. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Juniper repeated. Something kindled in her face — I would learn later that this was the expression she wore whenever a fight looked winnable. "And how long does this arithmetic take?"

"Days. Working carefully, with cross-checks — four days, perhaps five."

"We may not have five days." Ada moved to the window. Below, through the snow, I could see the lamplight of Main Street, and beyond it, at the hotel, the lit windows where our two scholarly guests were no doubt dining and making notes. "Their inventory reaches the grant materials by week's end. Once Marsh catalogs them, they ship east under company seal, and whatever we assemble afterward becomes the ranting of grieving cranks."

"Then we don't need five days," Juniper said. "We need to be faster than two men who think they've already won." She pulled out a chair, spun it, and straddled it backwards, arms folded along the backrest. "All right. I'll say it plainly, since nobody else will. Aunt Ada knows where every document in this building lives, including the ones she hid from me. Ishikawa can do the mathematics. And I can read a ledger faster than either of you and I know exactly what Marsh's boy looks like when he's snooping, because he's been doing it since Tuesday. We divide the labor. We verify the ledgers against the clock logs before the inventory reaches them. And we do it at night, when the gentlemen historians are asleep and dreaming of tenure."

"It's dangerous," Ada said. It was not an objection; it was a warning offered like a lantern.

"Everything worth doing is dangerous," said Juniper. "You taught me that yourself, only you taught it by counterexample."

For a moment I thought the old wound would close the conversation. But Ada Verlow looked at her niece — really looked, the way you look at someone whose growing you've missed while blinking against your own grief — and something in her face shifted, tectonic and quiet.

"Counterexample noted," she said dryly. "Very well. An alliance of strangers. We shake on it, or we don't do it."

We shook on it. Her hand was cold and strong; Juniper's was warm and quick. Standing between them in the lamplight with the snow hissing at the windows, I felt something I had no professional vocabulary for — the sensation, unfamiliar as a new tool, of having been *included*. Of being, provisionally but genuinely, among my own people.

"One condition," I added, as we separated. "The clock gets repaired properly first. If I'm going to use it as a witness, it has to be telling the truth. I'll start tonight."

"Tonight?" Juniper raised an eyebrow. "You've been up half the night already."

"Clockmakers keep strange hours. It's in the apprenticeship contract."

But when I got upstairs to fetch my tools, I stopped at the landing window, because down on Main Street, despite the storm, a lantern was moving — unhurried, deliberate — from the hotel toward the observatory hill. It paused at the foot of the path. It stood there a long moment, pointed upward at the dark tower where the great clock ticked away honestly above a town built on a lie.

Then it turned around and went back.

I fetched my tools faster.
