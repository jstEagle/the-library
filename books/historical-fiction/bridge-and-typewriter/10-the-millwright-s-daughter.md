## 10. The Millwright's Daughter

Juniper had a theory about old people and doors. Her theory was that the older a person got, the more they wanted to talk, and the less anyone let them — so that by the time you knocked on their door at half past nine on a Tuesday morning, you weren't interrupting anything. You were delivering.

She stood on Mrs. Halloway's step with her coat buttoned to the chin, the phonograph in its wicker case heavy against her hip, and rehearsed her opening line for the fourth time. *Good morning, Mrs. Halloway. I'm cataloging for the Verlow archive.* True. *We're preserving the town's history before it's lost.* Also true, though not in the way the railroad men meant when they said it. *Would you mind telling me about your father?*

That last one was the trap door under all of it. Everything depended on how she asked.

The door opened before she knocked. Mrs. Halloway was small and round as a teapot, with white hair pinned up in a knot that seemed to be holding on out of spite, and eyes the pale blue of skimmed milk that went straight through Juniper like light through a window.

"You're Ada Verlow's girl," she said. "I can tell by the scowl. Ada scowls exactly like that when she's about to ask for something."

"I don't scowl."

"You do. It's a good one. Very economical." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the cold does. And mind the cat."

The cat was an enormous gray beast draped across the stove bench like a fur stole someone had abandoned. It opened one eye, assessed Juniper, and closed it again, having found her unremarkable. Juniper respected this immediately. She wished more people would do the same.

The house smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and something baking that Juniper intended to identify and possibly acquire. Mrs. Halloway cleared a chair of knitting, then a second chair of newspapers, then a third chair of what appeared to be forty years of buttons sorted into jars, muttering apologies the whole time as though the chairs had been sat in by burglars.

"I've come about the archive," Juniper said, once she'd been installed in the least cluttered chair and handed tea she hadn't asked for. "We're making a record of the town. Properly. Not the way the railroad fellows are doing it."

Something moved behind those milk-pale eyes — quick, like a fish under ice. "Railroad fellows," Mrs. Halloway repeated. "In our archive."

"Two of them. Very polite. They have gloves."

"Gloves." She said the word the way other people said *rats*. "My father always said you could measure a man's intentions by his gloves. A workingman takes his gloves off to shake your hand. A thief keeps them on so he doesn't leave prints."

Juniper felt the whole conversation tilt toward her like a table with one short leg. She set down her cup carefully. "Mrs. Halloway. Your father was the millwright. Elias Halloway."

"Elias Halloway built the millrace, the mill gates, the bridge footings, and the coffin of every man in this town who died between 1871 and 1893, because he was the only one who could be trusted to make the corners square." She said it without pride, the way you'd recite a grocery list. "He also signed things. He signed everything. He'd sign a petition for a new schoolhouse and a complaint about the Hendersons' rooster in the same hour, with the same pen, and you could set a watch by his hand. Loops on the E like a bird flying into wind. My mother used to say she could pick his signature out of a crowd the way you pick your own child crying."

"And if you saw a signature that wasn't his—"

"I'd know it." The fish under the ice surfaced. "Is that why you've come? With your little machine there?"

Juniper looked down at the phonograph case, which she had been pretending was a picnic basket. "It records voices," she said. "On wax cylinders. Whatever you say onto it stays said. You could play it back in fifty years and it would still be your voice saying it."

Mrs. Halloway regarded the case for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the window, and looked out at the frozen millrace visible past her garden fence — the same race her father had built, still running dark water under its lid of ice.

"My father died in '94," she said to the window. "Before the flood. Before the grant office burned. He never knew what they did with his name after he was done using it." She turned around. "But I knew him. And I know his hand. And I have been waiting thirty years for somebody to ask me."

* * *

They set up the phonograph on the kitchen table, which required moving the cat, which required negotiation. Juniper threaded the horn, checked the cylinder, and then sat with her hands in her lap for a full minute, suddenly terrified. This was the moment. If she fumbled it — if the recording came out warped, if she asked the wrong question, if Mrs. Halloway's courage failed halfway through — there might not be another chance. The railroad men were in the archive every day now, box by box, getting closer. Somewhere across town, Oscar was probably elbow-deep in clock guts, and Ada was probably smiling at men she wanted to hit with a poker. Juniper was the only one here, and the only tool she had was a machine that recorded wax and a woman who remembered.

"State your name for the record," Juniper said. "And the date. And — whatever else you want the future to know."

Mrs. Halloway folded her hands. She looked, for the first time, exactly like someone's daughter.

"My name is Margaret Halloway. Born in this town in 1861, in the house my father built with his own hands, which is this house, which is why I am still in it." Her voice came out steady and clear, pitched for the horn the way people instinctively pitched their voices for church. "Today is the fourteenth of February, 1912. And I am going to tell you about signatures."

And she did.

She told it the way her father must have told stories at this same table — plainly, with the corners squared. In the autumn of 1884, a man named Corliss Ash had come through town representing the Meridian Land & Timber Company, all teeth and kid gloves, buying up parcels along the river for what he called a "shipping concern." Elias Halloway sold him nothing. Elias Halloway didn't trust a man whose boots were cleaner than his ledger, and he said so at the grange meeting, loudly, twice.

"But in the spring of '86," Mrs. Halloway said, "when the first survey stakes went in along the north bank — stakes on land Father had never sold — he went to the county office to see the grant book. And there was his name. His own name, or near enough, signed to a deed dated October the ninth, 1885, selling the millrace easement and eleven acres of bottomland for sixty dollars."

The needle hissed softly through the wax. Juniper barely breathed.

"He wrote to the company. He wrote to the county clerk. He wrote to the territorial governor, and got back a letter that thanked him for his interest. He spent two years and most of his savings trying to prove the signature false, and the trouble was—" her voice caught, just slightly, and steadied— "the trouble was that whoever forged it was *good*. They'd practiced. The loops were almost right. Almost. But Father kept every letter he ever wrote, and he had me fetch them, and we laid them side by side on this very table, and even I could see it. On the true ones, the E loops fly forward, like a bird into wind. On the deed, they hang backward. Like a bird shot down."

She paused. The cylinder turned. The fire ticked.

"He died believing everyone knew and no one cared. And the worst part, the part I've carried, is that he was right. Because in '89 the river took the grant office, and everyone said *well, that settles it, no proof either way now*, and I thought — no. No, it doesn't settle it. *I* am the proof. I sat at that table. I saw both hands."

She stopped. In the quiet, the phonograph's mechanism whirred, faithful as a heart.

"That's where I'll leave it for today," Mrs. Halloway said. "You can turn that off now, girl. My tea's gone cold and I've got rolls in the oven, and neither of us is going anywhere until you've had one."

* * *

Juniper played the cylinder back once, right there, because Mrs. Halloway insisted on hearing herself. When her own voice filled the kitchen — *loops like a bird into wind, like a bird shot down* — the old woman pressed her hand flat against her breastbone and laughed until she had to sit down.

"Well," she said, wiping her eyes. "Thirty years of talking to a cat, and now I'm in a machine. Father would have loved it. He distrusted anything that worked too well."

"It worked too well," Juniper agreed. "Which means I need to be careful with it. Wax melts. Wax cracks. I'm going to keep this one somewhere safe."

"Somewhere the glove-men won't find it."

"Exactly."

Mrs. Halloway studied her over the rim of her refilled cup. "Ada Verlow's girl," she said again, but differently this time. "You know, I knew Tom Verlow. Slightly. Decent man. Stubborn as a stump about documents." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "Whatever it is you three are doing in that archive — and don't tell me it's cataloging, I was born at night but not last night — you should know you're not the first to try. And the others are all either dead or tired."

"We noticed," Juniper said. "We're hoping to be a third kind."

"What kind is that?"

"The kind that finishes."

Mrs. Halloway considered this, then nodded slowly, the way you nod at a load-bearing wall. "Then take these." She rose, went to the bureau in the parlor, and came back with a tin biscuit box, dented, older than Juniper. Inside: letters. Dozens of them, tied in bundles with string gone soft as yarn. Elias Halloway's correspondence — the letters to the company, the county, the governor, and the replies. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a single sheet: a copy of the disputed deed itself, made in 1887 by a notary in Fairhaven who had apparently possessed both a conscience and a fear of fires.

"Father kept duplicates," Mrs. Halloway said. "He said paper was the only thing a poor man could hide that rich men couldn't burn all of." She pushed the box across the table. "I'm eighty-one. I can't climb down to any vaults or cross any millraces. But I can hand this to someone who can, and I can say my piece into your wax, and between the two of us, maybe the bird flies forward again."

Juniper took the box. It weighed almost nothing and entirely too much.

* * *

She walked home along the river road with the phonograph under one arm and the biscuit box inside her coat, against her chest, where it wouldn't fall in the river. The afternoon had gone gold and hard, the ice on the millrace shining like the lid of something sleeping. For the first time since the storm had shut the bridge and stranded her with a stranger and a secret, Juniper felt something that wasn't anger or dread. It was smaller and stranger than either. It felt like being useful. It felt, almost, like hope — fragile as wax, yes, liable to crack if you breathed on it wrong, but real, and hers, and warm from sitting next to another person's courage all morning.

She was so busy feeling it that she nearly walked past the two men standing at the foot of the observatory hill.

They had stopped to admire the view, or to pretend to. The younger one — Voss, the one with the smile like a locked drawer — tipped his hat to her. "Miss Verlow. Out enjoying the thaw?"

"Not yet enjoying it," Juniper said. "Just previewing."

"And what's in the basket?" Voss asked pleasantly, nodding at the phonograph case. "Picnic weather coming."

"Recording equipment," Juniper said. "For the archive inventory. I'm sure you'll see it soon enough."

Something flickered behind his pleasantness — quick, like a fish under ice. "Recording equipment," he repeated. "How modern."

"Progress," Juniper said, and smiled her mother's smile, the one that ended conversations, and walked on up the hill without hurrying, counting the seconds until she could get the biscuit box under a floorboard and the cylinder into cold storage, and wondering — with the first cold drop of dread landing in the middle of all that warmth — exactly how much a man like Voss already knew, and who he'd wire ahead to about it.
