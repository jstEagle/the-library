## 11. Inventory

They arrived at the observatory at nine o'clock sharp, which Oscar might have admired under other circumstances. Punctuality was a virtue he held nearly sacred. But there was something in the way Mr. Voss consulted his pocket watch before knocking — a little ceremony of it, the watch produced and read and returned to its pocket like a magistrate producing a warrant — that made punctuality feel less like a virtue and more like a threat.

"Master Ishikawa." Voss removed his hat on the doorstep, revealing hair the color of wet sand, combed with geometric precision. Behind him, old Marsh stood with his hands clasped at his back, looking past them both at the observatory dome the way a man looks at a church. "We understand from Mrs. Verlow that you're engaged on the great clock. We wouldn't dream of interfering. Mr. Marsh and I will confine ourselves to the archive rooms below."

"The clock is three floors up," Oscar said.

"Then we shall be very quiet indeed."

Ada let them in. She had dressed for the occasion as though for a funeral — which, Oscar thought, watching her lead the two men down the stairwell toward the stacks, was not far wrong. Her black dress was buttoned to the throat, her silver hair pinned without a single stray wire, and her voice had the flat courtesy of a woman reading terms aloud to an opposing counsel.

"The ledgers are catalogued by year," she was saying. "The grant materials are in the east room. Mind the damp near the north wall; the mortar's been failing since before my husband died."

"You've kept everything beautifully, Mrs. Verlow," Marsh said. His voice was soft, unhurried, genuinely admiring. "This is a remarkable collection. Most river towns lost half their papers to mildew and mice."

"My husband believed paper was a kind of promise," Ada said. "You don't let promises rot if you can help it."

Oscar carried his toolbox upstairs and tried very hard to think only about clocks.

* * *

The trouble with repairing a master clock under observation was that the evidence of the crime was inside the mechanism he was being paid to fix. The altered cam sat in its housing like a tumor, worn bright at the points where decades of deliberate contact had polished it. Any man who knew gearing could look at it and see what it was. Voss, Oscar judged, did not know gearing. But Voss did not need to know gearing. He needed only to see Oscar's face when Oscar looked at it.

So Oscar spent the first morning performing.

He stripped the escapement down to its jewels and laid each part on flannel in the order of disassembly, which was correct procedure and also theater. He cleaned pivots that were already clean. He adjusted the crutch by a degree and a half, listened to the tick, frowned professionally, and adjusted it back. Through the floorboards he could hear the railroad men at work below: the scrape of chair legs, the rustle of tissue paper, Marsh's low murmured commentary, and Voss writing — always writing — in a hand that moved fast and pressed hard.

At noon Juniper brought up tea and bread on a tray and set it beside him with elaborate indifference.

"They're doing the east room," she said quietly, not looking at him, arranging the teacup with the handle turned just so. "The grant ledgers are next. Ada put the 1884 volume in the bottom of a box of hymnals, but Voss has a system. He checks every spine. He doesn't trust anyone else's labels."

"How long do we have?"

"An hour. Maybe two." She straightened. "You're sure the cam proves what you think it proves? If they find nothing but hymnals and we've hidden the one thing that—"

"It proves it," Oscar said. "The wheel was cut for a forty-tooth count. The cam advances the detent by eleven minutes during thaw season, when land surveys were dated. I'd stake my journeyman's piece on it."

Juniper looked at him for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted — the suspicion draining out of it and leaving behind, briefly, something younger and more frightened. "Then don't let them see your face," she said, and took the tray downstairs.

Oscar ate the bread. It occurred to him, chewing, that four weeks ago his greatest worry had been whether his master would let him fit a regulator alone. Now he was hiding evidence of a thirty-year fraud inside a clock while agents of a railroad inventoried a town's memory box by box, and the strangest part — the part he would never have admitted to anyone — was that he had never felt more awake in his life.

* * *

The problem revealed itself at two o'clock, in the person of Mr. Voss, standing at the foot of the clock tower stairs with his hat once more in his hands and his smile once more in place.

"Master Ishikawa. Might I impose upon you for a moment? Mr. Marsh has found something he'd like your professional opinion on."

Oscar wiped his hands slowly, giving his heart time to settle. "I'm no archivist."

"No. But you're a man who understands mechanisms, dates, tolerances. And this—" Voss gestured upward, gracious as a hotel doorman, "—is a mechanism, isn't it? The whole town. Clocks, ledgers, deeds. All of it meshing. All of it telling a story about when things happened." His eyes were pale blue and entirely without malice, which somehow made them worse. "Mr. Marsh believes some of the dates in the east room don't mesh. I told him dates are like trains. Sometimes they run early. Sometimes late. But they arrive where the company says they arrive."

"I wouldn't know about trains," Oscar said. "I've only ever worked on clocks. In clocks, if a gear doesn't match its neighbors, the whole train stops."

Voss's smile held. "Yes," he said. "That is rather the point."

* * *

Marsh had spread six documents across a table in the east room, and Oscar understood immediately that the hymnal gambit had failed — or rather, that it had never been tested, because Marsh had found his discrepancies elsewhere entirely. Survey plats. A ferry license. Two letters from the land office, both dated within days of grants recorded months apart.

"This signature," Marsh said, laying one gloved finger beside an ink flourish gone brown with age. "Hollis Whitcombe, county recorder. It appears here on March the third, 1885, and here on November the eleventh, 1884. A man cannot sign a document before the transaction it records exists. Unless"—and here the old man's voice grew very gentle, almost sorrowful—"'March the third' was never true. Unless the dates were written backward into the record to suit someone's needs."

"That seems a question for scholars," Oscar said carefully.

"I am a scholar, young man."

"Then you know better than I how documents lie." Oscar met his eyes. "I repair clocks. When I want to know whether a clock has been tampered with, I take it apart. I don't read the label on its case."

The silence that followed lasted perhaps four seconds, and Oscar spent all four of them understanding that he had said too much. Marsh's gaze did not sharpen — it *settled*, the way snow settles, weightlessly and everywhere.

"A practical philosophy," Marsh said at last. "And does your clock tell the true time?"

"It will," Oscar said, "when I'm finished with it."

* * *

That evening the three of them gathered in Ada's kitchen above the archive, and the tension came off all of them like steam off a kettle.

"He knows," Juniper said. She was pacing, three steps, turn, three steps, turn; the kitchen was small enough that pacing it resembled caging something. "Not everything. But he knows the dates are forged, and now he knows we know, because Oscar practically drew him a diagram."

"I did not draw him a diagram."

"You told him to take the town apart and check its gears."

"I told him how clocks work. There's a difference."

"There will be," Juniper said grimly, "when he finds it."

Ada had not spoken. She sat at the head of the table with her hands folded around a cup of tea gone cold, and when she finally lifted her head there was a stillness in her face that Oscar had learned, in these weeks, to fear more than any storm.

"He won't find it," she said. "Because tomorrow it leaves this house."

Juniper stopped pacing. "Mother—"

"Don't." Ada's voice cracked like ice in a hull, then steadied. "Not 'Mother.' Not yet. Listen to me, both of you. Marsh is old, and old scholars can be reached through their scholarship — I watched him hesitate today over that ferry license, Juniper, I saw it. But Voss is not a scholar. Voss is an instrument, and instruments get replaced when they break, and the men who sent him will send worse. Every hour those ledgers sit beneath this roof, we are betting this whole town's memory on a locked door and a girl's floorboards." She unfolded her hands. "Tomorrow morning, while they work the west room, the 1884 ledger goes into the clock case. Behind the movement, in the dead space under the chime barrel. No archivist searches a clock. But every clockmaker in America knows a movement comes apart, and Oscar is the only clockmaker here."

Oscar set down his cup. "You want me to hide stolen evidence inside the machine I'm repairing. Under the eyes of the men hunting it."

"I want you to keep a promise safe," Ada said, "the way paper should be kept." Then, quieter, with something breaking open underneath the iron: "Edmund would have done it himself."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the river ground its teeth against the ice, patient as a debtor.

"Well," Oscar said at last. "It's a tight fit. The ledger's thick." He heard his own voice as though from a small distance away, calm, professional, already measuring clearances in his head. "I'll need to remove the silent-strike lever. And someone will have to talk to Voss for twenty minutes."

Juniper smiled — her mother's smile, but warmer, aimed like a tool at a task. "I can talk to that man until spring," she said. "What should we talk about?"

"Anything," said Oscar, already standing, already reaching for his coat, feeling the strange lightness that had been growing in him since the day the bridge closed settle at last into something like certainty. "Talk to him about progress."

* * *

He did the work at dawn, before the knock could come, by lamplight, with his breath fogging in the cold of the tower. The ledger slid into the case behind the movement with a quarter inch to spare — he checked twice, three times, that no page corner touched the chime barrel, that nothing would jam, that the clock would run true above its hidden cargo like a ship carrying contraband through honest water. Then the panels went back on, the dust went back where dust belonged, and at nine o'clock sharp the knock came, and Voss consulted his watch on the doorstep, and the inventory resumed.

But when Oscar climbed the tower stairs at noon, he found the door at the top already ajar.

He had locked it. He was certain — as certain as a man can be of anything at the end of a night like his. He stood on the landing with his toolbox suddenly heavy as an anchor, listening: the tick of the master clock, steady, unbothered, keeping perfect time. And beneath it, from the gallery above, the sound of someone shifting their weight — softly, patiently, the way a man waits who has all day.

Oscar started up the stairs.
