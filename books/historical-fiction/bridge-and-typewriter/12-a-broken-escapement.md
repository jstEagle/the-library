## 12. A Broken Escapement

I have always believed that a clock speaks to the man who knows how to listen. Most people hear only ticking — a flat, patient noise, like rain on a roof. But put your ear against the case of a master clock, palm flat on the oak, and you will feel it thinking. The escape wheel catching, releasing. The pendulum swinging through air that thickens or thins with every change in the room. A clock in good health hums. A clock in trouble mutters.

The morning after the railroad men arrived at the archive for supper and stayed three hours past it, I climbed the observatory hill before dawn because the clock had stopped muttering altogether.

I knew before I opened the door.

There is a particular silence to a dead master clock, and it is not an empty silence. It is the silence of a room where someone has just finished speaking and everyone else is afraid to answer. I stood on the threshold with my key still in the lock and felt the wrongness come through the floorboards, up through my boots, into my teeth.

"No," I said, to nobody. "No, no, no."

* * *

The great clock of Verlow's Landing had kept time for forty-one years through heat, cold, floods, and — according to Ada — one incident involving a raccoon that she refused to elaborate on no matter how many times Juniper and I asked. It was built like a cathedral and tuned like a violin. And someone had taken a bar of iron to its escapement.

I will try to describe what I found without sounding like a man describing a wound on his own body, though that is precisely what it was.

The pallets were the first thing. They are small pieces of hardened steel, shaped almost like fingernails, and they catch the teeth of the escape wheel as it tries to run ahead — tick, release; tock, release — the whole personality of a clock living in those tiny catches. Someone had pried them loose and bent them back with something blunt. Not snapped. Bent. Snapping would have been rage. Bending was patience.

And then the cam.

You will remember — because I told you, and because you would be right to wonder why I hadn't hidden it sooner — the worn brass cam bolted behind the motion work, the alteration added decades after the clock's construction, the little machine inside the machine that made the clock run fast during exactly those seasons when land grant deadlines came due. I had measured it. I had drawn it, twice, once from memory in my room at night like a schoolboy practicing his letters. I had planned to remove it properly, photograph it, document everything.

It was gone.

Not damaged. *Removed.* The bolts backed out clean, oiled first by the feel of them, set aside neatly on the clock bed where I would find them. In their place, nothing but two bright scars in the brass plate and a small rectangle of cleaner metal where the cam had sat against it for thirty years, keeping the shape of its own guilt pressed into the plate like a fossil.

Whoever did this had wanted the clock broken, yes. But more than broken, they had wanted it *silent* — stripped of the only part of it that could testify.

I sat down on the winding stool, which was the only chair in the place, and I confess I put my head in my hands for some length of time. When I looked up, the sun had begun to come through the east windows in long amber bars, and the pendulum hung dead-still, and I thought: this is what Ada must have felt every day since her husband died. Standing in front of something beautiful that other men had made into a lie.

Then I got angry, which was better.

* * *

By the time I came down the hill, the anger had organized itself into a list, and lists are how I keep from drowning. Item one: tell Ada. Item two: tell Juniper. Item three: determine who. Items four through nine were variations on item three, because I kept circling it.

The younger historian's name was Pell. He was perhaps thirty, neat as a pinned collar, with the unfortunate habit of smiling a half-second before anyone finished a joke, as though jokes were transactions he wished to conclude efficiently. His elder colleague, Marsh, was another creature entirely — gray, watchful, courteous in the way of a man who has read enough history to know how it usually goes for the villains in the end, and finds this insufficiently reassuring.

They lodged at the hotel across from the mill. But last night, after supper, Pell had excused himself early — "an early start on the inventory," he'd said, smiling his half-second early smile — and I had seen him pause in the entryway to pull on his gloves. And I remembered, with the horrible clarity that memory saves for exactly these occasions, that yesterday afternoon, while I measured the cam with my calipers, I had turned around at a sound and found the observatory door open six inches and Pell standing in it.

He'd said, "Fascinating old mechanism," and asked whether the original maker's plates were intact, and left before I could answer.

I had thought nothing of it. That was the unbearable part. I had thought *nothing of it*, because I was raised to believe that a man who loves clocks wishes only to look at clocks, and I still, at twenty-two, mostly believe the world means well.

Ada met me at the archive door before I touched it. She was already dressed, already holding her coat, and there was a look on her face that told me she had felt it too — the silence rolling down off the hill like weather.

"It stopped," she said.

"Someone stopped it."

We looked at each other, and something passed between us that I am not sure either of us would have been able to put into words, though I have tried since. It was the passing of a burden, I think. For years she had known the truth and carried it alone, afraid. Now the truth had been attacked in her presence, and there was no longer any question of whether we would fight about it. Only of how.

"Juniper," she called up the stairs, "put the kettle on. And then take it off again, because we won't have time for tea."

"We always have time for tea," said Juniper from above, in the voice of a woman who has never once in her life hurried for a man with a clipboard, "but I'll make an exception, because your face says I should."

* * *

Upstairs, around the kitchen table that would become, over the following weeks, the most important table in the county, I laid out what remained of the evidence: the bent pallets wrapped in cloth, the two bright scars photographed in my memory, the bolts with their oily threads.

"They took the cam," I said.

Juniper, who had been pouring tea with theatrical calm, set the pot down hard enough to slop. "They can't have. You showed me where it was—"

"And now it isn't. And they didn't take the pallets' damage away, mind." I unwrapped them. "Look. Bent, not broken. If they only wanted to stop the clock, one blow would do. This — this is a man making sure the clock can't be made to *speak*. Pallets bent so the wear pattern is destroyed. Cam removed so it can't be measured. Everything that proved the alteration is either gone or scrambled."

Ada had gone very still. She was looking at the bolts. "Oiled threads," she said quietly. "That's a careful man's work. That's a man who has dismantled things before and been paid for it."

"Pell," I said.

"You can't know that," said Juniper.

"I can't prove it. But I saw him in the doorway yesterday while I had the calipers on the cam. And he left supper early last night, and the damage was done before dawn, and the observatory lock—" I hesitated. "The lock shows no forcing. Whoever did it had a key or a pick, and picks leave marks. There were no marks."

"Or," said Ada slowly, "whoever did it had a key."

"The keeper's spare," said Juniper. "Which hangs in the hall closet, which our guests walked past on their way to bed."

None of us said anything for a moment. The kettle ticked as it cooled, which struck me, even then, as a small disloyalty — a clockwork sound in a house whose clock had been murdered.

"We should go to the sheriff," said Juniper, but she said it the way one reads a line in a play one doesn't believe.

"On what charge?" said Ada. "Vandalism? With what witness? With what proof? They'll say the clock failed of age, and half the town will nod, because half the town has spent thirty years being told the clock never lies and would rather not examine the arithmetic." She rubbed her eyes. She looked suddenly very tired and very much like a woman who had buried a husband and a fight in the same grave and was now being asked to dig both up. "No. We do this the way Eli would have done it. Quietly. Thoroughly. With paper."

"Paper," I said, "and a new escapement."

They both looked at me.

"The clock itself is still a witness," I said, and here I felt the old professional warmth kindle under all the fear, the thing that had pulled me across a bridge into a strange town in the first place. "A master clock keeps a record just by running, if you know how to read it. The maintenance log — Ada, you said there's a maintenance log going back to installation?"

"In the locked drawer," said Ada. "Which is open now." Something crossed her face — the ghost of a woman deciding, finally and forever, not to be afraid. "Which is open now," she repeated, as if sealing a vow. "Every oiling, every adjustment, every repair, signed and dated by whoever performed it, including entries I now understand were signed by men who were paid to make the clock lie."

"Then here's what the vandals missed." I leaned forward. "The log will show when the cam was installed and by whom. The wear on the *remaining* wheels — the ones they didn't touch because they don't understand them — will corroborate it. A wheel driven too fast for thirty years wears differently. Unevenly. I can read that wear like handwriting. They took the confession note off the table; they left the indentations in the blotter."

Juniper was grinning at me in a way that made me briefly worried for Pell's safety. "Oscar Ishikawa," she said, "you are the only person I have ever met who threatens a railroad with horology."

"Somebody has to," I said.

* * *

I went back up the hill that afternoon, because leaving a dead master clock alone overnight is poor practice even when it hasn't been assaulted, and because I wanted to take stock of the full damage in daylight.

It was worse than I'd thought, and better.

Worse: the second pallet had a hairline crack I hadn't caught by lamplight. Better: the great wheels themselves were untouched, the barrel, the train, the pendulum suspension — all intact. Whoever had done this had done it quickly and narrowly. Which meant they had been interrupted, or hurried, or both. Which meant they had come at night, in a storm's aftermath, with cold iron and colder nerve, and they had not enjoyed it.

I found one more thing, and I have debated for a long time whether to record it here, and decided that a testimony which omits inconvenient details is worth exactly nothing. So:

At the base of the clock case, on the floor, half-hidden under the winding stool, I found a glove. Left hand. Fine leather, city-made, with a small tear at the thumb seam repaired with thread far too fine for a workingman's kit. It was not mine. It was not Ada's. It was certainly not Juniper's.

I put it in my breast pocket, and I did not smile, and I did not let myself think the name yet, because an accusation built on a glove is a house built on frost. But I carried it down the hill like a coal in my chest.

* * *

That evening the historians came to the archive again — "to express concern," Marsh said, "about reports the tower clock has failed." Reports, plural, delivered presumably by themselves to themselves, a loop of gossip with no origin and no exit.

Ada received them in the parlor with tea and a face like a frozen lake. Marsh expressed regret. Pell expressed regret also, and smiled his half-second-early smile, and I watched his hands.

He wore no gloves. Of course he wore no gloves; it was indoors. But I noticed — because once a clockmaker notices a seam he cannot unnotice it — that his left glove, resting folded on top of his valise by the door, had a small repair at the thumb. Thread too fine for a workingman's kit.

He felt me looking. Perhaps he had been feeling me look for some time; men who break clocks at night develop a sensitivity to being watched. Our eyes met across the parlor for perhaps a second and a half, and in that second and a half neither of us smiled, and something was settled between us that neither of us would speak aloud for weeks.

Pell raised his teacup slightly, an inch, a toast to nothing.

I nodded back, once, the way a clock ticks: one beat, deliberate, and then the next.

Marsh was saying something about the inventory schedule — beginning Monday, box by box, the grant ledgers among them, "for preservation, of course, purely for preservation" — and Ada was answering him in her level archivist's voice, and outside the window the river groaned once under its ice, a long deep sound like a held breath being tested.

When they had gone, Juniper blew out her breath and said, "Well. They've broken our clock and stolen our proof and they're coming for the ledgers on Monday."

"Yes," said Ada.

"So," said Juniper, pulling the cork from the ink bottle with the air of a woman loading a weapon, "someone had better teach me everything that maintenance log says before Monday morning."

And I reached into my breast pocket, and set the glove on the table between us, and said, "First, I think, we should talk about where I found this."
