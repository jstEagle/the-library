## 13. The Long Night

The silence was the worst of it.

Ada Verlow had lived forty-one years in the observatory, and for thirty-nine of them the master clock had been her heartbeat. She had grown so accustomed to its tick that she no longer heard it, the way a person stops hearing their own pulse — until it stopped. Then she understood that she had been listening all along, every hour of every night, and that what filled the house now was not quiet but absence.

She stood in the great clock room with a lamp turned low and looked at the dead mechanism the way one looks at a husband's coat still hanging by the door.

Oscar had covered the broken escapement with a linen cloth before he went to bed, which she thought was kind of him, though she couldn't have said why. Perhaps because you cover a face. The boy had been up half the night examining the wreckage with his jeweler's loupe, saying very little, and when he finally spoke he said only: "It wasn't rust. Rust is slow. This was quick." And then he had looked at her with those dark, careful eyes and asked if she wanted him to say more, and she had said no, not yet, not tonight.

Tonight there was only the stopping.

She crossed the room in her dressing gown, cold seeping through the floorboards, and put her palm flat against the clock's case. The wood was already losing the warmth of its running. By morning it would be as cold as any furniture.

"Forty years," she said aloud, to nobody. Her voice sounded wrong in the room — too small, unaccompanied. "Forty years, Thomas."

There was no answer, of course. There hadn't been an answer since 1889, and she was long past expecting one. But she had fallen into the habit lately — these last weeks, since the boy arrived, since the cam, since the drawer stood open on her desk like a mouth that would not close — of talking to Thomas again. Juniper pretended not to notice. Oscar, bless him, treated it as perfectly ordinary conversation, as though widows addressing dead husbands were simply part of the maintenance schedule of any well-run observatory.

She let her hand rest on the case a moment longer. Then she turned down the lamp and went upstairs, and did not wind anything, because there was nothing to wind, and lay in the dark listening to the nothing.

* * *

Morning came anyway. It always did; it simply no longer announced itself.

Ada came down to find Juniper already at the stove, arms crossed, glaring at the kettle as if it had personally insulted her.

"It's snowing again," Juniper said, without turning around. "The historians are at the archive already. I watched Mr. Pell go in through the side door with his little white gloves on. His *little white gloves*, Aunt Ada. Who wears gloves to handle documents? A man who wants them dead, that's who."

"Marsh wears gloves too," Ada said.

"Marsh takes his off when he thinks something matters." Juniper poured the tea without ceremony, sloshing a little into the saucer, and pushed a cup across the table at her aunt. "That's how you can tell about Marsh. He touches things like they're alive. Pell touches things like they're evidence to be disposed of."

Ada sat. The tea was too hot and too strong, Juniper's usual failure, and Ada drank it anyway and loved her for it.

Below them, faintly, she could hear Oscar moving about in the workshop he'd commandeered — the scrape of a chair, the small musical clatter of his tools being laid out in order. He had been down there since five. She knew because she'd heard him pass her door. The boy worked the way drowning men swim.

"He hasn't slept," Juniper said, following her aunt's attention downward.

"Neither have you."

"I slept." Juniper sat across from her and wrapped both hands around her own cup. "Two hours. That counts. Oscar says two hours counts if you do it properly."

"Does he." Ada smiled despite herself. "He says a great many things with more confidence than accuracy."

"He says the wheel will take three days. Cutting the teeth by hand, matching the count, fitting the pallets — he drew me a diagram at breakfast yesterday and explained it twice and I understood perhaps a tenth, but he was so *pleased* with the diagram that I told him it was beautiful." Juniper's jaw tightened. "Three days, Aunt. And the river's talking already. I heard it under the ice this morning, that knocking sound. You know what it means when the river starts knocking."

The thaw. Early this year, everyone said so. Three days might be four days. Might be two.

"And until then," Juniper went on, quieter, "the clock is stopped, and the historians are downstairs in your archive cataloguing everything we love, and you—" She stopped. Recalibrated, the way she did, her sharpness folding itself away with visible effort. "You didn't sleep either. Don't lie to me, I can always tell."

Ada looked into her cup.

Because she hadn't slept, and worse than that — because somewhere in the small hours, in that terrible stopped-clock silence, something in her had quietly laid down its arms.

She had lain in bed and thought: *Thomas is dead. The ledgers are paper, and paper burns, and they burned once already, or enough of them did. The flood took the grant office and it took Thomas and it took the truth, and the railroad has been patient for forty years, and patience wins. Pell will catalogue the boxes. Marsh will write his report. The company will print its histories, and in fifty years nobody will know there was ever another version, and I will be buried next to Thomas under a stone that says BELOVED WIFE as though I hadn't spent my whole widowhood afraid.*

*I am tired,* she had thought, with a relief so vast it frightened her. *I could just be tired now. I could stop. Nobody could blame me. Even the clock has stopped.*

And she had slept, actually slept, two dreamless hours, the first real rest in weeks — and woken feeling like a traitor.

"Juniper," she said slowly, "what would you say if I told you I was thinking of letting it go?"

Juniper set down her cup very carefully, which was how Ada knew she was frightened. Juniper only handled things carefully when she was frightened of breaking them or of being broken.

"Letting go of what, exactly?"

"All of it. The ledgers. The testimony. The whole rotten business." Ada heard her own voice as though from a distance, level and reasonable, a voice giving directions to a neighbor. "Let the historians have the record. Let them write their clean little history. We are two women and a boy against a railroad, Junie. Thomas fought them with the whole grant office behind him and the river ate him for it. I have spent twenty-two years keeping this archive out of spite and calling it duty, and I am *tired*, and I think maybe God stopped that clock to tell me I'm permitted to sit down."

The kitchen was silent except for the kettle ticking as it cooled — a small, mocking echo of a heartbeat.

Juniper did not shout. Ada almost wished she would. Instead her niece studied her for a long moment with her father's gray eyes — Robert Verlow, gone ten years now, gentle and stubborn as his stepfather before him — and said:

"You don't mean it."

"Juniper—"

"You don't, though. You're *testing* it. Like testing ice before you commit your weight." Juniper leaned forward. "If you truly meant to quit, you'd have said it cheerfully. You always get polite right before you surrender to something, Aunt Ada. You were polite for a month before you decided to take in the Halloway papers against the county's wishes. You were polite for a week before you married Uncle Thomas against everybody's advice." Her mouth twitched, despite everything. "Politeness is your battle cry. It means you've already decided and you're bracing us for it."

"That is a slanderous theory," Ada said, "and completely accurate, and I hate that you're nineteen going on forty-five."

"I'm twenty-two, and you forget things when you're tired." Juniper reached across the table and took her aunt's hand. Her fingers were ink-stained and chapped and did not let go. "Here is what I think. I think you got quiet in the night because the clock stopped and it sounded like the year the river came, and you are eighty percent grief and twenty percent cowardice, which is the same ratio as everyone brave I've ever met. So we won't decide anything today. Today we do the next thing. That's all anybody does, the next thing. Oscar taught me that, actually, with his stupid beautiful diagram. One tooth at a time."

Ada's throat ached. "When did you become the sensible one?"

"The moment you weren't. It was Tuesday."

Despite everything — the dead clock below, the white gloves downstairs, the river knocking under the ice — Ada laughed. It came out cracked but it came, and Juniper held her hand tighter until the laugh finished whatever it needed to finish.

* * *

In the afternoon the snow came back, heavy and windless, falling straight down like the world being shaken through a sieve, and the light in the parlor went blue and dim, and Ada found herself doing the thing she had been avoiding for three days: opening Thomas's trunk.

It sat at the back of her closet under a quilt. She had not opened it since the move to the rooms above the archive — had not opened it, truly, since Juniper was fifteen and went looking for Christmas presents and cried for an hour over a letter she found and never mentioned again.

Inside, under shirts gone thin with age and a folded suit that still smelled, faintly, impossibly, of river water and pipe smoke, lay the letters.

They were not letters to her. That was the thing people never understood when Ada told them Thomas Verlow had been a writer of letters. He wrote to everyone — to the county surveyor, to members of Congress, to newspaper editors, to the millwright Halloway, to his brother in the territorial capital — hundreds upon hundreds of pages, arguing, documenting, pleading, cajoling. But the ones in the trunk were different. These were stamped and addressed and sealed and never sent, because the day he sealed them, the flood came, and the river kept its appointment before the postman could.

Ada carried the bundle to the parlor and sat with it on her knees while the blue light failed, and she did not open it, and after a while Juniper came in and saw it and understood immediately, because Juniper always understood immediately, and went without a word to fetch Oscar.

The boy came in still wiping solder-bright hands on his rag, hair wild, eyes shadowed, looking between the trunk contents and Ada's face with the anxious competence of a man diagnosing a patient.

"Is it broken?" he asked. "Can I help?"

"You can listen," Ada said. "I find I cannot read them anymore alone. My voice stops working around the third page. It has been that way for years. Juniper, however, has never once in her life had a voice stop working."

"Never," Juniper agreed solemnly, sitting down on the floor by the fire and taking the bundle. She weighed it in her hands. "These are the unsent ones? All of them?"

"All of them."

"Then we're doing this properly. Oldest first." She broke the seal on the topmost letter with a care that contradicted her entire personality and unfolded the brittle pages, and began to read.

*"'Dear Mr. Ellery Halloway,'"* Juniper read. *"'You asked me plainly whether your father's mark on the 1879 assignment was his own, and I will answer you as plainly as the law permits me, which is: no, and you already know it. Your father could not have signed that document on the fourth of September because your father was setting millstones in my cellar on the third and drinking my coffee on the fifth, and a man does not travel ninety miles between coffees. The signature is his hand and yet not his will, which is a trick the railroad learned from the forgers' guild and improved upon...'"*

Oscar had lowered himself onto the hearthstone. Ada watched him — the apprentice clockmaker who had come among them six weeks ago to fix an escapement and instead found the escapement of an entire town, altered decades ago by hands that wanted time itself to lie — and she watched his expression change as the letters went on, letter after letter, Juniper's clear voice filling the darkening room.

Thomas wrote about land, mostly. About the grants, the assignments, the quiet transfers of whole farms into company hands on the strength of signatures from men who had been drunk, deceived, dead, or twelve years old at the alleged date of signing. But woven through the legal arguments, the way gold runs through quartz, were the other things. The things he never said at the dinner table.

*"'I tell you, Ellery, I begin to understand why they burn records,'"* Juniper read, in her grandfather-in-law's voice, and Ada's chest tightened. *"'A document is a dangerous thing precisely in proportion to how boring it appears. No man has ever been hanged for a poem, but men have been ruined by a margin note. If I survive this business I shall found a society for the preservation of tedious documents, and its motto shall be: THEY LOOK LIKE NOTHING. That is the whole secret. They look like nothing.'"*

"Grandda," Juniper said softly, lowering the page. "He sounds like you."

"He sounded like himself," Ada corrected, automatically, and then heard what she'd said, and pressed her knuckles to her mouth, because *he sounds like you* was aimed at her, and it was true, and she had built her whole widowhood on the principle that she was merely curating him, keeping his flame lit, when all along—

"—Read the next one," she said thickly.

The next was to a senator. The next to his brother, worried and wry. The next, near the bottom of the bundle, thinner paper, hurried hand, made Ada reach out and stop Juniper with a touch on her wrist.

"This one," Ada said. "I'll take this one."

She unfolded it herself. She had not read it since 1889. She had thought she remembered every word. She had remembered almost none of them.

*"'My dear Ada,'"* she read aloud, and her voice held. *"'If you are reading this, then I have been optimistic about the ferry schedule again, or pessimistic about the weather, or the river has finally decided to collect the debt I have been flirting with for a decade. Do not let anyone tell you I was careless. I was extremely careful. I was careful for eleven years and I was carrying exactly the documents we needed and I judged the ice would hold, and I want it entered into the record that I was not reckless, merely certain, and history will eventually distinguish between the two, though possibly not in our favor.'"*

Oscar made a small sound — almost a laugh, strangled by respect — and Ada found she was smiling, wetly, absurdly, because that was Thomas, that was entirely Thomas, joking his way onto the ice.

*"'You will want to give up now, my darling, because you will be tired, and you are never so sensible as when you are tired, and the world will offer you a comfortable chair and tell you the past is drowned. Here is what I know about rivers. The past is never drowned. Rivers keep everything — every stick, every ledger, every body — and return them downstream eventually, at their own pace, to whoever happens to be standing on the bank when the water gives them up. You have always been the one standing on the bank, Ada. You were the one on the bank the day we met, and I fell in trying to impress you, which you have never once let me forget, and I regret nothing except the hat.

"'So when the river gives up its secrets — and it will, it is only biding, that is what rivers do — do not be the woman in the comfortable chair. Be the one on the bank. And mind the hat.'"*

The room was silent. The fire popped. Below them, muffled by floors, the house's stopped heart did not beat, and outside the snow fell straight and endless, and Ada Verlow sat with a twenty-two-year-old letter in her hands and discovered that she was not, in fact, finished.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was rough but it was hers, all hers, "he was insufferable."

"Aunt Ada—" Juniper began.

"He was completely insufferable and he is correct and I have wasted an entire night being eighty percent grief." She stood, joints protesting, and crossed to the window, and looked down through the falling snow toward the observatory hill where the master clock stood dead in the dark, waiting for a boy with a graver and three days of his life. "One tooth at a time. Isn't that the doctrine?"

"That's the doctrine," Juniper said.

"Then here is the next thing." Ada turned. The blue light made her shadow long across the floor, and for the first time in weeks she felt tall inside it. "Tomorrow, while Oscar cuts his wheel and you charm Mrs. Halloway into telling us everything her father ever said over supper, I am going to sit down across the table from Mr. Pell and his white gloves, and I am going to help him with his inventory. Cheerfully. Thoroughly. Page by page, box by box."

Juniper stared. "You're going to *help* them?"

"My dear girl, who knows that collection better than I? Every box, every folder, every misfiled scrap." Ada allowed herself the smallest smile she had worn in twenty-two years, and it felt wonderful, like a shoe finally broken in. "Nobody hides a thing better than the person officially in charge of finding it. He looks like nothing, Thomas said. So we shall make sure the right things look like nothing — until the clock runs and the river speaks and we put them somewhere the railroad cannot burn them."

Oscar rose from the hearthstone, holding his rag, and Ada saw the boy look at her the way apprentices look at masters — and something in her chest, some stopped mechanism of her own, caught its pallet and released, and began, very slowly, to tick.

From below, faint under the floors, came the sound of Oscar's tools being taken up again, one bright piece at a time.

And far off, beneath the snow and the fields and the frozen millrace, the river knocked on the underside of the ice — patient, certain, keeping its own count — as if it too had heard the letters, and meant, in its season, to give everything back.
