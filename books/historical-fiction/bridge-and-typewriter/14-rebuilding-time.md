## 14. Rebuilding Time

The sabotaged escapement lay on the workbench like a small murdered bird.

Oscar had not touched it since dawn. He had looked at it, catalogued its wounds the way a doctor catalogs a patient he already knows he cannot save — the pallet teeth bent back on themselves, the arbor scored where someone had forced it with a tool too blunt to be a burglar's and too ignorant to be a clockmaker's. The younger historian, Mr. Pell, had hands that were soft but strong. Oscar had watched those hands take notes for three days. He knew what they could do.

"You're glaring at it," Juniper said from the doorway. "Glaring won't fix it."

"No," Oscar agreed. "But it's honest work all the same."

Below them, through the floorboards, came the sound of the railroad men's morning routine: the scrape of chairs in the archive, the polite coughs, Marsh's low voice asking Ada whether she had slept well in a tone that suggested he hoped very much she hadn't. Ada's answers drifted up, warm as fresh bread and exactly as nourishing to an enemy.

Oscar pulled his notebook from his coat and opened it to the pages he'd filled the night before — or what passed for night, since he'd spent most of it with his eyes closed, reconstructing the escape wheel from memory. That was the trouble with sabotage done by amateurs: they had destroyed the wheel itself, but they could not destroy the fact that Oscar Ishikawa had held it in both hands, measured it with his calipers, and written down its proportions in shorthand of his own devising before young Pell's careful eyes had ever settled on him with that particular mild curiosity.

Mostly. He checked his figures again. There was one gap — the count of teeth at the wheel's outer edge, which he'd noted as *forty-something* because at the time he'd been more interested in the cam, in the beautiful, damning alteration bolted inside the great clock like a confession hidden in a wall. Forty-something would not do. A wheel with forty-one teeth kept different time than one with forty-two, and the whole point of everything — the ledgers, the photographs still damp in their hiding place, Mrs. Halloway's trembling testimony coiled in wax — was that time would finally be made to tell the truth.

He would have to count from memory. Not his memory — the clock's. The master clock had been running against the altered cam for forty years, losing precisely seven minutes every season the cam engaged. The maintenance logs recorded the correction Ada's husband had applied, week after week, year after year, a husband's handwriting growing older across four decades of small honest lies compensating for one large dishonest tooth. From those corrections, Oscar could derive the true ratio. From the ratio, the tooth count.

It was, he thought, rather beautiful. They had broken the wheel, but they could not break the arithmetic.

"I'm going to need brass," he said. "A blank. And three days where nobody asks me anything."

"Three days." Juniper leaned against the doorframe. She had her mother's way of making a doorway into a fortress. "The historians finish their inventory Friday. It is Wednesday."

"Then I'll be finished Thursday, or I'll be finished forever."

"That's very dramatic. Have you considered a career in fiction?"

"Juniper," Ada called up the stairs, sweetly, "the gentlemen are asking about your cataloguing system again."

Juniper's face performed a small, exquisite grimace — the expression of a girl who would rather be fed to the millrace than explain her cross-references to Mr. Pell — and she went down to be sharp at people on all their behalves.

Oscar picked up the dead escapement, kissed two fingers, and touched them to its ruined teeth, which was a habit his master would have mocked him for and which he intended to keep until he died.

*Right,* he told the wheel. *Once more, from memory.*

* * *

Cutting an escape wheel by hand is a form of prayer conducted with files.

By noon of the first day, Oscar had sawn the rough disc from the brass plate Ada had unearthed from the observatory's storeroom — stock old enough that it might have come over on the same boat as the clock's original works, which felt less like convenience and more like forgiveness. By evening he had trued the edge, found the center within the width of a hair, and divided the circumference into forty-two equal parts using a protractor, his own figures, and a patience so total it had stopped feeling like patience and started feeling like weather.

Forty-two. The logs confirmed it. When he worked the numbers backward from forty years of Ada's husband's corrections, there was only one ratio that fit, and it fit to the third decimal place. He had put his pencil down and sat very still for a moment, because somewhere in that column of figures a dead man he'd never met had effectively handed him a tooth count across thirty years of silence, and it seemed right to acknowledge the assistance.

The file work began after dark. Each tooth of an escape wheel has two faces: one that locks, one that lets go. Get the locking faces wrong and the clock runs fast; get the letting-go wrong and it runs slow; get either wrong by enough and the whole mechanism simply gives up in a sulk. Oscar cut each face by eye and feel, checking against the template he'd drawn full-scale on Ada's good writing paper, and when the candle guttered he trimmed the wick and kept going.

Somewhere past midnight, footsteps on the stairs. He swept the wheel into a drawer on reflex, heart hammering, and was pretending to oil a completely innocent gear train when Ada appeared with a plate of cold chicken and a look that took in the brass dust on his sleeves, the shavings under the bench, the template paper he had not quite covered with his elbow.

"Mr. Pell asked me what smelled like hot metal," she said. "I told him I'd taken up soldering as a hobby."

"Did he believe you?"

"He believes what he's paid to believe. Eat the chicken, Oscar."

She set the plate down and stood a moment watching his hands — not the wheel, which was hidden, just his hands, moving over the bench with the blind confidence of long training. Something in her face loosened.

"Thomas cut his own wheels," she said. "The first year we had the clock. Before we could order replacements. He said a hand-cut tooth carries the fingerprint of whoever cut it, if you know how to look."

Oscar didn't trust his voice immediately. "Then this one will carry yours as much as mine," he managed. "You're keeping me fed."

"Half the fingerprint, then." She turned to go, then paused on the stair. "The historians leave their coats at the hotel between two and four each afternoon. Juniper says you should know that. Juniper says several things, loudly, in the archive, while pretending to reshelve."

* * *

The second day, the war moved upstairs.

It arrived in the person of Mr. Pell himself, at half past ten, wearing the expression of a man who has been assigned to be pleasant and intends to discharge the duty thoroughly. He carried a clipboard. He admired the view. He remarked that the observatory must have been magnificent in its day, and stood in the doorway of the clock room breathing the air like a man who has smelled brass filings before and knows precisely what they mean.

"Master Ishikawa. How goes the great resurrection?"

"Slowly," Oscar said, which was true, and arranged his body with artless casualness in front of the bench where the half-finished wheel lay under a rag. "Delicate work can't be hurried."

"No indeed. Though I confess our schedule does hurry us. The inventory concludes Friday, and Mr. Marsh is anxious that everything be... accounted for." Pell smiled. His eyes went past Oscar's shoulder, around the room, touching the great clock's open case, the tools laid out in their rolls, the rag on the bench with its suspicious rectangular bulge. "You've been filing."

"Deburring. The mainspring barrel edges. Tedious, necessary."

"Mm." Pell stepped closer, and for one long moment the only sound was the tick of Ada's small regulator clock on the wall, keeping honest time for nobody. Then, mercifully, from below, came Juniper's voice — pitched high, breathless, carrying the unmistakable urgency of a woman who has discovered something in the stacks.

"Mr. Pell! *Mr. Pell!* There's water damage in the 1870s boxes — actual mold — you'll want to see this before it spreads to anything *important*—"

Pell's professional instincts collided visibly with his suspicions. Mold, in an archive, was a fire alarm for men like him; his entire employment rested on records surviving long enough to be rewritten. He excused himself with a bow that managed to imply Oscar was being observed, judged, and found pending, and clattered down the stairs.

Oscar exhaled, lifted the rag, and went back to his teeth.

Through the floorboards, faintly, he heard Juniper conduct the finest performance of her career: the mold was real (she had manufactured it herself overnight from damp newspaper and spite), the affected box was real (it contained nothing but the town's 1874 dog licensing register, which she described in tones of tragic loss), and Mr. Pell spent the better part of two hours supervising the quarantining of documents that were, to the last page, about dogs.

When Ada brought up the midday bread, she was smiling in a way that suggested she had watched the whole thing and had revised her opinion of her niece upward yet again.

"She's asked him to help her move the boxes," Ada reported. "He's carrying dog registers, Oscar. The man broke our clock with his own hands and now he's hauling licensing records for a terrier named Admiral."

"Good," Oscar said, and bent back to the wheel.

* * *

The third night, Ada forged.

Oscar came downstairs at two in the morning for more lamp oil and found the archive table transformed: papers squared, blotter down, and Ada Verlow bent over a county access pass with a steel-nibbed pen, copying a clerk's hand the way he copied a clockmaker's — stroke by stroke, weight by weight, until forgery became transcription.

She didn't look up. "Before you say anything," she said, "I practiced for six weeks in the spring of 1893, after Thomas died, intending to walk into that vault myself. I lost my nerve. The passes expired. The nerve apparently did not." She finished a letter-form, blew on the ink. "Hold this lamp steadier and don't moralize at me."

"I wasn't going to moralize."

"You were going to *want* to."

"I was," Oscar admitted, and held the lamp.

Her pen moved. She had the county clerk's slanted capitals perfectly — the slight tremor on the downstrokes, the odd little loop he made on his Rs. Where had she learned it? From thirty years of reading his office's returned correspondence, she said, filed and memorized out of a widow's stubbornness that had waited two decades to become useful. The pass read: *Bearer J. Verlow, authorized access to county land records, reference grant verification, Northern Pacific survey audit.* It even had the right smell of officious boredom.

"They'll check the reference number," Oscar said.

"They will. Against a survey audit request that genuinely exists — Marsh wired it himself last month, the arrogant man practically notarized my forgery for me." She sanded the ink and sat back, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face, and she looked like a woman who had been holding her breath since 1889 and had only now noticed. "Tomorrow Juniper takes the morning coach to the county seat. Tomorrow we find out whether the truth survives being photographed."

"And meanwhile I finish a wheel."

"Meanwhile you finish a wheel." She looked at him — really looked, the way she rarely let herself, the archivist's assessing gaze dropped for once in favor of something warmer and more frightened. "Oscar. If the count is wrong—"

"It isn't wrong."

"How can you be certain?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty. "Because your husband's corrections say so. Because the brass says so — I can feel when a tooth locks clean, it's like a key finding its ward. And because—" he gestured vaguely at the ceiling, the town, the frozen river lying out there in the dark like a held promise, "—things have been wrong here for forty years. Eventually the arithmetic comes due. I'd rather it came due correct."

Ada laughed — quietly, so the hotel where the historians slept wouldn't hear — and it was the first time he had heard her laugh since the ice closed.

* * *

The last tooth was cut at dawn on the fourth day.

Oscar fitted it to the arbor with hands that shook from three days of files and candle-smoke and almost no sleep, and for a long moment he simply held the assembled escapement in his cupped palms — a thing that had not existed on Tuesday, built from memory and mathematics and a dead man's bookkeeping, weighing less than a letter.

Then he opened the great clock's case, and installed it.

The setting of an escape wheel is done by touch, in the dark of the movement, blind. His fingers found the pivots, seated the jewels, eased the train forward until the first tooth kissed the first pallet. He let the weight off gently. The wheel locked. Held. Let go.

Tick.

The sound went through him like the river through the ice — one crack, then another, then the deep groan of a thing long frozen remembering how to move. The pendulum swung, gathered itself, settled into its long grave rhythm, and the master clock of Verlow's Landing began, for the first time in forty years, to tell time without lying.

Oscar sat down on the floor, quite suddenly, and laughed at the ceiling until Ada came running up the stairs with a poker in her hand because she thought he was weeping.

He wasn't. Quite. Below, through the floorboards, the corrected clock struck eight, and from the town came the answering bells — the church, the mill, the hotel — all of them a beat behind now, all of them wrong, all of them about to be set right by any boy with a ladder and a listening ear.

"The wheel?" Ada asked.

"Running. True. Within a hair of the photographed ledger's dates." He got to his feet, legs protesting. "Where's Juniper?"

Ada's face changed, and the warmth drained out of the morning.

"The coach from the county seat was due at eleven," she said. "It's nearly noon, and it hasn't come. And Oscar — Mr. Marsh left the hotel an hour ago. On foot. Toward the bridge road."

They looked at each other. Outside the observatory window, the river lay white and silent under thinning ice, and somewhere along its frozen bank, an old man walked fast, and a girl was coming home across the millrace with the truth in her coat pocket and no idea what was waiting between her and the door.

*End of Chapter 14*
