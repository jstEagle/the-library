## 15. The County Vault

The forged pass sat in my coat pocket like a live coal.

Ada had made it in three hours flat, hunched over her desk with the good ink and the county seal she'd inherited from a notary who owed her husband a debt that outlived him both. When she finished, she held it up to the lamplight and said, "Twenty years ago I'd have called this the work of ten minutes." Then she'd looked at the pass a long moment and added, quietly, "He would have been proud of the kerning."

I did not want to be proud of the kerning. I wanted to be home, cataloging boxes, being suspicious of strangers in the ordinary way — from behind a desk with a bell to ring for assistance. Instead I was going to walk into the county vault at Millbrook Junction, nine miles east, and photograph a ledger that powerful men had spent forty years pretending did not exist.

"You don't have to," Ada had said, the night before. "We can find another way."

"There isn't another way," Oscar said, not unkindly, from the corner where he was cleaning camera plates with the concentration of a man who wished he were doing something harder. "The clock proves the timestamps were altered. It doesn't prove what the true ones were. Only the original grant ledger does that."

"And the railroad's copy is in the vault too," I said. "Side by side, if we're lucky. Forged against true, so any fool can see where the signatures diverge." I had been saying this to myself for two days, like a catechism. "Mrs. Halloway's testimony says her father signed on the fourteenth of March. The official record says the sixth. Someone moved time itself to move that land. Well. We have the clock. Now we need the page."

Oscar had looked at me then — really looked, the way he does when he forgets to be shy about it — and said, "You're braver than you let on."

"I'm not brave," I told him. "I'm spiteful. It looks similar at a distance."

It was still dark when I left. That was the design: the historians took their breakfast at the hotel at seven sharp, the elder one — Marsh — reading while he ate, the younger one — Pell — talking enough for three men. Between half past six and seven, the archive stood unwatched. Ada would keep the lamp lit in my window so it looked as though I worked late and rose late. Oscar would wind the master clock at the usual minute, because whatever else happened, the clock must be seen keeping honest time.

I took the mill road, which was longer but lonelier, and by the first gray light I could see my breath and the frozen fields and, far off, the smoke of Millbrook Junction rising thin and straight as a plumb line.

I had never been nine miles from Verlow's Landing alone. I had barely been nine feet from a cataloged box since I was twelve. My aunt thought I was fearless because I talked back; the truth was I talked back precisely because I was afraid, and noise was cheaper than courage. This morning there was no one to talk to but myself, and the road, and the coal in my pocket.

"All right," I said to the road. "Let's go commit journalism."

* * *

The county records office at Millbrook Junction was a squat brick building with a cupola that had never held a bell and a clerk named Mr. Tumulty who had held his post since roughly the invention of paper. I knew this because Ada knew this; Ada knew everything about everyone within thirty miles, filed somewhere behind her kind eyes in a system no archivist has ever bettered.

I presented the pass at the little frosted window. Mr. Tumulty read it upside down first, then turned it around, then read it again over the top of his spectacles, which were set low on his nose in the manner of a man who trusted his nose more than his eyes.

"Verlow's Landing archive," he said. "Interlibrary request."

"Certified," I said. "For the railroad's historical inventory."

That word — *railroad* — did what it always did in small towns: it made everything easier and worse at once. Mr. Tumulty stamped the pass without further ceremony and led me down a stairwell into the vault, a room of iron shelving and brass fittings, cold as a root cellar and smelling of dust, leather, and the particular melancholy of things nobody has asked for in decades.

"The grant ledgers are aisle four," he said. "You'll want the big volume, 'eighty-three through 'ninety. Sign-out sheet's on the desk up top. Don't mark anything, don't take anything, and if you're staying past noon, tell Mrs. Tumulty, because she cooks for two regardless and it offends her when food goes unwanted."

"I would never dream of offending Mrs. Tumulty," I said, and he almost smiled, and went back up his stairs, and I was alone with the ledgers.

Aisle four. Third shelf. There it was: **LAND GRANTS & CONVEYANCES, VOL. VII**, spine cracked, corners furred with damp. Beside it, slimmer, newer, bound in railway green: **VERLOW'S LANDING TITLE CONSOLIDATION — N.P. HISTORICAL SURVEY, COMPILED COPY**.

Both. Side by side. Any fool could see.

My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, so I lectured them silently — you have handled documents older than the republic, you have turned pages with tissue guards, you will not tremble now — and they obeyed, mostly. I laid the true ledger on the reading table under the vault's single bulb. I set Oscar's camera on its wooden tripod, focused by the ground glass the way he'd drilled me until I could do it blind. *F-stop wide, shutter slow, hold your breath like you mean it.* I photographed the title page. I photographed March 1884, both weeks — the sixth and the fourteenth — because Mrs. Halloway's memory lived on the fourteenth and I wanted its neighbors in frame, innocent witnesses.

And then I turned the pages properly, because I am my aunt's niece before I am anything else, and reading is the family vice.

There it was. Elias Halloway, millwright, signature looped and confident, dated the fourteenth of March, 1884 — witnessed by two names I recognized from the archive's other boxes, men whose descendants still lived within sight of the mill wheel. And across the room, in the green compilation, the same parcel, the same hand — or nearly the same hand, a hand trying to remember being another hand, the *E* drawn twice over as if the forger had hesitated mid-letter — dated the sixth.

Forty years of a town's history, decided by eight days that never happened.

I photographing-steadied myself, reloaded the plate holder with fingers gone suddenly competent — fear does that to me in reverse, oddly; terror in the moment, precision after — and worked down the ledger page by page. Verlow grant office, 1887. The water rights. The mill race easement. Every entry the railroad's version contradicted, I photographed twice.

Which is why I did not hear the stairwell door.

* * *

"Miss?"

I knew the voice before I knew the word. Marsh. The elder historian, the quiet one, the one whose courtesy fit him like an old coat fits a different man. He came down the stairs slowly, hat in hand — hat *in hand*, as if manners could be worn as armor — and stopped at the foot of the steps.

"Miss Juniper Verlow," he said. "Or ought I say, the Verlow Landing archive, interlibrary division."

"Mr. Marsh." I did not stand. Standing is surrender in a reading room; every archivist knows it. "Small world."

"Larger than some would prefer." He crossed to the table and looked down at the open ledgers — the true and the false, lying side by side like a confession. His face did nothing at all, which told me everything: a man who has already decided shows no surprise. "May I ask how long you've been here?"

"Since the office opened."

"And before that?"

"On the road since dark. It's a long walk for a girl with a pass," I said, and heard, to my horror, my own flippancy arrive exactly on schedule, because flippant is what I become when I should run.

Marsh pulled out the chair opposite mine and sat. He looked at the camera. At the plate holders, six of them, exposed. Back at me.

"In 1903," he said, conversationally, terribly, "I catalogued the Northern Pacific's land office records in Saint Paul. Eleven thousand parcels. I found seventeen discrepancies between the field grants and the consolidated titles. I wrote a memorandum. My supervisor thanked me, complimented my thoroughness, and had the memorandum placed in a folder, and the folder placed in a cabinet, and the cabinet locked, and I was promoted." He turned the green ledger toward himself, gently, as one turns the face of someone sleeping. "Do you know what I learned, Miss Verlow? That thoroughness is only ever rewarded when it reaches the correct conclusion."

"That sounds like a hard way to learn it," I said.

"It was an educational decade." He looked up. His eyes were tired and intelligent and I hated them for both. "You have photographs. You have testimony — I'm told old Mrs. Halloway has been talking into a wax horn like a fortune-teller at a fair. You have a clockmaker measuring cams. And in perhaps a week, perhaps less, the river opens, and everything you have gathered becomes either evidence or embarrassment, depending entirely upon who is holding it when the ice breaks."

"What do you want, Mr. Marsh?"

"To ask you a question I have wanted to ask anyone for twenty years." He leaned forward. "If a record is wrong, and has been wrong for a generation, and whole lives have arranged themselves around the wrongness — houses bought, marriages made, graves dug — is setting it right an act of repair? Or merely a newer damage?"

I should have given him the speech. I had one prepared; I'd been composing it all down the mill road, all fire and epigrams. But sitting across from that tired scholar in the cold of the vault, I found I didn't want it. I gave him the truth instead.

"My father died before I knew him," I said. "Everything I have of him is paper. Letters he didn't send, notes in margins, a receipt for a watch he bought the month I was born. If someone had told me those papers were 'damaged and unreliable' and burned them for my own peace, Mr. Marsh, they'd have taken the only conversations we ever had. So no. Setting a record right isn't damage. It's the opposite of burning."

The bulb hummed. Somewhere above us, Mrs. Tumulty could be heard threatening breakfast.

Marsh closed the green ledger. He stood, put on his hat, and paused at the foot of the stairs.

"The plate holders," he said, not turning around. "In your satchel. You'll want to keep them close, and dry, and away from Mr. Pell, who is twenty-six and ambitious, which is the most dangerous combination short of thirty-five and successful." A beat. "The stairwell door sticks. Lift, then push."

And he went up.

* * *

I do not remember packing the camera. I remember the vault door, and the street, and the sudden terrible arithmetic: *he followed me here. Which means he left before breakfast. Which means Pell knows where Marsh is, or doesn't, and either way, by tonight Pell will know a Verlow woman photographed the grant ledger in the county vault, and Pell wires everywhere.*

Nine miles home. Except Pell had come out on the ferry-road coach, and coaches ran both ways, and the noon coach out of Millbrook Junction would carry, if I hurried, one more passenger — and if I didn't, would carry, by evening, one small piece of news dressed as gossip.

I ran.

I ran the four blocks to the depot with the satchel banging my hip, bought my ticket with Ada's emergency money — *the emergency fund exists for emergencies, Juniper, and this qualifies* — and was on the platform, breathless, when I saw Pell crossing the street from the hotel, talking to the station agent, laughing his easy laugh.

Noon coach. Twelve minutes.

Pell finished with the agent and strolled toward the platform. Of course. Of course he'd ride back; Marsh had presumably told him nothing, but Marsh's absence had told Pell everything a man like Pell needed — *something is happening in Millbrook Junction worth leaving early for.*

There was exactly one place to be that Pell would not look: gone. I slipped off the platform's far end, cut behind the freight shed, and took the millrace path north for home. Nine miles on foot, in February, with photographic plates wrapped in my petticoats. I have never been an athletic girl. I have always been a stubborn one, which is athleticness distributed over time.

* * *

The millrace freezes early and thaws late, because the mill's sluice keeps the water moving just enough to make the ice treacherous rather than absent. In summer it's a pretty ribbon. In winter it's a scar of gray glass running along the hillside, spanned at the middle by the old footbridge, and flanked by a path that peters out at the race's edge where the bank drops six feet to the ice.

I reached the race at midafternoon, legs burning, ears dead with cold, and saw — because God or the railroad arranges these things — Pell's figure far up the coach road, walking, having missed the noon coach himself, half a mile behind and gaining.

The footbridge was ahead, a quarter mile. Too far. He'd see me cross it, plain as a fly on a sheet, and then it was a public bridge and a public road and a man with long legs and a wire key at the Junction.

The bank was here. The ice was here. Six feet down, forty feet across, and underneath the gray surface, the water moving just enough.

I have crossed that race a hundred times in summer, on the stepping stones. I had never crossed it in winter. I thought of my aunt, who had lost a husband to a river and papers to it too. I thought of Elias Halloway's signature, looped and certain, dated on a day that truly existed. I thought of Marsh's voice: *keep them close, and dry, and away from Mr. Pell.*

I sat down on the bank and slid.

The ice took my weight the way a bad argument takes scrutiny — creaking, holding, barely. I went across flat-footed, arms out, plates clutched to my chest like a child, the black water sliding beneath the gray an inch from my boot soles, and halfway over the whole surface spoke — a long groaning *crack*, sharp as a rifle report, running from bank to bank —

— and held.

I scrambled up the far bank, tore my skirt, ruined one boot beyond all decency, and stood at the top gasping like landed fish as Pell came even with me on the road above. He stopped. He looked down at me — mud to the knees, satchel gripped white-knuckled, absolutely unmistakable in my guilt — and I looked up at him, and I discovered that terror, fully spent, leaves defiance lying around like loose change.

"Good afternoon, Miss Verlow," he called down.

"Mr. Pell," I called up. "Lovely day for a walk. Mind the ice — it's thinner than the company's story."

He smiled the smile I'd seen him use on waitresses and witnesses, tipped his hat, and walked on toward the Junction — toward the telegraph office, I had no doubt, though the office closed at five and the next coach left at six and the river was already muttering in its sleep.

I picked up the satchel. I turned north, toward Verlow's Landing, toward the observatory light I could just now see pricking the dusk on its hill, toward Ada and Oscar and the great honest clock, and I began to run again — slower now, laughing now, half-mad with it.

Because under my petticoats, dry and close, lay the truth of 1884, exposed and fixed and multiply witnessed, and the man paid to bury it had a telegraph key, and I had nine miles, a broken boot, and the early thaw coming down the valley like a deadline.
