## 16. Ice Cracking

The river announced itself at four minutes past three in the morning, and Oscar Ishikawa, who had not truly slept in six days, sat up in the dark of his room above the archive and listened to it groan.

It was a sound he would never quite be able to describe to anyone afterward — not to his old master, not to a client, not even to Juniper, though she was the only one who would have understood. It was the sound of something enormous changing its mind. The ice on the Verlow had held all winter with the patience of a locked drawer, and now, in the black hours before dawn, it was unlocking. A long, grinding complaint rolled up from the water, then a crack like a rifle shot, then silence, then the groan again, deeper this time, as if the river were clearing its throat before speaking.

Oscar dressed in the cold, fingers fumbling his buttons, and went down the stairs past Juniper's door — dark, she was finally sleeping, and heaven knew she'd earned it after the millrace — and out into the blue-black morning without a coat he later realized.

The observatory hill was slick with refrozen meltwater, and he climbed it anyway, because the clock had been running for two days now and he still made the pilgrimage at every odd hour, the way a new father checks on a sleeping child. The door gave its familiar reluctant sigh. Inside, the great mechanism filled the round room with its breathing — the slow swing of the pendulum, the tick-tock heartbeat he had rebuilt with his own hands, tooth by tooth, from memory and spite and three days without proper sleep.

The new escape wheel gleamed where the sabotaged one had been pried apart. He had cut it from a blank of hardened brass over seventy-two hours that he could only remember in fragments: Ada bringing him coffee gone cold beside his elbow, Juniper inventing errands to keep the historians occupied downstairs, his own hands moving as though someone else were guiding them. It was, he thought with a craftsman's honest vanity, some of the finest work he would ever do. The escapement ticked with a clean, true bite, no stutter, no drift. He had corrected the cam — removed it entirely, actually, and locked it in the iron strongbox in Ada's office alongside the ledgers and the wax cylinders and Juniper's photographs — so the clock now kept exactly the time God and gravity intended.

He checked it against his pocket watch, which he had set by the stars two nights running. Four minutes past three. Correct to the second.

"Good lad," he murmured to the clock, which was a habit now and one he'd have been mortified to be caught at.

From the tall east window, he could see the river below, a pale ribbon in the pre-dawn gloom, shuddering. Chunks of ice the size of wagons stood up along the banks where the current shouldered them. The thaw had come absurdly early — old Mrs. Halloway said she hadn't seen the Verlow break up in February since the flood year, and nobody in town liked being reminded of the flood year. Which meant the ferry might run within days. Which meant the bridge might open within days after that.

Which meant everything they had built was about to be tested against everything the railroad had sent to bury it.

* * *

By seven, the kitchen above the archive smelled of burnt porridge and juniper berries, because Juniper maintained — against all botanical evidence, Oscar felt — that the berry and her own name implied a kinship she ought to honor in cooking.

"It tastes like a tree fell in it," Oscar said.

"That's the medicinal quality," Juniper said, pushing the bowl toward him with the air of a woman administering cod liver oil. "Eat it. You've been living on coffee and solder fumes for a week. You look like something the tide brought in."

"I look like a man who cut an escape wheel in three days."

"You look like a man who's about to fall face-first into his own breakfast." But there was no real edge in it. She was studying him the way she studied documents, he realized — cataloging, checking for damage. Since the night at the millrace she had stopped treating him entirely like an outsider; the suspicion hadn't vanished, merely been redirected outward, at the enemy, where it belonged.

Ada came in from the office with the morning paper and an expression that made both of them sit straighter. She was a woman whose composure Oscar had come to think of as structural, like the clock's frame — you didn't notice it until it cracked. There was a hairline crack in it now.

"The telegraph office opened early," she said. "Young Mr. Pell couldn't help mentioning it when I collected the wire. He seemed to think I'd want to know."

"Wired ahead?" Juniper set down her spoon. "Who?"

"Our historians." Ada laid the telegram on the table, and Oscar read it upside down, which was how he'd learned to read most things in that house.

MARSH REQUESTING ARRANGEMENTS FOR MR. GRIEVE OF COUNSEL. ARRIVES FIRST FERRY. PLEASE EXTEND COURTESIES AS TO RETAINED EXPERTS STOP ALL INQUIRIES SUSPENDED PENDING ARRIVAL STOP

"Suspended pending arrival," Juniper repeated, in a flat voice that Oscar had learned meant fury under excellent control. "They suspended our own town records from us."

"They can't do that," Oscar said. "Can they?"

"They can try," Ada said. "And a company lawyer arriving on the first ferry means they intend to try before the ice is fully out. Mr. Grieve will want everything inventoried, boxed, crated, and shipped downriver on the same boat that brings him. That's how it's done. You don't steal a record in daylight — you preserve it. You sequester it. By the time anyone asks where it went, it's been sitting in a company warehouse in Tacoma for two years, misfiled beyond recovery."

The kitchen was quiet except for the archive clock in the hall, which Juniper wound herself and kept to the second because she said somebody in this family had to be reliable besides the big one on the hill.

"So we finish first," Oscar said.

Ada looked at him. "Finish what, precisely?"

"The deposition." He counted it off on his fingers, workmanlike, because listing things calmed him the way balancing a train of gears calmed other men. "Mrs. Halloway's testimony — done, sealed, witnessed. The maintenance logs showing the cam installed in '84 by persons unknown — copied, cross-referenced. The grant ledger photographs from the county vault — developed Tuesday, they're clear enough to read signatures across a room. The cam itself, with the wear patterns that prove seasonal use. All we lack is—" he paused— "well. Everything formal. Signatures. A notary. Witnesses willing to stand in the observatory and swear to what they know."

"And half this town," Juniper said slowly, "has spent forty years pretending it doesn't know anything."

"Half this town has spent forty years waiting for someone else to go first," Ada said. She rose and began, with sudden purpose, collecting cups. "My husband used to say fear is a debt — everyone in town owes a piece of it, and nobody wants to be the one holding it when payment comes due. Well." She set the last cup down harder than strictly necessary. "Payment is coming due on the first ferry. Let them see who's holding it."

* * *

The town, it turned out, had been waiting longer than any of them guessed.

Oscar spent the morning delivering notices — Juniper had written twelve of them overnight in her small, ferocious hand, and Ada had signed each one — inviting the property holders of Verlow's Landing to attend the sealing of a sworn deposition at the observatory, Saturday next, at noon precisely, time verified by the master clock.

He expected doors left shut in his face. He was, after all, still the outsider, the boy who'd come to fix the clock and stayed past all propriety. What he got instead was something stranger.

At the Halloway house, Mrs. Halloway pressed his hand in both of hers and said, "Tell Miss Verlow my father's name will be on it or I'll haunt the whole riverbank." At the feed store, Mr. Ostrander read the notice twice, looked over Oscar's shoulder at the empty street, and said quietly, "My grandfather bought this lot twice. Paid for it twice. I'll be there." At the shuttered mill — and Oscar had wondered all winter why nobody ever bought the mill — the widow Kessler met him at the gate and said only, "Noon Saturday. I've waited thirty years for a noon worth attending."

By the fourth delivery, Oscar understood what Ada's husband had known and the railroad's historians never would: the silence of Verlow's Landing had never been consent. It had been an audience, holding its breath for decades, waiting for the play to resume.

Walking back along the levee, he heard the river again — louder now, insistent. Great pans of ice rode the current past the pilings of the iron bridge, grinding, colliding, spinning slow circles like dancers deciding partners. The bridge itself stood black and patient above the wreckage, and Oscar found he could look at it without the old tightness in his chest. Two months ago it had been his only road home. Now it was just a bridge.

He stopped at the telegraph office on impulse, to send his master a progress report he owed three days late. Young Pell was alone behind the counter, and the moment Oscar mentioned the incoming wires, the boy went pink to his ears.

"Mr. Marsh has been sending a deal of traffic," Pell admitted, in the conspiratorial whisper of a small-town telegraph operator, which is to say, a man professionally incapable of keeping secrets and tormented by it. "Mostly to the company. But there was one yesterday I had to deliver personal, and Mr. Grieve's reply came back this morning, and—" he glanced at the door— "I probably shouldn't say."

"No," Oscar agreed. "You shouldn't."

Pell leaned in anyway. "There's a clause in it about *witnesses.* Something about *discredit as necessary.* And something about the ferry schedule — Mr. Grieve's asked if she could run early. If the channel clears by Thursday, he wants to come Thursday. Not Saturday."

Thursday. Two days.

Oscar thanked him, walked out into the bright, treacherous sunshine of the false spring, and stood a moment listening to the ice go out of the river with a sound like the world's largest lock turning.

Then he ran for the hill.

* * *

They gathered in the observatory's round room that afternoon — the three of them and the clock, which ticked through the whole council like an impartial chairman.

"Thursday," Ada said. She had gone very still, the way she did when grief and arithmetic arrived together. "Then the deposition must be sworn Friday. Before he arrives. Once it's sealed and copies exist outside this town — at the *Clarion* office in the capital, in the county clerk's hands, in three separate mailbags on three separate coaches — then Mr. Grieve can discredit us until doomsday and the record still stands."

"Notaries," Juniper said. She had a list; of course she had a list; Oscar privately believed Juniper had been born holding a list. "Justice of the peace Dunmore owes Ada a favor from the school board years. He'll swear it. Witnesses — we have eleven confirmed, maybe fourteen. Testimony cylinders — cataloged and sealed. Photographs — printed in duplicate. The maintenance logs—" she looked at Oscar.

"Copied in full, my hand certified by your aunt. The original cam stays in the strongbox until the deposition, then it goes on the record as Exhibit A." He hesitated. "There's one thing. The clock itself. Grieve's people broke it once to destroy the alteration evidence. If they suspect it runs true now — and Marsh has watched me regulate it through that window every morning this week—"

"They'll break it again," Ada finished quietly.

"Or take it. A master clock is company property if the observatory charter says so, and I'd wager Marsh has read the charter closer than we have." He looked around the round room, at the pendulum swinging its patient arc, at the brass he had bled three days of his life into. "Everything timestamps back to this clock. Every cylinder, every photograph notation, every signature. If it stops Friday at noon — or worse, if it gets *adjusted* — Grieve can argue the whole timeline is unreliable. It's the spine of the deposition."

Silence, except the tick.

"Then we guard it," Juniper said.

"With what? There are three of us and one of the clock, and Saturday—" Oscar caught himself. "Friday. From tonight onward, someone sits with it. In shifts. Until the record is sealed."

"I'll take nights," Juniper said immediately. "I sleep like a cat anyway, and I'd enjoy having legal grounds to be unpleasant to anyone who climbs that hill after dark."

"I'll take days," Ada said. "The archive opens at nine regardless. Let the historians come and watch me knit beneath their betters' clock. They find knitting disarming. It's the single useful thing I ever learned from my mother-in-law."

"And I'll take the gaps," Oscar said, "and every hour in between, because I'm not sleeping properly anymore anyway, and if that machine stops, I want it to be because something failed in it, not because something failed us."

Ada looked at him for a long moment — the appraising look he'd learned meant she was seeing not the apprentice but something else, something he couldn't name yet — and then she simply nodded, and it settled on him like a weight and a warmth at once.

"One more thing," she said. "Marsh."

"The elder one," Juniper said. "The one who signs his inventory sheets like a man signing a confession."

"He was a scholar once. A good one. I've read his monograph on the territorial surveys — rigorous, honest footnotes, the works. Somewhere along the way the railroad bought that honesty and put it to work." Ada's gaze drifted to the window, where the afternoon light lay thick and golden on the breaking river. "If there's anything left in him worth buying back, Friday at noon is when it goes to auction. I intend to make sure he attends."

* * *

That evening, Oscar climbed the hill for the start of the first watch and found the river had shed its winter voice entirely. Where it had groaned and muttered all week, it now ran loud and triumphant, ice riding the current in broken flotillas, water showing black and alive between the pans. The ferry landing lanterns were lit — the ferryman testing his chains, getting ready.

Two days. Maybe less.

Inside, he hung his coat, checked the clock against his watch — correct to the second, always correct now — and settled into Ada's chair by the mechanism with the maintenance logs across his knees for the hundredth reading. The lamp threw his shadow huge against the curved wall, and the pendulum swung through it, back and forth, cutting the light into equal portions like a woman dividing bread.

He thought about the letter that had started all of it, eight weeks and one lifetime ago. *Small work,* his master had called it. *A regulator repair, nothing more. Go, be back inside the month.*

He had wanted, on the coach, for things to happen to him. He understood now, with the humility of the thoroughly instructed, that he had gotten precisely what he wished for, and that things happening to a person looked very little like adventure while they were happening. They looked like sabotage and forged passes and a girl nearly drowning in a millrace and an old woman's voice cracking on a wax cylinder as she described her father's hands shaking over a paper he was told to sign.

But the clock ran true. That was the thing he returned to, in the small hours of the watch, the way a sailor returns to the pole star. Whatever happened Thursday or Friday or when the lawyer came up the hill with his courteous teeth — the clock ran true, and he had made it run true, and no wire sent ahead of a ferry could unmake that.

Near midnight, movement below the window: a figure crossing the observatory yard in the moonlight. Oscar was on his feet before his mind caught up, heart hammering — but it was only Juniper, climbing the hill with a covered basket and her chin set for battle, an hour early for her shift because, she informed him through the door, cats also distrust other people's watching.

She unpacked the basket on the instrument table. Bread, cheese, a jar of the berry concoction he recognized with dread.

"If you're going to keep vigil," she said, "you'll do it fed. This isn't negotiable. I've decided we're a household, and households have rules, and rule one is nobody faces a lawyer on an empty stomach."

"A household," Oscar repeated carefully.

Juniper poured the dubious tea with great dignity and did not look at him. "Don't make it strange, Ishikawa. Eat your supper."

Outside, the river ran loud under the moon, carrying the winter away piece by piece, and the master clock ticked on above it — true, and staying true — while somewhere downriver, past all that breaking ice, a ferry chained at its landing strained against the current like something impatient to begin.
