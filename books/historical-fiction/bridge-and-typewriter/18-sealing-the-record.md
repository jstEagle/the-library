## 18. Sealing the Record

By seven in the morning the observatory smelled of lamp oil, beeswax, and every person in Verlow's Landing who had ever been told to stay out of other people's business.

Oscar had counted forty-one of them by the time Ada unlocked the doors — mill hands and shopkeepers, two ferry men with their collars up, the reverend, Mrs. Halloway wrapped in a coat that had outlived three wars and possibly its original owner, and a scattering of children who had been brought along the way one brings children to something they will be asked about later for the rest of their lives.

"Forty-one," Juniper said, appearing at his elbow with a tray of coffee cups she had clearly commandeered from the archive's reading room and would deny it under oath. "Forty-two counting the Hendersons' dog, who I am not counting."

"The dog stays by the stove," Oscar said. "The dog has better sense than any of us."

She almost smiled. In the week since the county vault — since she had come over the millrace with ice in her hair and a photograph pressed inside her glove like a coal still burning — she had almost smiled four times, and Oscar had kept count the way he kept count of everything, because keeping count was what he was for.

The master clock stood behind them all, running.

He had wound it himself at dawn, checked the beat with his ear against the case, watched the new escape wheel take its teeth through the pallets the way a pianist takes a scale — evenly, without hurry, without lie. Three days of filing and hardening and one broken drill bit he did not intend to think about again, and now it kept true time. He had set it against his pocket watch, and Ada's marine chronometer, and finally against noon itself, when the sun came down the observatory's meridian slit and laid a hair-thin line across the floor like the river deciding where it stood.

Every timestamp from this day forward would be honest. That was the whole argument, really. Everything else was paper.

* * *

They had arranged the room as a courtroom without meaning to — Ada's table at the front, the deposition pages squared upon it, and behind the table the great clock face, its gilt numerals catching the morning light through the frost-furred windows. Perhaps it was the architecture of the place. Perhaps it was simply that people arrange themselves toward whatever they have decided is true.

Ada opened proceedings because nobody had appointed her to anything and therefore nobody could object. She had put on her good dress, the gray one, and Edmund's watch chain, and when she rose, the room went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

"You know why you're here," she said. "Some of you knew my husband. Some of you knew your grandfathers, which amounts to the same grief. For thirty years this town has been told that what happened in the eighteen-eighties is a matter for professionals — that the record burned, that the flood took the rest, and that the railroad's version is the same thing as the truth because the railroad can afford better ink." She laid her hand flat on the papers. "Yesterday a lawyer offered me money for my silence. I want that stated plainly too, so no one leaves here thinking there wasn't a price, or that we were above considering it."

A rustle went through the room. Mrs. Halloway said, loudly, "How much?" and her granddaughter shushed her, and Ada said, "Enough," and Mrs. Halloway nodded as though that confirmed everything she'd suspected about lawyers since 1874.

"We are going to swear a deposition," Ada went on. "Witnessed by everyone in this room, timed by that clock" — she pointed, and forty-odd heads turned to the great face, and Oscar felt absurdly as though he were being introduced — "and copied three ways before the bridge opens. What the clock says happened, happened. What the ledgers say was signed, was forged. And none of it is mine, or Juniper's, or Mr. Ishikawa's. It belongs to this town. We are only the ones holding it while you decide to take it back."

Then she sat down, and the swearing began.

* * *

It took most of the morning, and it was stranger and warmer than anything Oscar had imagined when he'd first come up this hill with a toolkit and an idea of clocks as machines that only told time.

Mrs. Halloway went first. Juniper played the wax cylinder back on the phonograph, and the old woman listened to her own recorded voice — thin, crackling, eighty years downstream from the millwright's daughter she had been — describe watching her father practice his signature until his hand ached, because the land office man had told him it "hadn't come out right" twice and the third copy would be the one filed. The room heard it. Then Mrs. Halloway raised her right hand, swore before God and the reverend and the dog, and signed beneath it in a hand that shook but did not hesitate.

"That's Papa's name being stolen twice," she said, sitting down. "Once then, once now if we let them. I won't be party to the second theft."

The ferry men testified to what the river had actually done in '89 — where the flood crested, which buildings drowned, and how the grant office records had gone into the water *already boxed for shipping*, which was a detail Oscar had not known and which made several people in the room go very still.

"Boxed for shipping," Ada repeated for the record. "By whom?"

"Men with a railroad wagon," said the elder ferryman, "and a bill of lading that never arrived anywhere."

The photographs came next — Juniper's negatives from the county vault, printed overnight in the archive's darkroom and still smelling faintly of fixer. She held each one up and read the relevant lines aloud, her voice flat and precise, doing the cataloging she did best. Oscar noticed she had dressed carefully today, collar buttoned to the top, hair pinned with military severity, and that she still kept one hand in her pocket closed around something. He guessed it was the glove. He didn't ask. Some talismans you don't examine.

And then it was his turn.

He hadn't prepared anything to say. He had meant simply to present the cam — the worn brass heart of the whole affair, the little altered gear that had made the master clock run fast by exactly four minutes each autumn and spring, season after season, year after year, so that every document stamped at the observatory during those windows bore a time that had never existed. But standing before all of them, with the cam in his palm and the clock ticking honestly at his back, he found he wanted to explain it the way his master had taught him to explain mechanisms: gently, so anyone could hold it in their mind.

"A clock doesn't decide what time it is," he said. "It only reports. Someone taught this one to report wrongly — added a cam here, cut these teeth deeper, so that for certain weeks each year it ran ahead. Anyone who asked why the town clock disagreed was told the observatory instrument was the accurate one. It was — except when accuracy was inconvenient." He turned the cam so the light caught the wear. "I've measured it. Four minutes, eleven seconds, plus drift. Over twenty-six seasons of use. That isn't wear. Wear wanders. This is arithmetic."

Someone near the back — Oscar thought it was the blacksmith — said, "Four minutes. You can prove four minutes?"

"I can prove four minutes eleven seconds," Oscar said, "and I can show you the file marks." And he could; he'd photographed them through the loupe, magnified until the tool strokes stood up like plowed fields, and Juniper passed the prints hand to hand down the rows while the room bent over them and murmured.

It was, he realized somewhere in the middle of it, the first time in his life he had spoken to more than five people at once, and the strange part was that it didn't frighten him. A workshop was a workshop. People were mechanisms too, mostly — gears of grievance and hope, escapements of fear and courage, all of it wanting only to be regulated to some honest beat. He had spent his whole apprenticeship learning to listen for the tick that was wrong. The whole town had been ticking wrong for thirty years. He was, it turned out, exactly the sort of person things like this happened to. It had just taken him until now to stand still long enough for it.

* * *

Noon came while the reverend read the deposition draft aloud for corrections, and the sun's line crossed the floor precisely as the clock struck twelve, and half the room looked from the meridian line to the clock face and back with the expression of people watching a trick and forgetting it isn't one.

"There," said Mrs. Halloway, pointing at the light. "That's the honest one. My father used to set the mill by that window."

"It sets itself," said Oscar. "That's rather the point."

Signing took another hour. They had planned for three copies; Ada, in the end, ordered five, because the reverend wanted one for the church safe, and the schoolteacher appeared at the door mid-afternoon demanding to know why history was happening without her class, and Juniper surrendered a page to her with visible pain. Forty-two signatures, counting the dog's paw print, which Juniper inked herself and defended to the room: "For completeness. Animals are witnesses to everything; they're simply bad at depositions."

The last signature was Marsh's.

The elder historian had not been invited. He had come anyway, alone, without the younger one — Prewitt was at the telegraph office, someone reported, wiring furiously — and he had stood at the back by the door for the entire proceeding with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at. When the pen came around to the witnesses, he stepped forward before Ada could decide whether to stop him.

"I'd like to sign," he said. "As a witness. Not for the company."

The room held its breath.

"I spent twenty years believing the record was lost," Marsh said, and his voice was quiet and terribly tired. "It's easier to work when you believe that. Then a boy takes a photograph through a vault gate that I walked past a hundred times, and I have to decide what kind of historian I am." He looked at Ada. "Your husband wrote to me once. 1893. I never answered. I'd like to answer now, if the offer stands."

Ada studied him for a long moment. Then she turned the deposition toward him and set the pen down in front of him, and said, "Edmund believed in second drafts."

Marsh signed. His hand did not shake, but afterward he stood there a moment as if something had come off him — a coat, a weight, twenty years — and Oscar, watching, understood that they had just witnessed a man repair himself, and that it was not so different from repairing a clock. You didn't make him new. You found the worn tooth, and you cut past it, and you let the mechanism run true.

* * *

They finished as the afternoon light went gold and low. Juniper sealed the copies in oilcloth; Ada locked two in the archive safe and gave the third to the reverend with instructions Oscar didn't hear, but which involved the words "millrace," "waterproof," and "if it comes to it, swim." The cam itself went into a small labeled box — *Exhibit A, altered escapement cam, removed and measured this date* — and Ada held it a moment before handing it to the safe.

"Edmund looked for this," she said softly. "Twenty years, he looked for the proof, and it was ticking over his head the whole time." She laughed, wetly. "He'd have found it funny. He found most things funny that I found unbearable."

"He sounds," Oscar said, "like someone I'd have liked to apprentice under."

"He'd have liked you. You both talk to machines as if they're listening." She closed the safe. "They are, you know. That's the secret nobody tells apprentices."

Below the hill, the river was giving voice — long groans and cracks rolling up through the frozen ground like furniture being moved in an enormous upstairs room. The thaw had come early and impatient. By tomorrow, maybe the day after, the first ferry would cross, and on it a lawyer with empty hands and a full briefcase, and after that nothing here would be quiet for a while.

But tonight the record was sworn, witnessed, multiplied, and honest to the minute.

Juniper appeared beside Oscar at the clock case, where he was checking the beat one last time out of pure happiness. Below them the townspeople were filing out into the blue evening, talking all at once, laughing, arguing about 1887 with the freedom of people who had just discovered the subject was no longer owned by somebody else.

"Well," Juniper said. "You fixed the clock."

"You photographed the ledger. Ada forged the passes. Mrs. Halloway swore at a dog. It was a group effort."

"Mm." She looked at the great pendulum swinging its slow silver arc, and then at him, and for the fifth time — he was keeping count — she almost smiled, and this time it got further than usual. "There's a coach due at the landing the morning after the ferry runs. First one across the bridge. If a person were leaving, that would be the one to catch."

"Yes," Oscar said. "If a person were leaving."

They stood a moment in the ticking.

Then the door banged open below, and the telegraph boy took the stairs two at a time, red-faced and shouting before he even reached them: "Ferry's moving up! Ice broke at Hartwell too — lawyer's booked the *first* crossing, he'll be up the hill by noon tomorrow!" He gasped for breath. "And ma'am — the younger one, Prewitt — he wired ahead to the state capital too. Word for word, I copied it." He handed Ada the flimsy, and she read it, and read it again, and her face did something complicated.

"What is it?" Juniper asked. "Bad?"

"No," Ada said slowly, handing it over. "It's addressed to the *governor's* office. And Prewitt has requested protection as a witness."
