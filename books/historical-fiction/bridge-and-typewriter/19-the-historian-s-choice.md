## 19. The Historian's Choice

Elias Marsh had signed his name to many documents in forty years of scholarship, and he had come, over time, to think of each signature as a small death. The first had been the worst — a monograph on Northern Pacific land surveys, 1889 edition, from which he had been instructed to remove three paragraphs concerning irregular grant filings in the Yellowstone division. He remembered standing at his desk with the proofs before him, striking the paragraphs one sentence at a time, and feeling something in his chest go quiet and never quite start again. After that it was easier. It is always easier after the first time. A man learns that he can survive the amputation of his own opinion, and having learned it, survives a great many further operations, until what remains of him can be folded into an envelope and mailed anywhere.

He sat now in the archive's back room at Verlow's Landing, at a table borrowed from the kitchen, with a lamp turned up too bright because Mrs. Verlow evidently believed that men with bad consciences preferred dim light and she meant to deny them the comfort. She was right. He wished fervently for dimness.

Across the table lay everything.

The altered cam from the observatory clock, wrapped in a linen cloth like a relic. Photographs of a county ledger, still curling from their development, showing signatures in a millwright's careful hand dated two years before the man could write. Wax cylinders in wooden cases, labeled in a young woman's slanting script: *Mrs. Halloway — testimony — second sitting — she wept once but continued.* Maintenance logs from the master clock, forty years of them, in which the seasonal regulation entries stopped abruptly in 1891 and resumed with a different hand and a different story.

And beneath it all, the deposition itself, drafted in the apprentice's neat engineer's lettering, every timestamp cross-referenced, every claim footnoted with a humility that shamed him. The boy wrote like someone who believed that if you were only precise enough, the truth would hold its own weight. Marsh had believed that once. He could not remember where he had put the belief down.

"You've read it," Ada Verlow said. It was not a question. She stood by the window with her arms folded, and behind her the curtains were drawn against a night that sounded, faintly even indoors, like the river clearing its throat.

"I have read it," Marsh said.

"Then you know it's accurate."

"It is more than accurate." He heard his own voice do the thing his voice did in lectures — the settling, the authority. He hated it. "The horological evidence alone is remarkable. Whoever altered that escapement understood the mechanism intimately. The cam engages only during the filing seasons — April through June, and again in October. Precisely when grant documents would bear observatory timestamps. It would shift the recorded time forward eleven minutes, which is enough, under the statute as it stood, to contest any filing made after four o'clock." He stopped himself. He was doing scholarship. It was like reaching for a limb that was no longer there. "Forgive me. You didn't ask for a lecture."

"No," said Juniper, from the doorway, "but it's nice to hear you say it out loud anyway. That it's real. That it holds."

* * *

He had been twenty-six years old when he joined the Historical Division, straight out of a doctorate nobody had wanted to read, and he had believed then that the railroad's historians were a kind of priesthood — keepers of records, preservers of the great machine's memory. His father had been a section foreman. He had grown up with creosote in his nostrils and the conviction that the road was civilization itself laid down rail by rail, and that to serve its history was to serve history entire. Nobody had told him, or perhaps somebody had told him and he had declined to hear it, that a company keeps historians the way a man keeps mirrors: angled to flatter.

For thirty years he had angled the mirror.

There were specific moments he had trained himself not to revisit, the way one trains oneself not to probe a broken tooth. The Verlow file. He had read the Verlow file in 1894, the whole of it, before it was culled — a grant office drowned in the flood of '89 conveniently destroying the originals, duplicate filings appearing with dates that marched in perfect, impossible order, and one archivist, a quiet man named Verlow, writing letters of objection that grew shorter and more desperate until they stopped. Marsh had recommended, in a memo he could still recite, that the objections be "handled administratively." He had not asked what handling meant. He had been promoted the following spring.

Now the quiet man's widow was pouring him tea he did not want, and her niece was watching him the way one watches weather, and a foreign apprentice with ink-stained fingers was somewhere upstairs keeping a clock running honestly, and Marsh understood that the mirror was about to be turned around whether he consented or not.

"The lawyer comes tomorrow," Ada said. "Mr. Pell. He'll make an offer. He'll offer me money and my husband's name back, and he'll use words like *damaged* and *unreliable*, and he'll be very reasonable, and when I refuse — and I will refuse — he will proceed to other measures. And the bridge opens within days. Whatever isn't sealed and witnessed and gone out on the morning coach will simply... cease. He has done it before. Your division has done it before." Her voice did not shake. That was somehow worse. "I'm not asking you to testify against your employer, Mr. Marsh. I'm asking you to witness. There's a difference. A witness doesn't have to believe. A witness only has to look."

"That's precisely the difficulty, madam," Marsh said. "I have spent thirty years not looking."

Juniper laughed — a short, sharp sound with no cruelty in it, which was the worst kind. "Well," she said. "What a convenient habit. Time to break it."

* * *

They gave him a room for the night — the good room above the archive, with a quilt that smelled of cedar and a window that rattled in the wind off the water. He did not sleep. He sat in the chair by the window with the deposition and read it a third time, and then a fourth, this time as a scholar rather than a man, which was the only mercy available to him: work.

It held. God help him, it held. Every claim chained to its source; the cam's geometry measured twice by the boy and photographed; the clock logs reconciled against the ledger photographs so that the timestamps matched to the minute across thirty years of filings. Somewhere below him the master clock ticked through the floorboards, steady and unbothered, keeping honest time for the first honest hour of its long corrupted life, and Marsh found that its rhythm had gotten into him, that he was breathing to it, that his heart — which had been quiet since 1889 — was keeping time with a machine repaired by a stranger's apprentice.

Near dawn he took out his pocket notebook, the one he had carried since his first year with the Division, and turned past thirty years of memoranda to a blank page, and wrote:

*A scholar who knows a thing and declines to record it has not merely failed. He has falsified by omission, which is the only forgery that leaves no ink.*

He looked at it a long while. Then he went downstairs.

* * *

Ada Verlow was already awake — he suspected she had never been asleep — and found him in the archive's reading room with the deposition squared on the table and his fountain pen beside it and his hands folded like a man at a funeral, which in a sense he supposed it was.

The last page had a line for witnesses. Half the town had signed it the previous evening, in the observatory, under the running clock: the blacksmith, the ferryman's widow, old Mrs. Halloway with her cane and her unanswerable face, the druggist, two of the Halloway sons, the schoolteacher. Their names marched up the margin in a dozen different hands, some confident, some cramped, one clearly written by a child guided by an adult — the schoolteacher's boy, perhaps. Ordinary names. Names that would appear tomorrow in no newspaper the railroad owned.

Marsh uncapped the pen.

His hand, he noted with the detached interest of a lifelong observer of documents, was perfectly steady. Forty years of signing things he did not mean had apparently trained the instrument past the interference of the operator. He wrote *Elias T. Marsh, Historian, Northern Pacific Railway, Historical Division (retired as of this signature)* — the parenthesis was Juniper's suggestion, delivered deadpan from the doorway, and it was the first joke anyone had let him in on in years — and beneath it the date and the place, and the observatory time taken by the boy Oscar directly from the master clock, entered to the second.

The nib whispered. The clock, floors away, kept its count. When it was done he set the pen down and found that his eyes were wet, which embarrassed him, and that Ada Verlow had put her hand briefly on his shoulder, which undid him entirely.

"My memo of 1894," he said. "It's referenced in the Division files. It will confirm the suppression came from the top — Halloran himself. Without my signature, they'll call this local grievance and settle it in a claims court for forty dollars and a public apology drafted by lawyers. With it—" 

"With it," Ada said quietly, "it becomes what it always was. History."

* * *

He heard the coach before he saw it — wheels on frozen mud, and the particular brisk jingle of a man in a hurry arriving in a town that had decided not to be hurried ever again. Through the archive window he watched Mr. Pell descend in his good coat, brush snow from his shoulders with two economical gestures, and look up at the observatory hill with the expression of a man pricing a building.

Marsh gathered his own coat. There was one more document to sign, he thought, though this one would never bear his name. Thirty years ago a young scholar had struck three paragraphs from a proof and felt something die quietly in his chest. This morning, walking out into the cold with the deposition sealed in oilcloth under his arm, bound for no office of his own but for the state press on the first coach across the bridge, Elias Marsh felt the thing in his chest begin — slowly, painfully, like a clock long stopped being wound — to tick.

Behind him, up on the hill, the ice on the river boomed once, loud as cannon, and did not break.

Not yet. But soon, and everyone in the town heard it, and no one but the lawyer coming up the street was surprised.
