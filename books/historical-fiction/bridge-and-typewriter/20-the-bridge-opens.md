## 20. The Bridge Opens

The sound woke me before dawn — a long, low groan from the river, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. I had been sleeping badly for a week, my body still keeping the hours of the three-day vigil at the lathe, so I was out of bed and at the window before I fully understood what I was hearing. The dark outside was not quite dark anymore; it had that gray, held-breath quality that comes just before first light.

Then the ice cracked. Not the small, treacherous sounds it had been making all winter, the little ticks and sighs of expanding frost, but a real crack, deep and splitting, followed by a rumble that traveled up through the foundations of the archive and into the floorboards beneath my feet.

I dressed in the dark, fumbled twice with my collar buttons, and went downstairs.

Ada was already in the kitchen, wrapped in her husband's old coat, standing at the open back door with her hands folded in front of her like a woman at prayer. Juniper sat on the table itself, which she knew Ada disapproved of, boots swinging, fully dressed as though she had never gone to bed at all.

"It's early," Ada said, without turning around. "The thaw. Marsh said the middle of March, and it's barely come."

"Maybe the river doesn't read the almanac," Juniper said.

We stood together in the doorway and watched the ice go. It did not melt so much as surrender — great plates of it heaving up along the bend, tilting, sliding under one another with a grinding protest, and then the whole gray sheet breaking into pieces that turned slowly in the black water and were carried away toward the falls. The river underneath ran high and brown and impossibly alive. Somewhere upstream a chunk the size of a hay wagon struck the bridge pilings and shattered with a report like a cannon.

None of us said anything for a long while. There didn't seem to be anything to say that the river wasn't already saying better.

It was Juniper who finally spoke, and she said it quietly, which for Juniper is roughly equivalent to another person weeping. "It's over, isn't it? The waiting part."

"The waiting part is over," Ada said. "Now comes everything else."

* * *

Everything else arrived on the nine o'clock ferry, which made its first crossing of the season through water still thick with drifting ice, and it arrived wearing a gray suit and carrying a leather case.

Mr. Calloway — the railroad's lawyer — came up from the landing on foot because the hack driver, a broad man named Ostrow who had lived in Verlow's Landing his whole life, had looked at him, looked at the fare in his hand, and driven off without him. By the time Calloway reached the observatory steps his shoes were muddy to the ankle and his composure had suffered accordingly.

I met him at the door. This was arranged, more or less; we had all understood, without anyone saying so aloud, that the lawyer would come on the first boat, and that someone should be waiting.

"Mr. Ishikawa," he said. He remembered my name. I gave him credit for that. "I'd like a word with Mrs. Verlow."

"She's expecting you," I said, which was true, and stepped aside.

He declined coffee. He declined a chair. He stood in the middle of Ada's front room with his hat in one hand and his case in the other and delivered, in the tone of a man offering a gift rather than a transaction, his offer: five hundred dollars. Restitution of her husband's name in the railroad's own published history — a full paragraph, perhaps two, describing Mr. Elias Verlow as a conscientious public servant whose records were tragically lost. All she need do was sign an affidavit attesting that the surviving ledgers in her keeping were water-damaged, unreliable, and of no evidentiary value, and surrender them for "preservation."

I will say this for Ada: she let him finish. Every word. She sat with her hands flat on the table and listened the way she listened to a clock that had come in for repair, letting the whole mechanism run out before she touched it.

"Mr. Calloway," she said, "do you know what my husband was doing the night he drowned?"

"I — understand there was an accident on the river, ma'am."

"He was rowing across with a satchel full of documents. Copies of the original grant filings. Signatures. Dates. He'd spent two years collecting them." She rose, went to the locked drawer — unlocked now, these many weeks — and took out a ledger, laying it on the table between them with both hands, gently, the way one handles something that has been broken before. "He never got them across. The river took him and took the satchel and for twenty-two years this town pretended it had taken nothing at all. And now you come here and offer me money to call what's left of his work *damaged.*"

"Mrs. Verlow—"

Elias's own words were written on the flyleaf, in ink gone brown. I had seen them; I had copied them myself for the deposition. *What is written true stays true no matter who burns the page.* Ada did not raise her voice. She simply opened the cover and read the line aloud, and then closed the book.

"You may tell your employers," she said, "that they can have the ledgers when the river gives back my husband. Not before."

Calloway stood very still for a moment. I watched him do the arithmetic lawyers do — the number of witnesses, the number of signatures already gathered, the presence in the corner of one E. A. Marsh, formerly of the Northern Pacific's historical bureau, who had signed the deposition the evening before with a hand that did not shake at all. Whatever sum Calloway had been authorized to spend, it was suddenly, visibly insufficient.

He put on his hat. "You'll regret this," he said, but there was no conviction in it. It was a line from an old script, and everyone in the room knew the play had closed.

"No," Ada said. "I regretted waiting. This is the other thing."

* * *

The deposition went out on the morning coach.

I want to record, because no one else thought it important enough to write down, the sheer ordinariness of the moment. After weeks of wax cylinders and forged passes and sleepless nights with a graver in my hand, the truth about Verlow's Landing left town the way everything leaves a river town: tied up in string, addressed in pencil, and flung onto the top of a mail sack by a coachman who complained about the weight.

Juniper rode with it as far as the county seat. She had argued the point the night before — argued it hard, with citations, which is Juniper's way of arguing — on the grounds that documents entrusted to the mails had a way of arriving damp or not at all when certain corporations took an interest. In the end it was agreed that she would carry the duplicate copy herself, sit beside it the whole way, and see it into the hands of the state press editor personally, along with sworn copies of Mrs. Halloway's testimony and the photographs from the county vault.

She hugged Ada at the foot of the observatory steps. She shook Marsh's hand, which a month earlier would have been unthinkable, and told him, "If you ever write anything honest again, I'll read it," which from Juniper is practically a love letter. Then she turned to me, and for a moment neither of us seemed to know what the protocol was.

In the end she settled it by reaching up and straightening my collar, which did not need straightening.

"Don't let the clock run slow while I'm gone," she said. "People notice things now."

Then she was up on the coach, chin set, satchel hugged to her chest like a child, and the team leaned into their collars, and the wheels crunched away down the road with the future riding on top of the mail sack.

* * *

That afternoon I walked out onto the bridge.

I hadn't been out on it since the night I arrived, when the last coach carried me across with snow beginning to fall and a girl warning me not to ask questions. The storm had closed it behind me within the hour, and for the whole of the winter it had hung there at the mouth of the town — iron and timber, shut like a door — and I had lived on the wrong side of it wondering if I would leave through it or stay behind it.

Now the door stood open.

The crew had been out since first light, clearing the ice from the approaches, testing the trusses where the floes had struck. They waved me through when I started onto the span; half of them had been in the observatory the night we sealed the deposition, and one of them, a mill hand named Corrigan, called out, "Give us a minute's warning next time you're planning to stop time entirely, lad — my wife hasn't slept right since."

I walked out to the middle and stopped.

The river ran below me, high and brown and fast, carrying the last of the ice toward the falls. Great slabs tilted and turned in the current, catching the afternoon light, and the water sang against the pilings in a way it had not sung all winter — freely, without resistance. Upstream I could see the observatory on its hill, small from this distance, its dome catching the sun, and beyond it the shuttered mills, and the rooftops, and the smoke rising thin and ordinary from a dozen chimneys of people who, as of yesterday, owned their own land again or soon would.

When I had crossed this bridge in December, I had been a young man hoping the journey would make him someone things happened to. I remember thinking it on the coach, feeling vaguely ashamed of the thought even then, the way one is ashamed of hunger. Things had happened to me since. A clock had shown me a lie sixty years deep. Saboteurs had broken an escapement in the night. A woman had taught me that grief kept too long turns to fear, and a sharp-tongued girl had taught me that fear kept too long turns to courage, given sufficient argument. I had cut an escape wheel from raw brass by candlelight while the world's historians waited on the other side of a door, and I had watched half a town stand up in an observatory and swear to what was true.

But standing there in the middle of the open span, with the wind coming up the river valley smelling of water and mud and the far-off promise of spring, I understood that the thing I had actually wanted was none of those happenings. What I had wanted was what the girl on the coach had, what the millwright's daughter had, what Ada had even in her worst grief: a place where my own history mattered. Where things happened *to me*, yes — but also *because of* me, and *with* me, among people who would remember.

I looked downstream, the way I'd be going if I left. The road ran along the bank, bare and muddy, heading out toward the county seat and the wider world and the workshop where my master was, at this hour, no doubt complaining that I had been gone a season over a job worth forty dollars.

I looked upstream instead. Observatory. Hill. Home, if I was honest about the word, which lately I was trying to be.

I didn't need the bridge as an exit anymore. That was the strange, light feeling in my chest, I realized — not relief exactly, not triumph. Release. The door was open, and I found I had no interest whatever in walking through it.

A piece of ice the size of a church pew went grinding past beneath my feet, caught the piling, spun once, and surrendered to the current. I watched it all the way to the bend.

Then I turned around and walked back into Verlow's Landing.

* * *

Ada was waiting for me at the observatory door, which I might have predicted. She had a way of being precisely where a thing concluded.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You stood in the middle of that bridge for the better part of half an hour, Mr. Ishikawa. A person doesn't stand in the middle of a bridge for half an hour unless he's deciding something."

"I already decided it," I said. "I was just enjoying deciding it."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, but the shadow a smile casts. She looked past me at the river, running free at last, and then back at me, and I saw that behind her, at the observatory window, a lamp had been lit and a third place set at the worktable, though dinner was hours off yet.

"Come inside, then," Ada said. "There's a letter on your bench. It came on the ferry."

"A letter for me?"

"From the state press office, by the look of the seal. Juniper must have wasted no time at all." She held the door. "They're asking after the clockmaker. Apparently the deposition mentions a certain timestamp, and the editor wants to know — his words — *how exactly the machine was made to lie, and how exactly it was caught.*

I stood a moment on the threshold, the river loud behind me and the warm tick of the master clock steady ahead, honest to the second for the first time in sixty years.

"They'll want it in writing," Ada added, with unmistakable satisfaction.

I went in to get my pen.
