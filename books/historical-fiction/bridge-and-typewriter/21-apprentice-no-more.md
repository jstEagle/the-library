## 21. Apprentice No More

The morning coach took the lawyer away at half past seven, and by half past eight the whole town had invented three new endings for the story.

Oscar heard all of them before breakfast. He heard them from Mr. Petch, the ferryman, who swore the lawyer had wept; from the baker's boy, who swore he had thrown his hat in the river; and from old Mrs. Halloway, who had actually been on the dock and reported — with the satisfaction of a woman who had waited eighty years to be the reliable source — that he had simply boarded without a word, clutching his case like a man carrying another man's luggage.

"Mark my words," she said, settling onto the archive's bench as though it were her pew, "that's how defeat travels. Quietly. With good posture."

Oscar poured her tea. It was the third cup he had poured that morning, and he noticed — not for the first time in recent weeks — that his hands no longer hesitated over other people's cups. A month ago he would have stood at the edge of a room like this one, waiting to be told where hands went. Now he simply knew. It was the same knowledge that lived in his fingertips when he handled the escapement: an understanding of where a thing belonged.

Juniper came down the stairs two at a time, which meant either catastrophe or triumph, and with the deposition gone out on yesterday's coach she was clearly out of catastrophes.

"The *Herald* printed the telegram," she announced, brandishing a newspaper like a captured flag. "Front page. Well — front page below the fold, but below the fold of a *real* newspaper, which is infinitely above the fold of a fake one." She slapped it down in front of Ada. "'LAND FRAUD UNCOVERED AT VERLOW'S LANDING — RAILROAD GRANTS DECLARED VOID — STATE ATTORNEY TO ACT.'"

Ada read it the way she read everything, slowly and twice, and then set it down flat and pressed her palm against it as if keeping it from blowing away. She did that lately — touched paper the way other people touched relics. Oscar suspected she would do it for the rest of her life.

"They spelled your name wrong," Ada said to him gently. "*Oskar* Ishikawa."

"They always do," Oscar said. "There's a clerk somewhere in this state who is certain of it."

Mrs. Halloway cackled and demanded more tea.

* * *

The observatory smelled of oil and cold stone and, faintly, of the river — that green mineral breath that got into everything in Verlow's Landing and that Oscar had stopped noticing weeks ago and now, suddenly, could not stop noticing, the way you hear your own name across a crowded room.

He climbed the tower steps with his toolkit under his arm, because climbing them with his toolkit under his arm was what mornings were now, and found Ada already at the clock, standing beneath the great pendulum with her hands folded, looking up at it the way she had looked at the newspaper.

"You've oiled it," she said.

"I always oil it."

"You've oiled it *tenderly*. I can tell." She turned. In the light from the east window her face was doing something complicated, and Oscar — who had learned to read mechanisms before faces and was still catching up — watched it resolve into resolve. "I have a proposition for you, Mr. Ishikawa."

His stomach performed a small mechanical fault. Propositions, in his experience, arrived with paperwork or consequences. "Ma'am?"

"The keeper's position." She said it plainly, the way she said everything that mattered. "It has been vacant since Elias died, though I have pretended otherwise for eleven years, because pretending was easier than admitting the town deserved better than a grieving archivist winding a clock she barely understood. The board will confirm whatever I recommend — they are currently so grateful to us that they'd confirm a goose." She paused. "You would keep the master clock. The maintenance logs. The records. You would be, officially, the man who makes sure this town's time is honest."

The pendulum swung between them, patient as ever, indifferent to being made metaphorical.

"I—" Oscar began, and found his throat had closed. He tried again. "I'm an apprentice."

"Were," said Ada.

"Were an apprentice," he agreed, and the word sat strangely in his mouth, both lighter and heavier than expected. "Master Hollenbeck hasn't released me. There are papers. There are — I don't even know what there are, there's never been a procedure, nobody leaves Hollenbeck & Sons, people *die* there—"

"Then wire him," Ada said. "The telegraph office reopened this morning. Mr. Petch is very disappointed; he enjoyed being the only line to the world."

She left him then, with the tact of a woman who had spent eleven years alone in this room and knew precisely the value of leaving a person alone in it, and Oscar stood beneath the clock he had repaired, sabotaged, rebuilt, and sworn upon, and felt the full weight of the offer settle onto him — not heavy, exactly. More like a coat put on by someone else's hands, warm already.

An apprentice no more. A keeper.

He thought of his first night here, when he had lain in the narrow bed above the archive listening to the wind worry the bridge and told himself he only wanted to fix the clock and go home. He had believed it, too. That was the funny part. He had genuinely believed a person could stand in the middle of something and remain outside it, the way a watchmaker stands outside time while mending it.

Nobody stands outside time. That was the first lesson the clock had taught him, though it had taken a broken escapement and a forged land grant and a frozen millrace to make him fluent in it.

* * *

He found Juniper in the archive's long room, up her ladder, reshelving boxes with the fierce efficiency of a general restoring order after a war. The railroad men's inventory slips lay stacked on the table — Marsh had signed each page before departing, and Juniper had annotated every one in pencil with corrections so savage they were practically editorial epigrams. *Incomplete. Misdated. This box contains teacups.*

"If you're here to help," she said without turning, "start with the 1880s. If you're here to gawk at the heroine of the hour, the light's better by the window."

"I was offered the keeper's position."

Her ladder stopped. For a long moment she stayed facing the shelf, and Oscar watched her shoulders perform a series of small negotiations. Then she came down, rung by deliberate rung, turned around, and looked at him with her arms crossed.

"Well," she said. "About time somebody noticed you're not temporary."

"That's nearly a kind thing to say."

"It's entirely a kind thing to say. Don't get greedy." She uncrossed her arms, then crossed them again, then gave up on them entirely. "Look. Here's the situation. Aunt Ada cooks for four now — five when Mrs. Halloway invades, which is nightly — and she has been setting your place at the table for three weeks regardless, and every single evening I say something like 'oh, look, an extra fork, how mysterious' or 'we keep setting this place for the ghost,' and every single evening you laugh politely and I die a little inside, because—"

She stopped. Took a breath. Started again, quieter, which for Juniper was roughly the volume of other people's declarations.

"Because what I mean is: sit down. Formally. As in, this is your chair, and this is your cup with the chipped rim that you insist is fine, and nobody is going to joke about it anymore, because you're not a lodger who happened into our storm. You're—" She gestured at him, at the room, at the eleven years of grief and paper and stubbornness around them. "You're ours. Professionally speaking."

"Professionally speaking," Oscar repeated.

"Don't make it weird."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"You're making it weird right now with your face."

"My face is fine."

"Your face is doing the thing where you're terribly moved and trying to look like you're checking someone's pulse." But she was grinning as she said it, and then, abruptly, she wasn't grinning — she crossed the aisle of boxes and hugged him, quick and fierce, before stepping back and pointing a finger as if the hug had been a diversionary tactic. "Dinner's at six. If you're late I'll tell Mrs. Halloway you doubted her flood testimony."

"I would never doubt her flood testimony."

"Then you'll be early."

* * *

The telegraph office occupied the front corner of the general store, its counter scarred by forty years of impatient elbows. The operator, a young man named Willis who had missed the entire affair due to a boil on the back of his neck and was therefore consuming the story secondhand with tremendous appetite, watched Oscar compose his message with the naked interest of a man reading over a shoulder at gunpoint.

Oscar wrote:

> MASTER HOLLENBECK HOLLENBECK AND SONS CLOCKWORKS BAY CITY.
> THE VERLOW LANDING CLOCK IS REPAIRED AND KEEPS TRUE TIME. THE WORK IS COMPLETE AND SO AM I. I ACCEPT POSITION AS OBSERVATORY KEEPER HERE. GRATEFUL FOR YOUR TEACHING. YOUR APPRENTICE NO MORE. OSCAR ISHIKAWA.

He counted the words twice, decided against economy, and paid for the lot.

Willis read the destination slip upside down. "*Hollenbeck and Sons?*" he said. "That's the famous one. The precision house. Fellas in the trade say an apprenticeship there is worth its weight in — well. In clocks." He looked up, baffled and a little awed. "You're giving that up? For a hill and a river town that smells like fish half of June?"

Oscar considered the question honestly, because Willis seemed owed an honest answer.

"When I came here," he said, "I thought belonging was something that happened to other people. Like weather." He slid the message across the counter. "Turns out you can build it. Same as anything else. Gear by gear."

Willis stared at him for a moment, then down at the form, then tapped out the message with unusual care, as though it might matter — which, Oscar reflected, watching the tape curl into the machine, it did. Everything here mattered now. Every timestamp, every signature, every word sent out along a wire toward a future that was, for once, unwritten by the victors.

The reply came the next morning. Oscar collected it himself.

> MESSAGE RECEIVED AND READ TWICE. YOU WERE THE BEST OF THEM AND THE ONLY ONE WHO ASKED WHY THE GEARS TURN INSTEAD OF ONLY HOW. THOUGHT YOU WOULD STAY FROM THE MOMENT I READ THAT SUMMONS LETTER OVER YOUR SHOULDER. THE SECOND BENCH IS YOURS IF EVER YOU PASS THROUGH. DO NOT SELL THE CHIPPED CUP. — HOLLENBECK

"He says don't sell the chipped cup," Oscar reported at dinner, holding the telegram aloft.

Juniper froze with the ladle halfway to the pot. "How does he know about the cup?"

"I never mentioned a cup in my life until Tuesday."

"Aunt Ada," Juniper said dangerously, "have you been *corresponding*—"

"There is a national association of horologists," Ada said serenely, serving Mrs. Halloway first, "and your uncle's old workshop subscribed to the same trade gazette as this office, and none of that is any concern of yours. Pass the salt."

Mrs. Halloway, mouth full, observed: "Family. All of you. Terrible at secrets, wonderful at everything else." She pointed her fork at Oscar. "Except you. You kept a whole conspiracy under this roof and I never once caught you. I'm having you over Thursdays to teach me card tricks."

"It's settled then," Ada said, and under the tablecloth — Oscar saw Juniper see it, and saw Juniper decide not to mention it — her hand rested briefly on his wrist, light as a mainspring coming to rest. "To the keeper of Verlow's time."

They raised their cups. The chipped one among them, held high, catching lamplight through its flaw.

* * *

That night he climbed the tower one last time before sleep, as had become his habit, and stood in the dark beside the running clock. Below, the town showed its scattered lamps. Beyond them the river ran black and easy, ice gone, the bridge silvered under a rising moon — open again, busy again, ordinary again. Somewhere south, a lawyer was drafting apologies. Somewhere east, a state attorney was opening files with Ada's husband's name in them. And here, above it all, the mechanism ticked with perfect, unaltered honesty, sixty beats to the minute, asking nothing of anyone.

Eleven months, he thought. Eleven months since the letter had come to the workshop and Master Hollenbeck had called it small work, a season's errand, nothing that would make a name.

He had wanted, back then, to become someone things happened to. Standing now in the warm smell of oil and brass, listening to the pendulum count out a town's forgiven history, he understood that he had gotten his wish backwards, and gotten it right anyway. Things had happened to him. And then — this was the part nobody warned you about — he had happened back.

He wound the striking train, checked the beat, and wrote the entry in the maintenance log in his neat apprentice hand, which he supposed he should stop calling apprentice now, though privately he hoped he never stopped writing carefully enough to deserve the name:

*March 30th. Time true. All witnesses accounted for. Keeper resident.*

Below, in the kitchen of the archive, three voices argued amiably about whether Mrs. Halloway's grandfather had indeed raced the millrace on a wager, and Oscar smiled and let the sound climb the stairwell to him, and pulled the door shut softly on the clockroom, and went home.

Down the hill, at the landing, the night boat came in with the mail, and among the letters and parcels and one crate marked FRAGILE — GLASS, AUDIO, a single envelope addressed in a hand Ada had not seen in eleven years waited in the sorting tray, postmarked from a town she had spent a decade believing was behind her.
