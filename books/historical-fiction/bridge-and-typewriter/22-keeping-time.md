## 22. Keeping Time

The first thing Oscar Ishikawa did each morning, before the kettle, before the bread, before he so much as laced his boots properly, was climb the observatory stairs and wind the clock.

It had been a year now, and the ritual had worn itself into him the way water wears a channel — not by force but by repetition. He came up the stairs in the dark of the winter dawn, struck a match, lit the lamp beside the great case, and put his ear to the wood the way a doctor listens to a chest. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* The heartbeat of Verlow's Landing, steady at last.

"Good morning to you too," he murmured, because a man who talks to clocks is eccentric, but a clockmaker who doesn't talk to clocks is lonely, and Oscar had decided a year ago that he was finished with loneliness.

He wound the going train first, then the strike, counting the turns without thinking about them anymore. His hands knew the work. That was the strange gift of a year: what had once required all his attention now required only his presence, which left his mind free to wander where it liked — usually to the window, where the river lay flat and silver in the early light, running open, running honest, carrying barges of wheat toward towns that would never know how nearly their histories had been rewritten for them.

The pendulum swung in its long arc below, and beside it, framed in walnut that Ada had chosen and Juniper had insisted on paying for out of her own wages, hung the oral histories. Six wax cylinders' worth of testimony, transcribed in Juniper's fierce upright hand and sealed under glass: Mrs. Halloway's account of watching her father sign nothing; the ferryman's memory of the grant office burning; old Tom Brandt's recollection of the survey stakes moved in the night, "and every man in town knowing it, and no man saying it, because the railroad paid better than the truth did."

Oscar read them sometimes while the mainsprings took up their tension. He never tired of them. They were, he thought, rather like the clock itself — mechanisms built to hold something true against the pull of everything that wanted it otherwise.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear the archive waking up. A door. The scrape of a chair. Juniper's voice, already arguing with someone, though the archive didn't officially open for another hour. She argued with the stove most mornings. The stove lost.

Oscar smiled, gave the pendulum a small unnecessary nod — the clock did not require his approval, but he enjoyed giving it — and went down to make breakfast for three.

* * *

The land titles had come back in the spring, all forty-one of them, restored by order of the state land commission after the deposition was published. There had been a ceremony on the courthouse steps. Ada had been asked to speak, and had spoken for four minutes exactly, which everyone agreed was very restrained of her, and Juniper had cried and denied crying about it for a week afterward.

The millwright's granddaughter still owned the mill parcel. Mrs. Halloway, who had given her testimony at ninety-one years of age with the clear-eyed relish of a woman who had waited fifty years to say her piece, lived another eight months after that — long enough to see the deed reissued with her father's name spelled correctly on the boundary record, and long enough to attend the dedication of the new plaque at the observatory, at which she had told Oscar, patting his arm, "You're the best thing the storm ever blew in here, young man," and then gone home and died peacefully in her sleep, which Oscar chose to believe was her idea of a perfect exit line.

Her cylinder still hung by the pendulum. He dusted the frame every morning.

"You talk to it more than you talk to us," Juniper said one morning over eggs, not looking up from the ledger she was correcting. She corrected ledgers the way other people sharpened knives.

"I talk to you constantly."

"You argue with me. It's different." She turned a page. "Mrs. Halloway never argues."

"She does, actually. On cylinder four. She argues with the county clerk for a full two minutes and wins."

Juniper's mouth twitched despite herself. "Well. Someone should have recorded *that* clerk."

Ada came in from the cold with an armful of firewood and the particular expression she wore when she had news she was pretending wasn't news. At sixty-three, Ada Verlow had the posture of a woman who had spent decades holding herself very straight so that grief couldn't bend her, and this past year had gradually let her unbend, degree by degree, like a spring releasing. She still wore black — she said she'd gotten used to the wardrobe — but there was color in it now. A scarf Juniper had knitted. A brooch shaped like a small brass gear that Oscar had made her for Christmas and pretended was nothing.

"The commission wrote again," she said, setting down the wood.

"And?" Juniper said.

"They've approved the marker for the flood of '89. The real one. With the names." Ada sat down and warmed her hands at the stove. "All of them. Including—" She stopped, the way she always stopped at that name, though the pause had grown shorter over the months. "Including Elias."

The room held its breath politely, the way rooms do when they have learned a family's weather.

"That's good," Juniper said quietly. "That's right, that is."

"He'd have hated the fuss," Ada said, and then, with the small crooked smile that had become more frequent lately: "He'd have stood at the back of the crowd checking everyone's dates."

"He'd have loved it," Juniper said. "He'd have pretended to hate it. Like somebody else I could mention."

"I have no idea who you mean," said Ada, and poured the coffee.

* * *

On Thursdays, the schoolchildren came up to the observatory.

This had been Juniper's doing, naturally. She had marched into the schoolhouse the previous autumn and informed the teacher that history was being kept in an attic above the archive by people who actually knew things, and that if the children weren't brought to see it she would begin bringing it to them, whether the curriculum approved or not. The teacher, a sensible woman, had surrendered immediately.

Oscar had been assigned the clock. He had protested that he was a repairman, not a lecturer, and Juniper had looked at him with one eyebrow raised until he understood that the matter was settled.

So now, every Thursday, a dozen children clattered up the stairs and stood in a semicircle around the great case, and Oscar showed them the escapement — the new escape wheel, the one he had cut by hand over three sleepless days while Juniper distracted spies and Ada forged passes, though the children got the shorter version of that story.

"This wheel here," he'd say, tapping the glass, "is why your supper is on time and the ferry leaves when the schedule says and nobody misses the mail boat. Every clock in town takes its orders from this one. If I set this wrong by a minute, the whole town runs a minute wrong and doesn't even know it."

A boy named Peter Aldous, who was eleven and skeptical in a way Oscar privately admired, raised his hand. "How would we know? If it was wrong?"

"Ah." Oscar crouched to the boy's level. "That's the question, isn't it. You'd check it against something true. The sun, mostly. The stars, if you were careful. And you'd write down what you found, and you'd keep the writing somewhere safe, and you'd make sure more than one person saw it." He straightened up. "Because a thing that only one person knows can be changed. A thing written down and witnessed — that's harder."

"Is that what happened here?" asked a small girl near the front. "With the bad men?"

"It is," Oscar said. "And do you know who fixed it?"

"The archivist lady," said one child.

"The niece," said another.

"A clockmaker," said Peter Aldous, grudgingly, looking at Oscar.

"All three," Oscar said. "That's rather the point. Nobody fixes anything alone. Believe me. I tried being alone for twenty-two years and it was terribly inefficient."

They laughed, and he showed them how the striking train worked, and let them count the chimes at noon, and sent them back down the hill louder than they'd arrived, and stood a moment in the quiet afterward with his hand resting on the case like a man resting his hand on the shoulder of an old friend.

* * *

In March, a letter came from his old master.

Oscar recognized the handwriting before he recognized the return address — that cramped, impatient script, pressing hard enough to dent the paper three sheets deep. He carried it up to the observatory rather than opening it downstairs, because some letters want privacy, and sat on the workbench stool with the afternoon light coming gold through the east windows.

*Ishikawa,* it began, no salutation, because Master Fenwick considered salutations a waste of ink. *Your wire last spring was insolent, ungrammatical, and correct in every particular. I have sold the shop. My eyes are done and my patience finished with it. Before you write back something equally insolent, know that I am not asking you to return. I am informing you that the lathe is yours if you want it shipped. It was my master's before it was mine. It should not go to a stranger.*

*Also enclosed is a bill for the tools you took without settling accounts. I have deducted it from your final wages. Do the arithmetic yourself; you were always slow at it.*

*You were the least promising apprentice I ever trained and the only one I will miss. This sentence is not to be repeated.*

*— F.*

There was no signature, just the initial, because Master Fenwick believed full signatures were for lawyers and men with something to prove.

Oscar read it twice. Then he laughed, once, loudly, in the empty observatory, startling a pigeon off the roof, and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, and sat for a long time listening to the clock tick.

That evening he wired back the shortest message of his life, having learned economy from the source:

*LATHE ACCEPTED. BILL DISPUTED. ARITHMETIC CORRECT BUT SENTIMENT NOTED. — ISHIKAWA*

He received no reply, which from Fenwick was a warm embrace.

* * *

Summer came to the river the way it always did — slowly, then all at once, the ice a memory by April and the water high and brown and busy by June. The bridge stood open to traffic again, and the morning coach crossed it twice a day, and Oscar watched it pass from the observatory window with the mild interest of a man watching weather he no longer needed.

Once, in July, a traveler knocked at the archive door: a young man with a leather satchel and an earnest face, introducing himself as a historian preparing a regional survey, asking politely about the town's records. Ada received him with impeccable courtesy and showed him the public registers, and Juniper sat in the corner copying documents with her pen scratching like a small angry insect, and the young man left after an hour with nothing but what any citizen might consult, and none the wiser that the room had assessed him, weighed him, and found him harmless within the first thirty seconds.

"We're getting good at this," Juniper observed after the door closed.

"We were always good at this," Ada said. "We just used to be good at it frightened. It's much easier unfrightened."

Oscar, mending a shutter hinge in the corner, said nothing, but he thought about the cam — the little altered cam, the size of a thumb, that had bent a town's time for forty years. It sat now in a velvet-lined box in the state historical society, catalogued, photographed, labeled as evidence in the matter of *Verlow's Landing Grant Dispute*, the first item in a collection that had grown to include photographs, affidavits, and six wax cylinders. Sometimes he imagined some future scholar lifting it out, turning it over, puzzling at it. *Why would anyone do this?* And the answer would be there in the file, in Juniper's upright hand: *Because someone wanted the record to say what it never said. And someone else refused.*

Two cams, he supposed. Two ways to shape time. One cut in secret to make a lie run smoothly; one cut by lamplight over three desperate nights to make the truth run at all. He knew which one he'd rather be remembered by.

* * *

In September, on the anniversary of the day the storm closed the bridge, the three of them walked down to the riverbank in the evening.

It was Juniper's idea, though she claimed it wasn't. "I'm going to check the water level," she announced, "for the archive," and Ada said, "Of course you are," and Oscar fetched a lantern without being asked, and somehow all three of them ended up standing on the bank where the ferry landing used to be, watching the current carry the last light downstream.

"One year ago tonight," Ada said, "a boy arrived on the last coach, planning to fix a clock and leave."

"I had a return ticket," Oscar said.

"You never used it."

"I never needed it." He turned the lantern down low, so the river showed through. "I used to think things happened *to* other people. History, I mean. Floods and fires and swindles and brave widows. I thought my job was to stand outside it all and keep the machines running for people whose lives mattered." He shook his head. "Then a bridge closed, and it turned out I'd walked into the middle of the story. Not beside it. In it."

"You didn't walk into it," Juniper said. "You were summoned. There's a difference. Somebody needed you before you ever knew you were needed." She paused, and then, because sincerity embarrassed her the way damp embarrassed a cat, added: "Don't let it go to your head. You're still terrible at arithmetic."

"Fenwick says the same."

"Fenwick sounds intelligent."

The river ran past them, dark and steady, carrying whatever it carried. Somewhere upstream the mills were turning again — two of them, reopened under the restored titles, hiring hands, paying fair rates, the whole ordinary machinery of a town resuming after a long stall. Downstream, the lights of the Landing came on one by one, each one timed, whether anyone knew it or not, by the old clock on the hill.

"Tomorrow," Ada said, "the commission men arrive to set the flood memorial stone. Elias's name on it. And Thursday the children come, and Saturday the county sends the new register books, and next month—" She stopped herself and laughed softly. "Listen to me. Planning next month. I spent five years unable to plan past breakfast."

"What changed?" Oscar asked, though he thought he knew.

"Nothing changed," Ada said. "Everything changed. The record is honest now. When the record is honest, the future is possible. That's the whole of it. That's what we fought for — not the past. The past is settled either way. We fought so that tomorrow would be a place worth planning for."

She took Oscar's arm on one side and Juniper's on the other, and they walked back up the hill together, three silhouettes against the lamplit street, and behind them the river went on keeping its own time, patient and impartial, rising and falling, remembering everything.

* * *

At dawn the next morning, Oscar climbed the stairs, struck his match, lit the lamp, and put his ear to the case.

*Ticking. Ticking. Ticking.*

He wound the going train, counted the turns, checked the beat against his own pulse out of habit, and found them agreeing. Through the window the sun came up over the hills and laid a stripe of gold across the pendulum's arc, and the framed testimonies caught the light — Mrs. Halloway's voice, the ferryman's voice, old Tom Brandt's voice, all of them hanging there in glass and walnut, keeping watch over the mechanism that kept watch over the town.

Below, the archive door opened. The stove was lit. An argument began.

Oscar smiled, gave the pendulum its morning nod, and went down to join them — a keeper now, in fact and in name, of a clock that told the truth, in a town where the history had finally happened to him, on a morning that belonged entirely to itself.

The kettle was on. The day was beginning. He had nowhere else in the world he needed to be.
