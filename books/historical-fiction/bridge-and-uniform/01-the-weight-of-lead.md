## 1. The Weight of Lead

My father taught me that everything in the world could be measured in what a person could carry.

Not weight, exactly. Not pounds and hundredweight, the way the wool merchants on the bridge reckoned things, or the grain factors with their brass scales that cheated by a hair and called it God's will. He meant something finer. A font tray of twelve-point Garamond, he could carry. A grudge against the Guildmaster of Binders — that he carried too, though it weighed less every year, like a coat worn thin at the elbows. A memory of my mother's voice singing while she inked the rollers: he carried that until the day he died, and I suspect it was the heaviest thing in the shop.

He died eleven months ago this week, which is not something I mention to customers, because customers do not care, and because grief is like wet ink — it transfers to everything you touch if you're careless about it.

I am not careless.

I was cutting a new lowercase *g* when the bells rang nine that morning, and I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I have ever kept: I had been at the *g* since seven, and it was fighting me. The old men say a typeface cutter should be able to finish a letter in an hour, two hours if the letter is proud. But my father's letters took as long as they took, because his rule — one of the three rules he left me, chiseled into the lintel of his own mind rather than any doorpost — was that lead forgives nothing. Pour it wrong and it stays wrong forever. Cut it wrong and ten thousand impressions carry your error down the years, each one a small accusation.

So I cut slow.

The shop sits on Tanners' Row, third building past the alley where the tanners themselves no longer work, ever since the river turned against them and the smell became unbearable even to men who made their living from it. We get their old smells anyway. Everything in Vellsmark comes back to the river eventually — the water brings the boats, the boats bring the news, the news brings the work, and the work, if you're a printer, is mostly people wanting other people to believe things.

That morning the work was a funeral notice for Alderman Prew, who had died rich and was hoping to die respected, and needed forty copies of his good deeds set in mourning black before Saturday. His son had brought the list of deeds himself, written in a hand so confident it bordered on forgery, and I had read through it twice and found four deeds that were exaggerations and one that was, as far as I knew, an outright fiction involving charity toward tanners. I corrected nothing. That is not my business. My business is the letterforms, not the lies. My father used to say a printer's conscience lives in his hands and nowhere else, and his hands were clean, and so are mine, mostly, in the way that hands are clean when you wash them often enough and don't look under the nails.

Tally was sweeping. She sweeps badly but enthusiastically, a girl of perhaps eleven — nobody knows exactly, least of all her — whom my father took off the church steps two winters back and I have kept on since, partly out of charity and partly because she is the only person alive who makes the shop feel like it contains more than one heartbeat. She had arranged herself at the far end near the composing stone, sweeping the same three feet of floor with the dogged persistence of someone who has learned that visible industry is safer than questions.

"You missed a spot," I said, without looking up.

"I'm doing a different spot now."

"That spot's worse."

"Then it needed doing more." She paused, and I heard the broom stop, which meant a question was loading. "Bram. Is it true there's soldiers crossing at Harwick Ford?"

The bell of St. Vellus finished striking nine, its last note hanging in the damp air like a held breath.

"Who told you that?"

"Marta. Her uncle drives the mail barge."

"Her uncle drives the beer barge," I said, "and the only thing crossing at Harwick is beer." I said it steadily, because that is what you do for children, you build them a floor to stand on even when you're not sure there's joists underneath. "Sweep."

She swept.

But here is the thing about rumors in a river town: they travel faster than barges, and unlike barges, nobody has to load them. By the time the funeral notice was set and locked in the chase — and it was locked well, I'll say that for myself, wedges true, furniture tight, a form you could drop down a staircase and reprint from — I had heard the soldier story three more times. Once from the paper merchant, who wanted payment in advance because of it. Once from Mrs. Hallow the ink-seller, who wanted payment in advance because of everything. And once from Master Quill, who came in at half-past ten smelling of pipe smoke and worry, and did not want payment at all.

Quill is the guild's man in our quarter — not the Guildmaster, nothing so grand, but the one who keeps the accounts straight between shops and carries the guild ledger when it needs carrying, which is rarely, because a ledger that heavy does not go out for walks. He is a soft-spoken man shaped like a question mark, with ink stains so deep in his knuckles that surgeons would call them scars. He looked at the funeral notice in the chase, nodded the nod of a man approving another man's knots, and then stood looking out the window at the street for longer than standing and looking warrants.

"Master Quill," I said. "You're wearing out my view."

"Bram." He didn't turn around. "Has anyone spoken to you about the guild meeting Thursday?"

"Tally says there's soldiers at Harwick Ford."

"There are no soldiers at Harwick Ford." He turned then, and his face did the thing faces do when a man knows something and wishes he knew less. "There will be no soldiers anywhere, I expect, and Thursday is about the apprenticeship rates, nothing else. Come if you like. Or don't. It doesn't matter."

Nothing else. It doesn't matter. In my experience, when a man tells you twice that a thing doesn't matter, he has already measured exactly how much it matters and decided you can't afford the number.

* * *

After Quill left I sent Tally out for bread, which was half an errand and half a way of getting her onto the street where the world, whatever it was preparing to become, would look ordinary a while longer. Then I went downstairs into the cellar where the paper is stored, because I needed to count it, and because cellars are honest places. No windows. Nothing to see but what you brought down with you.

We had perhaps six reams of good stock, three of the cheap grey stuff, and a quarter-ream of the heavy rag paper my father saved for the guild certificates, which he printed once a year in gold and black and which I have printed none of since he died, because the guild stopped asking. Another thing I don't mention to customers.

I counted the paper twice. Numbers are good company when you're lonely; they sit still and answer exactly when asked.

I want to be honest here, since honesty in a cellar costs nothing: I have been lonely for eleven months, and I was lonely for some years before that, if truth be told, growing up the daughter of a man married to his trade. My father loved me the way he loved type — precisely, deeply, with total commitment of craft and almost none of words. He could tell me the exact weight of lead in a completed font. He could not tell me, in any terms I understood at fourteen, why my mother's death had made him quieter instead of sadder, or why he spent whole evenings cutting letters nobody had commissioned, alphabets for languages nobody in Vellsmark spoke. I thought, for years, that the silence was aimed at me. Now I know it was just the sound of a man carrying something, and I would give a great deal to hear it again — the silence, I mean, with him inside it.

The shop is mine now, or mine as long as the rent gets paid and the guild doesn't object, and I run it the way he ran it, which is to say I measure everything and explain nothing. The town thinks of me as cold, I believe. Precise Bram Okafor, who quotes exact prices and keeps exact books and has never once let a customer talk her down over a pint the way her father did. They mean it as a complaint. I take it as a compliment. Cold is what lead is before you pour it, and lead before the pour can still become anything.

Upstairs again, I cleaned the press. This is a ritual, and I am not ashamed of rituals; a press is a temperamental animal partway between a beast and a cathedral, and it wants tending. While I oiled the platen threads I listened to the street, the way my father taught me without meaning to — he always said a printer knows the town through the window better than the priest knows it through the confessional. And the street that morning sounded wrong. Not loud. Wrong the way a clock is wrong when it stops: an absence with edges to it. Fewer carts. More clusters of people standing together who had no trade reason to stand together. Two men from the fish market talking to a woman from the wool exchange, heads bent, in the manner of people sharing weather news, except the weather was not weather.

At noon Tally came back with the bread and a face full of news she'd been saving like coins.

"There's red coats at the bridge," she said. "Real ones. Marta saw them. Scouts, she said, riding ahead of boats. They posted a paper on the toll-house."

"A paper."

"A proclamation. Nobody can read it except Mr. Ellery and he read it out and then a sergeant tore it down again, which Marta says means it was bad." She delivered this with the relish of a girl whose life has recently contained very little drama, and then, because children are barometers and barometers feel the pressure before the storm has any manners at all: "Are we in trouble, Bram?"

I looked at her — flour on her sleeve, broom-callus just starting on her palm, a girl I fed and taught and had never once thought of as anything I might lose — and I gave her the floor I'd promised her earlier.

"We're printers," I said. "Nobody troubles printers. Ink doesn't frighten anyone."

This was a lie. Even as I said it, some part of me that had inherited my father's measuring eye was doing sums I didn't want done: requisitions need presses. Presses print proclamations. Proclamations are how armies talk to towns, and armies do not like the towns to answer back, and the guild ledger — that great book in Quill's keeping, with every printer, debtor, apprentice, sympathizer, and creditor in the district recorded in a hand I knew better than my own —

In my father's hand. Every name. Written by him, year after year, in the clerk's script he'd learned as a boy and never lost.

I had seen that ledger exactly once, at fourteen, and I remembered it the way you remember a church: vast, ordered, faintly frightening, full of names.

* * *

We ate bread and hard cheese at the bench by the window, Tally and I, watching the bridge. From Tanners' Row you cannot see the bridge itself, but you can see the road to it, and the road told us enough. Red coats came down it in twos and threes through the afternoon, unhurried, the way men move when they own the ground they're walking on and want everyone to know it costs them nothing. They posted papers at the corner by the cooper's. They measured the customs house with their eyes. One of them bought an apple, paid fairly, and somehow that frightened me more than the rest of it put together — discipline is scarier than swagger, because swagger is just appetite, and appetite can be fed.

"What does requisitioned mean?" Tally asked, around the cheese. Someone had read the proclamation aloud by then, and the word had gone through the streets like dye through water.

"It means they borrow," I said. "Houses. Boats. Shops."

"Do they give them back?"

I thought of my father, and of every history he'd set in type for merchants and aldermen and once, quietly, for a committee of men who met behind the chandler's and talked about rights and rents and the price of obedience. Armies borrow the way rivers borrow valleys. They take the shape of everything they pass through and leave the shape changed.

"Sometimes," I said.

She nodded, satisfied, and went back to her cheese, and I envied her the simplicity of being eleven.

As the light went amber over the rooftops I banked the fire, covered the composing stone, and did the last thing I do every night before locking up: I checked the type. All of it. Drawer by drawer, case by case, the whole inheritance of lead and tin and antimony that my father spent thirty years accumulating — the Garamond and the Caslon, the fat-faced display roman he cut himself and named after my mother, the little italic he was working on the winter he died, unfinished, missing its final *z*, which I have refused to complete for eleven months because some things should stay open. I ran my thumb along the cases the way you'd check a sleeping child's brow. All present. All accounted for.

It occurred to me, standing there in the lamplight with the smell of oil and metal, that if I had to leave this shop tomorrow, I knew exactly what I would carry. The knife — his cutting knife, horn handle worn to the shape of his grip. One tray of the mother-face type, because a woman should keep her mother's name somewhere heavier than memory. And the unfinished italic, *z* and all its absence. Three things. I had never made the list before, and making it felt like a kind of prayer, and prayers, in my experience, are what people do instead of running while there's still time.

Tally had fallen asleep in the chair by the cooling stove, curled with her feet tucked up, the broom still leaning against her knee where she'd never quite put it down. I put my own coat over her and did not wake her, and stood a moment watching her breathe, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that my list was short by one item, and that the item outweighed everything else in the shop, and that lists are useless for measuring the things that actually matter.

Outside, the river kept moving in the dark, bringing whatever it was bringing.

And on the table by the door, where I'd left it unthinkingly among the day's papers, lay the funeral notice for Alderman Prew, pulled and stacked and ready for delivery — forty copies of a rich man's decent self, printed neat and true in my father's letters, in ink that would dry by morning and hold its shape for a hundred years.

I wondered, as I trimmed the lamp, how many other things in Vellsmark were about to be set in type — and who would decide which of them we could carry.
