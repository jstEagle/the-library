## 2. Ledger of Names

Master Quill came down the stairs at half past seven, which was late for him, and he came down the way a man descends when he has already decided something and is dreading the moment the rest of us find out what it is.

I knew his footsteps. Everyone in the shop knew Quill's footsteps — the soft drag of the left heel, the little hitch before the last three stairs, as if the stairs themselves had once offended him and he'd never fully forgiven them. I had been cutting a new face into a piece of type-metal that morning, a serif with a stubborn curl to it that refused to behave, and I kept my burin moving while he crossed the floor toward me, because in my experience the surest way to make a man lose his nerve is to look up at him expectantly.

"Bram," he said.

"Master Quill."

"You've ink on your cheek."

"I have ink everywhere. It's how you know I work here."

He did not laugh. That was the second sign. The first was the hour; the third was that he'd come downstairs at all, because Quill's knees had an arrangement with gravity and the arrangement did not include unnecessary trips between floors. He lowered himself onto the stool beside my bench with the care of a man settling into a boat, and he looked at the tray of half-finished sorts in front of me, and then at the window, and then at his own hands.

"Your father," he said, "kept accounts."

"He did. Meticulously. He once chased a man four streets over a debt of two coppers, and when he caught him, he made him pay it in one copper and a sincere apology, and he recorded both."

Quill almost smiled. Almost. "Yes. Well." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the bench, where thirty years of knives had worn a groove smooth as river stone. "There are accounts, Bram. And there is *the* account."

I set down the burin. When a man starts italicizing words with his voice, you stop working.

"The guild ledger," I said.

"The guild ledger."

It sat, as it always sat, in the locked drawer of the front office, in the cabinet with the brass fittings, under a copy of the Vellsmark Weavers' Charter that nobody had ever read past the second clause. Every printer in the district knew it existed the way every sailor knows the tide exists — as a fact of the world, vast and impersonal, something you worked around rather than thought about. Debts owed between shops. Dues paid and unpaid. Apprentices bound and released. The whole slow machinery of the trade, written down in one book so that no man could lie about what he owed or what he was owed.

"Has someone defaulted?" I asked. "Because if it's Hobb again, I told him last month that pressing his bills flat doesn't make them paid—"

"Bram."

That was all. Just my name, in the voice he used when Tally had nearly set her sleeve alight at the press. I stopped talking.

"I want you to see it," Quill said. "Before anyone else does. Before—" He stood, knees cracking like green kindling. "Come upstairs."

* * *

The office smelled the way it always had: dust, lamp oil, leather, and beneath it all the faint metallic ghost of lead, which clings to everything in this trade the way grief clings to everything in a life. The ledger lay on the desk already, which meant Quill had taken it out before he'd come down to fetch me, which meant he had sat alone with it for some time, deciding.

It was not an impressive object to look at. A folio bound in calfskin gone brown and soft at the corners, its spine re-stitched twice, the second stitching in my father's hand — I could tell by the tension of it, the even pull, the way he'd waxed the thread. My father could not leave anything in this world unimproved. If God had handed him the sky he'd have kerned it.

"Open it," Quill said.

"Whose hand is it in?"

"Yours will be in it someday. Open it."

I opened it.

The first thing that struck me was not the contents but the *handwriting*, and I am aware of how that sounds — a typeface cutter moved to feeling by penmanship — but you must understand that I had spent my whole life inside my father's letters without ever seeing them move. On the page they lived. His capitals stood like men who know their own weight. His numerals leaned forward slightly, eager, as if the sums themselves were urgent business. And the entries ran back and back and back, decades deep, the ink shifting from iron-gall black to a softer brown as the years turned, each entry dated, each name written out in full, no abbreviations, because my father believed a name abbreviated was a person diminished.

*Hobb, Marta — printer, bindery on Cordwainer Row — dues current — debt to Okafor & Sons, 4 marks, 3 shillings, carried against goodwill.*

*Vess, Aldous — compositor — bound apprentice 14th of March — released to journeyman status, fee waived, "shows promise beyond his station and knows it."*

Page after page. Every printer in Vellsmark. Every debtor. Every sympathizer — and there the word itself, *sympathizer*, written plainly, meaning everyone from the alewife who'd let guild meetings happen in her back room to the harbor clerk who'd looked away from certain crates. My father had recorded them all with the same unhurried precision, the same fairness, debts entered beside kindnesses as though the two were simply different currencies of the same economy.

And then, near the middle of the book, I found the gap.

It wasn't obvious. That's the point. It took me a quarter hour of reading before I felt it — the way you feel a wrong note in a familiar song, before you can say which note it was. There were printers missing. Small ones, struggling ones, the kind whose names should have appeared in a dozen small transactions and didn't. The Fenwick brothers, who printed ballads off a single battered press and owed money to everybody. Old Sarelle, half-blind, who set funeral notices. A widow named Corren whose husband had held a share in the guild before the fever year. I knew these people. I had grown up among their presses. And here, in the great book that held everything, they were simply — absent.

"Quill," I said. "Where are the Fennicks?"

He was standing at the window with his back to me, watching the street, which is what he did instead of looking at people when the truth was about to hurt one of us. "Read the marginalia," he said. "The early pages. Your father's notes to himself."

I went back to the beginning. And there, tucked into the margin of the third page, in pencil rather than ink — pencil, my father, who considered pencil a coward's instrument, using *pencil* — a single line:

*Some names do more good unrecorded. Let the book be true where truth protects, and silent where silence does.*

Below it, another line, later, the pencil pressed harder:

*A ledger is a weapon pointed at whoever is written in it. Mind the direction of the point.*

I sat down. I don't remember deciding to sit; my legs made the decision on behalf of the rest of me.

"He left them out," I said. "Deliberately. Years of them."

"Every year," Quill said, still at the window. "Anyone too poor to survive being written down. Anyone whose politics would hang them if the wrong clerk ever copied this book. Anyone your father judged — and he judged carefully, Bram, he agonized, he'd sit in that chair till the candle drowned — anyone he judged could not afford the weight of their own name in ink." He turned around. "You think this is a book of debts. It is. But it is also a book of *protections*. Every name in here is a name your father chose to stand behind. And every name missing is a name he chose to hide."

I looked down at the page. At the widow Corren, absent. At old Sarelle, absent. At the hundreds of names present, each one a small act of witness — *this person existed, this person worked, this person owed and was owed, and I, Joren Okafor, saw them.* My father had been dead eleven months and he was still doing it. Still standing behind people. Still hiding the ones who needed hiding, in a book I had walked past ten thousand times without once wondering what it weighed.

"Why show me now?" I asked. My voice came out strange. Smaller than I intended.

Quill came and sat across the desk from me, and for a long moment he just looked at the ledger with an expression I had never seen on him before, and I realized, with a slow cold turn in my stomach, that it was fear.

"Because of the proclamation drafts coming out of the governor's office," he said. "Because the army requisition orders are being drawn up in the capital and Vellsmark is on the list — you've seen the scouts on the bridge, don't tell me you haven't. Because when soldiers occupy a town, Bram, they don't want its grain first. They want its *records*. Grain feeds a town. Records feed a trial." He laid one knotted finger on the calfskin cover, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you might need to wake. "This book proves the guild organized. It proves who funded the pamphlets, who sheltered the organizers, who spoke against the tithe courts. Eleven years of quiet resistance, indexed. Alphabetized. In your father's beautiful, damning hand."

"It also proves half the town bought bread from the other half," I said. "You can't try a district."

"You can if you only enter the evidence you like." He tapped the page. "A ledger cuts both ways. It records the sympathy — and it omits the innocent. But an army clerk won't see the omissions. An army clerk sees a list of conspirators, complete, signed, and dated. Your father hid forty people in this book by leaving them out, and not one of them can prove they were hidden, because the proof *is the absence*, and absence proves nothing to a man with a rope."

The lamp guttered. Outside, somewhere down Cordwainer Row, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread, and I hated it a little for being ordinary.

"So we burn it," I said.

Quill flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck him. "Burn it?"

"That's what you're thinking. I can hear it thinking through your skull. Burn it, and the army finds ash, and ash convicts nobody."

"Ash convicts nobody," Quill agreed slowly, "and protects nobody either. Do you understand what's in this book besides danger, girl? Pardons. Proof of payment. Records of apprenticeships that mean the difference between a trade and a gallows for boys like young Vess. If we burn it, every honest entry burns with the dishonest ones, and the army writes its own version, and its version will have no mercies in it at all." He closed his eyes. "Your father faced this question every year of his life, and every year he chose to keep writing. I never understood why until tonight, holding it, knowing what's coming. He kept writing because a record is the only weapon the powerless get to keep. You don't throw away your only weapon because it's heavy."

"And if the weapon gets turned around on us?"

"Then we'll wish we'd thrown it away," Quill said. "And we'll wish it anyway if we do."

* * *

I stayed in the office after he went to bed. He offered me the lamp and I told him I'd bring it down when I was done, and he looked at me for a moment — that long, tired, fond look he reserves for moments when I remind him most of my father — and climbed the stairs without arguing, which from Quill was a form of blessing.

I read the ledger the way I'd once read stories as a child: hungrily, hunting myself in it.

I found my mother first, though I wasn't looking for her. *Okafor, Lira — died 9th of June, fever year — account settled in full by subscription of the guild, "she mended every man's apron in this district and asked nothing, which the guild found embarrassing and attempted to remedy."* I put my hand flat on the page for a while. The paper was cool. Paper is always cool; that's the trick of it, the way it holds heat for the living and gives none back.

Then I found the entry for the fire. Not our fire — the print-shop fire on Tanner's Lane, six years back, the one that killed the Delvard family and started all the talk about safety codes and guild inspections and, eventually, the pamphlets, and eventually the organizing, and eventually whatever it was that was coming for us now. My father had recorded it in four lines, dry as bone, and then added a fifth:

*Witnesses agree the lamp was sound. Witnesses disagree on who else was in the building. I record what witnesses agree upon and keep the rest in my head, where it cannot be subpoenaed.*

My father, keeping secrets in his skull. It should not have surprised me. It surprised me anyway. I had spent eleven months grieving a careful man and had somehow never considered that his carefulness had a second floor.

Near dawn I found the last entry in his hand. Undated, which was unlike him, and short, which was very like him:

*If you are reading this, then either the guild has fallen on hard trust, or my daughter has finally learned to pick the office lock, in which case — well done, and put the lock-picks back where you found them.*

I laughed. Alone in a dark office at four in the morning, over a book full of condemned names, I laughed until my eyes stung, and I'm still not entirely sure the laughing and the stinging were separate events.

Because underneath the joke, in smaller script, almost apologetic, he had written one more line:

*Bram. If trouble comes for this book, remember: what is written can be burned, but what is understood travels. Teach the names to someone. That is the only safe place a ledger has ever been kept.*

I sat with that a long time. Then I did what he'd have done. I closed the book, returned it to the drawer, locked the brass fittings, and went downstairs to the bench where my unfinished sort waited with its stubborn curling serif, and I picked up the burin, and I cut.

But I cut differently that morning. Slower. Listening. Because a man who hides mercy in the margins of an account book teaches you, even from the grave, to read everything twice — and because somewhere across the river, on the far span of the bridge where the water runs fast and cold, I could hear, faint under the morning noise, the drum of boots that was not yet an army but was practicing to become one.

Tally came in at eight with bread and news: red-coated scouts on the bridge, posting papers to the toll-post, and a crowd gathering to read them in the bad silence crowds use when the news is bad.

Quill looked at me over the top of his spectacles.

"Well," he said. "It seems the book has heard us talking."
