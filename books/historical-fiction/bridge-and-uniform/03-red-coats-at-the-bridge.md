## 3. Red Coats at the Bridge

I went to the bridge myself, because I have never trusted a story I couldn't verify with my own eyes. My father taught me that. He said a typeface cutter who trusts secondhand measurements will cut a crooked letter and swear it's straight, and there is no arguing with the proof once it's pulled. You look at the printed page and the page doesn't care what you believed. It only cares what's true.

The page on the toll-post was true enough.

There were six of them, the scouts — red coats gone rust-orange at the shoulders from river damp, horses stamping at the cobbles like they had opinions about Vellsmark and none of them flattering. Two nailed the proclamation up while the other four sat their saddles and watched the crowd with the particular boredom of men who had done this in eleven towns before ours and would do it in eleven after. That's what frightened me most, I think. Not the rifles. The boredom. You can fight a man who hates you. There's nothing to fight in a man who files you under Tuesday.

The paper itself was written in that beautiful, terrible hand the Crown uses for its cruelties — flourishes on the capital letters, a wax seal pressed so hard the ribbon had bitten into the vellum. I read it twice, because the first time my eyes kept sliding off *requisition* like a type set too loose.

*By order of the Crown Army, the town of Vellsmark and its works, mills, presses, wharves, and stores are hereby subject to requisition effective week's end. All property of trade shall be inventoried. All records of trade shall be surrendered to his Majesty's commissioners. Resistance or concealment of materials shall be prosecuted to the full measure of martial law.*

*All records of trade shall be surrendered.*

Every printer in Vellsmark knew what that sentence was for. It wasn't for the mills. It wasn't for the wharves. Armies don't post proclamations over grain sacks unless there's something in the sacks they want more than bread.

I stood in the crowd with my hands in my apron pockets and listened to the town read its own sentence. Old Marta Hessel got as far as *martial law* and made a sound like she'd swallowed a plum stone. The Ferro boys, who run the dye works and owe half their worth to creditors whose names live in guild ledgers, went pale in that specific way of men doing arithmetic. And near the front, a woman I didn't know copied the whole text onto a slate with quick, practiced strokes — which meant either she was a clerk for someone, or someone had already decided this document needed circulating faster than the Crown would like.

The scouts finished nailing and turned their horses around. As the lead rider passed, he looked at us — all of us, the whole silent crowd — the way you'd look at a shelf of goods you'd already priced. Then they clattered back across the bridge toward the garrison road, and Vellsmark exhaled all at once, a long gray breath, and began to talk in the way towns talk when talking is the only weapon left on the table.

I didn't stay to listen. I had a shop to close and an apprentice to fetch home and a partner to alarm properly, and I knew exactly where every alarmed printer in this district would be by nightfall.

* * *

Quill locked the door behind me with both bolts and the wooden bar besides, which was theatrical, since the bar wouldn't stop anything heavier than a determined peddler, but ritual matters to frightened men. It gives the hands something true to do.

Tally sat on the composing stone with her legs swinging, pretending not to be afraid, which is the bravest kind of pretending there is. I put bread in her hand partly for dinner and partly so her fingers had work.

"They posted it at the fish market too," she reported. "And the customs house. A soldier let me hold the end of the hammer while they nailed it." She said this with enormous pride, then caught Quill's expression and amended: "I washed the ink off after."

"Good girl. Never touch their ink without washing. Their ink gets into everything."

"It's just ink, Master Quill."

"No such thing," Quill and I said together, which startled us both a little.

Word came within the hour, as word does in a printing district — we are, after all, the people who move words for a living; messages travel through us the way water travels through a channel. The guild would meet tonight. Not at the guildhall, which any fool could watch, and not at the chandler's cellar where they'd met through the last two crises, which apparently some fool *had* been watching. At our shop. My father's shop. Because Quill had decided — without asking me, which was very like him — that the one place the army would eventually seal was also the one place they hadn't thought to watch yet, and because, as he put it, "the ledger should sleep where it was born."

I didn't argue. I wanted to see their faces when they said it out loud. That's the cutter in me — I trust the pull, not the promise.

They came after dark, in twos and threes, with collars up and lanterns shuttered: nine of them, ten counting Quill, eleven counting me at the doorway where I said I would stand and where nobody had the nerve to make me move. I recognized all of them. Harneth the broadsheet man, who could print a rumor faster than anyone could start one. Widow Sorrel, who ran the smallest press in the district and the sharpest pamphlets. The Brandt cousins, who did commercial work and had never printed a seditious word in their lives and looked furious about being in the same room as people who had. Old Fen, half-deaf, who brought his own stool. And the rest — faces I saw at market, at funerals, at the font-casting auctions, men and women bound together by the peculiar brotherhood of people who know what kerning is.

On the stone table between them, wrapped in oilcloth like a corpse at a wake, lay the guild ledger.

I had seen it before, of course. Everyone had seen it before — it sat in the guild cabinet at the hall like a family Bible, opened at meetings to record dues and debts. But it had never lain on a table in candlelight looking like *this*, like the thing the proclamation had been written about, like the reason six bored men in red coats had crossed our bridge.

Quill unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon.

"You've all read it," he said. "You've all signed it. Some of you have bled into it, if we're honest — the margin on page forty has what I believe is Fen's thumb, from the night the casting furnace took his hand." A low sound went around the table, almost a laugh, the way frightened rooms produce laughter the way wounds produce blood — automatically, and because something has to come out. "Everything the Crown wants to know about the printers of Vellsmark is in this book. Every name. Every debt. Every subscription to every pamphlet that was ever passed off as almanac commentary. Every sympathizer who ever slipped us coin. Every creditor, every debtor, every secret kept in this district for thirty years, because your fathers and mothers thought a ledger was a safe place to keep secrets, and they were right, right up until the week the army decided to come collect them."

"Then we burn it," said Marta Hessel — no, wait, that's wrong, Marta wasn't there, it was Sorrel who said it first, Sorrel with her small press and sharp pamphlets, leaning forward with her knuckles white on the table edge. "Burn it tonight. Here, in the bindery stove, and scatter the ash in the mill race. No book, no evidence. Let them inventory empty shelves."

"And hang anyway," said Brandt the elder. "That's what you're proposing. Hang anyway, just tidier." He jabbed a finger at the ledger. "That book isn't only evidence against us, woman. Half those entries are *debts owed to us*. Records of legitimate trade. If we surrender it, yes, they hang half the district. But if we destroy it, we destroy the only proof of what's owed, what was paid, what was agreed. We become twelve nameless criminals instead of a guild with accounts. And worse—" he dropped his voice, and the room leaned in, and I found I was holding Tally's shoulder harder than I meant to— "worse, we lose the proof of who informed and who didn't. There are names in that book that cut both ways. Some of us may need to prove we were *loyal*."

A silence followed that had weight, like wet snow sliding off a roof.

"Hide it, then," said someone — Harneth. "Bury it. There's the old salt cellar under the tannery—"

"They'll dig," Quill said mildly. "Armies dig. It gives the soldiers something to do between hanging people. They'll take up every floorboard and drain every well, and they'll enjoy it, because a search is the closest thing to entertainment martial law offers."

"So we burn it," Sorrel snapped. "So we hide it. So we surrender it and throw ourselves on whatever mercy the commissioners have, which is none, which we all know—"

"We could divide it," said young Brandt, speaking for the first time. "Copy the dangerous pages and distribute them. Ten houses, ten hiding places. No single seizure kills it."

"And ten times the chances of a copy walking into an officer's quarters in somebody's coat," his cousin shot back.

They went around and around. Burn it. Bury it. Split it. Surrender it and beg. I stood in the doorway and listened to nine experienced, intelligent tradespeople discover in real time that there was no good answer, only flavors of ruin, and I watched Quill sit at the head of the table with his spectacles pushed up into his hair, saying nothing, letting the argument spend itself the way he let arguments spend themselves in the shop — because he believed, and I now know he was right, that a room tells you its truth after it runs out of things to say.

It was nearly midnight when the room finally went quiet, and everyone looked at the ledger, and the ledger looked back at them with its thirty years of ink.

"There's something you should know," Quill said quietly. "Before you decide. Something about how this book was kept."

He turned to the middle pages, to the subscription rolls — the lists of who paid dues, who contributed to funds, who supported the guild's quieter activities. And he showed them what he had shown me only days ago, though I saw now he'd never shown *them*: gaps. Small, systematic absences. Names missing where names ought to be, dates interrupted, columns that ended cleanly where a thorough bookkeeper would have continued.

"For twenty years I wondered why certain families never appeared in these rolls," Quill said. "Families we knew contributed. Families whose sons carried our pamphlets in fish carts. I assumed sloppiness. Then Okafor died, and I inherited the keeping of this book, and I found his system." His voice did something complicated. "Your father"—this to me, across the room, in front of everyone—"kept two sets of omissions. Honest gaps, where the record simply ran dry. And deliberate ones. Whole households, whole streets, erased from the record. People who helped us, protected by not existing in it. If the army takes this book, it takes the map of everything the guild ever did. But understand—" his eyes moved around the table— "if we burn it, we burn the only proof that some of you were never in it at all. Your father's mercy is stitched into these pages. Removing the pages removes the mercy."

Every face at that table turned to me. I hadn't asked them to. I stood in the doorway with my father's shop at my back and his ghost at my shoulder and eleven printers waiting for the daughter of the man who wrote the book to say what must be done with it.

What I remember thinking, absurdly, was about weather. My father used to say dread arrives like weather — you cannot argue it away, you cannot outrun it, you can only decide whether you'll be caught in the open or get under a roof and wait it out with company. The red coats had crossed the bridge that morning the way weather crosses a valley: inevitable, indifferent, already here. And here we all were, under a roof, together, with the thing they wanted sitting in the lamplight like a heart laid out on a table.

"The book doesn't burn," I heard myself say. "Not yet. My father didn't keep those names off those pages so we could feed them to a stove the moment things got hard. Mercy you can't produce later isn't mercy, it's a feeling. The book is proof. Proof is the only currency that outlives us." I came off the doorframe. "But it doesn't hide, either, and it doesn't split, and nobody here is going to bury it under a tannery and pray. If the army wants it, the army will have to be made to chase it. And chasing means moving. Which means someone carries it."

Silence. Fen, half-deaf, had followed along by watching mouths, and now he said, in the too-loud voice of the very old: "Carried where?"

Nobody answered him, because none of us knew — not then. Not yet.

But even as I said it, some part of me was already measuring the ledger with my father's eye. Judging its weight. Its dimensions. Whether it would fit inside a font tray.

Whether it was the kind of thing a person could carry at all.

Quill closed the book softly, and the oilcloth went back over it, and he said, "Meeting adjourned. Bram, walk me to the door."

At the threshold, past the others' departing backs, he gripped my wrist and spoke low, so only I could hear:

"I didn't tell them the worst of it, girl. I didn't tell them why the scouts posted the proclamation *here* first, before any other river town." His eyes went to the dark window, toward the bridge, toward the garrison road beyond. "Because somebody told the Crown exactly what this district keeps in its ledgers. Somebody who knows this guild from the inside. And whoever they are — they haven't finished telling yet."
