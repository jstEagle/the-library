## 4. What Can Be Carried

My father used to say that a typeface cutter's whole life could be weighed on a letterpress scale. He meant it as a joke about the trade — we spend our days shaving slivers of lead and brass no heavier than a moth's wing — but he also meant it as a warning. Everything you own, he'd say, is either a tool or a stone. Tools go in the bag. Stones stay where they lie.

I had spent eleven years in this shop learning which was which, and I still couldn't always tell.

The morning after the guild meeting, I came down before dawn with two empty flour sacks and began the arithmetic of loss. The requisition was coming at week's end. Soldiers do not inventory gently. They pull drawers out and turn them upside down on the floor; they open ledgers to random pages and press their thumbs into the ink like children testing jam. Whatever we wanted to keep — truly keep — had to be gone from sight by the time their boots hit our stairs.

"Tally," I said to the dark loft above the shop floor. "Up. We have work."

A groan, then a thump, then the particular shuffling silence of a twelve-year-old deciding whether obedience was worth the effort of stairs. Tally appeared at the top of the ladder with her hair standing up like a startled hedgehog and one stocking missing, and looked at me with the flat betrayal only apprentices can manage.

"It's not even light out."

"Light is for people who aren't being robbed in four days."

That woke her. She came down fast.

* * *

We started with the obvious: what burns and what doesn't. Paper burns. That sounds like a tragedy until you understand that paper is the cheapest thing in a print shop and the most replaceable. The words survive as long as the matrices survive, and matrices are brass, and brass does not burn. My father taught me that too, though he taught it sideways, in the way he'd tap a matrix case twice before locking it, some old superstition or maybe just affection. Brass remembers. Paper forgets. If you can only save one, save the thing that remembers.

So we burned first. Pamphlet stock, spoiled proofs, three years of draft copy that Master Quill had marked *not fit for eyes, including mine*. Tally fed the stove while I stood over her like a general reviewing troops, and she kept looking up at me with questions she was too proud to ask.

"You're allowed to ask," I said finally.

"What happens if they find nothing? Do they just... leave?"

I considered lying to her. I want to be honest with myself here, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now: I considered it for a good long moment, because Tally is twelve and the truth is heavy and I had been carrying enough heavy things already. But my father never lied to me about the army, even when I was small enough to hide under the composing stone during the last census. He said a child who learns the world is soft will be broken by it later, and a child who learns it is hard early will only be bent, and bent things can be straightened.

"They don't leave," I said. "They take an inventory. They seal the doors. They write down everything we own and put a wax stamp on it, and then the stamp decides what we're allowed to touch." I fed another armful of proofs to the fire. "But they can't stamp what isn't here."

Tally nodded slowly, watching a page curl. "Like hiding sweets from Nan."

"Exactly like that, except Nan only hits you with a spoon."

* * *

By midmorning the shop looked wrong in a way that made my teeth ache. A print shop has a grammar, the same as any room. The type cases go here, the press there, the stone in the middle where all the work passes through. When you start moving pieces, the room stops speaking properly. It starts stammering.

We worked through the morning sorting tools from stones. Tools: my father's cutting knives, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked into the false bottom of the graver chest. The good burnisher. Two punches I'd cut myself, a lowercase g and a capital R, the g because it was the hardest letter I ever mastered and the R because it was the first one Father let me finish alone, and I am sentimental about exactly one thing in this world and it is that R. Stones: the furniture, the spare chases, the second composing stick — replaceable, heavy, dead weight. The great press itself, God help us, was a stone beyond all stones. Eight hundred pounds of iron and oak. You cannot carry a press. You can only hope whoever comes for it understands what it is, which they won't, and I had to make peace with that the way you make peace with weather.

Tally watched me wrap the R punch and asked, quietly, "Is your father coming back for any of this?"

The question went through me like a burin slipping. Three years, and the wound still knew where to find the soft grain.

"No," I said. "He left us everything he needed to leave."

She accepted that the way children accept things — completely, without suspicion, which is either a mercy or a reproach depending on the hour.

* * *

The matrices were the real work.

Under the floorboards behind the stone, my father had built a cavity years ago — I'd found it as a girl chasing a dropped quoin, pried up a plank, and discovered a tin box full of letters and a note in his hand that said simply: *Now you know. Forget it again.* I had forgotten it again, mostly, until this week. Now I knelt on the cold boards with the plank loose beside me and did the mathematics of survival.

A full set of matrices for a text face runs to better than two hundred pieces. Too many to hide in one place, too valuable to split carelessly. But my father hadn't hidden a full set. He'd hidden a *partial* — the common sorts, the workhorse letters, enough to set ordinary honest text if ordinary honest text ever needed setting in exile. And alongside them, wrapped separately in waxed linen, something else I didn't examine closely yet. Something that clinked differently.

I'll be honest: my hands shook. Not from fear, exactly. From the strange sensation of a dead man handing you tools across a gap of three years, saying *you'll need these soon*, and being right. Grief is mostly patience, I've decided. It waits for the moment you're weakest and then hands you something heavy and says, carry this too.

"Bram?" Tally was at my elbow. "What's under there?"

And here I faced the apprentice's dilemma. Every master faces it eventually: how much of the trade's darkness to pass down, and when. My father passed his down early and completely, and it made me precise and lonely in roughly equal measure. But precision, at least, had saved me more than once.

"Come here," I said. "Kneel down. Look."

She knelt. I held up a matrix so the lamplight caught the recessed letter, the little well of brass cut in reverse.

"This is a mold for a letter. Melt lead, pour it against this face, and you get type. This—" I turned it over, "—is the actual alphabet, Tally. Not paper letters. Not ink. The *thing itself*. Anyone can burn a pamphlet. Nobody can burn this."

Her eyes went wide in the way I've come to live for, if I'm honest, the way they go when a difficult idea suddenly becomes simple. "So if we keep these—"

"Then the words can always be printed again. Anywhere. By anyone holding these."

She reached out and touched the matrix with one finger, careful as touching a sleeping animal. Then she looked up at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone recalculating the size of the world.

"Why would your father hide them?"

Because he saw this coming, I almost said. Because printers are always the first ones up against the wall and he knew it and prepared for it the way farmers prepare for winter. Because he loved the trade more than the town and trusted brass more than neighbors.

"Because he was careful," I said instead, which was true, and kinder than the rest of it.

* * *

We spent the afternoon making the shop lie.

This is a skill nobody teaches and every printer learns: how to arrange a room so that a searching officer sees what he expects to see. Soldiers expect disorder in a raided print shop — scattered type, overturned cases, evidence of panicked flight. So we gave them disorder, but curated. I scattered a case of worn nonpareil across the floor, the sorts nobody would miss. I left the cheap pamphlet stock stacked conspicuously by the door, the literary equivalent of leaving coins on the windowsill for burglars. Let them seize paper. Let them fill their report with *seditious materials recovered, forty pounds' weight*, and feel clever about it.

What they wouldn't see: the graver chest with its false bottom. The matrices going under the boards behind the stone. The guild's duplicate subscription lists dissolved in the wash bucket, the water gray with ink, poured out the back onto the cobbles where it dried to a stain that said nothing to anyone.

Tally turned out to be gifted at this. Children are natural liars of arrangement — they spend their lives constructing rooms that say *nothing happened here* while sugar melts in their pockets. She hid the burnisher inside the flour crock without being asked and gave me a look of tremendous professional pride.

"Nan's going to wonder why the bread tastes like leather polish," I said.

"She'll assume it's your cooking."

"I deserve that."

* * *

Near dusk we took our supper on the composing stone, sitting cross-legged among the sorted trays like two sailors picnicking on cargo. Bread, hard cheese, the last of the plum preserve. Tally swung her feet. Outside, the town made its evening noises, but underneath them I could hear the other sound now, the one that had arrived with the proclamation — a hush, a listening quality, the whole district holding its breath between sentences.

"Tally," I said. "If it goes badly — if they seal the shop — where will you go?"

She stopped swinging her feet. "Nan's sister in Marrow Lane. You told me. First week I came."

Good. She remembered. "And if someone tells you to run and you can't find me?"

"Go to Marrow Lane anyway. Wait three days. If you haven't come, Master Quill knows the house."

"Quill might not be able to come for you."

"Then I wait longer." She picked at the crust. "You'll come, though."

It wasn't a question. That's the cruelty of children — they issue statements of faith you can't refuse without breaking something in them, and you can't promise without possibly lying, and so you sit there holding the cheese knife and doing arithmetic again. What can be carried. What must be left.

"I'll come," I said, "or I'll send word by someone I trust. One of those two things will happen. I won't just vanish."

"That's not a yes."

"It's the truest yes I have."

She thought about that with her whole face, the way she does, and then nodded once, satisfied, and stole the last of the preserve, which was the correct outcome of the negotiation.

* * *

After she went up to the loft, I stayed below with the lamp and finished the last job alone.

The plank behind the stone came up quiet — I'd oiled the joints that afternoon, thinking ahead for once in my life. Into the cavity went the partial set, wrapped in its waxed linen. Alongside it, after a long moment, I added the things I'd sorted as tools but understood, kneeling there, were really something else: my father's best graver. The R punch. A folded sheet of his handwriting I'd found tucked in the ledger's back binding, which I hadn't read yet and wasn't sure I wanted to.

Before I lowered the plank, I sat back on my heels and looked at the little dark square of the cavity and felt the full absurdity of it. Here was the sum of the Okafor inheritance: a hole in the floor, a fistful of brass, and a daughter trained to measure everything by whether she could lift it. Other families handed down land. We handed down escape routes.

But then — and I want to record this accurately, because the feeling didn't last and feelings like it rarely do — I felt him near. Not ghost-nonsense. Just the plain fact that every technique I'd used today, every false bottom and curated mess and split cache, was his hand moving through mine. He'd spent twenty years teaching me to carry, and I'd resented the lessons as poverty, and they had been armor all along.

"All right, old man," I said to the empty shop. "I see the shape of it."

The shop said nothing back. Shops are honest that way.

I pressed the plank home, swept the dust until the boards looked innocent, and banked the stove. Upstairs, Tally was already asleep, one arm hanging off the cot, her missing stocking finally located and draped over the bedpost like a flag of some very small nation.

I stood in the doorway a while, watching her breathe, and let myself imagine — briefly, dangerously — that the week would pass quietly. That the soldiers would come, poke through our arranged chaos, write their numbers, stamp their wax, and leave. That the ledger would stay shut and the names inside it would stay dry ink and the gallows would remain what gallows mostly are: furniture for other people's nightmares.

Then I heard boots on the cobbles outside. Several pairs, unhurried, in step — and stopping, not passing.

Through the shutter slats I watched lantern light pool in the street below, and a voice call out the kind of announcement that begins with *by order* and ends with your name on somebody's list.

They weren't waiting for week's end.

The requisition had come early.
