## 5. The Requisition

They gave us until dawn to open the doors ourselves, which I suppose was a courtesy. It is remarkable how much cruelty can be dressed up as procedure if you stamp enough seals on it.

I didn't sleep. Neither did Tally, though she pretended better, curled on the cot in the back room with her back to the wall and her eyes open in that way children have when they think darkness makes them invisible. I let her believe it. There was no kindness I could offer her at three in the morning except the fiction of privacy, so I sat at my father's old bench and ran my thumb along the groove he'd worn into the wood over thirty years of cutting letterforms, and I waited for the knock.

It came as light first touched the river. Not a knock, actually — a rhythm of iron on iron, polite and terrible, like a dinner bell rung by someone who knows you haven't eaten.

"By order of the Crown Army, Vellsmark district is under requisition. Open in the name of the law."

The law had a young voice. That was the worst of it, somehow. In my imagination — and I will confess I had spent the night imagining — the law was heavy, gray, faceless, a thing like weather. But the voice outside my father's shop belonged to somebody's son, probably somebody who missed somebody's cooking, and he said *open in the name of the law* the way you'd say it if you'd rehearsed it and were hoping nobody would make him say the next part.

The next part, for the record, was: "Or we will open it for you."

I opened it.

* * *

There is a particular feeling to watching strangers walk through your father's house with their boots on. I don't know that I have words for it that would print cleanly. It is a violation, yes, but violation implies force, and this wasn't forceful. It was *tidy*. They came in four at a time with a clipboard officer behind them, and they touched everything gently, respectfully, the way you'd handle another man's belongings at his funeral while deciding which of them you'd be keeping.

"That's a composing stone," said one soldier, who clearly knew something about presses, running a finger along the iron bed. "Quality."

"Log it," said the officer.

Everything got logged. That was the phrase of the morning, repeated like a litany: *log it, log it, log it.* The galley trays. The chase. My father's cutting tools, laid out on felt the way he'd left them — left them, I corrected myself, the way he'd *been leaving them these two years since he died*, because grief is a habit and I had kept the shop like a shrine and a workplace both, and now strangers were logging my shrine item by item.

Tally stood beside me at the door with her fists buried in her apron. I put a hand on her shoulder, partly for her comfort and mostly for mine.

"Don't talk," I murmured. "Don't help. Don't correct them even when they're wrong."

"They're wrong about nearly everything," she whispered back.

"I know. Let them be wrong. Wrong things get misfiled."

This is a lesson every printer learns early: an error in the record is a door left ajar. I intended, if the week allowed, to walk through several.

They worked through the front room, then the press hall, then found the storeroom and went quiet in the way men go quiet around anything organized, because organization suggests purpose and purpose suggests conspiracy, and conspiracy was what they'd come for. They turned over jars of ink. They unstoppered the pot of sizing. One of them held up a quire of rag paper to the light as if paper might confess.

Then the officer with the clipboard reached the tenancy papers nailed inside the pantry door — guild custom; the district required them posted where any apprentice could find them in a fire — and stopped.

I watched him read. I watched him read it twice.

And then I watched something happen in his face that I will spend the rest of this chapter failing to describe properly, because it lasted less than a heartbeat and contained, as far as I could tell, surprise, calculation, and something almost like regret. He looked at the paper. Then he looked at me. Then back at the paper, as though confirming the two of us existed in the same document.

"Bram Okafor," he said.

Not a question. A reading. Which meant he already knew the name before the paper told it to him, and knowing a stranger's name in advance is never comfortable news.

"That's me," I said. "The tenant of record. My late father held the lease before me."

"I know who held the lease," he said, and there it was again — that flicker, gone before I could fix its shape. He copied the details onto his board anyway, methodical, and I noticed he wrote more than the form required. His pen lingered. Then he straightened and became, abruptly, entirely an officer: crisp, distant, done with me.

"The premises are sealed as evidence," he announced, to me and to the room. "Nothing leaves. No one enters without writ. You'll receive notice of inventory disposition." He produced strips of army linen paper and a pot of paste, and two soldiers strung cord across the press hall doorway and sealed the seams with paper and wax, and I stood in my own shop like a guest at someone else's funeral, watching them shut the coffin.

Sealed as evidence. As if the shop itself had committed something. As if the typefaces I had cut at midnight with cracked knuckles and my father's knife were accomplices.

They left two sentries at the door. One was the young man with the announcement voice. The other picked his teeth and looked bored, which frightened me more.

* * *

Tally and I walked the town, because there was nothing else to do and standing still felt like drowning slowly. What we saw did not improve our tempers.

Vellsmark had been requisitioned before — twice in my lifetime, once for quartering during the border scare and once, absurdly, for a royal visit that ended up canceled. Both times the army had arrived like guests: loud, hungry, gone within the month. This was different. This army arrived like an owner.

They took the customs house first, then the granary, then the guildhall, and by mid-morning red coats stood at the market gate checking papers against faces. Wagons rolled across the bridge stacked with requisitioned flour, and a clerk at the weighhouse recorded each load in a ledger of his own, and I looked at that ledger the way you look at a rival: *there but for the grace of ink goes everything I know.*

"They're not leaving," Tally said. She has a gift for stating the weather while it's still a cloud on the horizon.

"No," I agreed. "They're not leaving."

At the fountain in Tanner's Square, a proclamation had already been pasted to the stone. I read it twice so I could carry it home word-perfect — a professional habit; text wants to be set, and I am helpless before a well-worded tyranny. It announced the requisition, curfew hours, the registration of all presses, binderies, and "instruments of public printing," and — this line, I savored — the requirement that all guild members present themselves for interview regarding "matters material to the security of the realm."

*Matters material.* Somewhere in some garrison office, a clerk had spent real hours choosing those words, sanding them smooth, testing their weight. Words are tools, my father used to say. Even theirs.

Around the square, the town performed its morning pantomime of normalcy. Fish sellers shouted prices at soldiers who weren't buying. Old Marta Voss swept her step with the ferocity of a woman who wished the broom were broader. And everywhere, in doorways and down alleys, people caught each other's eyes and looked away too quickly, because everyone in Vellsmark was doing arithmetic of the same kind: *who do I know, who knows me, what have I said, to whom, and can it be proven.*

Fear is a solvent. It dissolves towns faster than fire does. By noon I could feel the district's shape changing, neighbor pulling apart from neighbor like wet paper.

"He's watching you," Tally said.

"Who?"

She tipped her chin toward the fountain's far side without looking herself, which shows how fast she learns. A soldier stood there — not on duty, or pretending not to be, hands folded, watching the crowd with the patience of a man counting. Counting us, I thought. Or counting me.

He wore no rank braid I could see from that distance. But he carried himself like the clipboard officer had carried himself, and when I turned fully to look at him, he did not look away. Most people look away. It's a rule of the street as reliable as gravity.

This one didn't. He inclined his head — barely, a degree or two, the acknowledgment you give someone you've met before — and moved off into the crowd.

"You know him?" Tally asked.

"No," I said. And then, because lying to Tally felt like a debt I'd have to repay with interest: "But I think he knows me."

* * *

We went to Master Quill's, because misery seeks company and Quill kept the best company for misery: informed, pessimistic, and stocked with decent brandy.

His shop was three streets up and had also been visited, but only catalogued, not sealed — the army had priorities, and Quill's establishment dealt in bookbinding and small jobbing work rather than the broadsheets and pamphlets that made the guild politically interesting. He met us at the door before we knocked, which meant he'd seen us coming, which meant he'd been watching the street all morning.

"In," he said. "Quickly."

The shutters were closed inside, which in Quill's case was theater — he loved the dark, claimed it improved his judgment — but today it was theater with a purpose. Brandy appeared. Tally got watered wine and the good chair, which told me how serious Quill considered matters: he never gives anyone the good chair.

"Well," he said, settling across from me. "You've met them."

"This morning. They sealed the shop. Everything logged."

"Inventory first, arrest later. It's the proper order." He said it without drama, the way a physician says *the arm will have to come off.* "You want to know how I know? Because I've read the histories, girl, and because I'm old. Requisition is never about supplies. Supplies are the excuse. Requisition is about records. Whoever controls what a town has written down controls what the town is permitted to have happened. They'll take our ledgers, our registers, our correspondence, and they'll sit in a warm room for six months reading us into whatever shape suits the Crown. And we will have provided the paper ourselves, because printers cannot stop writing things down. It's the whole disease of the trade."

"You're cheerful today."

"I'm precise today. Cheerfulness is for people with worse information." He swirled his brandy. "They posted the interview order?"

"All guild members. Matters material to the security of the realm."

"Mm. And you'll go?"

"Am I given a choice?"

"There's always a choice. Sometimes the choices are just arranged badly." He studied me over the rim of his glass, and I realized he was deciding something, weighing whether to tell me a thing. I waited. Waiting is half of any negotiation and most of any friendship.

"There was an officer at your sealing," he said finally. "In charge."

"Yes. Clipboard, gray eyes, wrote down more than the form asked for."

"Describe him."

"A her. And she wasn't in charge of the sealing so much as presiding over it. The soldiers answered to a sergeant, and she walked the rooms like she was taking attendance." I hesitated, then gave him the part I'd been carrying around all day like a stone in my shoe. "She knew my name before she read it off the tenancy papers."

Quill set down his glass. It made a very small sound against the table, and somehow that sound was louder than the army's boots had been.

"Knew it," he repeated. "Or recognized it?"

"I don't know the difference yet."

"The difference," Quill said, "is the entire question." He rose and went to the window and peered through the shutter slats at nothing in particular, an old man's habit of pacing his anxiety. "Bram. Listen to me. An officer who knows your name is either a danger to you or a use to you, and you will not find out which by being clever. You find out by being careful, and by letting her spend the next move. Do you understand? Whatever she is — threat, tool, accident — she showed her hand this morning, however slightly. Don't force her to show more. Don't seek her out."

"I wasn't going to seek her out."

"You were going to seek her out the moment you finished that brandy. I know your face. Your father made the same one whenever someone mentioned a locked drawer."

I drank the brandy instead of answering, because he was right and I resented it professionally.

Because here is the truth I hadn't said aloud, even to myself, all day: when that woman had looked up from the tenancy papers and said my name — *Bram Okafor*, in a voice like a key trying a lock — something in my chest had turned over. Not fear exactly. Recognition meeting recognition. She had known me, or of me, and I had known nothing of her at all, and asymmetry like that is intolerable to a person whose entire craft depends on matching every piece precisely, kerning life so nothing sits crooked.

Someone walking around with knowledge of me that I didn't hold was an error in the record. And I had promised Tally, silently, that wrong things get misfiled — but she hadn't misfiled me. She had *found* me, deliberately, in a stack of papers, in a sealed shop, in a conquered town.

That evening a boy in army livery — not a soldier, a runner — knocked at Quill's door with a folded summons, and left without waiting for a reply.

It was addressed to *Mistress Bram Okafor, printer, of Okafor & Son* — my father's firm named, my sex noted, both accurate, neither comforting — and it requested my attendance at the garrison headquarters tomorrow at the tenth hour, for interview regarding matters material to the security of the realm.

Ordinary enough. Identical, Quill said, to the ones delivered to four other guild members that afternoon.

Except for the signature at the bottom, which was written in a hand I recognized, because I had watched it for a full minute that morning, copying names onto an inventory list with more care than the form demanded.

*Captain Ilse Verren.*

Quill read it upside down as I held it, and his eyebrows climbed, and he poured himself another brandy with a slightly less steady hand than before.

"Well," he said. "So much for letting her spend the next move."

* * *

Tally slept in the back room of Quill's that night, on the good chaise, under a quilt older than both of us. I sat up in the binding room surrounded by the smell of glue and leather, turning the summons over and over, reading the signature until the letters stopped meaning anything and then started again.

Somewhere across the dark town, in a garrison built to hold a hundred men and currently holding many more, a woman who knew my name was sleeping — or not sleeping — with my family's history somewhere in her head. And I could not decide, and would not decide until I stood in front of her, whether that history made me safer or marked me.

My father taught me that every font has a hidden counterweight — that the black of a letter only reads because of the white around it, and that a cutter who forgets the white cuts garbage. Fine. Then let me cut the white space around this woman and see what shape emerged.

At the tenth hour tomorrow, I would find out what Captain Ilse Verren saw when she looked at my name.

What unsettled me most, as the candle burned down, was that some small traitorous part of me already hoped she saw something worth sparing.
