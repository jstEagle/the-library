## 6. The Officer Who Knew Her

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy so young his uniform hadn't yet learned to fit him. He wouldn't come past the threshold of the shop — sealed shops apparently radiate some contagion that even the king's men fear — so he stood on the step and read from a folded paper in a voice that cracked twice on Captain's name.

*Okafor, B. Garrison headquarters. Ninth bell. By order.*

By order. I'd have gone anyway, which is the thing about orders: they only work because you were going to do it regardless. My father used to say that about the guild's rules, and he'd say it with the particular smile of a man who kept two sets of books, though I didn't know that yet, then.

I dressed carefully. Not finely — there is no fine left in a requisitioned wardrobe — but carefully, the way you set type for a document you expect to be read closely by hostile eyes. Dark skirt, no dye marks. Mother's old shawl, which has the advantage of looking poor and being warm. Hair pinned with the plainest pin I owned. No knife; they'd take it and its absence would tell them nothing, whereas my reaching for it and finding nothing would tell them everything about how rattled I was.

Tally watched me from the stairs, still in her nightshirt, arms wrapped around her knees like she was trying to become smaller than an apprentice.

"They can't arrest me for answering a summons," I said.

"That's not what your face says."

"You've been spending too much time with the proofreaders." I tucked my father's ledger-sense deep under my ribs where it lived now, a second heartbeat made of names and debts. "Mind the shop."

"It's sealed."

"Mind it anyway. It's had nobody minding it since Da died, and look what happened."

She almost smiled. That was worth more than any coin in Vellsmark that morning.

* * *

Garrison headquarters had been the wool merchants' hall before the army needed it more, and the army had improved it the way armies improve everything: by hanging flags over the good carvings and stationing bored boys at every door. The smell was wrong for the room — wet wool and ink and lamp oil where there should have been lanolin and pipe smoke and the particular sweetness of merchants who'd made money that day. Ink, though. That part felt almost like home, and I hated it for that.

A corporal took my name, pronounced it wrong — Oka-*for*, as if it were something to be gotten over rather than someone's father — and left me standing on a floor that still bore the ghost-lines of where the trading tables had been. I counted the flagstones to keep my hands busy. Forty-one to the far wall. My father taught me that trick: when fear comes, give it arithmetic. Fear is terrible at sums.

At the ninth stroke of the bell — punctual, at least; I could respect an institution that respected time — a side door opened and Captain Ilse Verren came through it alone.

She was smaller than I remembered, which is a stupid thing to think, because I had been eight years old at the famine winter and everyone is larger than you are at eight. But there it was. Gray at her temples now, cut short in a way that said *regulation* but worn in a way that said *convenience*. The same level gray eyes. She'd been the sort of officer who carried her own kit, I remembered that much — my mother had remarked on it, that winter, with the tone women use for things that surprise them about soldiers.

"Okafor," she said, and pronounced it correctly, all three syllables, the way my father had drilled into every lodger, customer, and passing stranger who'd ever asked. "Sit down."

There was one chair, on the near side of a table that held nothing but a lamp and a single sheet of paper turned face-down. Officers' furniture. She remained standing, which meant either that she intended this to be brief or that she wanted me off balance. Possibly both. I sat and put my hands in my lap like a girl in church.

"You're wondering why you're here."

"I assumed it was about the tenancy papers."

"It was about the tenancy papers." She turned the face-down sheet over, but didn't push it toward me. From where I sat I could see it was an inventory list, columns in a clerk's hand, and somewhere on it a line item marked with a small red tick. "And it isn't anymore. It stopped being about papers the moment I read the name on them."

"Names are like that," I said. "Very disruptive."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of a smile, fossilized somewhere around the rank of lieutenant. "You have your father's tongue."

"You knew my father?"

"I lodged with him." She said it flatly, the way you state facts you've decided not to decorate. "The winter of the grain failure. Six weeks. The Okafors took me in when the garrison rations failed and the town would sooner have fed its horses than a soldier."

I kept my face still, but inside, everything shifted like type spilled from a broken stick — all the letters suddenly in the wrong compartments, and every word I'd planned to say now spelling something else entirely.

The famine winter. I remembered pieces of it the way you remember a fever: the porridge stretched with sawdust-fine meal, my mother's hands, the extra cot by the press that smelled of bootblack, a soldier who stood in the doorway of the shop some nights as if she couldn't decide whether she was guarding us or apologizing to us. My mother had cut that soldier's hair once, with the good scissors, and given her bread with honey in it — real honey, from the jar we didn't open — and told her, in the voice mothers use for lessons they mean to outlive them, *You eat when you're here. Whatever they owe you, you don't eat it here.*

"The army let its own starve that winter," Verren said. She was watching me watch her, and I couldn't tell which of us was taking inventory. "Garrison rations were skimmed before they ever reached the mess. Skimmed by quartermasters. Skimmed by officers. I was twenty-three and hungry enough to be honest about it, and your family fed me for six weeks and charged me nothing, and your mother told me I could pay the debt forward instead of back, and I have spent eleven years failing to find anyone worth paying it to." She paused. "Until the paperwork crossed my desk with your name on it."

Gratitude rose in me, hot and immediate and unwelcome, because I did not want to be grateful to the woman whose soldiers had chalked *EVIDENCE* across my father's door. Gratitude is a debt, and debts are leverage, and I had learned enough about ledgers in the past fortnight to distrust any account I hadn't opened myself.

"That's very moving, Captain," I said. "What do you want?"

Now the mouth-corner moved again, further this time. Almost arrived. "Your father would have phrased it more politely."

"My father was polite to people who weren't sealing his shop."

"He'd have done worse than seal it, if he'd seen what I've seen." She set both hands flat on the table, and just like that the almost-smile was gone and something older and wearier stood in its place. "So listen to me, Okafor, because I'm going to say this once and then pretend I never said it. There is an investigation underway into the printers' conspiracy. It is being conducted above my head, by men who arrived with the requisition and will leave with whatever they came for. The evidence inventory is theirs, not mine. I command the occupation, such as it is; I do not command the case."

"And the case needs the guild ledger."

Her eyes flickered — the smallest hitch, a typesetter's slip, one letter out of place. Recovered in half a breath. "Everyone knows the ledger matters. Half the district could recite its contents from memory."

"Then why did your clerk tick a line on that inventory when I mentioned it?"

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us a soldier's boots crossed the ceiling in slow, bored strides, four steps and turn, four steps and turn.

"You were always quick," Verren said quietly. "That winter, you were seven and you corrected my grammar twice and stole my bootlaces once, and when I caught you, you gave them back and told me you were testing whether I checked my kit."

"I don't remember stealing anything."

"I remember everything," she said, and for a moment the flatness broke and I saw underneath it: a woman standing in a print-shop doorway with honey-bread in her hand, trying to memorize a kindness because she suspected the world wouldn't offer another. Then the shutter came down, officer again. "Here is what I know. The ledger vanished from a sealed evidence room two nights ago. Whoever holds it holds every printer, debtor, and sympathizer in Vellsmark by the throat. The men conducting this investigation intend to hold trials. Trials require confessions, and confessions can be *encouraged* by what a ledger says about a man's debts and associates. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the ledger is a weapon."

"I'm telling you it's a weapon pointed at your whole street, and that if you go looking for it — if you interfere, if you carry messages, if you so much as sharpen a quill near this investigation — I will not be able to protect you. And I want to be clear about what I mean by protect." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something meant for no one but me. "Because the men above me have already drawn up lists. Warrant drafts. And I have seen at least one name on those drafts that I fed porridge to when she was small enough to stand on a stool to reach the composing stick."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a page tilts when you realize the error too late to stop the press.

She'd seen my name. On a warrant draft. And she'd summoned me at dawn, alone, without witnesses, to tell me so — which was either mercy or the most elegant interrogation I'd ever heard of, and I genuinely could not tell which, and neither, I suspect, could she.

"Why?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Why warn me? You owe my family nothing. My mother told you to pay it forward, not back. A dead printer's daughter isn't forward, Captain. She's backward."

For a long moment she looked at me, and I watched her decide something — watched the decision move through her face like a line of type being composed, letter by letter, each one set with care because errors cost.

"Eleven years ago I swore an oath to the crown," she said. "It was easy. I was hungry and the uniform was food. Since then I've collected taxes, put down two riots, and hanged a smuggler whose only crime was moving grain past a tariff. I've told myself each of those was lawful, and lawfulness is the whole of justice, and if I repeat it often enough I might believe it." She straightened. "Your mother told me I'd know the day the difference mattered. I thought she was being sentimental. Mothers are allowed to be sentimental." A pause. "This morning I am not at all sure she was wrong, and I dislike her intensely for it."

"That sounds like a confession, Captain."

"It sounds like nothing. It will sound like nothing tomorrow, when the harbor searches begin and the market gate closes and you find yourself unable to buy flour without showing papers. Hear me, Bram Okafor." My name, unabbreviated, given back to me whole. "Stay away from the investigation. Stay away from the evidence inventories, the records rooms, the garrison clerks, the quay after dark. Grieve your father. Rebuild your shop when this is over, if there's anything left to rebuild. Let the law take its course."

"The law is going to hang eleven people on the strength of a book that walked out of a locked room."

"Yes," she said simply. "It may. That is what I'm asking you to let happen. And I am asking because I have never asked anything of anyone in fifteen years of service, and because your mother's honey is still the best thing I have ever tasted, and because I would rather disappoint the dead than bury the living." She picked up the inventory list, folded it, and put it in her breast pocket. "Go home, Okafor. Mind your apprentice. Touch nothing."

I stood. My legs were steady; I made a point of it, walking to the door with the unhurried tread of a woman with nothing in her pockets and less in her heart, and I had nearly reached it when she spoke again.

"One more thing."

I turned. She was standing exactly where I'd left her, hands flat on the table, and the lamplight caught her face from below so that for a moment she looked like the woman from the famine winter, the one who stood in doorways deciding things.

"The ledger," she said, "was never in the evidence room when it disappeared."

"What?"

"The seals on the door were intact when the loss was reported. Intact, and applied *after* the shelf was empty. Which means whoever took it had keys before the room was ever sealed, and access before the inventory began, and either the authority to rewrite the record afterward—" she paused, precisely, like a cutter finishing a serif— "or the rank to make questions about it disappear. So when I tell you not to interfere, understand that I am not protecting the investigation from you. I am protecting you from an investigation that is itself the crime."

The floor seemed very far away for a moment. Forty-one flagstones, and every one of them shifting.

"Then who—" I started.

"Go home," said Captain Ilse Verren, and looked down at the table, and became a closed book.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the river path, because the river doesn't report to anyone.

Fog sat on the water in gray ropes, and the bridge stood up out of it with soldiers on it like flies on a carcass, and the morning tide was pulling at the moored boats with that patient, indifferent strength water has — the strength of a thing that always leaves eventually, whatever you tie to it.

Gratitude and suspicion had spent the whole walk arguing in my chest, and neither had won, and I finally understood why: they were the same animal. She had sheltered under my father's roof and owed him bread; now she held my name in her pocket and called it protection. Every kind thing she'd said was also true. Every kind thing she'd said was also the perfect bait for a printer's daughter known to be quick, known to test whether people check their kit. If I went looking for the ledger — if I interfered, carried messages, sharpened quills — she had warned me, witnessed by no one, and whatever happened after would be my own stubborn fault.

Or she had torn a strip off her own career this morning, alone in a cold room, to hand a dead friend's daughter a map of the minefield.

I stood at the rail of the empty fish pier and worked it the way Da taught me to work a difficult line of type: don't force it, turn it over, look at the counter-space, the white around the letters. What does the shape of the silence say?

She had told me the theft predated the seals. That fact served her in no way unless I acted on it. An interrogator baits the trap and waits. But a trap wants you to blunder in blind — and she'd handed me a lantern on the way to the door.

Unless the lantern was the trap. Unless knowing was the bait.

The tide turned below me, the river shivering, changing its mind about which way it flowed. Everything in Vellsmark was doing that lately.

One thing I knew with a type-cutter's certainty, the moment I let myself see it: I could not stay away from the investigation. She'd known that too, or she'd have lied better. So the question wasn't whether to interfere. The question was whether Captain Ilse Verren was the wall between me and whoever held the ledger —

— or the person holding it.

Behind me, up the hill, the garrison bell rang the hour, and I realized with a cold drop of the stomach that I wasn't the only one counting. Tally had seen something two nights ago, something she'd been trying to tell me about between chores, something about a clerk and a wrapped bundle and the garrison steps — and I'd patted her head and told her apprentices shouldn't gossip.

I turned up the lane toward the shop, fast now, my breath making small ghosts in the fog.
