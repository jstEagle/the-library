## 7. The Ledger Vanishes

The news reached me the way bad news always does in Vellsmark: sideways, secondhand, and before breakfast.

I was at the shop — *my* shop now, though the army's wax seal across the door argued the point — chipping dried ink from the rollers with a bone spatula, because a sealed print shop still gathers dust and dust still settles into type metal, and if I was ever going to reopen I would not do it over a bed of grit. Tally was supposed to be sweeping. Tally was instead hanging out the window like laundry, watching the soldiers on the bridge, which is how she caught it.

"Bram," she said, in the voice she used for fires and stray dogs. "Bram, they're running."

Nobody runs in Vellsmark before eight o'clock. The town has a dignity about its mornings; even the gulls take their time. But down the hill from the garrison came a courier at a dead sprint, boots hammering the cobbles, and behind him two more, and then the bell above the market began to ring in that short, chopped rhythm that means *not fire, not flood — trouble of the two-legged kind.*

I set down the spatula. Some instincts you don't argue with. My father taught me that when a press starts making a new sound, you stop the press first and diagnose second. Trouble works the same way. Stop everything. Listen.

"They've taken the evidence room," Tally reported a quarter hour later, breathless, having extracted the whole story from the fishwife network, which moves faster than any army courier and charges considerably less. "The ledger's gone. Out of a locked room, out of a sealed building, with sentries on the door all night." Her eyes were enormous. "Bram, they're saying we took it."

"*We* being—"

"Printers. The guild. All of us." She swallowed. "They arrested Old Hobbes already. He was just buying bread."

I stood very still in the middle of my father's shop, among his presses and his type and his forty years of careful, invisible work, and I thought: *of course.* Of course the ledger vanished. Of course the army blamed the printers. And of course, underneath all of it, one cold clear thought rising like mercury:

*Whoever holds that book holds every name in Vellsmark by the throat.*

Including mine.

* * *

You should understand what the ledger was, because I did not fully understand it myself until that morning, and I had been cutting type beside it half my life.

It looked like nothing. A fat volume bound in scuffed calfskin, corners worn round, spine cracked in three places. Master Quill kept it in the guild office under a stack of tax papers so nobody would bother stealing it, which tells you something about how well secrets are dressed in this world. Inside were the accounts of every printing house in the district — debts, credits, wages, fines — and alongside them, in margins and between lines, the other accounting. Who had paid for pamphlets. Who had sheltered fugitive typesetters after the Cordon riots. Which merchants quietly moved forbidden texts upriver in flour sacks. Which officers, over the years, had accepted a little consideration to inspect those flour sacks carelessly.

My father's hand ran through most of it. Neat, upright, economical — the same hand he used for jobbing bills and my birthday letters. He'd been guild secretary for nineteen years. Nineteen years of writing down what everyone knew and no one said.

And now it was loose in a town full of soldiers who wanted eleven printers' necks for starters.

* * *

Quill came to me through the back lanes at midmorning, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the weather and a terror too heavy for the coat. He knocked our old smuggler's pattern on the rear door — two, pause, three — and slipped inside smelling of river mud and cheap gin, which meant he'd waded the mill race rather than cross any bridge, which meant he was frightened past the reach of dignity.

"I didn't take it." He said it before he'd finished coming through the door. "Bram. Look at me. I did *not* take it."

I looked at him. Seventy years old, hands stained permanently brown, a man whose entire strategy for staying alive had always been to be too unimportant to notice. He had not taken it. Taking it would require nerve, and Quill had survived this long specifically by having none.

"I know," I said. "Sit down before you fall down."

He sat on the type cabinet, which my father would have risen from his grave to prevent, and put his head in his hands. "They'll hang the guild for it anyway. That's the shape of it, isn't it? The book disappears, they blame the men who wrote it, and every search, every arrest, every sweating interrogation from now on is justified by *recovering stolen evidence.*" He laughed, a horrible dry sound. "It's the perfect theft. Whoever has it can commit any crime they like and call it policing."

"Who could get into the evidence room?" I asked. "Truly. Not theoretically."

Quill raised his head. He was, whatever else, a careful man, and I watched him actually consider it — count locks, count guards, count hours. "The sentries saw nothing, or say they saw nothing. The lock wasn't broken. The seals were cut clean and left on the desk, laid flat, deliberate — like a signature. That's not a printer's burglary, Bram. Printers are not subtle, God knows, we ink everything we touch. That's someone with a key and permission."

"An officer."

"Someone with an officer's access, or someone wearing an officer's face." He wrung his hands. "And here's the arithmetic that keeps me from sleeping: the army wanted that ledger as evidence against us. Now it's gone, and they can use its absence even better. Every printer in Vellsmark is suddenly guilty of conspiracy *and* theft, and the real holder of that book can trade names for confessions until he's got the whole district testifying against itself."

I turned away so he wouldn't see my face do what it was going to do. Because there it was, the thought I'd been keeping in the cellar since morning, and Quill had just carried it upstairs for me: whoever held the ledger didn't need trials. They had leverage on every family in the district. Debts forgiven or called in. Names whispered or withheld. Sympathizers exposed or protected. One book, and an occupying army could turn neighbor against neighbor without firing a shot.

And somewhere in that book, in my father's neat upright hand, was the record of exactly which officers had been paid to look away during the famine winter — including, if my memory of certain margin entries served, a young lieutenant named Verren.

* * *

By afternoon the proclamations were up. Fresh-printed — army press, leaden work, the kerning so bad it was almost a personal insult — announcing that evidence of the printers' criminal conspiracy had been *removed by persons unknown acting in concert with said conspiracy,* that the district was therefore subject to expanded search powers, and that any person with information should present themselves to the garrison, where *protection and consideration* would be extended to informants.

*Protection and consideration.* Four words doing the work of a hundred arrests.

Tally read it twice, lips moving. "They printed that lie themselves," she said. "On their own press."

"Armies always carry a press," I said. "How else would they tell you what to think by dinnertime?" But my heart wasn't in the joke. I was looking at the word *consideration,* and thinking about my father's margins, and about how a book full of names becomes a weapon precisely because names are the only currency everyone is forced to carry.

The guild scattered through the evening like embers on a wind. Hobbes sat in the garrison cells for the crime of buying bread while suspiciously elderly. The Fenn brothers loaded their press onto a barge at midnight and went who knows where. Old Marta Kessel presented herself at the garrison voluntarily, and came back two hours later walking strangely straight, and wouldn't speak to anyone, and I understood — with a sickness in my stomach — that *consideration* had already found its first customer.

That's how fast it happens. That's the genius of holding the book. You don't need to torture anyone. You only need to let them know the book exists, and that you have read it, and that mercy is available. Marta Kessel had hidden fugitives in her cellar for six years. It took the army two hours to turn her.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the back room with a single candle and my father's sample books spread around me, not reading anything, just being near his hand the way you sit near a warm stove. And somewhere past two in the morning, the panic — which had been sitting on my chest all day like a cat that decides where it lives — finally shifted, stretched, and became something else.

Here is the thing about panic. It's loud, but it's stupid. It says *run, hide, it's too big.* And I'd spent my whole life measuring things — widths of stems, depths of counters, spaces between letters smaller than a hair. Precision was the only inheritance my father left me that couldn't be seized, and precision says: *look again. Measure properly.*

So I measured.

The ledger was taken by someone with keys, skill, and purpose. Not the guild — we were the intended accused. Not some common thief — a thief sells a book back or burns it for spite; this theft was surgical and served a design. Someone who benefits when the printers hang. Someone who benefits more when the ledger never surfaces at all, because its contents implicate *them.*

My father's margins. The famine payments. The officers who looked away.

If I were an officer with famine money in my past, and a war's worth of political weather coming, and a book in an evidence room that recorded every coin of it — what would I do? I'd steal it myself. I'd blame the very people it condemns. I'd let the machinery of military justice grind them fine, and I'd keep the book alive in the dark, not destroyed, because a book like that is worth more whole than burned. Leverage doesn't rot. Leverage compounds.

The candle guttered. I trimmed the wick with my father's knife — his type-cutter, small and wickedly sharp, the tool he'd held more often than my hand in the last years — and I felt the panic finish its transformation into resolve, the way wet clay goes into the kiln soft and comes out able to hold water.

Whoever had the ledger, I had something they didn't account for. I had grown up inside that book's handwriting. I knew which debts were real and which were coded, which initials meant which merchant houses, which dates lined up with which shipments. If the ledger surfaced anywhere in this district — in a warrant, in an interrogation, in a prosecutor's file — I might recognize the page it was copied from. And whoever held it would eventually have to *use* it, because leverage unused is just anxiety with a calfskin cover.

They would make a mistake. Careful people always do, eventually, because careful people mistake caution for safety.

Until then, I needed to find it first.

* * *

Quill was still asleep against the type cabinet when the light came up grey over the rooftops, looking ten years older and twenty pounds lighter. I made tea. We drank it in silence, two conspirators in a sealed shop, and it occurred to me that this was the most honest company I'd kept since my father died.

"They'll come for you next," Quill said at last. "You're the secretary's daughter. Your family name is all through that book."

"I know."

"You should run, girl. Upstream, tonight, while the searches are busy in the lower streets. There are cellars. There are boats."

"There are boats," I agreed, and filed that away in the growing ledger behind my eyes — because if there was one currency I had that the army didn't, it was that my father had done favors for half the underworld of this river, quietly, in kind, in print, and had never once written *those* debts in the guild book. He'd written them somewhere else. Somewhere I hadn't found yet.

"Bram." Quill set down his cup. His hands were shaking, but his eyes, for the first time since the arrests began, were steady. "Whoever has it — you understand what they'll do. They won't hang everyone. They'll hang a few, and hold the rest over the survivors forever. A district on its knees renews its ransom yearly. That book isn't evidence anymore. It's a lease on all our lives, and somebody means to be landlord."

"Yes," I said. "Which is why I'm not running."

"Why? Because your name is in it?"

I looked at him a long moment. "Because everyone's name is in it. And because my father spent nineteen years writing the truth in the margins where it couldn't be prosecuted, and if that book dies in some officer's strongbox, then the only version of events left standing is the army's." I stood, and took down my coat, and felt — absurdly, terrifyingly — steady. "Truth needs a carrier, Master Quill. It always has. That's the whole trade, if you think about it. Somebody cuts the letters, somebody carries the pages, somebody stands in the square and reads them out loud knowing exactly what it costs."

Quill stared at me. "You sound like your father."

"He used to say something like it." I buttoned the coat. "He said: measure the world in what can be carried. I used to think he meant type cases."

A knock at the front of the shop — the official, unsmuggler-like knock of a man with a warrant and no imagination — made us both freeze. Through the dusty window glass I could see the blue of an army uniform, and behind the uniform, moving with unhurried purpose along the sealed storefront, a clerk's narrow silhouette I was beginning to know too well.

Doss. The requisition clerk. And he was not looking at the shop.

He was looking at me, through the glass, with the small satisfied smile of a man who has just confirmed that a certain name appears on a certain list — and who wants very much for the named party to know it.

"Back door," I breathed to Quill. "Now."

Because I had measured the situation all night, and here was the measurement confirmed: whoever held the ledger had decided I mattered. And in Vellsmark, that week, there were only two things a person could be told they mattered for — and one of them was the gallows.
