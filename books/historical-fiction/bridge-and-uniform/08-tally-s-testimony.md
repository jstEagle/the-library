## 8. Tally's Testimony

Tally came in through the back door the way I'd taught her — twice around the rain barrel, wipe your boots, don't touch anything with ink on it — and she was pale in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

I knew that paleness. It's the color a person goes when they've seen something they weren't supposed to and their body hasn't decided yet whether to be sick or proud. I'd worn it myself at eleven, the day I found my father crying over a font of missing capitals, and I recognized it now the way you recognize a bad smell from a room away.

"Sit down," I said, and put the quill knife out of reach, which is what my father used to do when he expected bad news. Old habits are just grief wearing work clothes.

"I didn't do anything," Tally said.

"Nobody thinks you did. Sit."

She sat on the good stool, the one without a wobble, which I'd been saving for customers I liked, and she wrapped her hands around each other like she was keeping them from running off without her.

"You know Clerk Doss," she said. "The one with the grey coat and the voice like wet paper."

"I know him."

"He was at the cooper's yard last night. Past curfew." She swallowed. "He had a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Big as a hymnal, but heavier — he carried it like it was heavy. And he went through the garrison's side gate, and there was an officer waiting for him with a lamp, and I saw — Bram, I saw the corner of it when the wind moved the cloth."

"Green leather?"

"Green leather. With the guild stamp pressed into it, faded, but there."

The ledger.

Here is a thing about hope: it is patient and stupid and will wait by the door long after the visitor has left. All night I'd been telling myself a comfortable story — that whoever took the ledger from the evidence room had taken it to destroy it. That some soldier with a conscience or some clerk with a grudge had fed it to a stove page by page, and that was terrible, yes, but it was a *clean* terrible. Names gone to ash. Debts forgiven by fire. The dead can't testify against you if the book of them is smoke.

But Doss hadn't burned it. Doss had carried it through the dark like a man carrying money.

"Tally." I kept my voice level, because children learn fear faster than letters. "Where were you? Past curfew?"

"The drain behind the garrison wall. You told me to watch who comes and goes." Her chin came up. "You told me, so don't shout at me for doing it."

I hadn't told her exactly that. I'd told her to keep her eyes open, which is the kind of instruction a clever child interprets broadly, and Tally is the sort of child who reads margins. My father would have called that initiative. My father is not here to call it anything.

"You did well," I said. "And next time you watch from farther off, and if anyone sees you, you run home and you don't look back, and you let them think whatever they like about a girl out past curfew. Do you understand? A girl out past curfew gets scolded. A girl seen watching soldiers gets remembered."

She nodded, and I could see her filing the sentence away somewhere permanent, the way children do, building the person they'll be out of whatever scraps we drop.

* * *

I spent the morning pretending to work, which is a skill I have practiced until it looks identical to working. Pull the lever, check the impression, curse the roller, sweep the dust. Behind all of it, my mind turned the ledger over and over like a stone in a streambed.

Because here's what it meant, and once I saw it I couldn't unsee it: the ledger wasn't evidence anymore. Evidence gets destroyed or produced. This was something else. This was *inventory*. Eleven hundred names in Vellsmark, every printer and paper-seller and debtor and sympathizer, every man who ever bought a broadside critical of the Crown, every woman who ever sheltered a journeyman with radical pamphlets in his kit — all of it written in my father's careful hand, and my father's hand was famous enough that no court would question it.

Names traded for confessions. That was the arithmetic now. Confess to the conspiracy, name your fellows, and perhaps the clerk with the grey coat would find your page mysteriously lighter than the others. Refuse, and your whole account stood against you, itemized, in a dead man's beautiful script.

The army hadn't lost the ledger. The army had *priced* it.

I thought about Master Quill, who swore before the guild that his hands were clean and his conscience was clean and the theft was nothing to do with him. I thought about the way he wouldn't meet my eyes when he said it, which I had filed under *guilt* but which might, I realized now, have been filed under *fear*. There are many kinds of a man not looking at you.

* * *

In the afternoon I went out for flour, because a laundress's daughter buying flour is the most boring event in any town, and boring is a costume I've learned to wear well. The requisition had made flour complicated — army stores first, civilian sacks second, prices third and rising like a fever — but the queue outside Miller Hane's was long and slow and, more importantly, full of people who talked.

That's where I saw the posting.

It was nailed to the granary wall, half-covered by an older notice about curfew hours, and at first glance it looked like the usual bureaucratic wallpaper — a warrant draft, the kind they post before trials so the town can enjoy its own doom in advance. I've read a hundred of these. They're set in standard army type, no imagination, cramped leading, the sort of layout a compositor should be ashamed of. I read them the way I read everything, professionally, left to right, top to bottom, and I was halfway down the list of names before my eye stopped moving.

OKAFOR, B.

Not Okafor, J., for my father Josias, dead two years and past hanging. Not even Okafor, B., tenant, as though I were some other Bram. Just my name, alone, in the accusative case of official type, sitting between a candle-maker I barely knew and old Fenwick who mends nets and has never in his life printed so much as a prayer card.

I stood there with my flour money in my fist and felt the street tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when you shift your weight.

They say your own name shouldn't surprise you. You've heard it every day of your life. But read it in that context — *whereas the persons hereinafter named stand charged* — and it becomes a different word entirely. It stops being yours. It becomes the state's. They take the sound your mother used to call across the shop and they press it into cold metal and they make it mean *condemned*, and there is nothing you can do about the minting.

Bram, I told myself, you are standing in a bread line staring at your own death warrant like an illiterate. Walk.

So I walked. Bought the flour. Made change. Thanked the boy. Did not run, because running is a confession the legs make before the mouth does. And the whole way home I held the sack against my chest and thought, with a clarity so cold it burned: they aren't going to try me for anything I did. They're going to hang me for a list of names I can't even read, in a ledger locked in a clerk's drawer, and the only person alive who knows exactly what that book says about me is a dead man who loved me too much to write it plainly.

Or so I'd believed, before Quill.

* * *

I found Quill at the chandlery where he'd been hiding among the tallow and rope, a big man trying to be furniture. He started when he saw me, which I pretended not to notice, because noticing is sometimes cruelty.

"They posted the draft," I said.

"I know."

"My name's on it."

"I know that too." He turned his hat in his hands. He has hands like hams and he handles his hat like an egg. "Bram—"

"Doss has it. The ledger. He carried it into the garrison last night, wrapped in oilcloth. It's not destroyed, Quill. It's being *used*. Every name in it is currency now, and mine is worth a hanging, and I want to know how a book sealed under army lock walked out of a guarded room and into a clerk's pocket while eleven honest printers sat in their shops wondering who to be afraid of."

"Bram."

"*Who, Quill.*"

Silence. Somewhere outside, a soldier laughed at something; the laugh of men with power is always louder than it needs to be. Quill looked at the floor, then at the wall of tallow blocks, anywhere that wasn't my face, and finally he said, in a voice smaller than a man his size should own:

"I don't know who has it now. On my mother's grave I didn't take it. But Bram — there are things in that book I'm more afraid of than the gallows." He wiped his mouth. "There are things in that book about *me*."

"Debts," I said. "Everyone has debts. My father wrote them all down, it's what he did—"

"Not debts." He said it fast, before courage could fail him entirely. "Bram, your father kept two kinds of truth in that ledger. What people owed, and what people *did*. He told me once — years ago, drunk on festival wine — that a ledger that only holds money is half a book. He recorded quarrels. Sins. Things done to each other in this trade that no magistrate ever heard of, because he thought someone ought to remember them truly, even if no one ever read it." His eyes finally came up to mine, and they were wet and terrified and old. "If that book is being read now, it isn't just hanging printers for sedition. It's settling every score in Vellsmark, and some of those scores were written by your father's hand about men who are still alive and still dangerous."

I stood very still. The tallow smelled like a church that had given up.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because your name is on that draft, and mine will be soon, and because—" He broke off. Gathered himself. "Because your father once wrote a thing in that book that saved me, and I never thanked him, and thanking his daughter is all the amends left to me."

"What thing?"

"That's the trouble," Quill said miserably. "I don't *know*. He never showed me. He only told me I needn't worry about a certain matter, and smiled, and poured more wine, and I've spent six years wondering what mercy looks like in a dead man's handwriting — and now the whole army is going to read it over my shoulder."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the river path, because the river doesn't ask questions and doesn't post notices. The evening light came off the water the color of old pewter, and the smugglers' lights were already showing downstream, little lies of lantern-glow where honest trade ended and other trade began.

Cold clarity. That's what I had, and I held onto it the way you hold a tool with a sharp edge — carefully, knowing it cuts the hand that grips it. Here is what I knew:

One: the ledger survived, in the hands of a grey little clerk, and it was leverage, which meant it could be *spent*, and things that can be spent can be stolen.

Two: names were about to become confessions, confessions about to become convictions, and the machinery of it would grind up this district alphabetically and call it justice.

Three: my father had not simply kept accounts. He had kept *record* — the true kind, the dangerous kind — and somewhere in that green-leather book was a page about me that I had never read, and I would die not knowing whether it was a kindness or a judgment unless I got that book back.

Four: my name was already on a wall in army type, and the only press in town that could print my rebuttal had been sealed as evidence against me.

There's a moment in every job at the bench when you're cutting a new letterface and the graver slips, and you see in the instant before the damage that it's going to be ruined — and there is also, in that same instant, the decision: throw the punch away, or rework it. Cut the mistake into something else. Turn the slip into a serif nobody planned but that ends up truer than the design.

I was past the slipping. I was at the decision.

They had made a list. Fine. Lists can be altered; I was raised on altering text. They had the ledger; fine; ledgers can be *recovered*, and better, ledgers can be *answered*. My father had taught me that no document is final until it's printed and distributed, and the army hadn't printed a thing yet. Drafts, postings, warrants — all of it was just type in a chase, loose, unset, waiting for someone with steady hands.

I got back to the shop after dark and Tally was awake, sitting up on her pallet by the stove, waiting, because she'd known all day that I'd come back needing her and she'd arranged herself accordingly, the little tyrant.

"Bram," she said. "The flour. You bought the coarse kind."

"I did."

"You hate the coarse kind."

"I do." I sat down across from her and looked at this child I'd inherited the way you inherit weather, and I said, "Tally. Tomorrow I need you to tell me everything you saw last night again, every detail, in order, and leave nothing out because you think it's small. Which gate. Which lamp. How Doss held the bundle. Whether the officer wore gloves. Everything."

"Why?"

"Because a man named Doss is holding every life in this town in a piece of oilcloth," I said, "and I'm going to take it back from him. And the difference between getting caught and getting away is always something small that somebody thought wasn't worth mentioning."

Tally pulled her blanket around her shoulders like a mantle and settled in to testify, and I fetched paper, and outside the window the garrison drums beat the curfew, steady as a press, printing the hours onto the town.

What she told me, in the end, was worse than anything I'd imagined — and it began with five words I'll carry as long as I carry anything at all:

"He wasn't alone. The captain helped him."
