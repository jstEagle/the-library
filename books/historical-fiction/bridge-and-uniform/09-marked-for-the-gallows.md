## 9. Marked for the Gallows

The list came to me the way bad news always comes to Vellsmark — folded small, passed hand to hand, and smelling faintly of someone else's fear.

Tally brought it. She came through the back door of the shop at an hour when no honest apprentice should be abroad, her cheeks blotched red from running, and she didn't say anything at first. She just stood there in the doorway with her chest heaving and her fist clenched around a scrap of paper like it had bitten her.

"Give it here," I said.

She gave it. I unfolded it under the lamp, and I read it twice, because the first time my eyes refused to do their work properly. They slid off the ink the way water slides off oiled cloth. The second time I made them stay.

Eleven names. Eleven names in a clerk's careful hand, each one followed by a date — trial dates, though the paper didn't say so, didn't need to. Everyone in Vellsmark knew what a name on a list with a date meant now. The army had been holding tribunals in the customs house, quick ones, the kind where the verdict is written before the accused finishes walking up the steps.

The third name was Quill. Master Aldous Quill, owner of this shop, keeper of its accounts, a man who apologized to chairs when he bumped into them.

The seventh name was Okafor.

My name. My father's name, rather — but the army has never been particular about such distinctions, and neither, apparently, was the clerk who wrote *Okafor, B.* in the same tidy script he'd used for the others. Not *Okafor, E., deceased.* Just Okafor, B. They had decided I inherited his guilt along with his tenancy and his cutting knife. In Vellsmark that was how inheritance worked now.

"You shouldn't have taken this," I said. "If they catch you carrying it—"

"They won't." Tally lifted her chin, which is what she did instead of believing herself. "Marta's brother works the garrison laundry. He copied it out while the officers' shirts were soaking."

"Marta's brother copied eleven death warrants between wash loads."

"He's very fast."

I looked at her — thirteen years old, ink-stained to the second knuckle, standing in my father's shop at midnight with stolen paper in her fist — and felt something crack open in my chest that wasn't entirely dread. Some of it was pride, which is a useless thing to feel about a child you're supposed to be protecting, and I pocketed it anyway.

"Go home," I said. "Sleep. Come at the usual hour tomorrow and touch nothing."

"I don't want—"

"Tally." I made my voice the flat one, the one I used on apprentices and stubborn type. "Home. Now."

She went, but she went slowly, pausing at the door to look back at me with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors all week: the look of someone memorizing a room in case the room doesn't keep.

* * *

By morning the whole district had the list, or some version of it, and the guild began to come apart the way wet rope comes apart — not all at once, but fiber by fiber, each strand deciding for itself.

They came to the shop in twos and threes, printers and binders and the widows of both, slipping in through the back as if the front door might be watched, which it might. Old Ferren came first, who ran the bindery on Cooper's Lane and hadn't set foot in our shop since the dispute over the almanac pricing eight years ago. He stood in the middle of the floor wringing his cap and told me the wisest thing any of them said that day:

"Bram. Take your matrices and go. Tonight. There are boats."

Behind him came the Hallow sisters, who printed hymnals and could not meet my eye. Then young Piotr from the press-house, then Widow Anselm, then a dozen more across the long gray day, and every one of them said some version of the same thing. *Go. Flee. You're marked. There are boats.*

And underneath it, unspoken but loud as a press run, the other thing: *and if you go, perhaps they'll stop looking at us.*

I understood that. I want it recorded somewhere that I understood it completely. Fear makes people arithmetic. Eleven names on a list means ten people doing subtraction.

"No," I said, to each of them, in turn, until the word wore smooth in my mouth.

Ferren grabbed my sleeve at the door on his way out, the last of them, dusk coming down behind him. "Child, listen to me. Running isn't shame. My grandfather ran from the border wars and lived to be eighty."

"And what did the ledger say about him?"

He blinked. "What?"

"The ledger," I said. "The guild ledger. Every name, every debt, every signature. It's sitting in the garrison right now, and everything written in it is about to become the official truth of this town, because nobody alive can contradict it except the people it names. And the people it names—" I tapped the folded copy of the list in my apron pocket. "Are being hanged. One by one. In order."

"That's not your fight alone."

"It's not mine at all if I'm on a boat watching the coast go by." I pulled my sleeve free, gently, because Ferren was old and frightened and had once, years ago, fixed my father's stitching frame without being asked. "Master Ferren, if I run, the ledger stands. Every lie in it stands. My father's name stands next to crimes he never committed, and there's no one left to say otherwise, because everyone who could is either dead, fled, or keeping their head down in Cooper's Lane. That's not survival. That's just a slower way of dying."

He looked at me for a long moment. "You sound like your father."

"He'd have said it better."

"He'd have said it longer," Ferren said, and almost smiled, and then he pulled his cap down and went out into the dark, and I never saw him again. I hope he found his boats. I hope he lived to be eighty. But I noticed, that night, that he hadn't asked me what I intended to do instead of fleeing. Nobody had. Because we all knew there was only one thing, and it was impossible, and I was going to do it anyway.

* * *

Quill came after dark.

He let himself in with his own key, which he still carried even though the army had sealed the shop, because Aldous Quill believed rules were things you observed carefully and then obeyed selectively, and he moved through the front room like a man visiting a grave. He stopped at the press and put his hand flat on the frame, the way you'd touch a horse's neck.

"You've seen the list," he said.

"Everyone's seen the list."

"Then you know I'm third." He laughed, a small dry sound with nothing in it. "Third. Not even first. Ashe's clerks couldn't even be bothered to make me the principal conspirator. After thirty years in the guild."

"Quill—"

"I informed on them." He said it to the press, not to me. "Years ago. During the interrogations after the bread riots. They had me three days in a cellar and I gave them names, and those names became the foundation of everything in that ledger that matters, and now the ledger is going to hang eleven people including a girl who cuts typefaces, and it's all built on what I said in a cellar when I would have said anything, Bram. Anything. I would have given them the alphabet."

I had known it, or half-known it, the way you half-know a thing you refuse to finish knowing. I felt fury rise in me like floodwater — at him, at the cellar, at the army, at the whole rotten architecture of a world where a frightened man in a dark room could build gallows that outlived him by decades.

And then I looked at him, really looked — at the trembling hands, the man apologizing to furniture — and the floodwater settled into something colder and more useful.

"Then help me unbuild it," I said.

"How? I'm the third name. I'm evidence, Bram. Everything I say is worth nothing because they own me."

"Not everything." I thought of the ledger, of my father's hand in it, of the names he'd kept off the list — the mercy hidden in the margins that the army hadn't found yet, or hadn't understood. "There are things in that book that contradict the things you told them. My father kept records too. If the ledger is the truth, then the ledger can also be the proof it's a lie. But someone has to get it out."

"And that someone is you." He turned from the press at last. His eyes were wet and steady, which is a combination I've only ever seen in people who have already decided something terrible. "Bram, listen to me. I'm going to do something, and I need you not to try to stop it, and I need you not to hate me for it."

"That's two requests too many."

"Three days," he said. "You have three days before the trials start. Whatever you're going to do — and I can see that you're going to do something, you have your father's face when you're planning, all sharp edges — do it fast. And if it fails—" He stopped. Started again. "If it fails, remember that some debts can't be paid in coin. Only in weight."

He left then, and I stood in the shop listening to his footsteps fade down Cooper's Lane, and I did not understand what he meant. I would understand later. Later, I would have given a great deal not to.

* * *

That night I sat at my father's workbench with the lamp trimmed low and the list spread out in front of me, and I did what I do. What I'm for. I measured.

Eleven names. Seven of them printers or bound to printers. Four were sympathizers — a chandler, a schoolmistress, two rivermen. Trial dates staggered across nine days, starting with the least important, working up. The army builds its cases the way you build a wall: small stones first, big ones last, so the structure holds while you work.

Okafor, B. — ninth day. They were saving me. Or saving me for last, which is the army's idea of courtesy.

I took out my father's cutting knife and held it and thought about what can be carried. A font tray weighs eleven pounds. A grudge weighs nothing and yet you feel it in your shoulders forever. A ledger full of names weighs four pounds and carries a whole district inside it.

Running meant the ledger stood. Staying meant I hanged. Those were the choices as the day had dealt them, and I sat turning them over like sorts in a composing stick, finding neither one would set a clean line.

But there was a third option, the one I'd been circling for days the way your tongue circles a broken tooth: the ledger didn't have to stand. Evidence could be moved. Evidence could be *carried*. Four pounds. Out of the garrison, past the checkpoints, onto a ship, into hands that would read it against the men who wrote it.

It was impossible. Every part of it was impossible. The garrison was sealed, the gates were watched, the harbor was crawling, and I was a typeface cutter with a knife and a grief, not a soldier or a smuggler or a spy.

I got out fresh paper and began, in my father's manner, to make a list.

Not of names. Of gates.

* * *

The knock came at dawn — soft, three raps, the rhythm Tally and I had agreed on for ordinary mornings — and I opened the door expecting her and the day's first errands.

Instead there was a woman on my step wrapped in a plain gray cloak, hood up, and for one heartbeat I didn't know her, and then I did, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

Captain Ilse Verren stood on the threshold of my father's shop in civilian wool, out of uniform, alone, at an hour when no officer has innocent business.

"Bram Okafor," she said. Her voice was low and rough, like a file drawn over iron. "We need to talk about the morning tide."

Behind her, down the lane, the river fog was lifting off Vellsmark, and somewhere on the water a ship's bell rang out the hour — clear, patient, counting down.
