## 10. The Smuggler's Offer

There are moments when your body understands a thing before your mind consents to it. When Captain Ilse Verren said *the morning tide*, my hands went cold and my feet stayed planted, and some animal part of me had already begun counting exits — the back door, the cellar hatch, the window over the type room that I'd climbed through a hundred times as a girl and could not fit through now without breaking it and myself.

"You'd better come in," I said, because standing in the doorway arguing with an army captain in civilian wool was a way to get us both hanged faster.

She stepped inside the shop the way a cat enters a room full of birds — everything noted, nothing pounced on. Her eyes went to the press first, tarped and cold, then to the font racks, then to the floorboards, and I watched her inventory my life in about four seconds flat. Officers do that. They can't help it. It's the same training that teaches them to read a battlefield reads a kitchen.

"I'm not here in any capacity," she said. "You understand that. If anyone asks, I was never here."

"Then who is asking?"

She took off her hood. Out of uniform she looked older and less certain, which I confess I found useful. "A woman who ate at this table for one winter of her life and has never once managed to repay it."

"There's bread," I said. "It's two days old."

"Bram." She said my name like a warning shot across a bow. "In three days there will be a search of every vessel leaving this harbor. Before that, there will be a search of every house on this street. Yours is already on a list. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you're here to arrest me?"

"That I'm here because if they find whatever they think you're hiding — and they *will* find it, Bram, Ashe has men who can find a stolen button in a haystack — then it goes better for everyone if it isn't found in your house."

*Ashe.* The name meant nothing to me then. It would come to mean a great deal later, but that morning it passed through me like weather.

"I don't have anything to hide," I said, which was true, technically, since the thing I had to hide wasn't in my house yet. It was in the garrison evidence vault, wearing a wax seal and my family name in my father's handwriting.

Verren looked at me for a long moment. She had gray eyes, the exact color of the river in November, and they did the same thing the river did — showed you a surface and kept the depth to themselves.

"No," she said finally. "I don't believe you do. Which is somehow worse." She pulled her hood back up. "Whatever you're going to do — and I can see by your face that you're going to do something — do it before the tide three days from now, or don't do it at all."

And she left, out into the fog, and I stood in my father's shop with my heart hammering like a compositor's stick against a full case, understanding two things clearly: first, that Captain Ilse Verren had just told me, in her careful sideways fashion, exactly how much time I had; and second, that she had done it knowing full well what I intended, and had chosen to hand me the clock instead of the noose.

I did not thank her. There was no time. There never is, at the moments when thanks would matter.

Nessa Kade kept no fixed address, which is the privilege and burden of people whose profession requires them to be difficult to find. In Vellsmark, if you wanted the smuggler Kade, you didn't ask after her — you asked after her ship, the *Heron*, which was easier because ships must declare themselves even when their cargo doesn't. The harbormaster's book listed her as moored at the mud berth past the ropewalk, the berth nobody wanted because at low tide the *Heron* sat in the muck listing like a drunk leaning on a fence.

My father had done business with Kade for years, though "business" is a generous word for what passed between them. He printed her bills of lading — clean ones, with dates and ports arranged in ways that flattered the truth — and she paid him partly in coin and partly in goods that appeared on no manifest anywhere in the kingdom. I remember being nine years old and watching her pay off a debt in pepper, saffron, and a bolt of blue silk, and my father laughing and saying, "Nessa, half this could hang us both," and her saying, "Only the saffron, Okafor. The rest is practically legal."

That had been eight years ago. I had no idea whether she remembered my father fondly, transactionally, or at all. Debts between smugglers and printers are settled in their own currency, and memory is negotiable like everything else.

I went down to the mud berth at dusk, dressed plainly, carrying nothing but a wrapped parcel of bread and cheese — food opens doors where money cannot, and it gives you something to do with your hands while you lie. The fog was coming up off the water again, thickening around the pilings, and the *Heron* sat in the shallows with her belly showing, ropes creaking, lamp lit at her stern like a single stubborn eye.

A boy rigger perched on the rail above me, skinny as a gaff hook, maybe twelve years old, watching me the way cats watch anything that moves.

"I'm looking for Kade," I said.

"She's not buying."

"I'm selling."

"She's not buying that either."

"I'm Okafor's daughter," I said, "and I've brought supper."

He considered this with the gravity of a customs official. Then he jerked his chin toward the stern cabin and said, "She's eating. You can wait. If she throws you off, mind the mud, it's deeper than it looks."

The cabin was low-ceilinged and smelled of tar, tea, and old voyages. Nessa Kade sat behind a table scarred with knife-marks and candle-wax, and she was not what I expected, though I couldn't have said what I'd expected — younger, perhaps, or more piratical. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with rope-burned hands and reading glasses pushed up into iron-gray hair, and a ledger of her own open before her, which struck me as either hypocrisy or professional courtesy.

"Well," she said, looking up. The glasses came down onto her nose and she studied me through them like a document of doubtful provenance. "You have his hands. The father. Same fingers. Type-cutters' fingers." She pointed with a pen. "Sit. What's in the parcel?"

"Bread. Cheese. The cheese is questionable."

"Honest of you." She took it anyway, broke the bread, and pushed half across the table at me, which I hadn't anticipated. Smugglers feed you first, I would learn. On the water, refusing to share food is bad luck, and Kade was a very superstitious woman wearing a very rational face.

"Eat," she said. "Then tell me why a printer's daughter comes to a mud berth at dusk, in fog, dressed like a widow, two days after soldiers seal half the print shops in this town. And take your time, because I'm enjoying the bread and I hate being rushed at meals."

So I told her.

Not all of it — not Tally, not Verren, not the parts that were still forming in me like type not yet set. But I told her about the ledger: that it existed, that the army held it, that it held eleven names slated for the gallows including mine, and that on the morning tide three days hence it needed to be out on the water and gone.

Kade listened while she ate, which I came to understand was how she did everything — in parallel, several things at once, none of them quite what she appeared to be doing. When I finished she wiped her knife on the table edge and looked at me for a while without speaking.

"Your father," she said at last, "printed me false papers for six years. Good ones. Beautiful ones. Never once did he ask me what I was carrying or why." She set down the knife. "Do you know what he charged me?"

"Fair rates," I said. "He told me once he undercharged you."

"He charged me nothing for the last two years. I found out only when I tried to settle accounts and his books said we were square." She tapped the table. "Square. As if feeding my crew's families out of his margin were arithmetic. So. You've come to collect a debt, Miss Okafor, and I'll tell you plainly: I pay my debts. Ask anyone on this river. It's the one reputation I've kept cleaner than my papers." She leaned back. "What do you want?"

"The ledger carried out on the morning tide. Past the harbor chain, past the cutters, down the coast to whoever will take it."

"That's cargo," she said. "Cargo I can do. What I can't do is explain to a magistrate why I was seen taking delivery of it from the daughter of a man on a warrant list, in a town crawling with red coats." Her eyes narrowed. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless whoever brings it aboard can't be traced back to me afterward. Unless it comes aboard at night, by small boat, from a point away from the quay, and the person carrying it steps on my deck and stays on my deck." She took off the glasses and folded them with terrible deliberation. "I'll carry your ledger, girl. On the morning tide, three days from now, I'll run the chain if I have to bribe it, blind it, or ram it. But you bring it to me yourself. Your hands, your risk, your name on the delivery. Not an intermediary. Not a dead drop. You."

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "If it's about protecting yourself—"

"It's entirely about protecting myself," she said briskly. "But it's also about this: a thing like that ledger changes hands wrong a dozen ways and right only one. If a stranger hands it to my boy on a dark dock, I don't know it's real. I don't know it's whole. I don't know who followed him. If *you* bring it, I know its history from the moment it leaves your grip to the moment it's in my hold, and so does anyone I ever have to answer to." She stood, which made the cabin feel suddenly smaller. "Besides. Your father never asked me to account for my cargo. I won't insult his daughter by letting her send one."

"And if I'm caught bringing it?"

"Then I sail without you and I never heard of you, and your father's debt dies with your arrest, because that's how debts work on the water." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd report the tide times. "I'll be sorry. I'll probably even drink to you. But I won't wait, and I won't come back, and I won't bargain with soldiers for you. Is that understood?"

"Perfectly."

"Do you have the ledger?"

"Not yet."

"Do you have a way to it?"

"I have three days and a plan with holes in it."

Kade laughed — a short bark, genuinely amused. "All plans have holes. The trick is choosing which holes you can swim through." She came around the table and studied me close, and for a moment something moved behind the practical face — something that might have been grief, or memory, or just the particular ache of seeing someone young walk toward a thing you can see coming and they can't. "Three days. Morning tide. Small boat, no lights, east of the customs sloop where the chain doesn't reach. If you miss it, I don't exist. If you make it—" she shrugged "—you're one of mine, and I look after what's mine. Ask anyone on this river."

"One more thing," I said. "How many can you carry? If things go wrong. If I'm not traveling light."

Her eyes went over my shoulder, toward the shore, toward the shop, toward — I realized — everything and everyone I hadn't mentioned. Smugglers count the unspoken things in a sentence better than priests do.

"My ship carries seven crew comfortably," she said. "Eleven in a squeeze. Thirteen if the squeeze is worth it." She put the glasses back in her hair and turned back to her ledger. "Go home, Okafor's daughter. Eat something. Sleep if you can. And don't come near this berth again until the night — the boy watches the quay, and boys talk."

I went up the ladder into the fog, and the mud sucked at my boots the whole way to solid ground, as if the river itself were reluctant to let go of me — or reluctant, maybe, to let me go where I was headed.

Behind me, the *Heron*'s lamp burned steady at her stern, and ahead the town lay dark under the army's occupation, and between those two points of light and dark stretched three days in which I had to steal a ledger out of a fortified garrison, keep a child clear of soldiers, and become, apparently, someone who belonged to a smuggler.

I thought of my father's rule, the one he carved into the shop lintel where I'd read it every morning of my childhood: *measure the world in what can be carried.*

Three days. One ledger. Everything else left behind.

I turned toward the garrison hill, where the evidence room windows glowed like banked coals, and started walking.
