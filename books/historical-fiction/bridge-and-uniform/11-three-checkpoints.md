## 11. Three Checkpoints

Nessa Kade drew maps the way other people told lies: confidently, and with an eye for what you wanted to see.

She'd commandeered the back table of the Silted Oar — a tavern so disreputable that even the rats used the front door — and spread three sheets of paper across it, weighted at the corners with a tankard, a candle stub, a knife, and a dried fish she declined to explain. The fish stayed through the whole conversation. I learned not to ask.

"This is Vellsmark as the army sees it," she said, tapping the first sheet. "Three ways in or out for anything heavier than a rumor. The market gate, north side. The bridge checkpoint, east. The harbor quay, south. Everything else is wall, water, or mudflat, and the mudflats will take a horse to the knee and keep it." She looked up at me. "So. Whatever you're carrying out of this town — and don't insult us both by pretending you're not — it goes through one of these doors."

I had told her nothing of the ledger. I had told Kade only that I needed passage on the morning tide in three days, for myself and a parcel. She had named a price that was frankly insulting, and then halved it when I mentioned my father's name, which told me everything about how my father's debts were remembered on this river. Now she was drawing me a map of my own town like I was a stranger to it, and the worst part was that I was learning things.

"I grew up here," I said. "I know the gates."

"You know the gates as a printer's daughter who walks through them with her head down and her hands clean. That's a different animal than walking them as a woman the army wants." She turned the first sheet toward me. "The market gate. Tell me what you see."

I looked. Her hand was quick and ugly and precise, which is a combination I respected immediately — it's the type-cutter's combination, done in ink instead of steel. The market gate was drawn as a throat: two stone towers, a covered passage between them, wagons queued on the near side, soldiers on both flanks.

"Cart traffic goes through slow," I said. "Foot traffic gets patted down on market days because of smuggled salt. Sergeant's name is Hobb. He's lazy but he isn't stupid, and his wife sells gossip out of the second stall past the gatehouse like other women sell eels."

Kade's eyebrows went up a fraction, which from her I understood to be applause. "And there's your door," she said. "Not Hobb. Hobb's wife. A gate is a wall with men standing in it; you don't beat the men, you beat the *habit* of the place. Every morning the carts come in from the farm road and every morning the laundresses come in behind them hauling wash for the garrison families, and every morning Hobb waves the laundresses through because searching forty women carrying linens is work, and Hobb has made a career of not working." She tapped the paper. "Be a laundress. Be boring. Boring is the best disguise ever invented, and no one has ever once suspected a boring person of anything."

"And if they do search?"

"Then you lose the parcel, and probably your freedom, and possibly your neck, in ascending order of certainty." She said it the way she might report the weather. "Which brings me to the honest part of this conversation, printer's daughter, and you'll forgive me because I only do one honest part per day." She leaned forward, and the candle caught the scar along her jaw I'd been pretending not to notice. "Three days ago I agreed to carry a parcel. Since then the army has sealed the print shops, posted warrant drafts on the courthouse door, and started stopping people on the streets to ask where they were on nights they couldn't possibly have been anywhere interesting. So before I draw you any further into this, I want to know what's in the parcel that's worth your life and half my cargo space."

I looked at her, and I thought about lying, and then I thought about the fact that in three days my life was going to depend on this woman's willingness to do something stupid on my behalf, and lies are a poor foundation for stupidity.

"Names," I said. "Every printer, debtor, and sympathizer in Vellsmark, written in my father's hand. The guild ledger. The army has it, and they're building gallows out of it, and mine is one of the names."

Something moved behind Kade's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd already priced in.

"Well," she said. "That's a parcel." She pulled the second sheet toward her and smoothed it flat with two fingers. "Then we'd best talk about the bridge."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about making a plan: it feels wonderful. That's the trap.

A plan takes the vast formless horror of *everything that could go wrong* and pours it into a neat row of small manageable containers: a gate, a lie, a costume, a time. And your chest unclenches, and you sleep that night, because the world has edges again. It never occurs to you until much later — usually while you're standing in front of a sergeant with a lie dissolving in your mouth — that the plan was never the world. The plan was just a drawing of the world, done confidently, with an eye for what you wanted to see.

But I didn't know that yet. That night, planning felt like being competent again, and after weeks of watching soldiers inventory my father's life, competence felt like a drug.

Kade laid out the bridge checkpoint the way a surgeon lays out instruments. "The bridge is the hard one," she said. "Market gate searches hands and parcels. Harbor quay searches papers. But the bridge—" she tapped the second sheet, where the river ran wide and gray under her pen "—the bridge searches *stories*. There's a sergeant there called Brandt, and Brandt doesn't care what you're carrying. He cares why. He'll ask you where you're coming from and where you're going, and then he'll ask you a third question that has nothing to do with either, because the third question is where liars live. Carrying something through means your story has to hold weight. Literally. You want whatever you're carrying to have an innocent reason to be heavy."

"Innocent reasons to be heavy," I repeated. "You make it sound like a trade."

"It is a trade. Everything is." She glanced at the third sheet, still face-down under the dried fish, and left it there. "Think. What crosses the east bridge that's heavy, sad, and expected?"

I thought of my father's shop. I thought of the trays of type, each character a few grams of lead, and how a whole font could weigh more than a child. I thought about weight, and what things weigh, and what we say about the dead.

"Funerals," I said slowly. "Coffins cross the bridge."

Kade smiled without much humor in it. "There it is. Nobody opens coffins, printer's daughter. Grief is the oldest smuggling route in the world, and superstition does the sentries' thinking for them. A coffin, a mourning party, a story about a cousin in the country churchyard — Brandt will grumble, and look, and let it pass, because the alternative is being the man who opened a coffin on the bridge and had everyone in Vellsmark remember him for it." She sat back. "It can work."

"But," I said. Because there was a *but*. There is always a *but*, and I was learning to hear it coming.

"But," she agreed, "it needs a body. You can't cross with an empty coffin; empty coffins get opened out of sheer spite. And you'd need someone on the far side who expects you — a real grave, a real sexton, a thread of truth running all the way through the lie, because Brandt pulls threads." She looked at me over the candle. "I can find you a coffin. The rest is your town."

I wrote it all down later, in the shorthand my father taught me for marking up proofs — little symbols in the margins, *transp. wrong f., kill this em, wait* — a language for fixing errors before they reached print. If only life worked that way. If only you could catch the error in proof, before the whole edition was struck.

Instead I slept four hours and dreamed of lead, and woke with the plan sitting in my chest like a well-set line of type: solid, justified, ready for the press. I loved that plan. I want that on the record, wherever records end up. I *loved* it.

That was the problem with plans. They love you back right up until the moment they don't.

* * *

The harbor quay was the last sheet, and Kade saved it for the second evening, in her own cabin aboard the *Merry Adela*, a boat that sat lower in the water than any honest vessel should and smelled of fish, tar, and what Kade insisted was "character."

"The quay is different," she said, dealing the third sheet onto the table at last. "The market gate wants to search your hands. The bridge wants to search your story. The quay—" she tapped the drawing, where the customs sloop sat moored like a fat spider at the center of its web "—the quay wants to search your *papers*. Dawn searches, starting tomorrow, Ashe's order. Every hull, every hold, every crate going out on the tide. Which means my ship gets turned inside out at first light, and whatever we're carrying had better not be aboard when it happens."

"So I bring it aboard after the search."

"After the search. Which gives you a window between the search party finishing and the tide turning, and that window is—" she held up her thumb and forefinger, almost touching "—this wide, and shrinking. And to reach my deck at all you cross the open water of the harbor, past the customs sloop, in a dark boat, during a lockdown, carrying the single most dangerous piece of paper in the province." She let that sit. "I won't dress it up. The quay is where plans go to drown. If the gate and the bridge fail you, you lose time. If the quay fails you, you lose everything, all at once, wet."

"Thank you," I said dryly. "Your honesty for the day."

"That was yesterday's. Today's is free." She poured two fingers of something into a tin cup and pushed it across the table at me, and I was surprised to find I drank it without grimacing, which says something about the kind of week I'd been having. "One more thing, printer's daughter, and then you can go home and not sleep properly like all my other passengers. Three checkpoints, three disguises, three lies. You've got the makings of a good liar — I watched you tell the laundress at the Oar that your mother was alive and well, and she believed you, and your face never moved. But a lie is a font, and here's the thing about fonts." She pointed at me. "A font holds exactly as many characters as it holds. Run out mid-line and the whole page shows the gap. Don't spend your lies early. Keep one true thing in reserve for each checkpoint — a name, a place, a grief — because when the lie runs short, truth is the only thing that prints clean."

I thought of my father then, suddenly and painfully — the way he'd test a new apprentice by handing them a galley of set type and asking them to read it upside down. *Anyone can read it the easy way,* he'd say. *The job is reading it when it's hard.* Kade was teaching me to read my own escape upside down, and she was better at it than I was, and I hated how much that steadied me.

"Why?" I asked her. "You owed my father money. That's paid twice over by now, what with the insulting fee. Why are you still at this table?"

Kade looked at the candle for a while. When she answered, she didn't quite look at me.

"When your father lent me money for this hull, I was twenty-three and every yard on the coast had turned me down, and he didn't even haggle. Just asked to see my charts, read them for an hour, and said 'she'll float.' First person in my life who looked at my plans instead of my face." She gathered up her three sheets, squared their edges with a practiced tap. "He measured people in what they could carry. I intend to see his measurement stand."

* * *

I walked home through the curfew-dark streets with the whole route built in my head: the market gate at dawn, dressed in borrowed linens, armed with gossip and boredom. The bridge at noon, behind a coffin, behind grief. The quay at last light, papers and water and a window this wide. Three doors. Three lies. Three chances for the world to remind me that it hadn't read my plan and didn't feel bound by it.

At the mouth of Printers' Row I saw a figure waiting under the lamp — Master Quill, hat in his hands, turning it the way he always did when something was eating him. He looked older than he had a week ago, if that were possible; he looked like a man rehearsing words he didn't want to say.

"Bram," he said. "We need to talk. About the ledger — and about what I did, years back, before your father died. There's a thing you should know about me before you trust anything I—"

"No," I said, and heard my own voice go flat. "Whatever it is, Quill, it keeps until the tide does. I have a route to memorize and a coffin to find, and I cannot carry your confession and the ledger both."

He went pale in the lamplight. And he didn't argue — which should have been my first warning, because Quill argued about everything, about kerning, about weather, about the proper way to hang a coat. A guilty man argues loudest about nothing at all. A man with a real confession knows its weight, and sets it down gently, and waits.

"Tomorrow, then," he said quietly. "Before you go through that first gate. Because if you walk through it not knowing who I am, Bram, you'll be carrying something heavier than either of us can lift."

He put his hat on and walked away into the dark, and I stood there under the lamp with my beautiful plan humming in my chest — three gates, three lies, one true thing in reserve for each — and felt, for just a moment, the cold draft of a fourth thing I hadn't planned for, coming up the row behind him.
