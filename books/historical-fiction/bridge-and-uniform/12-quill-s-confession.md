## 12. Quill's Confession

Master Quill had not always been an old man. I knew this the way you know a river was once a glacier — intellectually, from evidence — but it still surprised me every time I saw him move quickly, which was almost never. He ran the guild hall on Chandler's Lane the way a spider runs a web: patiently, from the center, pretending to be decorative.

I found him there two days before the tide Kade had promised me, sitting in his counting room with a single candle lit at midday, which in Quill's case meant either poverty, piety, or preparation for bad news. With Quill it was never poverty. The man owned four buildings and pretended to own one.

"You shouldn't be here," he said without looking up. He'd known my footsteps since I was nine. Everyone in Vellsmark's printing trade could identify each other by walk alone; we all had the same flat-footed shuffle from hauling trays of type, the same permanent lean toward one shoulder.

"Neither should you," I said. "The guild scattered three days ago. You're the only name on the list still sleeping in your own bed."

"Someone has to water the plants." He did look up then, and I saw what the candle was really for. Not atmosphere. Camouflage. He looked like a man who had decided how he wanted a room to look when other people found it.

"Quill." I sat down across from him without being asked, because if I waited to be asked I would stand in that doorway forever, and because sitting down in Quill's counting room uninvited was the closest thing to rebellion I'd managed since the army came. "I'm leaving on the morning tide in two days. There are things I need from you. Routes. Names of harbor men who'll look away. Whether Doss drinks before noon."

He folded his hands. They were beautiful hands, for a man's, ink-stained at every knuckle like he'd been caught stealing from his own ledgers. "Before we get to that, Bram, there's something you need from me first, though you don't know it yet, and you'll wish you didn't."

I should have left. That's the truth of it, written down here where no one will print it but me: I should have stood up and walked out and let the old man water his plants in peace. But he said *Bram* instead of *Okafor*, and nobody had used my first name since my father died, and I stayed. Sentiment is just another kind of trap, and it closes slow enough that you mistake it for comfort.

"Tell me," I said.

* * *

Here is what Master Aldous Quill told me, in a voice like a man reading someone else's obituary:

Twelve years ago, when I was eleven and mostly concerned with whether my father would let me cut serifs yet, there had been a crackdown. Not the big one everyone remembers — the Weavers' Rising that gets songs sung about it — but a smaller, quieter one, the kind the crown runs every few years to remind the trades that memory itself can be taxed. A royal magistrate named Corvel came to Vellsmark with a list of questions and a cellar below the customs house, and he started pulling printers off the street the way you pull weeds: a few at a time, so nobody panics.

Quill was thirty-two then, newly master of his own shop, with a wife — I hadn't known he'd ever had a wife — and a son of two. The magistrate's men took him on a Tuesday. What happened in the cellar took four days. Quill described it the way he described everything, precisely, without flourish, the way you'd describe a press mechanism: what was applied, where, how long, what it produced. His hands never stopped folding and unfolding on the table. His voice never once shook, which was worse. I have heard men cry about less. Quill recited it like inventory.

On the fourth day, they brought him paper.

"They didn't want names of conspirators," he said. "There were none. We weren't anything yet — a few men complaining over ale, my father-in-law's grudge against the excise house. Corvel knew that. He wasn't building a case. He was building an *asset*. A man in every guild hall, in every town, who belongs to the crown so thoroughly he can never belong to anything else. He wanted a signature. One page. And he wanted it to say what he told it to say."

"And you signed."

"I signed." He unfolded his hands and laid them flat, palms down, as if pressing a sheet to the stone. "It named six men as organizers of sedition. Two were already dead. Three fled that week and lost everything. The sixth was hanged. His name was Pieter Voss. He was a bookbinder. He had a stutter and a daughter, and he was guilty of nothing except being on a list I wrote for a magistrate who needed the number to come out even."

The candle guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

"The signature has never stopped working," Quill went on. "Corvel kept it. His successors kept it. Every few years someone comes to me — quietly, always quietly — and asks for a small thing. A name confirmed. A meeting reported. A letter copied. And every time, I do it, because the alternative is that the page surfaces, and everything I built, everyone I employ, everyone I've *protected*—"

"Protected." I heard my own voice come out flat and ugly. "You call what you did to Pieter Voss protection?"

"I called it survival, and I called it arithmetic, and I have spent twelve years doing sums in the dark to make it balance." For the first time the old composure cracked, just a seam, just enough. "Your father knew."

The room went very still, the way a room does when something large walks through it without touching anything.

"He found out eight years ago," Quill said. "Came to my shop after close, put a copy of the Corvel page on my counter — he'd found it, God knows how, he had ways of finding things — and he watched me fall apart in front of him. And do you know what Josiah Okafor did? He didn't turn me in. He didn't even hate me, which I would have deserved and could have carried better. He said, *"A confession under torture isn't testimony, Aldous. It's a wound. But a man who keeps feeding the wound to stay safe — that's a choice, and choices accrue interest."* And then he made me a bargain. He'd keep my secret, bury his copy somewhere I'd never find it, and I would spend the rest of my life paying the guild back. Quietly. In kind."

"The ledger," I said slowly. "The names he kept off it. The debtors he forgave. The sympathizers he recorded as dead ends."

"He taught me mercy like it was a trade skill," Quill said. "Like setting type. You do it badly at first and then it becomes your hand."

I wanted to throw something. I wanted to throw something very badly, and the only things within reach were Quill's ledger weights, which belonged to the guild, and the candle, which belonged to the dark, so instead I sat there with fury filling me up like floodwater, rising past my ribs, and I thought about my father. My patient, meticulous, grief-hollowed father, who had carried a blackmailer in his pocket for eight years and turned him into a philanthropist and never told his daughter, never once, because telling her would have made her carry it too.

He had measured the world in what could be carried. Apparently he'd decided I had enough weight.

"Why tell me now?" I asked finally. The water in my chest had gone cold, which was better. Cold water you can swim in. "Twelve years. Why open the vault today?"

"Because of the ledger," he said. "The real one. The army has it — or Ashe has it, which comes to the same thing — and it anchors the entire case. Every name, every meeting, every debt marked *sedition* in my father-in-law's old shorthand. But a case built on that ledger alone is a case built on my father's record-keeping." He paused. "There's a second anchor. Corvel's original page. My confession. It's been renewed twice since — once in the fifth year, once in the ninth — each renewal witnessed by a magistrate. Three signatures spanning twelve years. It proves the crown has run informants inside the printers' guild continuously, manufactured sedition charges, and hanged an innocent man on manufactured evidence. It proves the machinery, Bram. Not just this instance of it. The *machinery*."

"And where is this page?"

"In Corvel's archive," Quill said. "Which traveled with Corvel, and Corvel retired to the capital, and the page passed to whoever inherited his dirty work." He looked at me across the candle, and his eyes were dry, and somehow that was the most terrible thing in the room. "Colonel Ashe inherited Corvel's methods. I'd wager my remaining buildings he inherited the page too. It'll be in his strongbox or his dispatch chest, and he will burn it the moment this is over, along with the guild ledger and every witness, starting with the ones who can't run."

* * *

I got up then. I walked to the window, because if I'd stayed seated I was going to say things to an old man that couldn't be unsaid, and despite everything — *despite everything*, I want that recorded — he had taught me to cut my first italic capitals, and he had paid for Tally's boots last winter out of his own purse, and mercy learned late is still mercy, my father would have said, and my father wasn't here to say it, which meant I got to be angry on his behalf too, and anger doesn't divide. It compounds.

"You informed on us," I said to the window. "Not twelve years ago. Last month. When the requisition came, when Verren started building her case — you talked. Someone gave them the shape of the guild, the structure, who to pull in first. That's why they went straight for the evidence room. That's why the arrests came down in *order*."

Silence behind me. Then, quietly: "Yes."

"They have Tally in a cell right now because somebody drew them a map of who matters."

"Yes." And then, because he was Quill, and precision was the only courage he had left: "I gave them names, Bram. I did not give them Tally. She's a child and an apprentice; she knows nothing worth sweating out. But I know exactly how little that excuse weighs, so I won't insult you by calling it one."

"What do you want?" I turned around. "You didn't drag all this up out of twelve years of silence just to confess. Old men don't confess for their souls. What do you want?"

He rose. It took him longer than it should have, and I noticed — really noticed, for the first time — what the cellar had left in his body, the particular stiffness, the way he favored nothing because everything hurt equally. He crossed to the shelf behind his desk and took down a bottle of plum brandy that had to be older than me and poured two measures, which was the most extravagant thing I'd ever seen him do.

"I want you to let me surrender," he said.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out wrong, more like a cough. "You want me to — what?"

"Surrender. Publicly. Loudly. Tomorrow." He pushed a glass across the desk toward me. I ignored it. "Verren's case needs a confessor, Bram. A guild master, a name people know, standing up in front of the garrison and admitting conspiracy — it will satisfy her orders to produce someone, it will fill her quota of guilt for a week, maybe two. While the army spends its attention digesting me, nobody is looking at the harbor. Nobody is counting laundresses at the market gate. You take the ledger out on the morning tide with the whole town watching a better story."

"And what happens to you?"

"I give them the confession they want. The one from twelve years ago, the true one, the whole of it, in writing, with my name at the bottom." He drank, steadily, like taking medicine. "A confession naming Corvel's methods, naming the cellar, naming the machinery — submitted as *my* crimes, in such detail that any court that reads it carefully will understand the crown held the pen. Ashe can't publish it and can't destroy it while I'm useful. And I am very useful, Bram, for exactly as long as it takes you to reach open water."

"They'll hang you."

"Afterward. Probably." He smiled, and it was the first honest smile I'd ever seen on him, and it was awful. "Pieter Voss's daughter is in Merrowfield now, with children of her own. Your apprentice sleeps in a cell tonight. Eleven names sit on a warrant with mine among them, and half those people are only alive and free because I've spent twelve years buying them time with the coin of other people's blood. Let me spend the rest of the account the right way around. Let me be the diversion instead of the leak."

"You're asking me to carry your death out of this town along with everything else," I said. "That's a lot of freight for one tide."

"I'm not asking you to carry it," Quill said gently. "I'm asking you to leave it here, where it belongs. That's rather different."

* * *

I walked back across Chandler's Lane in the last light, past shuttered shops and requisitioned doorways, past a redcoat sentry who barely glanced at a woman in a work apron, and I thought about justice. I had always imagined it as a clean machine — you feed in the truth, it stamps out the verdict, the plates align. That's what my father's trade had taught me: ink finds the level, the impression is exact, what's set is set.

But Quill had confessed to me in a candlelit room and asked for the gallows like a man asking to settle a tab, and I couldn't find the crime in it anywhere. He'd broken. Anyone breaks. He'd spent twelve years breaking smaller, in installments, trying to pay it back in the dark where no one could credit him, and my father had *let him*, had engineered the whole redemption like a proof correction, and now the bill had come due in the shape of an old man volunteering for the rope to buy a smuggling run three days' head start.

Justice, it turns out, doesn't stamp clean. It smudges. You pull the sheet too soon and the letters blur; you wait too long and the ink bleeds through the page. All you can do is choose which flaw you can live with reading.

Tomorrow I would go to Nessa Kade and tell her the manifest had grown heavier by one confession and one martyr, whether she liked it or not. Tonight I climbed through the gap in the fence behind the burned cooperage where I was sleeping, lay down on someone else's wood shavings, and stared at the roof beams until the moon crossed them.

And it was there, in the dark, that the thought finally surfaced — the one that had been circling me all evening like a gull over fish waste, waiting for me to hold still.

Quill had said the confession pages passed to Corvel's heir. Corvel's methods, Corvel's archive, Corvel's *page* — all of it flowing downhill through twelve years of magistrates into the hands of the one man in Vellsmark ruthless enough to use them.

Ashe had the page. Which meant Ashe had Quill's signature on file, the original, the true one.

Which meant that when Quill walked into the garrison tomorrow and surrendered, Colonel Ashe wouldn't see a repentant informer coming home to the fold.

He'd see a loose end, walking in on its own legs, asking politely to be tied off.

I sat up in the dark, heart going hard, and reached for my boots.
