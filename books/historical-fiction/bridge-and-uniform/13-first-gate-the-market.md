## 13. First Gate: The Market

There is a particular kind of lie that works best when it is almost true. My father taught me that, though he taught it with type rather than words: *never cut a letter that only resembles the letter you need. Cut the thing itself, and no one will look twice.* A forged glyph passes inspection not because it is bold but because it is faithful. The forger's error is always ambition — the extra flourish, the too-perfect curve.

I thought about that a great deal on the morning I put on Marta Sorrel's spare dress and became a laundress.

The dress was honest, at least. Grey wool, mended at both elbows, smelling of lye and other people's sweat, because it had actually been worn by an actual laundress who had actually sold it to me for coin she badly needed and questions she badly wanted to ask. I paid her enough not to ask them. The basket was honest too — real laundry, borrowed from Kade's crew, who owned garments that could make a billy goat weep. On top of the washing, wrapped in a sheet and flat as a prayer book, lay the bundle I'd sworn to carry: my father's second ledger in its raw state — matrices, proofs, the coded plates — plus every scrap of paper I dared move out of the shop's ashes-to-be. Not the original ledger; that still sat in Verren's quarters, behind a lock I didn't have and a conscience I couldn't predict. But Kade's terms were plain: bring something worth sailing with, or don't come aboard. This was worth sailing with. Barely. It would have to do.

The rest of the disguise was dishonest, and therefore required craft.

Laundresses pass through the market gate every morning of the world. Nobody searches a woman carrying forty pounds of other people's dirty linen, because nobody wants to touch it. That was my whole plan, such as it was: smell like the truth. But Sergeant Harl, who had stood the market gate since before my father died, knew every laundress in Vellsmark by name, by gait, and by the specific way each of them swore at him. He knew Marta Sorrel. Marta Sorrel was home with bruised ribs and a story about a cellar stair, and if Harl looked at my face longer than two seconds, the arithmetic would fail.

So I gave him nothing to count. Head down, shoulders rounded under the basket's weight — genuinely rounded, the thing weighed as much as a small anvil — hands red from an hour of scrubbing in cold water for authenticity's sake. I let my hair hang loose and greased. I practiced Marta's cough on the walk over until a dog followed me for two streets out of sympathy.

*Cut the letter itself,* my father said in my skull, in his own voice for once instead of mine wearing his. *Don't perform a laundress. Be one, briefly. You can stop being her on the other side of the wall.*

That was the trick of it, of course. Every disguise is a temporary suicide.

* * *

The market gate was not really a gate so much as a gap in a wall that had been a town wall back when Vellsmark had mattered enough to fortify. The army had thrown a timber barricade across it, a sentry box, and a chain, which was one more chain than the town had ever deserved. Beyond it, the morning market was doing what markets do, which is pretending nothing has changed while everything has. Cabbages were being sold. Prices were being haggled. Two women argued over eels with the passion of people who did not know or cared not to know that eleven of their neighbors' names sat on a warrant draft with gallows written after them in a clerk's patient hand.

Mine was one of the eleven. I had read it three days ago, chalked on a slate in the garrison yard where someone had been careless, or cruel, or both. *OKAFOR, B.* My own name in an army clerk's handwriting looked wrong to me, the way a stranger's hand looks wrong when it signs your father's name. The letters were correct. Everything they spelled was not.

Harl was at the chain, warming his hands over a brazier and terrorizing a fishmonger about the proper display of permits. Beside the sentry box, on a stool brought from her own kitchen, sat the other half of my plan: Mrs. Anthea Harl, the sergeant's wife, who held court at the gate most mornings because their rooms above the guardhouse were small and her husband was large and her curiosity was larger than both.

I had spent two evenings earning this. The first evening I had learned — from Tally, before Tally was taken, God forgive me for how casually I'd said goodbye that day — that Mrs. Harl collected gossip the way my father collected matrices: systematically, jealously, and against future use. The second evening I had planted a seed: a rumor, passed through a washerwoman I trusted, that the quartermaster's office had been quietly selling requisitioned flour back to the bakers it came from, and that names were about to be named.

You cannot buy a woman like Anthea Harl with money. Money insults her. You buy her with inventory.

She saw me coming — of course she saw me coming, she saw everything coming, probably saw the revolution coming years ago and had opinions about the seating arrangements — and her eyes did the quick arithmetic of strangers: face unfamiliar, basket genuine, gait tired, elbows patched. Working class, working hands, unknown quantity.

"New girl at the Guildhall wash-house," I said, before she could ask, setting the basket down with a grunt that was entirely sincere. "They've sent me over the bridge route since the quay's locked."

"Mm," said Mrs. Harl, in the tone of a magistrate opening a file. "Never seen you."

"They don't let you see them, do they." I rubbed my lower back, honestly. "Marta Sorrel's down with her ribs. I'm filling in till she's walking straight."

"And where would a fill-in girl be from, then?"

Here was the moment the whole structure stood or fell, and I made the choice my father would have made: I stopped forging and told the truth, or a piece of one. Because the piece of truth I had was better than any lie I could build.

"Copper Street," I said. "My family kept the print shop there. Before."

Her face changed. Not softened exactly — recalculated. Everyone in Vellsmark knew about the Okafor print shop; everyone knew it stood sealed with army wax on the door like a tomb, and everyone had a theory about why. Grief was currency here now, and worse than that, it was *verifiable* currency. She could see it was real on me the way you can tell real lead from painted wood by the weight.

"Ah," she said. Quietly. "The printer's girl."

"There's no girl anymore," I said, and let my eyes go to the middle distance, which required no acting whatsoever. "Just the washing."

Mrs. Harl looked at me for a long moment. Then she did the thing that saved my life, though she'll never know it: she turned to her husband and said, "Harl. The girl's from the Okafor shop. Let her through, for pity's sake, the linen won't carry itself and you're not going to search a dead man's daughter."

And here is the wry note I promised myself I'd keep in this account, because without it I would have to feel the whole weight of it plainly: Sergeant Harl waved me through the chain without touching my basket, and the entire fortress of my planning — the cough, the grease, the rehearsed Marta-isms — went unused, defeated in four seconds by a dead man's reputation. My father, who had been in the ground for two years, smuggled me past an army checkpoint through sheer accumulated decency. I have since decided this is the most effective piece of work he ever did, and I include the entire Okafor Grotesque in that assessment.

I did not look back at the gate. Looking back is how they catch you in stories, and I had resolved to behave as though I were being watched by a particularly critical reader.

* * *

Inside the wall, the town continued its masquerade. Soldiers moved through the market lanes in twos and threes, not looting, not quite policing — *occupying*, in that particular way that consists mostly of standing where things used to stand freely. A corporal had taken over the fountain corner where the ballad-sellers used to work, and stood there with the profound satisfaction of a man who has always wanted a corner and finally been issued one.

I crossed the square with the basket, dumped the laundry at the Guildhall wash-house door — a real delivery, completing the alibi, and the wash-house mistress barely glanced up, which tells you everything about how interesting laundresses are — and walked out the far side with empty hands and a hammering heart.

The garrison records room occupied the old customs house, because the army, like mold, expands into whatever space is dampest and least convenient. I knew the building from childhood; my father had printed the harbor ledgers for the customs service for twenty years, and I had spent whole summers delivering reams of paper and eating plums off the tree in its yard while the clerks pretended not to notice. I knew which windows faced which rooms. I knew the yard door stuck unless you lifted the latch and pushed at the same time, a flaw I had exploited at age nine to steal a sheet of fine rag paper, and which I doubted the army had fixed, because armies fix walls and never doors.

But knowing the building was not the point. The point was to learn the room — the evidence vault, where Doss had carried the ledger, where it had vanished from, where whatever remained of the case against eleven names lived in its official form. If the theft from the vault meant the ledger had moved upward, I needed to know the shape of the machine that moved it. Who signed things in and out. Which clerks worked nights. Where a record room kept its real records, as opposed to the ones it showed people.

So I did the thing that felt most dangerous and was therefore safest: I walked in the front.

Not quite the front. The side entrance, where tradesmen delivered, where a woman with a basket and a bill could pass for commerce. I carried a genuine laundry invoice for the officers' quarters — Kade's forger, a melancholy ex-clerk named Pell, had drawn it for me, and it was beautiful, better than the army's own invoices, which I noted for later — and asked, in the flattened voice of a woman who wants only to complete her errand, whether the officers' washing went in through here or round the back.

The sentry, young and bored and cold, directed me around the building with the warmth of a signpost. And the route he sent me — around the back, past the yard — took me directly along the records room's outer wall, where the windows sit low, barred, and, in the third one from the yard corner, shuttered but not latched, because a hinge-pin had been pulled sometime that week and no one had noticed. I noticed. I measured the gap with my eyes the way my father measured a countersink: once, completely, forever.

I also counted steps from the yard door to the yard corner to the window. Eleven, fourteen, nine. I noted the sentry's circuit — he passed the yard every quarter hour, and lingered nowhere, because the yard smelled of the privy and he was seventeen. In and out in ninety seconds. Enough time to lift a shutter, see nothing useful, and curse. Enough time, with a pry bar and darkness, for more than seeing.

It was going well. That should have warned me.

* * *

Things had been going well for nearly ten minutes when I rounded the corner of the customs house onto the front lane and saw Clerk Doss standing in the doorway of the chandler's shop across the street, and my whole body forgot how bones work.

Doss. Tally's bundle-carrier. The man whose name was stitched through half the guild's misfortunes like a bad thread — the clerk who hated the army more than he loved it, according to my notes and hopes, and who had not yet answered my messages because I had not yet dared send any. He stood there in his army-grey clerk's coat with his collar up, doing nothing suspicious whatsoever except existing in my line of sight, and the effect on me was absurd. Ten minutes earlier I had talked my way past a military checkpoint using my dead father as a passport, and my pulse hadn't climbed past conversation pace. Now a soft-bellied clerk in a doorway turned my knees to wet rope.

Because exhilaration is a liar, that's why. Passing the gate had felt like competence. Seeing Doss reminded me what competence was *for*: a girl with a false name walking toward a man who could end her with one raised finger, in a town where my true name was already chalked on a slate with rope drawn after it.

He saw me. Of course he saw me; I was staring at him like a woman transfixed, which is to say like a woman caught. His eyes went through me the way a clerk's eyes go through a page — cataloguing, filing — and then stopped, and came back, and narrowed slightly.

"Bram Okafor," he said, very low, crossing the street with the unhurried gait of a man who had decided panic could be scheduled for later.

Every instinct I owned voted to run. I outvoted them, mostly because running would have confirmed whatever he was filing. I shifted my empty basket to my hip and became a laundress again, briefly, thoroughly — dull-eyed, tired, mildly inconvenienced by soldiers in general and this one in particular.

"You're mistaken," I said. "I do the Guildhall washing."

"I set the type for your father's trade bills for six years," Doss said. "You have his hands. Anyone who's watched him work would know them." He stopped an arm's length away, and his voice dropped further. "And anyone who's watched *you* would know you don't walk like a laundress. You walk like you're measuring the street."

He wasn't wrong. I had been measuring it. Eleven, fourteen, nine.

"What do you want, Doss?"

"What do *you* want?" He glanced up the lane, down the lane — a clerk's glance, checking margins. "A dead man's daughter, disguised, inside the wall, outside the records house, three days before the tide. I can add. Quill taught me addition before he taught me regret."

My chest did something complicated at Quill's name — fury and pity in one braid, as usual, with no way to wear either. "Careful," I said.

"No," Doss said. "That's just it. I've been careful for eleven years, and careful is why Ashe trusts me, and Ashe trusting me is why I know things that would turn your hair white, and I am *tired*, Okafor." Something moved behind his face that I recognized, having seen it in mirrors lately: a man discovering that the self he'd been performing had rotted away underneath while he wasn't looking. "You've been leaving word at the wash-house asking after papers. Very clumsy. Very brave. I'm answering, before you get yourself chalked onto a worse list than you're already on."

The lane tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. "Then answer."

"Not here." His hand went inside his coat and I confess I nearly died of fright before it emerged holding not a pistol but a stub of pencil and the back of a requisition form, because of course Doss carried paperwork into his treasons; he was a clerk down to the marrow. He wrote four words, folded the paper once, and pressed it into my basket between the folded linens with the deftness of a man passing a bribe, which, in a sense, he was.

"Tonight," he said aloud, in the ordinary voice of a soldier giving directions to a tradeswoman. "The old Custom House yard door sticks. Lift the latch and push."

And he touched his cap, turned, and walked back across the street into the chandler's shop, leaving me standing in the lane with an empty basket, a hammering heart, and a note I didn't dare read until I was three streets away, behind the tannery, where the stink covers everything including fear.

Four words, in a clerk's neat, cramped hand:

*The ledger travels tonight.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings refused to mean anything. The ledger — Verren's ledger, the original, the book with eleven lives sewn into its pages including mine — was not sitting safe in the captain's quarters awaiting some inscrutable officer's mercy. It was moving. Tonight. Out of reach of anything I could plan for, into whatever strongbox or saddlebag or burning barrel Colonel Ashe's judgment decreed.

Kade sailed on the morning tide. The tide did not negotiate.

I stood behind the tannery with the note shaking in my hand and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the plan has died, that everything I had cased and counted and memorized — eleven steps, fourteen, nine; the unlatched shutter; the ninety-second circuit — had just become useless, and that I had perhaps one night left, one door that sticks unless you lift the latch and push, and exactly one asset inside those walls:

A tired clerk who had just spent his courage without telling me the price.

I smoothed the note, folded it small, and swallowed it dry. Paper, my father always said, is only lead waiting to happen — and somewhere across the yard, behind the low barred window with the missing pin, the records room of the Vellsmark garrison waited for me to come and cut.
