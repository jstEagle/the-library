## 14. Doss's Price

I waited three hours in the back room of the Drowned Loom for a man I intended to threaten, which gave me ample time to reconsider my methods and no time at all to improve them.

The Drowned Loom was a tavern that had drowned some years ago in everything but name. Its wine tasted of the river it wished it had joined, and its clientele consisted of dockhands too tired to fight, widows too tired to weep, and clerks too tired to go home to wives who asked where the army's silver spoons went. Clerk-kind, in short. My quarry's natural habitat.

Doss came in at the fourth bell, shaking rain off his shoulders like a dog that had learned the gesture from watching soldiers shake off responsibility. He was smaller than I remembered from Tally's description — most bullies are, once you see them carrying their own umbrella — with ink-stained cuffs he'd tried to scrub and failed, and the particular stoop of a man who has spent his life making himself shorter in doorways so no one notices him entering or leaving.

He ordered cheap gin and took the corner table, because corner tables are where men sit when they are conducting business they'd deny under oath.

I let him take two sips before I sat down across from him.

"Master Doss," I said. "You don't know me."

His eyes went from my face to the door to the gin to my face again, doing arithmetic. "Don't," he said, which covered a great deal of ground: *don't sit there, don't know what you mean, don't ruin this for me.*

"My name is Okafor."

The arithmetic stopped. I watched the sum come out wrong behind his eyes — not the answer he'd been carrying around all week. The Okafors were on a list now. A list with his office's seal drying on it. And here was an Okafor sitting down at his table like an old schoolfriend, smiling like we were about to split a pie.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," Doss said quietly.

"Both run in my family." I set my hand flat on the table between us, palm down, the way my father used to before he made a point about kerning. "But I'm not here about the list. I'm here about the bundle."

That did it. His hand jerked toward his cup and missed by an inch, knocking it over, and the gin spread across the table like a small cowardly flood. He watched it drip into his lap and didn't move to stop it.

"I don't know what you—"

"A wrapped bundle," I said. "Carried to the garrison four nights ago, by a clerk with ink on his cuffs who thought no one saw him because no one ever sees clerks. My apprentice saw you. She's eleven, Master Doss, and she remembers faces the way you remember requisition numbers, which is to say better than you'd find comfortable."

He mopped at the spill with his sleeve, uselessly, buying time with motion. "Even if that were true," he said to the table, "even if. You think you can walk in here and threaten an officer of the provisioning corps?"

"You're not an officer. You're a civilian clerk seconded to the garrison, which means they can hang you twice as fast and pay you half as much. And whatever you're doing with that ledger, it isn't in your orders. Which means someone above you wants it done quietly, which means *you* are the quiet part. Do you know what happens to the quiet part, Doss, when the loud part goes wrong?"

He knew. I could see him knowing. The quiet part goes into the river with its boots on, and the loud part writes a letter of condolence to nobody.

* * *

Here is what I had learned about fear in the weeks since the red coats came to Vellsmark: it does not arrive like weather after all. Weather comes from somewhere. Fear comes out of people, the way water comes out of a squeezed cloth, and what it leaves behind is whatever the person actually was, wrung dry. Some men wring out into cruelty. Some into courage they never suspected. Doss, as far as I could tell, wrung out into arithmetic. Everything was sums to him — risk against reward, one betrayal weighed against another on scales only he could read, and every scale rigged slightly in favor of breathing.

So I didn't threaten him more. Threats would just add weight to one side. Instead I put something on the other side of his scales.

"I have a proposition," I said.

"Blackmailers don't usually offer propositions."

"Blackmail takes and gives nothing. This is trade." I reached into my basket — a laundress's basket still, because the disguise had cost me a morning of gossip with a sergeant's wife and I intended to get its full value — and laid a folded paper on the wet table. The gin wicked up into it immediately, blooming brown at the edges, but the writing underneath held. I write a fair hand; it's the first thing they teach you in a print shop, before they teach you anything else.

Doss unfolded it with the wariness of a man who had seen documents kill people. Which, to be fair to him, he had.

It was a discharge. Full honorable release from seconded service, citing a clerk's rheumatism — I'd invented the rheumatism, but clerks always have rheumatism, it comes free with the chair — signed in a captain's hand that I had copied from a requisition form Tally had charmed out of a drunk quartermaster, countersigned with a seal impression I had cut myself, in wax-red lead, over two nights of the finest work I have ever done and will ever be ashamed of.

"That's—" Doss began, and stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud would have been admitting he wanted it.

"Complete," I said. "Convincing. And worthless unless you make it true, which brings us to the price."

"You want the evidence room."

"I want ten minutes in it. Alone. What I take or leave there is my affair."

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow morning this paper goes to Colonel Ashe with a note explaining where I got the captain's signature, and you get to explain to him why a typeface cutter can forge his officers better than his own records office can." I paused, and then, because cruelty is cheap and I wasn't sure yet how much of it I could afford, I added the real knife: "And Tally testifies about the bundle. In front of whoever is asking."

He looked at the discharge for a long time. Rain ticked against the Drowned Loom's windows. Somewhere behind the bar a woman laughed at something that hadn't been funny in years.

"You hate them," Doss said at last. It wasn't a question, and it took me a moment to realize he wasn't talking about himself.

"Yes."

"So do I." He said it the way a man admits to a disease. "Twelve years. Twelve years of carrying other men's signatures and smelling their wine on the paperwork. Do you know what I did before the army seconded me? I was a copyist for a shipping house. Honest work. Ink and ledgers and nobody hanging at the end of it." He turned the discharge over, examining my forged seal with a connoisseur's eye, and I felt an absurd flicker of professional pride. "This is good work. The serif on the V is wrong, though. Army captains write a blunt V. They're taught it."

"Noted," I said, filing the correction away with the strange gratitude of a craftsman taking criticism from a hostage.

"They'll hang the printers whether I help you or not," Doss said. "That's the part you don't understand. It's decided. The trial is theater. The ledger just tells them which seats to fill."

"Then ten minutes of my affair costs you nothing, doesn't it?" I leaned forward. "Unless what you're really saying is that you've already read it. Unless there's something in that book with your own name in it, and you're not guarding evidence, Doss. You're guarding yourself."

I had guessed. I want to be honest about that, in whatever account of this eventually gets printed on honest paper: I guessed, the way you guess at a letter by its shape before the ink dries. But the guess landed. I watched it land. His face did something complicated and graceless, the way faces do when a debt is spoken of in public for the first time.

"Everyone's name is in it," he said. "That's what a ledger is."

"Not mine," I said, and heard my own voice go thin, because that was no longer quite true either, was it? Mine was in the warrant draft, posted on the garrison wall in letters tall enough to read from a moving cart. Mine was in it now.

Doss almost smiled. It was the nearest thing to kindness I would get from him, and it was made entirely of shared damage. "Not yet," he said.

* * *

We talked terms for an hour, and I will spare you the haggling except to record what it taught me, which is this: an alliance built on mutual blackmail is not a rope between two people. It is a rope over a pulley, and every foot one of you pulls, the other rises or falls by exactly the same measure. You cannot stop watching the pulley. You cannot sleep. You spend the whole arrangement calculating whether the knot at the other end is the kind that holds or the kind that slips.

Doss's terms: the discharge papers delivered complete, plus fifty crowns, plus my silence about the bundle forever, plus — this one he said quietly, staring into his empty cup — assurance that if the ledger ever surfaced, any testimony he'd given against the guild would be attributed to coercion. He wanted his treason pre-forgiven, in advance, in writing, in a form he could hold.

My terms: access to the evidence vault between the second and third watch tonight, when the duty rotated and the corridor stood empty for roughly the length of a slow prayer. His key. No escort. No questions after.

And one thing more, which I added at the end, watching his face while I said it: the name of the officer the bundle had been carried *for*. Not Colonel Ashe — I didn't know to ask for Ashe yet; that knowledge was still lying ahead of me in a coffin, waiting. I asked only who received it. Who had given the order. Whose hands the ledger rested in now.

Doss hesitated over that one longer than over the treason.

"You understand," he said finally, "that knowing it changes nothing except how afraid you are."

"I'm already as afraid as a person can be and keep walking," I said. "There's a kind of peace in it. Tell me where the bottom is and maybe I'll stop falling."

He looked at me for a while then — really looked, the first time in the whole conversation — as if reassessing the goods. Then he tore the corner off a gin-stained bill and wrote, in the cramped backward-slanting hand of a man who signs things he regrets daily, a single word and a title, and pushed it across the table with two fingers, the way you push something across a cell floor.

I read it. I felt the bottom.

"Her," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Verren."

"She requested it personally. Sealed orders. Whatever she wants with that book, it isn't the case file." Doss drained the last of the spilled gin from the saucer, which told you everything about twelve years of provisioning corps, and stood. "Second watch. The east corridor, the door with the brass plate. If anyone stops you, you're a laundress for the officers' quarters and you're lost, which every soldier in that building will believe without question."

"One more thing," I said, as he pulled his coat on.

"Don't push it."

"The serif on the V," I said. "Thank you. It would have hung me."

He paused in the doorway with the rain behind him and the forged discharge buttoned inside his coat against his heart, which is where cowards carry the things they intend to become, and he gave me the smallest nod I have ever been paid in.

Then he was gone into the weather, and I sat alone in the Drowned Loom doing my own arithmetic, and the sums would not come out.

Because here was the trouble, and I turned it over and over until the candle guttered: Captain Ilse Verren had summoned me privately and warned me away from the investigation — that I had read as threat, or perhaps as protection. Verren had paused at my name on the tenancy papers with recognition flickering across her face like a lamp in a draft. And now Verren held the guild ledger in her personal quarters, under sealed orders, away from her own colonel, away from the case she was supposedly building.

Officers who hide evidence do it for three reasons, my father used to say, cleaning his knives at the workbench while I handed him gravers too big for my hands. Because they're stealing it. Because they're selling it. Or because they're keeping it from being stolen.

Three reasons. Two of them meant I was walking into the garrison tonight to rob a predator. One of them meant something I couldn't yet afford to believe.

I finished the sergeant's wife's laundry delivery — cover is cover, and a laundress who skips her rounds gets remembered — and then I went home through the dark streets of occupied Vellsmark, past the shuttered guildhall, past the wall where the warrant drafts fluttered, and I sharpened my father's cutting knife and packed the forged key-copy wax and rehearsed my lost laundress act until midnight.

At the second watch, the east corridor of the garrison smelled of tallow and wet wool, and the door with the brass plate stood exactly where a coward had promised it would be.

The lock took my knife four seconds. The room inside was dark, and cold, and full of the army's idea of truth, stacked in crates.

And the shelf where the ledger should have been —

was empty.
