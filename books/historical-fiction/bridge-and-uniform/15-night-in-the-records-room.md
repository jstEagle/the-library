## 15. Night in the Records Room

Doss's key was warm from his pocket, which meant it had been warm from his hand, which meant his hand had been sweating, which told me everything I needed to know about the quality of my alliance.

"You'll want to count your steps," he'd whispered that afternoon, pressing the brass into my palm behind the tannery like a man handing off a stolen baby. "Forty-one from the side door to the evidence vault. Thirty-eight if the sergeant on duty has stopped for his supper, because then you can cut through the mess corridor."

"And how will I know if he's stopped for supper?"

"You won't smell mutton."

This was the intelligence network defending Vellsmark against the printers' conspiracy. I felt safer already.

I waited for the third bell, when the garrison's watch changed and the new sentries spent their first quarter-hour convincing themselves nothing interesting would happen tonight. Nothing interesting ever happened at the garrison; that was its whole architecture, brick and boredom stacked six courses high. My father used to say a lock is just a promise, and promises are made to be kept by the laziest person available.

I meant to break several promises before morning.

The streets between the tannery and the river gate were empty in the way streets are only empty under occupation — not sleeping-empty but held-breath-empty, every shutter a closed eye. I moved like a laundress late for no particular reason, because a woman hurrying at midnight is a confession, and a woman dawdling is furniture. I had learned that much from three days of being watched by men who thought watching was the same as seeing.

The side door Doss had described was exactly where he said, which surprised me, and unlocked, which did not. A clerk who hates the army more than he loves it does not waste courage on small things. He spends it all in one place, like a drunkard's wages.

* * *

Inside, the garrison smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular institutional despair of soup cooked for four hundred. I counted steps. Forty-one brought me to a door with a fresh lock plate — new brass, bright as a boast — and Doss's key turned it without complaint.

The evidence room was colder than it had any right to be. Someone had decided seized property should be stored like a corpse: dry, dark, and unvisited. The army had taken the guild's world and shelved it alphabetically. Press stones. Reams of paper gone damp at the edges. Boxes of type sorted into trays by some quartermaster who clearly believed letters were ammunition, which, in fairness, we had always thought so too.

And there, on the second shelf, where Doss swore the ledger sat wrapped in oilcloth — an absence. A rectangle of cleaner wood, dust shadowed around a space the size of a large book.

I stood very still and listened to my own hope die quietly, like a candle finding the bottom of its wick.

No. Not quite dead. Moved, perhaps. Misfiled. Armies misfile things constantly; their whole empire runs on paper nobody reads. I pulled open the cabinet beneath the window. Drawers of depositions. A box of confiscated pamphlets I recognized — Quill's presswork, his italics unmistakable even in the dark, that slight forward lean he gave every letter like a man perpetually about to stand up. Empty wrappers marked *EVIDENCE — GUILD LEDGER* in a clerk's careful hand, folded flat, the way you fold a wrapper after the thing inside has been carried elsewhere.

Elsewhere.

I checked the manifest nailed inside the door, because armies write down everything they do wrong. The entry was there in the same clerk's hand: *Guild ledger, one volume, removed [date] by order of Capt. I. Verren, for secure keeping.*

Verren.

I read it twice, hoping the letters would rearrange themselves into someone else's name. They declined. Captain Ilse Verren had taken the ledger out of the evidence vault and put it in her own quarters — the private rooms above the guardhouse, behind a door with her rank painted on the glass.

Here is what I knew about Captain Verren, arranged in the order I had learned it:

One. She had overseen the requisition of my home and paused at my name on the tenancy papers like a woman stepping on a stair she remembered wasn't there.

Two. She had summoned me, fed me tea out of army stores, confessed an old debt to my family, and warned me away from the investigation with a face that managed to be both kind and immovable.

Three. She now personally possessed the single object that could hang eleven people, myself included, and she had moved it closer to her own bed.

Despair is supposed to feel like falling. This felt more like the moment on ice when you realize the crack was under you the whole time and the sound you heard was yesterday's.

Because here was the terrible arithmetic: if Ashe's people had moved the ledger, it was leverage, and leverage could be traded, and traded meant bargains, and I understood bargains. But if Verren had moved her — Verren, who owed my family bread from a famine winter, who looked at me like she was reading a letter she'd written long ago — then the ledger was not leverage. It was *protected*. And protection cuts two ways. Either she was keeping it safe to bury — to keep names out of print, out of trials, out of the light — or she was keeping it safe from being buried.

I didn't know which woman stood behind that painted-glass door. And the difference between them was the difference between my life and my death, delivered with identical courtesy.

* * *

I should have left. Every sane instinct I owned — and I own several; I keep them in good condition, like type matrices — said take the coded knowledge you have, walk out the side door, count forty-one steps back to your life. But my life was currently scheduled for a gallows sometime after the assizes, and sanity had been outvoted.

Her quarters were directly above the guardhouse. There was an exterior stair, because officers prefer entrances that remind the world they are officers, and Verren struck me as a woman who took stairs rather than corridors — fewer salutes that way. The stair was iron, cold through my sleeves, and creaked exactly once, at the top, where the building itself seemed to clear its throat.

The door to her quarters was locked, but officers' locks are furniture locks, and furniture locks are cut by tradesmen, and I am a tradesman. My father taught me picks before he taught me italics. "A letterform," he used to say, holding a finished 'a' to the lamplight, "is a key that opens every eye that sees it." Then he'd wink, which ruined the gravity entirely, and show me how the counter — the little enclosed space inside the letter — was just a lock's keyhole wearing better clothes.

Four minutes. Her sitting room smelled of ink that wasn't printing ink — the iron-gall kind, officer's ink, the ink of orders — and under it, faintly, woodsmoke and something floral that might have been soap from before her commission. A camp bed, made with military corners. A table with a half-written report. And everywhere, everywhere, the tidy emptiness of a person who owns nothing she couldn't burn in an hour.

No ledger on the desk. Not under the bed. Not in the single trunk, which held uniforms and, tucked beneath them, a folded civilian dress that told me more about Ilse Verren than any deposition could — a woman who kept one set of clothes from a country that no longer existed.

Then I saw the strongbox.

It sat in the corner by the cold hearth, iron, old, and heavy enough that moving it would wake the guardhouse below. Not army issue — no stamp, no inventory number. Personal. It had the look of something carried through three postings and a war.

I knelt in front of it and put my ear to the seam, the way my father taught me, and turned the dial by feel, listening for the tumblers the way you listen for the click of type seating in its chase. First number. Second. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be; grief and terror had apparently negotiated a truce and assigned my fingers to logistics.

The third tumbler clicked. The lid rose an inch.

Paper. Stacks of it. I lifted the flap of the box fully and saw, on top, not the guild ledger but a thin bundle of letters tied with cord, and beneath those —

Boots on the stair.

Not the sentries' pattern — sentries walk like metronomes. These boots climbed slowly, with the specific weariness of a person coming off a twelve-hour shift who intends to fall into bed and resent the world on the way down. Verren's boots.

I had perhaps fifteen seconds and one exit, and the exit was past her.

There is a school of thought that says, when discovered, act as though you belong. I have never belonged anywhere strongly enough for that to work. So I did what the shop had trained me for instead: I became small, precise, and fast. The strongbox lid came down — I noted, with a thief's professional regret, that I'd seen enough of the letters' tops to recognize the handwriting on the addresses, and it was my father's. Letters in my father's hand, in Verren's strongbox. Filed for later, along with everything else tonight that refused to make sense.

The window was the answer. Of course it was; officers' quarters face the yard, and the yard was empty, and the drainpipe was the sort of generous nineteenth-century ironwork this town did well. I was through it and three rungs down when the door opened above me and lamplight spilled into the night like an accusation.

I did not look up. Looking up is a confession. I slid the rest of the pipe, landed soft in the mud of the yard, and walked — dawdled, furniture-like — toward the postern gate, my heart conducting a drumroll that would have embarrassed a regiment.

She saw me. I knew she saw me. From that window, with a lamp, at that angle, she had seen everything a person needed to see to identify a burglar: shape, height, the way I moved. And the guardhouse stayed silent behind me all the way to the gate.

That silence frightened me worse than shouting would have.

* * *

Doss found me at dawn where I'd told him to find me, in the back of the tannery among the hides, looking like a man who hadn't slept, which flattered him slightly.

"Well?" he whispered.

"The ledger isn't in the vault."

"I know that." He had the grace to look ashamed. "I found out yesterday afternoon. That's why I gave you the key anyway — I thought maybe you knew something I didn't. You printers always know something." He wrung his cap. "Verren took it. Personal custody. Nobody questions it — she outranks the paperwork."

"Who signs for evidence transfers?"

"She does. Or Ashe countersigns. Between the two of them they've got the whole case in their pockets and nobody knows whose pocket."

I sat down on a hide frame and let the truth settle over me like the tannery's smell: total, inescapable, and impossible to mention politely. Because here was the thing I hadn't told Doss, the thing I'd been carrying since the drainpipe like a stone in my apron:

If Verren wanted the ledger destroyed, it would be ash by now. She'd had it two days. One match, one report of accidental loss, one shrug — evidence misplaced in transit, regrettable, war. No one would ever question a captain who reported her own carelessness. It was the perfect crime and she hadn't committed it.

Which meant she was keeping it alive. Keeping *us* alive, eleven names on a warrant list, without saying so, without signing anything, in a strongbox beside letters written in my dead father's hand.

Or — and here the stone turned over in my chest — she was keeping it alive precisely *because* it could still hang us. An executioner needs the list intact until the work is done. Mercy and murder use the same filing system. From outside a locked door, they are indistinguishable.

"Bram," Doss said. "You went white as fresh stock. What did you see up there?"

"Nothing," I said. "Everything." I looked at him, this trembling coward who hated the army more than he loved it, and asked the only question that mattered. "Doss. Colonel Ashe. Does he trust Captain Verren?"

He laughed, short and unhappy. "Nobody trusts Verren. That's her armor. Ashe *needs* her, though — she's the honest face on the whole requisition. Town loves her. If Verren vouched for a hanging, Vellsmark would applaud." He paused. "Why?"

Because last night she watched a burglar climb out of her window and chose silence. Because she'd held my name on a tenancy paper like a keepsake. Because she owed my family, and debts are either paid or collected, and I could not tell, standing in that freezing evidence vault, whether Ilse Verren intended to pay mine or present the bill.

"Because," I said, "if the ledger's in her quarters, and Ashe wants it buried, and she hasn't buried it — then either she's the bravest woman in Vellsmark or the most patient enemy I have. And I need to know which before the tide leaves without me."

Doss shifted from foot to foot. Then, in the voice of a man spending the last coin of his courage, he said:

"There's something else. Ashe has been asking about you. Not to the soldiers — to clerks. Quietly. He wants to know if you've been near the print shop since the seal went on. And Bram — yesterday a squad went to the shop. They didn't search it." He swallowed. "They measured the floorboards."

The floorboards. Under which my father's matrices slept — and whatever else my father, in his endless posthumous cleverness, had hidden inside them.

Someone in the army already knew they were there.
