## 16. The Second Copy

Despair, I have found, is a lot like wet ink. It gets on everything you touch, and the harder you try to wipe it off, the worse the smudge becomes.

I walked home from the garrison in a grey drizzle that couldn't be bothered to commit to actual rain, and I rehearsed my failures in order, like a printer proofing a bad sheet. I had gotten into the evidence vault — good. The vault was empty of the ledger — bad. The ledger sat somewhere in Captain Ilse Verren's personal quarters, guarded by the one person in Vellsmark who had ever been kind to me and who was now, apparently, protecting the document that would hang me — worse. And I had three days until the tide, no plan, and hands that smelled like another woman's laundry soap because I'd spent the morning passing as a laundress, which even my hands didn't believe.

The print shop stood where I'd left it, sealed with army wax and a paper strip across the door that read *PROPERTY OF THE CROWN — EVIDENCE*, which struck me as the truest thing the army had written all year. My father's shop. Evidence. Evidence of what — of letters? Of ink? Of a man who'd spent thirty years making sure other people's words came out straight?

I went around the back, the way I'd gone around things my whole life.

* * *

Breaking into your own home has a particular flavor to it. Not quite theft, not quite grief. I pried the back window with the flat of my father's cutting knife — the one tool they hadn't inventoried because it lived in my apron pocket and not in the shop — and slipped inside onto the floor of the composing room, where I had learned to walk before I learned to read, because in our family the alphabet came before words.

Everything was under dust sheets, the way we'd left it before the requisition. The press wore its sheet like a shroud. I didn't lift it. Some things you don't look at twice if you can help it.

I lit no lamp. I knew this room by touch better than by sight — knew which floorboards complained, which ones lied about being solid, and which one, third from the type racks, under the sorting bench, hid the cavity where Father kept the duplicate matrices.

Here is what I had not done, on the night the requisition was announced, when Tally was asleep upstairs and Quill was arguing that the ledger should be burned and half the guild was arguing it should be hidden: I had not only packed. I had also buried. Chapter and verse, I could tell you what matters and what can burn, and I had put a full set of type matrices — the brass molds from which every font in the Okafor shop was cast — under those floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. It was sentiment, mostly. Matrices are years of work; Father had cut half of them himself, his initials filed discreet into the shoulder of each mold like a signature hidden in a handshake. You don't let the army have those. You don't let anyone have those.

I knelt on the boards, worked the knife into the seam, and lifted.

The oilcloth bundle was there. Heavier than I remembered. Which was strange, because I was the one who'd wrapped it, and I remembered exactly how much brass was in it.

Except I didn't, it turned out. Because when I peeled the cloth back in the thin grey light from the window, there were the matrices, right enough — and beneath them, wedged into the bottom of the cavity where I had certainly never put anything, lay a second book.

Small. Hand-sewn. Covered in the plain brown boards my father used for account books, corners worn soft from a pocket. I knew the binding before I knew anything else. I had bought that string for him. I had held the awl steady while he punched the holes, two winters ago, and asked him what he was binding, and he had said, *accounts,* in the voice he used when a question was finished.

My hands shook. That annoyed me. A typeface cutter's hands do not shake; it's practically the job description. But there is a difference between cutting steel and holding something your dead father sewed for you in secret, and my hands knew it.

* * *

He reached across death to help me. I want to say that plainly, because everything else I could say about him comes out sideways.

Tomas Okafor was not a warm man in the ordinary ways. He didn't embrace; he adjusted your collar, which was somehow more. He didn't say *I love you*; he said *that kerning is sloppy, do it again,* which, if you spoke the language, meant the same thing. When Mother died, he didn't weep where I could see. He cut a new font instead — a small, clean face, no flourishes — and printed her funeral leaflet with it, and never used the font again, and kept the molds oiled in their tray for the rest of his life. That was how the Okafors said things. In lead. In impressions. In what would hold.

So when I opened the little brown book and found it wasn't an account book at all — or rather, that it was, accounts being what they were in Vellsmark — I understood that he had been speaking to me for years, and I simply hadn't known the vocabulary yet.

* * *

The pages were filled with rows of characters that made no sense. Not gibberexactly — that was the maddening part. They looked almost like words. Letters from our own fonts, mixed faces, sizes jumbled together, arranged in short lines with numbers between them, the way a compositor might jot a job ticket. To any soldier who cracked this open, it would read as inventory notes. Shopkeeping. Boredom bound in brown boards.

To anyone else in the world, in fact, it would read as boredom.

But I am not anyone else. I am a typeface cutter's daughter, and I was raised on the differences between faces the way other children are raised on the differences between voices. Garamond's lowercase e has an angled crossbar; Caslon's is level. Our Dutch import has that long-tailed g you could hang laundry on. Every font in my father's shop was a distinct hand, as individual as a fingerprint, and my father — who could identify a printer's work from fifty feet by the set of his serifs — had just been handed the perfect cipher, waiting to be used.

Each line of the little book was set in a different font. And the font told you which ledger entry to read.

I sat down on the dusty floor of my ruined home, cross-legged like a child at lessons, and began to translate.

The system unfolded itself slowly, then all at once, the way a knot does. The body of each line — the letters themselves — was drawn from a substitution: our heavy blackletter stood for one set of names, the roman for another, the italic for a third. The numbers between were dates, but shifted — he'd used the founding year of the guild as an offset, the kind of arithmetic a clerk could do in his head and a soldier wouldn't bother doing at all. And the font of each line told you the *category*. Blackletter entries were payments. Roman entries were meetings. Italic — and here my throat closed, because I recognized the italic immediately, it was the funeral font, the one he'd cut for Mother, the one that existed nowhere else on earth — italic entries were names. Names of people. Names of people who had been protected.

I translated the first page in twenty minutes. By the second page I was crying, which made the ink-and-dust air taste salty, and I didn't stop working, because he would have wanted me to keep working, and because crying over a codebook is a luxury you schedule between tasks.

Page by page, my father assembled himself in front of me out of substituted letters.

He had recorded everything. Every payment the guild collected and where it went — not to sedition, mostly, though some of that, but to grain during the famine winter, to bribes at the granary gates, to doctors brought upriver under false manifests. Every meeting of the inner circle, dated and located. And the italic lines — God, the italic lines. Names crossed off one ledger and entered into another. Debtors whose debts he quietly absorbed. Sympathizers whose names he'd kept off the guild rolls so the rolls would stay thin if seized. Men and women who owed him nothing anymore because he had decided, privately, without telling a soul, that they wouldn't owe him.

Master Quill had shown me the great guild ledger and told me my father had a merciful hand. Quill didn't know the half of it. The great ledger was the public record. This was the true one — the one written in mercy's own cipher.

And then, near the middle, an italic line in Mother's funeral font, and I decoded it letter by letter with my breath held:

*Okafor, B.*

Not marked for the gallows. Marked as protected. Dated three years ago, the month before he died. He had put my name in the same list with all the people he'd saved, in the same ink, in the same hand, in the font he'd cut for my mother's farewell. As if I were simply one more soul he had decided to carry.

I put the book facedown on my knee for a while. The drizzle ticked against the window. Somewhere across the river, the army bugle called the change of watch, indifferent as weather.

"You absolute stubborn man," I said to the empty room. "You couldn't have left a key?"

But of course he had left a key. The key was me. The key was thirty years of apprenticeship at his elbow, learning to tell faces apart by lamplight. He hadn't hidden this from the world; he had addressed it to one reader, and folded the address into the letterforms themselves. Any soldier could seize it. Only I could open it. It was the most Okafor thing imaginable — a message that was also a lock, a lock that was also a legacy.

* * *

By full dark I had the whole book translated into notes in my head and pencil on scrap paper, and I sat back against the sorting bench and took stock, the way you take stock of a job that has come off the press wrong and must be assessed before you decide whether to fix it or burn it.

What the second ledger proved: everything. The conspiracy of the printers' guild — real, documented, dated, and far more benign than the army's version, which was precisely why it was dangerous. Payments to feed the hungry during a famine the Crown had caused through its own tariffs. Meetings to coordinate pamphlets calling for fairer grain prices — sedition only if truth counts as sedition, and in Vellsmark lately it does. A network of ordinary people helping ordinary people while the royal administration looked away and pocketed the difference.

What it did not prove: the army's crimes. Colonel Ashe's requisition graft. The officers paid to look away — and I had no names for that yet, only the shape of the suspicion, the sense that the investigation into the printers was itself the crime, a fire lit to cover a burglary. Quill's coerced testimony anchored the case against us; my father's book demolished the case's honesty; but the men who built the case would still walk free unless someone could show what they'd stolen and why. For that I needed the original ledger. Verren's copy. The one sleeping in an officer's quarters like a knife under a pillow.

Still. I spread the loose pages on the floor and looked at what my father had left me, and I confess I felt something I hadn't felt since the night the red coats crossed the bridge.

I felt armed.

They had the town, the guns, the gallows, and the original ledger. I had brass, ink, a cipher, and a dead man's stubbornness — and I would stack my inheritance against theirs on any market day. Ashe thought he was prosecuting a conspiracy of printers. He had no idea the printers had been conspiring for thirty years to make sure that when this exact day came, the truth would already be typeset.

There was a problem, of course. There is always a problem; problems are the interest we pay on plans. The matrices under these floorboards included the funeral font — the italic — and the funeral font exists in exactly one tray in the world besides this buried set, and that tray is in the shop of Tomas Okafor, deceased, sealed as evidence. Which means the cipher is crackable by anyone with access to both. If the army inventoried the type racks carefully — and army clerks inventory everything, it's their one genuine talent — some clever adjutant might someday notice that the mysterious brown book's letterforms match the mysterious unused font, and then my father's mercy becomes a map leading straight to everyone he tried to shield.

Which meant the matrices had to come with me. All of them. Tonight. Along with the little brown book, which was not staying in a hole in the floor one minute longer than necessary, because I had already lost one father to a fire and a fever season, and I would not lose him twice.

I rewrapped the bundle, heavier now by one small book and one enormous debt. Then I heard it.

Boots. Not the casual patrol shuffle I'd learned to tune out — the quick, purposeful kind, more than one pair, coming up the lane toward the back of the shop. I killed my candle stub with two fingers and went still among the dust sheets, listening the way you listen when breathing loudly could kill you.

Voices outside the window. A lantern's glow sliding across the wall like water.

"—the apprentice girl," someone said. "Sergeant says bring her in tonight. She was seen near the evidence store."

And Tally's name after it, spoken flat and official, the way the army says everything, as if the whole world were already confiscated.

I stood in my father's darkened shop with his last words in my arms, and the night, which had finally given me back a reason to fight, reached out and took hostage the only thing left in Vellsmark I loved more than the fight itself.
