## 17. Tally Taken

I had a rule, after my father died. Not a spoken one — he'd have laughed at it, or worse, written it down in that careful backslanted hand of his and filed it under something dry like *Household Maxims*. No, the rule lived in me the way scar tissue lives: silently, and forever.

*Everyone you love, you weigh.*

Not measure — weighing was different. Measuring told you how much space a thing took up in the world. Weighing told you what it would cost you when it fell on you. I weighed the font trays before I stacked them. I weighed the press before I moved it. I weighed Master Quill every time he came through the door, poor man, and found him lighter every week, which frightened me more than if he'd been heavy.

And Tally — Tally I weighed every morning when she came clattering down Bridge Lane with her hair escaping its braid and her sleeves rolled wrong, and every morning she came up light as a pamphlet, and every morning I let myself believe that light things don't break things when they fall.

That is the arithmetic of a coward. I knew it even then.

* * *

The morning they took her began like any other, which is to say it began with me lying about where I'd been the night before.

She arrived at the shop's side door just past seven, breathless from running, holding out a paper-wrapped bun like an offering at a shrine. "From Marta's. The good ones. She wouldn't take my coin."

"She always says that and then she counts it when she thinks you're not looking."

"I know." Tally grinned, gap-toothed still at eleven, and bit into the bun. Crumbs went everywhere. They went everywhere the way sunlight goes everywhere — without apology, without consequence. "Did you get it?"

"Get what?"

Tally stopped chewing. She looked at me the way apprentices look at masters who are pretending not to know something: with pity, mostly, and a little disappointment.

"Bram. You've got garrison ink on your cuff."

I looked down. There it was — a smudge of army blue-black, the cheap tallow-based stuff they used for requisition forms, distinguishable from proper printing ink the way ditchwater is distinguishable from river water. I'd scrubbed my hands twice and never thought about my sleeves.

"It's nothing," I said. "An errand."

"For the guild?"

"An errand," I said again, and put weight on it, the way you set type — firmly enough to hold, gently enough not to crack.

Here is what I did not tell her, because telling her would have made her part of it, and part of my rule — the coward's rule — was that the people you loved stayed unweighed, unmeasured, outside the ledger entirely. I did not tell her that I had spent the evening in the garrison records room with Doss's key warm in my pocket, standing before an empty shelf where the guild ledger should have been. I did not tell her that the ledger had been moved to Captain Verren's private quarters, and that this fact had rearranged the entire world inside my chest, because Verren — Verren who ate at our table during the famine winter, who taught me to whistle through a blade of grass, Verren who *knew me* — was keeping the book that could hang us all under her own bed. I did not tell her about the second ledger either, the partial one I'd found two nights ago beneath the floorboards, tucked in with Father's spare matrices — names and sums punched into the lead itself in a code only someone who cut letters for a living could read. My father reaching across death to hand me a weapon.

And most of all, I did not tell her that on the posted warrant draft outside the provost's office, between *Hessik, Alden* and *Imre, Pella*, sat the words **OKAFOR, BRAM — sedition, theft of military property** — that I had read my own name in army stencil and felt not fear exactly but a strange professional shame, as if they'd set my name badly, in a face too bold for the crime.

So instead I said, "The oven needs banking and Quill comes at noon. Mind the shop."

"I always mind the shop."

"You mind it beautifully."

She rolled her eyes at me — eleven-year-olds are fluent in contempt — and I went out the back toward the river to think, which is what I called it when I couldn't breathe inside walls.

* * *

I was gone perhaps three hours. I have done that arithmetic ten thousand times and it never comes out kinder.

When I came back down Bridge Lane, the shop door stood open, and open doors on that street meant one of two things: carelessness or soldiers. The Okafor print shop had never once in twenty years been careless.

Two privates stood in the street looking embarrassed, which should have warned me — embarrassment is not the uniform of men doing their duty; it is the uniform of men watching something happen that they will later claim not to have seen. A sergeant stood in the doorway with a paper in his hand.

Inside, the shop had been turned over with that particular thoroughness armies bring to searching small rooms — drawers pulled and set back crooked, the font cases dumped into overlapping silver rivers of type across the floor, Father's portrait askew on the wall. Tally stood against the composing stone with a soldier's hand on her shoulder, not gripping hard. She wasn't crying. That was the worst of it. She looked at me with fierce, furious dignity, chin up, and mouthed one word: *nothing.* They'd found nothing. Of course they'd found nothing; I'd hidden everything worth finding under her feet.

"You're Bram Okafor," the sergeant said. Not a question. He was young for the rank and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"I'm the tenant. This is my apprentice."

"The girl says she lives here."

"She does." My voice came out level. I remember being proud of that and hating myself for being proud of anything just then. "Which makes whatever your business is my business."

"My business is theft of military property." He held up the paper. "Report received this morning. Witness places a child leaving the garrison records annex on the night of the ninth, carrying a wrapped bundle consistent in size with a bound ledger volume."

The floor did not move. I made very sure the floor did not move.

"That's absurd," I said. "She's eleven."

"Colonel Ashe's office doesn't grade by age." He said the name Ashe the way you'd say *weather coming in* — no opinion offered, just conditions reported. "The girl will be held at the garrison until questioning concludes. Standard procedure."

"Questioning," I said. "A child."

"Sweated testimony is still testimony." He almost — almost — looked sorry. "There's a form you can sign acknowledging custody. It means nothing legally. Some families find it helps to sign anyway."

I signed it. My signature has been praised by three separate master printers for its evenness of stroke, and I produced the most even signature of my life while my whole body screamed at me to drive the pen into his throat. Then they took her out to the wagon, and she twisted once in the soldier's grip to look back at me, and she didn't cry then either, and I understood with total clarity that she wasn't being brave for herself.

She was being brave so I wouldn't feel bad.

Eleven years old, and she already knew my face well enough to manage it.

* * *

Quill found me an hour later sitting on the composing stone where Tally had stood, sorting type by feel because my hands wanted employment and my head refused to give them anything better. Lead is honest work. Lead weighs exactly what lead weighs.

"They'll sweat her gentle at first," Quill said. He sat down beside me with the groan of a man whose body has been negotiating surrender for years. "Questions about the shop, about you, about comings and goings. Children tell soldiers everything eventually. It's not cruelty. It's supply and demand."

"She saw Doss carry the bundle to the garrison. She told *me* that, weeks ago, plain as anything. And now there's a witness putting a bundle in *her* arms going the other way." I heard my own voice go flat and cold. "They've inverted it. Someone built this."

"Ashe builds everything," Quill said quietly. "It's why they keep him."

I set a piece of type down very carefully. "You informed on the guild once. Under torture, you told me. Years ago." I watched him flinch and kept going, because grief makes you cruel and I hadn't yet learned to ration it. "What does a person say under questioning, Quill? What does an eleven-year-old say?"

"Anything they're told to say. Anything that stops it." His old hands folded together like pages closing. "Bram. Listen to me. Whatever she says about you — whatever name comes out of her mouth in that room — it will mean nothing except that she wanted to live. Do you understand? Whatever she gives them—"

"She won't give them anything."

"Everyone gives them something."

"*She won't.*"

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Your father said the same thing about me."

I had no answer to that, so I sorted type. The letter **g**, the letter **h**, the letter **i**. Somewhere out on the river a barge horn sounded, long and low, and I thought: *the tide.* Three days. Kade sails on the morning tide in three days, and I have a coded ledger under my floorboards that proves a conspiracy but not the thefts, and a stolen original sitting in a captain's quarters, and now, now, a hostage wearing my second-best coat.

The mission had doubled in my hands like a cell dividing. Before, it had been one thing carried — the ledger, the truth, my father's last work — out past three gates. Now there were two weights on the scale: the book, and the girl. And here was the arithmetic I could not escape, the sum I kept doing and redoing on my fingers like a child: the ledger might save the district. But only a bargain could save Tally. And bargains with Colonel Ashe's office were paid in exactly one currency.

Names. Confessions. Other people.

* * *

I went at dusk, because dusk is when sentries change and clerks grow careless and the lamplighter works his way up from the bridge end of town. I did not go as a laundress this time; the market gate had begun remembering faces. I went as a widow — black shawl, black gloves, Father's funeral veil, which still smelled faintly of candle wax and which I wore like a woman wearing her own future.

Doss met me behind the cooperage, wringing his hands, which for Doss was simply how hands rested.

"You can't come near the annex anymore," he whispered. "There's a watch posted. Your name came up."

"My name comes up in every conversation in this town lately."

"Bram—" He glanced over his shoulder. "The girl. Tally. She's in holding block C, not the main cells. Ashe moved her himself. Do you know what that means?"

"It means he wants her somewhere questions can be asked without paperwork."

"It means he wants her somewhere *you* can be drawn," Doss said. "He knows whose apprentice she is. He knows whose door the witness pointed at. He's fishing, and he's using a little girl for bait, and everyone in that garrison knows it and nobody will say it because the word for that is *procedure*." His voice cracked on the last word, and for one strange moment I saw Doss clearly — not the gray little clerk, but a man who had spent years watching what procedure fed into the mill and grinding his teeth about it in private. "What are you going to do?"

"What anyone does with bait," I said. "Refuse to bite. Or bite so hard the fisherman loses a finger."

"There's another thing." He hesitated, and I learned later that everything Doss hesitated over cost somebody dearly. "The questioning is set for tomorrow morning. Ninth bell. But there's a form — an intake review — that crosses Verren's desk tonight. She signs off on holding arrangements. If anyone in that garrison could slow the process, move the girl, lose the paperwork—"

"Verren." I said the name and it tasted like a knife I'd swallowed sideways. "Why would Verren help me? She's the one sitting on the ledger like a hen on a bomb."

"I didn't say she'd help you," Doss said. "I said she signs the papers. What a woman does with her signature is between her and her commission."

He melted off into the dark the way cowards do — quickly and in a useful direction — and I stood behind the cooperage with the veil scratching my cheeks and thought about Verren. About the famine winter, and the woman who had eaten our bread and taught me grass whistles and left in spring with her debt unpaid and her eyes wet at the gate. About the way she'd paused at my name on the tenancy papers. About the ledger under her bed, and what names were in it — because I had seen the list, and officers' names were on it too, officers paid to look away while Vellsmark starved politely.

Maybe she was guarding it. Maybe she was *reading* it.

There was a difference, and the difference might be Tally's life, and I had until ninth bell to decide whether to bet a child on it.

* * *

I went home. Home — the burned-down shell of a phrase, the shop with its type spilled across the floor like the entrails of a language. I banked the cold oven because Tally would have wanted it banked, out of habit, out of the shape of her absence, and then I knelt on the floorboards and pried up the loose plank with my father's cutting knife and took out the matrices, all of them, and laid them in the firelight.

My father's code. Punches in reverse, depths for vowels, serifs for sums. Only a typeface cutter could read it, and reading it took me half the night, kneeling there, translating lead into names. Guild members. Debtors. Sympathizers. And columned alongside them — smaller, deeper, cut with a steadier hand than I ever managed — payments. Dates. Initials of officers.

Proof of the conspiracy. Proof of the bribes. Proof that Colonel Ashe had built his case on a fire he'd lit himself.

It proved everything, and it saved no one, because a document in my floorboards saves nobody. Documents only work when they travel. My father had known that too, I think — had cut this second ledger knowing it might be his daughter's cargo someday, knowing that the whole craft of the thing, the whole *meaning* of type, is that it exists to be carried: from hand to hand, press to eye, town to town.

"Measure the world in what can be carried," he used to say, trimming a letter, not even looking up.

I sat back on my heels among the matrices and finally let myself do the arithmetic I'd been dodging since the wagon pulled away. Not the coward's arithmetic. The real kind.

The ledger I needed was in Verren's quarters. The proof I *had* was under my knees. The ship sailed in two days, on the morning tide, and Nessa Kade did not wait for anyone, least of all for women who showed up late carrying causes instead of cargo. And between me and all of it stood a garrison, a colonel, a captain who owed me a childhood, and a girl of eleven in holding block C who would sit in a chair at ninth bell tomorrow and be asked questions designed to make her name *me.*

Guilt is not a feeling, whatever the poets say. Guilt is ballast. It sits low in you, below the waterline, where nothing can dislodge it, and it determines your draft — how deep you sit, how slowly you turn, whether you clear the shoals or tear out your bottom on them. I had loaded Tally into my hold the day I let her love me without telling her the price of it, and there was no port anywhere that could lighten me now.

But ballast does one other thing. Ask any sailor. Ballast keeps you upright in weather that capsizes everyone else.

I wrapped the matrices in oilcloth. I put them in the bottom of a flour sack, above the false seam where Kade's people taught me to hide the true cargo. Then I took out a clean sheet of paper, dipped a pen, and wrote in my best backslanted hand — his hand, inherited, exact:

*Captain Verren. You once lodged with a family that fed you when your own army would not. Tomorrow at ninth bell they begin questioning a child to reach that family's daughter. I am asking you to remember what bread costs. If the girl is harmed, I will assume your answer. B.O.*

I sealed it. I addressed it. And then I sat in the dark of my father's shop, holding a letter in one hand and a flour sack of revolution in the other, and realized I had just promised a serving officer of the Crown that if anything happened to Tally, I would consider Verren an enemy — which meant that somewhere between the oven and the inkpot, I had stopped being a fugitive hoping to escape.

I had become something with a deadline, a hostage, and a plan that required a captain to choose me over her own uniform.

Outside, ninth bell rang early — no, not ninth bell; I counted; it was only sixth — and down on the river, faint on the wind, came the sound of a ship's rigging singing in the night tide.

Two days. Two weights on the scale. One of them was sleeping on a plank in holding block C, and tomorrow at ninth bell, unless I moved faster than fear itself, the army was going to teach her what her silence cost.

I picked up the flour sack, and I went out into the dark to buy a child back from the Crown of Vellsmark — with a forged ledger, a dead man's code, and a letter addressed to the one woman alive who had the power to save us both, and every reason in the world not to.
