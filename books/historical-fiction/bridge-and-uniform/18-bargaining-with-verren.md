## 18. Bargaining with Verren

They took Tally at noon, in the way the army takes things — loudly, and without any interest in what it was actually taking.

I heard it from the type room: boots on the cobbles in that particular rhythm soldiers have, half march and half swagger, and then Tally's voice going up through the floorboards, high and furious, the way a kettle goes when you've forgotten it on the fire.

"She didn't take anything! She's *nine!*"

She's ten, I thought stupidly, from inside my own hiding place behind the font racks, and then hated myself for it, because correcting her age in my head while she was being dragged into the street was exactly the sort of useless precision my father had warned me about. Precision is a tool, he used to say, not a comfort. When the thing you're measuring is breaking, put the calipers down.

I put them down. Metaphorically. I hadn't actually been holding calipers; I'd been holding the coded matrices from under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heartbeat, and I stood very still between the racks of twelve-point and fourteen-point while the boots came into the shop and counted everything again, though they'd already counted it twice, because armies believe that repetition is the same thing as competence.

The sergeant in charge — not Doss, thank whatever small gods look after print shops, just some red-faced boy promoted past his judgment — read out the charge to nobody in particular, since I wasn't fool enough to show myself. Theft of army property. Suspicion of conspiracy. A child of ten years, accused of conspiring against the Crown, because she'd once carried a bundle of rags toward the garrison and someone with more rank than sense had decided the bundle looked heavy.

When they were gone, I sat down on the shop floor among the dust of my father's life and did the arithmetic.

They couldn't prove anything against Tally. They knew they couldn't. That wasn't the point. The point was that a child in a cell is a lever, and levers don't need proof, only purchase. And the hand on that lever belonged, ultimately, to Colonel Ashe — which meant that every hour Tally spent in the garrison was an hour Ashe spent tightening it, waiting for me to come running.

Which I would. Which he knew. Which meant the trap wasn't at the garrison door; the trap was the fact of the door itself, and my name already written on the lintel.

There was exactly one person in Vellsmark who could open that door without firing a shot. And she wanted something from me. She'd said so twice now, each time with the elaborate casualness of a woman pretending she hasn't already decided.

So I unwrapped the matrices, checked them one last time by candlelight — my father's coded names marching along the shoulders of the lead, legible to no living soul but me — and I wrapped them again, and I left them where no one would think to look, because the whole trick of what I was about to do depended on Captain Ilse Verren never learning precisely what she was bargaining for.

You can't lie well unless you lie generously. My father taught me that too, though I don't think he meant it as a lesson. He meant it as an epitaph for himself.

* * *

The cell block smelled of wet stone and old fear, which are nearly the same smell.

Verren kept to her own quarters after dark these days — everyone knew it, and everyone drew the obvious conclusion: the captain was troubled, or the captain was careful, or both. Her adjutant had stopped meeting her eye. Ashe's people watched her the way cats watch a door that has begun to move on its own.

She was reading when the knock came. Not the evidence inventories, not the warrant drafts. A book of river charts, bought years ago in a market she no longer remembered, its corners soft with handling. She had found she could not sleep without reading something that had nothing to do with Vellsmark, and there was very little left in the world that had nothing to do with Vellsmark.

"Captain." The sentry. "The printer woman. At the outer gate. Alone. Says you'll want to see her."

Alone. Of course alone. Verren set down the chart and considered, briefly, simply having her arrested — the girl was on a leaked list with eleven other names, and the paperwork would take ten minutes, and then this whole long uncomfortable business of recognition and debt would be somebody else's problem entirely.

She had considered arresting Bram Okafor perhaps forty times in the past fortnight. It was becoming a kind of habit, like biting the inside of her cheek.

"Show her to my office," Verren said. "Not the cells. If anyone asks, she's a civilian consultant on the inventory."

"And if the colonel asks?"

"The colonel," said Verren, "is asleep. Let's keep him so."

* * *

Her office was warmer than I expected, and tidier than any officer's I'd seen — maps squared to the desk edge, pens ranked like soldiers who'd actually been drilled. Only one thing out of place: a chair pulled slightly crooked from the table, as if someone sat in it often and always left in a hurry.

Verren stood behind her desk and did not offer me the crooked chair. Fair enough.

"You're either very brave," she said, "or you've finally run out of clever."

"There's a meaningful overlap."

Something crossed her face that on a civilian I'd have called a smile. On her it registered only as a loosening, quickly corrected. "The child," she said. "Tally Merrow. Apprentice compositor. You're here for her."

"I'm here because you sent word you wanted to talk." I let a beat pass. "And yes. The girl."

"I didn't arrest her."

"No. Your colonel's clerks did. But your signature released the warrant, Captain. I've seen enough of your hand to know it." I'd forged it twice by then, actually, but that seemed impolite to mention. "So we're both holding this particular rope."

For a moment neither of us pulled. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, a drunk was singing something patriotic and getting the words wrong, which struck me as the most honest thing happening in Vellsmark that night.

"Say what you came to say," Verren said quietly.

"The ledger."

The word landed between us like a dropped tray of type. She went very still — the stillness of a woman who has spent weeks being lied to by professionals and had learned to hear the specific silence where truth should be.

"You know where it is," she said.

"I know where it isn't. It isn't in your quarters. It isn't in the evidence vault. It isn't anywhere in the garrison, or you'd have closed the harbor by now." I watched her eyes and saw I'd guessed right, or close enough. "It's on a ship, Captain. It sailed into this conversation the same way it's going to sail out of your jurisdiction — on the morning tide."

That was the lie. Generous, as promised. The ledger sat three miles from where I stood, wrapped in sacking under Nessa Kade's galley floor, and I was betting my neck and Tally's that Verren couldn't tell the difference between a fact and a well-cut impression. We deal in impressions, in my trade. It's all ink and pressure, and if the pressure's even, the reader supplies the rest.

"That's a serious claim," Verren said slowly, "to make without proof."

"Everything serious in this town is being done without proof. That's rather the problem." I took a breath, and here came the part I'd rehearsed against the ceiling of my room until dawn. "So here's my price and my payment, laid out flat, the way you'd want it. The original ledger leaves on the morning tide, beyond anyone's reach — yours, mine, the colonel's. Gone. No trials built on it, no gallows fed by it, no leverage in anyone's hands ever again. In exchange: the girl walks free tonight, unmarked, and the harbor searches stop. You get what you've secretly wanted since the day you sealed my shop, which is for this case to quietly starve."

"And what," she said, "do you imagine I've secretly wanted?"

"To be the officer you were before you learned what this commission cost other people." I hadn't planned to say it. It walked out on its own. Her face didn't change, which was how I knew it had gone deep.

Silence again. Then, very evenly: "If what you say is true, you've committed treason, and I'm obliged to hang you."

"If what I say is true, then hanging me gets you a corpse and loses you the only copy of the only document that could clear eleven innocent names — including, Captain, one named Okafor, and one named Quill, and if you check your own precious famine records, I suspect you'll find the ledger holds a few lines about a certain junior officer whose men starved that winter while her requisitions fed a magistrate's cellar."

I hadn't known that for certain. I'd gambled it. Her knuckles whitened on the desk edge, and I understood I had not lost the bet.

"You've been reading my father's ledger," I said, softer now. "Or trying to. It's why you haven't burned it, whoever's told you to. You want to know what it says about you. Well — it says you owed us. And we paid that debt off feeding you thin soup through a famine winter, and I am here to collect the balance, Captain, and the collection is one small girl with ink under her fingernails."

* * *

Afterward — after the door closed on the printer woman and the office was hers again — Verren sat for a long time without moving.

The girl had lied to her. That was the first and clearest thing, and Verren distrusted herself for being sure of it. Twenty years of reading soldiers' faces had made her fluent in evasion, and Okafor's story had been smooth as poured wax and just as shaped. The ledger was not on any ship. The girl wanted her to think it was, wanted the harbor quiet, wanted the tide to belong to her. Everything about the bargain was a forgery — except the part about the famine winter, which had been spoken plainly, almost carelessly, the way people speak only what they're certain of.

So the daughter had inherited the father's hands and the father's habits both: true lines set clean, false lines dressed to match.

Verren rose and went to the window. Below, the garrison yard lay blue in the lamplight, and beyond the wall the river ran black, carrying on its surface the reflected lights of a town that would burn, one way or another, before spring.

Ashe wanted the ledger destroyed because it convicted him. She had understood that for days, the knowledge arriving the way bad news does — gradually, then all at once, then permanently. What she had only now admitted was the corollary: that letting the printers hang would bury it, and that she had gone along, filing her inventories, signing her warrants, telling herself procedure was neutrality. Procedure. The great armor of the mediocre.

And the girl across her desk tonight had looked at her — a marked traitor with a noose measured to her own neck — and offered a trade anyway, because a child was in a cell. There was a word for that arrangement of courage. Verren's mother would have called it character. Her instructors at the academy had called it a liability.

She thought about the soup. She had never told anyone about the soup — thin barley broth, and the printer father ladling her bowl fuller than his own while insisting it was an accident of the wrist. She had been nineteen and starving on officially sufficient rations, and the Okafors had fed her for a winter and never once mentioned it afterward, which was its own kind of unbearable generosity, because it meant the debt sat unspoken between them for years, accruing.

Tonight the daughter had named it aloud. Priced it. Collected.

Verren found, examining herself the way she'd examine a map, that she did not resent being collected. She resented only that it had taken a war's worth of rot to bring the collector to her door.

She returned to the desk, pulled a warrant draft toward her, and struck through Tally Merrow's name with a single ruled line. Release order: personal recognizance, Captain I. Verren, effective immediately. Then she rang for the sentry and gave instructions in a level voice: the girl to be delivered to the print shop within the hour, no escort past the door.

Then she sat back down and wrote a second order, and this one she hesitated over longer than the first, because it was the order of a woman doing the wrong thing correctly, or the right thing badly — she honestly could no longer tell the difference, which she suspected was the whole condition of being an honest officer in a dishonest army.

At dawn, the harbor would be searched. Every hull, every hold, every sacking-wrapped bundle on every deck from the customs quay to the shipwrights' mudflats.

If the ledger truly sailed on the morning tide, she would lose it — and good, whispered something in her that wore her mother's voice.

And if the girl had lied — if the ledger was still in Vellsmark, still moving through the town like a coal in dry straw — then the search would find it, and find her, and Verren would have arranged matters so that her own hands held the net at the exact hour it closed.

Either way, dawn would decide which of them had been merciful and which of them had merely been clever. Verren signed the order, blew out her lamp, and sat alone in the dark listening to the river.

* * *

They brought Tally to the shop door a little past midnight, deposited her like misdelivered post, and marched off without a word. She stood in the doorway glaring after them, hair escaping its braid, wrists ringed with the red marks of cord, and the first thing she said — before hello, before anything — was:

"They wrote down my age wrong. Said nine. I *corrected* them, Bram, I told them ten, and the sergeant wrote it down and I think that made it worse."

"It makes everything worse," I said, gathering her in. "That's what accuracy's for. Making things worse, beautifully."

She laughed into my shoulder, and then shook there, briefly, the small hard shaking of a child who has been frightened in a stone room and will not admit it. I held her and looked over her head at the dark river, where the tide was already turning, and thought about what I'd bought her with, and what the morning would demand as interest.

Because I'd seen Verren's face when I named my terms. I knew a forced line when I pulled one — I knew the tiny give in the paper where the impression is too deep, where the cutter pressed harder than honesty requires. Verren had agreed too smoothly. Somewhere under that level officer's voice, a second order was already drafted.

She would search the harbor at dawn. Of course she would. Duty demanded it, and Verren was a woman who'd rather break her own heart than her own regulations — unless she broke both at once, which I suspected was coming for her eventually, poor soul, the way weather is coming.

Dawn searched the harbor. Fine. Let it. The tide left two hours before sunrise, and Kade didn't wait for anyone — she'd sworn it twice, once soberly and once with feeling.

But Tally couldn't sail with me, and Quill was missing, and somewhere between here and the bridge checkpoint lay two gates, one coffin, and whatever Verren's real second order contained. I banked the stove, set the girl sleeping in my father's chair, and sat down to cut my last disguise before morning.

The knife felt light in my hand. Everything does, I've found, once you finally measure it against what can't be replaced.
