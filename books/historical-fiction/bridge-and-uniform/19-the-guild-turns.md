## 19. The Guild Turns

There is a particular smell to a guild hall the morning after fear has moved in. It's not the usual smell — ink and tallow and old wood polish — but something thinner underneath it, like a room where someone has been sweating with the windows shut. I'd smelled it once before, the winter my father died, when the creditors came through the shop taking inventory of a dead man's debts. Fear, I learned then, has a scent. So does shame. That morning they had both moved into the Weavers' Hall and made themselves at home.

I arrived early, because early is what you do when you're planning something illegal and want to look like you're planning nothing at all. The hall was half full — eleven printers, two binders, an ink-maker named Sorrel who owed his entire livelihood to my father's generosity and had never once said thank you in a register above a mutter. They sat apart from each other, which was itself a kind of confession. People who trust each other sit close. People who are deciding whom to sell sit where they can see the door.

Master Quill stood at the front, and he looked — there's no kinder way to put this — like a man reading his own eulogy and finding it inaccurate.

"The list is real," he was saying as I slipped in by the rain barrel. "Eleven names. I've confirmed six through—"

"Through what?" That was Hobb Marren, the binder, a big soft man whose hands could mend a folio so the tear vanished entirely. His voice had gone thin. "Through your sources, Quill? Your sources got us on that list in the first place, if you ask me."

Nobody asked him anything, which told me the question had been sitting in the room all night, growing teeth.

"I have served this guild for thirty years," Quill said.

"You served it right up until soldiers started nailing warrants to doors," said Sorrel. "Now it serves you, doesn't it? Now we're all standing in a row behind Master Quill while he decides how we die."

I want to be fair to them. I try, most days, to be fair to everyone, though I'll admit it's easier after breakfast. These were men who had spent their lives making things that lasted — books, ledgers, bindings sewn to outlive their makers — and now every solid thing they understood was being taken apart by people with rifles. Fear makes cowards of us all, but it makes *stupid* cowards of men who've never been afraid before. They don't know the terrain. They panic badly. They say things in rooms with thin walls.

Which is why, when Hobb Marren raised his hand like a schoolboy and said, "What about the Okafor girl?" — my stomach did not drop so much as it performed a slow, professional descent, like a typecase sliding back into its rack.

"What about her?" Quill said carefully.

"She's doing something." Hobb's face had the terrible earnestness of a man about to commit a sin he intends to feel good about later. "My wife saw her at the market gate Tuesday, dressed like a laundress, talking to the sergeant's woman. Talking *comfortably*. And Doss — the clerk — Doss was seen speaking with her near the tannery lane. You don't talk to garrison clerks unless you're buying or selling something."

The room turned toward me. Not all at once — first a few heads, then more, the way a fire catches along a row of drying sheets. I considered several responses. Denial was useless; Hobb's wife had eyes, and eyes don't perjure themselves. Outrage would only confirm I had something to be outraged about. Silence, my father always said, is the loudest thing a printer can publish.

So I said: "You're right, Hobb. I'm doing something."

The room went very quiet. Even the rain barrel stopped dripping, or perhaps I just stopped hearing it.

"And what," said Sorrel, "would that be?"

"Something that might save all our necks, if it works. Something that will certainly hang us all faster, if you keep discussing it in a building with three windows facing the street." I let that land. "But since we're trading confidences — Hobb, how much did you tell the army investigator last Thursday? Because he bought ale for you at the Anchor and Chain, and the army doesn't buy ale. It invests."

Hobb went the color of unbleached stock. "I never—"

"His name is Lieutenant Fenn. He writes everything down in a little book with a green cover. He's very thorough. My father trained me to notice thoroughness; it's a printer's habit." I looked around the room, at each face, giving each one the same gift: the chance to feel seen. "I'm not here to accuse anyone. I'm here to tell you that whatever any of you have already said, saying more won't make it better. The army doesn't reward loyalty. It rewards usefulness, and it uses up useful people last, not first."

"That's easy for you to say," Sorrel muttered. "You're not the one with a family in the district."

"No," I agreed. "I'm the one with a name on the gallows list. Forgive me if my perspective lacks variety."

Quill raised a hand before it could become a riot, which was generous of him, since a small part of me had been hoping for a riot. Riots are honest. Committees are where betrayals get their paperwork done.

"We should vote," Quill said. "On whether Bram's — undertaking — has the guild's sanction."

"On whether it has the guild's *silence*," I corrected. "Sanction implies you'd help. I'll settle for silence. Silence is cheap. You can carry it in one hand."

They voted. I stood outside by the rain barrel while they did it, watching the street, because a person planning to move stolen evidence past three checkpoints learns to watch streets even during guild meetings. Especially during guild meetings. When they called me back in, the tally was nine to four in favor of silence, with Hobb abstaining, which in the grammar of frightened men means *yes, but count me out of the consequences.*

I thanked them. I meant it, mostly. Nine silences is a fortune in a town full of ears.

I should have known better. I was raised on ledgers. I knew exactly how quickly a debt of silence gets refinanced into something worse.

* * *

Tally found me that evening at the shop — our shop, still sealed with army wax that we'd learned to peel and re-stick with a warm knife, a trick Tally invented and I chose not to ask how. She came in fast, the way she does when she's carrying news too heavy to walk with, and she didn't do her usual performance of checking the street twice first. That's how I knew it was bad. Tally checks the street when there's time. When there isn't, she just runs.

"They took Maren Ostrow," she said.

Maren Ostrow. Compositor, sixty-one years old, hands like driftwood, the woman who taught me to space a line by feel rather than measure because "the eye forgives what the rule doesn't." She used to slip me sugared plums during long press runs and claim they were for her rheumatism.

"When?"

"An hour ago. Soldiers walked right into her lodging house. No warrant shown, or if they showed one, nobody read it. They walked her down Cooper Street in irons, past the fountain, everybody watching from behind shutters." Tally's voice had gone flat, which is what happens to it instead of crying. "Bram. They walked her past the *fountain*. That's not arrest. That's advertisement."

Advertisement. Yes. The army had learned what I'd already feared: that Vellsmark's printers were held together less by loyalty than by arithmetic, and arithmetic can be changed. One old woman in irons recalculated everything.

We sat in the dark of the sealed shop, among the ghosts of fonts, and I felt the shape of what was coming the way you feel weather in an old scar. Somewhere across town, men who had voted nine to four for silence were looking at Maren Ostrow's empty chair and doing new sums. *She talked,* they'd think. *Or she'll talk. And I am not brave enough to be the last one silent.* Betrayal is contagious precisely because it feels like self-defense. Every man who sells a friend tells himself he's the last sale, the final betrayal, after which it stops mattering. There is no final betrayal. There's only the next one, each cheaper than the last.

"Tally," I said. "If anyone asks you about me — about where I go, what I carry —"

"I don't know anything," she said immediately.

"You know plenty."

"I know what I know," she said, with the stubborn precision of a thirteen-year-old who has recently discovered principles, "and none of it survives questioning. That's not lying. That's editing."

Despite everything — despite the coffin waiting, despite the tide, despite the stone of guilt I carried for having let her anywhere near this — I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, but it came out. "Your father would have made a fine printer."

"My father made a fine drunk," Tally said. "That's why I'm yours." Then, quieter: "Bram. What happens to Maren?"

What happens to Maren is that she sits in a cell while a lieutenant with a green book explains, gently, over and over, that cooperation shortens sentences, that names lighten loads, that a woman her age needn't suffer for other people's secrets. What happens is that Maren — proud, tired, frightened Maren — eventually gives them something, because everyone gives them something, and the something she gives will be true, because she doesn't know any lies worth telling.

"She holds," I said, and hated myself for the certainty I couldn't honestly feel. "And meanwhile we hold too. Two days, Tally. The tide comes in two days, and after that none of this matters anymore."

I believed it the way you believe a plank will hold: fully, and with your weight already committed.

* * *

The knock came at midnight, which is when honest knocks stop happening.

I was awake — I was always awake those nights, sleeping in shifts of an hour with the knife under my hand — so I heard the footsteps before the knock: more than one set, heavy, unhurried. Unhurried footsteps at midnight mean authority. Hurried ones mean thieves, and I'd have preferred thieves.

I went out the back, over the wall into the tannery yard, and lay flat under the lime pits' scaffolding where the smell covered everything, including thought. From there I could see lamplight in my own front room, moving politely, methodically. A search conducted with a warrant moves fast. A search conducted with permission moves slowly, savoring.

Three figures came around the side of the building, and the lamp caught the face of the middle one, and I stopped breathing.

Hobb Marren.

He was walking with two sergeants, pointing — I couldn't hear the words but I knew the geometry of pointing — toward the back wall, toward the tannery gate, tracing for them the route I'd taken not ninety seconds earlier. His hands were shaking. I remember noticing that, and hating myself for noticing, because pity arriving ahead of anger is a trap you build yourself. His hands shook, and he pointed anyway. That's the whole biography of a collaborator, written in one gesture.

They searched the yard. They found the loose board over the hidden matrices — no, wait. Let me be accurate, because accuracy is the only inheritance my father left me that the fire hadn't touched yet. They found the *place* where boards had been pried, because I'd already moved the matrices days before, but they didn't know that, and Hobb told them it was where "she kept things," and I watched a sergeant kneel and run his fingers in the dust of the cavity like a priest reading entrails.

Then Hobb said something else, and leaned close to the sergeant, and gestured — toward the river. Toward the bridge. Toward the harbor.

He was giving them my route.

Not guessing at it. Giving it. Kade's plan had passed through the guild meeting in outline — I'd told Quill, because Quill was the guild master and withholding it from him felt like a second theft stacked on the first — and Quill, poor ruined loyal Quill, had evidently trusted the room. Nine votes for silence. Nine. And somewhere between that vote and midnight, at least one of the nine had done what frightened men do: traded the silence for a receipt.

I lay under the scaffolding in the tannery stink and watched my childhood acquaintances sell my death to the army for whatever price the lieutenant with the green book had quoted them — a head start, a lighter sentence, maybe just the warm feeling of being on the winning side. And the worst part, the part I still turn over at night like a stone with something alive under it, is that I understood them. Completely. That's the filthiest thing fear does: it doesn't make you unable to forgive. It makes you able to *imagine*, and imagination is forgiveness's cowardly cousin.

They left. I counted to five hundred, which is longer than you think, especially flat on your back in a tannery.

* * *

Kade took the news the way she takes everything — standing up, chewing on a pipe that hadn't been lit in years, staring at the river as if it owed her money.

"So the market gate's dead," she said.

"The market gate's dead. The sergeant's wife knows my face now anyway, and by tomorrow the whole garrison will have my description memorized in three costumes."

"And the bridge?"

"The bridge was always going to be the hard one. Now it's the impossible one. They'll have the route. They'll be watching the coffin-tenders, the laundresses, the fish carts — every disguise I've got, they've got a description of."

Kade spat over the rail, an act of commentary rather than contempt, though with Kade the categories overlapped. "Then we change the cargo."

"The ledger doesn't change."

"The *route* changes. Cargo stays honest, routes lie. First law of the river." She turned and looked at me with those eyes that had appraised ten thousand cargoes and found most of them wanting. "Girl, I'm going to say a thing, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to agree with it, because it's true. You can't be the one to carry it through the gates anymore. You're not a smuggler. You're a famous face in a small town. Every eye in Vellsmark is learning your shape."

"I promised you I'd bring it aboard myself."

"You promised a lot of things to a lot of people lately. Promises are like type — good ones get reused, bad ones get melted down." She tapped the dead pipe against the rail. "Here's what's left. Tide's in two mornings. Gate's closed. Bridge is closed. That leaves the water itself, and the water doesn't read proclamations. There's a drainage culvert under the old salt warehouse — comes out below the quay wall, below the search line. It floods at half-tide. You'd swim it in the dark with the package wrapped in oilcloth, and you'd have maybe forty breaths of margin, and if you misjudge the current you come out in the estuary wearing a stone coat."

"Forty breaths," I said.

"Thirty, if you panic. Panic taxes everything." She almost smiled. "Or — and I say this as a woman who owes your father more than I ever paid — you give me the ledger tonight, and I take it out with the tide, and you stay here and hang with dignity, and I put a plaque on my ship."

"Charming."

"It's on the table."

It was on the table. And God forgive me, some exhausted animal part of me wanted to take it — wanted to hand the weight to someone stronger and sit down for the first time in weeks. But I'd watched what happened to people in Vellsmark who handed their weights to others. Hobb Marren had handed his conscience to a lieutenant and gotten a receipt. The guild had handed its courage to its fear and gotten Maren Ostrow in irons. Everything I loved about my father lived in the opposite transaction: he had carried names off a ledger at cost to himself, quietly, without receipts, and I had only just learned it, and I was not going to unlearn it in a tannery yard at midnight.

"I bring it myself," I said. "Culvert, oilcloth, thirty breaths. Teach me the current."

Kade studied me for a long moment, pipe smoke finally curling up from a coal she'd conjured from somewhere, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to settle an account of her own.

"Your father said the same thing once," she said. "Word for word. 'Teach me the current.' Stubbornness must run in the ink." She turned back to the river. "All right. But hear me, Bram Okafor, and hear me once: I sail on the morning tide whether you're on that quay or not. The sea keeps no appointments and neither do I. Miss the water, and everything you're carrying becomes ballast for the bottom of the river."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She glanced back, and for one unguarded second there was something in her face that wasn't appraisal — something older and wearier, the look of a woman who had buried enough couriers to know the odds by heart. "Because tomorrow night, when you're under that warehouse with black water in your mouth, understanding is going to feel very far away. All you'll have is the swimming."

* * *

I went back for Tally through the streets I no longer owned.

That's what it was, I realized, moving shadow to shadow along the cooperage wall: the town had been repossessed. Every doorway was a creditor now. The fountain where Maren had been marched past in irons stood dry and moonlit, and a paper was pasted to its rim — fresh, the paste still shining — and I crossed the square low and fast to read it, because a printer reads everything, it's a disease.

It wasn't a warrant. It was a request. *Persons with information regarding the movement of stolen guild property are asked to come forward. Protection and reward offered. Loyalty will be remembered.*

Loyalty will be remembered. Four words, set in official type, and I promise you no compositor in Vellsmark set that line without vomiting a little. It was an invitation to do what Hobb had done, printed in the passive voice that governments use when they want the whole population to sign.

At the shop, Tally was waiting where I'd told her to wait, and I gave her the new orders: stay away from the guild hall, stay away from the fountain, stay away from anyone who's suddenly kind to you. Kindness, I explained, is how the army shops for information. It buys at retail what fear sells wholesale.

"Bram." She was quiet a moment, picking at the wax seal on the door, peeling it up and pressing it back down, peeling and pressing. "Was it the guild? Who told them?"

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"Some of them. Maybe one. Maybe just Hobb." I crouched to her level. In the dark she looked younger than thirteen, which was an illusion; she'd been older than thirteen since the day the soldiers came. "Does it change anything, knowing?"

She thought about it with the seriousness she brought to everything, even peeling wax.

"It changes what I print," she said finally, "when I learn the trade. I'm going to print their names. All of them. Small letters, but I'll know which ones they are."

There are moments when a found family announces itself — not with warmth, but with a shared fury, a common enemy, a vow exchanged in the dark beside a sealed door. This was ours. I put my hand on the back of her head, the way my father used to put his on mine, and I said, "Set them well, then. If you're going to ruin a man, ruin him in clean type."

She grinned, wet-eyed, and for a moment we were just two printers' people in a broken shop, and the world outside the door could go hang.

Then I heard the boots. Not one pair — a patrol, turning onto our street, unhurried, thorough, working the block with a list. And I understood, with the cold clarity that had been my only reliable companion these two weeks, that the guild's betrayal had purchased more than a closed gate. It had purchased a hunt. My route, my disguises, my hiding places — all of it was in a green book now, and the man holding the book was done waiting for me to come to him.

"Tally," I whispered. "Back window. Now."

Below us, the patrol stopped at the fountain, and by the light of their lantern I saw the officer unfold a paper and check it against the darkness of our street — and I saw, in the instant before I pulled us both into the shadows, that the paper bore a sketch. A face. Mine.

They weren't searching for a laundress anymore. They weren't searching for a corpse-tender, a courier, a ghost.

They were searching for *me* — and the drawing was good.
