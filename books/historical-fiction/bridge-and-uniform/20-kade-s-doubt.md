## 20. Kade's Doubt

There is a arithmetic every smuggler learns before she learns to read a chart, and it goes like this: the ship comes first. Not the cargo — the ship. Cargo can be lost, replaced, written off to insurance men who will never set foot on water. A crew can be grieved. But the ship, the ship is the only promise you are ever truly allowed to keep, because everyone else's life depends on her floating.

The *Salt Lark* was mine, and by the third night of this business I was doing sums I did not like.

I stood at her stern rail in the dark of the harbor annex — a crook of mudflat and rotting pilings east of the customs quay where Nessa Kade had been quietly not-existing for eleven years — and I looked north toward Vellsmark's glow, which was too much glow for a town that was supposed to be asleep. Fires. Army cook-fires, mostly, but fires all the same, and somewhere under all that orange light was a print-shop girl carrying my father's debt around her neck like an anchor chain.

"She's late," said Pinch, my rigger-boy, who was fifteen and had never once told me something I wanted to hear, which is why I kept him.

"She's careful," I said.

"Careful people aren't hunted through three checkpoints," Pinch said. "Careful people don't have sketches of their faces nailed to fountains."

I had seen the sketch myself. One of Kade's shore-contacts, a fishwife with loose morals and tighter lips, had copied it for me for the price of two mackerel and a rumor. It was good work. The eyes were right. The stubborn line of the jaw was right. Whoever the army had hired to draw Bram Okafor had either excellent memory or excellent sources, and neither possibility made me warmer.

Two days. That was what remained of our pact. The tide on the morning after next would lift the *Lark* out of the annex, down the channel, past the narrows, and out toward open water and buyers who paid in silver instead of promises. If Bram Okafor was not on my deck when that tide turned, then the ledger went without her or I went without the ledger, and both of those options had teeth.

Her father, Tomas Okafor, had owed me four hundred crowns when he died. That was the story I told myself, anyway — that this was about the debt. Debts were clean. Debts had columns and interest and the pleasant fiction that both parties understood the terms.

But Tomas Okafor had never actually owed me four hundred crowns. He had owed me a different thing entirely, and I had spent twenty years pretending otherwise, because pretending is cheaper than gratitude and keeps better in salt air.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fathers who die owing money: they leave you a ledger of your own, whether you cut the pages yourself or not.

My father was Harl Kade, and Harl Kade was the kind of man rivers make — broad-backed, slow to anger, and utterly convinced that water would provide. He ran grain upriver in fat seasons and ran fugitives downriver in thin ones, and he never once in his life wrote anything down, because writing things down, he said, was how honest men became dead ones. He judged every soul he dealt with by eye and handshake, and his eye was good, and it killed him anyway, in the end, at the customs house in Vellsmark, in the famine winter, when a requisition officer decided our grain was the Crown's grain and our silence was treason.

I was nineteen. The army took the cargo, took the boat, and let my father bleed out on the quay while they argued over whose requisition applied. What they did not take was the manifest — a single page in my father's fist listing every hold and every soul aboard — because the manifest named an army captain among the passengers, and naming him was worth more than any grain.

That captain was Ilse Verren. She was thirty years old, half-starved herself on garrison rations that her own quartermaster had been selling out from under her, and she had paid her passage the way poor officers pay for things: with a signature and a look that said she was sorry about it.

I did not go to Verren that winter. I went to a printer.

Tomas Okafor sat me down in his shop among the smell of lead and ink, read my father's manifest twice, and then did the most dangerous thing I ever saw a man do: he put it in his guild ledger. Not the names — he struck those — but the fact. The date. The requisition order number. The quantity of grain taken from a licensed vessel, entered in a hand so neat it looked like scripture, filed in a book that no magistrate could ignore and no colonel could burn without explaining why he wanted to.

"That's your inheritance," he told me. "Not revenge. *Record.* Revenge gets one man. Record gets them all, someday, when the wind turns." And then he fed me dinner, lent me a leaky skiff against all sense, and told me the four hundred crowns could wait until I was standing on my own keel again.

It took me six years to pay him back the money. I never paid him back the rest. There isn't a coin for the rest.

And now his daughter wanted me to carry the very book that held his handwriting out of the town that had killed my father, past the army that had starved us both, on a tide that would not wait — and I sat in my cabin that third night doing honest arithmetic for the first time in years.

Ship first. Ship always first.

So why did the sum keep coming out wrong?

* * *

She came aboard an hour before dawn, and she came badly.

I heard the annex ladder creak in a rhythm no cat and no soldier makes, and by the time she swung over the rail I was already there with a belaying pin in my fist and Pinch behind me with the boat-hook, and we both lowered our weapons at more or less the same moment, which should have told me something about where my loyalties had gotten to without my permission.

Bram Okafor looked like a woman who had been three other women in the past two days and hated all of them. Her laundress's cloak was scorched along one hem. There was soot at her jawline and a cut above her eyebrow that someone had tried to close with wax, badly. She carried nothing — no bundle, no satchel, no ledger — and that absence was louder than the alarm bells still muttering across the water from the customs quay.

"You've got smoke on you," I said.

"The shop burned." She said it flat, the way people say things they haven't yet decided to feel. "Ashe's men. They found the matrices under the floorboards, so at least the fire had a purpose." A muscle moved in her cheek. "I saved the knife."

She held it up: a type-cutter's graver, plain-handled, worn to the shape of one man's grip.

"Well," I said. "Congratulations. You're now the richest pauper on this river."

"I need to know if you're sailing," she said. No greeting, no plea. She'd walked past my hospitality like it was furniture. "The tide after next. I need to know if the *Lark* leaves with or without me, because if it leaves without me, I'll spend my last night differently than if it doesn't."

"And how's that?"

"Honestly? Asleep," she said. "I've been awake for forty hours and it turns out even righteous fury needs naps."

Despite everything — despite the arithmetic, despite the smoke, despite the sketch of her face nailed to a fountain in a town that used to know her name — I laughed. It came out of me like something escaping custody.

"Come below," I said. "There's soup. It's terrible. Cook made it from a recipe he invented out of spite."

* * *

Cook's soup was, as promised, terrible, and Bram Okafor ate two bowls of it like a woman who had recently remembered food existed. We sat in the low cabin under the swing-lamp while the tide breathed against the hull, and Pinch hovered in the doorway pretending to splice rope that needed no splicing, because my boy had decided she was interesting and my boy's decisions about interesting people were usually correct and always inconvenient.

"The guild turned on you," I said. It wasn't a question; the fishwife had sold me that news along with the sketch.

"Fisker and two others," Bram said. "They told Verren's interrogators about the market gate. About my route." She turned her bowl slowly on the table. "Eleven years Fisker ate at my father's table. He taught me to ink a roller when my hands were too small for it. And he sold me for a promise that his name would come off the list."

"And did it?"

"No. Ashe doesn't remove names. He adds them." She looked up. "Does that surprise you?"

"That belonging has conditions?" I said. "No, girl. That's the oldest condition there is. Crews, guilds, families, armies — all of them are just debts wearing matching clothes. The question is never whether people will sell you. It's the price, and whether you'd have paid it yourself in their boots."

"Fisker's price was his neck."

"Everyone's price is their neck. Mine's the *Lark*." I said it to see what she'd do, and what she did was nod, slowly, like a woman checking my arithmetic against her own.

"Then we understand each other," she said. "You're wondering whether to sail."

"I'm not wondering. I'm deciding," I said. "There's a difference. Wondering is for people with slack in the schedule. The tide after next is real, it's measured, and it does not care whose father owed whose father what."

Pinch stopped splicing. In the doorway he said, quietly, "Kade."

"Not now, boy."

"You should tell her about your da'," Pinch said, and had the gall to look innocent about it, which is hard to manage when you're holding rope you were clearly eavesdropping with.

"He knows about my da'."

"He knows your da' died here. He doesn't know about the manifest, or the book, or why you really said yes when she sent word." Pinch looked at Bram with the merciless directness of the young. "She didn't take this job for four hundred crowns. She hasn't charged you a copper."

The cabin got very small and very quiet, the way cabins do when truth comes aboard uninvited.

"Tomas Okafor," I said at last, "kept my father's death in a book. Ink on paper, in a hand like a hymn. It's the only reason anyone will ever know Harl Kade wasn't a smuggler who got what smugglers get. He made grief into evidence, and evidence into patience, and patience into—" I gestured vaguely at the beam overhead, at the ship, at the whole crooked apparatus of my life. "Me. Sort of. Everything I run, everything I refuse to run, some part of it is a woman waiting twenty years for a record to matter."

Bram had gone very still. Under the lamp her face was all planes and shadows, her father's jaw, her father's stubbornness rendered in a younger hand.

"So carry the ledger," she said softly, "for him."

"No," I said. "That's just it, girl. I'm done carrying things for dead men. Dead men's causes sink ships. Ask any widow on this coast." I leaned forward. "I'll tell you what I'll carry it for. I'll carry it for a living person who ate two bowls of terrible soup and asked me straight whether I was sailing, instead of begging. Your father earned my debt. You're the first Okafor in twenty years to treat me like a party to a contract instead of a favor to be collected. That's worth a tide. One tide."

"One tide," she repeated.

"The morning after next, when the channel fills, the *Lark* goes. With you or without you, ledger or ash. If you miss the tide, I will grieve you efficiently and sell your story to a pamphleteer, and it won't even be a good pamphlet." I stood, because sitting down felt too much like tenderness and I'd already spent more of that tonight than I could afford. "But between now and then, you've still got two gates between you and my deck, and the second one is a bridge full of soldiers who own a very good drawing of your face. So drink the last of that soup, and then you and Pinch are going to sit down with a chart, because my boy knows the corpse-tenders' wharf better than anyone alive—"

Bram had stopped listening. I watched it happen — the stillness spreading across her face, the way her eyes fixed on the middle distance where ideas arrive.

"A coffin," she said.

"What?"

"The bridge checkpoint searches cargo. They demand bills and they open barrels. But a plague coffin going downriver to the burial ground at Marsh End—" she was talking faster now, that typesetter's precision snapping into place, each word set like lead, "—they don't like opening coffins. Superstition. But they *do* demand it, sometimes, when a sergeant wants to be thorough. So the coffin has to survive being opened. It has to be *true*, except for one thing hidden inside it."

"Bram," I said carefully, "what exactly are you proposing to hide inside a corpse?"

"Nothing," she said, and there was something in her voice then that I recognized, because I'd heard it in her father's shop twenty years ago — the sound of a person deciding, precisely and forever, who they were going to be. "Something that's already true. I just have to find it."

She wouldn't say more. She finished the soup, thanked Cook by name — which he would pretend to resent for a week — and went up the ladder into the grey beginning of morning. At the rail she paused.

"One tide," she said. "I'll be on your deck before it turns, Kade. On my honor."

"Keep your honor," I said. "Bring the book."

She dropped over the side into the tender, and Pinch and I watched the little boat pull away toward the pale line of Vellsmark, toward the bridges and the bayonets and whatever truth she meant to bury inside a coffin, and I stood at the rail long after the fog took her, doing my arithmetic one final time.

Ship first. Ship always first.

Except that the ship, I thought, watching the town smolder against the dawn — the ship had never once been the whole of the promise. Somewhere below, in the hold, in the ballast, in the tarred seams of her, the *Lark* was carrying more than I ever loaded aboard her, and most of it had come from a dead printer who believed a record could outlive a man, and a boy who knew when to stop splicing rope, and a cook who made soup out of spite for strangers.

Found families, Harl Kade used to say, watching strangers come down his gangway — found families are just crews that signed on with their hearts instead of their hands. He thought it was a warning. It took me twenty years and one stubborn girl to hear it as a blessing.

"Kade," Pinch said beside me. "She's going to make it, isn't she?"

I looked north, where the tide was already turning, hours early, as tides do when they've decided something.

"She'd better," I said. "Because I've a suspicion that coffin of hers is going to need a berth on my deck by tomorrow night — and whatever's lying inside it, girl, I don't think it's sleeping."
