## 21. Second Gate: The Bridge

There is a particular smell that comes off the river at low tide in autumn, and if you live in Vellsmark long enough you stop noticing it. I had grown up breathing it. It took hauling a coffin toward the bridge checkpoint at four in the morning to teach me that I had never stopped loving it, even now, when the river smelled like my whole life burning down behind me in stages.

The coffin was real. That had been Nessa Kade's first condition, delivered while she picked her teeth with a sail needle.

"No fakes," she'd said. "The army's not stupid, love. A plague box gets opened, and it gets opened *fast* if the sergeant smells paint and fresh lumber instead of death. You want a body that's been dead long enough to make an argument for itself."

So we had one. Kade's cook — a vast, gentle man called Hobben who fed fugitives without questions and named every soup after his late wife — knew the keeper of the pest-house on the marsh side, a woman who owed more debts than the ledger I carried had pages. For two silver bits and a promise, she'd released the body of a pauper who had died of the fever three days prior and no family to object. His name, as far as anyone knew or cared, was Nobody, from Nowhere, lately of the parish of Too Bad.

I told myself that mattered. I told myself a lot of things at four in the morning.

"You'll want to touch him," Kade said. She meant it practically, so I flinched less when the moment came. "Dead men don't putrefy on schedule, and fever-bodies mostly smell of sick, not death. The sergeant will want to see you handle him like you've handled a hundred. Corpse-tenders don't gag. They grumble."

She was right, as she usually was, in the flat way of someone who has been right at sea too long to bother dressing it up. So I touched him. Cold, waxy, heavier than I expected — dead weight is a phrase people use lightly, and then they lift a dead man's arm and understand it forever. I made myself arrange his hand where it had slipped. I grumbled about it under my breath, because grumbling was the costume, and by the third time I adjusted something I wasn't performing anymore. Grief does that. It finds its way into your hands and settles there like lead type locked in a chase, ready to print itself on everything you touch.

"Good," Kade said quietly, watching me. "Now the hard part."

"The hard part was the corpse."

"No." She looked at me with those grey eyes that took soundings of everything. "The hard part is that you're going to stand in front of a man with a sword and a list of names, and your name is on the list, and you're going to talk to him about wages and weather for as long as it takes, and you're not going to run. Running is the only thing a body wants to do when it's that frightened. Promise me you won't."

"Why?"

"Because I've seen people run from soldiers," she said, "and the river keeps what it catches."

* * *

The disguise came together out of the shop's leavings, which felt fitting. Everything I had left of my old life was either burned, seized, or being used as a costume. The corpse-tenders' guild — yes, Vellsmark had a guild for that too; my father used to say we were a town so organized it would form a committee to fall downstairs — wore undyed grey wool, waxed aprons, and a lead badge stamped with a crossed spade and torch. Hobben's friend had provided the badge and the tabard. The apron I waxed myself, in the hold of Kade's ship, by lamplight, and I will admit that somewhere between the second coat of wax and the third I stopped and pressed my forehead against the cold plank wall and let myself shake for exactly one hundred heartbeats. I counted them the way I counted em quads when setting a difficult line. Precision is a kind of mercy you can do for yourself.

Then I stood up, finished the apron, and went back to work.

The ledger itself was sealed inside the coffin, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the body, along the length of his left side where a searching hand would least expect a book's shape. That had been my contribution to the plan — Kade thought in terms of cargo and ballast, but I thought in terms of *forms*. In printing, the art of hiding something is the art of making it look like the page. A coffin's interior tells a story: body centered, shroud tucked, personal effects at the head — a spoon, a clay pipe, a bundle of the man's few clothes. Anything outside that composition reads as wrong before a conscious mind knows why. So the ledger went where a spine-width gap naturally lived, padded with shroud-linen to the exact profile of a wasted torso, and I spent an hour with my eyes closed memorizing how the coffin should look from above, the way I'd memorize a forme before locking the quoins.

If they opened it, I was dead. Not figuratively. There was no version of this where a sergeant pulled an army-sealed guild ledger out from under a plague corpse and said, "Ah, misunderstanding, off you go."

Which is why the plan's entire architecture rested on a single fact I'd pried out of a garrison laundress two nights earlier, trading gossip the way I'd once traded gossip about which magistrate watered his wine: plague coffins coming *out* of Vellsmark were opened only if the sergeant at the gate was a *sergeant*. A corporal could wave them through. A lieutenant wouldn't touch one with somebody else's hands. But a full sergeant — a man with thirteen years' service and a pension he could taste — had the authority, and therefore the duty, and therefore the overwhelming incentive, to *not* open anything he didn't absolutely have to. Plague doesn't care about rank, and everyone in uniform knew it.

My job was to make opening the coffin feel optional. And then to make *not* opening it feel like his own idea.

Simple. I'd have laughed at the simplicity if laughing wouldn't have cost me the use of my lungs.

* * *

The bridge at dawn is the most beautiful thing in Vellsmark, which is a low bar to clear for a bridge, but there it is: seven arches of grey stone, mist coming off the water in slow ribbons, the far tower black against a sky going the color of a fresh-washed sheet. My father used to stop on it mid-errand, every time, without fail, and look upstream, and say nothing, and I used to burn with embarrassment as a girl because he did it in front of people. I would give a great deal now to be embarrassed by him one more time.

There were torches on the bridge. Torches at dawn meant the lockdown had tightened overnight, which meant the guild members who had sold my route had been believed, which meant every gate in Vellsmark was now looking for a woman of twenty-six, dark-haired, ink-stained, last seen anywhere and everywhere. None of them were looking for a corpse-tender, because no army on this earth looks twice at the person whose job it is to take the thing everyone most wants gone and carry it away.

I checked the cart's axle for the fourth time. I checked it a fifth time anyway. The cart was borrowed, the mule was borrowed, and the mule was the single largest liability in the operation, because mules cannot be briefed. Kade's rigger-boy, Pip — fourteen, all elbows, local knowledge like a chart in his head — had sworn the animal was docile. "He's a philosopher," Pip said. "He's decided most things don't matter." I could only hope the mule's philosophy extended to torchlight and shouted commands.

The queue at the near gate was short. That was bad. Long queues are mercy; they give everyone time to be bored with you. A short queue means you arrive as an event.

"Halt. Name and business."

The sentry was young. Good. Young ones defer upward quickly. I kept my eyes down and to the left, at the level where a superior officer's chest would be, and I pitched my voice into the flat, over-it register of a woman who has been awake since three and blames the world.

"Tenna Marsh. Corpse-tender, pest-house charter. Fever death, marsh-side, going to the burying ground at Stonefleet." I held up the charter paper, which Doss had — gods keep him, coward that he is — forged beautifully, right down to the damp-stain he'd made by actually wetting it. "You'll want to touch the paper, not me, and I'd take it kindly if we settled which."

That landed. The sentry read the charter at arm's length, which is what you do when you've been told about plague and haven't been told enough about plague to know arm's-length reading is theater.

"Sergeant!" he called. "Corpse detail!"

And here we went.

Sergeant Brakeman — I never learned his name; in my head he will always be Sergeant Brakeman, because he had the build of a man who had once been braked and never fully restarted — came around the barrier wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, and he looked at the cart, and at the coffin, and at me, and he did the arithmetic sergeants do: effort against reward, risk against blame.

"Pest-house charter," he said. It wasn't a question but I answered it anyway, because silence reads as guilt and over-explaining reads as fear and the narrow road between them is walked one sentence at a time.

"Since the year of the flood. Ask for Mother Corve at the marsh house, she'll tell you I'm the only tender she's got left since the requisition took the rest." True, as far as it went. The best lies are load-bearing walls made of true bricks.

"Open it."

There it is. Every plan meets its sentence eventually, and that was ours, delivered in a voice like a dropped door.

I did not flinch. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps: I did not flinch, though my heartbeat went from a walk to a press-run. What I did was sigh — long, put-upon, the sigh of professional inconvenience — and say:

"Can. But then you're writing your name on the charter, and telling Mother Corve why her burying ground detail is short a man for two days while you sit quarantine, and I'll need your help lifting the lid, because I don't do it alone for fever cases. Regulation." I tapped the charter. "It's all in the schedule, sarge. Page four."

There is no page four. There was every chance he'd demand to see page four. But sergeants, like lieutenants, like colonels, like every rank of men I had watched run this occupation, share one glorious weakness: they will move mountains rather than read.

Brakeman looked at the coffin. He looked at the mist coming off the river, which at that hour does carry a certain suggestion of miasma if you're primed to see it. He looked at his own hands.

"Who died, then?" he said.

And this — this was the moment I'd rehearsed forty times and still wasn't ready for, because the answer was true, and the truth of it went through me sideways. "Nobody knows his name. Pauper, marsh-side. No effects worth mentioning." A spoon. A pipe. A bundle of clothes. A ledger's worth of other people's lives under his left side.

Brakeman chewed on that. Behind him, the sky was lightening, and somewhere downstream the tide was beginning its long patient turn, and I counted seconds the way I count em quads: *one, two*, the mule shifting its weight, *three, four*, the young sentry glancing at the growing light with a man's-end-of-shift longing, *five* —

"Fever's done with him in three days, has it?" Brakeman asked. "Safe to handle?"

"Done with him and hungry for the next fool who opens that box," I said. "Your call, sarge. You've the authority. It says so in the schedule."

I handed him the moment, wrapped and addressed, the way you hand a man a hot iron: he could hold it, but he'd very quickly want to put it down.

He put it down.

"Stonefleet burying ground," he muttered, stepping back and waving the sentry forward. "Straight across, no stops, and if anyone at the far tower asks, you sat here ten minutes waiting on paperwork."

"Ten minutes," I agreed solemnly. "Miserable wait. Terrible coffee."

And the barrier lifted, and the mule — bless his philosophic heart — walked on as if nothing in the world had ever mattered less, and I rolled the cart onto the bridge of Vellsmark with my father's killers' paperwork under a dead man's arm and my father's last secret beneath his ribs.

I did not look at the upstream view. He would have understood that too.

* * *

Halfway across, the mistake happened.

It was small. It was the kind of small thing plans die of. A squad of soldiers came off the far ramp on the double, boots hammering the stones, and swung around a supply wagon stuck at the middle arch, and their corporal shouted at me to clear the path, and I hauled the mule left against the parapet, and one of the soldiers — hurrying, careless, nineteen — clipped the coffin's corner with his pack as he passed and knocked it a hand's width sideways.

The body shifted. Anyone who has moved a coffin knows the sound: a soft, dense settling, like a bag of grain, except bags of grain do not have arms.

The young soldier stopped. Looked at the coffin. Looked at me.

"Sorry," he said.

"Mind the box, love," I said, "and it won't mind you."

He laughed — a startled, boyish laugh — and ran on, and the squad swallowed him, and I stood at the parapet of the bridge my father loved with my pulse slamming in my ears and my face arranged into mild professional annoyance, and I learned in that moment the final lesson of the day: terror and comedy travel together, and both will get you across a bridge if you can ride them.

The far gate was a formality. A bored corporal, the same charter, the same arm's-length reading, one question — "Plague?" — answered with a weary nod and a jerk of the thumb at the box, and then the barrier rose again and Vellsmark was behind me and the Stonefleet road lay open ahead, grey and empty in the first true light.

I made it a quarter mile past the tower before my legs informed me they were done negotiating. I brought the cart into a copse of alders off the road, walked the mule deep enough that the coffin vanished into shadow, and stood with my hands on the lid, breathing.

I had promised myself I would not open it until the burying ground, where I could dig the ledger free, wrap the poor man decently, and finish what the living owed him. That was the plan. Plans fail. But before I could lift the lid — before I could take back what I'd smuggled and send the nameless man to his rest — my fingers found the lid already loose. Shifted. Not seated flush. Not as it had been when Hobben and I nailed it at the pest-house, and not knocked askew by a soldier's pack, either; the movement was along the seam, deliberate, from *inside*.

Nails don't work themselves loose. Dead men don't adjust their own lids.

And underneath the fear that flooded me then — cold, total, the fear that the box had been tampered with at some checkpoint I hadn't seen, that the ledger was gone, that a trap lay waiting under three days of fever-death and waxed linen — underneath all of it, my hands went on without me, the way trained hands do, easing the lid up along the grain, one inch, two, morning light falling through the alders in thin gold bars across a dead man's face.

I knew him.

God help me, I knew him at once — the watery eyes closed now, the printer's stoop frozen into the line of the shoulders, the guild pin at the collar that no pauper from the marsh would ever have worn.

Master Quill.

And pinned beneath the pin, folded tight, a paper in a hand I recognized as well as my own father's — a confession, signed, dated yesterday.

The confession was not addressed to me. It was addressed to whoever opened the coffin next.

But the coffin was addressed to me, and Quill had known it would be.
