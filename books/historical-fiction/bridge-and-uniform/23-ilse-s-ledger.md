## 23. Ilse's Ledger

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in an officer's quarters in a requisitioned town. It is not the silence of peace. It is the silence of a room that belongs to someone else, furnished with someone else's griefs — a pressed-flower sampler on the wall that some grandmother made, a child's wooden horse abandoned mid-gallop on the sill. I had slept three nights in the Harrow house on Chandler's Row, and every night I apologized to it under my breath.

Tonight I did not sleep at all.

The guild ledger sat on the desk between the candlestick and a cold cup of chicory, and it sat the way a confession sits — patiently, with all the time in the world, waiting for you to be ready. Which is a joke, because no one is ever ready. You simply run out of excuses instead.

It had come back to my quarters four days ago, wrapped in oilcloth, carried by a corporal who did not meet my eyes. *Recovered,* the docket said. *Handler's discretion.* Colonel Ashe's discretion, which meant the ledger had been passed through his hands like a coin through a card sharp's fingers, and now it rested with me for one reason only: because he wanted me to read it. He wanted me complicit before dawn, and he wanted me to know I was complicit, and he wanted me grateful for the arrangement, because gratitude makes better glue than fear. Fear cracks. Gratitude sets.

I told myself I would not open it. I told myself this while trimming the wick. I told myself this while taking off my boots, as if undressing for bed were a ritual that could still be performed by a woman who intended to behave well. Then I put my boots back on and sat down, because I have never once in thirty-one years managed to go to bed with an unopened book in the room. My mother called it a sickness. My mother also read seed catalogs by tallow light until her eyes gave out, so she was hardly a reliable witness.

The cover was calf leather, gone soft as a dog's ear at the corners. Okafor's hand was on the first page — Bram's father, the printer, dead these six years but speaking in that neat, upright script that printers learn the way soldiers learn drill: every letter standing at attention, every margin square. *Guild Ledger of Vellsmark, kept by Solon Okafor, in the sight of God and the Guild of the Book.* Underneath, smaller, almost shy: *and in the sight of whoever reads after me, may they judge gently.*

I judged him gently within ten pages. He recorded debts the way a physician records symptoms — without cruelty, without flattery. A widow's paper stock advanced against winter. An apprentice's indenture bought out by the guild when his father drank the fee. Names, dates, sums, terms. It should have been boring. It was the opposite of boring. It was a town's whole circulatory system, inked.

That was what made it dangerous, of course. Not the debts. The names. A conspiracy needs a list of conspirators the way a fire needs a draft, and here was the draft, entire: every printer, every journeyman, every sympathizer who had ever slipped a pamphlet across a counter or paid a fine in secret or asked, in this very handwriting, whether truth might be set in twelve-point and sold for a penny. The army wanted it not primarily as evidence — evidence burns, evidence rots, evidence gets lost in transit, as I had good reason to know. The army wanted it as a lever. Every name in it was a man or woman who could be traded, turned, or hanged, whichever best cleared the colonel's path.

I knew all this going in. I want that written down somewhere, even if nowhere ever sees it: I knew what the book was for before I opened it. What I did not know — what no part of me had braced for — was that my own name would be in it.

* * *

It was past two when I found the famine pages.

You have to understand how the famine is remembered in Vellsmark, which is to say: barely, and sideways, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth. Sixteen years ago the grain failed twice running, and the army — my army, the army I had joined that same spring with a recruit's coat too big in the shoulders — requisitioned what remained anyway. Requisition is a clean word. It sounds like paperwork. What it meant in Vellsmark that winter was granaries locked under guard while children swelled with hunger in houses you could see from the guard posts. What it meant was garrison rations for the soldiers — thin, but thin on purpose, because the quartermaster was selling the difference downriver — while outside the walls people boiled hides and bark.

What it meant, for me, was Corporal Ilse Verren, nineteen years old, standing post at the granary gate with her stomach eating itself and her orders nailed to the inside of her skull: *no one enters, no one leaves, nothing moves.*

And what it meant, on the fourth of Deepmonth, was that a printer named Solon Okafor walked up to that gate in the dark with a sack over his shoulder and said, "Corporal. You look like a girl who hasn't eaten. That's a shame, because I can't fix your hunger, but I can fix mine, and I've brought enough for two."

He fed me. That is the whole of it; there is no version of the story where it becomes more complicated, though I have spent sixteen years trying to build complications around it like scaffolding, because the plainness shames me. He fed me bread and bean stew in the print shop's back room, and he kept feeding me, every third night for the rest of that winter, and his wife mended my coat, and their daughter — a sharp-eyed scrap of a girl who watched me cut type with more patience than any soldier I ever trained — taught me the names of letterforms. When spring came and the transfers came through, I left Vellsmark with a full belly and a debt I had no currency to pay.

I wrote to them, at first. Twice. Then postings, then promotions, then years. The debt didn't shrink with distance. Debts don't. They just wait for interest.

All of which is to say that when I turned the page and saw the heading *Deepmonth, 16 years past* in Okafor's careful hand, my own body betrayed me before my mind caught up. My heart went quick and light, the way it does before good news. Some foolish cellar of me thought: *here will be the record of it. Here will be proof that he was kind, entered fair, that the world keeps accounts of goodness too.*

Here is what the page held instead:

*Audit, by request of the Guild, of monies paid to garrison officers for the turning of eyes. Entered not in judgment, for I am not fit to judge men starving beside their duty, but in witness, lest it later be denied.*

Then a column. Dates. Sums. And names.

Quartermaster Havel — forty crowns, monthly, for the disposition of requisitioned grain "downriver." Lieutenant Corve — twenty-five, for scheduling patrols away from the mill road. Sergeant Brecht — twelve, for gate ledgers that showed less grain than the granaries held. On down the column, rank by rank, the winter's hunger itemized as income. Men I had saluted. Men whose boots I had blacked as a raw corporal. Men who had eaten that same bean stew in that same back room, I'd wager, and called it hospitality rather than wages.

And there, halfway down, in the entry for the fourth week of Deepmonth:

*Corporal I. Verren — gate post, north granary — eight crowns, once — noted by S.O.: taken under protest, wept afterward, ate none of it, gave the sum to the widow Ansker's children through the girl. Entered for completeness only. This one is owed, not owing.*

I read it four times. The candle had burned down to a stub by then and the light jumped, so maybe I read it more than four times; the words kept sliding as if the page breathed.

Eight crowns. Once. Taken under protest.

I remember those eight crowns. God help me, I remember them. A lieutenant I barely knew pressed the coins into my hand at the guard change and said it was "hazard consideration" and that refusing would be noted as insubordination, and I was nineteen and terrified and hungry, and I took them, and I hated myself so completely and so efficiently that night that I genuinely do not recall the walk to the print shop. I recall the widow Ansker's door. I recall putting the coins through the gap in the shutters and telling her it was guild charity, which was even true, in the sense that I stole it honestly from thieves. And then I went back to my post and stood until dawn, shaking, and told no one, because what was there to tell? *I was bribed and I laundered the bribe into charity?* No court in the province would have weighed it as anything but theft with extra steps.

Solon Okafor had weighed it. From across a dark yard, without my knowing, he had seen the whole of it — the taking, the weeping, the giving — and he had entered it in his ledger not as a crime but as a debt *owed to me*. He had protected me in ink, the same way I would learn much later he protected others, striking names free of the lists, carrying strangers' sins in his own hand so theirs could stay clean.

And the army — my army, Ashe's army — wanted this book burned. Wanted it buried under treason charges and gallows rhetoric, wanted every printer in Vellsmark swinging from the market bridge as traitors so that no one would ever ask why the case needed so much smoke around it. Because this book doesn't just list conspirators. This book lists *officers*. This book is a mirror, and Ashe means to make sure it hangs on no wall but his.

I understood it then, sitting in a dead woman's house with a stolen man's candle stub guttering: the ledger wasn't seized to build a case against the printers. It was seized to keep the case from being built against everyone else. Doss hadn't stolen it out of the evidence room on his own initiative — clerks don't gamble, they invest. Someone with rank wanted it moved, and wanted it moved into hands that could be trusted, and "trusted" in the colonel's vocabulary has a very specific exchange rate.

My loyalty cracked along the old wound the way ice cracks along a current line — you hear it before you see it, a long soft sound under your feet, and then the whole surface is moving and you understand the river was never frozen, only patient.

* * *

I did what soldiers do with unbearable documents. I copied them.

Not the whole ledger — dawn comes early to the occupied, and my hand would have shaken by the third hour anyway. I copied the famine audit entire, all four pages, and the page of names marked for trial that had been slipped between the boards at the back — eleven names, Okafor and Quill among them, my own signature scrawled at the bottom as witnessing officer because Ashe had put it under my pen and I had been too slow that day to be brave. Copying my own signature was its own small execution. Good. Let it hurt. Numbness is how they get you; the army teaches numbness in the ranks the way the church teaches kneeling.

While the ink dried I sat with the wooden horse on the sill and thought about the girl who had taught me letterforms.

Bram Okafor. She'd been at the inventory when we sealed the shop, standing behind the counter with her arms folded like a woman guarding a country that existed only inside her. I had known her instantly — the eyes are Solon's eyes, dark and measuring, the kind that price everything including you — but I had looked away too quickly, and I have replayed that cowardice every day since. She thinks I didn't recognize her. Or worse: she thinks I recognized her and chose the uniform anyway, which amounts to the same thing in the arithmetic of the heart.

I summoned her once, weeks back, and warned her not to interfere. I told myself it was protection dressed as threat — a warning shot across the bow of a sinking family, the only mercy my rank allowed. But there are no clean motives above the rank of sergeant; I have audited mine enough times since reading this book to know. Part of it was warning. Part of it was wanting to see her. Part of it — the part I dislike most — was establishing, for my own future defense, that I had done my duty and warned her, so that whatever she did next could not be laid at my door.

Well. Her door has been kicked in regardless. Her shop sealed, her guild listed for the gallows, her father's life's work inventoried as *evidence*, and her name — her actual, living name — on a death warrant drafted in an office I pass every morning and salute outside of every evening.

Eleven names. And a ledger that proves the case against them is built on a foundation of bought silence, and the man holding that ledger is the man who intends to hang them for the noise.

* * *

Near dawn I did something I had never done in sixteen years of service. I opened my campaign chest, took out my commission — signed, sealed, signed again by a minister who has never smelled a granary in his life — and laid it flat on the desk beside the copied pages.

I didn't tear it. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing this book has left me. I looked at it the way you look at a bridge you may have to burn, and I folded it back along its creases, and I put it away, and I felt relief and disgust in equal measure, which I believe is the exact emotional compound they call *being awake*.

Not yet. Because tearing it tonight accomplishes nothing except my ruin and the ledger's recovery. If I am to spend this — and I am increasingly certain I will spend it, the way Solon spent his bread on a hungry stranger, without arithmetic, without asking whether the investment was sound — then I must spend it where it buys something. A commission torn in a private room is a gesture. A commission spent at the right gate, at the right hour, in front of the right sentries, is a door opening.

So I need to know where the ledger must go, and when, and through whose hands. I need to know what Bram Okafor intends, because a woman with her eyes does not sit still while her name is drafted onto a gallows list, and if she moves, she will move toward the harbor, because in Vellsmark every road ends at the water eventually. And I need to know exactly how far Colonel Ashe will go to keep his winter quiet — though I begin to suspect the answer is *as far as fire*, and that the smoke rising over the lower town these last nights is not all chimney smoke.

One thing more, and I set it down plainly because a record should carry at least one clean fact: I am not doing this for Bram. Or not only. I am doing it because Solon Okafor entered eight stolen crowns in his ledger as a debt *owed to me*, and then died in a fire with the debt unpaid, and I have discovered in my thirty-first year that I cannot bear to live in a world where the only accurate account of my life is owned by the men who would burn it.

A knock at the door. Three measured raps — the adjutant's knock, always in threes, a man who counts everything.

"Captain Verren. The colonel's compliments. He requests your presence at the garrison at first light. There has been a development regarding the evidence."

I looked at the copies drying on the desk. I looked at the ledger, patient as a confession, and covered it with my spare shirt as if linen could hide a heartbeat.

"Tell the colonel," I called through the door, "I'll be there at first light."

My voice held. It's the voice that has held for sixteen years.

But underneath it, quiet as type being set, one letter at a time, something in me had already begun to compose its answer — and the first word of it was *no*.
