## 24. The Handler

The dead do not hurry, but they do not wait either. That is the paradox I learned in the bottom of a plague coffin on the Vellsmark side of the bridge, with a guild pin under my thumb and a confession folded into a dead man's coat like a letter he'd been carrying for years and had finally, at the last, decided to deliver.

His name had been Master Ambrose Quill for forty years, and it was Master Ambrose Quill still, even laid out on the tarpaulin in Kade's hold with the river smell coming off him and the candlelight making his face gentler than it had any right to be. He had been cruel to me once, or careless — which in a man of his age amounts to the same thing — and he had been an informer, and he had been afraid. And now he was none of those things, because death is the only cure for fear that has never once failed.

I sat on an overturned crate with his confession open on my knees and read it three times, because the first time I read it as a printer reads — checking the hand, the pressure, the way a man's letters lean when his nerve is going — and only the second time did I read it as a woman whose name was on a gallows list.

*They will hang us for conspiracy,* Quill had written, in that upright schoolroom script I knew from every ledger line I'd ever hated. *But there is no conspiracy. There never was one worth the name. There is only Colonel Merrick Ashe, and what he needed us to be.*

I looked up. The hold of the *Magpie's Debt* — Nessa Kade named her ship after her own sense of humor — was dim and rocking, stacked with bales that smelled of tallow and wet hemp. Doss sat against the far bulkhead with his knees drawn up, watching me the way a man watches weather he cannot afford. He had fled to us two nights ago with the location of the original ledger spilling out of him like blood from a wound, and he had not stopped apologizing since, in words and then in silence, which for Doss was an improvement.

"Read it," I said, and held the pages out to him. "You knew him better than I did."

"I didn't know this." He took them carefully, as if they might bite. "Bram, I swear by anything you like—"

"I know what you swear by. You've sworn by everything from your mother to the river these last two days. Read."

He read. I watched his face do what mine had done: the small flinch at the beginning, the stillness in the middle where the names started appearing — names he knew, officers' names, clerks' names, names that connected to other names — and then the slow draining at the end, when the shape of the thing finally stood up and looked at him.

"He was paying you," Doss said quietly. Not a question. "Ashe. All those years. The instructions came through me and I thought—" He laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "I thought I was the middleman. Turns out I was the errand."

"You were both," I said, without much mercy in it. "Welcome to the trade. Everyone in this story is somebody's errand. I'm my father's. You're Ashe's. Verren might be the army's, if she doesn't watch herself." I took the pages back. "The difference is some of us are finding out in time to choose differently."

* * *

Here is what Quill's confession said, and I will set it down plainly because plainness is the only monument I have to give him:

Colonel Merrick Ashe had been garrison commander in Vellsmark during the famine winters. The same winters, though I did not learn this until later, that a young officer named Ilse Verren spent lodging above a print shop on Weavers' Row, eating bread that a typeface cutter named Josiah Okafor split with her because the army's rations were being skimmed by the men at the top of them.

Ashe had been one of the skimmers. Worse than a skimmer — Quill's pages used careful language, the language of a man who understood that even a confession might be read by hostile eyes, but underneath the care the arithmetic was simple. Grain requisitioned for the garrison and sold through river factors. Relief funds that arrived on paper and evaporated before they reached bread. Officers paid to sign receipts for stores that never existed, and a ledger — our ledger, the guild's own book of debts and favors, kept in my father's neat and merciless hand — that recorded every transaction, because my father had believed, with the stubborn faith of a man who spends his life cutting letters, that a thing written truly could not be unwritten.

The guild ledger was not, it turned out, primarily a record of printers' dues. My father had run another column entirely, coded in the sort of way that only a typeface cutter would think to code it — I will come to that, because I did come to it, later, in the worst hour of my life — recording what the garrison owed the town. What the famine cost. Who signed for the missing grain. Every year, the debt grew. Every year, my father added a line.

And Colonel Merrick Ashe, who had built a career on the strength of that vanished grain, who had bought promotions with the proceeds and silenced witnesses with the residue, discovered three years ago that the Okafors' ledger was still being kept, and that the printers' guild of Vellsmark had become, without ever intending it, the custodian of enough paper to hang him twice over.

He could not steal it outright. A colonel has no honest reason to want a guild's account book. So he did what powerful men always do when they need a crime committed in daylight: he arranged for there to be a bigger crime to hide it inside.

Quill had written it in a paragraph I must have read six times before its meaning stopped sliding off me:

*Ashe came to me in the spring, before the proclamation, before any of it. He had known about my weakness since the winter of the bread riots — he had men who worked me over in a cellar while he waited upstairs drinking tea, and everything I told him then he had held against me like a knife at the throat ever since. He said the army would requisition the town, and the printers would be charged with sedition, and the evidence room would receive certain documents, and certain documents would go missing, and no one would ever look past the drama of it. Printers conspiring! It is beautiful. It is exactly the kind of story people want to believe about printers, that we are dangerous with our little letters. He will let us hang, all of us, and while the town weeps over its brave ink-stained martyrs he will burn the true accounts and no one will ever ask where the grain went, or the money, or the eleven years.*

*I am telling you this now because he no longer needs me alive, and I know it, and I would rather be useful dead than obedient dying. The confession naming his handler-network is enclosed. His adjutant keeps the strongbox key. He trusts no one, which is his one virtue, and trusts least the men he has paid most.*

*Tell Josiah's daughter — if she lives, and knowing Josiah she taught her how to live — tell her the ledger is true. Every line. Tell her I am sorry for the part I played, and sorrier for the parts I didn't play, which were more numerous.*

That was Quill. Even his apology had a full accounting attached.

* * *

Doss had gone quiet somewhere around the third page and stayed quiet. Now he said, "Ashe won't stop. You understand that. This isn't a man protecting a position anymore. If this gets out, it isn't demotion, it's a scaffold with his own rank carved on it. He'll sink the ship rather than let the paper reach shore."

"I know."

"And he outranks everyone. Verren, the harbor command, the whole requisition. One word from him and every gate in Vellsmark closes."

"I know that too." I folded the confession along its old creases and put it inside my shirt, against the ledger-shaped bundle I had already hidden there, so that I was carrying two documents and one dead man's trust against my ribs, which is more weight than lead and lighter than some things. "What I don't know is why he hasn't done it yet. Burned the ledger, sunk it, whatever. He had it in his strongbox aboard the customs sloop for days. Why keep the thing alive?"

Doss was silent long enough that I turned to look at him properly. When he finally spoke, it was in the flat voice of a man reciting something he had suspected and refused to say aloud.

"Because a dead ledger proves nothing and protects no one. But a living ledger — a ledger that can be produced selectively, a page here, a name there — that's leverage. He's not keeping it to destroy it, Bram. He's been using it. Trading names for confessions. Building a case against the guild out of the guild's own book, and cutting deals with anyone whose testimony he wants, holding their families' names over them like a coin he can spend." He swallowed. "That clerk you saw. The wrapped bundle to the garrison. That was me. I carried it to *him*. I thought I was moving evidence for the trial."

"You were moving ammunition," I said, "for a man who plans to shoot the jury."

The lantern guttered and righted itself. Somewhere above us, Kade's boots crossed the deck, unhurried, the sound of a woman who owned the few square feet of world she was standing on and intended to keep it.

* * *

I went up for air near dawn, because a person can only read her enemies in the dark for so long before the dark starts reading back. Kade was at the rail with tea that tasted like hot salt and forgiveness, which is to say, like Kade's tea.

"Well?" she said.

"Our colonel is a grain thief with a commission and a grudge, and he intends to hang half of Vellsmark to bury his ledgers."

"Mm. And the other half?"

"The other half is for buying." I leaned beside her. The river ran gray and patient toward the sea. Across the water, Vellsmark was waking — smoke from bakeries' chimneys, the bridge lamps going out one by one, the red coats changing their watch at the market gate with all the ceremony of men who believed themselves the plot of the story instead of its stain. "Nessa. He's been trading names. That's why Tally was taken — sweat a child, get a confession, spend it against the guild. That's why the trials haven't collapsed yet. He doesn't need the ledger to convict us. He needs it only to control *which* of us convicts."

Kade drank her terrible tea and considered the water. "Your father kept accounts on the army for eleven years," she said at last. "Eleven years of lines in a book, waiting. And the man who stole it thinks the theft settled the debt." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Printers."

"What about them?"

"Nothing. Only that your whole trade seems to believe the world runs on what's written down, and soldiers seem to believe it runs on who's holding the pen, and you're both wrong, because it runs on the tide. Which waits for neither." She drained her cup. "Morning after tomorrow. Then I go, with you or without you. I've told you."

"You've told me."

"Good." She looked at me sidelong, and for a moment the smuggler's armor thinned and I saw the girl who had once stood in my father's shop watching him cut type, back when Kade was nobody's captain and my father was alive to be disappointed in her choice of careers. "Bram. Whatever's in that confession — you're not carrying it alone anymore. That's what the crew is for. Ask."

I didn't answer. Some offers you take by using them, later, when the hour comes. Hers would come.

* * *

I spent the day doing what I do best, which is working small and precise. The confession needed copying — Quill's original would go to the council, but copies, many copies, scattered copies, were the only real safety; a thing that exists once can be destroyed, but a thing that exists thirty times becomes an idea, and no army has ever successfully arrested an idea. I had matrices. I had a hand press crated in Kade's hold under the tallow bales, because Nessa Kade did not become the best mover of unlicensed goods on the river by failing to anticipate that her passengers would eventually need to print something. I set Quill's confession in eight-point over four hours, my fingers remembering my father's discipline even when the rest of me was remembering Quill's face in the coffin, and pulled two dozen clean copies by dusk.

It was while the last sheet was drying that I understood the other thing, the thing that had been circling me since the bridge like a bird deciding whether to land.

Ashe had let the ledger live because a living ledger was leverage. But leverage requires knowing the code. And the coded column — the true accounts, the grain and the money and the officers' names — was written in a cipher only a typeface cutter could read. My father had built it that way deliberately, because my father trusted no one's hands but trained ones.

Which meant Colonel Merrick Ashe had been sitting on a book he could not actually read, trading pages like a man passing counterfeit coin, hoping no one would call him on it. And the only person alive who could read his stolen treasure fluently, who had grown up under the cipher the way other children grow up under a roof —

— was me.

I set down the brayer. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Fear had been my constant companion for weeks now, and I had gotten used to its weight, the way you get used to a bad shoe. But this was different. This was the moment the fog lifted off the river and showed me the far bank: I was not fleeing Ashe. I was the one witness he could not allow to reach shore, because I could testify not just to what the ledger said but to *how it said it*, line by line, cipher by cipher, in front of any tribunal in the world.

The enemy had a face now, and the face outranked everyone I knew, and the face was afraid of a typeface cutter's daughter.

I found I could work with that.

* * *

It was nearly full dark when the boy rigger — Pip, fourteen, fearless, with the local knowledge of every channel and sandbar between Vellsmark and the estuary — came down the hold ladder faster than ladders are meant to be descended.

"Bram. Bram, you'd best come up."

The night air hit cold after the lamp-warm hold. Kade was at the stern rail, and she pointed, and I followed her finger south across the black water to where the town stood against the sky.

Weavers' Row burned.

Even at this distance I knew it by the color — print shops burn differently than houses, the paper and the pitch and the solvent taking the flame hot and white-hearted, throwing light the color of fresh-cut lead. I watched the roof of the shop where I had learned my letters and my grief and my trade go through the stages of its ruin with the strange clarity of someone standing too far away to help and too close to pretend otherwise. The floorboards where my father's matrices lay hidden. The stone sill worn hollow by a century of apprentices' boots. The font trays I had packed and labeled and taught Tally to carry, back when carrying meant something simple.

Tally was safe. Tally was free; I had bought her freedom with a lie and a deadline. Say that to yourself, I told myself, say it again, she is safe, she is on the water, she is safe. The rest was wood and paper and it had already, in some sense, been gone for years — gone since the soldiers sealed the door, gone since the warrant posted my name, gone since my father died and left the shop to a woman instead of a son and the guild had pretended not to notice. Shops are not people. Shops are places people keep themselves until they must be carried elsewhere.

My father's knife was in my belt. I had salvaged that much, and it would have to be enough, because it had always been the only inheritance that mattered: not the type, not the press, but the knowledge of what a blade can do to metal, and metal to paper, and paper to the reputations of powerful men.

"He knows," Doss said softly, beside me. He had come up without my noticing. "The second copy, the matrices — someone talked, or he guessed. He's destroying everything he can't read, on the chance it can be read."

"Then he's confirmed the one thing he hoped wasn't true," I said. My voice sounded level to me, which was either composure or shock wearing composure's coat. "You don't burn what's harmless."

Below Weavers' Row, tiny at this distance, figures moved — soldiers, containing the fire rather than fighting it, letting it eat exactly as much as their colonel wanted eaten. And beyond them, at the edge of the glow, I could make out the customs sloop riding at her moorings, dark and patient, a strongbox somewhere below her waterline with a stolen ledger sleeping inside it and a colonel's whole life coiled around it like wire.

Morning tide, Kade had said. Morning after tomorrow.

Ashe had just shown me his fear. Now I had less than two days to show him what it cost.

I turned from the burning of my childhood and went below to break the code that would break him.
