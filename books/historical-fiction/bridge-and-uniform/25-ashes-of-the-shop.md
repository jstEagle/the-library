## 25. Ashes of the Shop

The fire came at dusk, the way cowardly things do.

I was on the *Kestrel's* deck, arguing with Kade about whether a coil of her rigging rope counted as "borrowed" or "stolen" — a philosophical distinction sailors apparently take seriously — when Tally came scrambling up the gangplank with her eyes too wide and her words coming out in the wrong order.

"Bram. Bram. The shop. Bram, it's *burning*."

There is a moment, I have learned, between hearing a thing and believing it. A sliver of time where the world offers you one last chance to keep the version of events you were living in before. In that sliver, my shop was still standing. My father's press still sat in its corner wearing his dust. The type matrices still slept under the floorboards where he'd hidden them thirty years before I was born.

Then I was running.

I went through the streets like a woman pursued by something only she could see, because that was precisely what I was. People turned to watch me pass. Some of them had already been drifting toward the river district the way townsfolk drift toward any disaster — hungry for spectacle, ashamed of the hunger. Nobody ran with me. Running toward a fire that soldiers set is a different sport than watching one.

I smelled it before I saw it. Hot lead. That was the first thing, and it nearly stopped me in the street, because hot lead is the smell of my whole life — molten type, fresh casts, my father's crucible — except this was wrong, this was too much of it, a furnace's worth poured into open air, and underneath it the other smells: pitch, tar, old paper going up all at once.

The shop was already lost when I rounded the corner.

Flames stood in every window like tenants who had finally paid the rent. The sign above the door — *OKAFOR & SON, TYPEFOUNDERS*, my father's joke, since there had never been a son, only him and then me — had burned through its chains on one side and hung crooked, catching the light like a dying thing.

Soldiers held the crowd back at a comfortable distance. Not fighting the fire. *Watching* the fire. That was how I knew, before anyone told me, before Quill's confession could put a name to it, exactly whose order this was. You fight a fire that happens. You watch a fire you paid for.

I should have kept walking. Every sensible instinct I owned — and I owned several, being a woman who had spent three days smuggling evidence past an army — told me to melt into the crowd, keep my face down, protect the mission. The ledger was safe. Doss was safe. The matrices under the floorboards were—

The matrices.

That was the thought that broke me. Not the press. Not the shop. Not the childhood. It was the knowledge that Colonel Ashe had read Quill's confession — or been told of it — and understood faster than I had what mattered most. The ledger proved crimes. But the matrices *were* the guild's voice, half a century of coded names in my father's hand, proof no fire could forge and no clerk could lose. Ashe couldn't hang everyone whose name lived in those floorboards unless the floorboards went first.

And so here we were. Here was my father's shop, forty years of his life, going up in orange because a colonel I'd never met had done arithmetic with it.

"Oi." A soldier's hand closed on my arm as I pushed forward. "Stay back, laundress."

"I live—" I began, and stopped myself. Careful. Careful. I swallowed whatever my face was doing and made it into the dull mask I'd worn through three checkpoints. "My mother worked near here," I said. "I wanted to see."

He looked at me with the flat incurious eyes of a man guarding a fire rather than fighting one, and let go. Behind him, another soldier laughed at something. They were warm. They had bread somewhere. The fire was their evening's entertainment, and they would sleep well after it.

I stayed at the edge of the crowd and watched my life burn in the order it had been assembled.

First the roof, which had leaked for as long as I could remember, my father balancing on the ridge each spring with new slate, swearing at the weather like it owed him money. Then the long windows, glass cracking and sliding out in golden sheets — the same windows I used to lick as a child in winter, until my tongue froze to them once and my father laughed so hard he could barely unstick me. Then the interior, invisible now but perfectly legible to me anyway: the composing stone where I'd cut my first letter at seven years old, a lopsided *b* that my father set into a frame and printed fifty copies of, announcing to Vellsmark the arrival of "BRAM OKAFOB, PRINTER."

The press itself I did not see go. I chose not to. There are mercies available even in catastrophe, if you curate them yourself.

Tally found me in the crowd. She didn't say anything. She just took my hand, the way she used to when she first came to the shop as an apprentice and thunder rolled over the river — small, fierce, offering more comfort than she knew she had. We stood together while the second floor came down into the first with a sound like the town exhaling.

"They took the matrices?" she asked quietly.

"The fire will," I said. "Stone doesn't burn, but lead does. And the paper—"

"What paper?"

I looked at her. She looked back, and slowly her expression shifted from grief to the particular squint she got when she was about to tell me something she'd been sitting on.

"Bram. You told me yourself. What matters and what can burn." Her grip tightened. "You hid the duplicates. But your da' — he taught *you*. And you said the code was in the cutting. In the *type*."

It took me four full seconds. Four seconds of watching flame eat the doorway my father walked through every morning of his working life, while my own mind caught up to my apprentice.

The matrices were gone. But matrices are only the molds. The *code* — the cipher my father built into his letterforms, the tiny deviations in serif and stem that spelled out names to anyone who knew how to read them — the code lived in two places: the metal that was currently becoming a puddle, and the person he'd spent twenty years teaching to cut letters exactly, *exactly*, to his specification.

Me.

"You're grinning," Tally said. "At a funeral."

"I'm not grinning."

"You're absolutely grinning."

"I'm recalibrating," I said, and she snorted, and for a moment we were just two printers watching a print shop burn, trading poor jokes because the alternative was screaming, and grief is a load best shared even when it's carried badly.

* * *

The crowd thinned as full dark came, and the fire settled from rage into sullen consumption. Soldiers rotated off and new ones arrived, bored now, the spectacle reduced to embers. Somewhere behind me the town went about its business of not caring.

I waited until boredom did its work. Then Tally and I crossed the rope line in the dark between two lanterns — a trick I won't describe here, since I may need it again, but which involves knowing exactly how far away a sentry's eyes actually are versus where he believes them to be.

The shop was a skeleton. Walls standing, floors mostly gone, the cellar mouth gaping black and breathing heat. Ash fell around us like grey snow, settling on my shoulders, in my hair, tasting of my father's ledger paper and my mother's kitchen and every book I'd ever loved. I moved through the ruin by memory, because memory was the only map left.

The press was a black shape, warped but recognizable, like a body respectfully laid out. I put my hand on it. Cold already on the outside, furnace-warm beneath. "You were a good machine," I told it, and felt immediately foolish, and did not take it back.

The floorboards above the matrices were char. Beneath them, I knew without looking, the lead had run and the paper wrappers had gone to smoke. My father's careful hiding place, maintained across decades and one generation of ignorance, had lasted exactly as long as it took a colonel to want it gone.

But Ashe hadn't known everything. He'd known about matrices because Quill's confession mentioned them. What no confession could mention — because only family ever knew — was the drawer.

Under the stairs, there had always been a drawer. My father kept his cutting knives there, wrapped in oilcloth, and he'd built the drawer himself, and he'd built it the way he built everything: overengineered, doubled, with a false back he installed the winter after Mother died, though he never told me why and I never asked, because children don't ask, and that is the whole tragedy of children in one sentence.

The staircase was half-collapsed but the space beneath it held. I got my fingers into the seam where ash had welded the wood shut and pulled until my nails tore, and the false back gave, and there it was.

Oilcloth. Warm to the touch but intact. I unwrapped it with hands that shook more than I wanted them to.

Six knives. His six knives, the ones he'd carried from his apprenticeship, the ones I'd been forbidden to touch until the day he handed me the smallest and said *your turn.* Five were pitted and scorched at the edges of their wrapping. One was clean.

His graver. The knife he'd cut his final master alphabet with, the blade worn to a curve that fit no other hand — I knew this because I'd tried, as a girl, to use it, and my father had gently taken it from me and said *not yet. It'll know when.*

It knew now. Or I did. Same thing.

I wrapped the knives again, all six, and tucked them inside my coat against my chest, and stood up in the ashes of the only home I'd had since I was born, and I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger of my own: I did not cry. Not because the grief wasn't there — it was there at a magnitude I had no units for — but because crying is a thing you do when something has ended, and standing in that ruin with steel against my heart, I understood that nothing had ended. Something had been *carried out*.

They can burn the shop. They cannot burn the trade. The trade lives in the hands, and my hands were full.

Tally appeared at the broken doorway, silhouetted against ember-light, and I saw her scan my face for damage the way she'd once scanned proofs for errors.

"You got something," she said.

"I got everything," I said. "Everything that counts."

She nodded slowly, and then, because she was twelve and honest, she said, "It's still not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is going to have to be typeset from scratch. Come on."

We climbed out through what had been the window I licked in winter, and I did not look back, because looking back is for people who've finished leaving.

Behind us the chimney held until almost midnight, and then it came down too, and the whole town heard the last of Okafor & Son fall.

Ahead of us, down at the harbor, the tide was turning — and Nessa Kade did not wait past the tide. I put my hand flat against my chest, over the knives, and started to run.
