## 26. Refuge Among Strangers

The *Salt Lark* smelled like onions, tar, and a lie everyone had agreed to believe.

I came aboard at dusk, wrapped in one of Kade's spare coats two sizes too big for me, my hair stuffed under a wool cap, looking — I hoped — like exactly the kind of person nobody asks questions of on a smuggling ship: tired, damp, and probably owed money. The knife went into my belt where it had lived since the fire. Everything else I owned fit inside my coat pockets, which is either poverty or freedom depending on who's doing the counting.

Kade met me at the gangplank, arms crossed, watching me take my first step onto her deck like a woman watching a stray cat decide whether to move in.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been having an eventful week."

"Uh-huh." She didn't ask. That was the thing about Nessa Kade I was learning to love: she understood that some stories cost more to tell than they're worth, and she priced accordingly. "Cook's got stew on. There's a crate by the mast that isn't a crate, if you understand me, and you sleep in the hold, aft side, where the cargo smell covers everything else. You don't come up top after dark without reason."

"And if someone comes looking for me?"

"Then they're coming aboard my ship uninvited," Kade said, "and they'll find out what my crew does with uninvited." She turned away, then paused. "Stew's good tonight. Mutton."

It occurred to me, walking down into that rolling, creaking dark, that this was the first time in eleven days anyone had told me when dinner was.

* * *

The cook was called Marta — no surname, or at least none she'd admit to — a woman built like a dock bollard with forearms that suggested a history of either butchery or professional arm-wrestling, possibly both. She handed me a bowl before I'd finished climbing down the ladder, as if she'd been counting my footsteps.

"You're the typeface girl," she said.

"Is that public knowledge?"

"On this ship, nothing's public knowledge. It's all just known." She jerked her chin toward a crate turned on its side to make a bench. "Sit. Eat. You've got the look of somebody who's been eating standing up in doorways."

She wasn't wrong. Since the shop burned — since I stood in the street and watched Colonel Ashe's men "lose control of a small blaze" with the leisurely incompetence of arsonists who'd brought their own kindling — I had eaten standing up in doorways, walking, once in a culvert, and once in a confessional booth of a shuttered chapel whose priest had fled months ago. None of it had been mutton.

The stew was so good it made me angry. That happens sometimes, when you've been running on fear and hard bread and something warm lands in you — the body takes it as evidence that things might be otherwise, and the heart files a complaint. I ate until the bowl was empty and Marta refilled it without being asked and without comment, which is the closest thing to tenderness I'd encountered since my father died.

"There's a boy up top who wants to meet you," Marta said, scraping her pot. "Rigger. Called Fen. He's been asking about printing."

"Asking what?"

"If you can print a ballad he wrote."

"Oh no."

"It's fourteen verses," Marta said, with the flat delivery of a woman reporting damage after a storm. "About a mermaid."

"I'll hear him out," I said, "but I make no promises regarding quality."

Fen came down the ladder the way boys do — three rungs at a time, all elbows — and skidded to a stop in front of me with the reverent expression of someone meeting a famous duelist. He couldn't have been more than thirteen. His hands were rope-burned and his grin had a gap in it, and I felt something twist in my chest that I recognized, belatedly, as missing Tally. She'd been safe with the Widow Harn's family in the weavers' quarter for two days now, hidden under a name that wasn't hers, and every hour I didn't think about her was an hour I paid for later at three in the morning.

"You're really a cutter?" Fen said. "You cut the letters yourself?"

"Somebody has to. They don't grow on trees."

"I thought they were cast."

"They're cast from matrices, and someone cuts the matrices first. That's the craft. Casting is just copying. Cutting is deciding how the world gets to look when it says something." I heard my father in that sentence, whole and alive, and had to look at my bowl for a moment.

Fen nodded slowly, absorbing this. Then: "So about the mermaid—"

"Fourteen verses is a novella, Fen."

"It's a *saga*."

"I'll tell you what. When this is over, if we're both still breathing, I'll set your saga in type myself. Eight-point. Small enough to fit on one sheet, which means you'll have to cut it down to six verses."

He negotiated me to nine. I regretted it immediately and agreed anyway, because watching his face while he haggled was worth more than typographic dignity.

* * *

The third stranger found me near midnight, when I couldn't sleep for the sound of the hull working against the water — a sound I'd decided was comforting and kept having to re-decide every twenty minutes.

He was sitting on a coil of rope near the stern lantern, and I knew before he moved that he'd been a soldier. There's a way soldiers sit: back straight even at rest, weight balanced, positioned where they can see approaches. This man sat that way even off duty, even holding a mug of something that steamed, and when he saw me clocking him he gave a small nod of professional acknowledgment. *Noted. You watch people. Good.*

"Bren," he said. "Late of His Majesty's Fourth Foot."

"Bram. Late of mostly everything."

He almost smiled. Up close he was maybe thirty, with the sun-scoured face and permanent squint of someone who'd stood a lot of watches, and a healing split in his lip that hadn't come from rope work.

"You're wondering why a deserter's on a smuggler's boat," he said.

"The thought had crossed the customs office of my mind, yes."

"I walked out of the garrison four days after the requisition started. Left my kit, left my pay, left a note saying I'd gone to see my dying mother." He turned the mug in his hands. "My mother's been dead six years. But it bought me a day's head start."

"What did they do?" I asked. Not *why did you leave* — I'd learned already, from Verren and from Doss, that soldiers rarely leave because of arguments. They leave because of things seen.

Bren was quiet long enough that the lantern hissed twice.

"There was a chandler on Rope Lane," he said finally. "Old fellow, half-deaf. A sergeant decided his cellar would serve for stores and that the old man's protests were obstruction. It got out of hand. He'll live, I think. Probably." He looked at the water. "I reported it. My captain thanked me for my candor and reassigned me to latrine duty pending review. The sergeant got commended the same week for efficiency in requisitions." A shrug that tried to be light and failed. "So. Here I am, guarding contraband instead of stealing legally. It's honest work by comparison."

I thought of the ledger. Of course I thought of the ledger; I thought of it the way other people think of a bad tooth, tongue going to it constantly. Every name in it, every debt and grudge and secret in my father's careful hand — and somewhere across the water, in a strongbox on a requisitioned sloop, it sat waiting to turn eleven lives into rope. Bren had been on the inside of the machine that wanted it. I found, examining myself the way I examine a piece of type — turning it, checking for flaws — that I believed him. Or wanted to. At this point wanting to believe someone was nearly the same thing, and I was too tired to audit the difference.

"Can I ask you something tactical?" I said.

"That's usually what deserters are best at."

"The harbor quay, locked down for dawn searches. If you were planning to get past it—" I stopped. Careful. Even warmth has ears.

But Bren just studied me, and then he laughed — short, quiet, rueful. "Kade said you'd get to it eventually. Everyone always does." He took a drink. "Ask me tomorrow. Tonight you should sleep, and I should pretend I didn't hear a question I'm not supposed to have heard yet."

* * *

Morning came grey and soft, and Kade's crew let me be useful, which I hadn't been in days and hadn't known how much I needed.

There's a particular mercy in being given tasks. On land, hunted, I had been nothing but a problem to be solved — hide here, move there, be smaller, be quieter. Aboard the *Salt Lark*, I was suddenly a pair of hands, and hands have dignity regardless of whose warrant is on them. Marta put me to chopping. Fen taught me knots and mocked my technique with the cruelty only apprentices and riggers possess. Kade worked me harder than anyone, sending me up and down hatches, hauling line, counting crates, and I understood it was a test and possibly also a gift: she was filling my hours so my head couldn't fill them with the shop, the smoke, the list of names with Okafor among them.

By afternoon I could tie eight knots and had ruined one pair of sleeves with onion juice, and when Bren appeared beside me at the rail I didn't jump, though I noted privately that he'd gotten close without my hearing, which was either reassuring or terrifying.

"All right," he said. "The quay."

We talked for an hour, low, while Fen pretended not to listen from twenty feet up the rigging and did a poor job of pretending. Bren drew the quay layout for me on a slat of wood with a piece of charcoal: the customs sloop moored bow-out, the officer changeover at the fourth bell, the gap between night watch standing down and day watch mustering that ran — on a good morning — about the length of a slow whistle. He knew the guard rotations because he'd stood them until four days ago. He knew which sentries were diligent and which were asleep on their feet, and he named them, and I watched something cross his face each time he named a former comrade as lazy or corrupt or merely cold, like a man counting small betrayals and finding he could carry them.

"Why help me?" I asked at last. "You don't know me. You owe me nothing. Your mother died six years ago and you've got no stake in Vellsmark's ledger."

Bren considered the question seriously, which I appreciated. Most people answer quickly, which means they haven't thought at all.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my village flooded. River took the low fields. And there was a miller — rich fellow, high ground — who opened his doors and his granary and fed half the parish for a month and never spoke of it after. Never let anyone thank him, either. Made it worse, somehow." He looked out at the brown river. "I joined the army to be the sort of man who could do that. Somewhere along the way the army made me the other sort. This seems like a chance to correct the books."

"You're doing it for bookkeeping?"

"Aren't you?"

I had no answer to that. My father's ledger was, in the end, exactly that — an account of debts and credits in human form, names protected, names saved, mercy recorded in a code only a cutter could read. Maybe justice was just good bookkeeping done by someone stubborn enough to keep the entries true.

* * *

That evening Marta made a feast out of nothing — salt fish and hard bread transformed by some alchemy of fat and onions into a meal, plus a bottle of plum brandy that Kade produced from nowhere with the smugness of a magician. We ate on deck because the evening was mild, seven of us around a lantern: Kade, Marta, Fen, Bren, a silent sail-mender called Ochs who communicated mostly in nods, two crew whose names I caught only in passing, and me.

Nobody asked me questions. That was the miracle of it. All day I'd waited for them — the polite interrogations, the sympathetic probes, the *so how did you come to be running from the army* — and they never came. Instead they argued about whether the mermaid in Fen's saga should drown the fisherman or marry him (Marta: drown; Fen: marriage; Ochs, in his longest contribution of the evening: "both, sequels sell"). They told stories about a botched run up the Aldern coast two years ago that grew more elaborate with each retelling. Bren sang, badly, when the brandy went around the second time, and nobody mocked him except Fen, mercilessly, and Bren took it with the grin of a man rediscovering that he had a face capable of it.

I sat among them with the plum brandy warming my chest and realized I was laughing. Actually laughing, more than once, at things that were genuinely funny and not gallows-humor funny, and the strange grief of it nearly undid me. Because this was what I'd had, before — in the shop, with my father humming off-key over his cutting table and Quill arguing politics through the floorboards and Tally sweeping type dust like it was gold dust. A room full of people who didn't need explanations from each other. I had thought that room burned down with everything else. And here it was, rebuilt out of smugglers and deserters on a deck above dirty water, temporary as anything, and mine for exactly one evening.

Warmth, I thought, is fuel. My father used to say something similar about anger, that it burns fast and leaves ash, but warmth banked properly will heat you for days. Tomorrow I would need to be cold again — calculating, precise, the girl who measures the world in what can be carried. But tonight, for a few hours, I let myself be carried instead.

Kade found me at the rail afterward, both of us watching the town lights across the water. Vellsmark glimmered there, innocent-looking, full of soldiers and warrants and one colonel who thought he'd already won.

"Tomorrow, then," she said.

"Tomorrow. Fourth bell. I bring the ledger aboard your ship or I don't come aboard at all."

"That's the arrangement." She was quiet a moment. "You know the tide doesn't wait for heroes. I sail on it, with you or without you. I told you that."

"You did."

"Just making sure it's settled between us." She turned to go, then stopped, and in the lantern light her face was older and kinder than she'd ever let it be before. "Your father once held a debt of mine over my head for six years, and when I finally came to pay it he tore up the paper in front of me and said, 'Now you owe me a kindness instead, and those compound.'" She tapped the rail twice, a captain dismissing a matter. "Get your ledger, girl. Come spend your kindness where it compounds."

She went below. I stayed at the rail a while longer, committing the lights of Vellsmark to memory — the bridge, the garrison roofline, the dark shape of the customs sloop at anchor where the original ledger slept in its strongbox like a loaded thing.

That was when Fen came sliding down from the rigging, faster than seemed survivable, and landed beside me with his grin gone and his eyes too wide.

"Bram," he said. "There's a boat coming out from the quay. One man, rowing like the devil's behind him. Watchman flagged him and he shouted something and they let him through." Fen swallowed. "He's yelling a name. He's yelling yours."
