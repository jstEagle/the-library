## 27. Doss's Choice

There is a particular sound a man makes when he realizes he has been caught. I have heard it in the garrison cells a hundred times — a small, wet exhale, like a boot pulled out of deep mud. What I had never known until that evening was that the sound changes when the caught man is yourself. It is not smaller. It is enormous. It fills the whole of your skull and leaves no room for anything except arithmetic: how many steps to the door, how many eyes between you and the door, how long a coward can run before his legs remember what he is.

I was in the requisition office, doing what I did every evening at the seventh bell — copying Colonel Ashe's correspondence into the dispatch ledger in my neat clerk's hand, the hand that had fed me for eleven years of uniformed mediocrity — when the colonel set down his pen, looked at me across the candle, and said, conversationally, as if remarking on the weather:

"Doss. Where were you on the night of the twelfth?"

Now, I am not an intelligent man. I want that on the record, somewhere, in print if possible, because it is the only defense I can offer for what came next. An intelligent man would have said "in the records room, sir, cataloguing evidence," which was plausible, dull, and true enough in its way. An intelligent man would have blushed on schedule and stammered just enough to seem honest.

What I said was: "The twelfth, sir?"

And Colonel Ashe smiled at me with all the warmth of a ledger being balanced against you.

"The twelfth," he repeated. "The night the guild ledger walked out of my vault on legs that looked remarkably like your legs. Sergeant Harn has remembered a great deal since we flogged him for losing it. He remembers a key signed out in your name. He remembers you coming off duty at the wrong hour. He remembers, most vividly, that you have never once volunteered for night duty in eleven years — and yet there you are in the roster." He turned back to his papers. "You may go, Clerk. Dismissed."

Dismissed. That was the terrifying part. Not arrest, not accusation — dismissal. Ashe knew. He had known for days, and he was letting me walk free because a free clerk is a fish with a line through him; you don't reel him in until you've seen where he swims and who he swims to. And there was only one person in Vellsmark I could possibly swim to.

I collected my coat. My hands were perfectly steady, which struck me as a betrayal — after eleven years of trembling before superiors, now, when trembling was justified, my body chose dignity. I saluted. The colonel did not return it. He simply said, to the candle flame rather than to me:

"Good night, Doss. Sleep well."

* * *

Here is the thing about being a coward that no one tells you, because cowards never write it down — we're too busy surviving to be honest. It is not that we feel no courage. It is that our courage arrives late. It comes knocking at the worst possible hour, when every door it could enter has already been bricked shut, and then it stands there in the rain demanding to be let in, insistent, embarrassing, useless.

Mine arrived at roughly the fourth bell, standing in the doorway of the smokehouse behind Kade's berth where Bram Okafor had been sleeping among strangers for three nights.

I should explain how I found her, because finding her was the one clever thing I did, and even that was an accident. I had spent the evening drinking cheap gin and composing my own suicide note in my head — I got as far as *To whom it may concern, I regret the inconvenience* — when I saw a boy rigger slip down the alley by the customs wharf with a covered dish of food. A boy carrying supper to someone hiding. There was exactly one such someone in Vellsmark, and I followed him the way I have followed trouble my whole life: reluctantly, sweating, already apologizing.

So when she woke — and she woke fast, sitting up with a boning knife somehow already in her fist, which told me everything about how well she'd been sleeping — I raised both hands and said the first honest sentence of my adult life:

"I'm the man who stole the ledger out of the vault, and I need to tell you why it wasn't you they took."

She didn't lower the knife. She was entitled to that. Behind her, in the dark of the smokehouse, a cook sat up swearing, and a defector soldier I'd half-recognized from the garrison put a hand on something that was probably a rifle. Strangers, all of them, and every one of them more dangerous than me. I had come, at the end of everything, to the safest place in town, because it was full of people who owed the army nothing.

"They know it was you," Bram said. Not a question.

"This evening. Ashe let me walk out of the office with a smile on his face, which means either he's following me or he's decided I'm not worth the rope until he's finished with the printers. Both possibilities end badly for everyone whose name is in that book." I swallowed. "Which brings me to the bad news."

"You mean there's worse."

"There is always worse. I work for the army; optimism was beaten out of me in the first year." I lowered my hands, slowly. "You've been hunting the original ledger. You thought it was in the evidence vault — it isn't, hasn't been since the theft was discovered. Ashe keeps it himself. Personal strongbox, iron-banded, chained under his bunk aboard the customs sloop *Wren*. He sleeps aboard twice a week. The rest of the time the adjutant guards it, and the adjutant is loyal the way dogs are loyal, which is to say completely and for meat."

Silence. The cook lit a stub of candle, and by its light I watched Bram Okafor do sums. I have copied enough accounts in my life to recognize a woman balancing a column that will not balance — risk against reward, her neck against her name.

"Why?" she asked finally. "Why bring this to me? You could sell it to Ashe. Buy your neck back."

And there it was — the question I had been dreading, because I had been composing answers to it all the way down the alley and every one of them was a lie dressed in its Sunday coat. I could have told her about conscience. About the famine winter, about the names in the ledger, about justice. All true, in the way that bones are true — underneath, structural, invisible.

Instead I told her the truth.

"Because I'm a coward, Miss Okafor, and I have been one professionally for eleven years. I informed on the guild because Quill had already broken under questioning and my name was next, and informing seemed survivable. I kept the ledger alive instead of burning it because Ashe ordered it and refusing seemed unsurvivable. Every choice I have made since I put on this uniform has been the choice that kept me breathing one more day." I heard my own voice crack and let it. "But I have done the sums tonight over gin, and the sums say that the road I'm on ends with me hanged anyway — hanged by Ashe for knowing too much, or hanged later as his witness when the trials are done and loose ends need trimming. Cowardice has stopped paying. So I am trying the other thing. Not bravery — I want to be clear about that. This is not bravery. This is a coward changing accounts because his current bank has failed."

The cook made a noise that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The defector soldier said nothing, but he moved his hand off the rifle.

Bram looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something I did not expect and have never stopped being grateful for: she believed me. Not because my account was honorable — it wasn't — but because it was accurate. A lifetime of clerking teaches you to spot a balanced book, and apparently mine balanced.

"All right, Doss," she said. "Tell me about the sloop."

* * *

We talked until dawn, and I will record here what was agreed, partly so that someone knows, and mostly because setting it down in orderly fashion calmed my nerves better than the gin had.

The *Wren* lay at the customs wharf, aft of the guard post, under orders to hold for the dawn searches Verren had set in motion. Ashe slept aboard tomorrow night — I knew his habits from eleven years of entering them into rosters; the man was regular as tide, which is the single mercy of serving a monster. Changeover of the deck watch came at the second bell of the night watch, a quarter hour of confusion during which sentries stamped, grumbled, and counted each other wrongly. The strongbox sat under the bunk in the stern cabin. Iron-banded. Locked with a key Ashe wore on a chain around his neck, which meant the lock would have to be persuaded by other means.

"It'll need a blade," Bram said. "A thin one. Springing the hasp."

Everyone looked at her. She looked at her hands, and I remembered — because I had inventoried it, God help me, I had personally carried it into the evidence room and logged it — that her father's cutting knife had come out of the fire at the print shop in her pocket. Type-cutter's steel. Thin enough to shave a letterform, hard enough to cut brass.

"That's poetic," said the boy rigger, who was fourteen and had no sense of occasion.

"It's practical," said Bram, in the voice of someone who has learned to distrust poetry.

I gave them the rest: the cabin's location, the sentries' sightlines, the fact that the adjutant — a lumpish man named Ferris who owed me money at cards — took brandy at the ninth bell and grew sentimental. I drew the deck plan on the smokehouse floor in chalk dust, and it was the finest piece of drafting of my career, which says something bleak about my career.

Then Kade herself came in from the night air, filling the doorway the way weather fills a window, and looked me over like cargo of doubtful provenance.

"So you're the clerk," she said.

"Ness—" Bram began.

"Hush. I'm talking to the clerk." Kade crouched to my eye level, which was unnecessary and humiliating, and therefore effective. "My ship sails on the morning tide after next. If this goes wrong tomorrow night, the army will lock the harbor, and everyone in this room hangs or rots. Do you understand the weight of what you're asking these people to carry?"

"Yes," I said. "That's why I brought it to them instead of keeping it."

"And if we pull it off? If the ledger goes aboard and the wind's fair — what happens to you, clerk? You think Ashe forgives? You think a man like that leaves witnesses swimming free?"

"No," I said honestly. "I expect he spends the rest of his short institutional life trying to drown me. But he'll be spending it chasing a rumor on open water instead of hanging children in a market square, and on balance I find I prefer that ledger." I realized what I'd said a moment after saying it — *ledger*, meaning account, meaning debt, meaning everything — and to my astonishment the cook did laugh this time.

Kade studied me a while longer. Then she stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow.

"All right," she said. "You're crew."

"I'm nothing of the kind—"

"You're crew," she repeated, with the finality of a woman closing a hatch in a storm. "Cowards, saints, fools — I've sailed with all three and they're frequently the same man. Crew means we sink together or none of us does. Get some sleep. Tomorrow you'll spend your courage, and I'm told it doesn't come back."

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat by the smokehouse door with the candle stub and wrote out, in my neat clerk's hand, everything I knew about Colonel Merek Ashe — dates, payments, the names of his informants, the arrangement with Doss's handler (which was me; I was Doss's handler; the joke of my whole service was that I reported to myself) — and sealed it, addressed it to no one, and put it in my breast pocket where it lay against my heart like a stolen plate of type.

Toward morning Bram woke again and came to sit beside me without a word. We watched the river turn from black to iron to silver.

"My father kept names off the ledger," she said at last. "People who owed the guild money, people who'd informed — he crossed them out in his own secret hand. Protected them. I used to think that was weakness in a man who kept accounts for a living." She glanced at me. "I'm revising my opinion."

"Your father sounds like the sort of man who understood that a ledger records debts," I said, "but doesn't have to collect them."

She almost smiled. "Did you learn that from him?"

"No. From watching what happens to people who do collect." I folded my confession into my pocket. "Miss Okafor — when this is done, whatever happens — the trials, the ship, all of it — I'd like it known that I was not brave. I want that in the record. In print, ideally. Your type, your press. 'Clerk Doss was a coward to the last, and merely changed sides when cowardice stopped being profitable.'"

"I'll set it myself," she said gravely. "In the largest face we have."

It was, I believe, the first joke I ever made that someone else found funny, and I confess it steadied me more than any oath.

* * *

The next evening I walked back into the garrison for the last time, past the sentries who nodded at me because I was furniture to them, up the stairs to the requisition office, and into the presence of Colonel Ashe with my resignation drafted in perfect regulation format and my heart going like a drum corps.

He read it. He took his time. Then he looked up with that ledger-balancing smile and said, "Denied. Sit down, Doss. We'll discuss your recent movements in the morning."

"In the morning," I agreed, "there won't be much left to discuss."

I want it recorded that I got out of the garrison before he understood the sentence. That is not courage. Courage would have stayed. But the plan needed the garrison quiet for one more night, and a missing clerk raises alarms, and so I performed my final act of service to His Majesty's Army: I fled efficiently.

By the second bell of the night watch I stood in the shadow of the customs wharf with Bram beside me, both of us dressed in the dark of men who haul rope, watching the lanterns cross the *Wren*'s deck as the watch changed over — sentries stamping, counting each other wrongly, exactly as promised.

"There's your quarter hour," I whispered.

Bram checked the knife at her belt — her father's knife, thin as a letterform — and looked at me once, not for reassurance, exactly, but for witness.

"Strongbox under the bunk," I said. "Adjutant takes his brandy at the ninth bell. After that, the cabin is ours for as long as our luck holds."

"And when the luck runs out?"

"Then we run very fast across water that is specifically designed to slow us down," I said. "After you, Miss Okafor."

We slipped over the rail of the *Wren* together, and above us on the quarterdeck a lantern swung, and a voice I knew too well — the adjutant's, earlier than his brandy, awake and alert — called out:

"Hold there. Who's aboard?"

Below us, in the dark of the stern cabin, the strongbox waited, and the tide — our tide, the only one that mattered — would turn in four hours.
