## 28. Third Gate: The Harbor Quay

There is a particular kind of arithmetic you learn as a typeface cutter: how many ems fit in a line, how many lines in a column, how much truth you can wedge onto a single page before the paper gives up and buckles. I had spent my whole life measuring the world in what could be carried. And now, crouched behind a stack of rotting crab pots at the landward end of Harbor Quay, I was doing the sums again, and they came out ugly.

The quay was locked down. Not the casual lockdown of soldiers leaning on pikes and complaining about their boots — this was the real thing. Lanterns strung along the mooring posts at intervals tight enough that the shadows between them were thin as thread. Sentries in pairs, facing *outward*, which is the posture of men expecting someone to come in rather than something to get out. Boats at anchor with lanterns swinging at their mastheads like slow yellow pendulums. And there, third berth from the seaward end, riding high and smug on the black water: the customs sloop *Vigilant*, requisitioned two days ago for what the manifest called "storage of sensitive materials." Which meant, in plain language, Colonel Ashe's private strongbox, and inside it, the ledger with my family's name written under the word *gallows*.

Every boat leaving Vellsmark was being turned and rummaged before it cleared the harbor mouth. Every crate on the quay was being pried open by lamplight. Dawn searches, Verren had ordered — or been ordered to order, depending which way her loyalty was bending this week. Either way, it meant I had until first gray light to do the single most stupid thing of my life, and I have done several things that competed hard for the title.

"You're doing the face," Doss said beside me.

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is the one that says she's about to do something a sensible person wouldn't." He shifted, and his knee knocked a crab pot, and we both froze for the length of four heartbeats while a sentry twenty yards off turned his head, decided the night was empty, and turned back. Doss exhaled through his teeth. "I hate this. I want to state for the record that I hate this."

"Noted for the record. You're very good at records."

"That's not fair."

"It's entirely fair. You carried a stolen ledger to the garrison once. You know exactly how records work." I hadn't meant it to come out with an edge. Or maybe I had. Doss had spent most of my acquaintance being the kind of man who survived by folding — and now here he was, folded small beside me in the dark, having fled Ashe's service with nothing but a location and a guilty conscience. A coward finally spending his courage, Kade would have said. I preferred not to say anything about it at all, because every time I looked at him I thought about Quill in a coffin, and grief made me cruel, and cruelty was a luxury I couldn't afford tonight.

"The changeover," Doss whispered, when the sentry had settled, "is at the fourth bell. The night watch is tired and the day watch is resentful, and for about six minutes nobody on that deck is in command of themselves, let alone anyone else. The wardroom ladder is on the landward side. The captain's cabin is aft, below it. The strongbox will be in there, because Ashe doesn't trust his own officers any further than he can throw the *Vigilant*."

"And the lock?"

"Iron, double-bitted, army pattern." He hesitated. "I've seen you work. With your tools."

"With my knife and about ninety seconds of quiet, if my hands don't shake."

"Will they?"

I flexed my fingers in the dark. They were steady. Steadier than they had any right to be, given what they'd done in the last three days — cracked a coffin lid, held Quill's confession while I read it twice, cut the pin off a dead man's coat because some part of my mind insisted on evidence even then. Hands are honest workers. It's the rest of me that's been shaking since the fire.

"No," I said. "They won't."

* * *

Let me tell you what I had left, because the list was short enough to recite.

Disguises: none. The laundress's dress was ash along with the shop; the corpse-tender's cart had served its purpose and nearly killed me; my hair was still stiff at the temples with river water from washing off the graveyard smell. Tonight I would walk onto an army sloop as myself, Bram Okafor, whose face was on a warrant draft nailed to the market post, whose name was on a list of eleven slated for trial, whose only remaining talent was cutting letters out of metal.

Allies: one reformed informer who hated himself more than he feared the army, and one smuggler anchored beyond the point who had told me plainly, the night before, that she would not wait past the tide. "Tides don't negotiate," Nessa Kade had said, in that voice like gravel poured slowly. "Neither do I. Not anymore. I've done waiting-for-people, girl. It's how you get caught." She'd said it while mending a net, not looking at me, and I had understood it was the kindest thing anyone had said to me in weeks: a promise with no mercy in it, which means it will actually be kept.

Tools: my father's cutting knife, a coil of tarred twine, a hooded lantern with a shutter, and the partial second ledger sewn flat against my ribs, because I refused to leave it ashore even though its coded names were worth nothing unless the original went with it. Proof of conspiracy without proof of theft — half a sentence without the other half. My father had taught me that too. He used to say a line of type missing its last letter isn't shorter, it's wrong.

Time: until dawn, minus the changeover's six minutes, minus however long my nerve held.

Nerve: unknown quantity. To be determined.

* * *

We moved along the waterline where the pilings threw deep shadow, Doss ahead of me because he knew the quay's rhythms and I didn't. Twice we stopped while patrols crossed our path, and both times I pressed myself against wet timber and thought about absolutely nothing, which is a skill I learned as a child, hiding in the type cabinet during air-raid drills my father insisted on conducting against an enemy that never came. Empty your head. Be a drawer full of loose sort. Wait to be needed.

At the third bell we reached the fish-curing shed forty yards downwind of the *Vigilant*'s berth, and Doss crouched, and I realized he wasn't going another step.

"This is where I get useful instead of brave," he said. "The moment they find the strongbox open, every lantern on this water is going to swing toward that ship. Someone has to be on shore making noise somewhere else when it happens. That's me. I'm good at noise. I've spent years making sure the right rumors reached the right ears — I can make a rumor with legs in about ten minutes. By the time they're organized enough to search the shore, there'll be a story going around that the ledger was smuggled out through the north mill gate, and half the watch will go chasing it."

"You could just run," I said. "Kade would take you aboard. You know that."

He looked at me, and in the dark his face was younger than it had ever seemed in the garrison corridors, and terribly tired. "Running is the thing I'm best at," he said. "Which is exactly why I shouldn't do it." Then, before I could answer something dangerous like *thank you*: "Fourth bell. Wardroom ladder. Ninety seconds on the lock. If you're not back over the rail by the fifth bell, I start the fire early."

"The *what*?"

"The rumor needs a spark." He was already moving. "Fish oil sheds burn beautifully. Don't look so shocked, Okafor. You knew what you were asking when you asked me."

I hadn't known, quite. But he was right. I had known exactly whom I was asking, and what.

* * *

The fourth bell rang thin across the water, and the changeover began, and it was exactly as Doss had promised: a shuffling, grumbling dissolution of authority. The night watch wanted their bunks and their bread. The day watch wanted them gone. Somewhere aft a corporal was reading names off a slate and getting surly answers, and nobody was looking at the dark gap between quay and hull where a woman in river-stiffened clothes slid over the side and found the ladder rungs with her hands.

The wood was cold and slick. I climbed the way my father taught me to feed a press: steadily, without hurrying, because hurry is what makes machines bite. At the top I paused with my chin level with the deck and counted. Two silhouettes by the mainmast, arguing about rations. One at the stern rail, watching the harbor mouth, bored enough that his shoulders had become a hammock. Six minutes. Maybe five and a half, accounting for corporals.

I came over the rail low and fast and crossed the deck in the blind angle between the mast and the deckhouse, and the wardroom ladder was where Doss said it would be, and I went down it into the belly of the ship that held everything.

The captain's cabin smelled of lamp oil and another man's ambition. Ashe had been aboard recently — I could tell by the arrangement, everything squared and catalogued, papers weighted precisely, a map of the river with pencil marks where a careful man had measured escape routes he'd never need. And against the after bulkhead, squat and certain: the strongbox. Iron, double-bitted, army pattern. Exactly as advertised. There is something almost restful about an enemy who does predictable things.

I took out my father's knife.

It is a strange inheritance, a type-cutter's knife. Most people imagine a blade and think of violence. But a graver is shaped for control — a rounded handle that seats into the palm, a tip fine enough to follow the inside curve of an *o*. My father had cut ten thousand letters with this knife and never once cut himself, and when he put it in my hands at fourteen he said, "The tool knows the hand. Keep yours steady and it'll keep yours." I had used it to cut matrices, trim leads, open crates, and — three nights ago — to pry a coffin lid while I tried not to look at the face inside. Now I set its tip against the strongbox lock, felt for the wards the way I feel for the counter of a letter, and worked.

Metal whispers differently than wood. Wood complains. Metal *confesses*, quietly, if you ask in the right language. First bit gave at a count of thirty. Second bit resisted, and sweat ran down my spine, and above me the changeover argument grew louder and then quieter and then louder again, and I thought about nothing, nothing, nothing, drawer full of loose sort, wait to be needed. The second bit gave.

The lid came up on hinges that had been oiled recently, because Colonel Ashe took care of his instruments.

The ledger sat inside on top of a bundle of correspondence — I took that too, because evidence is weight and I had shoulders — wrapped in oilcloth, tied with government cord, stamped with the seal of the requisition office. My father's handwriting showed at the edge of the first page. His *O*s always had a slightly heavy left shoulder, a quirk I could pick out of a crowd of a thousand hands, and seeing it there in lamplight, in the enemy's strongbox, did something to my throat that I decided I would deal with later, when later existed.

Then the alarm bells started.

Not the fifth bell. Not Doss's fire. Something else — a bell rung hard and repeated, the naval alarm, *man the deck*, and boots hit the planking overhead in a sudden rain, and a voice shouted something about a boat alongside, and I understood two things in the same cold instant: Doss's diversion had either failed or been preempted, and someone else was coming aboard.

I stuffed the ledger and the correspondence inside my coat, cinched it, and put out the lamp, and stood in the dark of the captain's cabin listening to the *Vigilant* come alive around me like a kicked hive.

A hatch opened above the wardroom ladder. Lamplight spilled down the steps in a long yellow blade.

"Bram!" A whisper, fierce and terrified, from the top of the ladder. Tally's whisper — Tally, who should have been safe aboard Kade's ship, Tally who had been released into my keeping by a bargain struck with Verren, Tally who was apparently standing on an army sloop's deck in the middle of a lockdown because she had followed me across the rooftops since she was nine years old and saw no reason to stop now. "Bram, they're casting off the boats — there's soldiers coming along the quay —"

And behind her voice, cutting through the alarm bells with the calm of a man who owned every bell on this water, a voice I had heard only once, through a doorway, giving orders about lists:

"Search the cabins. She'll be below. Printers always go below — it's where they hide."

Colonel Ashe had come to inspect his strongbox. And the tide, Kade had told me, waits for no one.
