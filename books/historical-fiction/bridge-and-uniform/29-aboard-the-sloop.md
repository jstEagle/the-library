## 29. Aboard the Sloop

There is a particular quality to a lie you've told so many times it stops feeling like one. Doss and I had been rehearsing ours since dusk: he was escorting me as a civilian contractor brought in to inspect requisitioned printing stock for Colonel Ashe's propaganda campaign. I was the typeface expert. I had papers. The papers were forged by me, which meant they were at least beautiful, which in Vellsmark has always counted for something.

"You keep touching your collar," Doss whispered as we came down off the customs quay.

"I keep noticing my collar."

"Stop noticing it. Noticing is what gets people hanged."

"Noticing is how I stay alive, thank you. You'd know all about not noticing, wouldn't you? Years of practice in the garrison."

He gave me a look that said he was choosing not to answer, which was fair. Of the two of us, I was the one who'd spent three weeks planning this and he was the one who'd spent them being terrified, and terror at least keeps a man punctual.

The customs sloop sat at the end of the military pier like a fat cat that had wandered onto someone else's roof and decided it lived there now. *The Vigilant*, her transom said, which struck me as the sort of name officers give ships when they want to be told comforting lies about themselves. She rode low in the water, heavy with confiscated cargo — bales of the guild's paper, crates of our type, probably someone's grandmother's silver — and aft of the mainmast there was a cabin with a skylight, and beneath the cabin's false floor, if Doss's information was worth anything more than his conscience, Colonel Harlan Ashe's private strongbox.

Inside that strongbox: the guild ledger. My father's hand on every page. Every debt, every name, every secret of the district — including eleven names marked for the gallows, mine among them, written in an ink that Ashe intended to dry into law.

The tide would turn at dawn. Kade's ship would go out with it or without us. Those were the terms, and Nessa Kade does not renegotiate terms; she only forgives them, occasionally, retroactively, after a great deal of swearing.

"One hour," Doss said. "Officer changeover at the second bell. The watch comes off the gangway for muster and there's a gap of maybe four minutes where nobody official looks at the stern ladder."

"Four minutes to get aboard, find a hidden compartment, crack a locked box, and leave?"

"The cracking part's yours. I got us aboard. Division of labor."

"I thought the division of labor was that you did the treason and I did the locksmithing."

"That too." He paused at the shadow line where the pier lamps ended. "Bram. If we're caught—"

"We won't be caught. We're two professionals committing a crime with excellent paperwork."

"That's not what I was going to say."

I knew what he was going to say. He was going to say *if we're caught, they'll hang you and press-gang me*, and then he was going to apologize again for the years he'd spent informing on the guild, and I was going to have to decide, once more, whether pity was a thing I could afford tonight. It wasn't. Pity is heavy, and my father taught me to measure the world in what can be carried.

So I said, "Collar," and touched my collar one last time out of spite, and we went over the stern ladder at the second bell exactly, while somewhere on deck a bored sentry discussed the quality of garrison sausages with no one in particular.

* * *

The cabin smelled like other people's property. That's the honest description. Under the lamp-oil and the wet wool there was a smell I recognized from the evidence room at the garrison — the smell of things taken from homes: book leather, tea chests, the ghost of somebody's lavender. Ashe's floating warehouse of other lives.

Doss posted himself at the door with his ear against it. I went to work.

The floorboards were good oak, recently laid, and recently laid floors are liars. I walked the cabin heel-first the way my father taught me to walk a suspect font — listening for the board that answers differently. Third plank from the skylight. A hairline seam, filled with fresh putty, putty that hadn't been painted over because Ashe was a colonel and not a carpenter and expected never to be audited.

My father's knife went into the seam. It's a small tool, the cutting knife — a hooked blade with a horn handle worn to the shape of his grip and, over the last months, to the shape of mine — and there are perhaps six people in Vellsmark who could use it properly and four of them are dead or fled. The putty gave. The plank lifted on a hinge of dust.

The strongbox sat in its cavity like a heart in a chest.

It was iron-bound cedar, army issue, the kind quartermasters use for pay chests, and it was locked with a lever tumbler of decent make. Not excellent make. Decent. There's a difference, and the difference is that decent locks are built by men who assume the person attacking them is in a hurry and ignorant. I am always in a hurry. I refuse the second part.

I knelt and put my ear to the lid the way Tally used to put her ear against the shop door on market days, listening to the town breathe. Inside, four tumblers, oiled recently, set light. Ashe wanted fast access to his own secrets. Men like him always do. Their security is theater — locks for witnesses, guards for appearances — because their real protection is rank, and rank has never once stopped a knife or a rumor or a woman with her father's tools.

The knife's hook went into the keyhole and found the first lever. I lifted until I felt the click travel up through the handle into my wrist — a small, exact, satisfying sound, the sound of metal admitting a truth. Second lever. Click. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Somewhere below decks a sailor coughed in his sleep and I didn't so much as tremble, because my hands had been trained in a shop where a slip of a graver ruined a week's work and my father's rule was absolute: *the work deserves your calm even when your life doesn't*.

Third lever. Click.

"Bram." Doss's voice was barely air. "Boots. Forward section. Moving slow."

"They're always moving slow at changeover. Fourth lever's stubborn. Give me ten seconds."

"We don't have—"

"Ten seconds, Doss. Count them out loud if it helps you feel useful."

He counted. He counted very fast, which didn't help either of us, but there's a kind of companionship in that.

The fourth lever sat high in the lock case, and it wanted a lighter touch than the others, and I thought — absurdly, in that moment, with boots coming and the tide running out and my whole future under my hands — of my father showing me how to cut the thin strokes of a serif face. *You don't push the blade through the fine parts,* he'd said. *You ask it.* And the blade asks back, and if your hand is angry or afraid the steel knows, and the stroke goes wrong, and you throw away the letter and start again.

I asked the fourth lever. It considered. It clicked.

The lid came up on hinges that needed oiling, and there it was.

The ledger.

It looked smaller than I remembered. That was the thing I wasn't prepared for — not the danger, not the relief, but the sheer ordinariness of the object. Leather boards scuffed white at the corners. My father's blocky capitals on the spine: *GUILD OF PRINTERS & ALLIED TRADES, VELLSMARK*. The book that held eleven gallows-strings and my family's name among them, and it weighed less than a Sunday roast. All that death, and it fit under one arm.

I lifted it out and set it inside my coat, against my chest, where it sat cold as a loaf from the winter cellar.

And that was the triumph. Right there. That half-second, standing in a thief's cabin with my father's book against my heart and the empty strongbox grinning up at me from the floor.

I should have known better. In my experience triumph lasts about as long as it takes the universe to notice you enjoying something.

The plank went back. The putty went back — badly; I'd re-lay it properly later, I told myself, which is the kind of lie craftsmen tell. The knife slid into my sleeve. Doss was still counting at the door, faster now, and the boots in the forward section were not moving slow anymore.

"—nine, ten, eleven—"

"Doss."

He turned. He saw my face. He saw the shape of the ledger under my coat, because I'd bound it clumsily and it made me look like I'd stolen a Bible and swallowed half of it.

"You've got it," he breathed.

"I've got it."

"Then why are the boots coming here?"

* * *

Here is what we learned afterward, pieced together from the court-martial transcripts that Kade's council friends eventually smuggled to us, and here is what I understood in the moment only as noise: Adjutant Pell, Ashe's right hand and left conscience, had come back to the sloop that night for his own reasons. Perhaps he suspected Doss. Perhaps he kept his own ledger of his master's secrets and wanted insurance. Officers' men are always doing that — keeping little accounts against the day the accounts come due. Whatever his reason, he boarded early, found the muster missing both Doss and the civilian contractor whose papers listed her as dismissed at dusk, and did the arithmetic.

Pell raised the alarm at fourteen minutes past the second bell. I know because I heard the first bell of it while I was still crouched over a floorboard pretending to be a cabinetmaker, and the second while I was flattening myself against the cabin wall, and by the third I was running.

"Deck," Doss hissed, and shoved the cabin door open onto chaos.

Not real chaos — army chaos, which is loud men asking each other questions. Lanterns blooming along the rail. The sentry who'd been discussing sausages now shouting about intruders with the moral certainty of a man who had definitely seen nothing. Below, feet on ladders. Forward, the watch scrambling back from muster with their belts half-done.

The stern ladder was thirty feet away across open moonlit deck. Thirty feet might as well have been thirty miles.

"Distract them," I said.

"With what?"

"You're a garrison clerk. You've spent years learning how to make authority hesitate. Go be confusing."

I would like it noted that Doss did it. He stepped out from the cabin shadow, held up his papers, and began, in his best injured-bureaucrat voice, "I'll need to see the duty officer's authorization before anyone boards this vessel, that's regulation—" and it was nonsense and everyone knew it was nonsense, but soldiers will always stop for a man quoting regulations at them, the way dogs stop for a whistle. It bought me the deck. It did not buy me the ladder.

Because standing at the head of it, lantern in one hand and pistol in the other, was Adjutant Pell.

He was a thin man with the complexion of someone who reads indoors professionally, and he looked at me — hooded, coat bulging with stolen history — and I watched him do the same arithmetic he'd done below, and arrive at the same total, and smile.

"Well," he said. "The printer."

Behind him, Doss ran out of words. Ahead of me, the harbor lay black and moving, and somewhere out in that blackness Kade's ship rode at anchor with her anchor chain already groaning, because Nessa Kade waits for no tide and barely for me.

"Drop it," Pell said. "Whatever you're carrying. Drop it over the side and I might write you down as a common thief instead of a conspirator. Common thieves only hang in batches."

Everything in me wanted to argue. I'm a printer's daughter; arguing is liturgy. But my father's other rule came up instead, the one for fires and floods and soldiers: *save the type*. Save the thing that can print everything else back into existence.

I threw the lantern.

Not at Pell — I'm not a murderer and my aim is honest only at close range — but past him, into the coiled line stacked by the rail. Oil spread, flame jumped, and Pell did what any careful officer does when fire breaks out on his commanding officer's floating vault of confiscated property: he turned to shout orders. Two seconds. Three.

I went over the rail past his shoulder, hit the ladder, missed two rungs, caught a third with hands that remembered the calm the work deserved, and dropped into the harbor.

* * *

Cold. February river cold, the kind that closes around your chest like a hand checking whether you're worth carrying. The ledger against my breastbone went from cold stone to cold friend. I swam the way I'd planned to swim, which is to say badly but horizontally, toward the piling shadows under the fish quay, while above me the *Vigilant* lit up like a festival and voices multiplied and a bell started ringing — the garrison alarm, the real one, the deep-throated one I'd heard only twice before in my life, both times followed by doors kicked in before dawn.

They were looking for a woman who'd come out of the water somewhere along the military pier. I came out under the fish quay, among the pilings, and lay on wet stone with my lungs full of knives and listened to the search organize itself overhead.

Feet. Lantern light sliding across the water in long yellow bars. Pell's voice, shrill and precise: "Seal the gates. Search every boat at anchor. Wake the harbor watch — *wake them*, I don't care what they were doing—"

And underneath all of it, closer, low, from a rowboat tucked in the quay's black shadow:

"Okafor. Down here. Try not to drown before you're worth something."

A boat hook extended into the dark, and holding it, wrapped in oilskin and radiating the specific displeasure of a woman whose patience has been spent precisely to its limit, sat Nessa Kade.

"You're early," I gasped, hauling myself over the gunwale.

"Tide's early. Everything's early except you." She looked me over — soaked, shaking, hood gone, coat sagging with the unmistakable rectangular weight of salvation — and something in her face unclenched by a degree, which for Kade is an embrace. "Tell me you've got it."

I pulled the ledger out and put it in her hands. Water had gotten at the edges; my father's capitals had gone soft at the corners. Kade turned it over once, weighing it the way I weigh type, and handed it back.

"No," she said. "You carry it. That was always the price. Get it to the ship yourself or it doesn't count." She took up the oars. "Besides — you're going to want to see the harbor from the water."

"Why?"

"Because," she said, pulling for the channel, "while you were busy being heroic on the colonel's boat, the dawn search order came through early. Verren's marines are already mustered on the quay. Every gangway, every ladder, every hull between here and open river gets turned out at first light." She nodded back toward the shore, where the alarm bell was still swinging and lanterns were multiplying along the pier like a fever rash. "And unless I've misread my bells, first light is about forty minutes off, and you are about forty minutes wet."

We pulled out into the current. Behind us the *Vigilant* blazed with confusion; ahead, the channel ran black and wide and, at its far end, impossibly, the riding light of Kade's ship — a single small flame bobbing on the water, the last fixed point between me and the rest of my life.

Forty minutes. One river. Half a garrison between.

"Row faster," I said.

Kade smiled for the first time all night, and it was not a reassuring sight. "Now she's a sailor."
