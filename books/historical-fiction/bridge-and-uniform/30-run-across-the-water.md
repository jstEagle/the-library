## 30. Run Across the Water

The alarm bell has a voice I know.

I have heard it ring for fires, for floods, once — memorably — for a runaway cart of festival beer that took out half the fishmongers' stalls. It is a town bell, a civic bell, a bell with a personality like a fat alderman: pompous, insistent, convinced that everything is its business. Tonight it sounds different. Tonight it is being rung by soldiers who want very much to find me, and it carries across the water the way my father's name used to carry through the shop when he was displeased with me — flat, cold, and inescapable.

"Left," Doss hisses at my shoulder. "Go left, not right."

"Why left?"

"Because everyone runs right toward Kade's ship. Ashe knows that. He's not stupid, which is the single most inconvenient thing about him."

We come out from behind the customs sloop's stern at a dead run, and the docks open up ahead of us like a bad idea. Lanterns are blooming along the quay one by one, orange dots multiplying, and beneath them boots — dozens of boots, hitting the boards in that rolling thunder only soldiers and impatient audiences can make. Somewhere behind us a whistle shrills three times, then again. They've seen us. Of course they've seen us. I am a woman in stolen officer's gloves carrying a ledger worth eleven lives, running down a pier with all the grace of a startled goose. We are not subtle. We were never going to be subtle.

"The ledger," I say, and even now, breathless, it comes out as an instruction rather than a question. Some part of me has been writing this moment since chapter one of my life, so to speak. "Take it."

Doss stops so abruptly he nearly trips over his own courage. "What?"

"You're faster than me. You're lighter. You know where Kade's crew will be waiting." I shove the wrapped bundle against his chest and feel him flinch around it like it's hot lead, which, morally speaking, it is. "I'll go the other way and pull them after me. You get aboard."

"Bram—"

"Doss." His name stops him better than any argument would. A month ago this man was a clerk forging names into other men's graves, and I hated him on principle. Now I look at him and see something worse: a coward trying on bravery for size and finding it fits better than he'd like. "You spent your whole life making sure you survived. Spend tonight making sure *this* survives instead. That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

He looks at the ledger. He looks at me. Behind us the whistles multiply, greedy.

"The pier at the end," he says quickly. "The old herring pier — it's rotten past the third piling, no soldiers use it, but there's a skiff tied under the shack. If they corner you—"

"They won't corner me."

"If they corner you," he insists, because he is, God help me, developing a spine at the least convenient hour, "you get in the skiff and you row north along the pilings until you hear our bell. Two rings. That's Kade. Two rings."

"And if I don't hear two rings?"

He doesn't answer, which is answer enough, and then he does the bravest thing I've ever watched him do: he turns and runs, clutching my father's ledger to his chest like a man carrying his own acquittal, and he doesn't look back.

I count to ten. Then I step out onto the main quay, into the lantern light, and I scream — actually scream, loud enough to hurt my own throat — *"He's here! The printer's daughter! She's headed for the fish gate!"* — and I run west, away from everything I'm trying to save, and the boots behind me turn like a compass needle finding north.

It works. That's the obscene thing. It works beautifully.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about being chased, because I've had an educational evening and someone ought to benefit.

First: fear is fast but stupid. The soldiers chasing me are afraid of their colonel, which means they run where the shouting points, and the shouting is pointing wherever I say it is. I've sent six of them charging toward the fish gate, which is closed, locked, and on the far side of a warehouse full of nothing but salted herring and disappointment.

Second: the body keeps accounts. My legs are already presenting their invoice — a night in a records room, a coffin carried, a strongbox cracked, and now a sprint across half a mile of dockside — and the interest compounds by the step. There's a stitch in my side with ambitions of becoming a knife.

Third: you cannot outrun a town you love. Every corner of Vellsmark is slowing me down. The cooper's yard where father bought his packing straw. The alley behind Maren's bakehouse where Tally burned her first batch of type-cleaning rags and swore she'd never touch fire again, and here we both are. I keep wanting to apologize to the buildings. This is not useful. I run anyway.

They're herding me. I can hear it happening — whistles left and right, boots cutting lanes across the quay, the unhurried confidence of people who own the map. Fine. Let them herd. I know where the map ends.

The old herring pier juts out past the customs sheds like a splinter nobody bothered to pull. It hasn't been used since the herring moved upriver, which was before the war scare, which was before the requisition, which was before everything. The army doesn't guard it because there's nothing on it. There is nothing on it except a rotting shack, a hidden skiff, and the last thirty yards of my plan, which is to say the last thirty yards of me.

I come out of the shadows between the sheds and stop, because there is a soldier at the foot of the pier.

Only one. Young. So young his uniform still has the shop-fold creases in it, collar stiff as a new page. He has a lantern and a halberd and the expression of a boy who volunteered to stand watch out here precisely so nothing would happen to him, and who is now watching something happen.

"That's far enough," he says.

I put my hands on my knees and breathe. It occurs to me, distantly, that I could lie to him. I've lied my way past sergeants, clerks, and one genuinely terrifying quartermaster tonight; my mouth has been doing piecework all evening. But I look at this boy — sixteen, maybe seventeen, the age father started me on the graver — and I find I've run clean out of lies. Or perhaps it's that the ones remaining are too heavy to carry another step.

"Yes," I agree. "It is."

"Turn around. Hands where I can see them."

"Do you know what's in that ledger they're hunting?" I ask. I straighten up slowly. My side is screaming. My voice, remarkably, isn't. "Eleven names. Eleven people marked to hang so that a colonel can bury his own thefts. One of those names is mine. Another was a man named Quill, who was so frightened of what he'd become that he lay down in a coffin to end it." The boy's face does something complicated. Word travels fast among the ranks about things that frighten them; Quill's death is already a story, I can see it, already growing teeth. "You're guarding the wrong end of this pier for the wrong man. But you're just going to do your duty, aren't you."

"I—" He swallows. "Yes."

"Good lad," I say, and I run straight past him.

To his eternal credit — and I mean that, I hope someone writes it down somewhere — he swings the halberd wide. Not at me. At the lantern, which goes over with a crack and a spill of burning oil against the shed wall, and suddenly the night behind me is full of shouting about *fire*, which scatters the pursuit like stones in a pond. Whether he did it for me or for himself or simply because some deep animal part of him chose chaos over complicity, I will never know. I owe him a debt I can't repay. I add it to the pile.

Then I'm on the pier, and it's just me and the boards and the black water moving underneath.

* * *

The tide is turning.

I feel it before I see it — the pilings beginning to groan in a different register, the current sliding past the pier's flank changing its mind. On the ebb it ran seaward, steady, patient, the river emptying itself toward the estuary where Kade's ship waits. Now, slack water, that held-breath hour when the sea argues with the land and neither wins. In twenty minutes, maybe less, the flood will start pushing *inward*, and every rower on this river will be rowing uphill.

Twenty minutes. Everything in my life has come down to tides lately. It's a vulgar way to measure a soul, but it's honest, and honesty is in short supply.

Past the third piling the boards go soft. I feel them give under my boots like stale bread, and I slow, testing each step, which is how I come to notice the shack — leaning, tar-black, door hanging by one hinge — and beneath it, exactly where Doss said, the dark shape of the skiff, tied low in the shadow of the pilings. My escape, floating patiently, waiting to be claimed.

And behind me, at the head of the pier, boots.

Not many. Three, maybe four — the fire split them, but soldiers are like ink stains, they regroup. Their lantern light reaches down the pier in a long yellow finger, touching the boards one plank at a time, coming for me the way dawn comes: inevitably, and without any interest in my opinion.

I stand at the end of the rotten pier with the water breathing below me, and I take inventory.

This is what I have: one knife, my father's, warm in my belt from my own body heat. One pair of stolen gloves, ruined. No ledger — the ledger is on its way to a smuggler's hold, tucked against the chest of the most unlikely hero this revolution will never credit. What I do not have: a way off this pier that doesn't involve the skiff, and the skiff means the flood tide, and the flood tide means I'd be rowing against the whole turning world while four men with a lantern watched me do it.

So this is where it happens, then.

I think you believe, right up until it arrives, that you'll meet moments like this with terror. I had imagined it often enough in the dark weeks behind me — imagined the cell, the rope, the small white pamphlet they'd print afterward listing my crimes in someone else's typeface. I always imagined fear. What I find instead, standing on rotten wood above black water, is something closer to arithmetic. Calm, cold, and clear as a freshly cut counter. *If I stay, they take me, and whatever I know dies with me slowly and loudly. If I go into the water and let the ebb carry me out past the point, I might make the estuary. I probably won't. Either way, they spend tonight hunting a drowned girl instead of stopping a boat.*

Either way, the ledger sails.

And here is the thing that undoes me, standing there — the thing I have been walking toward all this time without knowing the address. I understand my father. Finally, completely, the way you understand a typeface only after you've cut it yourself. Everyone thought he was a careful man, a small man, a man who measured twice and spoke once. He was. And underneath the care, hidden in the ledgers, in the matrices under the floorboards, in every name he quietly struck from the lists — there was this exact arithmetic, performed over and over, without applause. *My safety against theirs. My name against their lives.* He wasn't brave the way songs say brave is. He was brave the way I am right now: doing the sums, finding the total unacceptable, and paying the difference himself.

*Measure the world in what can be carried,* he used to say, weighing a font tray in his hands like it was nothing. I thought he meant tools. He meant this. He meant that in the end you carry only what you chose to save, and you set down everything else — including, if it comes to it, yourself.

"All right, Da," I say aloud, to nobody, to the water. "I see it now. Took me long enough. You could have just told me."

He would have said that telling spoils the cutting. You have to find the letter's shape inside the lead yourself, or it never truly belongs to you.

The lantern light touches the fourth piling. Voices now, close, calling for me to stop, gentle as wolves. I draw my father's knife — not to fight; there's no fight here, and we both know it — but because I want it in my hand, the last true thing, and because a knife cuts rope as well as anything else. I crouch, saw through the skiff's mooring line in three strokes, and shove the little boat out into the current with the last of my strength, empty. Let them chase a boat. Let them waste their flood tide on it. Let the arithmetic work.

Then the ebb takes it, and takes my breath with it, and I stand up at the end of the world with the river pulling at everything loose in me.

"Okafor!" The shout comes from the pier's head, ragged with run. "Hold where you are!"

"Holding," I call back, wry even now, because wryness is the last garment I own that still fits. "Though I should mention the boards past the third piling are rotten, and I wouldn't recommend—"

The first board cracks under the sergeant's boot like a gunshot, and the whole pier shudders, and for one absurd heartbeat every one of us — pursuers and pursued alike — stands perfectly still, listening to forty years of dry rot decide the outcome of a revolution.

The second board goes. The sergeant drops through to his waist, howling, lantern and all, and the men behind him fall back off the bad planks, and I am alone at the tip of the pier, untouchable for exactly as long as the rot holds, which is to say: briefly.

Below me the water slides past, black and silver, running seaward, running out. The tide is almost done. When it turns, I turn with it or I drown in the attempt, and both of those sound entirely reasonable from where I'm standing.

Two rings, Doss said. Listen for two rings.

Out on the dark river, faint as a memory, a bell rings twice.
