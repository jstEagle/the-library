## 31. Verren at the Pier

I have stood at the end of many piers in my life. I have stood there to watch ships come in, to watch them go out, once — memorably — to fish a drowned hat out of the water while Tally laughed herself sick behind me. But I had never before stood at the end of one with soldiers' boots drumming the planks behind me and the tide going out beneath my feet like sand through an hourglass.

The ledger was gone. Doss had it, tucked under his coat like a stolen loaf, running the long way around the customs house toward Kade's ship. I had sent him because someone had to carry it and someone had to be caught, and of the two of us, I was faster and he was more expendable — by which I mean he had convinced me, in his trembling clerk's way, that a coward who has finally found something worth being brave about should not waste it on carrying. He should spend it on standing still.

So I stood still.

"Stop her!" The voice belonged to Ashe's adjutant, a young man whose name I never learned and did not mourn. "She has documents on—"

Boots on planks. Lantern light swinging across the water in long yellow blades. I counted them the way my father taught me to count type: by weight, not number. Six men. Seven. Eight, fanning along the quay with the easy confidence of people who believed the pier ended in front of them and nowhere else.

It ended in front of me. Behind me was only the river, black and patient, and beyond it, somewhere in the dark, a smuggler's ship that would wait exactly as long as the tide allowed and not one breath longer.

I put my back to the water and my hands where they could see them, because I am many things but not stupid.

"There," the adjutant said. "Hold her."

And then, from somewhere behind the wall of lanterns, another voice — flat, unhurried, carrying the particular authority of a person who has never once needed to shout.

"Hold her is not an order I gave."

The lanterns parted, and Captain Ilse Verren came through them like a blade coming through wet canvas.

* * *

You want to know what she looked like. Everyone always wants to know what she looked like at that moment, as if the whole turning of a revolution might hang on the set of a woman's jaw. Fine. She looked tired. She looked like a woman who had been reading late — and I knew, by then, exactly what she had been reading, and whose names were in it, including her own. Her coat was buttoned wrong at the bottom. I noticed that. Of everything in that moment — the bayonets, the tide, the bells still clanging from the alarm aboard the sloop — it is the misbuttoned coat I keep.

"Captain Verren," the adjutant said, and his spine did the arithmetic of rank for him. "Ma'am, this woman is under—"

"This woman is nothing." Verren did not look at me. It was, I realized later, a kindness. You cannot interrogate a face you are not facing. "This pier is a requisitioned military asset, Sergeant, and I am ordering it cleared. There is a warehouse on the east quay — the salt house, third from the corner. Lights were reported inside it twenty minutes ago. That is consistent with the theft aboard the sloop. You will search it. Now."

The adjutant hesitated, which was either courage or confusion; with young officers it is usually both. "Ma'am, the thief went west, along the waterline—"

"The *report*, Lieutenant, went east." Verren's voice did not rise. It lowered, the way water lowers before it takes the door off its hinges. "Reports are evidence. Thieves are noise. Search the salt house."

He went. They all went — eight lanterns bobbing away down the quay toward a warehouse that contained, as far as I know, several hundred sacks of grey salt and absolutely nothing else worth a soldier's bootleather. And then it was just the two of us, and the river, and the far-off creak of Kade's ship getting ready to leave without me.

Verren turned to face me.

"Well," I said, because someone had to, and apparently it was going to be the criminal. "That was a very confident lie. Salt house. Was there ever anything in the salt house?"

"No." She came toward me slowly, boots quiet on the wet planks, and stopped an arm's length away — close enough to arrest, far enough to talk. "But they don't know that, and by the time they do, the tide will have made its decision." She glanced past me at the water. "How long?"

"Minutes. Fewer than I'd like."

"And the ledger?"

"Gone. Safe. On its way to a ship you're going to pretend you never saw."

"That remains to be pretended." She took off her glove — one glove, the left, folding it over her belt the way she used to when she was thinking, back when thinking happened in our kitchen over my father's bad coffee — and looked at me with an expression I could not read at all, which frightened me more than the eight soldiers had. "Do you know how this ends for me, Bram Okafor?"

"I have some idea."

"A court-martial. At best. Aiding a fugitive, obstructing a lawful investigation, misdirecting troops under fire conditions — they will find words for it, they always find words. At worst—" she shrugged, a small economical gesture, a soldier's shrug, "—a post and a rope, and a proclamation printed about treason, probably by your guild, if any of you survive to print it."

"You could walk away right now," I said. "Order them back from the salt house. Give me up. Say you arrived too late. No one would question it. You'd lose nothing."

"Nothing except the ledger staying lost, the eleven names hanging, and Colonel Ashe free to bury every theft he ever committed under a mountain of printer's corpses." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Yes. I could lose nothing. Except myself."

"Then why haven't you done it?" It came out harder than I meant. The tide was pulling at the pilings below us, and every second of this conversation was a second Doss was running and Kade was counting. "Why are we standing here? Why did you send them east?"

"Because I wanted to ask you a question first." She folded her arms, the other glove still on, formal as a tribunal even standing on a rotting pier in the dark. "One question. Answer it honestly and I'll walk you past every sentry between here and your smuggler under my own authority. Lie to me, and I'll give you to the adjutant and sleep tonight for the first time in a month."

"Ask, then."

"Why," said Captain Ilse Verren, "should I burn thirty years of service for a stranger's daughter?"

* * *

From Verren's own account, written later, in a hand that pressed hard enough to dent three pages deep:

*I knew her father before I knew her. That matters. Put it first.*

*The famine winter — 12 years ago, though the army's ledgers record it as a supply reallocation, which is the army's way of saying the granaries stayed locked while the town ate pigeons and shoe leather. I was a lieutenant then, newly posted, garrison rations cut to punish the district for smuggling. I remember the arithmetic of it: half a loaf a day, issued at dawn, gone by noon. I remember learning that hunger is not one feeling but many — boredom first, then shame, then a kind of anger that lives in the teeth.*

*I met Josiah Okafor because I arrested his apprentice.*

*The boy had lifted a side of salt pork from the garrison stores. Open-and-shut. I marched him to the shop so the master printer could witness the charge, and Okafor listened to the whole thing, thanked me politely, and then asked whether I had eaten. I said it was none of his concern. He said everything that walked through his door was his concern, that was the trade — ink sticks to everything — and sat me down and fed me smoked fish and dark bread until I was ashamed of how much of it I ate.*

*They fed me all winter. Not charity — Okafor was precise about that. Work. He claimed a lieutenant's letters home required good typography, and paid me in dinners I did not need and could not refuse. His daughter watched me across the table, nine years old, solemn as a magistrate, and asked me once why the army let its own people starve. I told her armies are machines, and machines eat what they are fed. She said that was a stupid answer. She was right.*

*I reported the rationing. In writing. Three times. Each report went into a file and the file went into a drawer, and the drawer, I later learned, was in the office of a colonel named Ashe, whose signature appears in the guild ledger beside payments marked "for discretion" during precisely those months. I did not know that then. I knew only that the machine did not answer, and a printer's family fed me anyway, and I owed a debt no regulation recognizes.*

*Debts like that accrue interest. Twelve years of it.*

*So: why burn thirty years of service? Because thirty years bought me the rank to choose, and most people never get to choose anything. Because the ledger in that girl's keeping contains my own name among the bought and the looking-away, and I would rather it hang me in print than save me in silence. Because a child once told me my answer was stupid, and I have spent twelve years failing to think of a better one.*

*Because she is not a stranger's daughter. She is the daughter of the man who fed me when my own uniform let me starve. There is no such thing as a stranger's daughter. There is only whose table you have sat at.*

*These are the reasons I assembled while walking to the pier. I intended to use the best of them.*

*Then I reached her, and saw her father's jaw set in her face, and understood I would not need any of them. One does not explain a debt to oneself. One pays it.*

* * *

"A stranger's daughter," I repeated. My hands had closed into fists without consulting me. "Is that what I am to you?"

"You know you're not." For a moment the tribunal fell off her face and underneath it was something older and rawer — the woman who had sat at our table through a famine winter, eating my father's smoked fish and refusing, every single night, to admit she was hungry. "That is precisely why I'm asking. If you were a stranger, Bram, I'd arrest you, sleep soundly, and be promoted inside the year. You are the single most expensive acquaintance of my life. So convince me. Tell me why the ledger is worth more than my oath."

"It isn't worth more than your oath." I said it before I could stop myself, and saw her eyebrows lift. "You asked for honesty, so here it is: the ledger is paper. Ink and paper. Worthless — my father would tell you so himself, and he'd say it in four languages. What's worth something is what happens when it's *read*. Eleven people in Vellsmark are scheduled to hang on testimony that ledger disproves. A colonel stole it to hang them and hide himself. Your oath says protect and serve, Captain. Somewhere between the famine winter and tonight, somebody moved 'protect' to mean protecting Colonel Ashe, and I don't think you signed that version."

The tide sucked at the pilings. Downriver, faint as a heartbeat, I heard the sound of a windlass — Kade's anchor coming up.

"You've rehearsed that," Verren said.

"Every step of the way here. Three checkpoints' worth of rehearsal."

"Mm." She looked out at the water, and I watched thirty years of service fight it out on her face with one winter of smoked fish and dark bread. "When you were nine years old," she said at last, "you asked me why the army let its own people starve. Do you remember?"

"I remember you gave a stupid answer."

"I remember you telling me it was stupid." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; the scar tissue where a smile might have been. "I have thought about that question for twelve years, Bram. Off and on. Usually at inconvenient moments. And I have finally arrived at an answer I can live with, which is this: the army lets people starve because the army is a machine, and machines cannot be ashamed. Only people can." She straightened. "So tonight I am resigning from the machine."

She unpinned her commission.

I had seen it before — a heavy thing, engraved, pinned above her heart the way other people wear lockets. She held it a moment in the lantern-light from the distant quay, and I watched her read it one last time, the way you reread a letter from someone dead. Then she took it by both ends.

"Captain—" I started, and didn't know how to finish. Thank you felt obscene. Stop felt worse.

"My mother cried when I received this," Verren said conversationally. "First officer in the family. She told the whole street. I often wonder what she'd make of this part." And she tore it in half with a sound like a small clean bone breaking, and let the pieces fall between the planks into the black water, where the tide took them the same direction it was taking me.

The silence after was enormous. Bigger than the river.

"Now," she said, picking up my satchel from the planks and settling it over her own shoulder with brisk, infuriating competence, "we are both criminals, which means we travel fast and trust no one. Walk beside me, look miserable — that part requires no acting — and if anyone challenges us, they answer to a captain with no rank, which confuses people long enough to get past them. Move."

We moved. Up the pier, past a sentry who snapped to attention at the sight of her coat and then visibly failed to compute why a captain was escorting a laundress-smuggler-fugitive at a dead-run hour of the morning — Verren said "Quay inspection," in a voice that closed the subject like a ledger — and past the salt house where eight lanterns were busy discovering nothing, and along the last stretch of dark water where Kade's ship sat low and eager on the turning tide.

A boat waited at the ladder. Doss huddled in it, white-faced, alive, the bundle safe against his chest. Nessa Kade stood in the stern, and when she saw Verren's coat her hand went to her knife, and when she saw Verren's face — bare, unbadged, the commission gone from above the heart — her hand stopped.

"Captain," Kade said carefully.

"Not anymore." Verren handed me the satchel. Then she took off her coat — the whole coat, the uniform coat, the last thirty years of it — and held it out to Kade. "There's a woman in the garrison laundry named Marta Helt who owes me a favor. She can dress me as crew by sunrise, if you have room for one more who knows every patrol route on this river."

Kade looked at the coat. Then at me. Then at the tide, which waits for no one, least of all the converted.

"Room for everyone who rows," she said. "Get in."

I got in. I turned once, at the last, and looked back — at Vellsmark smoking on its hill, at the empty pier, at Ilse Verren standing on the boards in her shirtsleeves in the cold with her career floating somewhere out in the estuary in two torn halves.

"Captain," I said — because whatever else she was now, she would always be that to me. "Why? Truly. One honest reason."

Verren considered. Out on the water, the anchor came up for good, and the current took the hull, and the boat swung toward the ship.

"Because when I was hungry," she said, "your father never once asked what it would cost him to feed me. I've simply decided to stop asking, too."

Then she took the oars, and rowed me out to the rest of my life.
