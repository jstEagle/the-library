## 33. Morning Tide

The anchor chain was already groaning when I came down the last stretch of quay, and I have never hated a sound more.

You'd think a woman who had just crossed a city of soldiers with a stolen ledger sewn into her coat, after a night that included a strongbox, an alarm bell, and a sprint across half a mile of wet planking, would be granted at least one small mercy — a gangplank that waited, say, or lungs that didn't feel like someone had packed them with river gravel for spite. The tide does not grant mercies. The tide keeps its own ledger, and it was calling in debts.

"Nessa!" I shouted, and my voice came out as a croak, because of course it did. "Nessa Kade!"

The *Magpie* sat low in the black water, lanterns doused, her rigging a scribble against the graying sky. Figures moved along her deck. Nobody stopped moving. Smugglers weigh a shouted name against a rising tide and a garrison waking up behind them, and the arithmetic is never sentimental.

Then a shape swung over the rail — not a plank, Kade would never waste a plank on me — and dropped to the quay with the grace of a cat that had made peace with its own clumsiness years ago.

"You're late," Nessa Kade said.

"I'm here."

"You're late *and* you're here. Only one of those counts." She looked past me, down the empty length of the quay, and her face did something complicated. "Where's Doss?"

"Ahead of me. He should be—" I stopped. The truth was I hadn't seen Doss since the customs sloop, since the alarm went up and I shoved the ledger into his arms and told him to run for the water while I made noise in the other direction. Making noise is easy. Knowing whether the person you sent away heard you is not. "He should be aboard."

"He's not aboard."

For one full second the morning tilted. Then a hatch clattered open amidships and a voice said, querulous, seasick, and unmistakably alive: "Is she dead? Tell me if she's dead, I don't want to look."

Doss's face appeared over the rail, pale as unprinted paper, clutching the wrapped bundle to his chest like a child who had been told to hold it *forever* and had taken the instruction literally.

"She's alive," Kade called back.

"Thank the saints," Doss said, and then, with feeling, "I'm going to be sick again."

"Get her aboard," Kade told the rail in general, and hands hauled me up and over, and the *Magpie* took my weight the way ships do — reluctantly, then completely.

* * *

There is a particular hour, just before dawn, when a river town looks like it is holding its breath. Vellsmark did not hold its breath that morning. It woke up shouting.

From the deck I could see it: torchlight blooming along the harbor front like fever spots, whistles cutting across the water, the garrison gates throwing out soldiers the way a kicked nest throws out wasps. Smoke threaded up from somewhere near the customs house — not the shop, I checked, God help me, I checked first, and the shop's chimneys stood dark and cold against the lightening sky — smoke from torches, from a brazier overturned, from a town discovering overnight that eleven of its printers were no longer going to hang and not yet knowing what to feel about it.

Kade stood beside me at the taffeta rail, arms folded, watching the same scene with a smuggler's professional detachment.

"They'll muster a boat," she said. "Question is whose boat, and how fast. Ashe has a cutter berthed upstream. Fast hull. If he's awake—"

"He's awake," I said. "He's been awake since the alarm."

"Then we don't linger for sentiment." She turned and put two fingers in her mouth and produced a whistle that scattered the gulls off the bridge. "*Magpie*! Tide's ours — warp off, fore and aft! Move like your mothers are watching and disappointed!"

The crew moved. I had met most of them in fragments over the past days — the cook, Marla, who fed me without questions and then asked questions anyway, gently, over bread; the boy rigger, Pip, all elbows and local knowledge; Hesk, the defector soldier, who still stood at attention when startled, which was often. They were strangers who had become, without my noticing the exact moment, the people I would trust with my life. That is either how found families work or how drowning works. That morning I couldn't have told you the difference.

The *Magpie* slid away from the quay with the shameless ease of a vessel that has left places it wasn't supposed to leave many times before. The gap between hull and stone widened. A soldier reached the pier's end as we cleared it, raised a musket, and then lowered it, because whoever had given the orders that morning had given confused ones, and confusion, I was learning, is a smuggler's best sail.

I looked back once. Just once. The piers were emptying, torches guttering in the gray, and above the warehouse roofs Vellsmark's bells had begun to ring — not the alarm pattern, something older, the market bell, ringing because bells ring in the morning regardless of what the night has done. Somewhere behind those rooftops a captain was tearing her career into pieces, or being arrested for having done so, and a colonel was commandeering whatever fast thing would carry him, and a print shop that had already burned was still burning in my memory, which is where it burns now, permanently, quietly, like a lamp left on in an empty room.

My father's knife was in my pocket. It was the only thing I had salvaged. I put my hand around it the way other people pray.

* * *

Below decks, the hold smelled of tar, salt pork, and forty years of cargo that had never quite been declared. Kade cleared a space between crates with her boot and said, "All right. Show me what we nearly died for."

We laid it out on a crate lid like relics on an altar. The guild ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth, damp at one corner but sound — every printer, debtor, sympathizer, and secret in Vellsmark, in my father's hand. Quill's confession, taken from his coat in the coffin, the ink blotted where his hand had shaken but every word legible, naming Colonel Ashe as the man who had tortured a confession out of him years ago and then used him ever since. And Doss's testimony, which Doss had written out during the crossing of the harbor in a hand that got steadily worse the closer the alarm bells got, swearing to the thefts, the forged evidence, the handler who had bought him with fear and kept him with more fear.

Three documents. Paper and ink. Together they weighed less than the font tray I carried every day at the shop. Together they outweighed everything in Vellsmark.

Kade read them in silence, which for her was a kind of reverence. When she finished Quill's confession she set it down carefully, as if it might go off.

"The man in the coffin," she said. "That was Quill."

"Yes."

"And you talked a sergeant out of opening it."

"For about thirty seconds longer than I deserved to."

She snorted — almost a laugh, which from Kade was applause. Then her face settled into something else. "Your father's name is in that book. Yours too. Marked for the gallows."

"It was."

"And now it's in there." She tapped the ledger. "Every debt, every name, every favor your father ever did or undid. Including," and here she gave me a long look, "a printing debt owed by one Nessa Kade, which I notice runs to sums I don't remember agreeing to."

"He inflated it," I said. "He always said you were good for it eventually, and that inflating the number improved your character."

"Wise man." She was quiet a moment. Out on deck, feet thundered, canvas cracked, and the *Magpie* leaned into the current like a dog slipping a leash. "So what happens to this now?"

"Now it goes where it can't be burned. There's a council — revolutionaries, whatever we're calling them this season — waiting on a neutral merchantman at the estuary. They copy it. They scatter the copies. After that, no colonel can burn it, no army can seize it, and Ashe can't make it disappear by hanging everyone who read it."

"And us?"

"We sail there. Fast."

Kade nodded slowly, and I watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing since the night she agreed to this: the value of the cargo against the risk of the voyage, the debt against the danger. I knew which way it had come out, or I wouldn't have been standing on her deck. But knowing a friend's arithmetic doesn't stop you from needing to hear the total aloud.

"Well," she said at last, closing the crate lid over the papers. "Your father carried my debt ten years without pressing. I suppose I can carry his book one river."

* * *

Doss found me at the galley, where Marla was force-feeding me salt pork and questions in equal measure. He hovered at the doorway looking like a clerk who had wandered into the wrong epic.

"You made noise," he said. "Back at the sloop. So I could run."

"That was the arrangement."

"I wanted to say." He stopped. Started again. Doss had spent his whole life drafting sentences he didn't dare send. "I've informed on people. Good people. I told myself it was survival, and maybe it was, but it was also cowardice wearing survival's coat, and I know the difference now, and—" His jaw worked. "I just wanted to say I know the difference now."

"Eat something," I said, because I am not equipped for men mid-repentance before breakfast. But I pushed the plate toward him, and he sat, and that seemed to count for more than anything clever I could have said. Cowards spend their courage in strange denominations. Doss had paid his in the worst currency he had — testimony against himself — and watching him eat salt pork with shaking hands, I found I didn't hate him anymore, which surprised me, and pitying him instead, which surprised me more.

Pip slid into the galley sideways, the way he entered every room, thirteen years old and made entirely of momentum.

"Tide's turned proper," he announced. "Captain says the narrows by midmorning if the wind holds."

"The narrows," I repeated. I knew the word from maps and from Kade's muttering. The place where the river pinched between shoals and the far bank leaned in close — the only place a fast cutter might catch a slower ship.

"That's the pinchy bit," Pip confirmed cheerfully. "Where boats catch other boats." He helped himself to bread. "Also where our keel draws less than theirs, which is the fun part."

Marla swatted him with a towel. Doss went slightly green at the mention of narrows. I sat with my father's knife in my pocket and thought about Colonel Ashe, who had a fast hull and no conscience and every reason in the world to see three documents at the bottom of the river rather than in a council's hands.

Escape, I was beginning to understand, was not the same thing as safety. We had gotten the ledger off the pier. Getting it off the river was another matter, and the river, unlike the tide, takes sides for no one.

* * *

I went up on deck as Vellsmark dropped astern. The sun had come up properly now, doing its indifferent work, gilding the smoke over the harbor front and lighting the bridge where Ilse Verren had once torn her commission in half like it weighed nothing, like thirty years of service was only paper, which I suppose in the end it was, good paper, the kind that holds an impression.

She had asked me, at the pier's end, why she should burn her career for a stranger's daughter. I never answered well. What I should have said — what I understood only as the town shrank behind us — was that she wasn't burning it for me at all. She was burning it for her own name in that ledger, for the famine winter, for every officer paid to look away while people starved, for the version of herself that the army had starved on garrison rations and never quite managed to kill. Verren hadn't saved me. She had finally saved herself, using my escape as the instrument. I could live with that. It was, in its way, the most honest transaction of the whole affair, and I deal in honest transactions. It's the family trade.

"Land's end by noon," Kade said beside me. I hadn't heard her come up. I was learning her footfalls and failing; the ship provided too much cover. "Weather looks fair, which I distrust on principle."

"Ashe's cutter," I said. "How much faster?"

"In open water? Enough." She squinted downriver, where the banks began their slow lean toward each other. "In the narrows, not enough. Pip knows a channel through the shallows that isn't on any chart the army owns, because the army doesn't employ anyone who grew up falling out of skiffs around here." A pause. "Assuming the wind behaves and nobody's clever."

"And if somebody's clever?"

"Then somebody's clever," Kade said, "and we find out what we're made of. In my experience, mostly water."

She went forward shouting about the jib. I stayed at the stern rail. Behind us Vellsmark was a smudge now, its bells faded into wind, its piers empty, its prisoners walking free without knowing it yet — Quill among the freed, though Quill had freed himself in the coldest way a man can, and I carried his confession against my ribs and tried to feel grateful instead of grieving and managed both badly.

Ahead lay the narrows, and beyond the narrows the estuary, and beyond the estuary a council of strangers who would turn my father's secret mercy into pamphlets the whole coast could read. The race wasn't over. It had only changed shape — no more checkpoints, no more gates, no more lies to sergeants' wives. Just water, wind, and one very motivated colonel.

I took out my father's knife and held it up against the sunrise, edge true, handle worn to the shape of his grip and beginning, faintly, to take the shape of mine.

"Hold together," I told it. "We're not done printing."

Upstream, faint but gaining, a sail appeared.
