## 34. Ashe's Pursuit

*Ashe*

Colonel Merrick Ashe had been called many things in his career — *ambitious*, mostly, behind his back and to his face; *ruthless*, by men who had mistaken cruelty for decisiveness; *careful*, which was the only one of the three he took as a compliment. He had never before been called a fugitive, and he found the word sat poorly on him, like a uniform tailored for a smaller man.

He stood at the rail of the *Vexing*, the fastest cutter on the river, and watched Vellsmark shrink behind him into smoke and morning haze. Not burning smoke — not yet, not today — but chimney smoke, ordinary smoke, the smoke of a town that believed its troubles were over because a ship had slipped away in the night. Towns always believed that. It was one of their most useful qualities.

"Colonel," said Adjutant Renner, touching his cap. "The harbor master wants it noted that you took the vessel without—"

"Noted," Ashe said. "By whom?"

Renner considered this. "No one, sir."

"Then it isn't taken without anything. It's requisitioned." He let the word do its work. Requisition was such a clean word. It turned theft into procedure, procedure into duty, duty into something no clerk would ever question, provided the requisitioning party won. That was the entire philosophy of empire in a single syllable, if you pronounced it correctly.

The ledger was aboard that ship ahead of him. The ledger with his name in it.

He did not think of it as evidence against himself. Evidence was a matter of perspective, and Ashe had spent thirty years learning to stand in only one. What the ledger was, properly understood, was unfinished business. A document that existed in the world meant a version of events that did not feature Colonel Merrick Ashe as its author. A document that lay at the bottom of the estuary meant nothing at all, forever. He preferred the second version. Most people would have. The difference between him and most people was that he was willing to say so out loud.

"How fast is she?" he asked.

"Nine knots under full canvas, ten with the tide, sir. She'll run down a smuggler's lugger inside an hour once we clear the narrows."

"And the lugger?"

Renner consulted his notes with the diligence of a man who had never once been promoted for it. "Kade's vessel. The *Magpie*. Shallow draft, twin keels, known to take the inner channel where nothing else will float. Her master has—" he hesitated over the word, "—*reputation*."

"She has a reputation," Ashe corrected mildly. "She is a woman with a record. Learn the difference, Renner; it will save you embarrassment at dinner parties." He turned back to the water. "She will take the shallows. Which means she must pass through them, and things that pass through narrow places are slow, and predictable, and cannot turn."

"And if she doesn't take the shallows?"

"Then she's in deep water with a cutter behind her, and I sink her there instead. You see? Every road leads to the same place. I've built my life that way." He smiled at the river, at the gray morning, at the whole forgiving landscape. "Bring us to speed, Adjutant. And find me a gun crew that can shoot straight. There's a book I need returned to the water."

*Kade*

Nessa Kade had been chased by revenue cutters, by a jealous husband with a fishing boat and poor judgment, and once — her favorite story, though she told it differently every time — by a swarm of bees off the marsh coast. So when she looked astern at dawn and saw the *Vexing* come round the bend with military canvas set and a bow wave like a drawn knife, her reaction was not panic. It was more of a professional assessment, followed by a long exhale through her teeth.

"Well," she said, "he didn't waste time being sad about it."

Bram Okafor came up the deck two steps at a time, hair loose, eyes already doing arithmetic on the horizon. Kade had noticed that about her in three days of knowing her: the girl could not look at anything without measuring it. Distances, loads, lies, men. Everything got weighed, everything got counted, everything got sorted into what can be carried and what gets thrown overboard. It was her father all over again, except the elder Okafor had done his weighing in lead and ink, and the daughter was learning to do it in lives.

"That's a cutter," Bram said. "That's not a patrol boat."

"That's *the* cutter. Fastest thing on this river, and now fastest thing on this river belongs to your Colonel Ashe, which tells you everything you need to know about how badly that man wants your book drowned." Kade spat leeward, ceremonially, in the general direction of authority. "Question for you, printer's daughter. How much does that ledger weigh?"

"What?"

"The actual book. In pounds. You've carried it through three checkpoints; you know better than anyone."

Bram frowned, and Kade could see her actually weighing it, hands remembering the shape. "Eleven pounds. Twelve with the wrapping."

"Twelve pounds of paper," Kade said, "and half this army rowing hard to catch it. You want to tell me again it's just names?"

"I never said it was just names."

"No. Your face said it. The first night, when you came aboard looking like someone had dug you up wrong. You thought: it's a book, they'll chase it a day and give up." Kade put a hand on the rail and felt the *Magpie* talk to her through the wood, the way she'd been talking to Kade through wood for eleven years. "Books are the heaviest cargo there is, girl. Gold you can spend. Guns you can sell. But a book makes people *behave differently*, and there's no ballast like that."

Behind them, Doss sat wrapped in a blanket near the hold, gray-faced, holding his ribs and pretending he wasn't. Tally was beside him narrating the rigging, because Tally narrated everything, and Doss had discovered that listening to a twelve-year-old explain blocks and tackles was easier than thinking about what he'd done and what was coming for having done it. Kade caught Bram glancing back at them and filed the glance away. That was another thing about the girl: she collected people the way her father collected typefaces, carefully, one at a time, each one accounted for.

"He won't stop," Bram said quietly. "Will he. Even past the narrows. Even past the estuary."

"Oh, he'll stop eventually." Kade watched the cutter grow by inches. "Question is whether stopping means giving up or means sinking us. And I've read enough of men like him to know which bet pays." She raised her voice. "Tam!"

The boy rigger appeared halfway up the shrouds as if summoned by witchcraft, which, after a year aboard, Kade had stopped ruling out. "Skipper!"

"How does she sit? Can we take the inner passage at this tide?"

Tam considered, hanging from ropes forty feet above the deck like a large thoughtful spider. "Inner passage draws four foot at slack water. We draw five and a half loaded. Tide turns in two hours." He paused, doing the sums. "So yes. If we're perfect."

"Have we ever been perfect?"

"Once," Tam said. "In spring. You said never again."

"I say many things." Kade turned back to Bram. "There's your answer, then. We run for the narrows, we thread the shallows where his cutter can't follow, and we pray the tide holds and my boy's sums hold and the river holds. Failing that—" she shrugged with the elaborate carelessness of a woman who had buried too many friends to be casual about it, "—failing that, we fight, and I'd sooner not. I'm a smuggler, not a martyr. Martyrs don't get paid."

"And what happens to the ledger if we don't make it?"

Kade looked at her. The girl's hand had gone to the satchel at her side without seeming to notice it, fingers resting on the strap the way other people touch amulets.

"You already know," Kade said. "You're the one who taught *me* the phrase. What can be carried. What can burn." She nodded at the satchel. "If it comes to it, that goes in the fire pot, and we scuttle her stern-first, and the river keeps your secrets the way it's kept everyone's. But we're not there yet." She grinned, sharp as a cleaver. "And between here and there, Colonel Merrick Ashe is going to learn why the revenue service gave up chasing me and started just writing angry letters."

*Ashe*

The trouble with rivers, Ashe reflected, was that they went places.

Land was honest. Land held its shape, offered itself to maps, permitted roads to be built where armies wanted them. A river was a collaborator. It changed its mind twice a day with the tide, kept secrets in its channels, and extended its hospitality to every smuggler and malcontent who ever needed to move something twelve pounds heavy out of Ashe's reach. He had grown up on this coast and had hated it since childhood, in the patient way a man hates a thing that has never once needed him.

He spread the chart on the companionway table and weighted the corners with pistol, spyglass, inkpot, and a biscuit he did not intend to eat.

"Narrows," he said, tapping. "Here. Two miles of channel barely wider than this cabin, shoals both sides, and according to this—" he tapped again, "—a secondary passage through the flats marked *unsurveyed*. Which is smuggler language for *we know the way and you don't*."

Renner leaned over the chart. "Could we block it, sir? Position the cutter across the main channel mouth?"

"We could. And she'd slip the flats while we sat there admiring our own reflection." Ashe straightened. "No. Speed is the answer. We stay in deep water, we keep pace, and when she emerges past the narrows — and she must emerge, Renner, all rivers end in the sea, it's the one promise they keep — we take her there, in open water, with guns. Sinking in the narrows risks the current washing wreckage and paper ashore. Sinking in the estuary buries it in sixty feet of honest mud."

"You speak as if the papers can't simply be copied, sir. Surely others have seen—"

"Others have seen *pages*, Renner. Pages in the hands of river radicals who can barely spell. Pages can be discredited, mislaid, drowned in a bilge." He kept his voice level, which was the trick of it; fury was a currency best spent in small denominations. "But a complete ledger, published intact, with dates and signatures and payments — that is a different animal entirely. That is an animal that eats colonels." He rolled up the chart. "How far to the narrows?"

"Four miles. We'll close the distance to gun range within two, given her load."

"She's loaded heavy?"

"Reports say so, sir. Passengers and crates."

Passengers and crates. Somewhere aboard that lugger was a print-shop woman named Okafor, whom he had never met and already disliked with unusual purity, carrying a book that contained every careful lie of the famine winter written down in a dead printer's tidy hand. And somewhere behind him was Vellsmark, where Captain Ilse Verren was currently explaining to a board of officers how the evidence vault came to be empty on her watch. He should have arrested Verren months ago. He had known it even then. But Verren had friends, and arrests were expensive, and Ashe had always preferred problems to solve themselves.

They were solving themselves now, he supposed. Just not in his favor.

"Gun crews to quarters," he said. "And Renner — when we come alongside, whatever happens, the satchel survives. The passengers are a secondary concern." He paused, and allowed himself the luxury of precision. "A tertiary one."

*Kade*

By noon the *Magpie* had her skirts up and was running.

Kade loved this part — loved it the way other women loved weddings or fistfights — the deck canted, the rigging singing, the whole vessel gathered up under her hands like an animal deciding to be magnificent. The *Magpie* was not beautiful by any harbor's standards; she was all belly and elbow, built shallow and wide to float on dew. But running free with the tide under her, she moved like gossip: light, fast, and impossible to catch once she'd left the room.

The cutter stayed with them anyway. Kade checked astern every few minutes and each time the *Vexing* had gained a little, patient and terrible, eating the miles while they ate the river.

"He's herding us," Bram said beside her. She had a spyglass braced on the rail. "Look at his heading. He's not following our wake, he's cutting the corner. He knows where we're going."

"'Course he does. There's one place we *can* go, and he's got a chart and a brain, worse luck." Kade squinted at the water ahead, reading it the way Bram read proofs — for errors, for traps, for the small deviations that meant disaster. Ahead, the valley walls drew in. The river narrowed, darkened, quickened. Gulls argued over something dead on the far bank. The narrows, right on schedule, faithful as taxes.

"Skipper!" Tam, from aloft. "Water's white over the middle shoal — channel's shifted with the flood! Inner passage marks are wrong!"

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong by fifty yards! Sand's moved east!"

Fifty yards. On a good day, with a fair tide and a cooperative God, fifty yards was nothing. Today it was the difference between threading a needle and sewing your own hand to the cloth.

Kade made her decision the way she made all of them — quickly, and then lived inside it afterward like a rented room.

"We take the main channel," she called. "Full sail. Let him think the flats frightened us."

Bram stared at her. "The main channel is where he's heading."

"It is." Kade took the wheel herself, feeling the spokes hum. "Because the only thing faster than running from that cutter, printer's daughter, is making him commit to a heading first. He thinks we'll hug the bank and dodge. So we won't. We'll come straight down his throat at nine knots and pass so close his gunners can't depress low enough to hit us — and by the time his captain understands what we're doing, we'll be through the narrows and he'll still be turning." She glanced sideways. "It's a stupid plan. It's also the only one where nobody has to be perfect."

"And if he guesses?"

"Men like him don't guess, love. They assume. Assumption's cheaper." Kade bared her teeth at the narrowing water, at the cutter looming beyond it, at the whole bright doomed gorgeous morning. "All hands! Stand by the sheets! We're going to say hello to the Colonel!"

Ahead, the narrows opened their jaws. Behind, the *Vexing* ran out her guns.

And Kade felt it then, through the wheel, through her boot soles, through eleven years of talking to this ship — the tide beginning to turn beneath them, early and hard, the river changing its mind at exactly the wrong moment, exactly as it always did, faithful only to itself.
