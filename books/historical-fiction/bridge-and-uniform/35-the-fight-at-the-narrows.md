## 35. The Fight at the Narrows

Dawn came up the color of old pewter, and with it came the cutter.

I was below when the lookout shouted — I'd been sleeping in the hold again, curled against the ledger chest like a dog guarding a bone it had no intention of eating — and the shout came down the companionway as a shape more than a sound. A shape with edges. Kade's voice could cut type, that morning.

"Sail! Stern quarter! Under army colors!"

I came up the ladder faster than dignity allows and found the aft deck already crowded: Kade at the whipstaff, barefoot, swearing with the fluency of a woman who had practiced; the cook, Marta, in the doorway of her galley with a ladle held like a saber; Hollis, the defector soldier, shading his eyes against the gray east; and Fen, the boy rigger, halfway up the shrouds before anyone thought to tell him not to be.

"One mast," Fen called down. "Lateen. Shallow draft. She's fast."

"She's *Ashe*," I said, because my mouth is sometimes quicker than my sense.

"Not yet," Kade said. "Could be any requisitioned hull in the fleet. But if it's your colonel, then he's spent his whole night's sleep buying speed, and speed he'll have." She turned the whipstaff a spoke and the ship leaned. "Question is whether we've got more."

The river had been widening all night, generous, almost lazy — a broad brown road between reed banks, gulls picking at the morning. But Kade had been talking about the narrows since Vellsmark, the way preachers talk about the end of days: often, gravely, and with relish.

"Past Halloway Point the river pinches," she'd told me two nights ago over tea that tasted of tar. "Half a mile wide, maybe less at low water, and a shoal bar runs diagonally across it — the Weir Bar, they call it, though there's been no weir since my grandmother's grandmother. Deep channel on the north side, deep on the south, and between them, water shallow enough to read the bottom by. Big keels have to choose a side. Small keels have another option."

"What option?"

"The one that makes grown sailors pray." She'd smiled then, and I understood I would either love this woman or be killed by her, and that both were fine outcomes as far as she was concerned.

Now the cutter came on out of the dawn haze, and it was no ordinary requisitioned tub. New paint, stripped deck, gunports open along one side — three of them, which meant three-pounders or swivel guns, which meant someone aboard wanted us stopped, not sunk, but was prepared to negotiate downward.

Hollis lowered his hand from his brow. "That's the *Vespid*. Garrison dispatch cutter. Fastest thing on this stretch of water."

"You'd know," Marta said, not unkindly. Hollis had served on her for two years before walking off a pier one Tuesday and into our lives.

"I'd know," Hollis agreed quietly.

Kade studied the cutter's line of approach the way my father used to study a page before cutting a new face for an unfamiliar letter — measuring, weighing, deciding where the metal needed to give. Then she made her choice, and her voice went round and easy, the captain's voice, the one that makes a crew feel like the decision was partly theirs.

"All hands. Fen, take the main sheet and be ready to crack off in a hurry. Marta, get below and stay there unless I scream, in which case still stay there. Hollis—" she paused, and something complicated crossed her face, "—you know the Vespid's captain?"

"Trent. Decent enough. Slow to decide."

"Then let's make his decisions difficult. Get to the bow. If they hail, you're the authority on this vessel now. Congratulations on your promotion."

"And if they fire?"

"Then you're demoted very suddenly."

We ran downriver with the tide under us and the wind quartering fair, and for perhaps twenty minutes I let myself believe in arithmetic: we had the lead, we had the tide, we had a hull that Kade kept trimmed so sweetly she seemed to be reading the water rather than riding it. Behind us the Vespid grew by patient inches, her white ensign hardening from smudge to flag. Ahead, Halloway Point rose — a blunt black shoulder of land with a broken beacon tower on it, and past it, visible even from here, the river narrowed and glittered wrong. Too bright. Too shallow. Water showing its bones.

"Bar's showing," Fen reported from aloft. "North channel looks deepest."

"North channel it is," Kade said, "for anyone with sense. Which is why we're taking south."

Marta, who had not gone below, said what we were all thinking: "South has no room to turn about."

"South isn't for turning about," Kade said. "South is for going through."

The first shot came while the words were still settling. Not a cannonball — a warning piece, the crack of it skipping flat across the water like a stone thrown by an angry god, and the smoke drifting back toward the cutter where Trent had finally decided something. A second later the thin tin voice of a speaking trumpet carried across the gap:

"*Vessel ahoy! Heave to in the name of the Crown! You are carrying stolen military property!*"

Stolen military property. I put my hand flat on the deck as if I could feel the ledger through forty feet of oak and salted timber. Eleven names marked for the gallows. A dead man's confession folded into its leaves like a pressed flower. Quill's last act, heavier than his body had been.

"They're not wrong about the property," I said to Kade. "They're just wrong about whose."

"That's the whole war in one sentence." She spun the wheel half a turn. "Hold on to something that loves you."

There wasn't time for anyone to obey. The Vespid's bow chaser spoke again — a real shot this time, aimed — and the ball came aboard with a sound like a giant's knuckle rapping a door, punching through our rail amidships and out the other side, splinters whistling. Nobody screamed, which I count among the stranger graces of that morning. Marta went below after all, taking the ladle. Hollis flattened himself behind the foremast. And Fen, God keep him, slid down the backstay to the deck, landed light as a dropped ribbon, and stood beside me.

"Shallows are close," he said. "Soundings will go quick. Captain'll want the leadsman singing."

"Do you know how to sing them?"

"I *am* the leadsman." He was already coiling the line, swinging the weighted lead in a slow circle, and I saw that he'd done this a hundred times in a hundred dreams, the way children rehearse the lives they intend to have.

The third shot took our spanker boom's after-end off. Not fatal — Kade pronounced it "an amputation, not a funeral" and put two men to clearing the wreckage — but it told us Trent had stopped negotiating, and it told me something else too: the Vespid was closing faster than the tide alone explained. Ashe had bought speed, all right. He was spending it.

And then we were at the narrows.

I wish I had language worthy of it. The river, so broad and motherly all night, gathered itself between Halloway Point and the southern flats and turned suddenly narrow, sudden quick, sudden *bright* — the bar showing beneath the surface as long pale serpentine bands, like veins under skin. To port, the deep northern channel ran dark and inviting, wide enough for the Vespid, wide enough for anything. Kade took us hard to starboard, straight at the shining shallows, and every instinct bred into every sailor alive must have stood up screaming in her chest. Her hands never shook.

"Fen," she said.

"On it." The boy swung the lead, cast it forward, hauled it up, and read the taut line like scripture. "By the mark five!" he sang, and the word *singing* earned itself that morning, because it was a song, high and clear and steady, a boy's voice doing a man's work over water that wanted to eat us.

Behind us, exactly as promised, the Vespid hesitated at the fork. I watched her luff slightly, watched her yards swing as Trent — decent enough, slow to decide — weighed the dark safe channel against the pale impossible one. He chose north. Of course he chose north. The army trains men to choose the channel marked on the chart.

What the chart did not mark, because no army cartographer had ever thought to ask a boy who fished these waters for eels, was that the Weir Bar had a spine and two hollows, and the southern hollow ran deeper than any chart guessed — deep enough for Kade's keel if you entered it at precisely the right angle at precisely the falling tide, threading between two shoals that showed their teeth only at low water, which this was.

Fen sang the depths. Five. Four and a half. Four. The hull began to speak in a new register, a shivering whisper along her seams, sand and shell whispering past a foot below our floorboards. I have stood closer to death in print shops, honestly. Ink fires are no joke. But I have never felt anything like that hour — the whole ship holding her breath, the crew motionless, Kade steering by some instrument no one else could see, and Fen's voice rising and falling, four, three and a quarter, *three*, each number a coin laid on the altar of our continued existence.

Aboard the Vespid, visible now across the narrowing gap of shallows — for we were drawing away from her not by sailing faster but by sailing *where she couldn't* — figures crowded the rail. One of them wore a colonel's coat. Even at that distance I knew him. You know a shape you've seen in nightmares.

He raised a glass. I would swear he looked directly at me. And then he did the thing that told me everything: he ordered the Vespid into the southern channel after us.

"He can't," Hollis breathed beside me. "Her draft's too heavy. He'll tear her heart out."

"He knows the tide's falling," Kade said grimly. "If he doesn't catch us before the bar dries, he's stuck till evening tide, and by then we're at the estuary. So he's going to try anyway, because he'd rather lose his ship than his secrets." She glanced at me. "Your colonel understands stakes, Bram."

"He's not *my* colonel."

"He is. That's what being from somewhere means, even when you hate it."

The Vespid entered the shallows. For a moment she seemed to fly — momentum carrying her, courage or folly substituting for water — and then her bow wave went wrong, flattening, spreading, and her whole hull groaned loud enough to reach us across the flats as eleven inches of ironbound keel met nine inches of river bottom at eight knots.

The sound was magnificent. I am not proud of the joy it gave me. I am slightly proud that I waited until the third grinding shudder before I laughed.

But Trent had loaded his guns facing forward as well as sideways, and a grounded ship can still shoot. As we cleared the far lip of the bar and slipped into deeper water beyond, the Vespid's stern chaser fired twice more, quick and angry, and the muskets began.

Here is where I must tell the part I have told badly ever since, and will tell badly now, because some things refuse to be well cut.

The bullets came aboard in ones and twos, spiteful little sounds, wood-ticks and wet slaps. Most went high or wide; soldiers shooting from a shaking, grounding hull at a moving target do not shoot well. One went through the mainsail and made a hole I later mended myself, badly, and kept mended badly for a year out of sentiment. And one came low across the deck, flat and fast, toward the hatch where the ledger chest sat — toward Doss, who had come up from below without anyone noticing, drawn by the noise the way cowards are always drawn to danger at the exact moment courage would be useful.

I saw it happen the way you see type fall from a composing stick — all at once, in the wrong order, irreversibly.

Doss looked at the chest. The bullet looked at Doss. And the clerk who had spent his whole life calculating angles of self-preservation, who had betrayed a guild and fled an army and sold his last secret to a smuggler's crew of strangers — Doss stepped in front of the chest.

The ball took him high in the chest, left of center, and folded him onto the hatch cover like a coat dropped from a hook.

I reached him before I knew I'd moved. There was blood on his collar, shockingly red against army-gray wool gone soft with wear, and his eyes were open and enormous and surprised — surprised most of all, I think, at himself.

"It's not — " he started, and coughed, and tried again. "It's not bravery. I just. Couldn't watch it happen twice."

"Twice?"

"Everything. I've watched everything I — " The rest went into the coughing.

Marta appeared with strips of linen and something fierce-smelling in a bottle, and Hollis came aft with his hands pressing where she told him, and between the four of us we made a small, sweating, profane surgery on the deck of a fleeing ship while Kade held us in the deepening channel and Fen sang soundings nobody needed anymore, sang them anyway, quieter, like a hymn for the occasion. The ball had kissed a rib and gone through muscle, missing everything that mattered by margins Doss would spend the rest of his life recounting to strangers in taverns, each telling wider than the last. It was not a killing wound. At the time I believed it was, and I held his hand, and I said the things you say, and none of them were true and all of them were kind.

"You absolute fool," I said. "You terrible, ridiculous man."

"Typeface cutter," he whispered, gray-faced, half-smiling. "Watch your language around the evidence."

We got him below to Marta's galley table, which became, over her objections that lasted four seconds, the finest sickbay on the river. And when I came back up, wiping his blood from my hands onto my apron because some stains want witnesses, the narrows were behind us. The Vespid sat heeled over on the bar like a beached whale in a uniform, small with distance, firing nothing. The river opened ahead, wide and brown and running for the sea, and the wind found us clean, and the whole crew — cook, deserter, boy, captain, widow's daughter, wounded coward — moved together in the sudden unhurried rhythm of people who have survived a thing and not yet said so.

Fen coiled his leadsman's line with exaggerated care. Hollis climbed to the bow and stayed there a long time, looking back at the cutter he used to serve, and I didn't ask what he felt, because I was spending everything I had not asking myself what I felt. Kade stood at the whipstaff and let the ship run herself and looked, for the first time since I'd known her, tired.

"Well," she said. "That's the last army hull between us and the estuary."

"Is it?" I asked. "Because the last one nearly killed Doss and it couldn't even sail."

"The estuary's different. Estuary's neutral water, merchantmen, council ships — your revolutionaries' friends." She said *your revolutionaries* the way she said everything, dry as gun cotton, but her eyes weren't dry at all. "Tide'll carry us to them by midday, if the wind holds."

I went below. The chest sat where it always sat, unmarked, its lock scratched from my knife, its contents heavier than any book has a right to be. I put my palm flat on the lid. Eleven names, and underneath them a dead man's confession, and underneath all of it the strange buoyant weight of a father's foresight and a captain's torn commission and a clerk's foolish blood, all of it carried this far by a crew of people who owed me nothing and had paid anyway.

*Measure the world in what can be carried.* I had the measure of it now, I thought. I knew exactly what it weighed.

Then Fen's head appeared at the hatch, upside down, and the look on his face rearranged everything I knew.

"Bram," he said. "There's smoke coming up the river behind us. Lots of it. And it's not the Vespid."

* * *

I gained the deck in time to see it myself: a smudge on the southern horizon, growing, and under it — when I borrowed Kade's glass — the low, lean silhouette of a boat built for exactly one purpose, coming down the last free stretch of river toward the estuary with the tide and the wind and whatever fury powered it.

Kade took the glass, looked a long moment, and handed it back with an expression I had not seen on her before. I had seen her face debt collectors, revenue men, and one memorable hurricane. This was new.

"*Swiftwater*," she said. "Private hull. Commissioned fast, built faster. There are three men on this coast who could handle her, and only one with reason to."

"Ashe."

"Ashe bought himself a proper hunter." She turned to the crew, and the captain's voice came back, round and calm and terrifying. "Change of plans, everyone. The narrows are behind us and the estuary's ahead, but there's water between here and there that he knows better than I do, because his pilots are bought and mine are honest. Honest costs speed." She looked at Fen. "How much do you know about the channels past Gullmark?"

The boy grinned, and there was blood-spattered linen on the deck behind him and a colonel's ship on the horizon, and he said, "Enough to make him pray."

Kade nodded once. "Then trim everything and trust the boy. We'll run for the estuary—" and here she looked at me, and the wryness came back into her mouth like a blade finding its sheath, "—and we'll hope your revolutionaries keep their rendezvous. Because Bram, if that meeting's moved, or missed, or mistimed—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Somewhere behind us, gaining, the *Swiftwater* raised her own ensign, and the race for the estuary had begun.
