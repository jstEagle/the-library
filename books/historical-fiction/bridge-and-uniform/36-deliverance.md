## 36. Deliverance

The river widened the way grief does — gradually, then all at once — until Vellsmark was a smudge of chimney smoke behind us and the water ahead had no banks at all, only the suggestion of sea.

I stood at the rail of the *Mercy of Debts* — Kade's ship, named, she told me later, for the only honest prayer she knew — and I held the ledger against my chest the way you hold a child you are about to hand to strangers.

"It's not going to dissolve," Kade said beside me. "It's paper. Paper floats, ironically."

"I know what it is."

"You've checked your pockets four times since the narrows."

"I check my type trays before bed too. Habit isn't madness."

"Sometimes," she said, "it's both."

She was right, which was annoying, so I didn't answer. The truth was I couldn't feel where the ledger ended and I began anymore. For eleven days it had been my heartbeat's neighbor, pressed under my coat, slept against, lied for, bled near. Doss had taken a musket ball in the shoulder at the narrows shielding it — shielding *paper*, which even he could not entirely explain, though he'd tried from his cot below decks between groans: "It seemed important at the time." A coward spends his courage once and then has to lie down for a while. That's the toll.

Ahead, anchored in the gray roadstead of the estuary, rode a merchantman flying no colors at all, which on that coast meant one thing: neutrality bought at a price nobody wrote down. The revolutionaries' council had come to collect.

"There," said Wick, the boy rigger, hanging off the shrouds like laundry with opinions. "They're lowering a boat. Three men and a woman in a gray coat."

"The woman's the one who matters," Kade said. "See how everyone else stands when she moves? That's not rank. That's trust."

The cook — a vast, gentle man called Harn who fed me for two days without asking my name and then asked it anyway as if names were soup, freely given — came up to say there was food below and I should eat it. I said I would. Neither of us believed me.

We came alongside with the clumsy courtesy of two ships meeting in open water, and the gray-coated woman climbed aboard without waiting for the ladder to be offered, which told me something about her before anyone spoke.

"Nessa Kade," she said. "You're punctual."

"I'm expensive. Punctual comes free."

"And this is the courier." Her eyes found me, and they were the flattest, kindest eyes I have ever been measured by. "Bram Okafor. Typeface cutter. Daughter of Joss Okafor."

"You knew him?"

"He printed our first pamphlet. Twelve years ago. Two hundred copies, set by hand, distributed in fish barrels." She smiled briefly, an expression that seemed borrowed rather than owned. "Half the council learned to read radical thought off his type. We wondered for years why he never joined us."

"He had a shop," I said. "A shop can't run from a hillside."

"And now the shop is ash and the daughter runs rivers instead." She said it without pity, which I appreciated. Pity would have undone me, and I had spent everything I had left standing upright. "Do you have it?"

I want to tell you I handed it over with dignity. I want to tell you I said something memorable, some line worthy of the pamphlets that would carry it down the coast. What actually happened was that I fumbled the oilcloth ties with shaking fingers, and when the ledger finally came out into the daylight — swollen with river damp, its cover scored by the coffin nails it had traveled under, one corner charred from a fire I did not yet know the full shape of — I held it out and then couldn't let go.

The woman waited. She was good at waiting. You could see it in her hands, which stayed very still.

"My father's hand is in every page," I said. "Every name. Every debt. It's the last thing he wrote that wasn't an invoice."

"Then we'll treat it as scripture," she said. "Which is to say: copied, disputed, distributed, and never trusted to a single keeper again."

My fingers opened. The weight left me, and for one vertiginous moment I felt myself tipping forward after it, as if the ledger had been the ballast keeping me from simply floating away. Kade's hand landed on my shoulder — heavy, seaman's grip, no tenderness in it and all the tenderness in the world — and I stayed on the deck of the world instead of drifting off it.

* * *

They set up to copy it in the merchantman's stern cabin, and here is a thing they do not put in songs: the copying of history looks like clerks with sore wrists.

Four copyists, two of them council members who apparently also knew their letters, working in shifts around the clock. They copied the guild ledger three times entire. They copied Quill's confession twice — the confession he'd written with the last ink he had, naming Colonel Ashe as Doss's handler, naming the famine-year payments, naming the officers paid to look away while Vellsmark starved politely. My name was in those pages too, marked for the gallows, and I watched a copyist transcribe it with the same neutral diligence he gave the shipping figures, and I confess it was the strangest comfort of my life: my own death sentence, rendered harmless by good penmanship.

I sat in the corner of that cabin for most of a day and a night, because I could not make myself leave the room the ledger was in. Not guarding it. There was nothing left to guard it *from*. Just — present. A witness to its dismemberment, if that's what you'd call it. You can't kill a book by copying it. You make it immortal and ordinary at once, the way a song survives.

Tally came to sit with me partway through the second night. She'd barely spoken since her arrest and release, since the hours in an army cell that I refused to ask about and she refused to describe, an agreement we maintained by both pretending nothing had happened, which is the family resemblance nobody mentions.

"Is that man copying your name?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are they going to cross it out?"

"No. It stays. It just stops meaning what Ashe wanted it to mean."

She considered this. "So it's still true, but it doesn't work anymore."

"That's exactly it."

"Like the proclamation posted on the bridge after the requisition. Still up, but everyone spat near it." She leaned her head against my arm. In the lamplight her face had the terrible translucent calm of children who have been frightened past their own knowing. "Bram. When we get wherever we're going. Is there going to be a shop?"

And there it was — the question underneath all my questions, asked by a twelve-year-old because adults lack the nerve. No shop. No shop ever again; the floorboards were ash somewhere over my shoulder upriver, the matrices burned or scattered, my father's last secret gone up with everything else except the knife in my belt and the font tray of memory I carried behind my eyes.

"There'll be type," I said. "Wherever there's type, someone will want it cut well. And where someone cuts type—" I heard my father's voice under my own and let it finish: "—someone builds a press."

"A floating one?"

"If necessary."

"Good." She closed her eyes. "Because I looked at the hold, and there's room."

* * *

By dawn the copies were done. I saw them packed — three identical satchels, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted for river work — and I understood the plan before anyone explained it, because it was a printer's plan and I recognized the logic the way you recognize your own handwriting.

Three couriers. Three directions. One copy up the coast to the northern councils, one overland through the hill passes, one to stay with the merchantman until it could reach a foreign port and a foreign press beyond any crown's subpoena. Scatter the truth the way you scatter type to sort it: loose, everywhere, impossible to seize whole. You cannot burn a pamphlet that has already been read.

"You built this," the gray-coated woman said to me, catching me watching. "The matrices, the coded ledger, the route. Your name won't be on the histories."

"Good. Histories get people hanged. Pamphlets get them angry, and anger is cheaper insurance."

She almost laughed. "Your father said nearly the same thing once. Word for word, nearly."

"He stole most of his good lines from me," I said. "He just got them published first."

The satchels went aboard their couriers' boats in the flat gray light, and I watched each one pull away until it was a dot, then nothing, and the ledger's contents ceased to exist as a single object anywhere in the world. Somewhere on that water, in that hour, Colonel Ashe's case died — though none of us knew it yet, and he least of all, powerful men being always the last to hear the tide turn.

I expected to feel triumphant. What I felt instead, standing at the rail with the wind coming salt off the open sea, was the sudden, catastrophic absence of purpose. For weeks there had been a next thing, always a next thing: a gate, a lie, a key, a coffin, a knife-blade's width between me and the gallows list. Now there was only morning, and morning demanded nothing.

I sat down on the deck planks without deciding to. And then — to my lasting embarrassment, witnessed by gulls, a smuggler crew, and God, if He takes commissions — I cried. Not elegantly. Ugly, honking, full-body grief, the kind you'd pay money not to be seen doing, and the crew of the *Mercy of Debts*, to their eternal credit, pretended very hard to be busy with ropes.

Kade sat down next to me and passed me a flask.

"I don't drink before noon," I managed.

"It's tea."

"Why is it in a flask?"

"Everything's in a flask at sea. It's the rule." She waited while I drank lukewarm tea that tasted of iron and cedar and possibly rope. "You're mourning."

"He's been dead two years, Nessa."

"You buried him twice, then. Once in the ground and once in the work." She looked out at the horizon, where sea and sky had agreed to stop distinguishing themselves. "Now the work's done, and the second funeral's come due. It passes. Mostly. It comes back at strange hours, like a cat, but it passes."

"What am I supposed to *be* now?" The question came out smaller than I intended. "I was a printer's daughter. Then I was evidence. Then I was a courier. All of those fit inside a day's coat. Now—"

"Now you're a woman on a ship with a crew that would gut a customs man for you, an apprentice asleep below, a wounded clerk who took a bullet for your father's paperwork, and a knife that opens strongboxes." She shrugged. "Terrible prospects. Utterly unemployable."

I laughed, wetly, which ruined the dignity of it, which was presumably her intent.

Below, faintly, I heard Tally's voice reading aloud to Doss — she'd found his letters, or he'd found hers, some prisoner-and-guard correspondence neither would explain to me — and Harn's voice rising over it announcing breakfast as if announcing mutiny. Wick dropped from the rigging onto the deck beside us, stuck the landing badly, and grinned up at me with a gap where a tooth used to be, lost at the narrows, worn like a medal.

"Bram," he said. "Harn wants to know if you're going to cry at breakfast too or just at railings."

"Railings only. Breakfast gets my best behavior."

"Shame. Harn had a bet going."

* * *

We ate, and the estuary light came up gold and ordinary, and I did the small arithmetic of survival: eleven days ago I had been a name on a warrant. Now the warrant was paper in three satchels rowing away in different directions, and I was a name on a crew manifest Kade had apparently entered without asking me, under "hands: type cutter, temperamental, useful."

Afterward I went below to check on Doss, because guilt is a debt and I keep the guild books even now.

His shoulder was bound and his color was bad but improving, and he was propped among cargo netting like a man who had surrendered to furniture. Tally had left her primer open on his chest. He was, I noted, reading it voluntarily.

"How's the arm?"

"It hurts in a very honest way," he said. "I find I prefer it to lying, which I did professionally for years. Everything about recovery is truthful. Hunger, pain, gratitude." He looked at me with those clerk's eyes that had seen every column of the army's accounts and finally, catastrophically, added them up. "Bram. When they publish it. The ledger. My name's going to be in it. On the wrong page."

"On several wrong pages."

"I know." His jaw worked. "I'm not asking to be left out. That wouldn't be the point of any of it. I'm asking — when it's printed, when everyone reads it — will you be the one who set the type? Because if it has to be in your hand, the way your father's hand is in the original, then maybe it'll come out honest. Even the ugly rows."

I looked at this man — informer, coward, accomplice, bleeding shoulder, borrowed courage — and I understood that he was asking for the only absolution my trade offers: to be set correctly. Not prettily. Correctly.

"When the time comes," I said, "I'll cut your name fresh. New matrices. Nobody inherits other men's letters. You'll stand in type of your own making."

Doss nodded, and lay back, and slept like a man whose account had at least been opened.

* * *

Evening came down on the estuary, and with the evening came a boat.

Not one of ours. A fishing smack, weathered and innocent, running up alongside under a legitimate enough license, and the fisherman who hailed us had the specific boredom of a man delivering news rather than mackerel. Kade went down to take the delivery, and I followed, because following deliveries had kept me alive this long and habits aren't madness. Usually.

The fisherman handed over a bundle of the newssheets the coastal towns printed — the cheap, ragged, glorious sheets that pass from tavern to tavern faster than any army can march — and a single sealed letter, addressed in a small precise hand.

To Bram Okafor, care of any ship, any shore, any address that will receive it.

The newsheets I skimmed standing there in the failing light, and my heart did things I haven't words priced for. Vellsmark. The condemned released — all eleven, walking out through gates that had lost their reason to exist. The trials collapsing like wet cardboard. And a paragraph, brief, official-toned, that stopped my eye and stopped the deck beneath it:

*Colonel Merrick Ashe taken into custody by order of the Army Tribunal at Corven, charges pending.*

Arrested by his own side. The machine eating its own hand. I read it three times and felt — not joy. Something older and quieter than joy. The feeling of type locking into the chase, every letter seated, nothing loose, ready for the pull of the press.

Kade read over my shoulder, and swore softly, and laughed once, and said, "Well. There's a man about to learn what requisition feels like from the other end."

Then she nodded at the sealed letter still in my hands. "That one's not a newsheet. Newsheets are for everyone."

I broke the seal. The small precise hand belonged, of course, to Ilse Verren, and the letter was short — three lines, no signature flourish, a soldier's economy:

*They have taken me. The hearing is set for the spring assizes at Corven. Do not come. I write only so you know I do not regret the cost. — I.V.*

*Do not come.*

I stood at the rail with the letter in my hand and the last light going off the water, and behind me a crew was becoming a family and ahead of me the whole coast lay dark and printable, and I heard myself say, in the voice I use for decisions:

"Tally. Get the primer. Nessa — how much lead do you carry, and how well does your ship keep a heading in spring weather?"

Kade looked at me for a long moment, reading my face the way she read water.

"Don't come, the letter says," she observed.

"It does."

"And?"

"We owe the army one more pamphlet," I said. "Set in a hand they'll recognize."
