## 38. What Verren Paid

The news reached us at Saltmere Reach, three weeks after the trials collapsed, carried by a fisherman who traded us mackerel for pamphlets. He had it secondhand from a customs man who'd read the garrison dispatches over someone's shoulder, which is how most news travels in this country — sideways, and slightly wrong.

*Captain Ilse Verren, arraigned before a general court-martial on charges of aiding the escape of a fugitive, dereliction of duty, and destruction of her own commission. Trial to convene at Corvenmoor garrison in eleven days.*

I read it twice, standing at the press with ink to my elbows, and then I set the sheet down very carefully, as though it might go off.

Kade watched me from across the deck. She has a way of watching that feels like being measured for a coffin. "You're going back," she said. Not a question.

"She tore up her commission for me," I said. "She's facing a rope because of me."

"She's facing a rope because she chose," Kade said. "There's a difference, girl, though I'll grant you it's a thin one, like most differences." She turned back to her charts, which is what she does instead of saying the other thing she means. "We'll be off Corvenmoor inside nine days if the wind cooperates. If it doesn't, I'll still get you there. I owe your father, not you — but you've been paying his debts so long I've lost track of whose they are."

That is Nessa Kade's version of affection. You learn to read it the way you read a badly kerned page: the meaning's there, just set crooked.

Tally heard about it before I could decide how much to tell her. Tally hears everything; she was eavesdropping before she learned to walk, probably, and I have no doubt she'll die mid-eavesdrop at some ripe old age. She came to my cabin that night with her arms crossed and her chin out — her negotiating posture, which she must have learned from me, God help us both.

"You can't leave me here again."

"I'm not leaving you anywhere. I'm going to Corvenmoor."

"Then I'm going to Corvenmoor."

"Tally—"

"They took me once," she said, and her voice did something it almost never does: it went quiet without going small. "They put me in a cell and told me I'd hang unless I said things. I know exactly what their courts are. You're going to walk into one on purpose. So either I come along and watch, or I sit on this ship imagining it, and I'm telling you now, Bram, the imagining would be worse."

I looked at her — thirteen years old, arrested once, orphaned once, apprentice typesetter twice over — and I thought about what my father would have said. Probably nothing. Probably he'd have cut a new typeface, an italic called something like *Stubborn Girl*, and printed ten thousand copies.

"Deckhand's duties," I said. "You carry nothing, you speak to no one, and when I say run, you run."

"When *we* say run," she said. "You always forget the 'we.' Da used to say it too."

She'd never once mentioned my father to me before that moment, not directly. It hit me harder than the news of the trial had. I agreed mostly so she wouldn't see my face do whatever it was doing.

* * *

Corvenmoor sits inland from the estuary, a garrison town with the particular grimness of places built entirely by one institution for one purpose. The army had requisitioned its own requisition, if that makes sense — every wall posted with warrants, every warrant bearing names I knew or had helped free. Mine was there somewhere, I didn't doubt, but warrants are only paper, and I had spent a season learning that paper obeys whoever cuts the type.

Doss came with us, which surprised everyone including Doss. He'd been living aboard as our cook's assistant, a position he held through sheer talent for not being asked to do anything dangerous. He was the one who knew Corvenmoor. He'd been stationed there two years; he knew which gate the officers' mess used, which clerk took bribes and which took bribes *and* kept records, where the court-martial room stood relative to the guardhouse and the latrines, which is the sort of geography that matters more than rivers or roads.

"I want to testify too," he said, on the second night upriver.

"No."

"He'll believe me. Colonel Ashe's own clerk turning evidence —"

"Doss. They'll hang you for desertion before you finish the oath. Your testimony lives in print now — it's in the pamphlets, it's scattered across four provinces, no one can unprint it. Your body is worth nothing to anyone dead. Sit down and eat your soup."

He sat down and ate his soup, but he sulked into it, and I understood the sulk. Cowardice, once spent, wants to keep spending. It's like opening a strongbox — hard the first time, easy after. But courage spent on a gallows buys nothing, and I've never been sentimental enough to buy nothing.

The plan, such as it was, came together the way plans do: from gossip, bribery, and one genuinely good idea.

The good idea was Tally's. Courts-martial, she informed us — she'd listened to soldiers talk for weeks aboard the ship, and unlike me she actually retains what she hears — require witnesses summoned under seal. A witness list existed. Witnesses were quartered in town, at an inn called the Haltered Ox, under light guard, because none of them had any reason to flee. Among them: Sergeant Harneth, of the bridge checkpoint. The sergeant of the coffin gate. The man who had demanded the box be opened while Quill lay dead inside it and I stood inventing lies by the second.

"He remembers you," Tally said. "But here's the thing — he doesn't remember you as Bram Okafor. He remembers you as the corpse-tender woman. Warrants describe faces. Memory describes circumstances. We're going to make the memory and the face disagree."

So I went into Corvenmoor as a laundress again — the trade that had gotten me through the market gate a lifetime ago — and Tally went in as my daughter, because nothing disarms a sergeant like a child tugging at your sleeve, and Tally had volunteered her acting services with terrifying enthusiasm.

Sergeant Harneth drank at the Haltered Ox every evening between the seventh and ninth bells. He was lonely, away from his wife, and — Doss confirmed this from old garrison friendships — proud of having been the only officer at the bridge that night not later implicated in Ashe's thefts. He considered himself a man who had done his duty correctly, and men like that will talk all night to anyone who seems impressed.

We were impressed. Tally was magnificent. By the third evening Harneth had told us the whole story of the coffin twice, embellished each time, and I had learned what we needed: the trial was being pushed hard by the provost marshal, Verren had refused counsel, and — the part that mattered —

"She won't speak in her own defense," Harneth said, shaking his head into his cup. "Won't call witnesses, won't cross-examine. Sits there like she's already sentenced. There's talk among the panel she means to plead guilty to everything. Man in my company says she told the examining officer, 'The facts aren't in dispute. Only the question of whether facts require obedience.' Whatever that means."

It meant she was going to let them hang her, and call it integrity.

I sat in that smoky taproom wearing another woman's apron, holding a mug I hadn't sipped, and understood something with terrible clarity: Ilse Verren had torn her commission in half expecting to pay for it, and paying was, for her, the whole point. Justice outside the law, purchased with herself. It was noble. It was unbearable. And it would leave Tally in a district governed by the same army, with my name still on a warrant, if the wrong verdict came down.

Nobility, I have since concluded, is like lead type: admirable in small quantities, fatal in bulk.

I needed her to fight. And she wouldn't fight for herself. So I would have to give her something else to fight for — or rather, someone. The panel needed a reason to demote instead of hang, and the only currency a court-martial respects is testimony, delivered properly, under seal, by a witness who shouldn't exist.

* * *

Getting a fugitive into a court-martial as a defense witness is a problem of typography, and I mean that sincerely. Every system of power is a font: it cares about shapes, repetition, alignment. Break the pattern cleanly enough and the eye reads what it expects to read.

Doss knew the witness-summoning clerk. The clerk owed Doss money, which Doss forgave in exchange for one insertion: a name added to the defense list, summoned under the seal, arriving from out of province. The name he inserted was not mine. It was *B. Okafor, scrivener and document examiner, Saltmere circuit* — a person who plausibly existed, whose expertise was plausible, whose presence was plausible.

The warrant for my arrest described a typeface cutter. Nobody reads past the trade. That is the whole lesson of my life, really.

I testified on the fourth day of the trial.

They brought me in through the side door of the assize hall, past two bored sentries who saw a scrivener in travel-stained grey with a document case and stopped seeing anything at all. The hall smelled of floor wax and fear-sweat, that specific vinegar note that collects wherever men wait to learn their fates. Verren sat at the front in undress uniform without insignia — they'd taken even her rank braid — her hands folded on the table before her like a woman at prayer who no longer believed in the recipient.

When the door opened she looked up, and I watched her face do three things in one second: recognition, alarm, and then — this was the worst — resignation. She thought I'd come to hang beside her. I could see her preparing to forgive me for it.

The president of the panel, a colonel with a voice like a drawer closing, read my credentials aloud and asked me to state my name for the record.

"Bram Okafor," I said. "Document examiner."

Let the record show that I told the truth throughout. There is a certain purity available when you stop lying: every fact becomes a weapon because no one thought you'd pick it up.

I testified to what I knew as a craftswoman. That I had examined the guild ledger before its seizure — true. That the hand recording the famine-year payments was consistent throughout — true. That a ledger recording payments to officers for looking away during the famine named serving officers, and that the army's interest in suppressing it was therefore not institutional justice but personal protection — true, and in the pamphlets, and verifiable by anyone who could read, which by then was several thousand people.

The prosecutor objected. The president sustained it, then unsustained it when I pointed out, mildly, that the documents in question had been published in four provinces and that the panel might wish to hear them discussed by someone who had actually handled the originals rather than the forgeries Ashe had circulated. This last was new information in that room. Ashe had produced forged excerpts at the preliminary hearing — I recognized the work the moment I'd seen the printed sheets; the ligatures were wrong, amateur work, cut in haste by someone who understood neither spacing nor shame. I said so. I demonstrated so, with diagrams. I am very good at demonstrating things; I was raised in a print shop.

And then I said the thing I had come to say, the thing I had rehearsed on the ship deck at midnight to an audience of rigging.

"On the morning of the eleventh, Captain Verren found me at the harbor pier. She was within her authority to arrest me. Instead she questioned me, established that I carried documents proving corruption reaching to the rank of colonel — documents she had independently verified during her own investigation of the evidence vault, which investigation the garrison log records." (This was true; Doss had confirmed the log entry.) "She then escorted me past the sentries under her remaining authority, and remained behind to face exactly these proceedings. She acted unlawfully. I do not ask the panel to pretend otherwise. I ask the panel to consider what the law is *for*. Captain Verren enforced the law against her own career, in the only direction it could still be enforced — upward. The alternative was enforcing it downward, onto a district of printers and children. One of those choices protected this army. The other would have protected Colonel Ashe. The panel should ask itself why the man who stole the ledger sits in this room as the accuser, and the woman who exposed him sits in it as the accused."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. Verren had not looked at me once since I began. She stared at her folded hands, jaw tight, and I realized — with the particular cruelty of hindsight — that she wasn't refusing to look at me out of contempt. She was refusing to let the panel see what my being alive and free and *there*, voluntarily, meant to her.

The panel deliberated for a day and a half. When they returned, the sentence was read in that flat parade-ground voice that makes mercy sound like arithmetic: guilty of dereliction of duty and destruction of official property; sentence reduced in consideration of service rendered to the discipline of the army; dismissed from the service, forfeit of half pay, *and no more*.

No hanging. Not even prison. A door closing instead of a lid.

Verren received it standing, saluted no one, and walked out between the sentries a civilian, which is to say a person.

* * *

I found her that evening on the sea-wall road outside the garrison, because of course she'd walk toward the water; everyone I love eventually walks toward water. She was in a plain coat, her hair down for perhaps the first time in her adult life, looking at the estuary the way you look at a room you've just been evicted from.

"You shouldn't have come," she said, without turning. "You should be on a ship beyond any warrant."

"I was. I came anyway."

"That was stupid."

"Yes," I said. "Almost everything I've done since October has been stupid. I've decided stupidity is just loyalty that hasn't been audited yet."

That got something out of her — not quite a laugh, more the shape a laugh leaves behind. She turned then, and we looked at each other properly for the first time since the pier, and I don't mind telling you my hands shook, though hers didn't. Hers never did.

"You fed me," I said. "That winter. My father told me about it years later — how the officer billeted upstairs ate less than her rations so there'd be more for the table, and pretended not to notice we were doing the same. I built you into something enormous after that. An officer who was secretly good. A proof that the uniform contained people."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the uniform from both sides of it. I watched your army take our shop, take Tally, take Quill, take everything my father touched. And I watched you tear the only thing they couldn't replace." I shook my head. "You're not enormous, Captain. You're just a person who paid a debt. I'm trying to stop making idols of people. It never survives contact with the actual human."

"Good," she said. "Idols are heavy to carry." Then, quieter: "I read the ledger. All of it. My name is in it, Okafor. Third column, famine winter. Paid to look away — except I never saw the payment, never signed anything; my colonel's clerk collected for the whole billet and I dined on garrison rations believing my lodging was simply unpaid charity. Your family fed me thinking I'd *refused* the army's money. We were both lied to by the same column of figures." Her mouth twisted. "I spent twenty years believing I owed you a kindness. It turns out the kindness was real but the debt ran the other direction. Your father was feeding a soldier the army had quietly sold. Does that change what you came to say?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and I was tired of carrying anything heavier than truth.

"It changes the story," I said. "Not the ending. Someone chose, in the middle of all those columns of numbers, to go hungry next to you and say nothing. That was my father. That was real regardless of who was keeping the books. Debts are just stories we tell so the arithmetic feels like morality. The morality was in the room, Captain. Both times. Yours and his."

She nodded slowly, and for a while we just watched the water, two women who had cost each other everything and saved each other completely, neither figure balancing the other, both columns open.

"They offered me transport east," she said finally. "Discharged officers get passage on the mail packet. I've never been east of the garrison in my life." A pause, and then, in a tone I suspected she'd practiced in a mirror and abandoned as insufficiently casual: "I'm told there are ships on this coast printing things."

I kept my face very still, which took effort. "I'm told the pay is half of nothing and the quarters smell of ink."

"I've slept worse."

"There's a boy rigger who'll steal your boots."

"I've guarded worse."

"There's a clerk who cooks abominably and cries when he talks about the trials."

"I've commanded worse." And then Ilse Verren smiled — small, crooked, the first I'd ever seen on her, and worth every mile of that stupid dangerous trip. "Measure me for a berth, scrivener. I find I'm suddenly between postings."

We shook hands. Evenly. Equals — one of us unemployed, the other unemployable, both of us free in ways the law hadn't authorized. Forgiveness, I decided somewhere on that sea-wall, isn't a ledger you balance. It's a page you turn together, agreeing to stop auditing.

I walked back to the harbor with her stride matching mine, and by the time we reached the pier the packet boat was blowing for the tide, and Kade's ship was riding at anchor beyond it with a lantern lit at the rail — and a second lantern, newly hoisted, burning alongside the first.

Two lights. Which meant, by the code Tally had invented herself and never explained to anyone, that there was news aboard worth waking the whole crew for — and that it concerned the south road, and smoke.
