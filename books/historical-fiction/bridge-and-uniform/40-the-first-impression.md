## 40. The First Impression

There is a particular sound a press makes when everything about it is wrong and it works anyway. A kind of complaint. A wooden groan that starts in the cheeks of the frame and travels down through the platen and out along the deck until the whole ship seems to be clearing its throat to say something important.

The *Mercy of Kade* — Nessa's joke of a name, which she refuses to explain and we have all stopped asking about — had never made that sound before. She had made sounds of cargo shifting, sounds of rope complaining, sounds of a cook named Pella arguing with a stove through the bulkhead. But this was new. This was the sound of a ship becoming something else.

We had spent nine days building the press aboard her, which anyone in the trade will tell you is madness. You do not build a press on water. Water moves. Type rolls. A forme that is perfectly level at dusk is a scandal by morning. Doss said this repeatedly, in the tone of a man who had spent his career watching other people's plans go wrong and wished only to be accurate about it in advance.

"You're right," I told him, on the first day, and every day after that I stopped answering, because being right was doing him no good whatsoever.

Tally, who at fourteen had developed the specific confidence of someone who has survived one arrest and considers herself thereby qualified for anything, stood on a crate and directed the raising of the frame with a authority she had entirely invented. The boy rigger — Fen, whose actual job aboard was to climb things no sane person climbs — rigged us a purchase from the mainyard so we could lower the bed of the press into the hold without dropping it through the hull. Sergeant Harl, late of His Majesty's army, current defector and current holder of the opinion that everything we did would end in the hold of a naval brig, held the ropes anyway. He always held the ropes. That was his way of dissenting: dissenting with his whole body while his mouth grumbled.

And Nessa Kade stood at the companionway with her arms crossed, watching a third of her cargo space disappear under a machine that printed sedition, and said nothing at all.

That silence should have frightened me more than it did. But by then I had learned to read Kade the way you read a page set in unfamiliar type — slowly, trusting the letterforms rather than the first impression. Her silence meant *I have decided*. Her speech meant *I am still deciding*. On the ninth day, when she finally came down into the hold and put her hand flat against the press frame the way a horse trader checks a leg, she said:

"She'll affect the trim. Stern-heavy in a crosswind."

"We can move ballast."

"I know we can move ballast. I'm telling you what you've cost me." And then, because that was as close as Kade ever came to affection, she added, "Your father owed me forty crowns when he died. I'm calling it settled."

My father had not owed her forty crowns. My father had lent *her* money once, during the freeze, and had never once mentioned it. I let her have the arithmetic. Some debts are shaped strangely, and you carry them anyway.

* * *

On the tenth morning, we were ready, which surprised everyone including the press.

The hold had been cleared and scrubbed. Pella contributed an oil lamp with a shade so the flame wouldn't gutter with the roll of the sea. Fen had rigged a small swing-table for the type cases, hung from gimbals like a compass, so that the letters would stay put when the water argued. It was Tally's idea, though she'd deny it — she'd claim Fen built it, and Fen would claim Tally designed it, and between the two of them they'd constructed a version of events in which neither needed to thank the other. I let them have their history too. I was learning that people will build remarkable things if you let them tell the story afterward however they like.

The type itself was mine. Not the matrices from under the floorboards — those had gone to the council at the estuary, copied and scattered, and were by now printing pamphlets in six towns whose names I knew and four I didn't. No, this was older stock. When Ashe burned the shop, he burned the wood and the walls and the floorboards and eleven years of my life, but fire is a blunt censor. Lead does not burn. We had sunk a crate of my father's best faces in the river cistern two days before the requisition — old habit, printers' insurance, the trade's quiet answer to the question of what can be carried. What can be carried is sometimes what can be sunk and found again.

So the type in the cases above the swinging table had been underwater, then in a smuggler's bilge, then in my hands on a night crossing, and now it sat in gimbals on a ship printing truth up and down the coast, and every letter of it had been cut by my father's hand or mine.

Doss had talked his way ashore at Corren's Landing three days prior — he had business there, he said, involving a customs clerkship he was perhaps not strictly entitled to, and a widow he was perhaps not strictly courting. He came back aboard at midnight smelling of pipe smoke and good news, which on Doss was an alarming combination.

"Ashe's trial starts next month," he reported, hanging his coat on a nail as though he had always lived here, as though he had not been a coward and then briefly the bravest man I knew and then simply one of us. "His own side is trying him. They're very embarrassed. Embarrassed armies are thorough." He paused, and looked at me the way he'd begun looking at me lately — not calculating anymore, just careful. "Verren testified."

The hold went quiet in the way a room full of people pretending to work goes quiet.

"And?" I asked. I kept my hands sorting type. Fifteen-point, lowercase, the *e*'s toward the front. There is always an *e* to sort. It is one of the mercies of the trade.

"Demoted. Stripped of command. Not hanged." Doss turned his hat in his hands. "Half the panel wanted to hang her for tearing the commission. The other half wanted to hang Ashe twice, once for each half of the ledger. She got demotion." He set the hat down. "There's a letter. She wouldn't send it through channels. It came to me because I'm apparently the sort of man letters come to now."

He handed it over and studied the ceiling with tremendous interest.

Her hand was exactly as I remembered it from the requisition papers — square, unhurried, the writing of someone who expected what she wrote to be obeyed and had learned to expect otherwise. It was short.

*Miss Okafor —*

*I am told you are alive and at large, both of which I confess I prefer to the alternatives. The court has seen fit to ruin me gently, which is more than the law owes either of us. I write less to claim your gratitude than to discharge my own. A famine winter is a long debt. I have watched you pay yours forward at some cost, and I find I could not have borne it if you had failed.*

*They say there is a press at sea. If the rumor is true, print this if you like — I imagine my name sells fewer copies than the Colonel's. But if you ever pass Vellsmark, there is a woman there who tends a garden badly and reads everything, and she would stand you a meal without asking what you've become.*

*Measure well.*

*I.V.*

I read it twice. Then I folded it and put it in the box where I kept my father's cutting knife and Quill's confession and the single guild pin I'd taken off a dead man's coat, because grief keeps its own evidence room, and I had stopped apologizing for mine.

"Well?" Tally demanded. She had no patience whatsoever for other people's private moments unless they concluded quickly.

"Demotion," I said. "She lives."

Tally nodded once, gravely, with the enormous seriousness of the young, and went back to distributing spaces. Fen let out a breath he'd been holding since the trial began, apparently. Pella, from above, shouted down that if we were finished with our feelings, breakfast was ready, and feelings did not keep porridge warm.

* * *

We chose the first line that evening, and by *we* I mean I chose it and everyone else offered opinions I pretended to consider.

It should have been something grand. Doss lobbied for a declaration — *THE TRUTH SAILS FREE*, that sort of thing, the sort of thing that looks magnificent on a broadsheet and makes typesetters wince at the kerning. Tally wanted the ship's name and the date, because documents matter and she had learned that lesson in an interrogation room at thirteen years old, which is a lesson no one should own. Kade, consulted out of politeness, said, "Print whatever you like; just don't print my name," which was, from Kade, practically a blessing.

But I kept coming back to the line my father used to set at the bottom of every job ticket, in a face so small most customers never noticed it. He wasn't hiding it, exactly. He was signing the world in a way only other cutters would find. *Measure the world in what can be carried.* I had thought, at twelve, that it was advice about luggage. At twenty-two, standing in the ashes of his shop with a knife in my fist, I had understood it differently. Now, at sea, with the gimbal table swaying and the lamp throwing steady light across the case, I understood it a third way entirely.

Everything can be carried. Every grief, every ledger, every name. The question is only what you choose to bear, and whether what you carry breaks you or becomes you.

I set the line myself. Of course I set it myself. Nine days of building a press in a ship's belly and no one had suggested otherwise, which is the closest this strange crew has ever come to ceremony.

The letters went into the stick one by one, upside down and backward, reading right to left the way all true things must before they become readable. My father's *M*, with the slightly heavy foot he favored because he claimed it gave the letter morals. The *e*'s, endless faithful *e*'s. My own *k*, cut at seventeen, still sharp, still slightly proud of itself. I justified the line, locked it in the chase, wedged it tight, and felt the small deep satisfaction of a forme that will not shift — the satisfaction my whole childhood had been made of, and my whole grief had been measured against.

Then I pulled the lever.

The press complained, the way I told you. The deck rolled, the platen came down, and for one held breath the entire world was the width of a sheet of paper.

When the platen rose, Tally reached in and lifted the proof with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping infant or a live coal, and laid it on the barrel head to dry.

*MEASURE THE WORLD IN WHAT CAN BE CARRIED.*

Backward in the forme, forward on the sheet. Black and wet and slightly crooked, because the sea had moved beneath us at the exact wrong moment, and perfect anyway. Perfect the way things are perfect when the crookedness is honest.

Fen leaned in until his nose nearly touched the paper. "It'll smudge," he said.

"It's supposed to," I said. "Ink dries. That's how you know it's real."

He considered this with the gravity Fen brought to all matters of rigging and reality. "Like scars?"

"Worse than scars. Scars only prove you survived. Ink proves what happened."

Kade appeared at my shoulder without making a sound, which is her principal talent and the reason she remains uncaught after fifteen years of smuggling. She read the line. She looked at it for a long time, longer than a woman who doesn't read much should need to, and I understood she was reading something else in it — debts, fathers, the arithmetic of the sea.

"He'd have liked this," she said at last. "A press nobody can seize. A shop with a tide."

"A shop with a tide," I agreed.

She straightened. "Tide turns at four. We run north — two towns, maybe three, before the revenue cutters get curious. Pella's made up broadsheet stock from old sailcloth, don't ask. Okafor—" She stopped, and for a moment the smuggler's mask slipped and underneath was just a woman who had lost people and found others and never once said so in plain words. "Whatever you're going to print. Print it well."

Then she was gone up the ladder, shouting at Fen about the jib, and the hold was ours again.

Tally inked the rollers. Harl — who had decided somewhere around week two that if this was mutiny it was the most comfortable mutiny he'd ever served in — worked the tympan and frisket with soldierly precision. Doss fetched paper and counted it aloud, because counting calmed him, and none of us teased him anymore, because we had all watched him spend his one great courage and knew what it had cost to keep count of smaller things since.

I set the second line. Then the third. The first pamphlet of the floating press took shape above a rolling sea: names of the condemned, cleared. Names of the guilty, printed. The confession of Master Aldous Quill, typesetter and informer and, in the end, a man who bought back what he could with what he had left. The testimony of Elias Doss, clerk, set in type by the sister of the girl whose arrest he'd once arranged paperwork for — he checked every word three times and I let him, because penance should be done in the penitent's own hand where possible.

Outside, the water ran black and silver under a sky going pale at the edges. Behind us, somewhere south, Vellsmark was waking to its own news sheets — my matrices, my father's letters, the record set straight one town at a time. Ahead of us lay the whole coast, and every harbor town with its own ledger of names and debts and secrets, and a press fast enough to outrun any army that tried to collect them.

Tally hung the first proof from a line Pella had strung across the hold for drying sheets, and stood back to admire it, ink still shining.

"It's crooked," she said.

"The sea's crooked," I said. "We match it."

She laughed, and Fen laughed, and above us the canvas filled with the turning wind, and the ink dried — slowly, in the sea air, the way true things dry — and I picked up the composing stick, and began again.
