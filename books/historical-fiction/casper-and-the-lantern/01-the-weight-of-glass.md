## 1. The Weight of Glass

The sleet came sideways on Christmas Eve, the way it always did when the sea had a grievance to settle, and Hazel Thornbury climbed the tower with his collar up and his mind made down—made down being the opposite of up, which was where his mind ought to have been, given that he was carrying a can of burning oil in one hand and his whole continued existence in the other.

*Watch the stair. Watch the oil. The grief can look after itself; it's had practice.*

Ninety-one steps to the lantern room, and he counted every one of them, because counting was a kind of handrail. He had learned the trick from Maren, who used to count her stitches aloud when the wind came off the Atlantic with its knife out. *Forty-two, forty-three.* She'd said a number was a small solid thing you could hold onto when everything else had gone to weather. She'd been right about most things and insufferably gracious about it.

At step sixty the sleet found the gap in the tower's northern face—the one the Trinity men kept promising to point and never did—and hit him like a handful of flung gravel. He grunted, shifted the oil can to his hip, and kept climbing. A man who has tended a light through two winters alone stops noticing cold about the way a fish stops noticing wet. It stops being an event and becomes a colleague.

*Seventy-four. Seventy-five.*

From this height he could see the Penhale farm below, laid out in the lee of the headland like a thing worth protecting: the long slate roof silvered with sleet, windows bleeding yellow light, and—faintly, through stone and wind and half a mile of freezing air—the sound of the gathering. Or perhaps he imagined the sound. Perhaps it was only the wind doing its impression of merriment, which the wind did very well this time of year, having had centuries of practice mocking the people who lived beneath it.

Bess Penhale had invited him down for the Christmas Eve supper. She did every year. She would do it again next year too, he suspected, on into eternity, long after they'd both gone wherever stubborn women go, her ghost hectoring his ghost across some eternal kitchen: *You'll eat a proper meal, Hazel Thornbury, if I have to send Wynn up that tower with a rope and a pulley.*

He had told her the light needed him tonight. Which was true, in the way that most of his excuses were true—the light *did* need him; it always needed him—but not true in the way she meant, and Bess Penhale was sharp enough to know it. She had looked at him across her gate that morning with those pale shrewd eyes, snow melting on her shawl, and said, "Aye, the light," in a voice that let him know she saw straight through him and forgave him anyway, which was somehow worse than being caught.

*Ninety-one.*

The lantern room door fought him, swollen in its frame, and then gave all at once and he stumbled through into the glass house at the top of the world. Here the storm wasn't sound anymore but substance—a great grey boiling thing pressing on the panes, the sleet ticking against the glass like rain on a coffin lid, though he tried very hard not to think in images like that, having resolved years ago to keep his metaphors dry.

The light itself stood in the middle of the room, vast and patient: the great lens, six feet of brass and crystal, the lamp at its heart a small steady flame no bigger than a fist. It struck Hazel, as it struck him every night, that the whole apparatus was a kind of joke God had allowed men to play on the dark. All that glass, all that weight, all those ninety-one steps twice a night, to multiply one candle-sized flame into a beam that could reach a ship ten miles out. One small fire, persuaded to be enormous.

Maren would have loved that phrasing. *Persuaded*, she'd have said, laughing at him. *That's a nice word for a man who talks to machinery because he's too proud to talk to people.*

"I talk to you, don't I," he said to the lens, and wiped the nearest pane with the sleeve of his oilskin, which was the closest thing to wit he'd managed all day.

He trimmed the wick. He checked the oil feed, the clockwork that turned the lens on its bath of mercury, the weights on their chain running down through the tower's spine. He polished the flash panes until the sleet began immediately undoing his work, which did not matter; the polishing was the point. Work done knowing it must be redone, Maren called that, is either a punishment or a prayer, depending on the heart of the man doing it.

Hazel did not examine which one it was for him. Some doors a man keeps his back to.

* * *

Two winters ago, almost to the day, Maren Thornbury had gone down the cliff path in a gale to check the workshop door, and had not come back up.

That was the whole of it, and none of it. People in the village still spoke of it carefully, in the hushed rounded way people speak of a drowning, though technically she hadn't drowned. Technically the sea had only taken hold of her—one wave, they reckoned, one green comber that came further up the rocks than any wave had a business coming—and put her down again badly, on the way, against the rocks. She had been found in the morning below the workshop, her apron pockets full of glass blanks she'd gone down to fetch, as though she'd been so busy with her work she hadn't noticed the ocean laying hands on her.

Hazel had spent the first year learning again how to breathe, and the second year learning how to stop apologizing for breathing. Neither course of instruction had concluded satisfactorily.

What he remembered, in fragments that arrived without invitation:

Her hands. Always her hands first, in any memory—broad, capable, nicked with a hundred small scars from glass edges, smelling faintly of cedar and turpentine. Hands that could cut a prism so fine it threw rainbows onto the workshop ceiling on sunny days, which she claimed was the real reason she'd become a lens-maker, the money being merely incidental.

Her voice, calling up the tower stair: *Your lamp's guttering, keeper! Even your light stutters when I'm near.* And his answer, deadpan, that it stuttered from pure admiration, and her laugh going back down the steps ahead of her, taking the dark with it.

The last ordinary thing: supper, the twelfth of December. She'd argued with him about whether the new lens design wanted seven elements or nine, and she'd won, because she was right, because she was always right, and he'd told her that living with a woman who was never wrong was like rowing with a current that happened to love you. She'd written that one down. She wrote his good lines down in her journal, hoarded them like a magpie, and he'd pretended to be annoyed and had secretly walked around for days afterwards feeling like a man who'd found a sovereign in the street.

Then the gale. Then the morning. Then Bess Penhale at his door with her arms around him before he'd said a word, holding him up the way a keel holds a mast, and Tom silent beside her with his hat in his hands, and four-year-old—no, five-year-old, it had been his birthday, poor lamb—Wynn peering around his grandfather's leg with enormous frightened eyes, asking no questions, which from Wynn was itself remarkable.

They had fed him. That was the plain truth of it and he had never found grander language for it. In the months after, when he could not cook and could not sleep and could not bear the sight of his own kitchen where she'd sat, the Penhales had simply appeared, day after day, with bread and stew and no conversation required, until eating became a thing his body did again without his permission. They had never once asked what he was feeling, these people. They had only made sure he kept functioning, on the theory—which Bess stated openly—that grief was like weather, and you got a man through weather by keeping him fed and giving him tasks, and philosophy could wait for spring.

And so he owed them. Not the sort of debt you speak of—he'd tried once to say something of the kind to Bess and she'd looked at him as if he'd spat in church—but the sort that lives in the bones. When the Penhales' fence wanted mending, Hazel mended it. When Tom's accounts went astray, Hazel sat up nights with them. When Wynn decided at age six that he would be a lighthouse keeper and nothing else would do, Hazel taught him knots with a patience he did not know he possessed, because the boy's grandfather asked, and because the boy looked at Hazel as though he hung the moon and wound it nightly.

It was, Hazel supposed, a life. Smaller than the one he'd had. Built out of duty the way a drystone wall is built out of whatever the field gives up—no mortar, just weight upon weight, and somehow it stood.

* * *

At midnight he wound the clockwork and settled into the keeper's chair with a blanket and a flask of tea gone lukewarm, because ritual demanded it even if comfort did not. Below, the storm worked the reef with slow heavy blows, and the light swept its ten miles of black water, and Hazel sat in the one room in Cornwall where a man could watch his own loneliness from a slight distance, which was the only way he could bear it.

Through the floor came the tower's old voice—groans and tickings, the weights descending, the wind playing the stairwell like a pipe. He had come to think of the sounds as company. This was either adaptation or madness and he had stopped caring which.

His eye fell on the shelf where he kept the logbook, and beside it, wrapped in flannel against the damp, the small brass compass that had been in Maren's apron pocket when they found her. The needle had bent in the fall. It pointed faithfully now at a spot some forty degrees wrong of north, and he had never had it repaired, on the grounds that a compass that pointed where it pleased was at least still trying, which was more than he could say for himself.

"Christmas tomorrow," he told the compass. "There'll be goose. There'll be Bess's plum pudding with the threepence in it, and Wynn will swallow his whole on purpose to win the bet again, and Tom will pretend he doesn't know the coin goes in last." He turned the flask in his hands. "And I'll be here."

The light turned. The beam went out over the water and swept the dark and came round again, patient as breathing. Somewhere out under all that black, he knew without seeing, the reef lay waiting with its old appetite—the Cornish coast had eaten ships for a thousand years and showed no sign of reforming—and his flame was the thin bright difference between the fishing fleet coming home and not coming home. He held onto that. On nights like this it was the only arithmetic that paid: *the boats are in because the light is lit because I climbed the stairs.* Purpose you could measure. Grief you could not.

He must have drifted, because the next thing he knew the clockwork had run down a quarter turn and the sky to the east had gone from black to the color of a bruise deciding. Christmas Day, arriving gray and mean over the sea. Down in the valley, the Penhale farmhouse windows still burned; he could see them between gusts, and he could see, or thought he could, a small figure in the yard looking up at the tower. Wynn, surely. The boy checked the light before bed the way other children checked for monsters—reversed, Hazel thought, checking *for* the guardian instead of against one.

He raised a hand, though at that distance no hand could be seen, and felt foolish, and did not lower it.

The storm was slackening toward dawn, as storms did, the sea spending its anger in smaller change. By eight it had gone to mere rain, and the world emerged dripping: the headland black and streaming, the white smear of surf on the reef, the cove below the tower with its sand the color of wet iron. After heavy seas the tide always brought things up. Driftwood, weed, the odd cork float, once a ship's figurehead that had frightened young Wynn for a month. Walking the tideline after a blow was habit older than his marriage, older than the light—his father's habit before him, the both of them compulsive beachcombers, certain the sea kept secrets and occasionally misplaced them.

Hazel descended the ninety-one steps, let himself out into the rinsed and shining morning, and set off along the sand with his boots in his hand because he liked the cold shock of it, liked anything that insisted he had a body and the body had claims.

The storm had been generous. Kelp in black ropes. A hatch cover. Half a lobster pot. And there, just past the high-tide line where the wrack lay thick and dark—

Something square. Oilskin-wrapped, tied with tarred cord, sea-stained but intact. A packet, sealed, the size of a prayer book, carried ashore with terrible gentleness and laid on the sand as if left deliberately.

Hazel stood over it a long moment, the rain quiet on his shoulders.

Then he picked it up, turned it over once, felt the weight of paper and wax within—and stopped, entirely, the way a clock stops.

Stamped into the oilskin, small and neat, pressed there with someone's seal ring, was a crest he had known for fifteen years. It hung on the wall of a farmhouse kitchen half a mile inland. A boy he loved wore it on a chain round his neck.

It was the Penhale crest.
