## 2. What the Sea Returns

The storm went out with the tide, the way a quarrelsome guest leaves a party—loudly, taking things that weren't its own, and slamming the door behind it.

Hazel Thornbury stood at the top of the tower and watched the aftermath instead of the horizon, which was a habit he'd have to break if he meant to keep the light properly. The horizon was doing nothing. The sea beneath it, however, was busy: heaving itself about in the gray morning like an enormous dog shaking off water, and strewing the beach below with everything it had swallowed over the past two days and now, apparently, regretted.

There was timber down there. There were spars, half a fish-smack's hull, enough rope to rig a church, and — he squinted — what looked very much like somebody's piano, or at least a piano's ambitions.

"Right," Hazel said to the empty lamp room, because two years of talking to yourself makes a habit you can't reason with. "Nothing for it, then."

He could have stayed up in the tower. The light had burned all night without complaint; the mechanism wanted oiling and the glass wanted cleaning, but neither task required him to descend ninety-three steps and then walk down a cliff path slicked with sleet into the wreckage-strewn cold. A sensible keeper would have made his coffee, seen to his wicks, and let the sea keep its secrets another day.

Hazel poured the coffee into a tin flask anyway, wound a scarf around his neck until only his eyes showed, and went out.

He told himself it was duty. Keepers walked their beaches after storms; it was in no rulebook he'd ever read, but Bess Penhale had told him once that her father-in-law had done it every blow, come rain or ruin, and so it had the weight of scripture. Debris on the tideline could mean wreckage that would foul the harbor chain. Could mean salvage someone would come arguing over. Could mean bodies, though he made himself not think about that part, and then thought about it the whole way down the path regardless, boots skidding on green stone while the wind tried its old trick of shoving him gently toward the edge, just to see.

"No," he told the wind, as he told it most mornings. It was the longest conversation he had with anything besides himself, these days, and it never ended well for either party.

* * *

The beach at low tide after a storm is one of God's stranger junk shops. Hazel walked it slowly, hands jammed in his coat, cataloguing.

A ship's door, torn from its frame, lying in the sand like something waiting to be knocked. A crate of oranges, burst open, the fruit scattered and rolling in the runnel water, absurdly bright against all that gray — the sea had been at them and spat most of them out sour with salt, but Hazel picked one up anyway and pocketed it, because waste offended him in principle even when the goods were ruined. Two gulls followed him at a respectful distance, having learned over the years that the tall gray man sometimes produced food from his pockets, and that patience was cheaper than diving.

"Don't get ideas," he told them. "It's one orange and it's mostly gone off."

The gulls exchanged the look of creatures who had heard this speech before and received bread regardless.

He found the piano, or what remained of it: a slab of the case, keys still attached in a broken row, lying nose-down in the wrack like a drowned choirboy. He pressed middle C with a gloved finger. It gave a wet, dying groan, half note and half seagull.

"Somebody's going to miss that," Hazel said. He tried to imagine the story — a ship's pianist, a captain who liked a sing-song on the crossing, some poor soul in Falmouth who'd saved up for it. The sea took things whole and returned them in pieces, and somewhere inland this morning there was a house with a gap in it shaped exactly like music. That was the arithmetic of storms. He did the sums every time, and they never came out fair.

Maren would have taken the keys home. She'd have had them strung on wire by teatime, wind chimes for the workshop door, and she'd have defended them against his objections — *Hazel, honestly, it's not grave-robbing, the piano is dead, the keys are just its teeth* — with that particular stubbornness of hers that treated every found object as an orphan needing placement. The workshop was full of the sea's orphans. Half the shelves held things she'd salvaged: brass fittings, a ship's bell with the clapper gone, forty-one corks in a jar for reasons she'd never gotten around to explaining and which Hazel now refused to throw away on the grounds that throwing them away would require knowing why she'd kept them, and he didn't, and somehow not knowing was the last piece of her he hadn't catalogued.

Two years. Some mornings the grief sat on his chest like ballast, dead weight, just weight. Other mornings it was more companionable — sat beside him, said little, kept its boots off the furniture. He'd stopped being able to tell which kind of day it would be until both feet were already on the stairs.

This morning it was quiet, walking the tideline with him, and he was almost grateful for the company.

* * *

He was an hour along, past the wreck of the smack and around the first black headland where the beach narrowed between cliff and water, when he saw the packet.

He nearly missed it. It lay at the far reach of the last wave, in that ribbon of sand the sea combs twice and then abandons — dark olive, oilskin, about the size of an prayer book, and looking for all the world like a hundred other bits of storm-tumbled leather and tarpaulin scattered down the shore. But it was too regular. Too square. The sea returns things in fragments; it does not return things *folded*, and this had been folded, deliberately, and bound, and — he crouched, turned it over with one finger — sealed.

Wax under a wrapping of oiled canvas. A packet made by someone who had intended it to survive water, which meant someone who had intended it to travel, which meant — his mind supplied the rest in the flat voice it used for unwelcome facts — someone who had expected it might go into the sea on purpose, or be lost at it, and wanted the contents kept dry either way.

Hazel looked up the beach and down it. Gulls, wrack, the long empty gray. No ship visible beyond the headland but plenty implied; the storm had scattered half the coast's traffic. Any of a dozen vessels could have shed this. Any deckhand's coat pocket, any mail sack washed overboard, any—

Then he turned it fully to the light, and stopped.

The wax bore an impression. Not a postal seal, not a merchant's stamp. A crest: three fish, arranged in a circle, noses to tails.

Hazel knew that crest. He'd known it for eleven years. It was carved into the beam above the hearth at Tregarrick farm, worn soft by a century of palms reaching up to touch it for luck on the way to bed. It was stamped into the leather of Bess Penhale's ledger covers. Tom had it inked, briefly and regrettably, on his left forearm at nineteen, a fact Bess referenced at minimum twice a year.

Three fish in a circle. The Penhales.

For a long moment Hazel didn't move. The tide breathed in and out at his boots. Somewhere behind him the gulls argued over the orange rind he'd apparently eaten at some point without noticing.

It's none of your affair, he told himself. It's flotsam. It's a letter that fell off a boat, addressed — he checked — addressed to no one, actually; there it was on the wrapping, blank where an address should be, no name, no port, nothing but the crest. A letter to nobody. The sea delivers to whoever walks the beach. That's the law older than any statute, and it doesn't make you a postman, Hazel Thornbury. It makes you a man standing on a cold beach holding somebody else's paper.

He should leave it. That was the thought, clean and simple: leave it where it lay, walk on, mention nothing. Whatever was inside belonged to whatever business the Penhales had that reached past this coast, and families were entitled to their paper the same as anyone — more entitled, in fact, families that had fed him through the worst winter of his life, that had shown up at Maren's funeral in their good coats, all nine of them, and stood in the rain an hour after the vicar had given up and gone in. Families that had a fourteen-year-old boy who'd spent last Tuesday up in the tower asking questions about Fresnel lenses with his chin on his fists, listening so hard his whole body leaned forward like a plant toward a window.

Leave it.

He picked it up.

His hands decided before his head finished ruling, which was the trouble with hands; they had no respect for deliberation. One moment the packet lay on the sand and the next it was inside his coat, inside the inner pocket where he kept the tower keys and the small folding knife and the photograph of Maren laughing at the camera, and the weight of it was nothing, less than the orange, less than a bar of soap — and yet he felt it the way you feel a splinter, located precisely, impossible to ignore.

"You old fool," Hazel said aloud. The gulls didn't disagree.

He walked back along the tideline faster than he'd come, and the sea followed him the whole way, unhurried, patient, like something that knew perfectly well what it had done.

* * *

The climb up from the beach put color back in his fingers and no sense back in his head. By the time he reached the tower door he had constructed, in strict order, the following arguments:

One. The packet was unopened. An unopened letter is a hypothetical; a man can carry a hypothetical for years without harm, the way you can carry a key to a door you never open. He would simply keep it. Indefinitely. Perhaps donate it to posterity.

Two. It might be nothing. Merchants sealed letters with whatever seal was nearest; perhaps some Penhale cousin in the shipping trade had borrowed the crest stamp. Three fish in a circle — plenty of Cornish families used fish. It wasn't as though fish were exclusive.

Three. Even if it were something, it was *their* something, and Bess Penhale had buried a husband and raised four children on this coast and needed his interference the way the lighthouse needed a second roof.

Four —

Four was the argument he didn't make, because four was the true one, and true arguments have a different sound in the skull than comfortable ones. Four went something like: *you picked it up because some part of you has been waiting two years for the sea to hand you something to do besides tend a light, and now it has, and you're frightened.*

Hazel unlocked the tower, climbed the ninety-three steps, and did not look at his coat hanging on its peg by the door, radiating its small heavy silence into the room like a cat that knows it shouldn't be on the table.

He cleaned the glass. He oiled the mechanism. He wound the clockwork that turned the lens, and stood a moment watching it rotate, the great glass eye of the place, sending out its patient pulse — flash, dark, dark, flash — the same message repeated ten thousand times a night to an audience of nobody: *here I am, here I am, here I am.* Maren used to say the light was the loneliest honest thing in Cornwall. She'd said it laughing, head tipped back, and he hadn't understood her then, and understood her completely now.

He ate half a biscuit without tasting it. He made tea and forgot to pour it.

At four o'clock, giving up on the pretense of an ordinary day, he took the packet from the coat and set it on the table under the lamp, and looked at it the way you look at a wasp that has settled on your sleeve: motionless, courteous, calculating.

Up close it was worse. The oilskin was good work, sailor's work, seams doubled and waxed. The seal was thick, deliberate, a proper job. And the blank space where an address should be — that was the detail that kept catching him. Letters are written to people. This one was written to circumstance. To whichever future happened to find it. And yet the crest said *family*, said *hearth-beam*, said a name he'd half-adopted himself these past two years, eating their bread, letting Wynn trail him up the tower stairs, letting Bess call him *dear* in that brisk way of hers that permitted no reply.

He turned it over. Turned it back. Pressed one thumb against the wax seal, lightly, the way you test whether a stove is hot.

The wax held. Of course it held. It was made to hold against the sea itself.

"None of my affair," Hazel informed it, firmly, and put it back in his coat, and hung the coat on the peg, and stood in the middle of his own kitchen feeling like a man who has agreed to smuggle something across a border he lives on.

Outside, the light came on with the dusk, automatic, faithful, asking nothing. Below the cliff, at Tregarrick, windows were going yellow one by one — the farmhouse, the barns, smoke rising straight in the calm after the storm. He could see the kitchen window clearly from here. In an hour or so Bess would set the table for six and send Wynn up the path to fetch him, because she always did, storm or shine, because in her arithmetic there was no such thing as a man eating alone within walking distance of her stove.

And he would sit down among them with a sealed letter burning a hole in his pocket like a coal, and pass the potatoes, and lie by omission to people who had never once lied to him about anything larger than the number of apples in a tart.

Hazel pulled on his better scarf, took up his stick, and went down the path toward the yellow windows, telling himself the whole way that he was simply going to dinner.

The packet rode in his inner pocket all the same, quiet as ballast, patient as the sea.
