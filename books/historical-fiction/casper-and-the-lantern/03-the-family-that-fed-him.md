## 3. The Family That Fed Him

The trouble with the Penhales, Hazel had long ago decided, was that they refused to let him be a ghost.

He had done his best. For two years he had kept his grief the way he kept the light: trimmed, polished, turned inward, burning at fixed hours and for no one's comfort. He had perfected the art of being present and unreachable at once — a man who could stand in a room like a piece of furniture that happened to breathe. Most people took the hint. Bess Penhale had never once taken a hint in her life, and she was not about to start at seventy years old.

So it was that on the third evening after the storm, when the sky had cleared to a hard blue enamel and the gulls were reclaiming the cliffs with their usual bad manners, Hazel came down from the tower to find Wynn waiting at the bottom of the path with his hands jammed in his pockets and an expression of elaborate innocence that would not have fooled a sheep.

"Grandmother says you're coming to supper," Wynn announced.

"Does she," said Hazel.

"She says if you say no again she'll come up the tower herself, and you know she will, because her knees don't work but her stubbornness does."

Hazel looked out at the sea, which was lying very still now, the way a dog lies still after tearing up a slipper, pretending nothing happened. "What's she cooking?"

"Pasties. And there's a ham. She killed the cockerel."

"The cockerel that chased me all spring?"

"That's the one. Grandmother says it was him or Tom's pride, and Tom's pride has more meat on it."

Despite himself — despite everything, including the small hard weight sitting in his coat pocket like a swallowed stone — Hazel felt the corner of his mouth move. "Tell your grandmother I'll come."

"I already told her," said Wynn. "Yesterday. She said, 'Good, and don't tell him till today or he'll invent a reason.'" The boy turned and trotted off down the cliff path, calling back over his shoulder, "She knows you, Mr. Thornbury. She knows you like a fisherman knows weather."

Hazel stood a moment longer, one hand resting against the pocket where the oilskin packet lay sealed and silent. It had been three days. Three days of climbing the tower stairs with his hand brushing that pocket, of feeling the thing there the way you feel a splinter too deep to draw — not painful exactly, only *present*, insisting on its own existence with every movement.

It was none of his affair. He had told himself this forty times, and by the thirtieth telling he had begun to suspect how much effort a lie takes when it is working properly. True things sit light. This sat heavy as ballast.

He went home first, to the keeper's cottage, and washed the salt from his hands and face, and put on the clean shirt that lived folded at the back of his drawer for occasions that almost never came. Then, because he could think of no honest reason not to, he walked down to the farm.

* * *

The Penhale farmhouse stood in the fold of the land below the lighthouse the way a mother hen stands over a chick — squat, weathered, wings out, having absorbed two centuries of storms without complaint and without moving an inch. Its walls were granite thick enough to swallow window frames whole, and its chimneys smoked with an authority that suggested they had opinions on the matter of weather and were not afraid to share them.

Bess Penhale met him at the door before he could knock. She was a small woman built along the lines of a ship's anchor, and she looked him up and down once with eyes the grey of the winter sea.

"You wore the good shirt," she said.

"It was the nearest one."

"It's been 'the nearest one' every Christmas for two years." She stepped back to admit him. "Sit by the fire before your bones rattle loose. Tom! Your keeper's arrived, and he's pretending he doesn't live here half the week."

"Bess, I don't—"

"Hush. You eat here four nights out of seven and you mended my gate in October without asking. A man who mends gates unasked is family whether he likes it or not. Them's the rules."

There was no arguing with Bess's rules. They had never been written down, but they were understood to be older than the house and considerably more binding than statute law.

The kitchen was warm and golden and smelled of baking pastry and rendered fat and rosemary, and the fire had been fed until it roared like something well-fed and contented. Tom Penhale rose from his chair by the hearth when Hazel came in — Tom was a big man gone slightly soft around the middle, with his mother's grey eyes and a face that did its feeling in plain view, which is a dangerous quality in Cornwall — and shook his hand with both of his own, as though Hazel might otherwise escape.

"Keeper. Good of you to come down off your rock."

"It's your rock as much as mine, near enough."

"Aye, but you're the only one of us doing anything useful with it." Tom grinned and pressed him into the best chair, the one with the cushion, and Hazel sat in it and felt the old familiar ambush of kindness closing around him on all sides.

This was how they got him, every time. Not with arguments, which he could withstand, nor with pity, which he could not forgive, but with this — warmth deployed casually, in passing, without ceremony, so that refusing it would require a cruelty he did not possess. Two years since Maren died, and the Penhales had simply absorbed him, the way that household absorbs anything that keeps turning up: a stray dog, a broken clock, a grieving man. No one had ever asked what he intended. They had merely set another place at the table until setting it became habit, and habit became fact.

Wynn came barreling in from the yard smelling of straw and cold air, saw the extra plate already laid, and beamed as though Hazel's attendance were a personal triumph he had personally engineered, which in fairness he had.

* * *

Supper was pasties first, then the cockerel roasted with apples, then a sponge cake that Bess claimed was ruined and everyone else claimed was perfect and which was, in truth, somewhere gloriously between the two. Hazel ate more than he had eaten in a month. There was something about Bess's table — some gravity exerted by it — that loosened the discipline he kept on himself everywhere else. He caught himself laughing at Tom's story about the cow that had gotten into the churchyard and eaten the wreaths off two fresh graves, "and the vicar didn't have the heart to be angry, seeing as the bereaved families laughed for the first time in weeks," and the sound of his own laugh startled him like a voice in an empty room.

Wynn talked through nearly all of it, which was his custom. Fourteen years old, all elbows and opinions, with a shock of dark hair that resisted every attempt at taming and his grandmother's sea-grey eyes, except that in Wynn they were always bright, always fixed on something just over the horizon of the conversation.

"—and Mr. Tregose says the *Providence* went down off the Lizard in the same blow, and her whole crew saved but one, and he says—" Wynn paused, fork suspended, "—he says the light on our headland was the only one on the coast that never wavered. Not once. He said whoever keeps that light must sleep standing up."

"He does," said Bess, deadpan, ladling custard. "I've seen him."

"Mr. Thornbury." Wynn had turned to Hazel with the particular intensity he reserved for Important Matters. "When I'm fifteen, can I start? Properly, I mean. Learning the lens. Grandfather says a man should know a trade that saves lives instead of just filling bellies, but Grandfather also says that about the bakery, and Tom says—"

"Tom says your grandfather liked filling bellies fine," said Tom, "when the filling was free."

"*Can* he?" Wynn pressed, ignoring his father entirely.

Hazel set down his fork. Under the table, unseen, his hand had drifted to his coat pocket, hanging on the chair back, where the packet lay. He made himself take the hand away.

"The light isn't a trade," he said. "It's a promise. You keep it whether anyone sees you keeping it or not. Most nights nobody does. Most nights it's just you and the glass and the wind trying to talk you out of caring." He held the boy's gaze. "If you want it anyway — truly want it, not the way you wanted to be a smuggler last spring — then yes. Come at fifteen. I'll teach you."

Wynn went pink to the ears and pretended he hadn't, and stabbed an apple slice with tremendous dignity.

"He'll pester you night and day now," Tom warned, but he was smiling, and Bess said, "Good. Boy needs a trade that gets him out from underfoot," and the conversation rolled on, easy as tide.

But Hazel had seen Tom's face in the moment before the smile — quick, there and gone, a flicker like cloud-shadow crossing a field — and he told himself it was only tiredness. Only the worry of debts and weather that every farmer carried. Only nothing.

* * *

After supper, Tom brought out a bottle of cider from the back of the cellar, dark and sharp and older than Wynn, and they drank it while Bess dozed in her chair in the manner of queens — upright, dignified, daring anyone to comment. The fire settled into embers. Rain started again against the windows, soft this time, apologetic.

"Storm tore a plank off the boat-porch," Tom said. "I'll see to it tomorrow. And the north wall at the top field took water — I'd take it kindly if you'd keep an eye on the light these next weeks. Winter's not done with us. Extra bright, eh?" He raised his glass. "For the fishing boats coming home late."

"They're always extra bright," Hazel said.

"I know it." Tom smiled. "But I rest easier knowing it."

Ordinary words. Farmer's words. Words about boats and walls and rain, spoken over a dying fire by a man full of good cider. Hazel nodded and answered something ordinary in return, and all the while the letter sat three feet away in his coat, and every word Tom spoke seemed to pass near it, and Hazel hated himself a little for the way he listened — not hearing, now, but *checking*. Weighing. A keeper learns to notice when a flame burns wrong-colored. Once you learn it, you cannot unlearn it.

Wynn fell asleep against his grandmother's shoulder around nine, mid-sentence, in the middle of explaining why the navy should hire boys of fourteen as lookouts since their eyes were better, and Bess woke enough to steer the boy upstairs with one hand on his collar like a wheelbarrow. When she came back down she looked at Hazel for a long moment — that look of hers, unhurried, total, taking inventory of a man the way she took inventory of a pantry.

"You've a care riding you," she said. "You've had it three days or more."

"It's the weather."

"The weather's passed."

"There's always more weather."

She came and sat, folding her hands in her lap. "Maren used to say that. Word for word. 'There's always more weather, Bess, so we may as well enjoy the sun between.'" Her voice softened around the name, careful as hands around glass. "She'd have had you talking by now, whatever it was. She had ways."

"She had ways," Hazel agreed. His throat felt strange.

"We're not her," Bess said. "We don't have her ways. But we've got a table, and a door that's never yet been locked on you, and two ears apiece among the four of us, most of them working." She reached over and patted his knee once, briskly, the way she might pat dough — firm, practical, no sentiment wasted. "Whenever you're ready. Or never. Both are allowed under my roof."

And there it was again — the ambush. Belonging offered flat-out, no conditions, no questions, which somehow made the thing in his pocket ten times heavier. If they had pried, he could have hardened. If they had accused, he could have defended. But they only *fed* him, and trusted him, and left the door unlocked, and asked nothing, and guilt walked in alongside the gratitude and sat down at the table like another guest.

He stayed another hour. He told them, carefully, nothing.

Walking home up the cliff path in the dark, with the lamp of his own tower swinging steady above him — his one uncomplicated relationship left in the world — Hazel put his hand into his coat pocket at last and closed it around the oilskin packet. It was damp still, faintly, at the seams. The seal inside was unbroken. Whatever it said had waited however long the sea had carried it, and could wait longer.

Except that it couldn't, and he knew it, and the knowledge climbed the path beside him all the way home.

He lit the cottage stove. He hung his coat on the nail by the door — then took it down again, and hung it on the back of a chair instead, nearer, within reach of the bed he would not sleep in for hours.

Tomorrow, he told himself, I will go down to the workshop. I'll uncover Maren's lantern. I'll open her journal and put my hands where hers were, and I'll have work to do that asks no questions and tells no truths and breaks no hearts.

Tomorrow.

But sleep, when it finally came near dawn, came shallow and salt-tasting, and in his dream the letter lay on Bess's supper table between the plates, its seal split open like a mouth, and none of the family could see it but him.
