## 4. Maren's Last Design

The workshop stood a hundred paces down the cliff path from the tower, close enough that Hazel could see it from the gallery rail on clear days, far enough that he had managed not to open its door since the spring. Two years of *not opening the door* required, he had found, a certain daily discipline—like winding the clockwork, like trimming wicks. He was very good at not doing things. It was, after all, the bulk of his character.

But this morning the tide was out and the light came slantwise through a rinsed sky, and the letter in his coat pocket—he still hadn't opened it; he'd decided with some firmness that it did not exist—seemed to weigh more than paper ought to. He needed somewhere to put himself. His hands wanted work, and if his mind insisted on supplying terrible work for them, well, there were worse fates than being too tired to think.

He took the key from the nail inside the tower door, where it had hung so long that the wall behind it showed a paler square, like a picture removed.

"Right," he said aloud, because Maren had always said he talked to himself only when he was about to do something foolish, and it seemed polite to warn her.

* * *

The lock turned grudgingly. Salt had gotten into everything, as salt does. The door swung inward on a smell that stopped him on the threshold with one boot in the air: linseed oil and cedar shavings and, beneath both, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, the rosemary soap she'd used. Two winters sealed up and the room had kept her the way amber keeps a fly.

He stood there rather longer than dignity would have approved.

Then he crossed to the window, which was furred green along its edges, and shoved it open, and the wind came in off the sea and rearranged everything at once—the dust sheets lifting like ghosts deciding to sit up, the shavings skittering across the floorboards—and the room became, abruptly, just a room. A cluttered one. Maren had never met a tool she didn't leave exactly where she'd finished with it, on the theory, she'd told him once, that a tidy workshop was the sign of an unoccupied mind. He had pointed out that her mind seemed occupied enough. She'd said that proved it, hadn't it.

The thing under the central sheet was what he'd come for, or told himself he'd come for. He pulled the cloth back in one motion, the way you pull a plaster.

It was a lantern. Of course it was a lantern; Maren Thornbury had been born into a family of Cornish lampwrights and had emerged from the womb, he suspected, already complaining about other people's reflectors. But it was like no lantern he'd seen before or since—a squat brass drum perhaps two feet high, mounted on a gimbal frame, its guts half-assembled: the burner seated but not plumbed, the clockwork turntable fitted but frozen mid-revolution, and above it all the great empty socket where the lens should sit. She'd built the body around an absence. That was so like her it made him laugh, one short bark that startled a gull off the roof.

"You always did build the argument first and find the evidence after," he told her.

The dust sheet over the bench held her journals, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. There were eleven of them. He had read none since her death—had not been able to read even her handwriting without his eyes going unreliable—but the twelfth lay apart, on top, its cover stained with something dark, and this one he remembered: the last.

He sat down on her stool, which creaked a complaint at his weight, and unwrapped the journal, and made himself look at her hand.

* * *

Her writing was quick and slanted, letters leaning into each other like conspirators. The early pages were inventory—brass stock, mirror silvering, the price of crown glass in Falmouth (outrageous; she underlined it twice)—and then, halfway through, the tone changed, and he could hear her voice coming up off the page.

*Spoke to the men at the Ship Inn re: the reef lights. Half of them won't meet my eye. Joseph T. says plainly what the others only hint: that a good light can be a bad neighbor, depending on who's steering by it.*

Hazel read that line three times. The wind worried at the window latch. Something cold walked down his spine and settled in, making itself comfortable, and he almost stopped reading there—almost, because his pocket felt suddenly heavy against his hip, heavy as the packet it held, and he was not ready to let the two facts stand next to each other in his skull.

He read on instead, because reading about lenses is safer than thinking about anything else, and grief makes excellent scholars of us all.

*Maren's design notes*, he corrected himself mentally. *Maren's notes on lenses.* Safer already.

She was chasing, that autumn, the problem that had defeated better men than either of them: how to throw a light past the Gullwing Reef in weather that swallowed ordinary beams whole. Sleet, fog, spume—the great storms didn't dim a light so much as eat it. Her answer, page after page of diagrams, was a lens cut unlike any Fresnel he knew: prisms ground at compound angles, arranged not in rings but in a spiral, so that the beam didn't sweep evenly but *stuttered*—a signature pattern, unique as a fingerprint, bright enough and strange enough that a helmsman would know it instantly from any other light on the coast.

*A light you cannot mistake for anything else,* she wrote, *is a light you cannot counterfeit. Every keeper's beam should be a name. Then when a false light shows from a headland where no named light lives, every master in the channel will know it for a lie.*

Hazel set the journal down.

His hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed him. Outside, the sea worked at the rocks below with the patience of a debt collector. He thought: *she meant it against wreckers*. He thought: *of course she did, she grew up here, she knew what the coast was*. And he thought—unwillingly, the way you probe a sore tooth—about the letter in his pocket bearing the Penhale crest, and about how his wife had died, and about how he had never quite let himself ask all the questions that gathered around that night like gulls round a fish cart.

No. He shut the journal. Not today. Today he was going to build a lantern, because building a lantern was a thing a man could do with his hands while his heart stayed quietly in its box.

* * *

The trouble with resolving to work was that the workshop was Maren's, organized according to a system comprehensible only to God and her, and God wasn't talking. Hazel spent a good hour of the morning hunting. The spanner, naturally, was in the biscuit tin. The biscuit tin was in the coal scuttle. The small screwdrivers lived in a clay pot labeled *BONES* in her handwriting, which he elected not to investigate further.

By noon he had the burner free and was scraping two years of verdigris off its jets with a bone folder—so that's what the bones were for—when footsteps came up the cliff path and Wynn Penhale's head appeared in the doorway, followed by the rest of Wynn, fourteen years old and built like a heron, all knees and neck.

"I saw the window open," Wynn announced, in the tone of a man presenting conclusive evidence in a court of law. "I told Gran you'd gone in."

"Did you." Hazel didn't look up. "And what did your gran say?"

"She said, 'About time, praise be, and take him his dinner.'" Wynn produced a cloth bundle from inside his coat with the flourish of a magician. "Pasties are mutton. There's two."

"There had better be two," Hazel said, "if you expect to share."

Wynn grinned and came in, and immediately began touching things, as boys do, as everyone does in this room, because the room invited hands. He picked up a spare prism blank from the bench and held it up to the light, turning it, watching the window break into pieces.

"What's it for?"

"It focuses," Hazel said. "Takes light going every which way and makes it go one way."

"How?"

Hazel considered explaining refraction to a boy who thought the moon followed him home. "It's like gossip," he said. "Light comes in scattered and leaves with a story."

Wynn laughed, delighted, and put the prism down—carefully, Hazel noted, more carefully than he touched anything at home—and looked at the half-built lantern with its empty socket, and then at Hazel, and asked nothing, which was the most eloquent question anyone had asked Hazel in two years.

"Eat your pasty," Hazel said gruffly, and they ate together on the bench, feet dangling, while the winter light moved slowly across the water and the gulls argued their eternal case.

"Gran says you'll be at Christmas supper this year," Wynn said, around crust. "She says it weren't a request."

"Your gran," Hazel said, "issues a great many decrees for a woman with no legal authority."

"That's what makes her gran." Wynn swallowed, went quieter, in the way of boys approaching a real subject sideways. "Da says the storms coming this winter will be the worst in living memory. Says the barometer's been falling like a paid-off debt."

"He'd know."

"Maren knew weather too, Gran says. Says she could smell a gale two days off." Wynn glanced at him, quickly, and away. Boys learned fast, on this coast, which names were stones you didn't pick up. But Wynn being Wynn picked it up anyway, gently, and held it out. "I don't remember her much. Just her hands. She let me hold a prism once, when I were little. Said I had steady hands and shouldn't waste them on farming."

Hazel found his voice required a moment's maintenance. "She was right about nearly everything," he said. "It was insufferable."

Wynn laughed, and the sound filled the workshop the way the wind had, clearing out the corners, and Hazel understood with a small internal lurch that this was why Bess kept sending the boy up the path—not to fetch him pasties, but to fetch *him*, the version of him that could laugh, out of whatever locked room he'd put it in.

They finished the pasties. Wynn carried the wrapping down the path whistling, and Hazel stood in the doorway watching him go, and thought, with the letter a hard little weight against his ribs: *whatever is in that packet, it cannot touch this boy. Whatever it costs, it will not touch him.*

Then he went back inside and got to work.

* * *

The days that followed established a rhythm he did not examine too closely, examination being fatal to happiness, like weighing a songbird.

Mornings: the light, its routines, its small dignities. Afternoons: the workshop. He cleaned the burner and found, tucked in its base, a spare wick rolled inside a twist of paper on which Maren had written *for the bad nights — use TWO, you stubborn man, you always cheat the second wick.* He laughed until his eyes stung, and used two wicks on the test burn that evening, and the flame stood up straight and honest as a soldier.

The clockwork turntable defeated him for three days. Its escapement was her own invention—or improvement, or crime against horology—and it ran backward to any mechanism he knew. He was within an hour of taking a file to it in frustration when he thought to check the journal again, and there she was, anticipating him across the grave:

*If Hazel ever services this: the third wheel runs REVERSE. Yes, deliberately. No, do not fix it. I know what I'm about, even posthumously, and posthumously I am STILL right.*

He had to put down the file and walk out to the cliff edge and stand in the wind for a while, doing arithmetic against his face, before he could go back in.

But here was the thing he hadn't expected, the thing nobody warns you about grief-work: it worked both ways. Every part he freed, cleaned, fitted—the gimbal swinging sweet again, the mirrors resilvered with his own breath held for minutes at a time—took a weight off his chest that he'd mistaken for his skeleton. He was dismantling the tomb and finding it a workshop. She had left him a machine designed, apparently, to be finished by exactly one person, and that person's hands remembered her even when the rest of him had spent two years pretending otherwise.

The lens socket waited at the center of it all, patient as an altar. The spiral prisms—her masterpiece, forty-one cut crystals, each ground to compound angles she'd calculated in those slanted pages—were wrapped individually in cotton wool in a crate beneath the bench. Thirty-eight of them.

Three missing.

He turned the crate upside down. He searched the shavings bin, the BONES pot, the coal scuttle. He unpacked and repacked every parcel on every shelf. Three prisms, gone—and with his memory of that last awful autumn still deliberately blurred, he could not say whether she had sold them, lent them, lost them, or set them aside for reasons the torn-out pages of the journal might have explained.

Because pages were torn out too. He'd noticed it that first day: a ragged stub near the end of the journal, several leaves gone, cut cleanly with a knife rather than ripped. Maren did nothing carelessly. If she'd cut pages out, she'd meant them for someone, or against someone.

Hazel sat back on the stool and looked at the beautiful incomplete machine, thirty-eight crystals waiting in their wool like eggs in a nest, and allowed himself, at last, one full minute of honest thinking.

She had been designing a light that could not be counterfeited. She had written, in her quick slanting hand, about lights that were bad neighbors. She had died on the rocks below this very cliff on a night in November, and the inquest had called it a fall, and he had been too broken to kick at that word until it confessed. Three prisms were missing. Pages were missing. And in his coat, hanging now on the workshop peg, was a sealed letter with the Penhale crest that had come out of the sea like an answer to a question he'd refused to ask.

The wind shifted. Down the coast, the sky over the headland had gone the color of wet slate, and the sea had that particular oiled look it got before it did something memorable. Another storm walking in from the Atlantic, right on schedule for this impossible winter.

Hazel rose, banked the test flame, and took his coat off the peg. The packet was exactly where it had lived for four days, patient as the lens socket, and his fingers closed around it with the resignation of a man who has finally run out of shelf.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tonight he would wind the light and eat Bess's stew and be a man among friends.

But his boots, walking home in the first spits of rain, felt heavier than boots ought to, and he knew—as Maren had known what she was about, even posthumously—that tomorrow the seal was going to break.
