## 6. Wynn's Questions

The thing about Hazel Thornbury—and Wynn had catalogued this with the thoroughness of a boy preparing a life's vocation—was that he walked the cliffs like a man who expected them to behave.

He didn't pick his way. He didn't test the ground. He simply went, coat collar up against the wind, hands in his pockets, and the cliff path seemed to arrange itself under his boots out of respect. Wynn had watched him from behind the gorse wall for three mornings running before he worked up the nerve to follow, and on the third morning he decided that if he was going to be a keeper someday—and he was, he had been certain of it since he was nine and first allowed to carry the oil can up the tower—he ought to learn how a keeper moved through the world.

So he followed.

Hazel was headed for the north point, where the old daymark stood leaning like a tired grandfather, and the morning was one of those grey-silver Cornish mornings when the sea couldn't decide whether it wanted to be pewter or pearl. Gulls argued over something on the tide line. The wind had teeth in it but not many.

Wynn kept twenty paces back and rehearsed his questions.

*How much oil does the lamp drink in a winter? What happens if you fall asleep on watch? Have you ever seen a mermaid, honestly? Grandmother says you haven't, she says it's all sailors' nonsense, but she also says her plum jam is ordinary and that is demonstrably untrue.*

The trouble with following a man who walked like the cliffs owed him a living was that keeping pace required attention, and Wynn's attention was largely spent on question rehearsal. Which was why, when he stepped on the slab of wet green rock above the little cove—the one every soul in the village knew to avoid after rain, the one his grandmother had named *the widow-maker* with typical Penhale cheerfulness—his feet went out from under him like a startled cat.

The world tilted. The sea came up to meet him at an angle he did not care for at all.

Then a hand closed on his collar.

Not a gentle hand. A hand with forty years of rope and iron and lighthouse staircases in it, and it hauled him back from the edge so hard his heels cracked against the turf, and there he sat, abruptly, in the grass, heart hammering, while Hazel Thornbury looked down at him with an expression Wynn had never seen on that face before.

It wasn't anger. It took Wynn a moment to identify it, because he'd mostly seen Hazel wear either nothing at all or a kind of polite weatherproofed sadness, and this was neither. This was fear, still draining out of it, like water out of a holed boat.

"The widow-maker," Hazel said at last. "In January. After rain."

"I know," Wynn said. "I know it. I just—" He gestured vaguely, as though the whole morning might be explained by gestures. "I was thinking about oil."

"Oil."

"How much the lamp drinks in a winter. I was going to ask."

Hazel stared at him. And then—Wynn would remember this all his life, file it away next to the important things, next to his mother's laugh and the smell of Bess's bread—Hazel sat down on the grass beside him. Not stood hovering. Sat, elbows on knees, looking out at the sea, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched.

"You were following me," Hazel said. It wasn't an accusation, exactly. More like a man confirming the weather.

"Since Thursday," Wynn admitted. "You walk very fast for a grieving man. Grandmother says grief slows a person, but you walk like you're late for your own funeral."

There was a silence. Wynn braced himself for the rebuke, and then Hazel made a sound that started as a cough and ended, unmistakably, as a laugh. Short and rusty, like a gate that hadn't been opened since summer, but a laugh.

"Your grandmother," Hazel said, "should write a book."

"She'd never finish it. She'd stop halfway to feed someone." Wynn pulled his knees up. Below them the sea worked patiently at the rocks, the way it had since before anyone had names for any of it. "I'm sorry about the rock. I know better."

"I know you know better. That's what frightens me." Hazel said it quietly, almost to the sea rather than to Wynn, and then seemed to hear himself and straighten, the shutters coming partway back up. "Fourteen-year-olds are immortal. It's a medical fact. You lot step off cliffs expecting the air to take pity."

"Not the air. The keeper." Wynn grinned sideways at him. "I reckoned you'd catch me. Reckoned right."

Something crossed Hazel's face then—there and gone, quick as a shearwater—and Wynn didn't have a name for it. He would spend years trying to name it. In the moment he just filed it away with everything else about Hazel Thornbury, which by now was a considerable archive: the way he counted the stairs under his breath without seeming to know he did it; the way he ate at their table like a guest who expected to be asked to leave; the way his eyes went, sometimes, to the little workshop tucked into the cliff below the tower, and went soft and wrecked at once, like a man looking at a photograph.

"Come on," Hazel said, standing, brushing off his coat. "If you're going to follow me, follow me properly. Twenty paces back is amateur work. Anyone watching would spot you in a minute."

"Anyone watching?"

"Anyone." Hazel set off along the path, and over his shoulder, in a tone of tremendous suffering: "And ask your questions. You've clearly got a list. You get it from Bess. She has lists too. Hers are just about jam and other people's business."

"They're the same list," Wynn said, scrambling up. "That's the beauty of it."

* * *

They walked the north point together, and Wynn asked everything, and here is what he learned, written later that evening in the marbled notebook Bess had given him for Christmas—a notebook he had titled, in careful letters, *THE KEEPER'S ART, being observations of Mr. H. Thornbury, Esq., Lighthouse Keeper & Generally Remarkable Person*:

One. The lamp drank roughly a gallon of oil a night in winter, more if the weather turned filthy, and "filthy" was a technical term with gradations that Hazel explained with the seriousness of a surgeon describing wounds.

Two. Falling asleep on watch happened to every keeper alive at least once, and the trick was not never sleeping but knowing your body's tricks well enough to outfox them. "I stand," Hazel said. "I recite things. Ports of the Mediterranean. Kings of England backward."

"Backward?"

"George the Third, George the Second, George the First. Then William. Then it gets complicated and you're awake again."

Three. He had never seen a mermaid, honestly, but he said it in a way that left a door open somewhere, a crack of lamplight under a door, and Wynn chose to believe in the door.

They came around the shoulder of the point where the old daymark leaned and the wind came clean off the Atlantic, and Hazel stopped, and Wynn saw him do the thing he always did here: turn his face toward the reef, that black jagged comb of rock a half-mile offshore where the water broke white even on calm days. The Gullet, the fishermen called the channel past it. The Teeth was what they called the reef itself, and they said both words differently than they said most words.

"That's where she—" Wynn began, because he was fourteen and had not yet learned which doors were load-bearing.

"No," Hazel said. Not cruelly. Just completely, like a lighthouse beam passing over: total, brief, and leaving no argument behind it.

Wynn looked at his boots. "Sorry."

"It's all right." And then, after a moment, in a voice that had clearly cost something: "She loved this headland. She'd come out here with her journal and sit right where you're standing and fill pages. I used to think she was writing about lenses." He paused. "She usually was."

"Lenses," Wynn said, gratefully seizing the offered rope. "What's to write? They're glass bits, aren't they? They make the light go round."

Hazel turned and looked at him, and for a moment Wynn thought he'd said something stupid—but no, Hazel's face had changed, opened, the way a window opens when you don't realize it was painted shut.

"They make the light *be*, Wynn," he said. "Any fool can make a fire. Maren used to say a lens is the difference between shouting and speaking. A naked flame says *here*. A proper lens says *here is exactly where you are, and here is where the rocks are not*, and it says it in a language a captain can read from six miles out in sleet. Every prism placed to the breadth of a hair. She could cut them herself, fit them, tune the whole assembly like an instrument." He stopped. His jaw worked once. "Nobody else on this coast could do it."

"Can you?"

The question came out before Wynn could weigh it, and he watched it land. Hazel looked away, out at the Teeth, and the wind pulled at his collar, and for several seconds he didn't answer at all.

"I can keep a light burning," he said finally. "That's a different thing. Keeping and making. She made."

"But you'll learn," Wynn said. It wasn't even a question; it came out the way certainties come out of fourteen-year-olds, flat and shining. "The workshop's still got her tools in it. Grandmother dusts in there every week and pretends she doesn't. You'll learn, and then you'll finish whatever she was building, and it'll be the best light on the coast, and everyone will say Thornbury's light, and I'll be able to say I knew you before."

Hazel was looking at him strangely. There was something almost frightened in it, and Wynn couldn't for the life of him think why, because he'd just said the most obvious thing in the world.

"You talk as if it's already done," Hazel said.

"Everything worth doing starts out already done," Wynn said. "Grandmother says that about bread. She says the loaf exists in your head before the flour does, and your job is just not to get in its way."

"Your grandmother," said Hazel, "is a philosopher disguised as a menace."

"She'd want that on her headstone. Come on—I can smell the noon bread from here, and if we're late Tom eats our share on principle."

* * *

But the cliff walk stayed with Wynn through the afternoon, through chores, through the enormous meal Bess laid down before them like a general deploying troops. It wasn't the near-fall he kept turning over, though his collar still felt haunted. It was the look on Hazel's face when Wynn had said *you'll catch me*. That flicker, quick as a shearwater.

He puzzled at it while splitting kindling, and while feeding the hens, who regarded him with the contempt hens reserve for everyone. And somewhere between the woodpile and supper it dawned on him, the way slow dawns do, all at once and then gradually forever:

Catching people meant caring whether they fell.

And Hazel Thornbury, who had lost a wife to the sea and two years of himself along with her, had arranged his whole life so as to care about nothing that could be taken. Wynn had heard the adults talk in low voices when they thought he was abed. *He keeps himself apart. Can't blame him. But a man can't live like a lighthouse, Wynn—all shine and no door.* That was Uncle Tam's phrase, dead these ten years, quoted around the table like scripture.

Except Hazel *had* caught him. Had sat down in the wet grass beside him. Had laughed—that rusty, astonishing laugh—and asked his questions and answered them, and let Wynn walk beside him on the headland where nobody else was permitted. The door was there. Painted shut, maybe, swollen shut, but a door.

Which meant, Wynn reasoned, sorting kindling with more force than the task required, that the thing to do was keep showing up. Keep asking questions. Keep walking the cliffs until walking beside him was simply where Wynn went, the way the tide went, unremarkable, inevitable. You couldn't argue a man into belonging. You just had to leave the gate open and let him find the yard was warmer than the road.

He carried this resolution into the evening, and it was holding up nicely, right until he carried the tea tray out to the workshop steps at dusk—one of his small quiet campaigns, tea left where Hazel would find it—and found Hazel standing in the doorway with the dust sheet half drawn back, staring at something in his hands.

A letter, Wynn saw. Oilskin. Salt-stained at the corners, the kind of packet that comes off wrecks, and sealed with wax, and stamped with the Penhale crest—and for one absurd heartbeat Wynn thought, *a letter for us, how fine,* and opened his mouth to say so.

Then he saw Hazel's face.

Wynn had seen fear on faces before—it was a village, fear came round regular as rent day—but this was different. This was a man reading his own sentence in a language he'd hoped never to learn. Hazel's thumb was pressed against the seal so hard the wax whitened, and he was looking at the crest the way you'd look at a snake in your boot.

"Hazel?" Wynn said. "Is it bad news? Whose is it?"

Hazel moved faster than Wynn would have believed. The packet vanished into his coat, the dust sheet dropped back over whatever lay beneath it, and he turned with a smile that reached his mouth and stopped there, a lantern lit but shuttered.

"Nothing. Sea salvage. Rubbish off a ship." His voice was easy. His hand, when he took the tea tray, was not quite steady. "Thank you for this, Wynn. You should get in before dark. Your grandmother will send a search party, and she arms them with wooden spoons."

"It's just—" Wynn hesitated on the bottom step. The wind had come around, and it carried the smell of the incoming tide, cold and mineral and patient. "That crest. That's ours, isn't it? Penhale. Why would a bit of sea rubbish carry our crest?"

For a moment neither of them moved. Down in the cove the waves went on knocking politely at the rocks, the way the sea does before it stops knocking and comes in.

"Old families put their crests on everything," Hazel said. "Buckles. Spoons. Probably the chickens, if Bess could hold them still." He stepped past Wynn and shut the workshop door behind him, and the lock clicked with a sound Wynn had never heard it make before, because he hadn't known the workshop door locked at all.

"Go on home," Hazel said gently. "It's nothing. Get in before dark."

Wynn went. But halfway up the field he stopped and looked back, and saw Hazel still standing outside the workshop, motionless in the last grey light, one hand pressed flat against his chest where the letter lay—as though it were something he was carrying wounded, or guarding, or both.

Wynn stood in the long wet grass and felt the first cold drop of a thought he couldn't yet shape: that whatever the sea had returned that morning, it had not washed up rubbish. It had washed up a question, and it was addressed to all of them.

Three days later, a stranger in a good coat took the corner room at the Ship Inn and began asking, over excellent brandy, cheerful questions about salvage rights.
