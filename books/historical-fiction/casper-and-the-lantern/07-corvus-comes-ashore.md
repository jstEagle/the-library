## 7. Corvus Comes Ashore

The stranger arrived on the Thursday mail coach, which was itself remarkable, because nothing arrived in Porthmoor on purpose if it could help it.

Hazel learned of it the way he learned of most things—secondhand, at the harbor wall, from Mrs. Tregaskis the fishwife, who delivered news the way other people delivered milk: daily, in quantities larger than anyone needed, and with a good deal of sloshing.

"Stranger at the Anchor's Rest," she said, sorting herrings with her thumbs. "Fine coat. Soft hands. Been asking after wrecks."

"Asking after wrecks," Hazel repeated.

"Wrecks. Salvage. Who owns what comes ashore." She looked up, and for once the gossip drained out of her face, leaving something older and warier behind. "Asking who'd sell."

Hazel stood a moment longer than was natural, then made himself walk on. The wind came up off the water with its usual January opinion of him, shoving at his shoulders like a drunk friend. He had oil to fetch, wicks to trim, a tower to climb before dark. There was no reason on God's cold earth to go sit in a public house among strangers just because one of them had an interest in dead ships.

He went anyway. He told himself it was because the Anchor's Rest poured the only coffee in the village worth drinking, which was true in the way that a drowning man is technically surrounded by water.

* * *

The Anchor's Rest at midday was mostly empty: old Pascoe asleep by the fire with his newspaper tented over his chest like a shroud, the landlady's girl stacking bowls, a dog that had claimed the warm flagstones by the oven years ago and defended them against all comers, including comers with legs.

And at the corner table by the window, the stranger.

He was not what Hazel expected. Hazel had expected something out of a penny dreadful—scarred, loud, rings on his fingers. This man wore gray. Good gray, well-cut but deliberately quiet, the sort of coat that said *I am respectable* so smoothly you never thought to ask respectable of *what*. He was somewhere past forty, clean-shaven, with pleasant creases around his eyes as though life had been mostly jokes and he'd been generous enough to laugh at all of them. His hands, folded neatly on the table beside an untouched cup, were indeed soft.

"Mr. Corvus," the girl said, setting down a fresh pot. "More hot water, sir?"

"Thank you, my dear. And how is your mother's knee this week?" He said it with such genuine warmth that Hazel felt his own face try to smile before his brain caught up and asked what a man who'd been in the village one day was doing knowing about the girl's mother's knee.

The answer, of course, was simple: he'd asked. That was the whole trick of men like this, Hazel thought. They asked. Nobody else bothered.

Hazel took the table at the far end, back to the wall, where the light was bad and the coffee was apparently brewed from tar and regret. He opened the shipping notices he'd bought in St Ives last week and had already read twice, and over their tops he watched.

Corvus ate slowly and well. He complimented the bread. He tipped the girl precisely enough to be remembered and not enough to be remarked upon. When Pascoe woke and dropped his newspaper, Corvus fetched it for him, smoothed it out, and asked—oh, so idly—if the winter had been hard on the fishing.

"Hardest in thirty year," Pascoe croaked.

"Hard times everywhere," Corvus agreed sadly. "Hard times make hard choices. I wonder—" and here he turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer, a small precise gesture, like a man adjusting an instrument, "—do you recall the *Merrow*, old fellow? Lost on the reef, must be six years back now."

Pascoe did recall it. Everyone recalled it. The *Merrow* had gone down in November fog with a cargo of hides and brandy, and half the village had spent a week dragging the shallows for whatever the sea saw fit to return. Two men hadn't come home.

"And the cargo," Corvus said gently. "It was recovered? Claimed? Or did it simply... wash about, ownerless, while the customs men slept?"

Pascoe's mouth worked. The dog lifted its head.

"Salvage rights is a funny business," Corvus continued, to the room now, pleasantly, as though discussing weather. "People think wrecks belong to nobody. In point of fact they belong to whoever is standing there when the sea gives them up. There are firms—honest firms, London firms—who pay handsomely for local men willing to simply... stand in the right place. At the right time." He smiled at Pascoe, at the girl, at the room at large, and the smile had creases in all the right places. "No law against standing."

"No law against standing," Hazel repeated under his breath, and the taste of it was worse than the coffee.

Because that was the genius of it. No forged papers, no pistols, no confession anywhere in the thing. Just men standing on beaches at the right hours, being paid for their boots' trouble. And behind that—behind the soft gray coat and the warm questions—the other part, the part that didn't get spoken aloud in public houses: ships don't wreck themselves. Not twice in the same season. Not on the same reef. Not unless someone is standing somewhere too, holding a light where no light ought to be.

Hazel folded his shipping notices very carefully, as though the fold mattered.

Tom Penhale came in at half past noon.

* * *

Tom came in the way Tom always did, taking up too much of the doorway and apologizing for it, stamping mud off boots that would never quite surrender it. He was Bess's son in the jaw and his late father in everything else—all shoulders and hesitation, a big man perpetually braced to be told he'd done something wrong.

"Hazel!" His face went bright with relief at finding a friendly anchor in the room. "I thought you'd be up the tower."

"Light's lit at dusk, not noon. Even I can't burn twice." Hazel raised his cup. "Coffee here could strip barnacles, mind."

"It grows on you," Tom said loyally, which was a lie everyone in Porthmoor had agreed to tell together years ago.

Corvus rose.

There was no hurry in it. That was what struck Hazel later, going over the scene again and again like a keeper rechecking a lock: nothing about the man hurried, and yet within four minutes he had crossed the room, been introduced, shaken Tom's hand with both of his, learned Tom's name, his grandfather's name, the location of the Penhale farm, the state of the winter grazing, and produced—apparently from nowhere, though Hazel would have bet his pension it came from the same pocket as the quarter-turn of the teacup—a card, which he pressed on Tom with the apology that he was new to the coast and knew nobody.

"Penhale," Corvus said, tasting it. "That's an old name here. Old names mean old rights, and old rights—" he tapped the card against Tom's palm, once, lightly, a man dealing a fortune in miniature, "—old rights sometimes come with salvage attached. If your family ever wonders what the sea owes it, Mr. Penhale, I'm the man who keeps accounts."

"I—well. Thank you. I don't reckon we—" Tom's ears had gone red. Tom's ears always went red when money came near him; it had been true since he was nine and it was, God help him, still true at forty-one.

"The debt at the yard in Penzance," Corvus said, so smoothly, so kindly, that it took Hazel a full second to understand he'd actually said it. "Forgive me. My trade is debts, you see—buying them, settling them, turning liabilities into friendships. It's dull work but someone must do it." A rueful laugh, shared generously with the room. "If ever a bill weighs heavier than it should, bring it to me. I pay fair. I pay fast. And I ask almost nothing in return."

*Almost.* The word sat in the air like a fishhook in clear water—invisible until you were exactly the wrong distance away, and then all there was.

Tom said something. Hazel never afterward could remember what, only that Tom's hand had closed over the card, and that his eyes had gone to the window, to the sea, and come back empty.

Then Tom saw Hazel watching, and smiled, and the smile was Tom's own, honest and terrible, and Hazel had to lift his cup so there would be something lawful to do with his hands.

* * *

They walked partway home together, because they always did, because the path past the Anchor's Rest forked toward the lighthouse and the farm both, and because eight years of shared winters will build a road between two men whether they pave it or not.

"Odd fellow," Tom said. "That Corvus."

"Mm."

"Says he's in salvage." Tom laughed, short and uncomfortable. "Said it like it was a church charity. 'Standing rights,' he called them. Standing on a beach with your hand out."

"That's one way to put it."

"He asked after the spring convoy." Tom said it lightly, throwing it away, the way you'd mention rain. "Whether it would come round the point this year or take the offshore channel. Customs schedules and such. I told him I wouldn't know a convoy from a congregation." Another laugh, thinner. "Why would I."

"Why indeed," Hazel said, and heard his own voice do something it almost never did around Tom: go flat.

Tom glanced at him. Whatever he found there, he looked away from it quickly, and the rest of the walk to the fork went quiet in a way their silences usually weren't. Their comfortable silence was an old garment, worn soft in all the right places. This one had pins in it.

At the fork Tom stopped. "Bess wants you Sunday. She's making the duck you pretend not to love."

"Tell her I'll pretend very hard."

"And Wynn's got questions. He's got a list, Hazel. An actual list, wrote out. Number one is 'how does the lamp turn.'" Tom smiled, and for a moment he was only Tom again, Wynn's father, Bess's boy, the man who'd shown up at Hazel's door with a covered dish every single evening for a year after Maren died and never once said anything stupid about it. "You're the only one he'll believe the answers from."

"I know," Hazel said. "That's what frightens me."

Tom's brow furrowed—he wasn't a man built for decoding—and then he raised a hand and trudged off toward the farm, broad shoulders climbing into the grey afternoon, and Hazel stood at the fork a long time with the wind worrying his collar.

*Spring convoy,* he thought. *He asked after the spring convoy.*

A coaster, maybe. A coaster lost in a storm is grief. But a convoy—naval escort, insurance ledgers, a dozen hulls in line astern—that's arithmetic. That's a wager placed months ahead. Men like Corvus did not travel to cold corners of Cornwall in January to ask idle questions about April unless April was already spoken for.

Which meant the plot was not a scar in Tom's past. It was a plan with a date on it.

And Tom—debt-ridden, soft-hearted, red-eared Tom—was already standing in the wrong place in the ledger, holding a card in his fist, being courted like a dowry.

* * *

Hazel climbed the tower at dusk as he always did, and lit the lamp as he always did, and stood in the lantern room while the great lens gathered the flame and threw it out across the black water in its patient, faithful wheel.

Eight seconds bright, four seconds dark. Eight and four, the rhythm Maren used to hum to, the rhythm Wynn could beat out on the table with two spoons without missing.

He watched the beam sweep the reef—the same reef, he thought, the same black teeth that had taken the *Merrow* and however many others, and might, come spring, be fed again by men standing politely on beaches asking no unlawful questions at all.

Below him the valley darkened toward the Penhale farm. He could see the kitchen window glowing, could imagine Bess at the stove, Wynn at the table with his list, Tom coming through the door stamping mud, and a card from his pocket laid down beside the salt cellar where a fourteen-year-old might find it and ask, *Da, who's Corvus?*

What would Tom say?

What, Hazel wondered, would *he* say? Because there was the harbormaster a hard ride away in Penzance, and there was a stranger's name that could be written down tonight, and there was a letter he could send that might stop all of it—except that a letter naming Corvus would drag Tom into the light with him, and Bess, and Wynn, and everything the word *family* had come to mean to a man who'd sworn off the word entirely.

And there was another thing, small and cold, sitting in the bottom of him like ballast:

He had seen the man's hands. Soft, careful, unhurried hands. And he had watched those hands adjust a teacup a quarter turn, the way a man adjusts an instrument he intends to use again.

Corvus had come ashore to take the measure of the village. Somewhere in that soft gray head, Hazel Thornbury was already entered in some ledger of his own—*lighthouse keeper, widower, drinks alone, watches too closely*—and instruments that have been adjusted once tend to be played.

Far out, past the reef, a light winked. Once. Twice. Then gone—not the tower, not the harbor, not any light that belonged there.

Hazel leaned to the glass, heart ticking slow and hard, and the beam wheeled round and showed him nothing but sea.

But he knew what he'd seen. And he knew, with the flat certainty of a man who has kept lights long enough to know every legitimate one on this coast by its signature, that it had not been there last week.

The clock had started.
