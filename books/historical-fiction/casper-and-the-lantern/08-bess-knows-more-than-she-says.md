## 8. Bess Knows More Than She Says

The charts had been in the tower's storeroom when Hazel took the post—rolled in a tin case, salt-stiffened, smelling of lamp oil and old decisions. He had ignored them for two years the way a man ignores a mirror when he doesn't care for what it shows. But tonight, with the letter's words still printed on the inside of his eyelids whenever he blinked—*wrecked upon the Gull Rock reef, payment to be rendered by the party known as Corvus*—he spread them across the kitchen table and weighted the corners with mugs and a stone from the windowsill.

The lamp above him turned its steady circuit. Below, the sea worked at the cliffs like a patient carpenter.

Hazel traced the coastline with one finger. Gull Rock lay a mile east of his light, a black fang at low water, invisible at high except by the white water breaking over it. A ship's master who didn't know these waters—and in 1897, no master out of Bristol knew them well—would take the headland lights for markers of safe passage. If the lights were wrong. If they were *placed* wrong.

He found himself doing arithmetic he did not want to do. Distances between his tower and the Penhale farm. Lines of sight. The angle from which a lantern shown in a farmhouse window might be mistaken for a harbor light.

"Stop it," he said aloud, and the word fell into the empty room like a dropped spoon.

But he didn't roll up the charts.

* * *

Bess Penhale had baked for forty years, and in that time she had learned that you could tell more about a person from how they sat with a loaf than from anything they'd say under direct questioning. Her mother-in-law had taught her this, standing at the same oven with the same burn-scarred hands: *Watch them eat, love. The mouth is honest even when the tongue is loyal.*

So when she carried the seed loaf up the cliff path to the lighthouse on Thursday morning—because a man alone eats badly, and Hazel Thornbury ate worse than most, all biscuits and cold tea and whatever guilt was currently in season—she was not surprised by what she saw through his kitchen window before she knocked.

He was hunched over the table like a scholar over scripture, and what lay beneath his hands were charts. Old ones. The ones that had come with the tower, which nobody but a coastguard or a coroner ever looked at twice.

Bess stood in the cold wind a moment longer than she needed to, the loaf warm against her apron, and thought: *Well.*

Then she knocked, briskly, three times, the way she knocked at every door in her life—as though the door ought to be grateful.

"Hazel! It's Bess, and I've brought bread, so don't pretend you're not home."

The door opened on a man arranging his face. She watched the shutters come down behind his eyes—not hostility, nothing so simple, but a tidying. He was putting things away because company had arrived. She had seen Tom do exactly that, at eleven years old, after he'd broken the dairy window and hidden the evidence in the hay loft.

"Mrs. Penhale," he said. "You shouldn't have walked up in this wind."

"The wind and I have an arrangement. It complains and I ignore it." She bustled past him into the kitchen, set the loaf down directly in the center of his charts, and pretended not to notice how his hand flinched toward the paper's edge. "There. Now sit down while it's hot, and I'll make tea, since God knows what you'd make if left to yourself. I saw your tea once at the funeral. It was the color of ditchwater and just as nourishing."

"You didn't have my tea at the funeral," Hazel said. "You brought your own."

"And thank heaven I did." She filled the kettle from his pump without asking, because asking would only waste both their time. "I'll say this for you, Hazel Thornbury—you keep a clean kitchen for a grieving man. Most widowers let the crockery go feral."

"Maren kept it clean. I've just failed to ruin it yet."

It was the most he'd said about Maren in a month, and Bess felt it land somewhere soft in her chest. She let the silence hold while the kettle began its low grumble, and she glanced—at first idly, then not idly at all—at the chart under the loaf.

Gull Rock was marked there. Someone had drawn a small circle around it, recently, in pencil. Pencil could be erased; that was the trouble with pencil, and the hope of every guilty soul since graphite was invented.

"Hard winter coming," she said pleasantly. "The gulls know it. They're coming inland early, sitting on the fields like they're owed rent. My father used to say a hard gull year meant a hard sea year, and a hard sea year meant—" she broke off, as though she'd merely run out of sentence. "Well. You know what it meant. Wrecks."

"Wrecks," Hazel agreed, carefully.

"Terrible winters we've had, some of them." She found the tea—he kept it in the wrong cupboard, of course, next to the candles where any sensible person would keep flour—and measured it generously. "The winter of 'seventy-one, now. I was a girl. Six ships on this coast between Advent and Lady Day. My mother fed sailors off the beach for a fortnight, whole crews half-drowned, and half of them never spoke again, just sat by the fire looking at the walls. You'd give them soup and they'd look through you like you were made of glass."

"What happened in seventy-one?" Hazel asked. His voice was very level. Level voices were interesting, in Bess's long experience. Flat roads usually meant someone had done grading.

"Storms mostly. And men being fools in them." She set two cups down, missing the saucers deliberately, an old habit of possession. "And false lights."

The word went into the room like a dropped match.

Hazel did not move. That itself moved her—it told her he knew the weight of what she'd said, and had chosen to stand very still under it, the way a man stands still when he's stepped somewhere he suspects is thin ice.

"False lights?" he said.

"Lamps hung where lamps shouldn't be. To make a dangerous shore look safe, or a safe shore look dangerous. Wreckers used them, back in my grandfather's day—lure a ship onto the rocks and call it salvage. Everyone knows the stories." She poured the tea, watching him over the pot's steam. "Nobody does it anymore, of course. We're all Christians now."

"Of course," said Hazel.

She smiled at him, warm as the loaf, and gave him the truth sideways, the way she'd given truth to Tom and before him to her husband John, because sometimes a family could only swallow truth in small doses: "Tom's father drowned saving men from a wrecked ship. Did I ever tell you that?"

"...You said he drowned. In 'eighty-four."

"He did. The *Speranza*, out of Cardiff, went on the reef in February fog. John rowed out four times. On the fifth trip the sea took the boat and him with it, and the men he was after lived, which is either a great joke or no joke at all depending on how you're feeling about it." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "They gave me a medal for him. It's in a drawer somewhere. Medals are heavy things to dust."

"That's a brave story, Mrs. Penhale."

"It's a *complicated* story, is what it is." She looked at him straight then, over the rim of her cup, and let the old grief show plainly, because plainness was her best weapon and her only honesty. "Because here's the thing nobody says about wrecks, Hazel. Half this coast made money off them one way or another, back in the day. Salvage. Cargo washing up. My husband's grandfather—God rest the old sinner—was rumored to have gotten his first boat that way, and the second boat too. Every farm along here has a grandfather like that, if you dig. We dug ourselves out of poverty with other people's shipwrecks, some of us, generations back, and then we rowed out to save the drowning to balance the books. That's the Cornish coast for you. Sin and rescue in the same pair of hands."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. The lamp overhead ticked through its rotation.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked finally.

"Because you're sitting at that table looking at charts with circles on them," Bess said, "and I thought you should know that whatever questions you're asking, this family has already answered harder ones. Or failed to. Either way, we've bled for it." She rose, brushed crumbs from her apron, and collected her shawl. "Eat the bread. All of it, mind, not just the ends. And Hazel—"

She paused at the door, and the wind came in around her skirts, smelling of salt and something colder underneath.

"—when you've finished asking your questions, come ask them to my face. I'm an old woman. I haven't the patience to be found out slowly."

Then she was gone, down the cliff path, walking into the wind with her chin up as though it owed her money.

* * *

Bess made it halfway home before she stopped on the headland and let herself feel what she was feeling, which was fear.

Not of Hazel. Of the shape of things. She had raised Tom on stories of his father's heroism, and she had never once, in thirty years of widowhood, told anyone the whole of it—that the *Speranza*'s wreck that February night had been preceded by a light on the north headland that no one could account for; that John had gone gray when she mentioned it and forbidden her to speak of it; that he had rowed out those five trips like a man trying to pay a debt he hadn't incurred but hadn't refused either. She believed, in her bones, in the place where belief needs no evidence, that John had known what the light was, and who hung it, and that he had spent his last night on earth hauling drowning strangers out of the sea in penance for sins committed by men whose names she had never learned.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps it was only fog and bad luck and a good man dying the way good men die, stupidly and bravely. She had held both versions in her mind for thirty years, turning them like a coin, and had never let either side land.

But Hazel was asking chart questions now. And Tom—her Tom, her boy who cried at the death of lambs and lied about card debts the way other boys lied about being somewhere else—had been strange lately. Quiet at meals. Out at night. Asking last week, so casually it had taken her three days to hear how casually, whether Hazel would keep the light burning extra bright through the spring.

*Extra bright.* As if the sea cared about brightness. As if ships steered by kindness.

Bess stood on the headland with the wind pulling her hair loose from its pins and looked down at the white water breaking over Gull Rock, and she made herself think the unspeakable thought all the way through, start to finish, the way you lance a boil: *My son could be hanging lights for Corvus.*

She waited to see what she would do with the thought, once she'd allowed it.

What she did was pick up her basket, walk home, and put a joint of beef on to roast, because whatever was coming—and something was coming; she could taste it on the wind the way the gulls tasted weather—the family would face it fed. Forty years of baking taught you that much at least. People tell the truth at a full table. Or if they don't tell it, they at least stop carrying it alone, which is halfway to telling.

That evening Tom came in late again, boots wet past the ankle, and Bess watched him across the kitchen and said nothing, and loved him, and was afraid.

Wynn chattered about Hazel the whole meal—about the lens assembly in the dead wife's workshop, about how Mr. Thornbury had let him grind a prism blank, about how he was going to be a keeper someday, wasn't he, Gran, wasn't he—and Bess answered him warmly and listened to none of it, because she was watching Tom watch Hazel's name in the boy's mouth, and Tom could not hold his fork steady.

Afterward, when the boy was abed, she found Tom at the sink with his sleeves rolled up, scrubbing a pan that was already clean.

"Tom," she said.

"Mm."

"There's a circle drawn in pencil on a chart up at the lighthouse. Around Gull Rock."

The pan stopped. Just for a heartbeat—a hitch in the rhythm, a stitch dropped and caught again. Then the scrubbing resumed, steady as ever.

"Coastguard business, likely," Tom said. "All that wreck talk lately. Hazel keeps the light; he'd want to know the hazards."

"Yes," said Bess. "Likely that's it."

She dried her hands, kissed the top of his head the way she had when he was small enough to fit under her arm, and went up to bed, where she did not sleep. She lay listening to the wind rise out of the southwest and counted what she knew, the way she counted sheep, the way she counted spoons after company left.

Her son was lying to her. The keeper she loved like a son was investigating her family. And somewhere out in the dark, past the reef, the winter's first real storm was assembling itself over the sea like a debt coming due.

Near dawn she rose, took the medal from its drawer—brass, cold, heavier than it looked—and set it on the mantel where everyone could see it.

If questions were coming to her house, they could come in the front door. And they could look at John's medal while they asked.

By Friday morning, the barometer at the lighthouse had begun to fall.
