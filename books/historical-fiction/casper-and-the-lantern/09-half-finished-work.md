## 9. Half-Finished Work

The trouble with a dead wife's genius, Hazel decided on the fourth morning of his campaign against Maren's lantern, was that she wasn't there to explain herself.

He had cleared the dust sheets off her workbench two weeks ago, in that first flush of purpose that had felt almost like happiness, and he had been humbled by brass ever since. The storm lantern sat on its stand like a patient saint awaiting a miracle it knew would not come cheaply. Its body was finished—Maren had built the housing years ago, copper and glass, elegant as a bird's wing—and its burner was finished, and its gimbal was finished. What remained was the heart of the thing: the prism assembly, eight lenses arranged in a ring, each ground to an angle Maren had specified in her journal to four decimal places and no explanation whatsoever.

Hazel held one of the prisms up to the grey window light and turned it slowly.

"Maren," he said to the empty workshop, "you brilliant, infuriating woman."

The journal lay open beside him, propped against a jar of linseed oil. Her handwriting marched across the page in confident loops: *Seventh prism cut at 41.3 degrees. Refracts clean but the ring loses cohesion past the fifth element. The light must not scatter. It must be a voice, not a shout.* And then, as if that settled anything: *Try the eighth again.*

Try the eighth again. As though prisms grew back on trees.

He had tried the eighth again eleven times. He had tried it at every angle between forty and forty-three degrees. He had tried it upside down once, out of pure spite, which had accomplished nothing except making him laugh alone in a cold workshop at midnight—a sound that startled him so badly he'd knocked over the linseed oil.

The problem, as near as he could work it, was not any single lens. It was the relationship between them. Each prism was correct on its own—he checked the angles until his eyes watered—but fitted into the ring they fought one another, bending the light into fringes and ghosts, a halo of false images around the true beam. A shout, scattered. Not the voice she wanted.

And what did she want the voice to say? That was the question that stopped him every time. He understood lighthouse optics; he could have built a working lantern in a week. But Maren hadn't been building a working lantern. She'd been building *this* lantern, this impossible signature of hers, a beam so distinctive that—well, he didn't actually know why. The journal circled the reason without landing on it. *A ship's master should know which light speaks to him,* she wrote. *No more anonymity on the water. No more lights that lie by looking alike.*

That last phrase had kept him up two nights running. Lights that lie by looking alike. It was poetry, probably. Maren had been full of poetry. He turned the page anyway, for the hundredth time, hunting for the practical note beneath it.

* * *

The workshop smelled of cold metal and old cedar shavings, and by afternoon the rain had worked itself up into something earnest, drumming on the roof like a landlord demanding rent. Hazel built the fire up in the little stove, put the kettle on out of habit—for two, still, after all these years, and he no longer apologized to himself for it—and sat down with the journal properly, reading it from the beginning as a whole book rather than a collection of fragments.

This was how he had missed things before, he realized. Reading it in scraps, in grief-sized portions, a page here and a page there when his heart could bear it. Read straight through, it told a story.

It began conventionally enough. Entries about glass, about orders from a lens-grinder in Penzance who charged scandalous prices and delivered late ("*the man is an artist and knows it, which is the whole difficulty with artists*"). Then, a third of the way in, the entries began to change. Dates without weather. Sentences crossed out so hard the pen had torn the paper. And then:

*Went down to the reef road at night. Counted the flashes from the headland. Four, then dark, then four again. No keeper I know shows four.*

Hazel read it twice.

He paged forward carefully, the way you'd handle something with an edge. There were perhaps a dozen such entries scattered through the last year of her life, wedged between notes on gimbals and complaints about the price of wick. She never named anyone. She never said outright what she thought she'd seen. But the pattern was unmistakable now that he read them together: she had been watching the coast at night. She had been counting flashes. And she had been designing a lens that could prove—

Could prove what?

*A light that cannot be counterfeited,* she wrote, six weeks before she died. *If they will use darkness as a weapon, then let there be one lamp on this coast that tells only truth. Let a master know, beyond doubt, whose hand made the light he steers by.*

The fire ticked in the stove. Rain ran down the window in ropes.

Hazel set the journal flat on his knees and pressed his palms against it, as though it were alive and might bolt. He had always believed Maren died of the fever, plain and simple, the fever that came through the village that wet autumn and took three souls besides. He had believed her final months were quiet ones—the slow tapering of a woman tired out, her last project a private fancy, a lens nobody needed. Grief had made a soft shape of those months and he had lain down inside it.

But this journal said otherwise. This journal said his wife had spent her last year hunting something in the dark along this coast, alone, telling no one—not even him. Especially not him, perhaps. There was a page where the writing went small and hurried:

*I nearly told H. last night. Could not. If I am wrong, it is only an old woman's fancies and no cause to frighten him. If I am right—*

The sentence stopped there. The next entry resumed about prisms as though nothing had happened.

"If you are right," Hazel said aloud. His voice cracked on it. "Maren. If you were right, you should have—"

Should have what? Told him? He heard how that would have gone: *Hazel, love, someone on this coast is showing false lights, wrecking ships, drowning sailors, and I intend to catch them with my own lens.* And he would have done exactly what any sensible husband would do—locked the workshop, written to the authorities, wrapped her in cotton wool. And she had known it. Of course she had known it. Two decades married; she'd known the shape of his worry before he did.

He turned pages faster now, hunting the end. Her final month. The prisms. The ring. The eighth element, cut and recut. And then, three days before the fever took her speech, a single line:

*Torn the dangerous page out. Safer in pieces than whole. H. must not carry what isn't his.*

Hazel went cold, then hot. He flipped to the end of the book. Blank pages. Back further. Between the entry about tearing the page and the entry before it, the stitching of the binding showed a gap—a stub of paper caught in the spine, ragged-edged, torn close. One leaf removed.

He sat with that for a long while while the kettle sighed itself hoarse and nobody drank any tea.

She had hidden something from him on purpose. Something she judged too heavy for him to carry, and had carried instead into her grave—except that she hadn't buried it. Torn out. Hidden somewhere, or given to someone, or lost. *Safer in pieces than whole.*

Somewhere on this coast, presumably, was the missing page of his dead wife's journal, and whatever it contained about the four flashes and the lying lights.

Hazel looked at the half-built lantern, its ring of defeated prisms, and felt the ground shift under his certainties. He had thought he was building a memorial. It occurred to him, sitting in the rain-hammered workshop with the fire dying, that he might be building evidence.

* * *

Tom Penhale came up the cliff path just before dusk, when the rain had thinned to a drifting mist and the sky over the sea had gone the color of pewter. Hazel saw him coming from the tower gallery and met him at the foot of the stair, wiping his hands on a rag. Tom was carrying a basket, because Tom always carried a basket; Bess Penhale regarded a man living alone as a standing emergency requiring regular supply drops.

"Bess sent pie," Tom said. "And she says your chimney needs sweeping, and don't argue, she's already spoken to the sweep."

"Your mother runs the most efficient invasion on the Cornish coast."

"She does." Tom set the basket down on the wall and stood there a moment, working his cap in his hands. He was a big man, Tom, built like his father had been, with the same slow smile—but tonight the smile wouldn't quite settle. "Mind if we walk a bit?"

They walked along the cliff path toward the point, mist beading in their whiskers, the sea below working itself up for the night. Tom talked about Wynn first—Wynn had got top marks in navigation from the schoolmaster, or near enough, and wouldn't stop talking about lamps and focal planes and other vocabulary clearly imported wholesale from Hazel himself—which gave the walk its comfortable stretch. But halfway along the point, Tom stopped.

"Hazel." He was looking at the sea, not at him. "I've a favor to ask. Small thing."

"Name it."

"The light. Yours." Tom rubbed the back of his neck. "There's nights coming—shipping nights, when the coastal traffic runs close in bad weather. It'd ease my mind if it burned extra bright, you know? Full oil, all wicks trimmed, none of your economizing. Bess worries about the coaster trade, with winter coming on. Whole village depends on those ships finding the channel." He finally glanced over, and his grin appeared, brief and effortful. "You'd be doing the parish a service. I'd take it kind."

It was, on its face, the most innocent request in the world. Extra bright. Of course extra bright—every keeper on the coast was asked for extra bright by every anxious farmer and fishwife from here to Falmouth. Tom had probably rehearsed it in the yard for an hour, bless him; the man couldn't ask for salt without apologizing for the presumption.

Extra bright. On certain nights.

Hazel heard himself answer easily—"I'll see to it, Tom, no favor in it at all"—and watched the relief move through Tom's big frame like wind through wheat. Tom clapped him on the shoulder, thanked him twice, remembered the pie, and went back down the path whistling, and it was only when the mist had swallowed him entirely that Hazel noticed his own hands had closed into fists inside his gloves.

Because he had read the journal that afternoon. Because he knew now that Maren had counted flashes from the headland—four, then dark, then four again—and that she had torn a page out to keep it from him. Because *lights that lie by looking alike* was not poetry after all.

And because Tom had said *certain nights*, and had not said which nights, and had not met his eyes when he'd said it.

Stop, Hazel told himself sternly, walking back to the tower in the failing light. Stop it now. Tom Penhale had pulled Hazel out of black water after Maren died—literally, had rowed out into the January surf himself when word came that a body might wash, and had sat with him whole nights after without saying a word, because Tom was a man who understood that some things couldn't be helped with words. This was the family that fed him. Bess, who claimed his Sundays. Wynn, who followed him like a gull follows a plough. Tom, who asked for brighter lights because his mother worried, and worried because that's what mothers did.

Every kindness reads twice now, he thought. That's what a letter in an oilskin packet does to you. It doesn't change the world; it changes your eyes.

He climbed the tower and lit the lamp and set it turning, white and steady over the reef. Below him, far down the coast, the windows of the Penhale farmhouse glowed yellow through the mist—supper, surely, and Bess laying two extra places out of habit, and Wynn chattering about focal planes. All that warmth going on without him, offered nightly, asking nothing.

He thought: *extra bright, on certain nights.*

He thought: *four, then dark, then four again.*

Then he thought of the torn page—*safer in pieces than whole*—and of a sea cave he'd walked past a hundred times without looking twice, and of Corvus, whoever Corvus was, and he found that he had already, without deciding to, begun composing in his head a list of which nights the coastal traffic ran close inshore.

He trimmed the wicks to full brightness, as promised.

After that, he kept watch.
