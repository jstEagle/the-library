## 10. The Smuggler's Path

The fog came in on the third evening of December like a debt collector—it did not knock, it simply arrived and took possession of everything.

Hazel had watched it from the gallery of the tower while he trimmed the wicks, a grey wall moving in from the Atlantic with the unhurried confidence of something that owned the water it traveled over. By the time he'd climbed down, it had swallowed the Penhale farm, the harbor wall, the crooked line of cottages along the shore road. The fog bell at the point rang its dull bronze note every ninety seconds, and between those notes the silence was so complete he could hear his own blood.

He should have gone to bed. He was forty-six years old, he kept a lighthouse, and lighthouse keepers who stayed up prowling cliffs in fog were lighthouse keepers who eventually fell off cliffs. He told himself all of this in the firm tone he imagined Maren would have used, and then he put on his oilskins anyway, because he had seen the man come ashore.

He'd noticed him first two nights ago, though he hadn't known what he was noticing. A broad-shouldered fellow in a fisherman's jersey that no fisherman would have been caught dead in—too clean, too new, the wool still carrying the sharp smell of the shop rather than the honest reek of tar and herring. The man had taken a room at the Siren's Rest under the name *Mr. Tregose*, which was a Cornish enough name to be unremarkable and which Hazel had nevertheless never once heard attached to any actual person in the village or the three parishes around it.

Tonight, from the tower gallery, Hazel had watched Mr. Tregose leave the inn at half past nine and walk—not toward the harbor, not toward the farm track, but up along the cliff path toward Gullwrath Head, carrying a hurricane lantern turned down so low it was less a light than a rumor of one.

Nobody walked to Gullwrath Head at night. There was nothing there. There had never been anything there except gulls, wind, and the kind of rocks that ate boats and asked for more.

*And yet here I am,* Hazel thought, following him, *walking to Gullwrath Head at night, because a stranger did it first, and apparently that is now my profession.*

Maren would have laughed at him. She had laughed at him often, in the good years, for the way he could turn any worry into a procedure. *You don't have feelings about things, Hazel,* she'd said once, mending a net in the workshop doorway while he reorganized his tools for the fourth time that month. *You file them.* And she'd smiled when she said it, because filing was how he loved things too—by keeping them in order, by knowing where each one went—and she knew that better than anyone.

He filed this particular feeling under *duty* and kept walking, because duty was a drawer that never stuck.

* * *

The fog changed sound before it changed anything else. Footsteps vanished within ten feet; his own breathing seemed to happen somewhere just outside his skull. Twice he lost Tregose entirely and stood frozen on the wet path, listening to the fog bell count out its patient nineties, until the faint scrape of boots on stone resumed ahead and he moved again.

He kept his own lamp dark. He knew these cliffs the way other men knew their kitchens—every bend of the path, every shelf of rock, the place near Kettle Point where the turf gave way to bare granite and one wrong step put you on the ledges forty feet below. It was a knowledge built by two years of walking alone in weather that would have frightened a sensible man indoors, and he supposed he ought to feel something about that—the fact that grief had made him an expert in precisely one square mile of the world—but mostly he just used it.

Tregose turned off the path at the old coastguard cut, the steep stair hacked into the headland sixty years ago and abandoned forty. That was wrong in itself; the cut led nowhere but down to a boulder beach that no boat could approach except on the calmest summer neap tides, and tonight was neither calm nor summery. But the man went down without hesitation, and his lantern glow sank through the fog below Hazel like a coin dropped into milk.

Hazel followed.

The stairs were slick with spray. At the bottom, the boulder beach spread out black and grinding, the sea working among the stones with a sound like slow chewing. The fog down here was thicker still, mixed with spray, and the world had contracted to the size of a room. He found Tregose's light again—moving north along the base of the headland, toward—

Ah. Toward the cave.

Every child in the village knew Smuggler's Notch, as the sea cave under Gullwrath was called, and every child had been told for three generations never to go into it, which meant of course that every child had. Hazel himself had gone in as a boy, holding a candle, awed by the booming hollow of it, and had emerged with his boots full of sea water and his ears full of his mother's scolding. In his grandfather's time, people said, it had actually held contraband—brandy, silk, tobacco—back when the line between free trade and piracy was drawn differently depending on which side of it your family stood.

But nobody used it now. Nobody had used it in decades. It was common knowledge, which made it, Hazel realized as he crouched behind a barnacled boulder and watched Tregose's light disappear into the rock, either the worst possible place to hide anything—or, if you assumed everyone thought exactly that, the best.

He waited until the light had been gone a full five minutes. Then he waited five more, counting the fog bell, because patience was the only weapon he'd ever owned and he kept it well oiled.

Then he crossed the boulders.

* * *

The cave mouth was taller than a house and twice as wide, its arch furred with bladder wrack, and the sea ran in and out of it in long breathing surges. Hazel stood inside the entrance with his back against cold rock and let his eyes adjust, and listened.

There were voices. Two men, low, echoing strangely off the wet stone so that the words arrived scrambled, directionless. One was Tregose. The other was unfamiliar—a voice like gravel poured slowly. And beneath the voices, a third sound: the careful clink of metal on metal, the particular noise of lantern frames being stacked, which Hazel recognized instantly because he had spent twenty years around lamps and could identify their assembly by ear the way a mother identifies her own child's footsteps on a stair.

He moved inward along the left-hand wall, where a ledge of rock ran above high-water mark, narrow and dry and mercifully clear of the tangle of rope and weed that littered the floor. The cave bent, and the light grew ahead of him, and he flattened himself behind a fallen slab the size of a cart and looked.

They had built a small warehouse in the dark.

Crates, perhaps a dozen, stacked against the inner wall above the tideline. Coils of new rope—manila, not the cheap local sisal—hung from iron pins driven into the rock. And lamps: Hazel counted seven of them standing in a row on a flat stone, assembled and trimmed, their glass chimneys catching Tregose's lowered flame. They were not fishing lights. They were not even honest ship's lanterns. They were signal lamps, mounted on swivels, with shutters—shuttered signal lamps, the kind used to speak across water in coded flashes, the kind a naval man or a wrecking crew would want, and nothing else on God's gray ocean needed both a swivel and a shutter.

Beside the lamps lay something else, and it took Hazel's eyes a moment to make sense of it because it did not belong in this company. It was paper. A single folded page, weighted under a stone, and even at thirty feet and by poor light, something about the handwriting on its visible corner—the slant of it, the cramped rightward lean, the little hooks on the ascenders—made his chest close up like a fist had gotten inside it.

No. Impossible. You're seeing what you fear. Everyone's hand looks like someone's in bad light.

But he was already moving. Later he would not be able to explain the decision; it simply happened, the way a man reaches into fire without consulting himself. He came around the slab on silent feet, crossed six yards of wet stone while the two men argued in low voices on the far side of the crate stack with their backs to him, and put his hand on the page.

The stone lifted clean. The paper came away dry—it had been wrapped in oilcloth; he could see the wrapping folded beside it—and he slipped it inside his coat and reversed his steps and was back behind the slab before the gravel-voice finished saying whatever it was saying about *the spring convoy*, a phrase that landed in Hazel's memory and stayed there, glowing, like a coal dropped down his collar.

Then the sea decided to have an opinion.

A surge larger than the others ran deep into the cave, and the water climbed the floor stones and found the very edge of his ledge and licked at his boot, and in the sudden loud hiss and suck of it Tregose stopped talking.

"What was that?" Tregose said.

"Sea," said the gravel voice. "It does that."

"Somebody's here."

"Nobody's here. Who'd be here? It's a cave."

"It's *their* cave," Tregose said, and the word *their* had a shape Hazel didn't like—an ownership, a familiarity—and then boots were moving, and Hazel did the arithmetic of a man with no weapon, no light, and one exit.

He went up instead of out. There was a chimney in the cave wall near the entrance—an old blowhole, a crack wide enough for a thin man, through which he'd seen gulls issue as a boy—and he got his shoulders into it and hauled himself upward with the desperate fluid strength of a man who has climbed this tower's stairs eleven thousand times and knows exactly what his body is owed. The crack narrowed, squeezed him, scraped the coat off his ribs, and then opened, and he rolled out onto the turf at the top of the headland into fog and rain and the enormous indifferent night, and lay there breathing like a bellows while below him, faint through the blowhole, he heard Tregose say something and gravel-voice laugh and say nobody in their right mind would come out on a night like this.

Hazel, flat on his back in the rain, allowed himself one dry breath of almost-laughter.

"Well," he told the fog. "That's the difficulty with your plan, isn't it."

* * *

He did not open the page on the headland. Some instinct older than sense told him to wait, so he walked home through the fog with the paper burning its rectangle against his ribs the whole way—another letter against his heart, he thought grimly; the sea kept handing him documents and he kept accepting them like a fool clerk—and let himself into the tower and lit the lamp on his desk and sat down with hands that were not entirely steady.

The paper was not a letter. It was a journal page, torn cleanly from a bound book along the gutter.

He knew the book before he read a word. He knew the paper—he had bought that paper himself, at Truro, in a lot of two quires, because Maren went through journals the way other women went through gloves. He knew the ink, iron-gall, the brownish purple of it fading at the ends of strokes. And he knew the hand, of course he knew the hand, he had known it across breakfast tables and over shoulders and in the margins of his own logbooks for eleven years, and knowing it now, here, tore something open in him that he had spent two years mortaring shut.

*Maren.*

Her words. Her torn-out page. Wrapped in oilcloth in a smugglers' cave under Gullwrath Head.

His eyes wanted to slide off the writing; he forced them to travel it line by line, the way he forced himself through everything.

*—confirmed it three times now, so I must stop telling myself I am mistaken. The light on Stennack Point is not a homing light. It is shown only when the weather is foul enough to hide what it is doing, and it is shown on the WRONG side of the reef. I have checked the charts until my eyes ache. Any vessel trusting it would stand directly onto the teeth.*

*Corvus pays for it to be shown. I do not yet know whose hand holds the lamp. I know the payment goes through the inn at Pentowan, and I know the name they use for the scheme — they call it "fishing," which tells me everything about the sort of men they are.*

*I will write to the customs man at Falmouth tomorrow. If I am wrong, I shall look a foolish woman, and I have been called worse. If I am right—*

Here the writing stopped mid-sentence. Below it, in different ink, darker, a second hand had written a single line:

*She asked too many questions. Corvus says the winter sea asks them back. — T.C.*

Hazel read that line four times. The room got very small and very bright at its center, and outside it the fog bell counted, and counted, and counted.

Two years he had believed the sea had taken Maren—taken her the way the sea took things, blindly, without malice, without meaning, a wave, a slip on the lower path, a body the tide kept for three days. He had hated the sea for it, and hating the sea was bearable, because the sea had no face and no name and no friends in the village, and a man could carry a hatred like that for a lifetime without it costing anyone else anything.

Now the sea had a name, and the name was Corvus, and the page proved something worse than murder, if worse could be measured: it proved that Maren had died *working*. She had found the wreckers, the same ring, the same rotten trade, and she had been reaching for the evidence the way she reached for everything—both hands, no hesitation—and they had torn the page from her book and somehow, some way Hazel did not yet understand, the page had ended up wrapped in oilcloth in a cave full of shuttered lamps, kept like an inventory item, like insurance, like leverage.

*She asked too many questions.*

His wife had asked too many questions two years ago, and Tom Penhale was asking Hazel to keep the light extra bright on certain nights, and a man named Tregose was paying debts at the Siren's Rest, and the spring convoy was coming, and grief and conspiracy, it turned out, shared a root—they had grown from the same dark soil under the same headland, twined together for two years while he tended his light and filed his sorrow in drawers and called it discipline.

Hazel set the page flat on the desk. He weighted its corners with the brass weights Maren had given him one birthday—*so your charts won't run away, love*—and he sat looking at her handwriting for a long time, until the lamp burned low and the fog bell tolled the half hour past midnight and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like her hand on his shoulder, what she would say to him if she could say anything at all.

She would not say *avenge me.* Maren despised vengeance; called it arithmetic done by angry people.

She would say: *Finish the work, Hazel. I was nearly done. Finish it.*

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, and downstairs in the flooded-dark workshop under its dust sheets lay the last design she never finished—the storm lantern, the lens that could throw a signature beam clean past the reef—and upstairs in the tower his logbook lay open, and in his pocket, against his heart, lay a dead woman's last testimony.

Tomorrow he would have to decide what truth was for. Tomorrow he would take out paper and try to write to the harbormaster and discover whether naming names could be done without destroying Bess, and Wynn, and everything warm left in his life. Tomorrow.

But tonight he sat in the dark of his own tower, listening to the fog bell toll and toll, and admitted at last what he had felt since the moment he read the second hand's cold line on her page:

This had never stopped being about Maren. And whoever Corvus was, whatever he had bought and bribed and broken—he had no idea yet that the keeper of the Casper light had just joined the other side of the ledger.
