## 12. Storm Warning

The barometer at the Penhale farm had a way of falling that made the whole house feel it, like a stone dropped into every stomach at once. Wynn had watched Grandmother Bess tap the glass face of it every morning of his life, but on this particular morning she tapped it twice, said nothing, and went straight to the pantry to begin counting.

That was how Wynn knew. Bess Penhale only counted stores when weather was coming that might make counting matter.

"Barrel of flour's half gone," she announced to the kitchen at large, which meant to Tom and Wynn and Cissy the girl who came up from the village three days a week. "Salt fish is adequate. Candle wax is not. Wynn, you'll run down to Tregoney's after breakfast and buy everything Mr. Tregoney has in the way of tallow, and don't let him talk you into those fancy stearines with the pretty smell. Pretty smells don't burn past midnight."

"Yes, Gran."

"And take the handcart. Don't be proud. Pride can't carry sixty pounds of candles uphill."

Tom was at the table but he wasn't eating, which was the second strange thing about the morning, because Tom Penhale missing one of his mother's breakfasts was like the tide refusing to come in. He'd buttered his bread and then just held it, turning it over, looking out the window at a sky the color of wet slate.

"You're not eating," Bess said.

"Not hungry."

"You're always hungry. I've fed you through fevers, funerals, and one memorable case of heartbreak involving the vicar's daughter." She put her hand flat on his forearm. "What's amiss, my son?"

For a moment — half a moment, less — Wynn saw something move behind his father's face, some animal turning over in a den. Then Tom stood, kissed the top of his mother's head, ruffled Wynn's hair hard enough to hurt a little, and said, "Tide won't wait," and was gone with his oilskins still smelling of last month's rain.

Bess looked at the empty chair for a long time. Then she began counting again, out loud this time, and her voice was perfectly steady, and Wynn knew it was steady because it had to be.

* * *

The whole village was battening down by noon, and battening down in Porthmora was a kind of theater everyone knew their part in. Shutters came down and were tested with fists. Boats came up the slip on rollers, each hull groaning its own complaint. Old Mrs. Chegwin, who claimed to have been blown from her own garden in '57 and had dined out on it for forty years, stood at her gate directing traffic no one had asked her to direct.

"Wynn Penhale!" she called as he hauled the handcart past, stacked with candle boxes and a sack of oatmeal riding on top like a small snowy hill. "They're saying it'll be worse than the gale of '87."

"They said that about the last three storms, Mrs. Chegwin."

"And they were wrong all three times, which proves nothing except that weather lies low to surprise you. Where's your father?"

Wynn hesitated. "Down at the boats, I expect."

Mrs. Chegwin's eyes narrowed in a way that suggested she had expected a different answer, or the same answer given faster. "Mind your gran," she said. "And mind yourself. A storm like this takes the careless first and the curious second."

He thought about that on the climb home. *Curious second.* He'd been called curious so many times it hardly registered as a warning anymore — it was practically his family title. Curious like Hazel, who could spend an evening taking apart a lamp mechanism just to see the inside of it. That was the best thing anyone had ever said about him, in Wynn's private opinion: *he's curious like Thornbury.*

Hazel. Wynn turned the cart onto the cliff path and there he was, exactly where Wynn's thoughts had put him, up on the gallery of the tower, checking the storm panes. Even at this distance you could tell the difference between Hazel's movements and other men's — economical, deliberate, the movements of someone who had decided long ago that wasted motion was a form of disrespect to whatever work was in front of you. Wynn had been practicing moving like that for two years. His mother said he now set the table like he was defusing something.

Hazel raised a hand. Wynn raised both of his, nearly tipping the oatmeal, and heard — or imagined he heard — a laugh come down off the tower on the wind.

Two years since Mrs. Thornbury died, and in all that time Wynn didn't think Hazel had laughed loud enough to hear at any distance. But lately — a little. Lately there was something different about him. He'd been down at Maren's workshop half the night more than once; lamplight under the door well past midnight. Building something, or fixing something. Wynn had asked once what it was, and Hazel had said, "Not finished yet," in a voice Wynn had never heard him use before, soft and almost happy, and Wynn hadn't asked again, because some things you don't poke at. You wait for them to hatch.

The wind was rising properly now, coming in long shoves instead of gusts. Down on the water, the sea had changed its color from gray to a deep ugly green-brown, and white water was already breaking over the reef, which it didn't usually do until the tide turned. The reef. Everyone watched the reef when storms came. It had taken ships before. It had taken—

It had taken Hazel's wife's boat, though everyone agreed the fault there was weather and nothing else, weather and God and bad luck, and nobody said otherwise in front of Hazel, and certainly not in front of Wynn, who had been twelve and had cried about it in private for reasons he couldn't have explained even to himself.

He got the cart home. Bess counted the candles, approved them, and put both hands on his shoulders.

"Now," she said. "If the glass goes lower tonight, you'll sleep in the kitchen with us, not up in your loft."

"I'm fourteen, Gran, I don't need—"

"That wasn't a question about your age. It was a statement about roof slates." She held his eyes a moment longer than necessary. "And if your father comes home late, you'll let me answer the door."

* * *

He did not let his grandmother answer the door, because his father never came through the door at all.

Wynn woke near midnight to the sound of the window frames working loose in their casements, lay listening to it for a while, and then heard something else underneath: boots on gravel, careful, unhurried. He got up and looked through the cold glass into the yard.

His father was standing by the gate talking to a man Wynn didn't recognize. Lantern between them, held low, the way you hold a light when you don't want it seen further than it must be. Money passed — or something flat and pale did; Wynn couldn't swear it was money. The stranger touched his hat brim, and Tom said something too quiet for the wind to carry, and they parted, the stranger going down toward the coast path and Tom standing alone at the gate with his head bowed, like a man praying without much faith that anyone was listening.

Then Tom came in through the yard door, and Wynn was back in bed with his eyes shut before the latch clicked, heart going like a gull in a sail loft, telling himself a hundred sensible things: it was salvage business, his father bought and sold anything that washed up, it was rope or timber or bales of cork, men met at night over honest goods sometimes, everyone knew the coast did its trading in the dark—

He fell asleep eventually, and dreamed of a lantern burning on the wrong headland, and woke with the word *wrong* stuck in his mouth like a coin.

* * *

In the morning the sky had lowered itself right onto the rooftops, and the wind had a new voice in it, a high whistling note that meant business. School was cancelled — school being, in winter, whichever children happened to be in whichever warm kitchen — and Wynn was sent down to warn the fishermen on the slip that the harbormaster wanted everything either hauled out or double-anchored by noon.

Which is how he came to be on the beach below the cliffs at the exact hour when he saw the strangers.

There were four of them, and they were not fishermen and not revenue men and not any variety of person who belonged in Porthmora. They came along the base of the cliffs in single file, bent under loads: casks, coils of rope, long wooden cases that moved wrong for their size — too carefully, like things that would break, or things that shouldn't rattle. Wynn pressed himself against the rock beneath the overhang of Gull Ledge and watched them file into the sea cave below the old quarry path, the one the village children were forbidden to enter and therefore had all entered, though Wynn less than most because Hazel had once told him, mildly, that caves that breathe are caves to respect, and Wynn had adopted Hazel's cautions the way other boys adopted saints.

The men came out twice more for loads. On the third trip, the last man paused at the cave mouth and looked straight up the cliff, and Wynn's heart stopped, but the man was only checking the sky. He said something to his fellows, laughing, and the wind shredded the words, and they went in.

Casks. Rope. Long cases. In the cave, in this weather, two days before the worst storm in living memory.

Wynn stayed against the rock until his shoulder blades ached with cold, and then climbed the quarry path the long safe way, running most of it, and stopped at the top because there was one thing left he could do with what he'd seen and it required thinking about first.

He could tell Hazel.

That was the whole list. That was the entire inventory of options: tell Hazel. Not the harbormaster — what would he even say? Not Gran — Gran had enough weight. Not Father —

Not Father.

He stood at the top of the path with the wind trying to take his cap and made himself look at the thought squarely, the way Hazel looked at a broken lamp. Four men. Night meetings at gates. His father's bowed head. His father missing meals. His father asking Hazel, weeks ago, to keep the light extra bright on certain nights — and hadn't Wynn been there for that, in the workshop doorway, and hadn't it seemed innocent enough at the time?

Nothing felt innocent anymore. The whole coast had tilted, like the barometer glass, and everything ran downhill toward the reef.

* * *

He found Hazel at the tideline below the tower, testing a mooring ring, collar up, hair plastered flat. Wynn skidded down the shingle to him and grabbed his sleeve before any of his prepared sentences could arrange themselves.

"Hazel. There's men. In the cave — the breathing cave, under the quarry — putting things in it. Casks, and rope, and long cases, and they're not from here, and—" He ran out of air.

Hazel went very still. Stillness, Wynn had learned, was what Hazel did instead of alarm, and somehow it was scarier than shouting. "How many?"

"Four. Maybe five. One of them looked right up the cliff, but not at me."

"When?"

"Within the hour."

Hazel looked out at the sea. The reef was fully white now, the whole length of it, combing foam off the breakers like a long bone showing through the water. When he spoke again his voice was ordinary, which Wynn understood was effort, not calm. "You did right to tell me. Now listen. Go home. Stay in tonight, all of you, and keep your father home if you can — invent something, a broken slate, a lost boot, whatever a boy can invent." A ghost of a smile crossed his face. "You're good at inventing."

"What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to think," Hazel said, "which is what I do before I do anything, and why I do so little." But he put his hand briefly on the back of Wynn's neck, warm through the wet wool, and Wynn felt the weight of it all the way home.

* * *

Supper that night was mackerel and potatoes and a silence with edges on it. Tom came home early — Wynn had done his work well, presenting a loose slate from the loft as a crisis requiring paternal ladders — and ate like a man performing the memory of appetite. Bess watched him over the rim of her teacup. Once, when Tom pushed his plate away half-finished, her cup stopped halfway to her mouth for the space of a breath, then completed its journey.

"The glass is lower than I've seen it since '87," Bess said, to the table generally. "Harbormaster sent round that the convoy's sheltering at Falmouth till it passes. Small mercies."

"Small mercies," Tom repeated, in a voice Wynn had never heard him use, and Wynn looked up sharply, because it had sounded almost like disappointment.

After supper Tom stood at the window a long while, watching the dark where the sea should be, and then he said, abruptly, "Mother. If a man does a thing because he's forced to do it — if his family's threatened, if there's no way out that doesn't drown everyone he loves — is the thing still his?"

Bess set down her sewing. The fire ticked. Outside, the wind found the chimney and played it like a pipe.

"A thing is what it is," she said at last, quietly. "But a man is what he does next."

Tom nodded slowly, as if weighing the answer against something, and picked up his coat, and went out into the rising night before anyone could stop him. Bess did not call after him. She sat looking at the closed door with an expression Wynn had never seen her wear — old, and tired, and braced, like a woman who had been waiting thirty years for a wave she could finally hear coming.

"Bed," she said to Wynn, without turning. "Kitchen pallet tonight. And Wynn—" Now she turned, and her eyes were bright and fierce. "Whatever happens in the next few days, remember whose son you are, and remember whose boy you've chosen to be besides. Both of those will hold you up, if you let them."

He didn't understand her. He understood only that the storm was coming from two directions at once — from the sea, where you could watch it build; and from somewhere inside his own house, where you couldn't.

Lying on the pallet by the banked fire, he listened to the gale arrive in earnest, the first slates lifting off barns down the lane, the boom of surf carrying up over the fields like distant cannon. Somewhere out in it, his father was walking toward the cliffs. Somewhere out in it, Hazel was climbing his tower. And far below the quarry path, in a cave that breathed, the long cases sat waiting with whatever was inside them, patient as the sea itself.

Near dawn, Wynn woke to the front door opening — his father, soaked, smelling of salt and smoke and something else, something like lamp oil — pausing in the kitchen doorway to look at them all sleeping, one by one, the way a man looks at a room he fears he's seeing for the last time.

And out beyond the point, through the roar, Wynn heard the storm change its mind and gather itself for the real blow — and knew, the way you know your own name, that this night coming was the night everything would break, all at once, upon the rocks.
