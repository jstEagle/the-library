## 13. The Night of False Light

The barometer had been falling all day like a stone dropped down a well, and by four in the afternoon it had settled at the bottom of its glass with a kind of grim finality, as though even the instrument had given up pretending the weather would be anything but catastrophic.

Hazel Thornbury wound the clockwork, filled the oil reservoirs to their brims, and trimmed every wick twice. These were the rituals that kept him upright on nights like this one—the small, countable tasks, each one following the last like footprints through snow. Check the lamp. Check the spare. Check the fog signal. Check the stores. He said them aloud sometimes, in Maren's cadence, because her voice had always made the list sound less like drudgery and more like a prayer.

*"Oil, glass, wick, nerve,"* she used to say, ticking items off on her fingers. *"A keeper needs four things, Haz, and only one of them is kept in the tower."*

He climbed the ninety-three steps with the sleet already beginning to rattle against the eastern panes, and by the time he reached the lantern room the wind had found its full voice—a long, rising howl that came in off the Atlantic and bent the sea flat white for half a mile before it broke against the cliffs.

It was going to be the worst of the season. Perhaps the worst in a generation, if old Tregose at the inn was to be believed, and Tregose was generally believed because he'd been wrong so rarely that being wrong once a decade kept everyone properly humble.

Hazel set the light turning and stood a moment watching the beam sweep out across the chaos—darkness, then a great fan of gold, then darkness again, faithful as breathing.

"Keep her honest," he told the lamp, as he always did. It was a foolish thing to say to a machine, and he knew it. But somewhere below him, past the farm and the harbor and the reef lying in wait like a pickpocket at a fair, other lights were burning tonight too. And after everything he had read in that letter, after everything Corvus had been asking about salvage rights and spring convoys, he no longer trusted any flame he couldn't see with his own eyes.

*Keep her honest,* he thought. *And God help me, let me find out who isn't.*

* * *

The storm came ashore proper just after seven, arriving not gradually but all at once, like an unwelcome guest shouldering through the door. The rain went from sleet to sheets. The wind rose through registers of sound—first a moan, then a roar, then something beyond sound entirely, a pressure that Hazel felt in his back teeth and behind his eyes.

At half past eight, Wynn Penhale appeared in the tower doorway soaked to the skin and grinning like the weather was a personal entertainment arranged for his benefit.

"You're mad," Hazel said, hauling him inside and shutting the door against a fistful of spray. "You're fourteen kinds of mad, boy."

"Fourteen's my number," Wynn said happily, dripping on the stairs. "Bess says I've got to fetch you down for supper but I think she mostly wanted me out from underfoot while she and Tom argue."

"They're arguing?"

Wynn shrugged, wringing out his cap. "Tom's been odd all day. Wouldn't eat his breakfast, wouldn't hardly look at anyone. Went out in this twice already and Bess near took the broom to him." The boy's grin faltered slightly. "Is something wrong, Hazel? There's a feeling in the house like when the dog knows thunder's coming."

Hazel looked at him—that open, salt-scoured face, entirely without guile—and felt the letter's weight as physically as if it were sewn into his coat lining.

"There's nothing wrong," he lied, gently. "Storms make everyone strange. Go home, dry off, mind your grandmother. If the wind takes a chimney, you'll want to be indoors to hear it fall."

Wynn laughed and went, and Hazel watched him pick his way down the cliff path until the darkness took him, and then stood a long moment with the wind trying its best to peel him off the gallery rail.

*A feeling in the house like when the dog knows thunder's coming.*

The boy missed nothing. That was the trouble. One day soon—if there was anything to miss at all—Wynn would stop missing things, and then what would Hazel say to him?

He went back inside. Ninety-three steps. Oil, glass, wick, nerve.

* * *

By ten the world had been reduced to noise and water. The sea came at the cliffs in hundredweight masses, hitting with a concussion he felt through the tower's stones, and between the gusts the rain fell sideways with a hiss like a thousand kettles. The light turned. The light turned again. Hazel kept to his post with tea gone cold beside him and the log book open on his knee, recording intervals, noting the barometer's continued collapse.

At seventeen minutes past eleven, he saw it.

He had been facing seaward, watching the beam sweep, when the flash caught the corner of his eye—to the *north*, where no light should be. Where no light *was*, unless someone had put one there. Gull Rock headland was dark country: cliffs, furze, a ruined watch-hut, and nothing else for two miles. No house. No harbor. No reason under heaven for a lamp.

But there it was. A light, steady, swinging in a slow deliberate arc—not a torch, not lightning, nothing natural. It swung, held, and swept again, laying its glow out over the water toward the channel where the coasting vessels ran on nights like this because nights like this were exactly when schedules forgave shortcuts.

Hazel's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"That's not navigation," he said aloud. His voice sounded very small. "That's invitation."

He seized the telescope, braced himself in the window frame, and fought the glass steady. The light swung again. Through the streaming lens he caught it—a hand-lamp, ship's pattern, mounted or held high, its beam masked on one side and thrown wide on the other. Someone up there on Gull Rock was painting a false channel onto the black water, telling some tired master *this way, safe water here, come along, come along—*

And out in the dark, right on cue, he heard it: the far-off cry of a lookout, thin as a gull's note, carried inland on the gale.

"No," Hazel breathed. "No, no—turn, man, *turn*—"

The coaster never had a chance. She came out of the murk with her canvas double-reefed and her bow pressed hard toward the shore, trusting the friendly light on the headland, and the reef reached up for her the way it had reached for ships since before either of their grandfathers was born. Even at three miles, through the telescope, Hazel heard the strike—a sound like the end of the world played on a single cracked piano note, felt rather than heard, a shudder in the bedrock.

Then she heeled. Then she broke her heart on the rocks.

Hazel was moving before his mind caught up with his body. Log book down, oilskins on, lifeline coiled over his shoulder, the box of rescue gear by the door that he checked monthly and prayed over weekly. He doused nothing—the tower light must keep turning; it might yet guide survivors off the reef's far side—but he took the stairs ninety-three at a time, which at his age was a decision his knees would invoice him for later, and burst out into the screaming dark with the cliff path a river under his boots.

* * *

The wreck lay on the reef's teeth a quarter mile offshore, already breaking apart, her stern a black splinter against the white fury of the surf. Her people—there were always people, eight or ten on a coaster of that size—were in the water or clinging to her bones.

Hazel worked the shoreline for what felt like a year and could not have been more than forty minutes. The gulley below the old fish stairs funneled the sea into a churning pit, but it also funneled things *out*: spars, casks, and once—a moment that stopped his heart—a man, face-down, rolling in the wash like a piece of driftwood.

Hazel went in after him on the lifeline, which was either courage or stupidity and was probably both, and the sea picked him up as though he weighed nothing and tried its level best to introduce him to the reef personally. He caught the man by the collar. He hauled. The line went taut. Somewhere above, the cliff anchors held, groaning but holding, and the two of them came up out of the surf together like something being born backwards.

The man coughed half the Atlantic onto the rocks and lived.

"One more," he rasped, gripping Hazel's sleeve with fingers like iron hooks. "Boy. My brother's boy. He was on deck—"

Hazel went back in.

He found the second one snagged in the kelp at the gulley's mouth, a lad of no more than nineteen, blue as a whiting and tangled in rope. Getting him loose cost Hazel skin he'd miss, and getting him up the cliff cost everything else, but at some hour deep in the smallest part of the night, Hazel knelt on the fish-stairs with two half-drowned strangers breathing raggedly beside him and a third figure lying still further along the rocks that no power on earth could now help—and he watched the wreck burn itself out on the reef, and he shook, and shook.

* * *

He got them to the tower. Built the stove hot, stripped them, wrapped them in every blanket he owned, forced brandy into the older man and broth into the boy, working with the automatic tenderness of long practice. The older man—Master Marner, coaster's mate out of Falmouth—talked in broken pieces between shivers.

"Following the lights. Two of 'em, we thought—yours on the point, and another north of it, and we reckoned—" He stopped, and his face did something terrible. "We reckoned the second was a harbor light. New, maybe. We'd have never—Captain knew these waters forty years. He'd have never."

"I know," Hazel said quietly.

"He's dead. Captain's dead. Boy on the wheel is dead." Marner stared at the stove. "Someone lit us in, keeper. Someone *lit us in*."

"Yes," Hazel said. "I know that too."

The boy slept. The mate drifted. Outside, the storm raged on with no interest whatsoever in human accounting, and Hazel climbed the tower steps one more time because the light needed tending, because oil, glass, wick, nerve, because the ritual was the rail he had to hold.

And standing in the lantern room at three in the morning, looking north across the boiling water toward Gull Rock, he saw it one last time.

The false light flared again—briefly, guiltily, a single sweep before it vanished altogether. But the wind had shifted, and for one clear second the beam came straight across the water toward him, and the telescope was somehow already in his hands, and the image leapt into focus with a cruelty only the sea can manage.

He recognized the lamp housing. That was bad enough—ship's pattern, brass, a familiar silhouette.

But it was the lens that undid him.

Maren had ground it herself, three years ago, in the workshop under the cliff—a spare for the storm lantern she never finished, cut with her own peculiar facets, seven flats instead of six because she'd insisted six was "what everyone does, Haz, and everyone is wrong." He had dusted that lens a hundred times. He had held it up to the window and watched her strange, lopsided, beautiful flare scatter across the workshop wall while she sketched equations in the margins of her journal.

That same unmistakable starburst hung in the air over Gull Rock, swinging on a rope or a pole in the hands of someone who had taken it from his workshop, from *her* workbench, and used it to call forty years of a captain's seamanship onto the rocks.

The telescope lowered slowly. Hazel stood in the light he kept honest, and felt the floor of his whole world tilt.

The spare lens was kept locked. Only three keys existed. His own hung at his belt. Maren's had gone into the sea with her, per her instructions, to her evident satisfaction and the harbor court's considerable confusion.

Which meant that tonight, on Gull Rock, in the worst storm in a generation, someone had carried a light that could only have come from the Penhales' keeping.

* * *

Down in the tower's warm room, the young sailor stirred and mumbled, and Master Marner sat bolt upright with the particular terror of a man who has remembered something important.

"Keeper," Marner said hoarsely. "There's a thing you should know. Before the strike—when we were still making our approach—the captain sent me forward. Said he'd seen that second light swing *from the landward side*. Said it hadn't come round the head, it had come up the coast path." His eyes found Hazel's. "Whoever held that lamp walked to Gull Rock, friend. In this. On purpose."

Hazel poured the man more brandy with a hand that did not shake, because thirty years of discipline is good for exactly that—one unshaking hand, when everything beneath the wrist has come apart entirely.

"Drink your brandy," he said. "You're safe now."

Safe. He looked at the fire, at the boy sleeping, at the rain hammering the little window that faced, beyond the darkness, toward the Penhale farm—toward Bess's kitchen where a lamp still burned late, toward Tom's empty chair, toward the room where Wynn lay asleep believing the world was a place where heroes kept the lights and villains were found in storybooks.

Toward the workshop under the cliff, where he would go at first light, and break his own lock if he had to, and learn exactly what had been taken and when.

The storm screamed. The light turned. And Hazel Thornbury sat down slowly among the blankets and the brandy and the breathing strangers, and understood that whatever he had been carrying since the letter washed ashore, it had only been the shadow of the thing.

Now he knew its shape.

Now he knew whose hands had held Maren's lens against the night sky—and whose son those hands belonged to.
