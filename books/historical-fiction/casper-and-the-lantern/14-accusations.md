## 14. Accusations

The harbormaster arrived at half past nine in the morning, which Hazel thought was a coward's hour. Men who came to deliver trouble after breakfast were men who wanted witnesses.

His name was Tregose, and he wore his authority the way some men wore a wet coat — deliberately, so everyone could see how it chafed him too. He had come up from Falmouth on the mail coach and then walked the last two miles from the village because the cliff road had gone to soup under three days of rain. His boots announced him to the whole of the Penhale farmyard: squelch, squelch, squelch, like the tide coming in with an opinion.

Hazel was in the yard, mucking out the byre for Bess because his own hands needed occupying or they would find the letter-shaped hole in his pocket again. He straightened, shovel in hand, and watched Tregose take in the farm with a slow turn of his head — the whitewashed walls, the leaning barn, the hens conducting their eternal committee meeting by the gate.

"Keeper Thornbury," Tregose said. "You'll know why I've come."

"I'll know why you think you've come," Hazel said. "Whether it's the same thing remains to be seen."

Tregose almost smiled. They had met twice before, both times over paperwork, both times agreeing that the sea was the true harbormaster and they merely kept her books. Now the man's face was shut like a ledger at year's end.

"A coaster went on the reef two nights back," Tregose said. "The *Merry Alice*, out of Bristol. Six souls aboard, four saved. And before she struck, someone showed a light from the wrong headland."

"The wrong—"

"From Penhale Point, Keeper. Not your tower. Not the church beacon. From ground that belongs to this farm."

The shovel suddenly weighed a great deal. Hazel set it against the wall with care, as though it might otherwise fall and say something incriminating on his behalf.

* * *

Bess Penhale met them in her kitchen, and Bess met trouble the way she met everything — with the kettle already on.

She was seventy-one years old and had buried a husband, two daughters-in-law, and every illusion she'd ever owned about the gentleness of the sea, and none of it had succeeded in making her small. She filled the kitchen doorway the way the cliff filled the horizon: permanently, and with weather behind her.

"Sit," she told Tregose, and he sat, because a man would have to be made of driftwood not to.

He laid it out plainly, which Hazel grudgingly respected. The *Merry Alice*. A light shown from the point at half past eleven, low and steady, then doused. The wreck. The survivors' testimony — a light where no light should be, moving like a lamp carried by a man walking.

"False lights," Tregose said. "Wrecking. I know it sounds like our grandfathers' stories, but there it is. Somebody on this coast lured a ship onto Casper's Reef, and somebody profited when she broke." He paused, delicately as a man stepping into a bog. "The nearest dwellings are this farm and the lighthouse."

"My keeper doesn't wreck ships," Bess said. "He saves them. It's his whole character. Ask anyone."

"And your family?"

The kettle screamed. Bess rose, took it off the heat with unhurried hands, and poured tea for four as if she hadn't heard. Then she sat down, folded her hands, and gave Tregose the full force of a gaze that had stared down customs men, tax collectors, and one memorable bull.

"Mr. Tregose, my family has fed shipwrecked sailors off these rocks for sixty years without charging so much as a shilling. My husband drowned pulling men out of the surf. You'll find no wrecker at my table."

"You have sons?"

"One. Tom. He's at market in Pentowan today, if you want to waste a walk."

Hazel watched her say it. Tom's cart was in the barn; he'd left at dawn and come back within the hour, gray-faced, saying the roads were flooded. It was the first lie Hazel had ever heard from Bess Penhale, and it went through him like a splinter entering skin — small, clean, and destined to fester.

Tregose looked at her for a long moment. Then he drank his tea.

"I'll need to speak with everyone eventually, Mrs. Penhale. Neighbors, hands, family. Routine." He set down his cup. "But understand me — the Commissioners will send their own men if I don't give them answers first. I'd rather it was me asking the questions. I've known this coast since I had teeth. They haven't."

"Then ask," said Bess. "And while you're asking, ask who benefits. Because it won't be us. Wrecked ships wash their cargo onto whoever's got the strongest horse and the fewest scruples, and we've neither."

Hazel said nothing at all. Across the table, Bess's eyes touched his once — briefly, warningly, the way a hand touches another hand under a table during grace — and moved on.

* * *

Tom came home at dusk, properly this time, riding up from the village with fish smell generously applied to cover any other smells a man might wish covered. Hazel intercepted him at the gate.

"They were here since morning," Hazel said quietly. "Tregose. Asking after false lights."

Tom's face did something complicated. It tried for blankness, achieved confusion, and settled somewhere in the neighborhood of guilt wearing confusion's borrowed coat.

"Wrecking," Tom repeated. "On *our* point? That's mad. Who'd—" He stopped himself, and Hazel watched the sentence die behind his teeth.

"Who'd what?" Hazel asked softly.

"Who'd dare," Tom finished. "With you watching. That's all."

For a moment they stood in the cold, breath steaming between them, two men who had shared suppers and sorrows and the mending of nets for two years, and the silence between them had never been so wide. Tom couldn't look at him. He studied the gatepost, the mud, the middle distance, anywhere but Hazel's face, and each avoided glance was its own small confession.

"There's talk the Commissioners will come," Hazel said. "Talk of arrests."

"Let them talk. We've done nothing." Tom's voice cracked on *nothing* like a green branch underfoot. "Good night, Hazel."

He went into the house. Hazel stayed at the gate a long while, listening to the sea work at the reef below, patient as a jeweler.

* * *

Wynn had a genius for hearing things he wasn't meant to hear, which he regarded as a natural talent, like his mother's singing voice before the fever took her, and which Bess regarded as a nuisance requiring management.

He was fourteen, all elbows and questions, and he had spent the entire day of Tregose's visit in a state of suppressed ecstasy, because nothing interesting ever happened in January and now the law itself had walked up the cliff path. He positioned himself in the scullery with a bucket of water he had no intention of carrying anywhere, and listened through the door.

"—witness saw the light move along the headland, south to north—"

"That path is used by shepherds. By walkers. By any soul checking stock in a storm."

"By someone with reason to be out at half past eleven in the worst weather since 'sixty-eight."

"Half the county has reason to be out in weather like that. It's called living on a cliff."

Wynn grinned in the dark. Gran was giving him what she called *the full rigging* — her best storm-voice, reserved for tax men, vets, and the vicar whenever he suggested the Sunday service might run shorter.

But then the voices changed, and the grin drained out of him.

"—your son's debts, Mrs. Penhale. There's talk in Pentowan. Talk that Tom owes money in certain quarters. Talk that a man deep in debt can be bought cheap."

A silence. Wynn held his breath until his chest hurt.

"My son's debts are his own affairs, paid from his own pocket, and if you believe every word blown in from a tavern then you'll believe the moon's made of pilchards." A pause. "Now. You'll be wanting supper before the walk back, or you'll be wanting to leave my kitchen, and only one of those comes with bread."

When Tregose finally squelched away down the lane, Wynn stood frozen in the scullery, the bucket forgotten, cold seeping through his boots into his bones.

*Debts.* Papa owed money?

He thought of his father's long silences at table lately. The way he'd taken to going out at odd hours and coming back with excuses that didn't fit together, like planks cut for different boats. The way he wouldn't meet anyone's eyes — not Gran's, not Hazel's, not even Wynn's own, and especially not Wynn's, which hurt worst of all because Wynn had always believed that between him and his father there was nothing that needed hiding.

He thought, too — shamefully, instantly — of the way Hazel had been watching Papa. Not unkindly. Watchfully. Like a man counting waves and waiting for one that's too tall.

Wynn set down the bucket. He did not go inside. He went instead around the back of the house, over the wall, and up toward the cliffs, because there was one person in the world he trusted absolutely, and that person lived in a tower with a light in it, and towers were where you went when the ground stopped being solid.

He found Hazel on the gallery, halfway up the outside of the tower, oiling the railing bolts against the salt. Hazel glanced down and something in his face shifted — softened — and for one moment Wynn almost told him everything right there. Almost.

"Hazel," he began. "If a person knew something. Something bad. About someone they—" His voice betrayed him and cracked down the middle. "About family."

Hazel climbed down slowly, wiped his hands, and looked at Wynn with those gray eyes that always seemed to be seeing slightly more than was shown to them. And Wynn realized, with a lurch, that whatever he said next, he might break something that could never be glued back.

"Then that person should tell me," Hazel said gently. "Because I promise you, Wynn — whatever it is, it's already something I've been carrying alone, and two people carrying it is half the weight."

That was exactly the problem. That was the whole entire problem, laid out neat as Sunday linen, and Wynn couldn't do it. He couldn't be the one whose mouth broke his father.

"I have to check the goat," Wynn said, with the desperate logic of the fourteen-year-old, and fled.

He ran. Down the cliff path, past the workshop with its dust-sheeted windows, along the top field where the wind came off the water hard enough to shove a boy sideways. Rain caught him halfway, fine and driving, and he let it. Better rain than the kitchen. Better rain than Tregose's questions. Better rain than the picture that would not stop forming in his head: his father, on the point, in the dark, holding a lamp.

"No," Wynn said aloud, to the wind. "No, that's not — he wouldn't—"

But debts. But the eyes. But the lying about market day, which Wynn had heard too, through the scullery wall, and understood now for exactly what it was.

Behind him, up the slope, a lantern kindled in the lighthouse gallery — steady, white, faithful. Below him, far out, the reef growled over the bones of the *Merry Alice*. Between the two lights, somewhere, his family was cracking like ice in spring, and he was running away from the sound of it.

He ran until the path forked, and there he stopped, gasping, soaked through, rain running off his chin.

One path led home. One led down toward the caves.

Wynn stood in the rain between them for a long time.

Then he turned, and started down toward the water.
