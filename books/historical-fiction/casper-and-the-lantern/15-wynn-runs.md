## 15. Wynn Runs

Wynn had decided, somewhere between the second slice of bread and the sound of his mother's door not opening all morning, that he was done being fourteen and useless.

The house had a feeling to it he couldn't name — like the air before thunder, only indoors. His grandmother Bess moved through the kitchen with her jaw set the way it did when she was kneading dough against an enemy. His father Tom hadn't come down at all. And Hazel — Hazel had looked at him last night in the yard like Wynn was made of glass, which was ridiculous, because if anyone was glass it was Hazel himself, all thin shining surfaces with something cracked inside.

So Wynn did what any sensible boy would do with a house full of secrets: he took his coat, stuffed two oatcakes in his pocket, and went out to find out things.

He told himself it was about the light. It was always about the light, lately. Someone had shown a lamp from the wrong headland three nights past and a coaster had gone onto the reef, and the whole village now walked around looking at each other as though the sea itself might be questioned. The harbormaster had come. Men had stood in the Penhale kitchen saying *Penhale* the way you'd say *damp* or *rot*. Wynn had heard every word of it from behind the pantry door, because nobody ever remembers a boy is small enough to fit behind a pantry door.

And then he'd run into the rain, and everyone had shouted after him, and he hadn't stopped.

But this morning the rain had gone off to bother someone else, and the sky hung low and grey as dishwater, and Wynn meant to walk the cliffs until something made sense.

* * *

The trouble with walking cliffs until something makes sense is that the cliffs don't talk. They just stand there being old. Wynn walked the whole southern stretch, past the gorse and the shags drying their wings like laundry, past the spot where he'd nearly gone over the edge last month and Hazel had caught him by the collar and held on so hard his knuckles went white. He thought about that grip for a while. It had frightened him a little, how hard Hazel had held on. Frightened him in the way that made him feel warm, which is a confusing thing to be frightened by.

By midday he'd come round to the northern shore without quite deciding to, which was how he found himself above the sea caves.

Everyone knew the caves. Children were warned off them the way children are warned off everything worth doing. There were three of them, mouths cut into the black rock, filling and emptying with the tide like the coast was breathing through them. Wynn had played in the smallest one a hundred times.

It was the middle one that had a light in it today.

Just a glimmer, deep back where the daylight gave up. A lantern, moving. Voices under it — low ones, men's voices, the kind that don't want carrying.

Wynn did what his legs decided for him, which was to get down flat behind the rocks at the cave's rim and slither forward like an eel. His grandmother would have skinned him alive. His father would have gone white as sailcloth. Hazel would have held onto him again, hard enough to hurt, and Wynn found, lying there on cold stone with salt spray in his ears, that he wanted very badly to be held onto right now and didn't know why.

He could see them now. Three men, strangers, working by a shuttered lantern — and one of them was Mr. Corvus himself, the smooth gentleman from the inn, except he wasn't smooth out here. Out here he moved quick and sharp, like a man doing sums he didn't like.

They were loading a handcart. Signal lamps — Wynn knew signal lamps the way other boys knew marbles; he'd grown up in Maren's workshop shadows hearing Hazel explain prisms at supper. These were wrong somehow, mismatched, their glass smoked and filed. Rope. Oil in sealed tins. And wrapped in sacking, handled like a baby, a lens assembly that made Wynn's stomach do something strange, because he'd seen its twin sitting on Hazel's workbench under dust sheets for two years.

"Move it all," Corvus was saying. "Tonight, before the equinox gales make liars of us. The cutter's been sniffing the shipping lanes and that keeper's got eyes like a gull."

"The workshop?" one of the men asked. "You said—"

"Workshop burns with the rest. Shame. Good stone." Corvus shrugged, and the shrug was worse than anything else he'd said. "But the lens goes with us or doesn't exist, and neither does anyone who can say they saw it."

"And the Penhale lot?"

Here Corvus smiled, and Wynn felt the smile go through him like a fishhook.

"Tom Penhale was ours the moment he signed for the debt. If it comes to questions, Tom held the false light. Tom lured the coaster. Tom's name will be on everything we leave behind, and the harbormaster will hang him with our compliments." He picked up the lens, turned it so the light slid across it. "A family of wreckers. It's almost tidy. They'll believe it of the son of a man who drowned pulling people off the rocks — people say grief runs in blood."

Wynn's whole body went hot and then cold and then hot again. His father. His stupid, kind, hopeless father, who mended fences badly and laughed too loud and had been missing meals and meeting his own eyes in reflections the way guilty men do.

Tom had held the lamp. Tom had put the light on the wrong headland and the coaster had broken on the reef and men had drowned, and these men in the cave meant to see him hanged for it — meant to *make sure* he hanged for it, tidy as arithmetic.

And then burn the workshop. Burn Hazel's workshop. Burn everything Maren had left in the world.

Wynn lay there shaking, and part of him — the sensible part, the part that sounded like Bess — said *stay still, stay hidden, wait them out.* But the larger part of him was already running, in his mind, up the cliff path, and his body followed it before his mind caught up. He scrambled back from the rim. A stone shifted under his knee. It went over the edge and clattered down into the cave mouth like a gunshot.

The voices stopped.

"*What was that?*"

Wynn ran.

* * *

He ran the way he hadn't run since the day of the near-fall, except this time nothing chased him but knowing. The cliff path blurred under his boots. Gorse tore at his sleeves. Wind came off the sea sideways and shoved him and he shoved back. Somewhere behind him, faint and getting fainter, a man shouted — but the wind ate it, and Wynn ran faster than shouting.

His head was full of pieces, all trying to assemble themselves: *the letter* — because he'd pieced that much together, the oilskin packet from the tideline, the way Hazel had flinched whenever anyone mentioned the harbor court; *the fever* — no, that wasn't yet, that was coming; *the lens* — the duplicate lens, Maren's design copied by thieves, which meant Maren had known, Maren had found them first, and that was why her journal had a torn page, and oh, Hazel didn't know, Hazel was grieving a wife who'd died fighting the same fight—

He had to tell Hazel. That was the whole of it, the entire plan, elegant as anything: tell Hazel. Hazel would know what to do. Hazel always knew what to do, even when he pretended not to know what to feel.

The rain came back while he crossed the top field, sudden and needling, and the temperature dropped like something falling off a table. Wynn noticed distantly that he'd stopped sweating. Noticed distantly that his teeth wouldn't quit chattering even when he clenched his jaw. Noticed distantly that his legs felt like they belonged to somebody taller, somebody whose knees worked properly.

The tower showed ahead, grey-white against grey sky, its lamp turning its patient circle. Home of the only person who could hold all of this. Two hundred yards. A hundred.

His feet tangled in nothing at all.

He went down hard on the wet grass, and the world tilted, and when he pushed himself up the tower had doubled — two towers, swaying gently toward each other like conspirators. He laughed at that. He heard himself laugh and it didn't sound like laughing; it sounded like something with a fever in it.

"Get up," he told himself, out loud, in his grandmother's voice. "Get *up*, Wynn Penhale."

He got up. He walked, then ran, then walked, the ground rising and dipping like the deck of a boat. The rain had soaked through his coat. His thoughts came apart into bright unconnected fragments — *Tom's name will be on everything* — his father's face at breakfast, hollowed out, not meeting anyone's eyes — *workshop burns with the rest* — Hazel's hands, careful as surgery among glass and brass — *grief runs in blood* —

The tower was ten yards away. Then five. Then it swung away entirely and came back and hit him in the shoulder, and he realized his hand had been reaching for the door and had found stone instead.

He got the door open. Stairs. He knew stairs. Stairs went up to Hazel, and Hazel was the answer to every question, so he climbed, one step and another, and each step cost more than the last, and halfway up the spiral the stairs politely rotated ninety degrees without asking and Wynn sat down on one to consider this development.

Just for a moment. Just to catch his breath.

The lamp turned above him. He could hear it — that soft mechanical breathing the clockwork made, tick and sweep, tick and sweep, the sound he loved best in the world after his grandmother's humming. He put his head against the cold stone wall.

"Hazel," he said. It came out very small. "Hazel, I've got — I've got things to tell you."

The words were heavy. All of them. Every word he carried was a stone from the cave, and he'd been carrying them up a cliff, and now he set them down just for a moment, just to rest, and the moment took him the way the tide takes a footprint.

* * *

Hazel Thornbury found the door standing open at dusk with the wind moaning through it, and swore with a fluency he usually reserved for rusted fittings, and climbed.

He found Wynn two turns from the lamp room, curled on the stairs like something the sea had tried and failed to return, soaked to the skin and burning with a heat Hazel could feel through his coat sleeve before he even touched him.

"Wynn." He shook the boy's shoulder. "Wynn, look at me."

Wynn's eyes opened — glazed, unfocused, and underneath the fever, desperate. His hand came up and fisted in Hazel's collar with a strength that had no business in it.

"They're going to hang my da," he said. Each word cost him visibly, dragged up from somewhere deep. "Corvus — the cave — they've got her lens, Hazel, they've got *Maren's* lens, and the page, the page from the journal, and they're going to burn the workshop tonight — they said Tom's name would be on everything — you have to — I brought it, I brought all of it, I ran the whole way—"

"Wynn—"

"I'm sorry I sat down." The boy's voice cracked, and his eyes were swimming. "I only meant to rest. Tell Gran — don't tell Gran the hanging bit yet. Or — no. Tell her. She'll want to fight." His grip loosened. His head sagged. "You always hold on hard enough. Hold on."

And then he was gone — not dead, thank God and the sea and every saint of sailors, but away, sunk down into the fever like a stone into deep water, breath rasping, skin alight.

Hazel gathered him up — all arms and knees, heavier than a boy should be, heavier the way fear is heavy — and carried him down the stairs he'd fought his way up, and out into the wind, and toward the farmhouse lights below, holding on exactly as hard as he was asked.

Behind them, far along the darkening coast, the tide was coming into a certain cave, and in the cave, men were working fast.

The sun went down on the last day but one before the equinox gales, and somewhere over the water, the storm that would end the century was already gathering itself to blow.
