## 16. Fever Watch

The fever came in with the tide, the way trouble always did on this coast.

Hazel had carried Wynn up from the cliff path himself, the boy limp across his arms like a bundle of wet sailcloth. One moment the lad had been running toward him through the rain, mouth open around words that never made it out; the next his knees had folded and the world had done the rest. Hazel had caught him — it was getting to be a habit, catching Penhales — and felt the heat of him even through soaked wool.

Now Wynn lay in the narrow bed under the eaves, the one that had been his mother's when she was a girl, and burned like a wick trimmed too short.

"He's talking," Bess said from the doorway. She had a basin in her hands and forty years of fevers behind her eyes, and neither was steady.

"I know."

"What's he saying?"

Hazel listened. The boy's lips were moving, quick and urgent, the words tumbling out in fragments that broke apart as soon as they surfaced.

*"The cave... they're moving it, all of it... they said his name, they said Tom's name..."*

Bess set the basin down very slowly, as though it might go off.

"Water," she said. "He needs water, and cool cloths, and we'll have Dr. Pengelly out at first light if the roads let him come." Her voice was brisk and sensible and did not tremble, which told Hazel precisely how hard she was working at it.

"Bess—"

"Water first," she said, and went downstairs like a woman with somewhere to be.

Hazel sat back down on the stool beside the bed. He had kept vigil before — for Maren, three nights at the end, her hand going quieter in his by degrees until it wasn't a hand anymore but something the sea had given back. He knew the geography of these hours: the long flat dark between two and four, the way candle stubs burn faster than they ought, the particular cruelty of a fever that lets a patient surface just long enough to recognize you before pulling them under again.

Wynn's head thrashed on the pillow. His hair was drying in cowlicks that would have made Hazel smile on any other night.

*"I heard them,"* the boy whispered. *"I heard them, Mr. Thornbury, I ran, I ran to tell you—"*

"You told me," Hazel said softly. "You got there. Rest now."

But of course the boy hadn't gotten there. Not in words. He'd collapsed fifty yards from the lighthouse door, and what he'd shouted into the wind had been scattered syllables — *cave*, and *moving*, and a name that might have been Tom's or might have been the rain. Hazel had spent the walk up the hill telling himself he'd imagined the name. He was still telling himself that now, though he suspected the effort was wasted. Some truths came in through love's back door whether you opened it or not.

* * *

Bess returned with water, honey, a strip of linen, and a bottle of the elderflower cordial she made each August and guarded like state secrets. She moved about the room with the efficiency of a general, wringing cloths, bathing the boy's face, propping him up to drink in small measures while he fought her and murmured and finally swallowed.

They worked without speaking. It struck Hazel, somewhere in the second hour, how naturally their hands fell into rhythm — hers lifting the cup, his steadying the shoulders; his folding the cloth, hers taking it warm and wringing it cold again. Two years of Sundays and borrowed tools and meals eaten side by side, and somewhere in all of it they had become a thing that worked in tandem, though neither had ever said so aloud.

"He's hot," Bess said, for the fourth time.

"Fever's doing its work."

"Don't tell me what fevers do, Hazel Thornbury. I raised three children in this house and buried one of them, and I'll thank you not to lecture me on the habits of illness." She dabbed Wynn's brow with unnecessary firmness. "I'm stating a fact. He's hot."

"He's hot," Hazel agreed peaceably.

She glanced at him then — one sharp, quick look, there and gone, the kind of look she probably thought he hadn't catalogued. It was the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked. Tom was up, walking his kitchen like a man pacing a cell. He had been at it since midnight. Every quarter hour or so the boards spoke beneath them, and every time, Bess's jaw tightened by a degree, and every time, her hands stayed gentle on her grandson's skin.

*"The lantern,"* Wynn said suddenly, loud enough that both of them flinched. His eyes were open but unfocused, fixed on some middle distance only he could see. *"It's your wife's, isn't it? They took the page out of her book. There's a man, he laughs like a gull, he says the keeper won't finish it, he says the keeper's half-dead already—"*

"Hush, love." Bess gathered him close, rocking, and over the boy's damp head her eyes found Hazel's and held them.

There it was, then. Laid out plain between them on a feverish boy's pillow: the cave, the page, the man who laughed like a gull. And underneath it, unspoken but no less present, the shape of the thing they were both declining to say — *Tom*.

"He talks such rubbish when he burns," Bess said quietly. "Always has. When he was small he swore blind there were horses living in the chimney."

"Yes," Hazel said.

"He once told me the moon followed him home from Port Isaac and wanted its dinner."

"Yes."

Neither of them believed a word of the rubbish. Neither of them said so. Bess tucked the blanket higher and turned back to her task, and Hazel looked at the candle, because looking at her was more than either of them could afford just now.

* * *

Toward three o'clock the fever crested, and it was bad.

Wynn arched and cried out — not words now, just sound, the raw animal complaint of a body fighting itself — and Bess held him and sang. It was a Cornish lullaby, low and old, the kind of song that had been sung in that valley since before there were charts of the coast, and Hazel sat very still and let it do its work on him too. He had forgotten, or had pretended to forget, what it was to be inside a family's fear. Fear shared is a different animal from fear alone. It has somewhere to sit.

At half past three, footsteps on the stairs. Tom filled the doorway.

He looked terrible. That was the first thing — grey-faced, hollow-eyed, three days of guilt worn openly like a rash. He looked at his son, and his face did something complicated, and then he looked at his mother.

"Mam. Might I have a word."

"Not now."

"Mam." Something in his voice made her go still. "It wants saying."

The room held its breath. Even the storm seemed to lean closer at the window.

Bess rose from the bed with the unhurried care of a woman setting down something breakable. She crossed to her son, and Tom bent — he had to bend, he was a head taller than her and always had been — and put his mouth near her ear.

Hazel looked at the window. He watched the rain make rivers of the lamplight on the glass. But a confession carries, in a small room, in a quiet house; it travels along the beams the way a whisper travels along a hull. He caught pieces. He could not help catching pieces. *Debt.* *Coerced*. A word that sounded like *never lit it*, and another that sounded like *broke*, and last of all, barely above the rain: *don't let the boy hear it wrong.*

When Hazel made himself look back, Bess was standing exactly where she had been, facing her son, and her face —

Her face was the worst thing he had seen since Maren's bedside. Not shock. Shock is quick and passes. This was slower: the whole architecture of her understanding rearranging itself, beam by beam, while she stood perfectly still so nobody would notice the building falling. Grief and fury and a ferocious, ruinous love, all of them arriving at once through the back door, exactly as the truth does.

Tom reached for her.

She let him take her hand. Then she drew it back, gently, and laid it instead on his cheek, and held his face the way she had held Wynn's all night, and said nothing at all.

Tom nodded, once, like a man accepting a sentence. He looked past her at the bed — at his son, flushed and muttering in the shallow water of sleep — and his mouth worked, and then he turned and went back down the stairs, and the kitchen boards began their slow pacing again.

Bess stood a moment longer. Then she came back to the bed, sat down, picked up the cloth, and wrung it out.

"Well," she said, to no one in particular. "Elderflower?"

"...Please," Hazel said.

She poured two cups. Her hand shook, just slightly, and she watched it shake with a kind of detached interest, as if it belonged to somebody else and was behaving rather badly. Then it steadied, and she handed him his cup, and they drank scalding sweet cordial at three in the morning like it was the most natural thing in the world.

"You knew," she said at last. It wasn't an accusation. It was inventory.

"I found things. I didn't know what they were, not all of it. Not till tonight."

"And now?"

"Now I know a boy saved us," Hazel said, "and doesn't know he did."

Bess looked at Wynn for a long moment. The fever had broken — Hazel had felt the shift in it a quarter hour past, the heat banking like coals settling — and the boy lay quiet now, breathing in long, even pulls, one fist curled against his chin in a way that made him nine years old again instead of fourteen.

"My husband drowned reaching for strangers in the water," Bess said. "Twenty-six years I've told that story like it was a comfort. It isn't. It's an instruction. We are Penhales. We go into the water." She set her cup down. "My son forgot the instruction, and my grandson remembered it, and here we are."

"Bess—"

"Come morning," she said, in the voice she used for weather and other unavoidable things, "you will do whatever the truth requires of you. I know you, Hazel Thornbury. You've been chewing on that letter for a fortnight and it shows in your face like a bruise. Do what it requires. But you'll do it knowing this house stands behind you — and knowing that my Tom walked into that debt with his eyes open and walked out of the worst night of it with his nerve broken, and there's a difference between a wicked man and a frightened one, whatever the harbormaster's court thinks."

"There is," Hazel said. "I've been both."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, but its cousin. "Yes. Well. So have most of us." She reached over and straightened his collar, briskly, the way she'd done a hundred times at Sunday dinner, and the smallness of the gesture after everything nearly undid him entirely.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old pewter. The storm had spent itself in the night; what remained was a thin, persistent drizzle and a sea gone strangely calm, like a man whistling after an argument.

Wynn slept truly now, deep and even, the fever gone down to embers. Twice he stirred, and twice he found Hazel's eyes with his own — clear ones this time — and the second time he whispered, hoarsely:

"I got there."

"You got there," Hazel said. "You slept the rest of the way."

The boy's mouth curved. "Knew it," he murmured, and was gone again, and Bess pulled the blanket up over his shoulder with a tenderness that would have embarrassed them all had anyone remarked upon it.

Hazel rose stiffly. Every joint he owned filed a complaint; he overruled them. At the window he looked out over the white morning — the cliffs dripping, the reef bared and glistening at low tide like the spine of something enormous, and beyond it the gray line where the convoy would come when spring came, and Corvus with it, unless—

Unless.

In his coat pocket, downstairs, lay the notes he had made and abandoned. In the workshop under the cliff, under its dust sheet, Maren's lantern waited with its unsolved heart. And somewhere in the wreckage of the night, among the elderflower and the confessions and the boy's ragged breathing, a thought had assembled itself that he had not yet dared to look at directly: that the torn page and the finished lens and the court at the harbor were all one question, and that Maren had left him the answer, and that he had simply been too long in learning how to read her handwriting.

Behind him, Bess spoke without turning from the bed.

"Go home, Hazel."

He looked at her.

"Go home," she said again, gently, "and finish her work. Whatever it costs."

He went down the stairs past the kitchen where Tom sat with his head in his hands, and did not stop, and did not judge, and stepped out into the washed silver morning with the tide turning below him and the whole day leaning toward the workshop on the cliff.

The dust sheet, when he drew it back, was colder than he expected.

And pinned beneath the journal on the bench — a page he had searched for twice and mourned once — lay the missing leaf, folded neat as a church letter, with a sprig of dried thrift laid across it like a seal.

Someone had been in his workshop.
