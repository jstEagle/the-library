## 17. Tom's Reckoning

I had rehearsed it a hundred times on the walk over. In the bathhouse mirror that morning, shaving with a hand that would not hold steady, I had opened my mouth and said the words to my own reflection: *It was me, Hazel. It was me.* Four words. A child could carry them. But a child does not have to say them to the man whose wife is dead, whose boy is fevered, whose light has kept us all honest for two years while I went about with rot in my chest pretending it was only worry about the price of barley.

I got as far as his door and then stood there like a fool with my knuckles an inch from the wood, listening to the sea throw itself against the cliffs below as if it had some personal grievance against the whole of Cornwall.

He opened it before I knocked. He always did that. Said once that a keeper learns to hear footsteps through weather, and I'd laughed then, two years ago, back when laughter between us cost nothing.

"Tom," he said. Not cold. That was the worst of it — not cold at all. He stepped back and let me in, and the warmth of the stove hit my face, and I thought: *this is the last room where anyone will look at me kindly, and I am about to burn it down.*

* * *

The kitchen was small and smelled of tar and tea and, faintly, of lamp oil — Hazel's smell, the smell I'd come to think of as safety these past two years. Wynn's boots sat drying by the fire, too big for him yet, laces trailing like something drowned. The boy's fever had broken in the night; he slept upstairs under Bess's ear, and it was for him I had come, though I could not yet say so.

Hazel poured tea without asking. Set the cup before me. Sat down across the table and waited, which was the cruellest kindness he could have done, because waiting gave me room to run, and I found — God help me — I did not want to run anymore.

"You were at the cave," he said quietly.

"I heard what Wynn heard." My voice came out wrong, half-strangled. "Bess told me. She told me everything the boy said before the fever took his tongue."

"And?"

"And you're sitting there pouring tea for the man who killed four sailors on your reef."

The cup stopped halfway to his mouth. He set it down again with exaggerated care, the way he handled glass, and I understood that I had finally said a thing heavy enough to slow his hands.

"The coaster wasn't yours to claim," he said. "Not yet. Tell it properly."

* * *

So I told it properly. Or as properly as a man can tell a thing he has spent two years teaching himself not to know.

"It started with Father's boat debt," I said. "You remember the *Merrow*. You helped recaulk her the spring after—" After Maren. I still couldn't say her name in front of him without feeling like a thief. "After. The spring after. She needed a new keel timber and I hadn't the money, and the bank in Falmouth had stopped being patient, and there was a man at the Ship Inn buying rounds and listening to every hard-luck story in the parish. Corvus. Though I didn't know that name then. Then he was just Mr. Vellander, a salvage broker out of Falmouth, very civil, very generous."

Hazel's face did nothing at all. I pressed on, because stopping would have been drowning.

"He lent me sixty pounds against the boat. No paper, he said. Handshake between gentlemen. And then the paper appeared anyway, and the interest appeared, and by eighteen months on I owed him near a hundred and forty, and he came round one evening — this would be October past — and he said a man in my position ought to think about salvage rights along this coast. Free salvage. Wreckage washing up belongs to whoever finds it, that's the law's kindness. Only wreckage doesn't wash up often enough to suit a man with debts, he said. Sometimes the sea needs pointing."

I looked at my hands. They were my father's hands, broad and cracked, hands that had hauled men out of surf. They looked like strangers'.

"He wanted a light shown from the wrong headland," I said. "Not yours. Yours is honest — he knew yours was honest, that's why he never came near you, why he courted me instead. There's a shelf of rock past Gull Point where a lamp shows at exactly the bearing a master would read as the harbor light in bad visibility. A lantern carried up there on a dark night, held steady for ten minutes, and any coaster running before a gale would stand in for harbor and take the reef instead. He had charts. He had the timings of the spring convoy. He'd done it before, Tom, he told me — he *told* me, laughing, in the inn, with his hand on my shoulder — off Dover, off Whitby. Men who'd never once been wet."

"And you agreed."

"No." And here was the part I had to get right, because Bess would forgive me anything but a lie dressed as truth, and perhaps Hazel would too, though I had no right to expect it. "No, I didn't agree. I laughed at him. Told him to take his chart and his manners back to Falmouth. And I went home and I didn't sleep, and three days later the *Merrow*'s mooring was cut and she went onto the rocks at Marazion with the tide going out, and Corvus sent a boy round with the insurance valuation and a note that said *we can do this slowly or we can do it once.*

"You understand what that boat was. It was Father's last thing. It was the thing Wynn was to inherit. I'd promised — I'd stood on this very cliff and pointed at her and told my son, *that's yours, lad, when your legs are long enough.* And this man owned it now, owned it with a cut rope and a piece of paper, and there was nothing in law I could reach. So when he came back in December and said, one night, one lamp, ten minutes, and your boat walks free and your debt burns —"

I stopped. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere above us Wynn turned in his sleep and the floorboard creaked, and both of us looked up at the ceiling like men caught.

"So I agreed," I finished. "That's the truth entire. I agreed in December and I hated myself hourly and I told myself every night that I would find a way out, and every morning I didn't look for one."

* * *

Hazel had gone very still. Stillness was his native country; I'd seen him sit through gales like furniture. But there was a different quality to it now, a stillness like water backing up behind a dam.

"The night the coaster foundered," he said. "The night I pulled two men out of the surf. Was that you on Gull Point?"

And here was the strange thing, the thing I had not expected to say and could not stop saying once it started:

"Yes. And no."

"Tom."

"I went." My voice broke and I let it; shame met squarely, that was what I owed him, all of it, unvarnished. "I carried the lantern up. Corvus's man walked half a mile behind me the whole way so I'd know I wasn't alone in the dark, if you take my meaning. I got to the shelf. I lit the wick. And I stood there with the storm coming in over my shoulder and the convoy reported off the Lizard, and I held the light up, and I — "

I put my face in my hands. Two years of holding it together, of being the strong one for Mother, the steady one for Wynn, the cheerful one at market, and it came apart in Hazel Thornbury's kitchen like wet paper.

"I held it for maybe a minute," I said through my fingers. "One minute. And then I saw — you'll think this mad — I saw Father. Not a ghost, nothing like that. Just remembered him. On the *Maiden's Reward*, thirty years back, when she went down in the ice-storm, and how he went into that water four times and pulled four men living out of it, and died on the fifth, and how Mother used to say we were born on the right side of that story. And I tipped the lantern and snuffed it and shouted into the wind — screamed, actually, like a madman, *stand off, stand off* — knowing full well nobody a mile offshore in that filth could hear a human voice."

"But someone showed a light," Hazel said. Very low.

"Someone showed a light." I made myself lift my head. Made him see my face while I said it, because ducking away even now felt like one more theft. "Corvus had a second man. I never learned his name. When my light died he must have taken the spare lamp and walked the last stretch of the point himself. Ten minutes, steady, just as planned. I was already halfway home, running, sick, telling myself I'd stopped it, *I'd stopped it* — and then I heard the rocks take her hull from three miles off, and I knew I hadn't stopped anything. I'd only arrived late to my own crime."

"The spare lens." Hazel's voice had gone strange. "The light they used. I saw it across the water, Tom. I knew its character — it was one of Maren's spares, out of the workshop. They took it from the cave cache."

"They took everything from the cave. That was always the plan, I understand that now — the wreck was to be ours. Mine. If it ever came to inquiry, there was a man ready to swear he'd seen Tom Penhale on the headland with a lantern, and the duplicate journal page planted to make it look like your wife had been in their pay before you, and the lens traced to your workshop." I laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "They built a noose with three names in it, Hazel. Yours, hers, mine. And mine was the only one I put there myself."

* * *

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The storm worked at the cliffs below like a patient carpenter. I watched Hazel's hands around his cup and thought: *he will send me to the harbormaster tonight, and he will be right to.*

But what he said was: "You snuffed it."

"What?"

"You lit it and you snuffed it. One minute of light against ten minutes of dark. If you had not gone up that point at all, they'd have shown it anyway and called it done. If you had stayed home, the *Providence* founders all the same and you're a clean-handed man with no testimony worth having." His thumb moved once along the cup's rim. "I'm not excusing you. Don't mistake me. Four men are dead and the sea keeps that account whether you flinched or not. But there's a difference between a man who aims the gun and a man who stands on the cliff unable to fire — and I have spent two years learning the exact weight of standing somewhere unable to act, so I'll not pretend I don't recognize it."

I don't know what my face did, but something in me that had been clenched since October loosened half a turn — not forgiven, not remotely, just *witnessed*, which I had begun to suspect was rarer than forgiveness.

"Mother knows," I said. "I told her yesterday, after Wynn woke raving. She didn't shout. She sat with her hands in her lap and then she made tea, and when she brought it she said, 'Your father held lamps for the living, and you held one for the dead, and now you'll spend the rest of your life making up the difference.' And then she cried in the scullery where she thought I couldn't hear."

"She'll want you to testify."

"She'll want me to *survive* it. Both can be true." I leaned forward. This was the last thing, the thing I'd actually come to say, and my heart went at it like a man wading into cold water — best done fast. "Hazel. Whatever happens at the harbor court, whatever sentence comes — transportation, prison, I'll bear it, I've made my peace with bearing it. But Wynn cannot learn this from a stranger. Not from a magistrate reading charges aloud in a public room, not from some fisherman's gossip, not from Corvus's people, who will poison it worse than it is. He's fourteen and he thinks the sun rises out of my collar. If he hears it wrong, he'll spend his life hearing it wrong."

"And if he hears it from you?"

"Then he hears that his father was a weak man who was caught by a clever devil, and who found his nerve exactly one minute too late to save four strangers but perhaps soon enough to save a coastful more. That's the true shape of it. That's what I want him to have. Not clean. Just true." I swallowed. "Will you help me tell him? When the time's right? Because I have tried to open my mouth around that boy for two days and produced nothing but questions about his fever, and I fear I will die with the words still in me unless there's someone in the room holding the lamp steady. As it were."

Hazel looked at me for a long time. Outside, the wind changed note, dropping to that low steady roar that meant the real weather was still gathering out west — the equinox gales, the papers had been calling them. The barometer on his wall had been falling all week; I'd seen him glance at it twice since I came in.

"Wynn idolizes you," Hazel said at last. "I know the weight of that better than you think. I've been carrying it since the letter washed up." He almost smiled. "Yes. I'll sit with you. When it's time — and it will be time soon, Tom, sooner than either of us would choose — I'll be in the room."

"Thank you" seemed too small, so I said nothing, and he seemed to understand that too.

* * *

I stood to go. At the door I paused, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in my chest all evening refusing to be said, and I found that after a night like the ones I'd had, the saying of true things had gotten easier the way a torn callus stops hurting once it's fully torn.

"Maren found them first," I said quietly. "The journal page in the cave — I saw it too, before they moved the cache. Her hand. Dated March, two years back. She knew there was a ring working this coast before she died." I made myself meet his eyes. "I don't know if it means anything. I don't know if her death was — I don't know anything except that the same devil who owns my debt was working these waters while she was designing that lantern of hers, and I've lain awake wondering whether those two facts touch each other somewhere in the middle. I'd want to know, if it were my wife. I'd have to know."

Hazel had gone absolutely white. Not moving, not breathing, the way the tower looks in lightning — lit up from inside and utterly silent.

"Say that again," he said. "The date. Say the date again."

"March. The ninth, it looked like. Why? Hazel — why?"

But he was already turning to the stairs, shouting up softly for Bess to mind Wynn, dragging his oilskins off the peg with hands that shook, and when he spoke his voice had in it something I hadn't heard in two years of knowing the man — not grief exactly, and not hope, but some live wire strung taut between them.

"Because March the ninth is the page that was torn out of her journal," he said. "And because the equinox storm makes landfall tomorrow night — and if Corvus means to wreck one more ship before the century turns, then Maren's lantern has to be burning in that window by dusk. Come on, Tom. You held a false light once. Help me finish a true one."
