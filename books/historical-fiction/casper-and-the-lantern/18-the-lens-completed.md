## 18. The Lens Completed

The recovered page had been drying on the workshop sill for three days, pinned flat under two panes of scrap glass and a horseshoe Bess Penhale had pressed into my hand without explanation. Sea-water is cruel to paper. It swells the fibers, bleaches the ink to the color of weak tea, then hands the whole thing back to you as if it has done you a kindness.

Maren's handwriting came up out of that tea-colored fog slowly, like something surfacing from deep water. I sat across from it each morning with my own breath fogging the air, waiting for the words to be legible enough to copy before they faded altogether.

*The fault is not in the glass,* she had written. *It is in my stubbornness about the glass.*

I laughed when I first read that — an actual laugh, alone in a workshop full of her ghosts, startling a gull off the roof. Two years dead and she was still correcting me. Still patient with me. Still writing as though she knew exactly how long it would take her husband to understand her.

Because she was right, you see. For months I had been wrestling with the prism assembly the way a man wrestles a door that only pulls when he keeps pushing at it. The lantern needed eight prisms arranged to gather a low, wide beam and throw it into a single concentrated shaft — a light that would carry past the reef in foul weather, unmistakable, *particular*, as Maren put it. Her sketches showed the arrangement plainly enough. What the sketches did not show was why mine kept shattering the light instead of focusing it. I had cut and re-cut the angles until my fingers were bandaged and the floor glittered with failed glass, and every attempt scattered the beam into a soft useless bloom, like trying to read by moonlight through a wet window.

Then this page. The one torn from her journal. The one that had traveled, by routes I could not bear yet to think about too long, into a sea cave full of smugglers' stores, folded around the arithmetic of wrecking men.

She had abandoned the eight-prism design entirely. Halfway down the sheet, her neat figures stopped, were crossed out once, and began again in a tighter, faster hand — the hand she used when she'd seen something and couldn't write fast enough.

*Six prisms. Asymmetric. One ground flatter than its brothers — deliberately wrong. The imperfection is the signature.*

I read it perhaps forty times that first morning. *The imperfection is the signature.* She had discovered — God knew how, God knew what sleepless night of chalk dust and broken glass had shown it to her — that if one prism of the six was ground just slightly off-true, the finished beam carried a flaw. A seam. A thin dark thread running through the heart of the light, steady as a pulse, visible for miles.

To any other keeper, a flaw. To anyone who knew the Thornbury light, a name written in fire across the water.

And no false lamp, no borrowed lens, no wrecker's cheap imitation could reproduce it. You could steal her drawings — someone had — but you could not steal the ten thousand small judgments of a woman's hands. The wrongness had to be *exactly* wrong.

She had known, at the end. That much I understood now and would understand better later, though understanding it cost me things I am still paying. She had found the ring. She had designed a light that could not be forged. And somewhere between those two discoveries, the sea had taken her, and I had spent two years calling it accident, weather, God's indifference — because the alternative was a thought I could not hold.

That morning I let myself hold it for exactly as long as it took to sharpen six blanks.

* * *

The equinox gales arrived the way they always do on this coast — not arriving at all so much as simply being there one afternoon, as though they had been standing behind you the whole time waiting to be noticed.

I had carried Maren's journal page to the workshop and back three times now, and each time I did it I felt like a man transporting something live. Wynn noticed, of course. Wynn noticed everything that summer and autumn; the boy had eyes like gull-eggs and about the same talent for pretending he wasn't looking.

"You've got your reading face on," he said, finding me at the workbench with calipers in hand and the page weighted open beside me.

"I haven't got a reading face."

"You've got four faces. Reading, cross, sorry-for-yourself, and Sunday." He counted them off on his fingers, grinning. "This is reading."

"Go fetch me the fine emery, then, if you're going to stand there cataloguing my features like a magistrate."

He went — but he paused at the door, and his grin dropped away, and he said, quietly, "Grandmother says the barometer's fallen a thumb's width since breakfast." Then he was gone before I could answer, which was becoming his habit, and mine to notice.

A thumb's width. I looked at the barometer myself after that, and did not like what it told me. The glass was sinking with the slow certainty of a stone lowered into a well. Somewhere out over the Atlantic, the equinox was gathering itself.

Good, some reckless thing in me said. Let it come.

There is a kind of work that grief cannot touch, or rather a kind of work grief makes possible — the hours go down like water and you do not feel them pass. Grinding the sixth prism took me eleven hours across two days, and I remember almost none of them, only snapshots: the wheel turning, the slurry running gray off the table into the drain Maren had cut herself; lamplight; my own reflection warped in the blank until I looked like a stranger, which was restful. The angle Maren specified was wrong by four minutes of arc. Four minutes. Less than the width of a hair held at arm's length. I measured it nine times, and the ninth time I set down the calipers and said aloud, to the empty room, "You were showing off."

The room agreed with me in the way empty rooms do, which is to say it smelled briefly of her soap, and I got back to work.

* * *

Assembly day was the fourth of the equinox gales, and the workshop rattled like a drawer of cutlery.

Bess had sent Tom down the cliff path that morning with a basket — bread, cheese, a jar of her gooseberry preserves, and, wrapped separately at the bottom in a scrap of flannel, a small tin of her black tea, the good stuff she claimed not to have. Tom stood in the doorway holding the basket and did not quite meet my eye, which had become, since everything, our arrangement. Not forgiveness. Not yet even honesty. But bread.

"Bess says—" he started.

"I know what Bess says. Bess always says."

"She says the wind's going to back to the southeast tonight."

"Then Bess is right, because Bess is always right, and if you repeat that to her she'll deny it and feed you anyway."

Almost — *almost* — he smiled. Then his eye caught on the lantern frame standing open on the bench, its six prisms racked and ready like a choir waiting for the organist, and the smile died, and I watched him look at the thing that would undo him and think whatever thoughts a man thinks standing at the foot of his own gallows-hill. He set the basket down gently, as though the workshop were a church, and left.

I worked through the afternoon with the storm building outside like an orchestra tuning. Seating the prisms was delicate business — each one bedded in her specified compound of rosin and beeswax, each one adjusted by lamplight against a target card she had drawn on the inside of the case lid. Her target card. All these years it had been sitting under the dust sheets, and I had taken it for a child's drawing of a sunburst.

At half past four, with the light failing and the sea making a sound below like a train passing through a tunnel forever, I set the sixth prism into its seat.

The wrong one. The deliberately wrong one. Four minutes of arc of a dead woman's defiance.

I lit the wick.

For a moment nothing — just the ordinary bloom, the soft scatter I had fought for months — and my stomach dropped with the certainty that I had failed her again, that four minutes was five or three, that—

Then the beam gathered itself. It drew in at the edges the way a flock draws in before it turns, and it *lengthened*, out the seaward window and over the cliff and across the boiling dark water, and there — riding in the very heart of it, steady as a heartbeat —

the seam.

A thin dark thread through white fire, running out over the reef, over the shipping lane, over the whole wrecking dark, visible and particular and *hers*. No other light in the world looked like it. No light ever would.

I am not a man who weeps easily; Maren used to say I rationed tears like wartime sugar. But I stood in that rattling workshop with the rain coming horizontal past the windows and the beam holding its dark thread steady across the water, and I wept like something newly made. Not only for her. That surprised me. Some of it was for me — for the two years I had spent as a caretaker of my own ruin, polishing my isolation daily the way I polished the tower glass, keeping grief alive and warm as a tame animal because it was the last piece of her I had.

The light went out over the water and it said *Thornbury*, clear as a signature, and nobody — not Corvus, not his bought men, not the devil himself — could forge that hand.

* * *

I should have gone up to the house. Bess would have fed me; Wynn would have talked the ears off me; the fire was doubtless lit and the kettle doubtless on, because in that house the fire and the kettle were less amenities than articles of faith.

Instead I did what she would have done. I trimmed the wick, checked the oil, closed the case, and carried the lantern out onto the cliff top to see it against honest dark.

The wind took my coat and tried to negotiate it off my body. Rain came in gusts like thrown gravel. Below, the reef showed its teeth in the surge, white and intermittent, and I thought — as I had been refusing to think for a week — about the cave at its base, and the signal lamps stacked in it, and the rope, and the folded page that had taught me everything. Spring convoy, the smooth man at the inn had said. Salvage rights. Debts courted in low voices. And Tom, asking me in September to keep the light burning extra bright on certain nights, *innocently*, he'd sworn, and every kindness since then reading twice.

But the spring convoy was spring. There was time. That was what I told myself, carrying the lantern back along the path with the beam throwing my shadow thirty feet long ahead of me — a giant with a stitched seam of darkness down the middle of him, which felt about accurate.

I was halfway home when I saw it.

Down at the headland where no light had any right to be — a flicker. Brief. Deliberate. Not lightning, not a fisherman's torch, not any honest thing. Once. Twice. A pause long enough to count to five.

Once more.

I stood in the rain with Maren's finished light burning warm in my hands, and I counted the flashes, and I thought of a phrase from the recovered page that I had read a hundred times and understood only now, in my bones, in the teeth of the wind:

*They will not wait for spring if they think the evidence burns.*

Smoke, I thought. Or the smell of it, faint under the salt — or maybe only my own dread, which by now had a smell of its own. Either way, my feet were already moving toward the cliff path down to the cave, and the lantern was already swinging in my grip, its dark seam cutting the storm like a scar across the world.

Behind me, up at the farmhouse, a door banged, and a boy's voice called my name into the wind.
