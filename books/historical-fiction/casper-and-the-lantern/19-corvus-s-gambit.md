## 19. Corvus's Gambit

The barometer had been falling for three days, and by Thursday morning it had given up all pretense and dropped like a stone down a well. Hazel Thornbury tapped the glass case with one finger, the way he'd tapped it a hundred times before a storm, as though the instrument might reconsider its opinion if sufficiently encouraged.

It did not reconsider.

"Equinox gale," he said to the empty lamp room, and the lamp room, being made of glass and iron and forty feet of nothing, said nothing back. He was finding he talked more to rooms these days. Rooms didn't ask questions he couldn't answer.

He went down the tower stairs and out into a morning that couldn't decide what it wanted to be—pale sun one moment, then a scud of cloud like smoke across the water, then sun again, thin and apologetic. The sea was the color of pewter and moving in that particular wrong way it moved before weather, long swells coming out of the southwest with nothing to show for themselves yet, gathering their breath.

From the cliff path he could see the Penhale farm below, and the sight of it did what it always did now: warmed him and hurt him in the same instant, like holding a cup of tea with a cracked hand. Tom was in the yard splitting wood, working harder than the job required, which was Tom's way lately when anyone watched him. Bess's washing line stood full and defiant against the wind, sheets snapping like flags of surrender raised by someone who refused to surrender. And there was Wynn, halfway up the cliff path toward him, taking the rocks at a run the way only fourteen-year-olds and goats could.

"Hazel!" The boy arrived in a clatter of boots and breath. "The harbormaster's sent word down the coast. There's a convoy assembling at Falmouth. They'll come past the reef week after next—the equinox tides, they're saying, first fair window."

"Did the harbormaster send *you* word personally?"

"He told Mr. Tregowan at the inn, and Mr. Tregowan told Mrs. Chegwin, and Mrs. Chegwin told Gran while I was carrying her basket." Wynn shrugged, entirely unashamed. "That's how news travels properly. Post is for people in a hurry."

Hazel felt the old cold move through him, the one that had lived under his ribs since the letter, since the cave, since the night of false light. A convoy. Spring convoy—they had said it in his hearing once, Corvus's smooth voice at the inn talking of salvage rights, and Hazel had hoped, foolishly, that he had misheard the season. He had not misheard.

"Wynn." He kept his voice level. "If you ever saw strangers on the cliffs at night. Men you didn't know. You'd come tell me, wouldn't you? Not go looking yourself."

The boy looked at him with those clear eyes that saw far too much. "I'd tell you," he said. "Eventually."

"*First.*"

"First," Wynn agreed, in the tone of a boy agreeing to the letter of a thing. Then he grinned and was gone again, downhill, arms out against the wind, and Hazel watched him go and thought: *there it is. That's the whole of it. That's what Corvus is worth hanging.*

He had spent two years learning to want nothing so much that losing it would break him. He had been very proud of that learning, once. Now he understood it for what it was—not discipline but cowardice, not grief but a locked door—and the door was standing open whether he liked it or not.

* *

The workshop still smelled of cedar shavings and Maren's linseed oil, even after everything, even after the dust sheets and the damp and his own months of careful, frightened work. The storm lantern stood on her bench where it had stood since before her death, half-built no longer: the prism assembly had yielded to him at last, three nights ago, the recovered journal page propped against the oil can, her handwriting walking him through it the way she used to walk him through everything—impatiently, affectionately, as though he were a very tall and rather slow apprentice.

*Nobody needs a new lens, Maren,* he'd said to her once, early in their marriage, watching her file glass by lamplight. *The Trinity House lenses are fine.*

*Fine,* she had said, without looking up. *Fine is what people say about weather and other people's children. I don't make fine.*

The finished lens sat in its cradle now, catching the grey daylight and breaking it into a slow wheel of color on the far wall. Her design. Her signature—a beam with a character all its own, a triple flash and a long dark, unmistakable, impossible to counterfeit. That had been the point of it, he understood now. She had built a light that could not be lied with. She had known, somehow, near the end, that lights hereabouts were going to be asked to lie.

He was fitting the last retaining ring when he heard the whistle.

Two notes, low-high, repeated. It was Wynn's signal—devised between them weeks ago for exactly the conversation they'd had on the cliff path, and Hazel had thought, had truly believed, the boy would never need to use it.

He found Wynn crouched behind the gorse above the workshop, windblown, white-faced, and something in the set of the boy's shoulders made Hazel's stomach drop before a word was spoken.

"There were men," Wynn said, low and fast. "Down by Gull Rock, this afternoon—I wasn't *looking*, Hazel, I was after a gull's nest for Tom, he collects the eggs, it's a whole thing—" Hazel must have made some sound, because the boy had the grace to wince. "I was leaving. I was leaving, honestly. But then I heard them, and one of them was the smooth one from the inn. Corvus. Talking to the other two."

Hazel crouched beside him. "Tell me everything. In order. Leave nothing out because you think I won't like it."

Wynn swallowed. "They said the convoy would pass on the equinox tides. They've a light ready—a lamp from the cave cache, the big one—and a man who knows the headland. They said—" His voice thinned. "They said the Penhales' name would be on it. That the revenue would find things at the farm. Rope, flasks. They've been *putting* things there, Hazel, at night, I've seen—God, I've seen and I didn't know what I was seeing." He dragged a breath. "And then they talked about the cave. The cache. Corvus said once the wreck was done, there'd be no need of it, and evidence is a thing you don't leave lying about. He gave one of them a can. Oil, I think, or spirits. They mean to burn it. The whole cave, all of it—"

"And?" Hazel said quietly, because the boy hadn't stopped, hadn't looked away from the ground, and there was more.

Wynn's jaw worked. "And the workshop. They know the lantern's here. Corvus said—" He made himself say it. "He said the keeper's woman built a light that tells the truth, and a truth-telling light is worse than a witness, because a witness can forget. Burn the cave and burn the workshop the same night, and there's nothing left to prove anything but a Penhale rope-bag in a Penhale barn."

The wind came up the cliff and combed through the gorse, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Above them, faint and steady, Hazel's great lamp turned in the tower, keeping its faithful count of the dark to come.

"You should have run straight home," Hazel said at last.

"I ran straight here."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's better," said Wynn. "You're here."

Hazel looked at him—that fierce, foolish, beloved boy, who had gone pale as bone and planted himself anyway because Hazel was here—and felt the last of his own locked doors come off their hinges. Two years he had kept the world at arm's length, telling himself a keeper's discipline was the same as peace. Well. The equinox would have its say about peace soon enough.

"All right," he said. "Listen to me carefully, because I'm going to ask you to do the hardest work you've ever done, and it's going to feel like doing nothing. You're going to go home. You're going to eat your supper and complain about it the way you always do. You are not going to say one word of this to Tom or your gran—one word, Wynn, not even a look—and you are not going near Gull Rock or the cave or anywhere the wind blows cold until I come for you. Can you do that?"

"Why can't I tell Tom? If they're framing him, he should—"

"If Tom hears it, Tom will do something brave and stupid, and brave and stupid is exactly what Corvus wants from him. Do you understand? This stays between us. It has to."

Wynn held his gaze for a long moment, and Hazel watched the boy measure him and decide to trust him, and understood that this, too, was a weight—the weight of a child's faith, which is heavier than any cargo the convoy would carry.

"All right," Wynn said. "But Hazel—if you're thinking of going down there yourself tonight—"

"I'm not thinking of anything tonight but sleep," Hazel lied, warmly and completely.

"Liar," said Wynn, with admiration, and went.

* *

Hazel sat alone in the workshop until the light failed, turning the pieces of it over the way he'd once turned over Maren's journal pages, looking for the shape of the thing underneath.

Corvus was cornered, and cornered men were dangerous in direct proportion to how smooth they'd been when uncornered. The plan was plain once you saw it: one last wreck on the equinox tide, blamed on the Penhales' light or lack of it, the evidence burned, the family ruined, and a stranger gone over the water with the profits before the ash was cold. Against that, Hazel counted his assets on his fingers like a miser.

One: Maren's lantern, finished, its beam a signature no imitator could fake—which meant that if her light burned true and visible on the night, and a *false* light showed elsewhere, the difference would be legible to any mariner and any court that cared to ask.

Two: the cutter. Wynn could ride for Falmouth when the weather turned—no, too far. The revenue patrol was at Port Isaac; nearer, and Wynn knew the moor paths better than any grown rider. That was a card for the night itself, played only when the storm broke and the roads were already lost to everyone else.

Three: the cave cache. Corvus meant to burn it. Which meant, Hazel thought, rising slowly, that every barrel and flask and signal lamp down there was about to become exactly the kind of evidence that burns best—unless it wasn't there anymore when the fire came.

He looked around the workshop, at her bench, her tools hung in their patient rows, the lens throwing its quiet colors on the wall. *Burn the workshop,* Corvus had said, casual as a man discussing fence posts.

"Not this one," Hazel said aloud, to the room, to her. "I'll be honest, love, I've grown attached to it."

He began packing. Tools first, then the journal, then the lens itself wrapped in flannel and laid in the fish crate he kept for exactly such indignities. He would carry it up the tower tonight and mount it beside his own lamp—two lights burning where Corvus expected one, the true signature showing clean over the reef while the false light strangled itself on its own lie.

Outside, the sea had changed its voice. The long swells were shorter now, steeper, and the first hard spits of rain came across the cliff like thrown gravel. The barometer hadn't been wrong. Nothing that fell that far ever was.

He closed the workshop door behind him, took the crate in both arms, and had gone perhaps twenty paces along the cliff path when he saw the light.

A spark, far down at the waterline, near Gull Rock. Then another. Not lamps—lanterns being lit, one after another, a small procession of them moving toward the black seam of the sea cave. Hours ahead of schedule. Corvus was done waiting for the storm to arrive; he was going out to meet it, and he was bringing fire with him.

Hazel stood in the rising wind with Maren's lens safe in his arms and the cave—his proof, the village's proof, Tom's only salvation beyond confession—already in other men's hands, and understood with perfect clarity that the equinox gale was no longer coming.

It had begun.
