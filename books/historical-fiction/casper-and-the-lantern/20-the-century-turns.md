## 20. The Century Turns

### Hazel

The barometer had been falling all day, the needle sagging like a tired man into a chair, and by four in the afternoon the sky over the Cornish coast had gone the colour of wet slate. Hazel stood in the doorway of the workshop, Maren's lantern cradled in the crook of his arm like something newborn, and watched the sea assemble itself into a fist.

It was the last day of the century. The papers in Truro had made a great fuss about it—galas, fireworks, church bells at midnight—but the sea kept no calendar, and Corvus kept one of his own.

Hazel had spent thirty years reading weather, and everything he knew told him this was the storm Maren's lantern had been born for. The equinox gales had been rehearsals. This was the performance.

He set the lantern on the bench and lit the wick with hands that did not shake, which surprised him. He'd expected to be afraid. Instead there was only a terrible clarity, the kind he imagined a man felt stepping off a cliff: nothing left to decide, so nothing left to fear.

The lens caught the flame and threw it through Maren's prisms—the ones he'd failed to solve for two years until a torn page of her journal had shown him her trick, the offset crown-glass she'd called *her little lie*. The beam that came out of it was unlike anything on this coast: white shot through with a green flicker at the edges, clean as a struck bell. A signature no imitator could fake. That was the whole point. That was why he was here and not safe in the tower.

Corvus meant to wreck the spring convoy; the storm had moved his schedule up. Tonight, somewhere on the far headland, a false light would burn, luring ships onto the reef while they believed it was the harbour's guidance. And tonight, the revenue cutter would be too far out to see it—unless Wynn reached her.

Hazel checked his pocket watch. Half past six. The boy should be on the road by now, if Bess's cob had any sense of urgency in him, which the animal historically had not.

"Go on," Hazel murmured to the horse across three miles of darkening country. "Just this once. Be a racer."

The wind arrived properly at seven. It came off the water in long shoves rather than gusts, leaning against the workshop walls until the timbers spoke in creaks and groans like an old man getting out of bed. Rain followed, then sleet, then rain again. Through the seaward window Hazel watched the waves come in—not waves anymore but moving hills, black hills with white crowns, marching out of a darkness that had swallowed the horizon whole.

The convoy would be standing off the headland by now. Eleven merchantmen and their naval escorts, riding out the worst of it, waiting for a guiding light that would murder them.

He turned up Maren's lantern to its fullest and set it in the workshop window, facing the sea.

*There*, he thought. *One true light. One false. Let them choose.*

Then he took up the axe—not for fighting; he was past believing this night would be decided by anything so small as himself—and began bracing the door.

* * *

### Wynn

The cob hated every second of it, and Wynn loved every second of it, and both of them were terrified.

The road to Falmouth had become a river that occasionally remembered it was supposed to be a road. Water ran down the lanes in brown torrents, carrying fence rails and a hen coop and once, alarmingly, an entire haystack that loomed out of the rain like an island. Wynn rode bent low over the horse's neck, hat gone somewhere behind him, rain finding every gap in his coat with the enthusiasm of a thing that had been waiting all year for this assignment.

Fourteen years old. Grandad would have said too young for a man's errand. But Grandad had also said the Penhales were honest, and Grandad had been wrong about that, so perhaps his opinions on age could be taken under advisement.

He thought about Hazel's face when they'd worked it out that morning—Hazel, who never wasted words, saying simply: *You're the lightest rider, and you're the only one Corvus's men won't remember seeing. If I'm wrong about the cutter's position, we've lost nothing but your supper.* As though supper were the stake. As though Wynn couldn't see what it actually was: trust, handed over like something breakable.

The cob baulked at the ford below Tregavone, the water already over its knees and pulling. Wynn leaned forward, put his mouth against the wet hot ear.

"I know," he said. "I know. But you've got to."

Whether the horse understood or merely surrendered, they went through—water climbing Wynn's legs, cold as a held breath—and came out the far side scrambling, and Wynn whooped into the wind, because he was alive and fourteen and the century was ending and he was, for tonight, the most important boy in Cornwall.

Falmouth harbour, when he reached it, was chaos wearing a coat of rain. The cutter *Vigilant* lay at her moorings, lamps swinging, men visible on her deck in oilskins hauling at lines. Wynn rode straight down the slipway until the sea stopped him, stood in the stirrups, and shouted until his voice cracked.

"Week after next they'll say I'm mad!" a lieutenant shouted back, half-drowned already by the gale. "Who sends a boy out in this?"

"The lighthouse keeper at Porthmoor!" Wynn roared. "And a man called Corvus is burning a false light on Garrow Head *tonight*! There's a convoy standing off that coast right now thinking it's yours!"

That changed the lieutenant's face entirely. Wynn watched disbelief lose the argument to arithmetic—a ship's worth of cargo, a crew's worth of lives, weighed against the word of a soaked child on a spent horse.

"Mr. Quill!" the lieutenant bellowed into the ship. "Get the master up! We're putting to sea!"

Wynn sat down where he was, on the wet stone, legs shaking now that they were allowed to, and watched the cutter come alive. It occurred to him, distantly, that he should probably be frightened of what was happening back along the coast—at the workshop, at the cave, at Hazel alone with the storm.

But Hazel was the steadiest man Wynn had ever known. Steadier than the tower itself.

He was sixteen days short of fifteen, and he had delivered his message, and the century had one hour left in it, and none of those facts felt real. Only the work felt real. He clung to that.

"Can I come?" he yelled at the lieutenant, as the lines came off.

"You can stay exactly there," the lieutenant yelled back, "and pray."

So Wynn prayed, in his fashion—which was mostly to imagine Hazel's face until he could picture it calm—and watched the lights of the *Vigilant* dwindle into the boiling dark toward home.

* * *

### Hazel

At half past eleven, the false light appeared.

It bloomed on Garrow Head like a malignant star—steady, warm, respectable-looking, everything a master would want in a harbour guide and everything it wasn't. Hazel saw it from the workshop window and felt something go cold and permanent in his chest. So it was true. All of it. The man was doing it while the century died.

Maren's lantern answered from the workshop window, its green-edged beam cutting out across the water, unmistakable, arguing with the liar on the headland. Two truths could not occupy the same night. Hazel had built his life around that principle without ever once applying it to himself.

Through the glass, distorted by streaming rain, he watched the sea between the two lights. Somewhere out there, eleven ships were making their choice.

The workshop groaned. Hazel looked up in time to see a seam along the ceiling open like a slow smile, shedding dust and forty years of Maren's chalk marks. The sea had come up over the rocks below—he could hear it hitting the underside of the floor, a drumming that had no rhythm and no mercy. By midnight the water was through, sheeting across the stones, reaching for the bench legs.

He should leave. Every instinct honed by thirty years of keeping said: *the building is lost, save yourself, buildings are replaceable.*

But the window faced the sea, and the lantern faced the window, and between them hung eleven ships' worth of living souls, and so Hazel Thornbury did the arithmetic nobody sane would do and stayed.

Water climbed his boots. Then his knees. The wind found a loose slate somewhere above and played it like a fiddle, then tore it free and threw it into the dark. He wedged himself against the bench, one arm hooked around Maren's lantern's base—not to move it, just to be touching it—and watched the century's last minutes drown.

The clock in the tower, when he'd wound it that morning, had read five minutes to twelve when he left. He had no idea what it read now. Time had come loose from its moorings along with everything else.

Somewhere past midnight—by the feel of it, by the way even the storm seemed to hold its breath for a heartbeat—a bell began ringing far off across the water. Ship's bells. More than one. And beneath them, faint as memory, a whistle: the cutter's steam horn, arriving like an answer to a letter.

They'd made the turn. They'd seen the true light and refused the false one.

Hazel laughed. It came out cracked and salt-burned, a laugh with rust on it, and he laughed anyway, standing knee-deep in his dead wife's drowning workshop with his hand on her finished work.

"Maren," he said out loud, to the noise, to the dark, to the woman who had solved her little problem with prisms two years before anyone knew there'd be a question. "You did it. You absolute marvel. You did it from the grave, my love, and I only had to hold the lamp."

The roof disagreed with the sentiment. It came off the eastern corner all at once—not gradually, not politely, but like a lid being removed by someone impatient to see what was inside. Rain fell straight down onto the bench, onto the tools, onto Hazel's upturned face. The sea, offended at losing its monopoly, surged higher, waist-deep now, tugging at him with a thousand small hands.

The lantern guttered. His heart stopped with it.

Then the flame steadied—sheltered, he realized, by the very design of the housing, Maren having anticipated gales the way other women anticipated callers—and the beam swept out again, green-edged, insistent, *true*.

"All right," Hazel told it. "All right. We stay till they're round the point."

* * *

### Wynn

The *Vigilant* found the coaster first—or what was left of finding it: a longboat, swamped, two sailors clinging to it and a third already gone under, and beyond them, on Garrow Head, the false light burning its honest-looking lie into the night.

Wynn saw none of this from the harbour wall. What he saw, later, in the account the lieutenant gave the harbormaster with Wynn standing close enough to breathe the same air: the cutter standing in under the headland, guns out, boats lowered, men hauling sailors from the surf while the storm tried to take the whole transaction as payment.

And Corvus's men, caught between their lamp and the water, running—and Corvus himself, that smooth-voiced gentleman, going down the cliff path in his good coat to a waiting boat that never came, because the boat's crew had better sense, and so the most elegant man on the coast ended the century chest-deep in the surf with a revenue man's hand on his collar, screaming about salvage rights to nobody.

"They took him in the shallows," the lieutenant said, when the story finally made it ashore two days later, but Wynn wasn't listening by then, because Wynn was already halfway home.

* * *

### Hazel

Dawn came grey and stunned, the way dawn does after a night like that, and found Hazel sitting on the rocks above what remained of the workshop.

What remained was not much. The eastern wall had gone into the sea entire; the roof was scattered across the cliff like a dropped hand of cards; the bench had floated, turned, and lodged upside-down against the doorframe, and pinned beneath it, dry, unbroken, still smelling faintly of lamp oil, sat Maren's lantern. Hazel had carried it out at first light, when the surge finally retreated enough to permit argument, and now it sat beside him on the rock, catching the weak sun, throwing small green sparks across the stone.

He supposed he should feel the loss. The workshop had been Maren's, and then theirs, and then hers again in every way that mattered. Two years of grief lived in those walls.

But grief, Hazel had learned, was not a house. It traveled. It fit itself into whatever shelter you gave it. And this morning it had a great deal less room to move, because the thing it had been grieving *for*—the unfinished work, the interrupted sentence—was finished. Said. Complete. Sitting beside him in the sunlight, green-eyed and whole.

Down in the bay, the *Vigilant* lay at anchor, her boats shuttling between her and the shore. On the road above, figures were coming down toward him—Bess in her shawl with her chin set like a woman attending a funeral she intended to argue with, Tom behind her looking ten years older than yesterday, and ahead of both of them, spurring the cob down the path with more courage than skill—

Wynn.

The boy was off the horse before it stopped and across the rocks at a run, and then he hit Hazel the way the sea had hit the seawall, arms around him, face buried in his coat, shaking.

"You *idiot*," Wynn was saying into the wool, muffled and furious. "You absolute—you stayed in a *collapsing building*—"

"I did," Hazel agreed. He put a hand on the boy's head, the way his own father never had, and found it came naturally, the way breathing came. "And you rode forty miles through the end of the world. Between us we've given the new century a hard act to follow."

Bess reached them and said nothing at first, only looked—at Hazel, at the ruin, at the lantern glowing green on the rock—and whatever passed over her face had the shape of a decision being made.

"Hazel," she said. "There's something Tom has to say. And something I have to say after." Her voice didn't waver, but her eyes did, just once. "You'll want to sit down for one of them."

Hazel looked from her to Tom, who could not lift his gaze from the wet stone, and understood that the storm had not entirely finished with them yet—that some wreckage had washed ashore only now, in the daylight, and would have to be picked through by hand.

He picked up Maren's lantern instead, and held it.

"All right," he said quietly. "Tell me."
