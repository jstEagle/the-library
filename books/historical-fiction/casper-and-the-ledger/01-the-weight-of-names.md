## 1. The Weight of Names

Rafael Marchetti had kept the light for eleven years and had learned, in that time, that the sea did not care whether you were grieving or not. It came in on schedule. It went out on schedule. The fog arrived when it pleased and left when it pleased, like a rich uncle at a funeral, and your job — the whole of it — was to be awake when it mattered.

He climbed the spiral stair at half past six each evening with a can of oil in one hand and his own stubbornness in the other, because his knees had opinions now, and they were all negative. Ninety-one steps. He counted them without wanting to. Elena had once painted a small blue fish on step forty-four, just to see if he'd notice. He noticed every single night.

The lamp took its wick, took its flame, took its slow rotation with the sound of a contented stomach. Rafael stood a moment watching the beam sweep out across the water, touching nothing, promising everything, and then he went back down to the room where the ledger lived.

* * *

The ledger was not officially his business. That was worth saying, and he said it often, to anyone who'd listen, which was mostly the cat. The ledger belonged to the town — it sat in the town hall behind the council table, in a drawer with a lock whose key hung on a nail beside the door because nobody had ever trusted locks more than neighbors. But every keeper before him had copied its pages into a second book, and every keeper's wife after them, until the habit had come down through the years like a coat passed hand to hand, and Elena had worn it last, and now it fit him badly and he wore it anyway.

Twice a month he walked down into town with his notebook and his pencil stub, sat at the council table while old Ferrero unlocked the drawer, and transcribed what was new: births, deaths, debts of record, the price of herring, who owed whom, who had married whom, who had sworn in front of witnesses never to speak to so-and-so again and how long that vow had lasted. (The record holder was still the Costa brothers at four years, two months, broken only when one of them fell off his boat and the other had to fish him out.)

"History," Rafael told the cat, whose name was Admiral and who had earned it by refusing to move for anyone, "happens in capitals. In parliaments. In places where men wear coats that cost more than my boat." The cat blinked at him with the patience of an animal who has heard this speech many times. "Nobody writes history about a harbor. They write about what harbors are *for*."

This was his creed, and he held to it gently, the way you hold a railing on a wet stair. Kings made history. Ministers made history. Armies made history, God help them all. A lighthouse keeper kept a light, and a town kept itself, and if the town wanted to write down that Matteo Costa owed the widow Ferran three shillings for mackerel and had paid two, well, that was bookkeeping, not history. Bookkeeping was humble work. He respected humble work. He simply didn't confuse it with the kind of thing future generations would trouble themselves over.

He said as much to Elena once, years ago, standing right where the Admiral was now sitting. She had laughed — not unkindly, but with that particular laugh of hers, the one that meant she knew something he didn't and intended to enjoy the knowing.

"You think names get heavy all at once?" she'd asked. "No. They get heavy one at a time, and then one day somebody tries to lift them all together and finds out what they've been carrying."

He hadn't understood her. He wrote that down nowhere, and regretted it later, though he couldn't have said why.

* * *

It was the first Thursday of October, and the wind had come up from the southwest with the smell of a long journey in it. Rain hit the windows sideways. Rafael lit the lamp early, fed himself boiled fish and bread — cooking for one remained a skill he refused to develop — and settled at the kitchen table with the fair copy, the second ledger, the one that was his alone to keep.

Elena's handwriting filled most of it. That was the strange mercy and the strange cruelty of the thing: he could not open it without seeing her, and he could not leave it closed either, because the entries were falling behind and the town would notice. The town noticed everything. That was the other thing about ledgers nobody told you — they weren't just records. They were a kind of promise. As long as the names were written somewhere, in a steady hand, in order, the harbor held together. People paid their debts and forgave small ones and remembered their dead, all because a book in a locked-but-never-locked drawer said they should.

Rafael dipped the pen. His own handwriting was worse than hers — cramped, leaning east, forever apologizing for itself — but it was legible, which he maintained was the entire point of writing.

*October 3rd.* Birth: Lucia Okonkwo, daughter of Joseph and Adaeze, eight pounds, born during the gale of the 29th, "which explains her lungs," he added, and then hesitated, because adding commentary wasn't strictly proper. Then he thought of Elena's marginalia — she'd once recorded that a goat belonging to the Ferran household had eaten an entire Sunday hat, with witnesses named — and he left his sentence where it was.

Death: none this fortnight, thank God.

Debt: Tomás Ribeiro to the chandlery, rope and pitch, eleven shillings, six paid. He knew Tomás's handwriting on the original even before he saw the name, big and cheerful, letters like buoys bobbing in a row. Tomás always paid. Sometimes late, but always, and always with a fish attached by way of interest.

Marriage: none. Grudges: the Costa brothers had been seen speaking to each other outside the net shed, duration forty seconds, subject unknown. Rafael recorded it with a smile and noted *watch this space.*

Outside, the wind leaned harder against the tower. The lamp above him turned and turned, patient as bread rising. Somewhere below in the dark, boats rocked at their moorings, and families ate late suppers, and the widow Ferran was no doubt telling someone exactly what she thought of them, and every one of those households existed quietly inside the pages drying under his hand.

He worked until midnight. When he finished the last entry he sat looking at the book — at her handwriting flowing into his, page after page, the way her voice used to flow into his silences — and he pressed his thumb flat against the margin where she had drawn, once, idly, a small bird. A gull, wings up, caught mid-turn. She'd drawn dozens of them over the years, in margins, in corners, next to sums. He'd never asked her why. There had seemed no need to ask; she drew birds the way other people hummed.

"I'm keeping up," he told the empty kitchen. "In case you're wondering. Badly, but I'm keeping up."

The rain eased near dawn. He slept three hours and woke with the feeling — familiar now, almost furniture — of having dreamed of her and lost the dream on waking. He put the kettle on. He did not look at the ledger again, because if he looked at it every time he felt like it he would do nothing else, and the light required tending, and the day required tending, and grief was a lighthouse keeper too, in its way: it demanded oil and trimming and to be kept burning at a manageable size.

* * *

Thursday was also market morning, which meant that by nine o'clock Rafael had been told, in no particular order, that the herrings were running thin, that the herrings were running thick (contradictory reports from fishermen who hated each other), that his tower was leaning (it was not), and that he looked tired (he was). He carried his basket through the square past the town hall, where the requisition notices usually went up, though there was nothing on the board today but a faded poster about cholera precautions from a government that had otherwise never troubled itself to remember the harbor existed.

That suited Rafael fine. Distance from the capital was the town's oldest tradition.

Ferrero caught him at the fish stalls, holding the ledger under his arm like a briefcase of state. "Marchetti. You're due at the table Saturday."

"I know. I have the date circled."

"You have a date circled? You?"

"For the ledger, yes." He shifted his basket. "It's the only appointment I keep."

"Good man." Ferrero patted the book without seeming to know he was doing it, the way some men touch a saint's medal. "My father's writing's in here. And his father's. Whole family, start to finish. Debts, weddings, the lot." He squinted out at the gray water. "You ever think about what happens to all this? Hundred years from now?"

"Hundred years from now," Rafael said, "some archivist in the capital will glance at it, decide it's provincial, and put it in a basement."

"That's a gloomy thing to say."

"It's a comforting thing to say. Basements are safe. Nobody starts wars in basements."

Ferrero laughed and moved off, and Rafael stood a moment longer among the stalls, listening to the ordinary noise of the harbor — haggling, gulls, a child being called home twice — and felt the peculiar peace that came of being a small part of something small. History happened elsewhere. Thank God for elsewhere. Let the capitals keep their wars; this town could barely keep its fishermen on dry land through winter, and that was catastrophe enough for anyone.

He walked home along the quay road with the wind at his back. From the top of the path he paused, as he always did, to look down at the harbor: the boats, the breakwater, the smoke rising from chimneys, the whole arrangement of it, fragile-looking as lace and tougher than anything he could name. Elena had loved this view. She used to stand exactly where he was standing, hands on the rail, and count the boats coming home.

"Sixteen tonight," she'd say. "All accounted for."

All accounted for. He hadn't thought of the phrase in months. It sat strangely in his chest now, heavy in a way he couldn't have explained, the way a word repeated too many times goes strange in the mouth.

At the top of the tower that evening, trimming the wick, he looked south along the coast where the horizon should have been clean, and stopped.

There was smoke out there. Not the low smudge of a steamer making for port, not the woodsmoke of a fishing boat's stove. This was heavier, darker, riding low on the water, and there was more than one plume of it — three, perhaps four — arranged in a line, the way ships arrange themselves when they are not fishing, not trading, not carrying passengers, but arriving.

Rafael stood very still, the cloth forgotten in his hand, and watched the shapes crawl in from the deep water while the light turned above him, sweeping its patient arc over a sea that had just begun, quietly, to change.

History, it appeared, was done happening elsewhere.
