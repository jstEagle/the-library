## 2. Smoke on the Water

Rafael Marchetti had a theory about dawn, which was that it happened too early and to other people. For thirty-one years he had risen in the dark to trim the wick and wind the clockwork that turned the great lens of the Point Corvo light, and for thirty-one years he had watched the sun come up over the water with the mild resentment of a man who has done his shift and would like the world to do its own.

This particular morning, the world had brought guests.

He saw the smoke first — three smudges on the horizon where there should have been none, gray against a sky still pink at the seams. He stood at the gallery rail with the oil can still in his hand and counted them the way he counted everything: twice, then once more to be sure. Three ships. Not merchantmen; they sat wrong in the water, high-bowed and impatient, and they came on under steam though the wind favored sail.

"Now that," Rafael said aloud to the gulls, "is someone else's history."

The gulls, who had heard him say similar things about weather, taxes, and the new parish priest, declined to comment.

He went down the spiral stair with the practiced haste of a man whose knees had opinions but knew when to keep them, fetched the signal flags from the storeroom, and ran up the warning hoist out of habit more than hope. The harbor below was waking. Lamps kindled one by one along the quay like a slow thought, and the smell of woodsmoke and frying fish began its daily argument with the salt air.

By the time the first ship cleared the point, half the town was on the seawall, and Rafael was among them, cap pulled low, ledger tucked under his arm because he carried it everywhere now the way other widowers carried a watch. It had been Elena's before it was his — the town's ledger of names, debts, births, grudges, fishing shares, fence lines, and one memorably bitter dispute over a goat — and she had taught him that a thing written in a fair hand outlives the hand that wrote it.

The ships came in without asking. They took the deep water anchorage with the confidence of bodies that had never once been made to wait, and their anchors went down with a sound like doors closing.

"That'll be the army," said Tomás Reyes beside him. Tomás fished the eastern grounds and had the permanent squint of a man reading a sea that kept lying to him. "Fortification party. They've been talking about it in Salinas for two years. Gun emplacements." He said *gun emplacements* the way you'd say *landslide* or *fire*.

"Emplacements of what?" asked Widow Ferran, who was seventy-three and considered ignorance a form of bad manners.

"Guns," said Tomás.

"What a waste of a good harbor," said Ferran.

A launch dropped from the lead ship's side and pulled for the quay. In it sat four soldiers and one officer who stood the whole way, holding onto nothing, as if balance were a courtesy he extended to the boat rather than the reverse. He stepped ashore before the bow line was fast, brushed his coat sleeves, and surveyed the gathered townsfolk with the expression of a man arriving at a property he had already purchased in his mind.

"Good morning," he said, in the tone of a man who did not require it to be. "I am Lieutenant Prosser, advance party, Corps of Royal Engineers. By order of the Admiralty and the Colonial Office, this harbor and township are requisitioned for works of national defense. Notices will be posted."

He nodded to a soldier, who began pasting papers to the customs house wall with a brush and pot of glue, each notice going up with a wet slap that echoed off the water. The town read them in silence:

*BY ORDER: all civic records, registers, and accounts to be surrendered for inspection within seven days. All quays and foreshore pass to military control. All persons to render such labor and materials as required.*

"Records," said Ferran, loudly, to no one. "They want our *records*."

Prosser's eyes found her, then moved on, cataloguing: the quay, the chandlery, the lighthouse, the people, in roughly that order of interest. His gaze snagged briefly on the ledger under Rafael's arm — a professional glance, the glance of a man who saw paper the way Tomás saw water — and moved on.

"The works begin within the month," Prosser said. "Your cooperation is noted and expected." He turned back toward his launch, paused, and added, almost kindly, "You'll find the army a better neighbor than you fear."

Nobody answered him. The launch pulled away, and the notices dripped glue in the morning sun, and Rafael stood among his neighbors feeling something shift under his feet like shingle in a retreating wave. All his life he had believed — comfortably, lazily, he could see that now — that history was a thing that happened to larger places. To capitals. To coastlines with real harbors and towns whose names appeared in newspapers. Here at Point Corvo, history had always been content to arrive as weather, or prices, or the occasional shipwreck, and Rafael had kept his records and his light and his grief in an orderly house above an orderly sea.

The tide, this morning, had brought the rest of the world to his door, and it had not knocked.

* * *

By noon the harbor had organized itself into the shape towns take when frightened: everyone busy, nobody saying anything. Rafael climbed back up to the lighthouse intending to work on the log, and found Tomás sitting on his doorstep, hat in his hands, which was how Rafael knew it was serious, because Tomás treated his hat as a fixed feature of his person.

"There's coffee," Rafael said.

"There is that." Tomás followed him into the kitchen, where the stove kept its faithful little warmth and the window gave onto the whole gray sweep of the anchorage. The warships rode there still, patient as creditors.

Tomás drank half his coffee, looked into the cup as though the rest of it might contain advice, and set it down.

"Rafa. The pages. When they take the ledger — and they'll take it, you heard him — there are pages that can't go. There are names in it that can't be in a soldier's hands."

Rafael put his own cup down carefully. "What names?"

Tomás's squint intensified, doing arithmetic it didn't want to do. "Fishing shares. Some of the older entries. The famine years."

"The famine years were twenty years ago, Tomás. What could Vane's—what could *anyone's* army want with grain shares from twenty years ago?"

"I don't know what they'd want," Tomás said. "That's what frightens me. A man who doesn't know what he's looking for looks at everything." He leaned forward. "My grandfather's name is in there. My father's. Half the boats in the harbor drew 'irregular' shares those years, and the word irregular is doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. You know what I'm saying to you?"

And the terrible thing, the thing Rafael would turn over at 3 a.m. for years afterward, was that some part of him *did* know. He had inherited the ledger from Elena with its full memory intact, and there were entries in the middle years written in her small upright hand that he had never fully understood — sums that balanced in ways that felt less like accounting than like mercy, deliveries of grain logged against names that owned no land to grow it in. He had told himself it was charity, tidily kept. He had told himself a great many tidy things.

"You're asking me to hide official records from the Crown," Rafael said.

"I'm asking you to hide *our* records from the Crown. They were ours before they were anybody's evidence." Tomás's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and Rafael understood suddenly that this was not only about old grain. Tomás's boy, Pau, was fourteen. Fourteen-year-olds became laborers; laborers became conscripts when paperwork made them eligible. A name in the right column, at the wrong moment, in front of the wrong officer—

"They post notices saying records," Rafael said slowly, "and I keep hearing the word *obligations* underneath it."

"So does the sea hear storms under calm days," said Tomás. "Doesn't make them less coming."

They sat with that. Out the window, a ship's boat was measuring the quay with a long tape, two soldiers walking it end to end with the unhurried thoroughness of men counting another man's spoons. Rafael watched them and felt the ledger under his elbow on the table not as a duty but as a weight — Elena's handwriting, the town's whole remembered life, about to become somebody's exhibit.

"Elena kept duplicates of some things," he heard himself say, and stopped, surprised. It was true, and he had never said it aloud to anyone, possibly had never thought it aloud. "In the storeroom. Old copy-books. I don't know everything that's in them."

Something moved across Tomás's face — relief, and under the relief, guilt, quick as a fish under a hull. "Then maybe," Tomás said, "before Wednesday, you should find out."

* * *

Wednesday, according to the notice, was when the records were due. Tuesday evening, Rafael carried the ledger to the town hall himself, wrapped in oilcloth, because he reasoned — correctly, as it turned out, though it comforted him not at all — that if the thing must be taken, better it walk in the door held by a keeper than be pried out of a locked house.

But that was Tuesday's grief, and Tuesday is another chapter. This Monday night, after Tomás had gone down the hill with his empty cup and his heavy face, Rafael lit the storeroom lamp and stood among the smells of his working life — oil, rope, brass polish, the ghost of paint — and looked at the shelf where Elena's things ended and his began.

Her copy-books sat where they had sat for six years, spines faded, dust on the topmost one wearing his fingerprints around its edges from all the times he had lifted it, weighed it, and put it back. A man can circle a truth for six years the way a moth circles a lamp, admiring the light and declining the lesson.

Tonight he took the first one down.

It fell open, as books do, to the page its owner had last pressed, and there was her hand, upright and unhesitating, the hand he had watched write birthday cards and storm reports and, once, furiously, a complaint to the coal merchant. But the entries were not storm reports.

*Delivered, 14th March: meal, two sacks, to the Okonkwo household, against spring work. Marked:*

And beside the entry, small and sure as a signature, a tiny drawing of a gull.

He turned pages. Gull after gull, a whole flock of them scattered through the famine years, each one anchored to a name and a sack and a season. Grain that had never been grown in the valley. Shares that had never been landed through the customs house. And in the margin of one page, in Elena's hand, a single line he must have read a hundred times and never once *seen*:

*The sea keeps its own books.*

Down in the harbor, the warships showed their riding lights, one, two, three, patient as a held breath. Rafael stood in the lamplight with his dead wife's arithmetic open before him and understood, with the slow cold turn of a tide changing, that he had never known the woman at all — that she had run something through this town for years, under his roof, under his nose, feeding people he knew with money that had come from nowhere he could name — and that tomorrow the army was coming for the books.

All of them, he suspected, if they knew to ask.

He blew out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening to the sea work at the rocks below, and it sounded, for the first time in thirty-one years, like it was trying to tell him something.
