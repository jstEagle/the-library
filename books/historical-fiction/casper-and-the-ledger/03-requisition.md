## 3. Requisition

The notice went up on the town hall door at seven o'clock in the morning, and by half past seven old Piet Voss had read it aloud three times to an audience that grew with each recitation, because Piet had never in his life been the bearer of news and intended to milk the occasion.

"'By order of Captain Aldous Vane, His Majesty's Army,'" Piet intoned, one finger holding his place on the page, "'the township of Greywater Harbour is hereby requisitioned for the construction of coastal fortifications. All civic records are to be surrendered to the office of the commanding officer by noon this day. Failure to comply—'"

He stopped.

"Failure to comply what?" asked Marta Okonkwo, who had come up from the fish market with a basket of herring and no patience for dramatic pauses before breakfast.

"That's where the ink smudged," said Piet.

Rafael Marchetti stood at the edge of the gathering with his hands in his coat pockets and felt something cold settle into his chest that had nothing to do with the morning fog. *All civic records.* He turned the phrase over the way he turned over a knot in rope, testing its give, and found none. The ledger was a civic record. The ledger sat in its drawer behind the council table not ten yards from where Piet stood squinting at the door, and at noon, if nobody did anything, soldiers would come and take it.

He should have felt prepared. He had spent the previous night telling himself that papers were papers, that history happened in ministries and parliaments, that whatever these soldiers wanted from a harbor town of eleven hundred souls would be stamped, filed, and forgotten. But watching Marta's face as she read the notice herself — watching her lips move over the words *surrendered* and *failure to comply* — Rafael understood that the thing being taken was not paper. It was the town's memory of itself, and memories do not survive confiscation the way ledgers do.

"Well," said Piet, folding his spectacles with great ceremony, "I expect they'll find our records very dull reading."

"I expect," said Marta, "they'll find them exactly interesting enough to ruin us."

* * *

The council convened at nine, which for the Greywater Harbour council constituted unprecedented emergency speed, and Rafael attended because he was keeper of the light and custodian of the ledger and because Elena had always gone, and he had learned that grief leaves you performing the habits of the dead for years without noticing.

There were six of them around the scarred oak table: Mayor Craddock, whose gout had made him a chair-bound man but whose voice still filled rooms; the brothers Halloran, who between them owned every net in the harbor and agreed about everything except which brother was right; Doctor Ilse Brandt, who kept the birth records in her own house and had brought them wrapped in oilcloth like an offering; young Aldous Pettibone — no relation, he insisted furiously, to anyone — who served as clerk; and Widow Ferran, who held no office at all but had attended council meetings for forty years on the principle that somebody had to watch them.

"They cannot mean it," said Halloran the younger. "Records. What use has an army with our records?"

"The same use," said Doctor Brandt, "that a butcher has with a list of sheep."

The mayor rapped his stick on the floor — he could not reach the table anymore — and called them to order in a voice like gravel in a tin cup. Rafael watched him and thought: here is a man who has presided over arguments about drainage ditches for thirty years and now must preside over this.

"The notice says noon," Craddock began. "It is now—" He consulted his pocket watch, winced at it, consulted it again. "It is now nine minutes past nine. I propose we spend those three hours determining whether we have any lawful grounds for refusal."

"We haven't," said Widow Ferran.

Nobody argued, because she was correct.

"Then I propose," the mayor continued, with the dignity of a man rearranging deck chairs with great care, "that we determine how to surrender in a manner that preserves our self-respect."

"There is no manner," said Ferran.

And then everyone looked at Rafael, because the ledger lived behind his seat, and he supposed it was time he said something.

"It isn't only debts and births in there," he said. His own voice sounded strange to him, rusty. He spoke so rarely to rooms. "There are judgments in it. Fines. Feuds settled. Old Corrigan's apology to the Delacroix family, written out in full, witnessed by four people, dated. When a storm took the Brandt wharf in '81, the rebuilding was recorded page by page — who gave what, who owed whom. It's—" He stopped, surprised by what he was about to say, and said it anyway. "It's the only history this town has ever kept. And we kept it ourselves."

Silence around the table. Somewhere outside a gull screamed at another gull about a fish head.

"You've caught Elena's habit," said Ferran softly, "of saying the true thing when everyone else is being sensible."

"Elena would have hidden it," Rafael said.

"Yes," said Ferran. "But Elena isn't here."

That landed harder than she could have known, or perhaps she knew exactly, because her eyes were on him with something terrible and gentle in them. Rafael looked down at the table.

Mayor Craddock cleared his throat. "We are not in the business of heroics," he said. "If I may say so, we are a fishing town. Our business is weathering. We will surrender the records, we will comply precisely, and we will outlast them. Armies are like storms, gentlemen, ladies. They pass."

"Storms," said Doctor Brandt, gathering her oilcloth bundle, "do not build forts."

* * *

Captain Aldous Vane arrived at the town hall at five minutes to noon, and Rafael, waiting in the council chamber with the ledger closed on the table before him like a patient awaiting surgery, had three full minutes to study the man through the window as he descended from his gig.

He was not what Rafael expected. He was young — perhaps thirty-five — and slight, with the kind of neatness that seemed less like vanity than like law. His boots were polished to a shine that made the harbor mud look personally offended. He wore his cap straight. He walked up the steps of the town hall the way other men walked into churches, and Rafael thought: *this one believes.* That was somehow worse than contempt. A man who despised you might be careless. A man who believed in what he was doing would be thorough.

Vane entered the chamber with two sergeants and did not remove his cap.

"Good afternoon," he said. It was two minutes to twelve. "I am Captain Vane. You are the civil authority of this township?"

"I am Mayor Craddock," said the mayor, making a valiant attempt to rise from his chair, achieving roughly forty degrees of vertical, and subsiding. "Welcome to Greywater Harbour, Captain, though I wish it were under better circumstances—"

"Circumstances are circumstances," said Vane. He had a dry, precise voice, the voice of a man reading aloud from a document he himself had drafted. "I require all books of record maintained by this council. Births, deaths, marriages, property transfers, debts, levies, licenses, and any other instrument of obligation. I am given to understand the principal volume is kept by the lighthouse keeper." His eyes came to Rafael and rested there with mild, cataloguing interest. "You are Marchetti."

"I keep the light," Rafael said. "And the ledger."

"How long?"

"Fourteen years. Before me, my wife."

"And before your wife?"

"Aldous Brandt. The doctor's father. And before him, others. It goes back a long way." He heard how it sounded — like a warning — and could not decide whether he meant it as one.

Vane produced a notebook — a small, immaculate one — and wrote something in pencil. "A private family custody of public records," he said, half to himself. "Irregular." He looked up. "Where is this volume?"

Rafael put his hands on the ledger's cover. The leather was worn soft at the corners from fourteen years of Elena's hands and six of his own. Under his palms he could feel the thickness of it, the weight of names, and his fingers pressed down as though the book might otherwise simply walk away.

He slid it across the table.

It seemed important, afterward, that he had not needed to be persuaded twice — that whatever resistance he had was spent in the space between the notice on the door and this table, and that when the moment came he had done the sensible thing, the weathering thing, the thing the mayor proposed and the whole council endorsed. But watching Sergeant One take the ledger up in both hands like confiscated goods, watching Sergeant Two tie it into a canvas sack with a length of cord pulled tight enough to bite the binding — that was when Rafael's chest went hot and his mouth opened before his judgment could catch it.

"Careful with that," he said. "Some of the pages are older than your country's claim to this coast."

Sergeant Two paused, glancing at his captain.

Vane stepped forward and untied the sack himself. He lifted the ledger out, set it on the table, and opened it — not carelessly, Rafael noted; almost respectfully, the way a surveyor opens a building. He turned perhaps twenty pages. His eyes moved down columns of handwriting, and Rafael watched his face and saw nothing in it, no recognition, no reaction at all, until—

"This hand," Vane said. He turned the book around and laid it flat so Rafael could see. An entry from eleven years ago. Grain shipments. Small sums forgiven. And beside each name, small as a period, a little mark like a bird.

"That's my wife's writing," Rafael said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he had gone very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"She kept accounts of grain disbursements?"

"She kept everything. She was better at it than I am."

Vane studied the gull marks. Then he studied Rafael, and it was the longest anyone had looked at Rafael in years — a full, unhurried examination, like a man checking a knot for weaknesses. Whatever he found there seemed to satisfy him, because he closed the book.

"Your wife is deceased?" he asked.

"Six years."

"My condolences." And the strange thing, the thing Rafael would turn over for nights afterward, was that Vane sounded like he meant it. There was no cruelty in the man. There was something almost worse: procedure executed perfectly by someone who believed procedure was kindness. "These disbursements will require review. Irregular obligations are frequently the root of larger irregularities. A town that feeds people off the books is a town whose real economy is invisible, and an invisible economy is where sedition learns to walk."

"With respect, Captain," said Doctor Brandt from the corner, "a town that feeds hungry people is a town that fed hungry people."

"With respect, Doctor," said Vane, repacking the ledger with his own hands now, carefully, squaring the pages, "hunger is a supply problem, and supply problems left to amateurs become security problems. You'll have your records returned after review." He tucked the sack under his arm. "I will need the keeper available for questions regarding these entries. Mr. Marchetti, report to the town hall tomorrow at nine."

"Why me? I didn't write them."

"No," said Vane, at the door now, cap straight, boots bright. "But you married her. That makes you either her accomplice or her archive. In my experience, widowers make excellent archives."

* * *

Rafael did not go home afterward. He walked the seawall instead, all the way past the breakwater to where the stones gave out, and stood looking at the gray water and the gray sky and the two warships sitting in the roads like furniture too heavy to move.

*Her accomplice or her archive.*

Grain disbursements. He had seen those entries a hundred times and never once asked about them. Grain arriving that nobody remembered buying, sums struck through with a small forgiveness, the gull mark standing sentinel beside each line — and he had assumed, vaguely, lazily, that Elena had simply been Elena, meticulous about everything, keeping even the church charity in order. Fourteen years of marriage and six years of widowhood, twenty years of living beside a woman, and he had read her handwriting daily without once reading her.

A fishing boat came in below the wall, and Tomás waved up at him — a small wave, half-raised and then lowered again, the wave of a man who wanted to talk and didn't dare yet. Rafael lifted a hand in return and kept walking.

At the lighthouse he climbed the stair on habit and checked the lamp on habit and wound the clockwork on habit, and only when he came back down into the storeroom, chasing a dropped wick-trimmer that had rolled beneath the shelving, did he kneel in the dust and see, at the very back where the light from the doorway died, the corner of a wooden box that did not match anything else he owned.

He had inventoried this storeroom. He had swept it. He had, he was quite sure, moved every object in it at least twice since Elena died. And yet the box sat there behind the spare lenses as though it had grown into the wall, and when he dragged it out into the light it was heavier than it should be, and locked, and the lock was not rusted shut the way a forgotten lock goes but oiled, recently, deliberately.

Inside — when he broke it open with a chisel, hands shaking more than the job required — he found paper. Good paper. Reams of it. Bottles of iron-gall ink, sealed. A brass press. And notebooks, three of them, filled edge to margin with Elena's hand: names, dates, sums, deliveries, routes — and everywhere, beside everything, the small mark of the gull.

On the first page of the first notebook, in letters larger than her usual hand, as though written for someone to find:

*Rafael. If you are reading this, then someone has come asking questions, and you will need to know what I never found the right hour to tell you.*

Rafael sat down on an overturned crate in his own storeroom, in his own lighthouse, and read his wife's handwriting, and understood that he had never once met the woman who wrote it.
