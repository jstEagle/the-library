## 6. Vane's Arithmetic

Captain Aldous Vane believed in mornings the way other men believed in God: early, quietly, and with the conviction that everything would be set right by evening if one simply applied oneself.

He rose at five, as he had every morning for thirty-one years, first in the King's army and now in its administrative shadow. He washed in cold water. He brushed his mustache with the little ivory comb his mother had given him at seventeen, an act performed with the solemnity of a sacrament. Then he sat at the town hall desk — formerly the council's, now his — and opened the ledger of Harbor Cess before the light was even good enough to read it.

It was a beautiful object. That had been his first thought when they'd surrendered it, and it remained his thought now, four days into its examination. The binding was oiled canvas gone soft as chamois at the corners. The hand that had filled most of its pages — a woman's hand, fine and slanting and utterly consistent across what must have been twenty years — belonged in an archive, not a fishing village. Vane had spent his career among requisition orders written by lieutenants who spelled "flour" three different ways on the same page. This was something else. This was *order*.

Which made what was hidden inside it all the more troubling.

"Serjeant Bray," he said, without looking up.

Bray appeared in the doorway with the punctuality of a man who had learned that appearing early earned no praise and appearing late earned a great deal of something else. "Sir."

"Read me the entry for the ninth of March, 1881, again."

Bray consulted his notes. "'Paid to Widow Ferran, two pounds ten, for mending of nets.' Then underneath, sir, there's the bird again. The gull."

"And the sum paid out is eleven shillings more than any reasonable mending of nets would cost, and the widow Ferran owns, by the harbormaster's inventory, exactly one boat, which she has not put to sea since the famine." Vane closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a sickroom. "Eleven shillings, Serjeant. Multiply that irregularity by the pages in this book and you begin to see the shape of it."

"The shape being theft, sir?"

"The shape being *rot*." Vane stood and went to the window. Below, the harbor lay gray and glittering under a rinsing sky, the fishing fleet at anchor, smoke rising from chimneys in tidy threads. It looked, from this height, like a place that functioned. That was the trouble with small places. From a distance they looked honest. "Somewhere behind that pretty handwriting, money moved that no one can account for. Sums went out marked only with a bird. Obligations were created — debts, favors, dependencies — that exist nowhere in law. Do you know what happens to a community built on obligations that exist nowhere in law, Serjeant?"

"I'd say it gets by, sir, mostly."

Vane turned and regarded his serjeant, a broad red-faced man from Carmarthen who was, Vane had long suspected, deliberately obtuse as a form of self-defense. "It rots," Vane said. "The strong take. The weak flatter. Every kindness becomes a leash. I have seen it in Calcutta and in Cork and in a dozen garrisons between. A town run on favors is a town run by whoever flatters best."

"Yes, sir."

"You think I'm wrong."

Bray considered this with the honesty of a man who knew lying badly was worse than disagreeing safely. "I think, sir, that my mother took flour off the chapel fund for six winters when the pits were closed, and nobody called it rot. We called it Tuesday."

Vane almost smiled. This was the danger of Bray — that beneath the obtuseness ran a vein of plain sense that could fell a man if he wasn't braced for it. "Your mother," Vane said, "was receiving from an institution with rules, however humble. She knew what she owed and to whom, and the debt could be read. What happened here" — he tapped the ledger — "had no rules anyone can find. Which means the people who depended on it never knew where they stood. That is not mercy, Serjeant. That is weather. Mercy you can rely on; weather you merely survive."

Bray looked at the ledger the way he might look at a sermon he'd already heard twice. "Very good, sir."

"Bring me the summary sheets after breakfast. And Bray — the Okonkwo file. Set it on top."

* * *

The arithmetic took him until noon, and it was good work, honest work, the kind he had been made for. Vane had entered the army as a second son with no fortune and discovered early that while other men dreamed of glory, he dreamed of *columns*. Reconciliation. The perfect satisfaction of an account. There was a peace in numbers that people never found anywhere else, because numbers did not lie to you, did not flatter you, did not die of fever at Gibraltar leaving you a ring and a letter you still kept in your breast pocket though you told no one.

He had reduced the ledger's irregularities to a schedule of forty-three items. Of these, thirty-seven were trivial — rounding errors, charity dressed as wages, the sort of small disorder any community breeds like damp. But six mattered.

Three families occupied properties whose rents, per the ledger, had been "adjusted" year upon year downward, each adjustment marked with the gull, each adjustment traceable to no authority. Whatever arrangement had excused their rent died with whoever had made it. The Crown now owned those buildings, by right of requisition. Families in them without lawful tenure were squatters. The law was not ambiguous. Vane had checked it twice, because he wished to be crueler than it and could not manage it.

Two households had sons of conscription age whose exemptions from the militia rolls rested on payments into a fund that appeared nowhere. The exemptions were void. The boys would sail with the levy.

And one item — item forty-three, flagged and set aside — concerned a woman named Elena Marchetti, deceased, wife of the lighthouse keeper, and sums so large and so regular that Vane suspected he had not yet found the bottom of them.

He rubbed his eyes. Through the window he could see the lighthouse itself, white against the headland, patient as a monument. He had met Marchetti once, briefly, at the surrender of records. A tall, quiet man with a mourner's stoop, hands like driftwood. Vane had taken an instant dislike to him, then examined the dislike and found it was actually envy wearing a uniform. The keeper had a grief Vane understood too well and a life ordered around something constant — the lamp, the sea, the hours. Vane's own life was ordered around paper, and paper, whatever he told himself, was constant only in the way that ash is.

Still. Sympathy was not jurisdiction.

At one o'clock he received the town's notables in the hall — the harbormaster, the vicar, a councilman named Pell who sweated like a cheese in summer. Vane laid out the schedule of six. He watched their faces as he did it, because faces were columns too, and readable ones.

"The Okafor family at Number Nine," he said. "Rent unpaid these nine years under a fraudulent adjustment. They will vacate within the week."

Councilman Pell made a sound like a kettle taken off the heat. "Captain — the father works the quay. The boy's lame. Where would they—"

"There are houses inland. The army compensates displacement at standard rates." Standard rates were eleven shillings per family, which would cover perhaps a fortnight's lodging in a barn, and both men present knew it, and Vane let them know that he knew, and hated himself with a small controlled fire that he had been tending for thirty years and which warmed no one.

"The Adeyemi sons," he continued, "report for the levy on the fifteenth. And the Ferran woman will account for the disbursements marked with her name."

"The gull mark," the vicar said quietly. He was a young man, unreasonably handsome, with the tired eyes of someone who had come to a poor parish full of plans and stayed past the point where plans survived. "Half this town has eaten bread that bird bought. If you prosecute the mark, Captain, you prosecute the town."

"Then the town should have kept honest books, Reverend."

"The town kept *kind* books."

"They are mutually exclusive," Vane said, "more often than either of us preaches." He gathered his papers. He wanted, suddenly and fiercely, to be done with this room and these men and the way the younger one looked at him — not with hatred, which he could answer, but with disappointment, which he could not. "Gentlemen. Appeals may be submitted in writing. In triplicate."

Pell laughed, one startled syllable, and caught himself. Vane permitted it. Humor at the army's expense was one of the few liberties he could afford to grant, being unable to afford anything else.

* * *

That evening he dined alone in the requisitioned front room of the cooper's house — the cooper having been billeted elsewhere at rates Vane had personally verified as fair, because the alternative was becoming a man who did not verify things. The meal was mutton, boiled past ambition. He ate it with the table cleared except for the ledger and one candle, because he had dined alone with paperwork for so many years that company at meals now felt like reading in a wind.

He was halfway through cross-checking the grain deliveries against coastal shipping manifests when he stopped.

There was a pattern in the deliveries he hadn't seen before. During the famine years — '74 through '78, the years the herring failed and children in this parish had gone hollow-eyed — the irregular shipments didn't stop. They *increased*, precisely in step with the fishery's collapse, month by month, as though some hand had been measuring the town's hunger and answering it. Not charity doled out by committee. Not poor-relief with its forms and humiliations. Something faster. Something that arrived before the need was confessed.

Vane set down his fork.

This was the problem with the woman's handwriting, he thought. It was so orderly that it kept making arguments for itself. Every time he traced one of her irregularities toward condemnation, the columns arranged themselves into something that looked less like theft and more like — what? Administration? An administration with no warrant, no audit, no accountability. Precisely the thing he existed to prevent.

*But it worked,* said a voice in him that sounded unpleasantly like Bray's mother. *Six winters of famine and no one in this parish starved. Show me the Colonial Office scheme that can say the same.*

"It worked," Vane said aloud to the empty room, "until it didn't, and when it doesn't, no one knows how to fix it, because no one but a dead woman understands it. That is the whole of the argument for law, madam, and you are in no position to reply."

The candle guttered. He trimmed the wick, and in the recovered light his eye fell again on item forty-three, Elena Marchetti's final entries, dated barely a month before her death. The sums there were different in character — larger, hurried, the handwriting losing for the first time in two decades its perfect slant. As if she had been afraid. Or angry. Or both.

He would need to question the husband properly. Not the dazed exchange at the record-surrender, but a real examination, in daylight, with the ledger open between them. The keeper claimed ignorance of his wife's accounts. Perhaps he was ignorant. In Vane's experience, spouses were rarely ignorant; they were merely agreed to be.

He closed the book and sat a while in the dark, listening to the sea work at the seawall below the window. Thirty-one years, he thought, and what have you ever kept order *of*? Storehouses. Paybooks. The movement of grain from one empire's mouth to another. You have spent your life straightening ledgers in burning houses and calling the straightening the victory.

He blew out the candle.

In the morning, Bray reported that the Okafor family at Number Nine had received their notice, and that the town had received it in silence — the particular silence, Vane noted, of a held breath. He ordered the eviction for the following day, ten o'clock, conducted correctly, with the family given time to pack and a soldier assigned to help carry furniture, because order without courtesy was merely force and he had never consented to be merely force.

"Ten o'clock, sir?" Bray said. "Might make it nine. Sooner done, sooner everyone settles."

"No, Serjeant. Ten." Vane returned to the manifest. "A man should be able to finish his breakfast in his own kitchen on his last morning in it. Even a squatter. *Especially* a squatter."

Bray went out. Vane worked. And at some point in the grey afternoon he became aware that he had read the same line about coastal grain tonnage four times without comprehension, because item forty-three had followed him up from the page and was sitting at the edge of his mind like a guest who refuses to leave.

Elena Marchetti had known she was dying — the vicar mentioned it, offhand, weeks of illness at the last. A woman who kept perfect books does not spend her final month making large, frightened, illegible entries unless she is recording something that must survive her.

Something in this ledger was not an account at all, Vane realized. Something in it was a *message*. And the man at the lighthouse, with his driftwood hands and his mourner's stoop, had either never read it —

— or was waiting, very patiently, for the army to read it for him.

Vane reached for his coat and sent for the keeper.
