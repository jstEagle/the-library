## 7. Eviction at Number Nine

Rafael had always believed that a man could measure the moral weather of a town by what its people did with their hands when something shameful happened in front of them. In the years since he'd inherited the ledger, he'd watched this little harbor work through quarrels, funerals, and one memorable dispute over a fence that both parties swore had been moved — and he'd noticed that in moments of public grief or public anger, hands found work. Hands mended nets. Hands bailed boats that didn't need bailing. Hands held other hands, or found their way into pockets, or gripped gateposts as though the whole street might otherwise tip sideways into the sea.

On the morning the soldiers came to Number Nine, Rafael stood in the crowd outside the Okonkwo house and discovered what his own hands wanted to do. They wanted to close into fists. And then — because he was who he was, because he had spent eleven years perfecting the art of standing slightly apart from history with his hat in his hands — they wanted to find something useful to hold instead.

There was nothing. He had come out without a net, without a bucket, without any of the small props by which a bystander convinces himself he is present for practical reasons. So his hands simply hung at his sides, empty as two unlit lamps, and he hated them for it.

* * *

The trouble had begun, as troubles did now, on paper.

Three days after Captain Vane took possession of the town hall, notices appeared on doors and posts all over the harbor: crisp printed things, army paper, each one bearing a red stamp like a wax seal on a letter nobody wanted to open. *Irregular obligations*, they said. *Pending adjudication*. The words had the flavor of medicine — designed by someone who believed that if you made a thing sound dull enough, no one would notice it was killing them.

The Okonkwo notice had been nailed to their door at dawn. Rafael had seen it on his walk back from trimming the wicks, and he had stood before it longer than he cared to admit, reading the same four lines over and over as though repetition might soften them. Adaeze Okonkwo's name was there. Her husband's name, posthumously listed — Emeka had drowned six years ago, but the ledger did not forget debts just because the debtor had gone ahead to settle them in person. And beneath the names, a sum: forty-one pounds, twelve shillings, entered in Elena's hand sometime in the famine winter of '86, marked — Rafael knew now, though the soldiers did not — with a small gull.

Forty-one pounds, twelve shillings. Bread and meal, most likely, carried ashore on dark nights and left on doorsteps by people who never knocked. A debt to no one. A gift recorded as a debt, because Elena had insisted every grain be written down — *"so we know who we were when it mattered,"* she used to say, and Rafael had nodded the way husbands nod at wives who are right about things the husbands don't yet understand.

He understood now. He understood it entirely too late for Number Nine.

* * *

The soldiers arrived at nine o'clock, which struck Rafael as a civilized hour chosen precisely so the town would have time to gather. Six of them, plus a corporal with a clipboard and the particular upright stiffness of a man who has been told he is an instrument of order and has decided to enjoy it. They came up Harbor Row in step, boots ringing on the wet cobbles, and the sound preceded them the way thunder precedes rain — everyone had time to shut their shutters or open their doors, according to their temperament, and the street sorted itself into witnesses and cowards with remarkable efficiency, though Rafael suspected the categories overlapped more than anyone would say aloud.

Adaeze Okonkwo met them on her own doorstep.

She was a small woman, Adaeze, small the way a knotted rope is small — all of it load-bearing. She wore her good blue dress, and Rafael understood with a pang that she had dressed for this deliberately, that she had looked at the notice and looked at her wardrobe and decided that whatever else was taken from her today, her dignity would walk out the front door upright. Behind her, half-hidden in the dim hallway, he could see the children: Grace, who was ten, holding the baby, and little Emeka Junior, named for a father he'd never met, gripping his sister's skirt with both fists.

"Corporal," Adaeze said, before the soldier could speak. "You'll want the inventory."

The corporal blinked. It was clearly not the opening he'd rehearsed.

"The inventory," Adaeze repeated, and produced from behind the door a sheet of paper covered in her careful, school-taught hand. "Everything in this house my husband paid for, everything given to us by the church, everything owed under your arithmetic, and — separately itemized, so there's no confusion — everything that is mine. You'll take the second list last, and you'll take it over my objection, which I am making now, formally, for whoever reads your report."

There was a silence in which Rafael thought, absurdly, of Elena. Of the way she could dismantle a man's certainty with nothing but a raised eyebrow and a correctly filed fact.

The corporal recovered. He was, after all, carrying the greater weight of paperwork. "Ma'am, the requisition order—"

"I can read." She stepped aside — not yielding, Rafael noticed; admitting them the way one admits weather. "Mind the floors. My mother-in-law laid them."

And so the eviction began, and the town watched.

* * *

It went slowly, which was somehow worse than if it had gone quickly. Speed would have been violence, and violence is legible — the town would have known what to do with violence, or at least known what it felt. But this was procedure. The soldiers worked down the corporal's lists with a methodical courtesy that made Rafael's teeth ache. They carried out the table first, then the chairs, then the sea chest that had crossed from Lagos with Emeka's mother and still smelled, faintly, of another ocean. Each item was checked, noted, stacked in the drizzle on the cobblestones, and the drizzle began to spot the furniture, and nobody had thought to bring oilcloth, because the army had not planned for the furniture's feelings and the furniture had not planned to be outside.

The neighbors gathered in knots of three and four. Tomás stood by the fish racks with a knife in his hand and a fish long past needing any attention from it. Widow Ferran sat on her bench across the row — she seemed to have been installed on that bench since before the town existed — with her hands folded and her eyes moving like a woman keeping minutes of a meeting no one had called to order. The Okonkwos' next-door neighbor, Mrs. Prynne, stood on her own doorstep wringing a dishcloth into a shape no dishcloth had ever been intended to hold.

And Rafael stood among them, doing nothing, and catalogued with terrible precision exactly how it felt to do nothing. It felt like standing in shallow water watching someone go under, and telling yourself the water between you was deeper than it looked.

Grace Okonkwo came out with the baby on her hip and began, wordlessly, to carry the smaller things into the pile — her mother's Bible, a tin of buttons, a wooden bird her father had carved. Each trip she passed through the line of soldiers and the line of neighbors, and each time she came back through the neighbor line, her eyes were a little wider, asking a question that ten-year-olds should not have to ask with their eyes: *Are you going to let them?*

Rafael looked at his hands again. Empty. Still empty.

Here is what he told himself, in the voice of a reasonable man, in the tone he used for balancing columns: that intervention would accomplish nothing but adding his name to a list; that names on lists, he had learned of late, had consequences; that there were two sons in this town already facing conscription and adding the lighthouse keeper to their number would help no one; that the wise course, the strategic course, the course Elena herself might counsel—

No. Even in his own head, that last one rang false. Elena would have been halfway up the corporal's clipboard by now, demanding to see the original entry, the countersignature, the authority under which a dead man's bread debt had become an instrument of the Crown. Elena had once argued with the harbormaster for forty minutes over a mooring fee and won, and the harbormaster had liked her better afterward. That was the difference between them, the one he had called her fire and she had called his floorboards: she believed you could stand in front of a rolling thing and slow it, and Rafael believed you mostly got flattened, and so far his life had been arranged very carefully to test only his theory.

"Corporal." He heard his own voice and was startled by it. The street turned. The corporal turned. "The chest. The large one. It belonged to the girl's grandmother. It isn't on any ledger of mine — of the town's — I keep the records, I'd know."

For one bright moment Rafael Marchetti, keeper of the light, stood inside history with his fists half-made.

The corporal consulted his papers with the serenity of a man who had outlasted harder things than lighthouse keepers. "Entry fourteen, irregular obligation, household goods assessed against outstanding balance. It's on somebody's ledger, sir." He almost smiled. "Yours, I expect."

And there it was — the whole trap of the thing, laid bare in one sentence. It was his ledger. His wife's hand. The record he had kept faithfully, believing faithfully was neutral, believing ink was just ink. Every object coming out of that house came out trailing a line of handwriting he had bound and shelved and dusted for eleven years, and he had no argument to make that didn't indict himself.

His fists opened. His hands found his hat brim, adjusted it, found nothing else.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I expect so." And he stepped back into the crowd, and the crowd closed around him like water around a stone that has decided not to be a breakwater.

* * *

They put the family's things under tarpaulin in the market square, which the army had designated a *holding area*, as though the tables and chairs and the grandmother's chest were troops awaiting transport. The Okonkwos were granted leave to occupy the disused pilot's cottage at the head of the quay pending adjudication — a word Rafael was coming to hate, *adjudication*, with its smell of ink-dried mercy.

By noon the street was itself again, or wearing itself's clothes. Nets came out. The fish rack got honest attention at last. Mrs. Prynne brought Adaeze a pot of soup, because towns are towns even when they are occupied, and shame and kindness live on the same street and borrow each other's cups.

But Rafael walked home along the seawall, and the shame stayed with him like gulls after a fishing boat.

He climbed the tower as dusk came on, out of habit more than duty, and stood in the lamp room while the great lens woke and began its patient turning. Below him the town lit its windows one by one. Somewhere down there, Grace Okonkwo was putting her brother to bed in a cottage that smelled of strangers. Somewhere down there, Widow Ferran was sitting on her bench in the failing light, keeping her minutes. And somewhere below his feet, in the storeroom where he had found her duplicates, Elena's handwriting waited in the dark with all its quiet, dangerous patience — the gull marks, the sums, the record of who they had been when it mattered.

*"History belongs in bigger places,"* he had told her once, laughing at her ledgers, at her fierce little columns of names. She hadn't laughed back. She had looked at him the way she looked at a misfiled page, and said, *"History is whichever room you're standing in, Rafa. The only question is whether you're furniture in it."*

He had spent today as furniture. He had watched a family's whole life get carried into the rain and he had adjusted his hat.

Rafael pressed his forehead briefly against the cold brass of the lamp housing, the way he used to press his hand to Elena's door when grief first came to live with him, and he made the kind of promise a man makes alone at the top of a tower, where the only witness is a light.

Then he went down the stairs two at a time, took the cliff path in the dark, and did not stop walking until he stood at the door of the old chandlery — and heard, from within, a match strike, and a dry voice say:

"Took you long enough. Fetch the lamp. We've pages to talk about."

Widow Ferran's silhouette filled the doorway, and behind her, faintly, the smell of paper and pressed ink.
