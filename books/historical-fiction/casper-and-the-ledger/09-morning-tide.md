## 9. Morning Tide

Rafael learned the date from a boy selling mussels.

The boy came up the lighthouse stairs two at a time the way boys do, as though stairs were an insult to be conquered, and arrived breathless in the lantern room with a basket of shellfish he had no intention of selling.

"Signor Marchetti. The ship. The big gray one at the quay."

"What of it?" Rafael trimmed the wick without looking up. The lamp was dark by day, but his hands kept its hours anyway, the way a widow still sets two cups out.

"Soldier on the pier says she sails on the morning tide. Fourteen days from yesterday." The boy shifted his weight. "She takes everything, my mother says. The papers. The men. Old Diogo's boy, and the Costa brothers' cousin."

Rafel — no. Rafael set down the trimming knife with great care, because his hands wanted to drop it.

"Fourteen days," he said.

"Thirteen now," said the boy, who had clearly done this arithmetic already, several times, probably aloud, possibly to the mussels. "My father says tides don't wait for anybody. Not even captains."

"No," Rafael agreed quietly. "They don't wait for keepers, either."

The boy left the mussels on the sill — payment, apparently, for being listened to — and clattered back down the stairs. Rafael stood a long while at the rail, looking down at the harbor, where the requisition ship sat like a piece of the mainland that had broken off and drifted too close. Gray hull, gun ports closed, laundry strung along her deck like flags of surrender nobody had asked for.

Fourteen days. Thirteen.

He thought of the seized ledger sitting somewhere in the town hall under a soldier's boot or in a locked drawer, Elena's handwriting folded up inside it like a moth in a drawer of linens. He thought of the Okonkwo family carrying their beds out onto the street. He thought of two boys — hardly more than boys, Diogo's son with his crooked smile and the Costa cousin who once, memorably, fell asleep standing up during a funeral — who would be marched aboard that gray ship and turned into soldiers of an empire they had never seen and owed nothing to.

And beneath it all, patient as ballast, he thought of what Widow Ferran had shown him in the chandlery: the half-finished second ledger, the presses, the paper, Elena's gull mark stamped faint and stubborn on every page.

*Then we finish it,* he had said, with more courage than plan.

Now the tide had given him a number, and numbers were honest in a way that courage was not.

* * *

He went down to the harbor that afternoon and found Tomás mending nets on the quay, which is what Tomás did when there was nothing to mend, and also what he did when there was something he didn't want to discuss.

"It's true, then," Rafael said. "Morning tide."

Tomás pulled a knot tight enough to hurt somebody, someday, far away. "Aye. I heard it from the pilot's mate. She goes on the spring tide — deepest water of the month. They'll want all of it loaded the night before. Papers. Provisions." He did not say *men*, and the not-saying sat between them like a fourth person. "Two weeks, Rafa. Less."

"Can you get me the exact hour?"

"The mate will know. Why?" Tomás looked up, and his weathered face did something complicated. "Rafa. What are you planning?"

"I'm planning," Rafael said, "to count."

"That's never what you're planning. You're a man who plans things behind counting." Tomás sighed and went back to the net. "There's a council meeting tonight. Vane's clerk posted notice. He wants the townsfolk assembled so he can explain the schedule of prosecutions." He pronounced the word carefully, having only lately acquired it. "*Prosecutions.* Like it's a harvest festival."

At six o'clock the town filed into the hall that had been theirs for sixty years and was now, by virtue of a desk and a flag and one very tidy officer, Captain Aldous Vane's.

* * *

Vane had a blackboard.

This, Rafael thought, watching him arrange it before the assembly arrived, was somehow the most alarming thing about the man. Anyone could post soldiers at a door; it took a particular kind of conviction to bring a blackboard across the sea to a fishing town and chalk a timetable on it as though the future were simply waiting for someone organized enough to write it down.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Vane began, when the benches were full and the doors were guarded. "I will be brief, because I respect your time even if circumstances do not allow me to share it."

He tapped the board. On it, in handwriting so neat it looked printed, was a list of dates.

"On the twenty-ninth, the transport *Resolute* departs on the morning tide. Aboard her go the civic records currently under review, for audit at the colonial office. Also aboard go those persons whose conscription has been adjudicated." He did not look at the families of the two boys, which was its own kind of looking. "Between now and then, my office will conclude its review of irregular obligations recorded in your ledger. Those households found holding obligations contrary to statute will receive notice of disposition."

He paused, surveying them with the mild, patient expression of a physician delivering a diagnosis he considers merciful precisely because it is clear.

"I understand," he said, "that some among you regard this as theft. I want to be plain with you. Debt unrecorded is freedom for no one — it is merely fear with no end date. What we are doing here is *closing accounts*. When this process concludes, every person in this town will know exactly what they owe and to whom. That is mercy. Order is mercy. I have seen what happens where there is no ledger, no law, no schedule." His gaze went briefly somewhere else — a place none of them could follow, and perhaps did not want to. "You would not like it either."

Silence. Somewhere near the back, a baby made a small sound and was hushed.

Old Manik Okonkwo raised his hand, slowly, the way a man raises a hand when he suspects it will do no good but does it anyway out of principle.

"Captain. When you say the records sail with the ship." His voice was careful. "Our names are in those records. Our debts. Our grandmothers' debts. If they go to the colonial office — how do we answer them? From where? From a fortification wall, three hundred miles north?"

Vane considered him with what appeared to be genuine attention.

"You may petition in writing," he said. "Forms will be provided."

Manik lowered his hand.

And that was the whole of it, Rafael thought. That was the entire machine in one exchange. The names would sail, and the people whose lives were written in those names would remain ashore with a form, and the sea between would be filled with paperwork instead of water. Vane was not cruel — that was the terrible part. Cruelty could be argued with. This could only be *outworked*.

Afterward, in the doorway, Rafael found himself beside the captain quite by accident, the way one finds oneself in weather.

"Captain Vane."

"Keeper Marchetti." Vane inclined his head. "Your light burns well. I've noticed it from the ship. Regular as anything."

"You'd have liked Elena," Rafael heard himself say. "My wife. She kept the ledger before me. Everything in its column. Nothing out of place."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — recognition, perhaps, of a fellow creature of order, offered and then withdrawn.

"She sounds like she was an asset to this community."

"She was," Rafael said. "More than you know."

He walked home along the cliff path in the last light, and the number followed him like a gull. Thirteen days. Twelve, after midnight. He stood in the lantern room and did not light the lamp yet, though the sky was bruising toward dusk, and let himself feel the full weight of what the tide had told him.

It was not enough time. It was not remotely enough time. But it was a number, and numbers could be beaten the only way numbers ever are: one page, one name, one hour at a time.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of rope, tar, and — faintly, impossibly — of Elena's ink.

Rafael had been coming every evening since Ferran first showed him the workshop, sitting at her bench with the half-forged ledger open before him, teaching himself her cipher the way other widowers teach themselves their wives' recipes. He was slow. His letters came out stiff where hers flowed. Twice he transcribed a sum wrong and had to scrape the page with a razor, and both times he heard, distinctly, in the voice of memory, Elena laughing at him over some ruined draft of a manuscript years ago: *You hold a pen like you're afraid of it, Rafa. It's a pen. It has never once bitten anyone.*

"It has bitten me several times," he told the empty workshop now. "Metaphorically."

The door scraped. Ferran came in with a covered dish and the expression of a woman who intended to feed him whether he required it or not.

"You missed supper at my table," she said.

"I ate."

"Mussels on a windowsill is not eating. That is a bird's life." She set the dish down — fish stew, from the smell, heavy on the saffron because pity in Ferran's household always came with saffron — and looked over his shoulder at the page. "How many entries recovered?"

"Forty-one. Maybe thirty that would survive scrutiny." He rubbed his eyes. "Ferran. The ship sails in twelve days. With the ledger, with the boys, with every name in it. Even if we finished the copy, what good—" He stopped. Started again, honestly, which cost him something. "I sat through Vane's meeting tonight and I understood something. He isn't going to be argued with. There's no argument that reaches him. He believes he is saving us. You cannot argue a man out of a rescue."

Ferran ladled stew into a bowl and put it in front of him with the firmness of a woman setting a stone.

"So we don't argue," she said. "We finish."

"And then what? A copy proves nothing. He'll call it forgery, and he'd be right, that's rather the point of it—"

"A copy," Ferran said, settling onto the stool across from him with the slow ceremony of knees that had opinions, "is not for Vane. Think, keeper. You've spent forty years believing history happens in capitals. Where do you imagine the colonial office gets its account of this town? From Vane's ledger alone? No. Audits ask questions. Discrepancies stop ships." Her eyes glittered. "Elena knew that. Why do you think she kept duplicates? Not sentiment. *Leverage*. Two books that disagree make every official in the chain nervous, and nervous officials write letters, and letters travel faster than transports." She nudged the bowl closer. "Eat. And then tell me what you need from me tomorrow, because tomorrow I am collecting testimony from the Netmenders' Row households and you will want their sums in your hand before the week is out."

Rafael looked at her — at this small, iron, saffron-wielding woman who had spent decades feeding his town from a boat he'd never noticed — and felt the strange vertigo of belonging again, the sense of having been inside something all along without knowing its shape.

"Ferran," he said. "When Elena started this. The gull mark, the grain, all of it. Did she ever say why she didn't tell me?"

Ferran was quiet a moment, turning her spoon.

"She said keeping you clean was the last luxury she allowed herself," she said at last. "And then she said — this was near the end, mind, when she knew her heart was going before she told you — she said, *'One day Rafa will find the duplicate book, and he will finally understand that he was never outside any of it.'*" Ferran smiled, thin and fond. "Arrogant woman. She was usually right."

Rafael ate his stew. Outside, the tide turned, and the gray ship shifted gently at her moorings like a dog dreaming of running.

Twelve days. He dipped his pen.

By the time the lamp above him swept its first beam across the water, he had recovered nine more names — and when Ferran left, she took with her a list of every household in town, marked in Rafael's own hand, each name assigned a day and an hour to come to the chandlery and give their account.

"We'll need more hands than yours and mine," she warned at the door.

"Then tomorrow," Rafael said, "we start asking."

What he did not say — what he had already begun, that same night, to suspect — was that asking would turn out to be the easy part. Because word travels in a small town faster than any tide, and by morning there would be lamplight burning late in a window that no longer belonged to an abandoned chandlery, and someone, sooner or later, always watches a lit window and wonders what is being written there.

But that night he only wrote. Name after name, sum after sum, and beside each one, small and deliberate, he drew the mark he was learning to make his own: a gull, wings wide, flying against the wind.
