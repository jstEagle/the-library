## 11. The Informer

Captain Aldous Vane had a theory about lamplight.

In fifteen years of service in colonial ports from Bridgetown to Bombay, he had learned that a town's honest business was conducted in daylight. Lamplight after hours was where the irregular things lived — the unlicensed stills, the unregistered debts, the letters that should never have been written. Order, he believed, was a matter of illumination. What a garrison could see, a garrison could govern.

So when Corporal Hewett came to him at the customs house on the ninth evening of the occupation and reported "a light burning at the old chandlery, sir, past the point where any honest man would be varnishing anything," Vane set down his tea with the satisfaction of a man whose theories had just been confirmed by the universe.

"Who saw it?"

"Young Pellow, sir. He's been walking his girl along the quay because her father won't have him indoors since the requisition notice." Hewett hesitated. "The girl says it looked like — she said it looked like a workshop light, sir. Like people working."

"People," Vane repeated. The plural interested him more than the light. One man alone at night was melancholy; several men together at night was conspiracy. He had seen enough conspiracies begin exactly this way: a lamp, a shuttered window, a group of citizens who had discovered that they preferred their own company to that of His Majesty's representatives.

He rose, buttoned his coat, and felt the small, clean pleasure of purpose. These past days had been paperwork — endless interrogations of the seized ledger, cross-referencing obligations against rolls of assessment, deciding which families owed what to whom and whether what they owed could be converted into labor. It was necessary work. He did not enjoy it, precisely, but he believed in it the way he believed in drainage ditches and vaccination schedules: unglamorous instruments of mercy. Left untended, human obligation curdled into feud. He had watched whole districts rot from the inside over a goat sold twice in 1861. Better a clean accounting. Better an eviction decided by arithmetic than by whoever owned the loudest dog.

But paperwork did not burn lamps at midnight.

"Fetch Sergeant Bray," he said. "And four men. No more than that. I don't want it looking like an invasion."

Hewett glanced out the window, where rain was beginning to stipple the harbor. "It is an invasion, sir. Technically."

"It is a *requisition*, Corporal. The distinction matters to me." Vane took his hat from the peg. "Though I take your point. Keep it quiet. If half the town turns out to watch us kick in doors, we'll have a riot on our hands, and riots are terribly inefficient."

Outside, the rain had that coastal quality of falling sideways, and the lamplighters had given up entirely, so the streets belonged to the wind and anyone foolish enough to be abroad in it. Vane counted himself among the foolish but told himself it was duty rather than stubbornness. Behind him came Bray and the soldiers, boots ringing on wet cobbles, and ahead — there, just visible past the fish sheds — a thin amber line under the chandlery's door, glowing like a coal someone had tried to bury.

Vane stood before the door a moment and listened.

Voices. Low ones, indistinct through the wood, but unmistakably *voices* — several, murmuring in the cadence of people reading aloud and being corrected. Someone laughed softly and was hushed. The sound was so ordinary it was almost worse. Conspirators whispered; this sounded like a sewing circle.

He knocked.

The voices stopped at once, which answered most of his questions. There was a scramble, a thump, the scrape of something heavy — and then silence, thick as tallow.

"Open in the name of the requisition authority," Vane called, and winced slightly at his own phrasing. He had drafted the proclamation himself and had never quite managed to make it sing.

Inside, footsteps crossed the floor unhurriedly. Then the door opened on its hinges, and Rafael Marchetti stood framed in the lamplight, holding that lamp itself aloft like a man greeting carolers. He was wearing an apron spotted with ink to the elbows, and behind him the great room of the chandlery lay open to view — and utterly, magnificently empty.

Rope coils. Tar barrels. A forest of oars racked along the wall. Nets in various states of despair hanging from the beams. And in the center of the floor, one long workbench, swept clean, bearing nothing but a single cold cup of tea and, inexplicably, a dead gull laid out with a certain tenderness on a cloth.

"Captain," Rafael said pleasantly. "You're soaked through. Come in before you rust."

* * *

Vane entered the way he entered all rooms: completely, taking inventory without appearing to. Four men behind Marchetti — Ferran the widow, astonishingly, who looked for all the world as though she had been interrupted while knitting; the ferryman Osei; the cooper's daughter Prue, sixteen if a day; and old Sam Hollis, who had been deaf since the flood year and was now smiling at the wall. None of them would meet his eye except Widow Ferran, who met it with such cheerful defiance that Vane briefly reconsidered his position on riots.

"We received a report of suspicious activity," he said. "Unusual hours. Lights."

"Lights, yes," Rafael agreed. "I've been cataloguing the chandlery stores for the requisition inventory. Rope, tar, canvas, chain — all of it to be assessed before your quartermaster takes possession, I assumed. Nobody gave me a schedule, so I've been doing it evenings, when the shop's mine anyway." He gestured at the bench. "Widow Ferran has been helping me read the old markings. Her husband's hand. Half these barrels are marked in a code only the older families remember."

"That's true," said Widow Ferran serenely. "I can't account for young Prue, though. She's here for the gossip."

"Auntie," said Prue, mortified.

"And Mr. Hollis?" Vane asked. "Does he also remember the code?"

"He remembers nothing," Ferran said fondly. "That's why we like having him. Very restful."

Sam Hollis, catching his name, waved cheerfully at a barrel.

Vane walked the room slowly. Everything was plausible. That was what troubled him. Plausibility was the costume conspiracy wore to church. He ran a finger along the workbench — clean, recently wiped, smelling faintly of vinegar. He examined the tar barrels, sealed and stamped. He lifted a coil of rope and let it fall. Beneath the floorboards: solid. Behind the racks: stone wall. In the cold hearth: ash, ordinary ash, from ordinary driftwood.

The gull bothered him. It lay on its back on the cloth, wings spread, stiff and dry, prepared — he realized with a small lurch — for taxidermy. Its breastbone had been split neatly for mounting.

"Why is there a dead bird on your table, Keeper?"

"Elena's," Rafael said. Something moved behind his face, quickly banked. "She meant to have it mounted. I keep forgetting it's up on the loft shelf, and then I find it again, and it seems rude to put it back in the dark." He paused. "It seemed ruder tonight, somehow, to leave it up there alone."

Vane regarded the man for a long moment. Grief made a fine disguise; he had seen it worn deliberately by smugglers in three colonies. But grief also made men strange, and strangeness explained more midnight lamps than sedition ever had. He thought of his own mother, after his father died, keeping her late husband's coat brushed on its stand for eleven years. People kept things. It was not a crime.

Yet.

"Your lamp was reported by a citizen," Vane said at last. "I want you to understand that I am obliged to follow up such reports. It is nothing personal."

"I didn't think it was personal," Rafael said. "I assumed it was arithmetic."

Vane blinked. For a moment — a very brief moment, gone as soon as it arrived — he had the odd sensation of being understood, which he found more unsettling than hostility. Hostility he knew how to use.

"The chandlery will be included in the general search rotation," he said. "I'd advise against evening work. Accusations are like damp; they get into everything, and nobody believes you aired the place properly afterward." He turned to go, then stopped at the door. "The inventory. You said you were compiling it. Where is it?"

"In my head," Rafael said mildly. "I'm writing fair copies. You'll have them when they're accurate. An inaccurate inventory is worse than none, wouldn't you say? Wrong figures do more damage than no figures."

It was, Vane admitted privately, a good answer. It was exactly what he himself would have said. That was the problem.

Outside, the rain had settled in for the night. As his party splashed back toward the customs house, Vane glanced once over his shoulder. The lamp in the chandlery window had already gone dark. But the darkness behind the glass had a quality of held breath about it, and Captain Aldous Vane went home thinking about salt cellars.

He did not know why salt cellars. The phrase simply arrived, the way phrases do, and lodged.

* * *

Rafael stood in the dark chandlery until the boot-steps faded, then felt his knees inform him, politely but firmly, that they would like to sit down.

"That," said Widow Ferran, emerging from behind the rope rack where she had positioned herself with the instincts of a born tactician, "was the worst hour of my life, and I buried three husbands."

"You seemed perfectly calm."

"I was terrified *perfectly calmly*. There's a difference." She lowered herself onto the bench next to the cold tea and fanned herself with a barrel stencil. "When he picked up that rope I thought my heart would stop. And then he goes and looks at poor Elena's gull, and I thought, well, now we're done for, he'll see it — the mark, the bird, all of it, sitting right on the table like a signed confession—"

"He didn't see it," Rafael said quietly. "He saw a keepsake."

"Men see what they expect," Ferran agreed. "That's the whole art of it. Your Elena understood that better than anyone. She used to say the best hiding place is inside something sad, because nobody searches grief."

Prue had gone pale and stayed pale. "Mr. Hollis slept through the entire raid," she reported from the doorway. "He's snoring against the tar barrels. Should we wake him?"

"Let him sleep. He's earned it. He's the best actor among us." Rafael's hands were still trembling, and he studied them with clinical interest, as though they belonged to a braver man. They had spent the last quarter hour, during the knock and the scramble, doing something he had not authorized: shoving the ledger pages into the salt cellar.

The salt cellar was a great squat crock the size of a churn, built into the corner of the storeroom by some chandler who had believed, with reason, that the sea would one day make hunger a business. It held perhaps forty pounds of coarse grey salt. Beneath the salt, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the reconstruction — six weeks of testimony, memory, and Elena's duplicates copied out in Rafael's careful hand. Forty names so far. Two hundred entries. The sums of decades, the births and debts and grudges and quiet generosities of a town, packed down under a crust of preservative.

It had been Prue's idea, which surprised everyone including Prue. "Nobody checks the salt," she had said. "My mother's had the same crock since before I was born and Father's never once looked in it. Salt's like furniture. It doesn't count as hiding if it's always there."

Rafael retrieved the packet now, wiped the grains from the oilcloth, and held it a moment in the relit lamp's glow. Six weeks of work. Three nights' worth remaining, Ferran estimated, if the pace held — the last sections were the hardest, the famine years, where Elena's cipher grew dense and the duplicate entries thinned out, as though even she had begun to distrust paper.

"She was right, you know," Ferran said, watching him. "About hiding things inside sadness. That officer looked straight at your face tonight and saw a widower tidying a workshop. He'll write his report and it'll say *nothing found, keeper eccentric, harmless.*" She sniffed. "Which is more or less what the whole town's thought about you for years, dear, so there's poetry in it."

"There isn't," Rafael said, but he almost smiled.

Then the smile died, because through the rain-hammered window he caught movement on the quay: a figure hurrying past the fish sheds, collar up, moving with the particular hunched urgency of a man going somewhere he shouldn't. Tomás. Going, Rafael would learn within the day, toward the customs house — though he could not know that yet, and so what he felt was only a cold settling, the sailor's instinct that the weather had turned.

Because Vane's visit tonight changed everything. Until now the work had been secret in the way of all secrets — theoretically safe, practically invisible. Now it had a shape. Now there had been a report, which meant there was a reporter, which meant somewhere in a town of four hundred souls was a person who watched windows at night. Perhaps out of loyalty. Perhaps out of fear. Vane had been quietly interviewing households for a fortnight; everyone knew it; everyone knew too that a son's conscription papers could be made to vanish or multiply depending on a family's helpfulness.

Paranoia, Rafael thought, is the tax an occupation collects in advance.

"From tonight," he said, "no more than three of us here at once. Different nights. We arrive separately, we leave separately, and we never, ever walk here together." He looked around at them — the widow, the girl, the sleeping old man, the ferryman who had slipped out the rear door ten minutes before the knock, on pure instinct, and thereby escaped questioning entirely. "Ferran, you said the last pages need the cipher worked. Those stay with me. Nothing leaves this building except in salt."

"And the lamp?" Prue asked.

Rafael considered. Then he crossed to the window, drew the shutter almost closed — leaving, deliberately, a finger's width of amber light escaping into the storm.

"Leave it lit," he said. "A little. Every night, different rooms, different hours. Let them report it again. Let them come again. Let them find rope and tar and a grieving man and a dead gull until the words *chandlery* and *suspicious* wear smooth from use and mean nothing at all."

Widow Ferran looked at him with new appraisal. "Elena would have liked that," she said. "Making the watchers waste their eyes."

* * *

At the customs house, Captain Vane wrote his report by candlelight, in the neat hand of a man who had never once been accused of spontaneity:

*Chandlery premises investigated following civilian report. Found: stores consistent with declared inventory; five persons engaged in stock-taking; no irregular documents. Keeper Marchetti cooperative to a degree bordering on the theatrical. Recommend continued observation. Note: the town has begun watching itself, which is either very good news or the opposite.*

He sat back, re-read the final line, and did not strike it out.

Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the sentry coughed. Vane poured the last of the tea, gone lukewarm, and thought about salt cellars again, for no reason he could name, and about the way Marchetti had said *arithmetic* as if it were a language they shared. He disliked being shared with. It complicated the categories.

In fourteen days the requisition ship would sail on the morning tide, carrying the ledger, the evidence, the condemned conscripts, and the tidy solution to everything. Until then: observation. Illumination. Order.

He dipped his pen and added one line to the duty roster for the morning: *Interview the fishermen. Begin with the ones who have sons.*

Across the harbor, under a crust of grey salt, two hundred entries slept, waiting to become three hundred — and out on the black water, a small boat rowed toward shore carrying a man who had already decided, God forgive him, to talk.
