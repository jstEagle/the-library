## 12. Tomás's Confession

Tomás Peireira had been a liar for exactly eleven days, and it was the longest he had ever managed it.

He came by his dishonesty honestly — that is to say, badly. His wife had been able to read his face like weather. When he had once tried to surprise her with a birthday ring hidden in the flour bin, she'd known within the hour, not because she found it but because, as she put it, "You've been smiling at the bin, Tomás." He was a man built for open water and open speech, and concealment sat on him like a coat cut for another man's shoulders.

So it was that on the eleventh day after the raid on the chandlery, when the lamplighters of the town had nothing left to light and the suspicion had settled on nobody and everybody at once, Tomás found himself standing outside Rafael Marchetti's lighthouse in the blue hour before dawn, working up the courage to confess to a crime that had, so far, hurt no one — except possibly everyone.

He carried two mackerel as an offering. It was a habit. You did not go to a man's door empty-handed on this coast; your grandmother rose up in your throat and made you turn back. The fish were decent ones, caught the previous afternoon, and Tomás had spent half the evening deciding whether bringing them made the apology better or worse. He had concluded that it depended entirely on whether Rafael accepted them, which was the sort of circular reasoning that had kept him awake since the raid.

The door of the lighthouse stood open, as it often did now. Rafael kept fewer locks than any man in town, which Tomás had always considered either faith or foolishness, and lately he suspected it was grief wearing the costume of both.

"Keeper," Tomás said to the dark stairwell. "I've brought fish."

There was a pause, and then Rafael's voice came down the spiral, tired and dry as old rope: "Is this a bribe, a payment, or an apology?"

Tomás looked at the mackerel. "At the moment it's a conversation starter. The rest depends on how it goes."

"Come up, then."

* * *

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and storm. Rafael sat at the small table beneath the great lens, and the rotating light turned above him with its patient, enormous sigh, throwing its shadow around the walls like a man pacing a cell he had grown fond of. There were papers on the table — copies, Tomás realized, hand-inked, fragments of entries. The work had not stopped. That was something. That was also, Tomás thought grimly, exactly what he had reported, in his way, by pointing soldiers toward a building where such work happened.

"I'll say it plainly," Tomás said, setting the fish down, "because if I circle it I'll lose my nerve and we'll end up talking about the weather for an hour, which we could do anyway, and I'd rather get the bad thing done first."

Rafael said nothing. He had a way of saying nothing that filled a room. Tomás had watched him do it at council meetings for twenty years and had never learned to defend against it.

"It was me," Tomás said. "The informer. The lamplight at the chandlery — I told them. I told Vane's lieutenant where to look and when to look and that there'd be people there worth catching." His voice held, which surprised him. "Eleven days I've sat on it. Eleven days you've all been looking sideways at each other, and the Okonkwos sleeping in the church cellar, and Ferran pretending her rheumatism is why she doesn't come to the quay anymore, and it was me. All of it. Me."

Above them the lens turned. The beam swept out over the black water and came round again, faithful as breathing.

Rafael's face did not change. That was the worst part. Tomás had braced for anger — anger he could answer, anger was a language he spoke fluently, he and his brothers had fought their way through three childhoods on it. But Rafael only looked at him with those keeper's eyes, eyes that had watched ten thousand ships come home or not come home, and waited.

"You'll want to know why," Tomás said. "Everyone would want to know why, and the why is worse than the doing, I think. Or smaller. Smaller and worse."

He sat down across from Rafael without being asked, because his legs had decided the matter for him.

"They have my boy," he said. "João. My João, who is seventeen and thinks he is thirty and cannot keep his mouth shut in front of a uniform, God preserve him. They took him aboard the requisition ship eleven days ago. Not arrested — they didn't bother with arresting, that would require paperwork, and Captain Vane does love his paperwork — just *invited*, is how they put it. Invited to help the navy with some questions about sailors who'd gone missing from muster rolls. And he's been aboard ever since. They let me see him twice. Twice in eleven days. They give him bread and a hammock and they let him wave at me from the rail like it's a treat, like it's a day trip, and every time I walk away from that ship I leave a piece of my chest on the gangplank."

Tomás heard his own voice crack and hated it and let it crack anyway.

"The lieutenant — the thin one, Whitlock, the one who smiles like he's remembering a joke nobody told him — he sat across from me in the harbor office with a cup of tea I didn't drink, and he never once threatened João directly. You understand? Never said *your son will be conscripted*, never said *your son will sail east and you'll never*. Nothing so honest. He just talked about how boys mature at different rates. How some seventeen-year-olds are ready for a man's duties and some aren't. How the muster rolls are flexible things. How it all depended — he must have said *depended* forty times — on the cooperation of the local community." Tomás laughed, and it came out ugly. "Cooperation of the local community. Like we're a parish. Like we're all pulling together for the regatta."

"And so you told them about the chandlery," Rafael said quietly.

"I told them about the chandlery." Tomás pressed his fists against his knees. "I told myself a story while I did it, too, that's the part I can't forgive. I told myself it was nothing. Rope and tar and old women's memories, that's all that's in that workshop. I told myself they'd find nothing, that the ledger was safe wherever you'd put it, that I was being clever — giving them a little smoke so they'd stop sniffing for fire, keeping João fed and free, protecting the real work by sacrificing an empty room. And maybe some of that was even true. Maybe it was even good soldiering, the way Ferran plays daft for patrols. But here's what I know in my gut, Keeper, and I've known it eleven days and it's eaten me like salt in a hull: I wasn't being clever. I was being afraid, and dressing it up clever afterward. A man can lie to the army and still be telling the truth to himself, and I couldn't even manage that."

The light turned. Its shadow crossed Rafael's face, and in that sliding darkness Tomás saw something move there — not forgiveness, not yet, but the place where forgiveness might someday be built, cleared ground at least.

"Whitlock showed you João's name on a list," Rafael said. It wasn't a question.

"Aye. Two names down from Okonkwo's eldest." Tomás swallowed. "You knew?"

"I guessed. Vane doesn't take hostages; it offends his sense of legality. He takes *musters*. Every boy on that coast between sixteen and nineteen went onto a roll the week he arrived, and rolls are wonderful instruments, because a name on a roll is neither free nor conscripted until someone decides which, and the deciding can be traded on forever." Rafael rubbed his eyes. "It's the same arithmetic he runs on the evictions. Nothing cruel. Everything lawful. A machine that grinds slow enough that every man feeding it can tell himself he's only passing along a pebble."

"So you understand," Tomás said, and then immediately shook his own head. "No — don't let me have that. Don't you dare understand me too quickly, Rafael Marchetti, I've seen you do it, I've seen you find the soft explanation for everyone in this town for twenty years, widow and wastrel alike, and it's a fine quality in a neighbor and I'm asking you not to spend it on me today. Because here's the thing I need you to hear straight: I knew what the chandlery was. Not everything — I didn't know about the gull mark till Ferran told the cellar meeting like everyone else — but I knew it mattered. I knew Elena built it. I fished beside that woman for fifteen years and helped carry more than one crate that never saw a customs house, and when I handed Whitlock that information I handed him *her*, dead these four years, and you, alive, and every family she ever fed. I didn't sell a room full of rope. I sold all of us. João's bread cost more than I've got words for."

For a long moment there was only the light and the sea below, the endless working of the water against the rock, sound so constant you stopped hearing it and then heard it all at once, like your own heart.

Then Rafael did something Tomás had not expected. He reached across the table and took one of the mackerel.

"These are good fish," he said. "You cleaned them properly. Most men bring me fish like an excuse and haven't bled them."

"Rafael—"

"I'm not finished." But there was something in his voice now, a thread of warmth pulled through the weariness, and Tomás grabbed hold of it like a man grabbing a line in heavy seas. "Eleven days ago I sat in that workshop after the raid and I was furious. Not at anyone — at fog. At not knowing. And I thought, whoever did this, when I find them, and I spent a shameful night imagining finding them. And then the days went on and nobody was found and the town started eating itself instead, sideways glances, doors closing early — and do you know what I understood? Whoever it was, they were living inside it every hour. Whatever fear moved them, they were carrying it alone through a town that trusts each other precisely by not suspecting, which means they were drowning politely among friends." He turned the fish over in his hands, examining it as though it were evidence. "I didn't guess it was you. For what it's worth. I guessed everyone else first. Ferran, obviously, she enjoys deception far too much. Even young Marta. Never you."

"Because I'm too stupid to lie."

"Because you brought fish," Rafael said. "Nobody brings fish to a betrayal. Betrayals arrive with excuses and lawyers' phrases. You brought mackerel you'd bled properly. Your body's been confessing for eleven days; the rest of you finally caught up."

Tomás laughed despite himself, a wet, cracked sound, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "Elena would've known in a day."

"Elena would have known before you did," Rafael agreed. "She'd have come to *you*. She'd have sat in your kitchen and said, 'Tomás, you're going to do something desperate for João, so let's talk about it first,' and she'd have arranged the whole betrayal better than the army could, and Whitlock would have ended up holding an empty sack and paying for the privilege." A ghost of a smile crossed him and was gone. "I miss being known like that. Someone reading your weather before the rain arrives."

"She read everyone's weather."

"She did." Rafael set the fish down gently, as if laying it aside required ceremony. "Now listen to me, Tomás, because here is where we stand. You gave Whitlock a room, and the room was empty, and the ledger survived in a salt cellar, and the work continues — slower now, colder, but it continues. What you bought with your confession is time for João, and I will not pretend that's nothing. A father trading a little safety for his son isn't a traitor on this coast; it's Tuesday. Half the smuggling Elena ever ran was exactly that trade, done a hundred times over, grain for guilt, crates for children. You didn't betray the town's way of life, Tomás. You performed it, badly, under duress, with terrible self-forgiveness afterward. Which makes you nearly a textbook case."

"But?" Tomás said. There was always a but. He could hear it coming like weather off the water.

"But you don't get to be the only one who knows," Rafael said. "Not anymore. The cellar meeting — tomorrow night. You stand up and you say what you said to me. Not because we need your shame on display. Because right now the town suspects everyone, and suspicion is the thing Vane actually wants, the thing he's harvesting. A town that watches itself stops watching him. You can unpoison that well with one sentence, and João's safety may depend on a town that's watching the right enemy." He leaned forward. "And there's one more thing. You've been aboard that ship twice. You've seen the deck, the muster office, the watch rotations, the mood of the marines. Nobody else in this town has that. You're not just my informant now, Tomás — you're my intelligence on the requisition ship, and I need every plank of it. When she sails, and she sails soon, I intend to know her schedule better than her captain does."

Tomás stared at him. "Sails soon? Sails with what — the ledger?"

"With the ledger, with the Okonkwo sons, with whatever other names Vane has decided constitute irregular obligations. With João, if the trade goes badly." Rafael's jaw tightened. "Which is why I asked you here tonight, Tomás, and why the fish were unnecessary — though appreciated — and why your confession, painful as it was, is going to turn out to be the most useful thing anyone's given me since Elena died."

"How do you make that sum?" Tomás asked. "I inform on you and it comes out in my favor? That's not arithmetic, that's alchemy."

"It's bookkeeping," Rafael said. "The kind this town has always practiced. You write down what happened — all of it, the debts and the deliveries and the betrayals — and then you keep the page. You don't tear it out because it shames you. A ledger that keeps only the flattering entries isn't a record; it's a rumor." He pushed back from the table and stood, and the great lens turned above him, painting his shadow long across the floor. "Tomorrow night, the church cellar. You speak first, before I do, before anyone can wonder. Then you tell me everything about that ship. Every boat that services her, every officer who drinks ashore, the tide tables her bosun swears by. Can you do that?"

Tomás stood too. His legs felt strange, lighter, as though eleven days of ballast had been pumped out of him and he didn't yet trust the new draft. "Aye," he said. "I can do that."

"Good." Rafael picked up the second mackerel and handed it back to him. "Take this one home to your kitchen. You'll want breakfast tomorrow, and I want it on record that the Peireiras pay their debts in fish, promptly, even under occupation."

Tomás took the fish. At the top of the stairwell he stopped, one hand on the cold iron rail, and turned back.

"Keeper. The thing I told myself during — the story about the empty room, about being clever. Is any of it true? Any of it at all?"

Rafael considered him from beneath the turning light.

"Ask me again after the ship sails," he said. "If we win, it was cleverness. If we lose, it was fear. History gets written by the survivors, Tomás — and starting tomorrow night, we're going to survive on purpose."

* * *

The cellar was full before Tomás even reached the bottom of the stairs. Word had traveled, the way word traveled in a town of four hundred souls where secrets moved faster than fish prices: *Peireira's been seen at the lighthouse, Peireira looks like a man going to his own funeral.* Candles guttered on upturned crates. Widow Ferran occupied the best chair with the entitlement of seniority and rheumatism. Marta sat near the back with her arms crossed, and beside her the Okonkwo patriarch, whose eldest son's name sat two lines from João's on a muster roll somewhere aboard the gray ship in the harbor.

Rafael nodded to him once, and Tomás stepped into the candlelight, and his mouth went dry as a summer channel.

But his grandmother did not rise in his throat and turn him around. He had brought no fish tonight. Tonight he had brought only the truth, bled properly.

"My name is Tomás Peireira," he said, and his voice carried better than he expected, "and eleven days ago I told Captain Vane's men where to find you all."

The cellar went silent — but it was not the silence of a town breaking. Tomás had feared that silence, the sound of doors closing. Instead it was the silence of people leaning in, of a community taking a weight into shared hands to see if it would hold. And when he finished — João, the tea he hadn't drunk, the muster roll two names long — it was old Ferran who broke it, thumping her cane once on the stone floor.

"Well," she said, "at least it wasn't Marta. I lost two silver pieces on Marta."

Laughter, ragged and disbelieving, moved through the cellar like warmth returning to a cold house, and Tomás felt something unclench in his chest that had been clenched since the harbor office. He looked at Rafael, who was watching from the shadows by the stairs with an expression Tomás recognized at last: not a judge, not a forgiver. A keeper. A man recording everything, tearing out nothing.

Rafael stepped forward into the candlelight and unrolled a sheet of paper covered in cramped cipher, Elena's gull-marked hand transcribed and half-translated.

"Now," he said quietly, "listen carefully, because last night I finished decoding a page I believe Elena wrote in her final week. And it changes everything we thought we knew — not just about the smuggling, but about Captain Aldous Vane, and about why he was really sent to this coast."
