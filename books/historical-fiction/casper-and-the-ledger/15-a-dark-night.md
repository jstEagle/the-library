## 15. A Dark Night

Rafael had told himself a hundred lies in the weeks since the chandlery work began, but the one he treasured most was arithmetic: *ten minutes*. The excavation beneath the lighthouse foundation had gone deeper than he'd planned — the stones were mortared by some mason with a grudge against shovels — and the packet of letters had been wrapped not in oilcloth as Elena's cipher promised but in tarred canvas stitched shut like a wound. Ten minutes to open it, ten minutes to read, and the lamp would want only its routine trimming, which could wait, because the storm, though loud, was an honest storm, coming from the southwest where storms always came from, and the light would hold another quarter hour without him.

The letters were worse than he'd hoped. Vane's predecessor, a Captain Merrick, had taken payment from grain merchants to look away while requisitioned stores rotted on the quay and was sold back — *sold back* — to the very towns it was seized from. Elena had known. Elena had documented it. Elena had buried it under his feet while he trimmed wicks and complained about damp boots, and she had never said a word.

He read the third letter twice because his hands were shaking, and that is why he did not hear the ship come apart.

What he heard instead was Marta Okonkwo hammering on the lighthouse door as though the door owed her money.

"The *Providence*," she shouted before he'd fully opened it, rain swallowing half her words. "She's on the Teeth — Rafael, she's *breaking up* — where is the light?"

He looked behind him. The great lamp stood dark. Not dimmed. Dark. The wick had burned low in the untrimmed housing and guttered out sometime during the reading of the second letter, and the storm outside was so total, so black, that the absence of the beam made the whole room look like a photograph of itself.

He did not remember climbing the stairs. He remembered the smell of the match, the oil catching, the beam swinging out over water that was not water but chaos given a color. And he remembered — this would stay with him longer than anything else that night — seeing, far below, the small lantern of the *Providence* already underwater, its light glowing green through the waves like something alive trying to get home.

* * *

Marta Okonkwo was thirty-one years old, mother of two, widow of nothing yet, and until that night she had believed herself incapable of hating a man she liked. She had liked Rafael Marchetti. She had brought him fish soup when his shoulder ached in winter, and once, memorably, she had talked him into dancing at the midsummer fire, where he'd stepped on her foot twice and apologized in Italian both times, which she'd decided was charming.

Now she sat on the rocks below the lighthouse with a rope around her waist, held by six men from the town, because her brother Emile was on the *Providence*, and someone had to try, and everyone else had decided no one could.

"Let me tell you what I saw," she said later, when there was no later left to avoid, when the bodies had been laid out in the fish cellar and covered with sailcloth because there weren't enough proper shrouds. "I saw your light come on. I saw it come on *late*, Rafael. And I heard the men on deck cheering when it did — cheering, do you understand, because they thought they'd live — and then the wave came."

Three of the *Providence*'s crew of five survived: the boy Nils, seventeen, who had been thrown clear and washed into the harbor mouth; old Søren, who clung to an overturned dory for an hour; and the cook, whose name nobody could remember at first, which somehow made everything worse. Emile Okonkwo was not among them. Neither was Tomás's cousin Baptiste, who had taken Emile's shift so Emile could earn double for his sister's rent — the rent on the house the army had already marked for eviction, though nobody knew that yet except the Okonkwos and God.

The town gathered the next morning in the rain, which felt appropriate and also cruel, and it did not shout at Rafael. That was the terrible part. They simply looked at him. Widow Ferran looked at him the way you look at a ledger entry you've found in the wrong column. Old Søren, wrapped in three blankets, blue-lipped and alive, said nothing at all, which was louder than shouting.

"There was a wave," Rafael said, to no one and everyone. "There was a wave and the boat was too close to the Teeth in that weather. The light — the light buys minutes, not miracles—"

"Ten minutes," said Marta. Quietly. That was all. Ten minutes, and then she walked home through the rain, and Rafael understood that the sentence would follow him the rest of his life, arriving punctually whenever happiness threatened.

* * *

For two days he did not go down to the town. He kept the light — perfectly, obsessively, trimming the wicks every ninety minutes by his watch, sleeping in a chair beside the lens like a dog beside a grave — and he let the chandlery workshop sit dark, the reconstruction of the true ledger half-finished on its press, forty families' names still unwritten, the evidence against Merrick and the administration still smelling faintly of tar.

On the third day, Marta came up the hill.

He saw her from the gallery and considered, briefly and shamefully, not answering the door. Then he answered it, because hiding from her would be one more lie in the arithmetic.

"I'm not here to forgive you," she said. "I want that understood before I'm inside."

"Understood."

"I'm here because Emile is dead either way, and my boys need to eat, and your papers are the only thing standing between my family and the road." She stepped past him into the tower's lower room, dripping on his floor, and turned to face him with the expression of a woman who had rehearsed cruelty and found it insufficient. "Ferran says the ledger isn't finished. I've come to finish it. My hand is fast and my spelling is better than yours. Don't make this kind. It isn't kindness."

They worked, that first day, mostly in silence, broken only when Rafael misread a name and Marta corrected him with a flatness that stung more than any insult. But somewhere near dusk — he would never be able to say how — they arrived at the entry for the Okonkwo household itself, rents paid, debts forgiven by the gull mark during the famine year, and Marta stopped writing. She looked at her own family's name in Elena's careful hand, and her shoulders shook once, and Rafael pretended very hard to be examining an inkwell.

"He used to dance," she said finally. "Emile. Badly. At weddings he'd clear a circle just by starting." A wet laugh. "You'd have liked him. He forgave everybody. It was infuriating."

"I'm told forgiveness is infuriating," Rafael said. "I'm hoping to find out if anyone ever lets me practice it."

* * *

Widow Ferran came on the fourth day, carrying bread and the particular silence she used when delivering verdicts.

"The town is saying things," she began.

"I know what they're saying."

"You don't. You think they're saying *careless*. They're not. Careless would heal." She set the bread down and folded her hands. "They're saying a man who spent his whole life watching history from a lighthouse decided, at fifty-four, to become a historian, and the sea collected the fee. They're saying the letters weren't worth three men. Some are saying worse."

"And what are *you* saying?"

Ferran looked out the window at the gray sea, and for a long moment Rafael saw her as she must have been at twenty — the woman who'd rowed contraband grain past customs cutters, laughing, probably, the whole way. "I'm saying I started this," she said. "Elena and I. The ring, the mark, all of it. You inherited a war you never enlisted in, Rafa, and I am sorry for it. But I'll tell you what I told the council last night when they voted to ask you to surrender the papers." Her eyes came back to him, sharp as gulls. "I told them that Vane's mercy costs exactly what it looks like it costs. Give him the letters, and the evictions pause — pause — and the names still sail on the morning tide, and in two years a new Merrick writes new entries in a new ledger, and our children learn to read them. I told them the drowning wasn't the price of the truth. It was the price of waiting too long to tell it."

"That is a generous sentence for a man who let a lamp go dark."

"It's not a sentence," Ferran said, rising. "It's a fact, and facts don't absolve, they only orient. You were wrong, Rafa. Fatally wrong. Now stand up straight and be wrong about something else before the right moment passes."

She paused at the door. "Also, Marta has corrected your spelling of 'chandlery' four times. Take correction gracefully. It's the closest thing to grace anyone's offering."

* * *

That evening, Rafael climbed to the gallery and lit the lamp, and stood watching the beam sweep the water where the *Providence* had died. The sea was calm now, obscenely calm, wearing its violence the way Vane wore his legality — tucked away where the damage couldn't see its source.

He spoke aloud, to Elena, because there was no one left who would listen without flinching.

"You kept secrets to keep people safe, and you died before telling me whether it was worth it. I kept records to keep myself safe, and it took a war and a drowning for me to notice the difference." He pressed his palm to the cold brass of the lamp housing. "I don't know how to carry this. Marta won't let me put it down and the town won't let me hold it up. So I'll do the only thing I know. I'll finish the book. I'll write every name, including theirs — including Emile, including the cook, God rest him, *what was his name* — and I will carry it onto that quay myself if I have to."

The beam swung. Somewhere below, in the dark chandlery, Marta's candle still burned; he could see it through the trees, patient as punctuation.

It was Marta who came running up the hill an hour later, and the fact that she came at all — that she had chosen him as the messenger worth waking — told him the news before she opened her mouth.

"Boat in the harbor," she said, breathless. "Army cutter. Flying parley colors. There's an officer on the quay asking for you by name." Her face was unreadable, rain-wet, lit by the lighthouse beam turning above them like a slow clock. "Rafa — it's Vane. And he's alone."
