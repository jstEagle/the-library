## 17. The Town Decides

The cellar of St. Brigid's-by-the-Harbor had been built to hold candles, casks of altar wine, and the particular dampness that coastal stone collects like a debt. On the night Rafael Marchetti called the town together, it held instead forty-three souls, one storm lantern, and a smell of wet plaster and old incense that Widow Ferran declared was "exactly right for the occasion, whatever the occasion turns out to be."

Rafael stood at the head of the room beside Elena's duplicates — pages fanned across an upturned barrel like a card hand nobody wanted to be dealt. He had rehearsed a speech on the climb down the stairs. He abandoned it somewhere around the third step.

"You know why we're here," he said.

"We know why *you're* here," said Silas Crane, the cooper, arms folded. "We're here because you knocked on our doors at an hour decent folk are eating supper."

"Decent folk," said Ferran, "have been evicted. It thins the supper crowd."

---

From the back of the cellar, where he always stood when standing was required, Tomás watched Rafael's hands move over the papers. He had seen those hands a thousand times across a thousand ledger lines, steady as tide tables, and tonight they were not quite steady, and somehow that steadied *him*. The informer's confession sat in his chest like a swallowed fishhook. He had told Rafael in the chandlery two nights ago, and Rafael had not struck him, which Tomás half-expected, nor forgiven him outright, which Tomás fully feared, because forgiveness freely given is a debt you carry longer than a grudge.

His son Joaquim was fourteen and had shoulders like a young ox and eyes that followed soldiers the way gulls follow plows — hungry, wary, calculating distance. Vane's men had made their arithmetic plain: one boy's name on a conscription roll, or one fisherman's eyes on a lamplit cellar. Tomás had done what fathers do. He still didn't know if it was what men do.

"He'll ask us to vote," Tomás murmured to Marta beside him.

"He'll ask us to choose," she corrected. Her hands were wrapped around a mug of chicory someone had pressed on her, undrunk since the wreck. She had come off a boat that took four men down into the dark, and she had come anyway, because the alternative was staying home and letting the dark win twice. "Vane offers terms. Rafael's asking for something else."

"What else is there?"

Marta looked down the rows of heads — the Okonkwos with their youngest asleep across both parents' knees, the Crane brothers who hadn't spoken to each other since a disagreement about barrel staves that had outlived most marriages, old Pilar who was ninety-one and claimed to remember the last time soldiers came, though her claims about the past grew more elaborate every winter.

"There's us," she said.

---

Rafael laid it out plainly, because Elena had taught him — without ever once saying so — that plainness is a kind of respect.

He told them what Vane offered. Surrender the letters from beneath the lighthouse, the evidence against Vane's predecessor and the colonial administration above him, and the evictions would stop. The Okonkwos could keep Number Nine. Joaquim's name would stay off the rolls. A clean transaction, lawful on its face, merciful in its effect.

"And the ledger sails," Rafael said. "Every name in it. Every debt, every birth, every grudge and kindness written in your own hands over sixty years. It goes to an archive in the capital, gets stamped and shelved, and becomes what the government says it became. Irregular obligations. Evidence. And everything Elena wrote — everything the gull mark fed into your houses during the famine years, everyone who was kept alive by hands that never asked credit for it — all of it becomes contraband retroactively. Some clerk decides which of you were victims and which were criminals, and none of you are in the room when he decides."

Silence, except the drip of the cellar's permanent damp.

"Or," Rafael said, "we finish it. The true ledger. Three nights' work in the chandlery, maybe four. Every entry reconstructed, witnessed, signed by the people it belongs to. When the requisition ship sails, it carries Vane's seized book — and we hold the real one, with the evidence bound into it, copies already mailed to naval auditors in the capital. We make ourselves un-prosecutable by making ourselves undeniable."

"And if we're caught?" Silas Crane asked. "Midnight scribing in a requisitioned building. What's the word for that?"

"Treason," said Ferran pleasantly. "Or 'irregular literacy.' They haven't settled on terminology."

Laughter, small and startled, the kind that escapes before permission.

"I won't dress it up," Rafael said. "If it goes wrong, some of us go to prison. Maybe all of us. I've already cost this town—" his voice caught, and the whole cellar heard it catch, "—I've already cost enough. So I'm not going to decide. This stopped being my ledger the night Elena first put a name in it, and it has never once been only mine. It's yours. So you'll decide."

He set the lantern on the barrel so its light fell across the duplicate pages, and across the single sheet where Vane's terms were copied out in the captain's beautiful, merciless hand.

"All those for finishing the ledger — raise your hands."

---

Pilar raised hers first, before he finished speaking, which caused a brief parliamentary crisis about whether early hands counted.

"I buried a husband and two sons in famine years," Pilar announced, to a room that had heard this perhaps four hundred times. "Nobody buried them. That was the problem. If a woman with a gull on her page had been feeding us then, I'd have raised my hand for her from the churchyard." She kept her arm up with the endurance of the very old, who have learned that persistence outperforms strength.

Marta raised hers next, slowly, the left one, the one that still ached in weather. She did not explain. The room did not need her to.

Then Tomás, whose hand came up like something being pulled on a line, against its own weight, and who found that halfway up it stopped being heavy. Joaquim, sitting beside him, raised his too, and Tomás did not tell him to put it down, and that felt like the first honest thing he'd done in a month.

The Crane brothers looked at each other for the first time in two years.

"I'll vote yes if you do," said Aldous Crane — named, God help the town, after no relation to the captain but cursed with the name regardless.

"I was going to say the same to you."

"Then we're both voting yes and neither of us gets credit."

"Story of my life," said the other brother, and raised his hand, and his brother followed, and somewhere in the back Ferran wiped her eye and claimed afterward it was the incense.

One by one, then in bunches, the hands went up. Adaeze Okonkwo lifted her sleeping daughter's small hand with her own, and her husband laughed quietly and did the same for the son who was drooling on his coat. The blacksmith voted. The midwife voted. The man who sold bait and cheated at dominoes voted, and so did the man he cheated, which even Ferran admitted was generous.

When Rafael counted, his voice roughened on the number.

"Forty-one for finishing. One abstention." He looked at the abstainer — young Nils, the chandler's apprentice, who shrugged apologetically. "You want to say why?"

"I want it noted," Nils said, "that I'm abstaining because I'm seventeen and legally an idiot, and if this goes wrong my mother will kill me before the army can, and I'd rather die unforgiven by her than treasonous to either of you." A pause. "Also I'll be at the chandlery tomorrow at dawn to mix ink. Abstaining isn't absent."

"It is not," agreed Ferran, with approval. "It's the oldest trick in the book. I invented it."

---

* * *

Afterward, while chairs scraped and coats were gathered and Adaeze Okonkwo held her daughter a little tighter before handing her back to sleep, Rafael stood by the barrel collecting pages, and people came to him the way they used to come to Elena's kitchen table — sideways, casually, pretending to leave.

Ferran was last, or pretended to be last, which amounted to the same thing.

"She'd have liked tonight," Rafael said. "All of them, raising hands for her mark."

"She'd have hated it," Ferran said. "She'd have called it dangerous theater and worked twice as hard to deserve the applause." She patted his arm with a hand like a knotted rope. "That's the thing about belonging, keeper. It doesn't wait for you to be worthy. It just keeps signing your name until you grow into it."

Down in the corner, unnoticed, young Nils paused at the foot of the stairs and looked back at the room — at the lantern, at the pages, at the whole improbable congregation — for a moment longer than innocence requires.

Then he climbed the stairs into the night, and walked, not toward home, but toward the harbor, where the lights of the requisition ship burned steady through the fog, and a sentry at the quay straightened as a boy came toward him out of the dark, already talking.
