## 18. Three Nights' Work

**The first night — Rafael**

The chandlery smelled the way it had for forty years: tar, hemp, and candle smoke, with a new note underneath now — iron-gall ink and the sour breath of wet paper drying by the stove. Rafael stood at the head of the long workbench and counted his scribes the way he counted turns of the lamp mechanism: twice, and then once more to be sure.

Tomás, who wrote like a man rowing against a headwind but never stopped rowing. Marta, whose hand was small and exact, who had lost a husband in the wreck and come anyway on the first night without being asked. Widow Ferran, who claimed she could no longer see past her own nose and then transcribed an entire famine-year column from memory without pausing to blow her nose. Young Inés Okonkwo, seventeen, evicted from Number Nine and furious about it in the productive way, the way that sharpened pencils instead of tempers. And old Father Bram, who had declared from the pulpit that rendering unto Caesar did not include Caesar keeping your grandmother's debts, which was as close to a sermon on sedition as he dared.

"Six," Rafael said. "And me."

"And the gull," said Ferran, nodding at the rafters, where Elena's symbol had been chalked large above the bench. "Seven."

Rafael did not correct her. He had learned over the past weeks that grief was not a door that closed but a room you learned to furnish. He had put Elena's chair at the end of the bench, though no one sat in it, and no one moved it, and this seemed right.

They worked by three candles shielded behind tin, the shutters barred, the windows rubbed with tallow so lamplight wouldn't leak. Outside, the patrol boots came down Quay Lane at the quarter hour — Rafael could set his watch by them now, or they by him, he sometimes thought grimly, because the soldiers were nothing if not punctual, and punctuality was the only mercy Vane's arithmetic allowed.

The plan was simple, and simple plans were the only ones that survived soldiers. The true ledger — the one Vane had seized, full of Elena's gull marks and twenty years of grain that had never officially existed — would sail with the requisition ship unless something better took its place. Something complete. Something signed by every living hand that remembered the truth, so that when the naval auditors came — and Ferran swore they would come, once the evidence beneath the lighthouse reached the right desks in the capital — there would be no version of events left standing but the town's own.

They had fourteen days, then twelve, then nine. Now they had three nights.

"The last pages tonight," Rafael said quietly. "Famine years first. Then the births. Births last." He looked around the table. "Births last because we should end on the names we gave rather than the names we owed."

Nobody said anything sentimental, which was how he knew they all felt it.

* * *

**The first night — Tomás**

Tomás's hand cramped after two hours and he flexed it under the table like a boy avoiding chores. He had informed once — coerced, forgiven, and neither fact ever spoken aloud between himself and Rafael since, only folded into their working silence like a crease in a chart. His son Nico slept upstairs in the loft above the workshop now, which was either protection or hostage-taking depending on how you looked at it, and Tomás chose most hours to look at it as protection.

His assignment was the smuggling runs of '71 through '74, and here was the trouble: he had been eleven years old in '71, and what he remembered mostly was herring and hunger and his mother singing while she mended nets that were older than the town. But then he'd start writing, and the rest came back the way water comes back into a tide pool. The cove behind Gull Rock. The French boats that flew no colors. His mother carrying flour up the cliff path at night with a shawl over the sack so it wouldn't show white in the moonlight, singing louder on the dark stretches so the children walking with her wouldn't be afraid.

He wrote the names of the families. All of them. Even his own, even the entry that read *T. Almeida, sen., 40 lbs meal, paid in fish, refused charity, took it anyway.* His father had insisted that be recorded exactly so, and Tomás laughed out loud in the candlelight, a short bark that made Marta look up.

"Sorry," he whispered. "My father. He wanted the ledger to say he didn't want help. While taking it."

"That's half this town," said Ferran without looking up. "The other half is my half, which is giving it while pretending I'm being paid."

"You *were* being paid," said Inés.

"In complaints," said Ferran serenely. "Best currency there is. Never devalues."

Boots on Quay Lane. Everyone's pen lifted as one, like a flock startled off a wire. The steps passed. Twelve strokes of the harbor bell. Pens down again.

Tomás wrote until dawn grayed the shutter-cracks, and when he read back the night's pages his eyes stung, and he decided it was the ink fumes, and everyone let him believe it.

* * *

**The second night — Inés**

Inés had been given the births, and she resented it, because she wanted the dangerous work, the cipher work, the pages that would hang Captain Vane if the auditors ever earned their pay. Instead she got sixty years of christenings copied out of memory and parish books smuggled to her under Father Bram's cassock, which the good father referred to as "ecclesiastical privilege" with a straight face Inés deeply admired.

But somewhere past midnight, copying the births became something else.

She found herself, on line after line, writing mothers' names she knew. Her own mother's name appeared twice — once for her brother, once for herself, *b. 12 March, 1877, w. Rafael Marchetti attending* — and she sat very still for a moment, looking at her keeper's handwriting from six years before she'd ever spoken to him, before soldiers, before any of it. He had written her into a book when she was one hour old. She hadn't known. Nobody had known, really, what the ledger was, until men came to take it away, and then everybody knew at once, the way you know the worth of a well only when it goes dry.

"What?" said Marta, noticing her stillness.

"Nothing." Inés blew on the page. "It's just — he wrote me. When I was born. He wrote all of us."

"He writes everyone," said Marta. "That was always the trouble with him. Thought history happened somewhere else, in parliament or whatever, while holding the whole harbor in his lap."

Inés grinned and kept writing. Around three in the morning, boots stopped outside — not passing, stopping — and the whole workshop went breathless. A key scraped. Not the door; the salt cellar hatch out back, the one where the ledger hid during the raid. Inés's heart performed gymnastics her gymnastics instructor would have applauded.

Then a low whistle: three notes, up-down-up. Ferran's signal. It was young Pieter from the quay, bringing the pressed paper from the third hiding place, and also half a smoked eel, which under the circumstances was the most heroic delivery in the history of the resistance and was consumed entirely within four minutes.

"Eat," hissed Ferran, distributing eel like sacrament. "You cannot be martyred on an empty stomach, it's undignified."

* * *

**The second night — Ferran**

Widow Ferran had told Rafael she was too old for fieldwork, and this was true, and she went out into the fog anyway, because she had been lying about her capacities since 1861 and saw no reason to reform.

Her task was the decoy. Word had come down from Pieter that Lieutenant Hesse — Vane's terrier, a thin man who smelled irregularity the way sharks smell blood — had heard again about lights at the chandlery and meant to walk the lane tonight with two men and a lantern.

So Ferran put on her good black shawl, took up her cane, and positioned herself at the mouth of Quay Lane at half past eleven, groaning theatrically at a gatepost.

"Madam?" said Hesse, arriving precisely on schedule. Punctual people were so easy.

"Is that you, soldier? Thank heavens. There's a light. Up at the old rope-walk, I'm certain of it — moving, like a lantern being carried. Thieves, I shouldn't wonder. They've had their eyes on Widow Prue's copper boiler these three weeks—"

"At the rope-walk." Hesse squinted into the fog, away from the chandlery, toward the far side of the harbor where the derelict ropewalk leaned against the hill like a tired old dog.

"Moving light," Ferran confirmed, gripping his sleeve with the strength of four generations of women who had hauled nets. "You'll want to hurry. And mind the ditch past Cooper's Row, it's flooded, take the long way round by the fish sheds—" which added ten minutes and was, she felt, the true artistry of the whole operation.

Hesse and his lantern hurried off into the fog toward an empty building containing, as Ferran well knew, one copper boiler (Widow Prue's, genuinely at risk), several rats, and no thieves whatsoever.

Ferran watched the light recede, then stood a while in the cold because her knees had opinions about the gatepost. She thought about Elena, thirty years ago, standing in this same fog with a shawl over a sack of meal, laughing because the whole vast criminal enterprise of feeding the poor ran on old women, canes, and nobody ever suspecting anyone who complained enough.

"We're still here, girl," she told the fog, and went home the long way, whistling three notes up-down-up past the fish sheds just for the pleasure of hearing Pieter whistle it back.

* * *

**The second night — Vane**

Captain Aldous Vane did not sleep well in occupied towns. He slept better in fortresses, where the walls agreed with him. Here the walls were someone's houses, and they disagreed with him constantly, silently, in the way of all subject populations: by enduring.

He sat in the town hall with the seized ledger open before him and a cup of coffee gone the gray color of the harbor, and he tried again to make the numbers confess.

He was not a stupid man, whatever his subordinates feared he might be becoming. He understood irregular obligations; he had grown up watching his father, a customs clerk, keep two sets of books for a port master who was either a saint or a smuggler, possibly both. Order was mercy — he believed that the way other men believed in weather. Every unrecorded debt was a knife waiting for someone's throat; every name properly entered was a person the state could protect, feed, conscript, and bury with dignity. That some of those persons objected to the arrangement struck him as ingratitude, but forgivable ingratitude, the way children object to medicine.

What troubled him was the absence. For three days his clerks had cross-checked the seized ledger against testimony, and testimony kept arriving that matched entries Vane had never seen. A widow named Corregan, owed meal in '73. A birth recorded on a night no midwife would admit to attending. Small things. A shadow ledger, glimpsed at the edges, like a coastline seen from deep water.

"They're copying it," said Lieutenant Hesse, dripping fog onto his superior's floor. "The chandlery, sir. Lights. Ferran woman says rope-walk, but she would, she's — she complains, sir."

"Everyone here complains, Lieutenant. It's the local currency." Vane closed the ledger. His thumb rested on the cover a moment. He had begun, lately, to think of the book less as evidence and more as a hostage, and he distrusted his own sentiment. "Tomorrow night we search the chandlery thoroughly. Take the floor up. If there is a second ledger, I want it aboard the ship with this one."

"And if there isn't?"

Vane looked out the window at the harbor, where the requisition ship rode at anchor, patient as arithmetic.

"Then I will have disturbed a great deal of rope and tar," he said, "and the town will go on knowing something I don't, which is intolerable. Good night, Lieutenant."

After Hesse left, Vane poured the coffee out the window, watched it vanish into the dark water, and thought — briefly, traitorously — of his father's second set of books, and of the port master, who had been arrested to general approval and whose funeral, five years later, the whole town had attended.

He put the thought away with the others, in the drawer that was getting fuller, and slept badly.

* * *

**The third night — the press**

They nearly lost everything at eleven o'clock to a dropped inkwell — Marta's, her only lapse in three nights, and she apologized so gravely that Inés had to hug her, and for a minute the whole workshop paused in the smell of spilled iron-gall while a woman who had buried her husband three months prior was held by a girl who had been evicted, and no one said anything wise because there wasn't anything wise to say, only the hug, and then back to work.

By two in the morning the pages were done. All of them. Famine years, smuggling runs, births, debts forgiven, grudges settled (and several grudges, at Ferran's insistence, faithfully preserved, "for honesty"), the whole unwritten history of the harbor in forty-one quires of rag paper.

Then the pressing.

The chandlery's old book press still worked if two people threw their weight on the lever, and it was Tomás and Rafael who threw it, grunting, while Ferran supervised with the air of a general at a siege.

"Tighter. You've built ships, man, this is a ship, press it like a ship."

"It's a *press*, widow."

"Everything is a ship if it matters enough."

The boards went on, then the pages in signatures, damp and aligned, then more boards, and the screw came down, and the whole apparatus groaned like a moored boat in weather, and then it was simply a matter of hours — hours they did not have, because dawn was coming and with it Hesse and his thorough search of the floors.

"We bind at five," Rafael said. "Ship sails at eight. Vane walks the ledger to the quay at seven, per schedule. We finish at five, and at six I carry it to the hall myself."

Silence around the bench. It was Marta who said it, evenly, not accusingly, which made it worse and truer: "Carry it yourself. And then what happens to you?"

Rafael looked at the chalked gull on the rafter, at Elena's chair, at the six faces candle-lit around the bench, and he found — this surprised him, a little, like finding spring flowers under snow — that he was not afraid, or was afraid in the way of a man stepping off a solid quay onto a boat, which is to say afraid and going anyway.

"Then the town keeps my name in the book," he said, "the same as everybody else's. Fair's fair. I've kept all of yours long enough."

Nobody argued, which is how he knew they'd already decided to let him, and had probably decided it days ago, and had probably discussed it in his absence, and had probably voted. Belonging, he was learning, meant your sacrifices got committee-reviewed.

* * *

**The third night — five o'clock**

At five, the press opened, and the pages came out pressed flat and pale and smelling faintly of tar and candle smoke, and Father Bram — who had arrived for the binding with needle, thread, and the steady hands of a man who had sewn many things, mostly vestments and once, memorably, a sail — sewed the quires into their spine with waxed linen cord in the old manner, double-stitched, built for a century.

Ferran brought forward the covers: boards covered in gray sailcloth, and on the front, stamped in blind — no gold, nothing to catch a soldier's eye unless he knew to look — a single gull, wings spread, flying over one horizontal wave.

Elena's mark. Cut by Ferran from a template fifty years old, kept wrapped in oilcloth through famine, occupation, and one husband's stubborn ignorance.

Rafael set the finished book on the bench under the three candles, and the gull's wings threw a small shadow across the sailcloth, and no one spoke for a moment because there are moments that ask for silence and this was one.

Then Rafael picked it up, and it weighed what a book weighs and also considerably more, and he put on his coat, and Tomás opened the door a crack and checked the lane — empty, fog-thinned, the sky at the harbor mouth going the color of unpainted wood — and Rafael stepped out into the last hour of the old town's history with the true ledger under his arm.

Behind him, softly, three notes rose from the chandlery doorway: up, down, up. He answered without turning, whistling as he walked, an old man's tune carrying down Quay Lane toward the harbor, where the requisition ship lay at anchor, where Captain Vane stood at the town hall window watching a lone figure approach through the fog, and where, at the water's edge, the tide had already begun to turn.
