## 20. Trade at the Quay

Rafael had expected to sleep badly and was not disappointed. He lay in the keeper's quarters with his boots on, listening to the wind work at the gallery rail, and somewhere past two o'clock he gave up the pretense entirely and went down to the kitchen to make tea he would not drink.

The finished ledger sat on the table where he had left it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Beside it, Elena's cipher pages, copied fair into his own hand. Beside those, the letters from beneath the lighthouse, tied with the same bit of tarred string that had held them shut for twenty years.

Three bundles. He looked at them the way a man looks at a hand of cards he has been dealt at the very end of a very long night.

"You'd have laughed at me," he told Elena's chair. "You'd have said, *Rafael, you have arranged your papers beautifully and done nothing.*"

The chair said nothing, being a chair. But he heard her voice anyway, the way he always did in this kitchen — dry, fond, impatient with ceremony. *Well? The tide won't argue with you. It never has.*

He put the letters and the cipher copies into a second oilcloth packet, smaller, flat enough to slide inside his coat. The ledger he carried under his arm. Then he did something he had not done since the morning of her funeral: he stood at the little mirror above the washstand and made himself presentable. Combed the white hair flat. Buttoned the collar. If he was going to walk out and offer a captain of His Majesty's Army a trade, he would do it looking like a man worth trading with.

At the door he paused. On the hook where she had hung it for thirty years, Elena's shawl still smelled faintly of lamp oil and rosemary, or perhaps he only wanted it to. He took it down and draped it over his shoulders anyway, which was ridiculous, and which he did regardless.

* * *

The town at half past four was gray wool and salt. Gulls argued over the fish sheds. Somewhere behind him a dog gave up barking as an unprofitable career. Rafael walked the cliff path down toward the quay with the ledger under one arm and his heart going like a drum in a parade he hadn't agreed to join.

He had meant to slip through alone. He got as far as the chandlery before he found the road watched over by a small hunched figure sitting on an upturned dory, smoking a pipe that appeared to be mostly ceremonial.

"Widow Ferran," Rafael said. "It's half past four."

"It is," she agreed, making no move whatsoever to leave.

"Did Tomás tell you?"

"Tomás tells me everything. He thinks I don't notice, but he does." She drew on the pipe, which produced no smoke, having gone out hours ago. "You're going down there this morning."

"I am."

"To give yourself up."

"To make a trade," Rafael said. "There's a difference. Trades are respectable. My wife ran a smuggling ring for twenty years; I've had quite enough of things that aren't respectable."

Ferran considered him for a long moment. In the pre-dawn gray her face was all angles and patience, a face like the coast itself, worn down to what mattered.

"Elena walked that same path," she said at last. "The night they nearly caught the *Merrow*. I told her to stay away from the quay and she went anyway, and I stood on this exact spot and thought, that fool woman, that brave fool woman." She knocked the dead pipe against the dory. "I've decided I'm too old to be surprised twice."

"I'd rather you weren't seen with me."

"I'm seventy-nine years old and I'm sitting on a boat. Nobody has ever been arrested for sitting on a boat." A pause. "Take the shawl off before you get there, though. Sentiment is fine on a cliff path; it reads as defiance on a quay."

Rafael looked down at the faded roses over his chest. "She'd have wanted—"

"She'd have wanted you alive," Ferran said. "That's the thing about the dead, keeper. We spend so much time imagining what they'd want us to do for them that we forget all they ever wanted was for us to keep going. Shawl off. Go."

* * *

The requisition ship rode at anchor in the outer harbor, a black shape with its riding lights still burning, patient as a spider that knows the web will hold. In front of the customs house, a squad of soldiers had begun stacking crates — the seized ledger among them, Rafael knew, packed in straw like something fragile and precious, which in Vane's arithmetic it was.

Captain Aldous Vane was already awake. Rafael saw the lamplight in the customs house window and felt something loosen in his chest that might have been relief or dread; it was easier, somehow, that the man would be there. Easier than waiting.

Vane opened the door himself before Rafael could knock. For a moment the two men simply regarded each other across the threshold — the officer in his undress coat, spectacles pushed up into his graying hair, and the lighthouse keeper with a bundle under his arm and the east wind pulling at his collar.

"Keeper Marchetti." Vane's voice was carefully level. "It is five o'clock in the morning."

"The tide leaves at seven."

"I know when the tide leaves. I have known when the tide leaves since the day I arrived." Vane stepped back from the door. "Come in, then. Men who come at five o'clock in the morning want either to confess or to accuse, and neither business improves with daylight."

The customs house interior was spare to the point of monasticism: a desk, two chairs, a map of the coast weighted at the corners with musket balls. On the desk, in a neat stack, sat the requisition rolls. Rafael's eyes went to them and stayed there, and he saw Vane notice.

"Tea?" Vane asked.

"No, thank you."

"That's a pity. It's the one civilized habit I've kept." Vane poured two cups anyway, set one within Rafael's reach, and sat down. "Well. You've come to bargain. I can see it in how you're holding that bundle — men carrying Bibles hold them differently. Say your piece."

Rafael laid the ledger on the desk. He unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, because his hands were not entirely steady and slow hands look steadier than fast ones, and because — though he would never admit it — he wanted the moment to have some weight. The book sat between them: bound in sailcloth, corners pressed true, three hundred pages in eleven different hands, every name in the harbor town written fair and witnessed twice.

Vane looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he suspects is poisoned.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get this?"

"We made it. Fourteen days. The whole town. Every entry in your seized ledger, reconstructed from memory and testimony — and more besides, which yours never had. Signatures. Dates. Witnesses. The grain shipments. The famine years. All of it, accounted for honestly, by the people it happened to."

"This is forgery."

"This is a ledger," Rafael said. "Yours was the forgery. Yours recorded debts that were never owed and obligations that were invented by a man who took bribes from the same smugglers he was supposed to be hanging. I have his correspondence, Captain. Signed. Witnessed. Dated. It's on a mail boat to the Admiralty as we speak, along with copies of everything else, so burning this one will accomplish precisely nothing except to add arson to your difficulties."

The silence that followed had texture. Vane did not move. Outside, a soldier shouted something about crate weights, and the gulls answered.

"You are threatening me," Vane said at last.

"No," Rafael said. "I'm offering terms. There's a difference. Trades are respectable."

He slid the second packet across the desk — the cipher pages, the letters, the evidence against Vane's predecessor — but kept his hand on it.

"Here is the trade. You strike every name from the requisition rolls. Every family stays in its house. No evictions. No conscriptions. The Okonkwos go home to Number Nine before breakfast. Tomás's boy comes off your list. All of it, gone, this morning, in your own hand, posted where the town can read it."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange you take me." Rafael leaned forward. "One man, who admits to organizing the whole network. One man, whose confession matches this ledger, which you may keep as your evidence — it implicates me on every page; my hand wrote the headings. You sail with your prisoner and your proof, your report says order was restored, the irregular obligations were resolved, and everyone concerned gets to be promoted or commended or simply forgotten, whichever they prefer."

Vane sat back. His fingers had gone to the bridge of his nose, pressing, and Rafael realized with a small cold shock that the man was tired. Not performing tiredness — actually, humanly tired, in the way of someone who had been doing arithmetic with lives for weeks and found the sums wouldn't balance.

"You understand," Vane said, "that if I do this, and the Admiralty audit finds the discrepancy — if they learn the names were struck improperly —"

"Then you say a lighthouse keeper blackmailed you with forged evidence. Which is even true, depending on where you stand."

"It is not true from anywhere I stand."

"Then stand here." Rafael tapped the desk. "Captain. I've spent most of my life believing history happened in big rooms, in capital cities, to important people, and that the best a man could do was keep his lamp lit and stay out of its way. I was wrong, and it cost a fishing crew their lives for me to learn it, and I will carry that until I die, which may be sooner than I planned. But these names—" he touched the ledger "—these names belong in this town. Not in a straw-packed crate sailing to an archive where nobody who loves them can read them. Take me instead. I'm old, I'm a widower, I run a smooth operation — I am, if we're honest, exactly the sort of criminal your reports were designed for."

Something happened to Vane's face then that Rafael had never seen on it: it moved. Not much. A twitch at the corner of the mouth that might, in a different man, in different light, have been the ancestor of a smile.

"My wife," Vane said, "has been writing to me for six weeks asking why I cannot resolve a matter involving fishermen and flour without becoming—" he paused, selecting the word "—*monumental*. I have begun to suspect she is right." He straightened. He pulled the requisition rolls toward him and uncapped his pen, and for a long moment he simply looked at the first page, where the names began.

"If I strike them," he said, "it must be done properly. Irregular obligations resolved; subjects released from further obligation; recommended for exemption on grounds of administrative settlement. There will be an audit."

"There will be," Rafael agreed. "And it will find a ledger, a confession, and a captain who ended a smuggling ring with one arrest. That's not a failure, Captain. That's a commendation wearing plain clothes."

Vane wrote. He wrote for some minutes, the pen scratching, striking names line by line, and Rafael sat perfectly still and watched. When he reached the Okonkwo family he stopped.

"They are living in the net shed," he said. It was not a question; he had signed the eviction himself.

"They'll want to go home," Rafael said. "Today."

Vane struck the name. Then Tomás's son, with a note: *exempted, minor, sole support of siblings.* Then the rest, down the page, the ink drying in the gray morning light, and Rafael found that his eyes were stinging, which he attributed to the wind off the harbor and knew better.

* * *

They came out onto the quay together at half past six, which had not been the plan, but Vane insisted on doing the thing publicly — "Order that isn't seen," he said, "is just rumor with a uniform" — and by then the town had heard, because the town heard everything, and they lined the quay in the raw dawn like gulls on a rail.

Rafael walked ahead, the ledger handed over now, carried by two soldiers in a manner almost respectful. The crowd was silent. Then Marta Okafor, standing wrapped in a blanket at the head of the steps, began — quietly, terribly — to clap, and the sound spread down the quay like fire catching along a fuse, and Rafael kept walking with his jaw tight because if he opened his mouth he would disgrace himself in front of the army.

Tomás broke from the line and gripped his hand, both hands, crushing hard. "You great stupid brave man," he said, which from Tomás was a eulogy, a love letter, and an apology all at once.

"Watch the light while I'm gone," Rafael said. "Trim the wicks. Don't let Ferran talk you into anything adventurous."

"She'll talk me into everything adventurous. She always has." Tomás's grip tightened. "There'll be a boat. Whatever happens aboard — there'll be a boat."

"Don't be absurd," Rafael said. "Go home. Kiss your boy. Tell him the list lost."

Soldiers walked him up the gangway. At the rail he turned and looked back once — at the quay, at the crooked rooftops, at the lighthouse standing white against the brightening east, at the whole improbable town that had, without asking his permission, gone and made itself his.

Vane appeared beside him at the rail, hands clasped behind his back, watching his own crew cast off the lines.

"For what it's worth, Keeper," he said, "the audit will want to interview you. Formally. Possibly at length. The Admiralty keeps prisoners for such interviews in conditions I would describe as *maritime*."

"I've slept in worse," Rafael said. "I keep a lighthouse."

"Quite." The ship gathered way; the town began, slowly, to slide backward. "Though between us — I mailed my own report to the Board of Ordnance three days ago. Before your letters, before all this. Describing certain irregularities I had discovered in my predecessor's administration." Vane's face remained perfectly composed, but the corner of his mouth was doing that thing again. "It occurred to me that a man ought to have his paperwork in order before the auditors arrive. Whichever auditors. Arriving however."

Rafael stared at him. "You mean—"

"I mean," said Captain Aldous Vane, "that I suspect we shall both be interviewed, Keeper, and that it would be convenient for us both if our accounts agreed. Now." He produced a battered notebook. "Begin at the beginning. This smuggling network of yours. Tell me everything — and do try to make it dull. The truth, mind. But dull."

Ahead, out past the point, the open sea waited, gray and glittering — and far down the shipping lane, small yet, a sail that had no business on this station was standing in toward the harbor, flying colors Rafael did not recognize.

Vane saw it too. His notebook lowered slowly.

"Now *that*," he said, "is early."
