## 1. The Weight of Small Hands

The lamp had burned low enough that the oil inside it made a sound like breathing, a small wet pull at the wick every time the draft came under the door. Wendell Montrose did not look up from the net in her lap. She had learned, in four years of nights like this one, that looking up was a luxury, and luxuries were the first thing the Halloway Trading Company taught its indentured girls to do without.

The net spread across her knees like something drowned and washed ashore — which, in a sense, it was. River hemp, stiff with cold and the ghost-smell of fish, its mesh torn along the bottom third where a snag or a sturgeon's spine had done its work. She worked the wooden needle through the knots with her eyes half on the thread and half on nothing at all, because mending nets required only the hands, and the hands had long ago memorized the rhythm: pull, loop, draw tight; pull, loop, draw tight. The hands could be trusted. It was the rest of her that needed watching.

Outside, Brannock's Landing slept the way river towns sleep — not truly, but in shifts. Somewhere down on the ice a sled runner groaned against the frozen channel. A dog on Wharf Street gave three barks and thought better of them. The workshop behind her breathed with eleven other sleepers in the loft above: the slow tidal sound of girls who rose before dawn to sort pearl-shell and tally barrels and keep their heads so low they wore grooves in the floorboards with their downcast eyes.

Wendell was sixteen. She knew this the way she knew the date the ice usually broke — by counting backward from things written in ledgers. Her birthday appeared nowhere in the company's books. In those pages she was *Montrose, W., indentured, term seven years, debt of forty-one pounds twelve shillings sixpence*, and a ledger has no use for the day a child was born. A ledger has use only for the day a child becomes payable.

Forty-one pounds, twelve shillings, sixpence. She could recite it the way other girls recited prayers.

She pulled the knot tight and held her fingers near the lamp glass, not for warmth exactly — the warmth was thin and stingy, a beggar's warmth — but for the proof of feeling. In January her hands went a color she did not like to think about, a white that was not the white of pearls no matter what the poets said. Pearls were warm when they came up from the riverbed. Her father had told her that. Everything else about him lived in her memory like figures in a ledger, but that he had said, plainly, more than once, crouched on the dock with his palms open and dark with river water: *A pearl's been sitting in mud all its life, girl, but it comes up warm. Cold is only what never got held.*

He had been a diver. Not the kind the town romanticized — there were no songs sung about the pearlers of Brannock's Landing, no bright coins thrown to their children — but the real kind, the men and women who walked the river bottom in weighted boots with a rope around the waist and a stone in the arms, filling a net bag with mussel shell while the lungs screamed and the cold pressed in behind the eyes like thumbs. Eleven years he had dived the beds below the bluff. He had brought up shell, and shell meant pearls now and then, and pearls meant money, and money meant that Elias Montrose owed nobody.

Until he owed everybody.

The winter Wendell turned twelve, the company called in his debt — a debt she had never seen made, for gear advanced against future catch, for a boat repair, for the interest that grew in the dark of the account book like mold. Forty-one pounds, twelve shillings, sixpence. Her father had gone out on the ice in February to work the early beds alone, because a man in debt dives when the diving is bad, and the ice that year had been rotten from the first thaw-fog. They found his skiff pinned in the shifting floes two miles downstream, upright and empty, the net bag still tied to the thwart. The rope he dove with was coiled neat as churchwork.

The divers said the river had simply taken him, as rivers do. Aldous Pike, the company foreman, had said something else, in the yard, to another man, thinking no one small was near: *Convenient, that. Debt dies with the man unless there's kin. We'll bind the girl.*

We'll bind the girl.

Four years, and Wendell still heard it in Pike's flat gravel voice whenever the yard gate squeaked. She had signed — her mark, because she wrote a careful hand but her signature had not been asked for, only her obedience — and the debt of a drowned man had passed into her body like a fever. Seven years of service against the sum. Deductions for lodging, deductions for candles, deductions for the coat she wore and the bread she ate. Some months the ledger went the wrong direction entirely, and Pike took a sour pleasure in reading the new total aloud to her as though it were weather.

She did not hate him. Hatred required looking at a thing straight-on, and Wendell had made an art of the oblique. What she felt toward Pike and the company and the fine brick counting-house with its brass-lettered door was quieter and colder and, she suspected, more durable: she watched them the way you watch ice on the river. You note where it thins. You remember it until spring.

Pull, loop, draw tight.

The lamp guttered and she trimmed the wick with a fingernail, and in the fresh little bloom of light she saw her shadow thrown huge against the far wall — a hunched giantess with needles for fingers — and thought, not for the first time, that shadows were allowed to be large even when their owners were not. That was the whole discipline of her life, distilled: be small where they can see you. Be large where they cannot.

She had gotten good at unnoticed. Good at entering the mess hall last and leaving first, at being the girl the foremen reached past when handing out tasks either foul or favored. There was a freedom in it that she had discovered slowly, guiltily, the way you discover a loose board in the floor. Nobody asked the invisible girl to carry messages she shouldn't carry, or to smile at buyers she didn't want to smile at. Nobody remembered her name except the ledger, and ledgers never flatter, but they also never lie twice. She kept accounts for the workshop now, in the evenings after her own work — small neat columns in her small neat hand — and Mr. Halloway's junior clerks had twice remarked, in her hearing, that the Montrose girl figured true. They said it the way you'd praise a well-made scale. She had taken it as it was meant, and given back nothing.

Her father would have hated it. *You've got my eyes, Wendie, but your mother's spine* — he'd say it when she stood up to boys twice her size on the dock, before the fear came into their household like damp into plaster. Her mother was nine years gone, taken by the same river in a gentler season, a fever after a birthing that hadn't finished anything. Wendell kept both of them folded inside her the way she kept her needle: close, sharp, useful, never displayed.

But her father had also taught her to hold breath. Properly, the divers' way — not the gasping game of dock children but the true discipline, chest still, mind empty, letting the body become a thing the water tolerated. *The river doesn't take the ones who don't fight it*, he said. *It takes the ones who thrash.* She had not understood, at twelve, that he was giving her a lesson for the life she would actually live — that the company was a kind of water, deep and cold and patient, and that the girls who thrashed were the ones whose names got entered in the punishment column.

So she did not thrash. She mend nets. She kept her totals true. She let the winters pass over her like weather.

And yet.

There were nights — this was one — when the quiet endurance curdled into something with teeth. When the numbers in her head refused to stay numbers. Forty-one pounds, twelve shillings, sixpence, and how many knots was that? She had once tried to figure it: if each knot were worth a farthing against her debt, how many knots to freedom? She had filled half a slate with the arithmetic before understanding that the answer would break her heart by being too large, and had wiped the slate with her sleeve and never tried again. Some sums you don't perform. You survive instead of calculating.

The baby of the house — not a baby at all, but Petra Kessel, ten years old and sharp as a broken bottle — shifted in the loft above, and a shower of straw sifted down through the floorboard gaps and caught in the lamplight like slow snow. Wendell watched it fall. Outside, the wind had changed, come around to the south with a wet edge to it that hadn't been there at supper. Thaw-wind, the old divers called it, though it was weeks early. The ice would grumble tonight.

She flexed her fingers, blown red at the knuckles, and looked at the mended stretch of net laid out across her knees. Good work. Even meshes. No one would ever know her name from it, and that was the point of good work, wasn't it — that it held whether or not anyone praised it?

*A pearl comes up warm.*

"Stop that," she whispered to herself, and the whisper was shocking in the empty room, her first words aloud in — she counted — nearly two days. Her voice came out rusty, lower than she expected, like a hinge remembering itself. "Sentiment doesn't hold stitches."

But the lamp breathed, and the wind worried the eaves, and the river under all its ice — she could feel it even here, a vibration in the floorboards so faint it might have been imagination, might have been her own pulse — the river kept moving. It always kept moving. That was the terrible secret of Brannock's Landing, the thing the company's brass lettering never admitted: everything they built here, the wharves and the warehouse and the counting-house and the debts, all of it sat on top of something that never stopped moving and never forgave and always, eventually, broke free.

Her father had belonged to the river. The river had taken its due. And she — she belonged to the ledger, and someday the ledger would try to take its due too, and she did not know yet what shape that would come in, only that it would come, the way spring came, the way ice broke. Quietly for years and then all at once.

She tied off the last knot, bit the thread, and held the repaired section up to the light. Whole. She began rolling the net into its coil, working from the edges inward the way her father had taught her, so the mesh wouldn't kink — a dead man's habit living on in a living girl's hands, unbilled, unrecorded, hers.

Then she stopped.

Under the wind, under the river's buried mutter, there was another sound. Faint. Irregular. From the front of the building, from the direction of the workshop door that faced the lane and the wharf road and the frozen dark beyond it.

A knock. Soft — softer than a knock had any right to be, more like something falling against the wood than something striking it. Then silence, long enough that she almost let it go. Then again: a small, weak, doubled knock, like knuckles that had forgotten their strength.

Nobody answered. Nobody would. The other girls slept like the dead, and even awake, even in daylight, nobody at Halloway's answered a knock after hours — that was rule and instinct both, drilled into them by Pike's rages and the general law of the small, which is: *let whatever is outside remain outside, and be glad it isn't inside*.

Wendell sat very still with the rolled net in her lap and listened.

The wind came around again, wetter, and the lamp breathed in, and the river muttered under its lid of ice, and out in the lane, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something on the other side of the workshop door began — very quietly, very badly — to cry.
