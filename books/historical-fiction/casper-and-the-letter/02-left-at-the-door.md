## 2. Left at the Door

The cold came down off the hills that night like something with intent.

Wendell felt it before she heard it — a change in the pressure of the workshop walls, the old boards ticking as they contracted, the single pane above her cot frosting over in a slow fern pattern. January in Brannock's Landing did not arrive; it laid siege. By midnight the stove in the corner of the mending room had burned down to a red eye of coals, and the draft under the door ran across the floorboards like a thin river seeking the sea.

She should have been asleep. She was meant to be asleep. But sleep had never come easily to her in winter, and winter had settled into her bones the year her father went under the ice and never came up again, and now it lived there, a tenant she could not dislodge. So she sat wrapped in her coat at the long mending table with a lamp turned low, working a needle through a seine net whose meshes wanted re-knotting, and she let the rhythm of the work keep her company. Over. Under. Draw tight. Over. Under. Draw tight. It was the same motion her father's hands had made when she was small enough to sit inside the coil of his net and watch, and though he had been a diver and not a mender, all of them who lived by the river lived by the same arithmetic: one knot at a time, until you had something strong enough to hold what mattered.

The workshop slept around her. The Halloway Trading Company's net loft and drying shed and counting room were stacked three stories against the bank, leaning together like tired men sharing warmth, and on nights this cold every timber groaned and shifted and muttered in its sleep. Above her, in the loft where the spare nets hung in their great shrouded rows, the other indentured workers breathed in their bunks — old Halvorsen's wheeze, the twins' matched sighs, the silence of Mr. Pryce, who slept like a man rehearsing for something. Below her feet, the counting room lay dark and orderly, its ledgers locked away, its ink bottles stoppered. The whole building was a body lying still, and Wendell was the only part of it awake.

That was how she liked it. That was how she had learned to survive here: by being the part nobody thought about. A girl of sixteen with quiet hands and quiet boots and a face that asked nothing. Foreman Pike could walk the length of the mending table and not see her unless she willed herself visible, and she almost never did. Being unnoticed was a skill, like knots. She had practiced it four years.

The knock came at half past midnight.

It was soft — so soft that at first she folded it into the sounds of the building, the tick of boards, the wind worrying the shutter on the drying shed. Then it came again. Two knocks, spaced carefully, like someone trying very hard not to wake anyone and yet unable to leave without trying once.

Wendell set down her needle. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

Nobody knocked at the workshop door at half past midnight in January. The dockhands kept to the taverns until closing and then stumbled home. The company's business was done by lamplight in the counting room and sealed with signatures. And no one sane stood on a doorstep on a night when the cold could take a man's fingers before he finished deciding what to say.

The knock came a third time. Softer than the others. Almost apologetic. And then — nothing. No shuffling of feet, no voice raised against the storm, no third attempt. Just the wind, and the coal-eye of the stove pulsing, and the frost growing its ferns on the glass.

She waited a long while. Five minutes, perhaps ten. The sensible thing — the thing she had trained herself four years to do — was nothing. Whatever was outside the door belonged to whatever was outside her life, and her life was nets and ledgers and the narrow cot and the debt she served, and the fastest way to lose even that little was to be found awake and asking questions at half past midnight.

But her hands had already risen from the net. Her boots were already in her hands, so she would not wake the house with heels on bare board. She crossed the mending room the way she crossed everything: quietly, along the wall, breath held.

The bolt was cold enough to burn. She lifted it slowly, easing the metal so it would not sing against the strike plate, and opened the door onto the night.

The wind hit her first, a flat white hand pressed against her whole body, carrying grits of snow that stung her cheeks. The porch lantern — Pike only lit it when he expected a shipment, and he expected none till thaw — swung on its bracket, throwing light in drunken arcs across the steps. Beyond the steps the yard ran down to the ice-road and beyond that to the river, which lay black and white under its lid of frozen spray, groaning somewhere out in the middle the way it had begun to do these last weeks, a deep uneasy sound like a giant turning over in bed.

On the top step, centered precisely in the middle of the planks as if placed by a surveyor, sat a bundle.

Her first thought was that it was cargo. Someone had left a parcel — oilcloth, good quality, tied with cord — and the company dealt in parcels, and parcels were safe, and she could bring it inside to the counting room and log it in the morning and be invisible again by dawn. Her second thought arrived all at once and stopped her heart in her chest, because the bundle moved.

Not the way cloth moves in wind. Not the way anything else in the world moves. The oilcloth shifted with a small, rooting, hungry motion, and from within it came a sound Wendell had heard perhaps twice in her life and never once inside these walls: a thin, furious, newborn cry, quickly spent, trailing off into a hiccup and a silence that seemed somehow louder than the crying had been.

She stood on the threshold with the wind pushing at her back and the warm air of the workshop escaping past her ankles, and she could not move. Dread is a strange animal; hers had always taken the form of arithmetic. *A child*, said the voice in her head that sounded like Pike. *An infant on company property. A liability. A complication. A debt with no ledger line.* Every instinct honed over four years told her to close the door. To unsee it. To be, in the morning, exactly the person she knew how to be — the one who noticed nothing, who was noticed by no one, whose safety lived in the narrow space between other people's ignorance and her own silence.

Instead she knelt on the frozen step, and gathered the bundle into her arms, and carried it inside.

* * *

The weight of it undid her a little. She would remember that afterward — not the fear, not even the note, but the simple, shocking heaviness of the child, the way an infant weighs more than you expect, denser than any parcel, alive all the way through. The baby could not have been more than some few weeks old. Its face was screwed shut, its fists curled beneath its chin like two knots drawn tight, and under the oilcloth it wore a flannel gown that had been fine once and washed gray now, and it smelled of milk and woodsmoke and something faintly of lavender, as if someone had tried to make it comfortable before doing this terrible thing.

Someone had warmed the stones. She found them when she unwrapped the outer fold — river stones, smooth ones, heated and wrapped in flannel and packed around the child's small body. Whoever had done this had known about cold. Had known how fast it kills something this size. Had stood somewhere in the dark and heated stones on a stove and tucked them around a baby with deliberate, loving hands, and then had walked to the workshop door and set the child down and walked away.

Wendell got the stove door open and fed it two splits of birch, and the flames took hold with a rush that filled the mending room with orange light and the smell of resin. She dragged the child's cot — really a packing crate lined with sacking, kept near the stove for the warming irons — closer to the fire and laid the bundle in it, and the baby stirred and whimpered and then, in the heat, began at last to relax, fist by fist, breath by breath.

Only then did she notice the note.

It had been pinned to the oilcloth with a fishhook — a pearler's hook, she saw, the barbed kind the divers used, not the company's stamped tackle — and the paper was folded once, dry despite the snow, meaning it had been covered or cased until the last moment. She unfolded it with fingers that did not feel entirely like her own.

Three words, written in a fine hand, pressed hard enough into the paper that the letters had ridges she could feel with her thumb, as though the writer had needed the physical act of pressing to get the words out at all:

*Forget this face.*

No signature. No date. No plea, no explanation, no apology. An order. That was what made her breath go shallow — not a mother's farewell but an instruction, the kind of sentence that appeared in company correspondence, impersonal and absolute, the passive grammar of power. Somebody with authority had written those words, and somebody with grief or terror had carried the child through the snow to obey them.

Wendell looked from the note to the baby. In the stove-light the child's face had gone slack with warmth, lips working faintly at nothing, eyelids blue-veined and impossibly thin. It was not a beautiful face, exactly — infants' faces rarely were, all gums and fury and unfinished architecture — but it was utterly, defenselessly present. It had been put on a step in the worst cold in years, ordered to be forgotten, and it had no idea what forgetting meant. It only knew that the stones were going cool and someone must be near.

"I can't," Wendell told it softly, absurdly, her breath fogging in the cold room. "You don't understand what happens to me if—"

The baby opened its eyes.

They were dark, darker than seemed possible, catching the firelight in two points like the wet gleam of pearls fresh from the shell, and they fixed on her face with the grave, total attention that only the very young and the very old ever manage. They looked at her the way the river looked at the shore: patiently, without hurry, certain of eventually winning.

And Wendell Montrose, who had spent four years perfecting the art of looking away, found that she could not.

* * *

She went back out once, before she could think better of it, holding the lantern high against the wind. She told herself it was sense — that she needed to know what the yard could tell her, because come morning there would be questions, and she had better know the shape of the answers before Pike began shaping his own.

The snow told her plainly.

One set of footprints led from the gate at the top of the yard, down the trodden path toward the porch, and stopped. There they stood — two prints side by side, deeper at the toes, the impression of someone who had paused, standing still, bearing weight forward. Standing, she thought, on the exact spot where the bundle had rested. How long? A minute? An hour? Long enough to listen to a child cry and turn away from the door of the one lit room?

From those two prints, a single line of strides continued — not back toward the gate. Around the eastern side of the workshop, toward the lane that ran behind the drying shed and out past the cooperage to the river road. The stranger had not returned the way they came. They had chosen a path no window overlooked.

Already the snow was filling the prints. The fine granular cold-snow of deep January does not hold a heel long; by dawn the yard would be a page wiped clean, and by noon the forecast snowsqualls would bury even the memory of the marks. Whoever had planned this night had planned the weather too, whether by luck or knowledge. One set of footprints, already filling with snow. No second visitor, no accomplice, no witness. Just the one person, and the child, and the order.

Except — she lifted the lantern, turning in the wind, and swept the light along the fence line, the ice-road, the black shoulder of the warehouse next door. Nothing moved. Nothing showed. The river groaned out in the dark like something restless underneath its lid.

Still, she stood there longer than sense allowed, with the lantern guttering and her ears burning, gripped by a feeling she could not at first name. It was not quite fear, though fear was in it. It was the feeling of being watched by absence — of knowing that a person had stood exactly here, minutes ago, looking at this same door, this same light, perhaps at the shadow of her moving behind the frost-furred window — and that everything about them had been designed to leave no trace except the one they could not help leaving: the child.

They had forgotten one thing, though. Or perhaps they had not known. Wendell knelt at the edge of the porch light and saw it plainly, half-buried already: beside the deepest of the standing prints, a small dark stain in the snow where something had dripped — wax, she judged, tallow candle wax, yellowed, the kind the divers bought from Maren Oduya's stores because it burned true even in river wind. A diver's candle, dropped by a diver's boot.

She filed it away the way she filed everything, in the silent ledgers of her head, and went in, and shot the bolt.

* * *

The baby slept in the crate by the stove, and Wendell sat where she had sat all evening, needle in hand, net across her knees, and did not mend a single knot for a very long time.

She thought about her father. She did not often let herself, because grief was a luxury the indenture did not pay for, but the child's breathing — that small tidal sound, in and out, so like the river under ice — pulled it up out of her whether she willed it or no. He had been a diver too, before the debt took her; a pearl man of the old beds, with forearms like mooring cable and a laugh that could be heard across water. He had drowned in the spring break when she was twelve, when the ice went out early and took three boats and him with it, and the Company had been very prompt, very correct, and very cold in presenting her with the arithmetic of what he owed and what she would have to become to pay it. Four years of nets and ledgers. Four years of being no one.

*Forget this face.*

She looked at the note where it lay on the table, and at the fishhook pin glinting beside it, pearler's ironwork, river-made. She looked at the wax stain she had carried in on the memory of her own eyes. Somewhere out in the freezing town was a person who had heated stones and walked a sleeping child through the snow and stood listening on a step, and that person was bound up with divers and with orders and with a face somebody powerful needed erased — and Wendell understood, with the calm clarity that had always been her curse, that the note was not really addressed to whoever found the baby at all.

It was addressed to her. It was a test she had already failed, sitting here with her hands gone still and her heart gone over the wall.

Above her, old Halvorsen coughed in his sleep and rolled over. The birch collapsed glowing in the stove. The baby sighed, and turned its face — that face, the face worth ordering forgotten — deeper into the warmth of the flannel, toward her, and slept.

Wendell picked up her needle. Over. Under. Draw tight. But her mind was already moving downriver in the dark, past the ice-road, past the cooperage, out along the frozen flats to the cluster of houseboats and smokehouses where the diver families wintered — and to Maren Oduya, who bought tallow candles like the one that had melted into the snow, and who had known her father, and who owed the dead certain debts that the living sometimes came to collect.

By morning, Foreman Aldous Pike would find footprints filling with snow and ask his questions. By morning, the arithmetic would begin.

But that was morning. For now there was only the stove, and the storm, and the small hot weight of a life that had been left on her doorstep like a letter demanding an answer — and Wendell, mending nothing, keeping watch, answered it the only way she had left.

She stayed awake.
