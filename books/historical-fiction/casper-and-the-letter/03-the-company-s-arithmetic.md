## 3. The Company's Arithmetic

The baby slept through the whole of the next day, which was a mercy, because the workshop did not.

Morning brought the menders in at six, twelve women and three old men who took their benches without speaking, and the great room filled with the small industrious sounds of the Halloway Trading Company making its money: the click of net needles, the rasp of twine through wax, the cough of Tobias Finch two benches down, who had been coughing since before I was born and would go on coughing after, I suspected, out of sheer habit. The oilcloth bundle lay where I had left it, wedged in the shadow behind my barrel of tarred cord. All night I had kept my hand on it the way you keep a hand on something in a dream, certain it would be gone by morning, or worse, that it would cry.

It did neither. It breathed. That was all. A small rising and falling under the wrappings, patient as a tide, and I found that if I matched the rhythm of my needle to it — stitch, stitch, breathe; stitch, stitch, breathe — I could work without my thoughts running off the edge of anything.

I had decided, in the gray hours, on a story. Stories were currency in the Landing; everyone traded them, and the good ones were simple. Mine was this: I had heard a knock, opened the door, found nothing but snow. That was true as far as it went, and the best lies are only truths with one board pried up. If anyone asked why there were wet boot-prints inside the door that weren't mine, I would say the wind had blown drift in through the gap and I had mistaken it for a visitor. People wanted to believe a girl like me saw nothing. Being unnoticed was my trade, and I meant to practice it now with all the skill my years had bought.

But Aldous Pike did not ask. That was his way. He never asked.

He came in at half past seven, when the room was warm with bodies and the lamps still burning against the white light at the windows, and he made his slow circuit of the benches the way a man walks a fence line looking for the gap. Pike was not large. He had been, once — there were photographs of him young, broad as a dock bollard, from his days hauling pearl crates on the wharf — but age and arithmetic had narrowed him, worn him down into a shape that fit easily between shelves. His face was the color of old ledger paper, ruled with lines where disapproval had been written so often it had scarred. He carried a slate pencil behind his ear and touched it, always, when he was calculating something about a person. I had seen him do it a hundred times: touch the pencil, look at a worker, look away. Somewhere behind those eyes, numbers moved. Tonnage, wages, waste. The company's arithmetic ran in him day and night, and everything alive that came within reach of it got entered somewhere, weighed, and priced.

He stopped at Finch's bench first. He looked at Finch's hands, then at Finch's output basket, then touched his pencil. He moved on. He stopped at Mrs. Harrow's bench and picked up a length of finished gill-net, held it to the window light, found the one knot she had snugged wrong in forty yards of mesh, and set it down without a word, and Mrs. Harrow went gray to the lips. He moved on.

I stitched. Breathe. Stitch. The bundle behind my barrel did not stir.

Then he stopped walking, and I knew, before I saw what he saw, that it was over. There is a particular silence a room makes when everyone in it realizes the foreman has noticed the same thing at once. The needles did not stop — we were too well trained for that — but the sound of the room changed pitch, went thin, the way air goes thin before a squall comes down the river valley. Because there on the floor beside my barrel, half out of the shadow where I had tucked it, lay the child's other wrapping: a flannel blanket, mine, that I had used in the night and left folded too near the edge. It was plain as a flag lying there. No one in this workshop owned such a thing anymore. Indentured girls did not own soft flannel.

Pike looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at me. Then he crouched, with the care of a man lifting something heavy from a boat, and picked it up between two fingers, and held it the way you hold a dead rat by the tail.

"Montrose."

"Sir." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Fear does strange bookkeeping of its own; it debits your legs and credits your voice.

He did not ask what it was. He walked to my barrel, set the flannel on top of it, and waited — just stood there with his hands clasped behind his back, touching the pencil once, twice — until I understood that he intended me to move it myself. To show him myself. That was the cruelty of Aldous Pike: he made you do your own betraying. He saved the company the cost of labor even in this.

I reached behind the barrel and drew out the bundle.

The oilcloth had shifted in the night and come open at the top, and the child's face was visible — that impossible, unremarkable face, red-cheeked and sleeping, her fist curled against her cheek as though she were holding something no one could see. The room behind me had gone very quiet indeed now. Twelve needles clicked alone in the silence, and then even those slowed.

"Well," said Pike.

Not *what*. Well. As if I had turned out my pockets and shown him a handful of river stones. He leaned in and studied the baby the way he studied a consignment of fish: assessing condition, weight, likely market. She chose that moment to open her eyes, and I watched Aldous Pike look into the face of an infant he had every reason to fear, and I watched him find nothing there worth entering in any column. To him she was not even contraband yet. She was an expense that had not happened.

"Where."

"On the step," I said. "Last night. After the bell. I heard a knock and—"

"There is no entry for a delivery." He said it flatly, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Nothing came through the gate ledger. Nothing came off a boat. Therefore nothing is here." He straightened up. "Except that here it is. Which means somebody has made an error, Montrose, and errors cost." He touched the pencil. "How long have you held indenture?"

"Four years in June, sir."

"And your father's debt stands at—" he closed his eyes briefly, consulting some interior page, "—eleven pounds, nine shillings, sixpence, less what your labor's retired. Call it nine pounds outstanding." He said my father's debt the way other men said the weather. "You've three years to run. Room, board, candle-oil, your keep against the company books. Do you know what an infant costs per week? Milk. Wet-nurse or goat, either way milk. Cloth. Coal in winter. Sickness — and they're always sick, they bring fever through a house like smoke through a net." He shook his head slowly, and for one strange moment he sounded almost weary, almost human, a man tired of the world's refusal to balance. "A child is a liability, Montrose. On any honest ledger, a liability. And this company keeps honest ledgers."

Behind me, someone's needle snapped. Nobody laughed. Nobody breathed, either, except the baby, who yawned enormously, entirely without fear, showing her toothless pink mouth to the ceiling of the Halloway Trading Company as though it were merely another sky.

"It isn't mine," I said.

"I didn't say it was." Pike picked up the flannel again, folded it — actually folded it, corners squared, the gesture so automatic and so gentle that it frightened me more than anything else he had done — and laid it back on my barrel. "Doesn't matter whose it is. Found property on company premises becomes company property. That's the ordinance. And the company doesn't carry liabilities it didn't choose." He looked at me over the bundle, and his eyes were pale and clear as river ice, and I understood that he was not cruel, that this was the horror of him, that he was a man who had spent so long converting the world into columns that he could no longer see a living thing standing in front of him without weighing it. "There's a family up on Chandler's Row lost their youngest last autumn. The wife still has milk. They'd take it in, might even pay a finder's consideration. You'll take it there tonight."

*They'd take it in.* Something in my chest went hard and cold, harder than the ice on the river, colder than the step where I had found her. I looked down at the small face, at the fine dark hair plastered to the small skull, at the fist still clenched around its invisible thing, and I thought: *she was left with a note that says forget this face. Forget it. And the first thing this company wants is to make her someone else's forgetting.*

"Tonight," Pike repeated. "After the supper shift. Take the lane behind the cooperage, it's shorter. And Montrose—" he was already turning away, already moving toward Finch's bench, the arithmetic pulling him onward, "—don't let it cry in here again. Sound carries. Carries to the office."

He meant: carries to Mr. Halloway. He meant: if the owner learns there is an unentered child under this roof, the error will be corrected at the source, and the source may include a sixteen-year-old girl who has nine pounds outstanding. He did not say any of it. He never had to say any of it. That was the genius of Aldous Pike, that he could ruin you with a sentence about lanes and ledgers and leave your name clean for the record.

He reached the far end of the room. He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and without turning around he said, quietly, so that only the nearest benches heard:

"They're liabilities, children. Every one. I was one myself once. Best thing that ever happened to me was the day somebody put me on a ledger and made me worth something." And he went out, and the cold came in behind him, and the door shut.

* * *

The rest of the morning I worked with my heart going like a fish line pulled taut, and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one wage nobody can garnish: part of me meant to obey.

I did. I swear it. Part of me — the part four years of indenture builds, the part that knows the price of everything because you've watched prices paid in skin and breath — that part stood obediently ready at the supper shift with the baby wrapped and the flannel folded and a story prepared for the woman on Chandler's Row, and I got as far as the door. I stood in it. The snow had stopped sometime in the afternoon and the evening sky was doing that terrible beautiful thing the sky does here in deep winter, burning low and orange along the river gap while the rest of it went iron, and the cold outside was the kind that finds the space between coat and throat in the time it takes to cross a threshold.

And the baby woke up in my arms. She woke, and did not cry, and looked up at me — at my chin, because her eyes couldn't travel further yet — and made a sound. Not a word. A sound. The smallest possible announcement of a self: *here. I am here.* And her fist came loose from whatever it had been holding all day and fastened instead, with astonishing strength for something so new, on the lapel of my coat.

She held on.

I stood in the doorway of the workshop that owned three more years of my life, holding a child the company's foreman had priced as a liability, and I did the arithmetic myself. Nine pounds outstanding. Three years to run. Milk, cloth, coal, sickness. Set against: one note in my apron pocket that said *forget this face*, and whoever wrote it, and the certainty — grown enormous in me over one long day, fed on every small thing I had ever seen the company absorb and erase — that a child whose own kin ordered her forgotten would not survive being handed to strangers on Chandler's Row. Not this winter. Maybe not any winter.

The company taught me figures. It never asked whether I'd use them.

I stepped back inside and shut the door.

* * *

The net loft ran the length of the building above the workroom, reached by a ladder behind the drying racks — a low dark country under the roof beams hung with nets in various states of death and repair, smelling of tar and hemp and old river. It was cold up there, but it was private, and privacy was the rarest commodity the company sold, and it sold it to no one. In summer the loft baked; in winter it held the chill of the roof slates like a cellar. But it had one virtue: no ledger line ran to it. Pike counted nets, not lofts. For four years it had been my place to sit alone at noon with bread I wasn't supposed to carry up. Now it would be hers.

I made a nest of it in the far corner behind the stacked cork floats, where the roof sloped lowest and the drafts died. I took the worst of my own blankets and did not feel the sacrifice, and then felt it acutely around two in the morning, and did not take it back. I rigged a screen from a hung seine so the lamplight — a stub of candle, nothing more; flame near dry hemp is a madness I did not allow myself, so mostly it was dark up there and I learned her face by feel — wouldn't show through the roof vents. I learned the schedule of her small body the way my father learned the riverbed: by patience, by repetition, by keeping my mouth shut and my eyes open. When she needed feeding I carried warmed milk up from the workroom stove in a company flask I fully intended never to return, and if that was theft then let the record show it was my first, and I have never once repented it.

The second night, lying beside her in the dark with the nets swaying faintly overhead like drowned weed, I heard her breathing slow into sleep, and I listened to it the way other people listen to church bells, and I understood that something had permanently changed in me. Fear was still there — fear sat beside me every hour, cold and patient as Pike's pencil — but it had changed places with something. It had gone from being the whole of me to being only one voice in me, and a new voice had spoken up, quiet and flat and absolutely certain, and the new voice had said: *no.*

Not *no, I'm afraid.* Just no. The first no of my life that I had said out loud, in my own head, where the company could not enter and levy it.

Because here is what I had worked out, lying awake in the loft while she breathed: the note said forget this face, and I could not. Whoever left her — whoever wrote those careful, freezing words — had wanted her erased, and the Halloway Trading Company, which had erased my father's drowning into a debt and my mother's grief into a line item, was the finest erasing instrument on this river. Hand her to Pike's machine and she would vanish as cleanly as a figure struck through in red ink. And someday, maybe, someone would come asking after her. Someone with money. Someone polite. And there would be nothing to find, because that is what companies do with things that cost more than they earn.

Well. Let them come and audit the net loft.

On the third night, I heard footsteps below — Pike himself, doing his late rounds, lantern swinging, his boots crossing the floor beneath me in their slow measuring rhythm. He stopped directly under my corner. I lay rigid, one hand resting feather-light on the small warm rise and fall of her side, and I listened to Aldous Pike stand alone in the empty workroom for the better part of a minute, saying nothing, calculating God knew what. Perhaps he smelled the milk. Perhaps he saw the missing blanket. Perhaps he simply knew — men like him always simply know — and was deciding what column to enter it in.

Then he touched his pencil — I heard the small scrape of it, absurdly loud in the dark — and walked on, and his lantern went out through the far door, and he did not come back.

He knew. I was sure he knew. And he had chosen not to see, and I could not decide whether that was mercy, or contempt, or merely the arithmetic — a girl and a baby hidden in a loft costing the company precisely nothing, whereas an incident on the books cost time and paper. Whatever his reason, I lay back in the dark and found I was shaking, and found also that underneath the shaking was something I had no name for yet, something with edges, something that felt like the river must feel in February, all of it still frozen on the surface and moving underneath.

The baby sighed in her sleep and gripped my finger, and held on.

Three nights later I wrapped her in the flannel and climbed down into the blue end of the afternoon and went looking for Maren Oduya — because a hiding place is only a pause, not a plan, and my father's oldest friend was the only soul on this river who had never once, in all her days, been afraid of the Halloway Trading Company.
