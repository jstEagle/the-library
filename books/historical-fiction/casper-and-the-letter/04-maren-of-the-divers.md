## 4. Maren of the Divers

The divers' camp sat below the town like a secret kept at the water's edge — a scatter of tar-paper shanties and beached skiffs on the mudflats south of the wharves, where Brannock's Landing ended and the river began to belong to itself again. Wendell had not gone down there since her father's funeral, three years past. The path was still there, though: a ribbon of frozen mud stitched with cinder, winding down the bluff through bare willows, and her feet remembered it even when the rest of her wanted to forget.

She carried the baby beneath her coat, against her chest, wrapped in the woolen shawl she had bought with three months of mending coins from Peddler Voss's cart. The morning was bitter enough that her breath cracked in the air, and she had told herself the whole walk down that she was carrying the child this way only for warmth. That was a lie, and she knew it. She carried her that way because letting go felt dangerous — because the weight of those small hands against her breastbone had become, in two days, something her body had begun to plan around.

*Forget this face,* the note had said. Wendell had not forgotten it. She doubted now that she ever would.

* * *

Maren Oduya's shanty stood at the far end of the camp, closest to the water, as if the old woman wanted the river to know exactly where to find her. It was built of driftwood and salvaged hull planking, silvered by weather until it looked less constructed than grown, and a rack of diving gear stood beside the door like a sentinel: the copper helmet, dented and dull, its round glass eyes clouded with age; the breastplate; the coiled air hose hanging in slack loops, like something asleep.

Wendell had seen that helmet a hundred times in childhood, gleaming brass-bright while her father oiled its joints. Seeing it now, green with verdigris, made grief rise in her throat like river water.

She knocked. Her knuckles barely touched the wood before a voice inside said, "It's open, girl. Been open since you started down the hill."

Inside, the single room smelled of lamp oil, dried fish, and the particular mineral coldness of a house that lived close to water. Maren sat in a chair made from a barrel top, her hands busy with a netting needle even though no net lay before her — just working the shuttle through empty air, keeping her fingers honest. She was small and dark and folded up like a knot, gray-threaded wool wrapped round her shoulders, and her eyes were the deepest brown Wendell had ever seen, so dark they seemed to hold depth the way the river held it: you could look into them a long time and never find bottom.

Those eyes went to Wendell's face first. Then to the bulge of the shawl at her chest. Then back up.

"Emory Montrose's girl," Maren said. Not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Hm." The netting needle did not stop. "He said you'd have his patience. Said you'd sit still longer than anybody he ever met. Sat in the bottom of a boat four hours once, six years old, watching him work a bed, never said a word." A pause. "Come in, then. Shut the door. You're letting my fire hear the winter."

Wendell shut the door. The stove in the corner was a rusted potbelly, breathing heat through its seams, and beside it a kettle ticked as it warmed. She stood uncertainly, dripping snow onto the packed-earth floor, until Maren jerked her chin at a stool.

"Sit. And unwrap whatever it is you've smuggled in here under your coat, because I've been expecting a baby for two days and I imagine you're tired of pretending you aren't carrying one."

Wendell's breath left her all at once. "You— how could you possibly—"

"Girl." Maren set down the needle at last. "I'm seventy-one years old. I've pulled drowned men out of that river and put living babies into the arms of women who couldn't feed them. Word travels downriver faster than the current. There's talk at the fish market. A child left at Halloway's workshop door in the night, in the cold, and nobody in this town claims her? Nobody comes looking?" She shook her head slowly. "In Brannock's Landing, that silence has a shape. And that shape has money in it."

Wendell sat. With fingers gone clumsy from cold, she worked the shawl loose, and the baby stirred — woke halfway, blinked her astonishing dark eyes at the firelight, and made a small sound like a question.

Maren leaned forward in her barrel chair. For a long moment she simply looked, and Wendell watched the old woman's face do something complicated: soften and harden at the same time, tenderness and calculation arriving together like two sides of one wave.

"Well," Maren said quietly. "Look at her."

"She was left at the workshop door," Wendell said. "Two nights ago. In oilcloth. With a note pinned to the blanket." Her voice thinned. "It said to forget her face."

"And did you?"

"No, ma'am."

Something moved through the deep of Maren's eyes — approval, or recognition, or both. "No," she said softly. "I didn't think you would. Your father couldn't forget a face either. Couldn't forget an injustice, couldn't forget a debt owed or a kindness given. It's what killed him, likely, and it's what kept every poor soul on this river loving him anyway." She reached out one gnarled hand, palm up — an offering, not a demand. "May I?"

Wendell hesitated. Then, carefully, she rose and laid the baby into Maren's arms.

The old woman held her the way a diver handles a pearl: sure-handed, reverent, turning her gently toward the light. The baby stared up at her with that grave, unblinking attention, one small fist working free of the blankets to grasp at the fringe of Maren's woolen wrap.

"Strong grip," Maren murmured. "Good lungs — she'll cry when she means it. Somebody fed her regular before whoever it was walked her through the snow to your door." Her thumb brushed the infant's cheek, feather-light. "Somebody loved her, and then somebody buried her. Those are two different somebodies, most times. Or sometimes they're the same body, and that's a sadder story than either."

"I don't know who she belongs to," Wendell said. "I don't know anything. Only that whoever left her wrote *forget this face*, and that Mr. Pike wants her out of the workshop by morning, and that—" Her voice caught on the edge of everything she had been holding since the door, since the snow, since the note. "That I can't do it. I can't put her back out in the cold. They'd dock my wages for losing my place, they'd extend my indenture, they'd— but I can't do it. So I came here. Because Papa trusted you, and because I don't trust anyone else in this town farther than I can throw them."

Maren was quiet a long time. The stove popped. Outside, the river muttered under its ice, a sound like a giant turning over in sleep.

"Your papa brought me pearls from beds I couldn't dive anymore," Maren said finally. "Bad knees, bad ears — the water takes us apart piece by piece, year by year, and we keep going back because there's nothing else that feels like standing on the bottom of the world and looking up through forty feet of green light. He brought me my last good season's take when my own hands were too stiff to work a bag. Didn't ask a share. Just said, 'You looked after me when I was green, Maren. Debt's mine.'" She looked up, and her gaze was steady and terribly kind. "So whatever you ask of me tonight, understand that I'm not doing it for you. I'm paying Emory Montrose back. That's the arithmetic of this river. We don't forget faces here either."

"Then you'll help me hide her?"

"I'll help you keep her alive," Maren corrected. "Hiding's part of it. But hear me now, girl, and hear me plain, because your father's patience in your face doesn't make you ready for what I have to say." She shifted the baby to one arm and leaned forward, and the firelight made her shadow loom huge across the driftwood wall. "Whoever left that child did not leave her to be found. They left her to be *erased*. Understand the difference. A person leaves a baby where someone will find her — a church step, a family porch, a warm kitchen with lamps lit. They don't carry an infant half a mile through falling snow to a locked workshop on the company lot, in the dead hours, and pin a note to her blanket telling the finder to forget her. That's not abandonment. That's disposal."

The word landed in Wendell's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it struck.

"You think—" Her mouth was dry. "You think someone meant for her to die."

"I think someone meant for her to never have existed," Maren said. "And that the person who carried her here instead — the somebody who loved her — chose the one door in this town where a soft-hearted net-mender works late nights alone. That somebody took a terrible risk, betting on a stranger's heart. And somebody *above* that somebody — somebody with money and fear enough to hire it done — will not stop at a note pinned to a blanket. Listen to me." Maren's hand closed gently around Wendell's wrist, and her grip, though old, was strong as river stone. "They will come back. Not for gratitude. Not ever for gratitude. They will come back for *silence*. Because that child is not just a burden to them, she is a *witness*. She is proof, walking around in the world with their blood in her face. And people with things to bury don't rest until the ground stops shifting."

"But she's a baby. She can't testify. She can't tell anyone—"

"They're not afraid of what she'll say," Maren said. "They're afraid of what her face says without her opening her mouth. Blood tells, girl. Ask any family that's tried to hide a child in the wrong cradle." She released Wendell's wrist and settled back, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "I'm not saying this to frighten you. I'm saying it because you need to decide, right now, awake and clear-eyed, what you're about to do. Because if you keep her — if you truly take her up, not for a night, not for a week, but *keep* her — then you're not just breaking company rules. You're stepping between that child and whoever wants her gone, and you're doing it with nothing but your two hands and my old bones behind you. There's no walking away after. Once you're between, you're between."

Wendell looked at the baby — at the fine dark hair escaping the shawl, the small fist still tangled in Maren's fringe, the utter, unguarded seriousness of that tiny face. Two nights ago she had been a girl who kept her head down, whose whole art was being unnoticed, who had learned that survival meant making yourself small as a stitch in a seam. Some other girl might have handed the child to the church orphanage and let the ledger close.

But the church orphanage sent its unclaimed infants to families upcountry, unasked and untraced, and the note had said *forget this face*, and somewhere in this town or above it someone was waiting for news of silence.

"I already decided," Wendell said quietly. "Two nights ago, when I picked her up. Everything since then has just been me catching up to it."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a slow, creased thing, like sunlight finding cracks in a riverbank.

"Good," she said. "Then we've work to do, and not much night left to do it in. She stays here nights, in my cot, where no company man sets foot. The divers watch this camp better than the town watches its jail. You'll bring her at dusk, take her back before dawn so nobody marks the coming and going, and in between you'll act exactly as dull and dutiful as you've always been. Can you do that? Be invisible?"

"It's the one thing I'm good at," Wendell said.

"Mm. Well. You may have to get better at the opposite, before this is done." Maren rose, creaking, and began moving about the little room with brisk purpose — pulling a tin basin from a shelf, a bundle of soft rags, a clay jar of milk-thistle and dried goat's milk from the cache beneath her cot. She moved with the economy of a woman who had provisioned expeditions all her life, only this expedition was an infant, and the territory was a town full of people who might be hunting her. "Here. Feeding schedule. She'll want goats' milk warmed to blood-heat, not a degree more. Rags boiled daily. Keep her out of drafts and away from anyone who asks too kindly after your health."

She pressed the supplies into Wendell's arms, and then paused, and did something Wendell did not expect. She bent, slowly, painfully, and pressed her lips once to the baby's forehead — an old woman's benediction, brief and fierce.

"There," she said roughly, straightening. "Done. She's got river salt in her blessing now. That's more protection than most of us ever get."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, blue shadows pooling among the shanties, smoke rising thin and straight from a dozen stovepipes. Somewhere down the beach, men were hauling a skiff above the tide line, their voices carrying flat across the ice. Wendell stood in the doorway with her arms full of supplies and her heart full of something she didn't yet have a name for — this warmth, this sudden weaving of strangers into a rope, this first thread of a family not born but *built*.

"Maren." She stopped at the threshold. "The note said 'forget this face.' Do you really believe they'll come back?"

The old woman did not answer at once. She looked past Wendell, out at the darkening river, where the ice stretched shore to shore, white and patient and full of hidden movement, groaning now and then like something enormous dreaming.

"I believe," she said, "that in thirty years on this river, I have never once known the water to give back a secret without wanting something in return. Whoever put that child on your doorstep owes the river a secret, girl. And folks like that — folks with pearls and ledgers and good names to protect — they always, always come to collect." She turned back, and her dark eyes found Wendell's. "Keep your lamp lit low tonight. Lock the workshop door. And if you hear footsteps in the snow that walk soft and stop often — you run for the water, and you don't look back."

Wendell walked home along the frozen path in the last light, the baby warm against her chest, the supplies bundled under her arm, the river muttering behind her. Above the town, windows were lighting up yellow one by one, and the great dark mass of the Halloway warehouse stood against the sky like a headstone.

She did not see the man leaning at the rail of the coal wharf, hat brim low, watching her small figure descend toward the lights of the company lot. She did not see him push off the rail, unhurried, and start down the hill behind her, his boots silent in the fresh-fallen snow.

But the river saw. And the river, patient as it was, had already begun to count the days until spring.
