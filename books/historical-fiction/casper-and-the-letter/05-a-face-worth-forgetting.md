## 5. A Face Worth Forgetting

The cold in Brannock's Landing had a texture to it, Corliss thought — not the clean bite of the northern counties where he'd learned to hunt, but a wet, river-fed chill that got into the joints of a man's hands and stayed there like an old debt. He sat in the back room of the Ferryman's Rest with his boots propped on a rail, a glass of something brown and cheap sweating on the table beside him, and he did not drink it. He never drank on the job. That was the first rule his father had taught him, back when the job had been rabbits and the money had been honest.

The room smelled of lamp oil, old tobacco, and the particular sourness of a river town in February — fish scales and coal smoke and the green rot under the ice. Through the floorboards came the murmur of the taproom below: a fiddle badly tuned, laughter with too much whiskey in it, the eternal argument about the spring flood. Every year they argued about it. Every year the ice went out and took something with it — a dock, a shed, some years a man — and every year the town rebuilt in the same foolish place and argued again.

Corliss turned his glass a quarter-turn on the tabletop and watched the lamplight swim in it.

Three days now since the night at the workshop door.

He had been paid, in advance and generously, to make sure that no one would ever have cause to speak of what happened on the fourteenth of February, in the year of our Lord 1893, behind the Halloway Trading Company's net-mending shed. The instructions had been simple enough that even a stupid man could have followed them, and Corliss was not a stupid man. Take the child from the woman's house on Marsh Lane after midnight. Carry her to the workshop door — the one place in Brannock's Landing where a foundling might be taken in without questions, because the company men who slept there were paid not to ask them. Leave her where the cold would do its work quietly if charity failed, as it usually did. Walk away. Forget this face.

That last part was written down. Folded twice, in a fine hand, in ink the color of dried blood. *Forget this face.* He'd read it so many times the creases in the paper had gone soft as cloth.

He had done everything as instructed. He had carried the bundle — lighter than any man would guess, lighter than a sack of meal, warm through the oilcloth against his chest — down the frozen lane with the snow squeaking under his heels like new leather. He had set her on the step, tucked against the door frame where the wind couldn't get at her easy. He had knocked, once, soft, the way you knock when you want the door answered but not too fast. And he had walked away without looking back, which was the second rule his father taught him: *a man who looks back starts remembering, and a man who remembers is worth nothing to anyone.*

He was halfway across the yard when the child cried.

Not a wail. Nothing so demanding. Just one thin sound, small as a needle, punched through the quiet of that frozen night — and Corliss had stopped, boot half-lifted, snow already filling his own tracks behind him, and he had stood there in the dark like a fool and listened until the sound stopped on its own.

Then he had walked on. He had walked on.

* * *

The trouble was that the child had not died.

Corliss knew it the way a hunter knows a trail is cold or warm — not by any single sign but by the accumulation of them, small and certain. On the morning after, he had made his circuit of the town the way he always did, hat low, buying nothing, greeting no one, listening to everything. A town like Brannock's Landing was a single animal with ten thousand mouths, and if you fed it silence it would talk anyway, out of sheer hunger for a topic.

He heard nothing at first. A dead infant found at a doorstep was the kind of story that traveled — sad, brief, told in the low voice people used for drownings and fevers. Its absence should have been the loudest thing in town.

By the second day he understood the shape of it. No body. No burial. No gravedigger at the Fenchurch yard drinking off his wages and complaining about frozen ground. No constable's entry, and Corliss had paid the constable's clerk two bits to let him glance at the book. Nothing.

Which meant the child had been taken in. Which meant she was alive. Which meant somewhere in this soggy little river town, wrapped in somebody's coat, sleeping in somebody's drawer lined with flannel, was a face he had been paid to forget — and had not.

He thought about the crying. One thin note in the dark. He hated himself for thinking about it, and he thought about it anyway, all day, while he walked the streets and looked at windows.

* * *

The employer did not come in person. Of course not. In three months of work Corliss had seen nothing but letters — the same fine hand, the same blood-dark ink, arriving care of the Ferryman's Rest, addressed to a name that wasn't his, containing sums that were. He answered the way he was told to answer: by leaving a chalk mark on the third piling under the north end of the toll bridge when he had news, and a different mark when he didn't. Within a day of the mark, a letter came. It was an arrangement built for men who would never shake hands, and Corliss had worked such arrangements before. They ended one of two ways, and neither way was with a drink between friends.

Tonight there was news, so tonight he had put up the mark, and tonight — as expected — the barkeep had slid him an envelope along with his untouched glass, saying only, "This come for Mr. Cole." Mr. Cole. His third name in as many months. He wondered idly whether the employer believed in the aliases or simply enjoyed the theater of them.

He broke the seal now, alone in the back room, angling the paper toward the lamp.

The hand was as elegant as ever, and colder than the river ice.

*Mr. Cole —*

*Circumstances require confirmation. You will understand that a matter of this delicacy cannot rest on assumption. Return to the place of deposit and satisfy yourself. If anything remains there — anything at all — it must be removed and disposed of where it will never be found. The river is convenient and thorough. I am given to understand it does this work every spring without complaint.*

*You were retained because you were described to me as careful. Be careful. There is a family's good name in your hands, and I will not pretend to you that the sum attached to that good name is smaller than the sum attached to yours.*

*Do not write. Do not mark the bridge unless it is finished.*

*Forget this face.*

The same words at the bottom, underlined once. Corliss read the letter twice, laid it flat on the table, and held the corner of it to the lamp flame until the fire had eaten almost to his fingers, then dropped the last curling scrap into his glass, where it blackened and sank.

*If anything remains there.*

He closed his eyes and saw the step behind the net shed, three days ago. Snow falling in fat, unhurried flakes. The oilcloth bundle tucked in the lee of the door frame. His own tracks filling in behind him, the snow erasing his going even as he went, so that within the hour there had been exactly one set of footprints leading to that door and none leading away — a detail that had seemed, at the time, like elegance. Like tidiness.

Now he understood it differently. Now he understood that the snow had recorded him precisely, faithfully, the way such places record everything: the one visitor, the knock, the cry. Somewhere behind that door, someone had opened it. Someone had bent down into the cold and lifted the weight of a small sleeping child out of the snow, and carried her inside, and shut the wind out.

And whoever it was had seen the note. Had read those three words. And had not forgotten the face.

* * *

He went back to the workshop that same night, because a careful man verifies, and because — though he would not have admitted this under torture, would not have admitted it to himself above a whisper — he wanted to see the place again. Wanted to stand where he had stood and feel whatever it was he felt, nameless and uncomfortable, like a splinter working toward the skin.

The Halloway workshop sat low near the water, a long building of grey weathered boards, its roof weighted with the season's snow. Nets hung in the loft window like curtains of knotted hair. Down at the dock the river lay under its lid of ice, groaning now and then in the dark — long, tectonic complaints, the sound of a thing enormous turning over in its sleep. The whole town talked about the ice this year. Early thaw, the ferrymen said. Bad break. Water high.

Corliss kept to the shadow of the woodpile across the yard, breath shallow, and studied the building the way he'd study a den before putting a terrier in.

There was a light in the loft window. Small. Oil lamp, low flame. And as he watched, a shadow crossed it — quick, slight, the silhouette of someone bending over something out of sight below the sill. Then it crossed again. Back and forth. Pacing? No. Rocking. The rhythm of it was wrong for pacing and right for something else, something his mother's arms had known and his had not, and Corliss felt the splinter go deeper.

A girl. He caught the outline of her when she leaned into the light — young, sixteen perhaps, dark hair pinned carelessly, sleeves rolled as though interrupted at some mending. She held the bundle against her shoulder. She swayed with it. Her lips moved, counting or singing, he couldn't tell, and the lamp threw her shadow huge against the frost-furred glass: a giantess rocking a kingdom.

He stood in the cold until his feet went numb, and he memorized her face, because that was the job now. Not the child's face — hers. The face that had refused to forget.

She was nobody. That was the mercy of it. An indentured girl, a net-mender, the kind of person towns keep the way barns keep mice — present, useful, unseen. Nobody would miss her testimony; nobody would believe it if they heard it. Whatever she had taken in off the step, she could be persuaded to forget it too, one way or another. Money first. That was the civilized approach, and it worked more often than men guessed, because poverty was a kind of cold and everyone eventually wanted to come in out of it.

But he watched her sway in the lamplight, patient as tide, and he thought: *this one carried it inside. This one knocked the snow off and brought it to the fire.* Some people did that. He'd met them before, in other towns, other jobs. They were rare and they were trouble, and they could not be bought as cheaply as the arithmetic said they should be.

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest had gone quiet around him by the time he came back in — just the barkeep stacking glasses and one drunk asleep with his cheek on the table among the rings of old bottles. Corliss climbed the stairs to the rented room he kept under the name of Cole, sat on the bed with the mattress complaining under him, and took out the little notebook he kept in his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, because habits die hard and some of them are useful.

He wrote down what he had seen. The girl. The hour. The loft window. The light.

Then he sat with the pencil still touching paper, and he thought about the next letter. *Do not mark the bridge unless it is finished.* Finished. He turned the word over. He knew what finished meant in this trade; he had been finished himself, in the sense meant here, twice before he was twenty, and both times it had been animals, and both times he had told himself the same comfortable lie hunters tell: that the thing done quickly does not suffer, that the woods are full of worse deaths, that a man who takes coin takes consequences entire.

But this was not a rabbit. This was a child whose weight he still knew, exactly, the way you know a number you have carried once — nine pounds, maybe ten, warm through the oilcloth, one fist escaped the wrapping and gripped his coat button the whole way down Marsh Lane with an astonishing, furious strength, and he had let her hold it, had uncurled the tiny fingers himself at the workshop step, gently, like a man taking back a loan.

He blew out the lamp and lay down in his clothes and listened to the river.

The ice was talking tonight. All along its miles it spoke — creaks and cracks and sudden reports like distant rifle fire, and under it all a long low moan, the sound the whole frozen river made at once, which old ferrymen swore was the water asking permission of the land. Thaw was coming early. Everyone said so. The ice would heave and split and grind itself to pieces, and the river would rise brown and muscular and take whatever was in its path, and it would take it without malice, without memory, without one set of footprints or any at all.

*A convenient and thorough servant,* the letter said.

Corliss stared into the dark and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own sentence, how the matter would end if he let it end the way these matters ended. The child would go into the water, and the girl who rocked her would follow — quietly, an accident, a fall at the dock, another drowning in a town that collected drownings the way other towns collected taxes — and the fine hand in the blood-dark ink would pay the final sum and cross the last name off whatever private ledger it kept, and the face would be forgotten by everyone except the river, which forgets nothing, only keeps it all down there in the cold silt with the pearls and the bones.

He turned onto his side. Below the window, faint with distance, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a note held and bending, and somewhere under that sound — he was nearly sure of it, or wanted to be — he heard the thinner sound from three nights past, the needle-scratch of a cry in the snow, one small voice insisting against all arithmetic that it existed, that it had a face, that faces were not for forgetting.

Corliss got up, dressed fully, and put the notebook in his pocket.

Tomorrow he would introduce himself to the girl at the workshop. He had a speech prepared, the polite kind, the kind that opens purses and closes mouths. He had used it before and it had always worked, and he would offer the money first, exactly as instructed, and watch her eyes while he did it.

But underneath the speech, underneath the coin and the calm and the careful man's whole careful architecture, something had shifted its weight in him like the river under the ice — and he knew, walking down the dark stairs and out into the freezing street, that whichever way that crack ran, the break was coming early this year.
