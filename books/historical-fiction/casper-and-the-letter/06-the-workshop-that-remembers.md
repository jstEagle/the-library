## 6. The Workshop That Remembers

The baby needed cloth, and the workshop had none to spare.

That was the arithmetic of it, plain as a ledger line: three days old and already she had ruined every rag Wendell could beg or borrow — flour sacks from the kitchen, the hem of an old shift, even the soft-worn towel that had hung by the stove since before Wendell's time and which Cook would miss within the hour. The net loft was warm now, walled in with bundles of twine and the good hemp Pike didn't know Wendell had claimed as a cradle-nest, but warmth was only half of keeping a child alive in February at Brannock's Landing. The other half was dry linen, and there wasn't a dry linen inch to be had between the river and the company store.

Which meant the loft above the drying shed. Which meant the place where the old sailcloth was kept.

Wendell stood at the foot of the ladder with the lamp turned low and told herself, for the better part of a minute, that she had no business up there. The loft belonged to no one anymore, not really — Halloway's men hadn't climbed those stairs since the pearling fleet stopped wintering on the landing, five years past — but belonging to no one was different from being free, and everything under the Halloway roof was counted, even the things they'd forgotten counting.

Then the baby stirred in her sling against her chest, making a small sound like a gull far out over water, and Wendell put her foot on the first rung.

She had learned young how to move through a sleeping house. Her father had taught her without meaning to — *soft feet, soft hands, girl; the river hears everything* — and she went up the ladder now the way he used to go down one, hand over hand, weight balanced, breath measured. The loft door stood open on blackness. She raised the lamp.

Dust lay over everything like grey snow. It was a long room, longer than the shed below, running the full spine of the building, and it held the ghosts of the trade her father had died inside of: coiled lines gone stiff with age, oars racked against the wall, crates stenciled with ports she'd never seen — *KAGOSHIMA, THURSO, PONTA DELGADA* — and everywhere the pale humped shapes of sailcloth and canvas, sheeted like furniture in an abandoned house. The air smelled of tar and mouse and old rope, and beneath all of it something fainter, something almost sweet, like a flower pressed long ago in the pages of a book.

Wendell crossed to the nearest stack and pressed her palm flat against the topmost bolt of cloth. Heavy duck, waxed stiff. Too rough. She worked along the row, unrolling corners, shaking out dust that swirled gold in the lamplight and settled again. Her father had been dead six years and here was his whole world, moth-eaten and patient. Here was the sailcloth he'd cut his hands on. She wondered if he had climbed this ladder. If he had stood where she stood, breathing this same sweet-rotted air, thinking about the river below the ice.

At the far end of the room the stacks gave way to a jumble — broken dories, a capstan wheel, a heap of net floats like a pile of enormous eggs. And behind them, half-hidden, a partition: boards nailed across what might once have been a closet or a storeroom corner, closing off a wedge of darkness perhaps six feet deep.

Someone had pried one board loose and never bothered to nail it back.

Wendell knew she should leave it. She heard the thought clearly, in her own voice, in her father's voice both — *not your business, Wendell, nothing in this building is your business* — and then she heard the baby sigh against her breastbone, and her hands were already pulling the loose board wider.

Curiosity, her father used to say, is just hunger wearing a different coat.

* * *

The space behind the boards smelled older than the rest of the loft — drier, darker, as if the air itself had been shut in and forgotten. Wendell held the lamp through the gap and saw a sea chest, small, iron-bound, its lid thrown open long ago and never closed. Not looted, though. Not emptied. Whoever had opened it had left things behind, and that was stranger than theft. Things left behind on purpose were either worthless or dangerous, and the shape of the contents told her neither.

She reached in.

Letters first — a bundle of them, tied with ribbon gone the color of weak tea. She lifted them into the light and turned them over. Perhaps two dozen, folded twice, addressed to no address she could read because they bore no addresses at all, only dates, going back — she fanned them like cards — eleven years. Eleven years. Before her indenture. Before her father's drowning. The paper had the softness of things handled often, read and reread, and several bore stains she chose not to name.

Beneath the letters lay a ring.

It was a woman's ring, and even in a dying lamp's glow it did something to the dark around it — drank the light and gave it back warmer, the way deep water does. A pearl, large and slightly baroque, set in gold worn thin on one side, as if a thumb had worried it for years. Wendell did not touch it at first. She looked at it the way you look at something asleep, afraid of waking it. Then she picked it up, and it was heavier than it should have been, or colder, or both, and she felt — she could not have said why, and would never afterward try to say why — felt *watched*. Not menacingly. Just witnessed. As if the ring had been waiting in the dark for someone to find it and had opinions about who came.

She slipped it into her pocket. She could not have explained that either.

At the bottom of the chest, wedged into the angle where lid met base, lay one more thing: an envelope, sealed, thick with enclosed pages, addressed in a hand that stopped Wendell's heart for a full three seconds before her mind caught up with her eyes.

She knew that hand. You cannot keep ledgers for four years without learning every clerkship in the company the way a choir learns each other's voices, and she knew this one particularly — the sharp downstrokes, the little hooks on the terminals, the *Halloway Trading Co., Brannock's Landing* written with the flourish of a man proud of his own name. It was the hand of Elias Halloway himself. She had copied figures out of his correspondence books a hundred times, transcribing his instructions into fair copy, and she had always thought his writing looked like fence wire — orderly, and made to keep things in.

This envelope said, in that fence-wire hand: *To be delivered upon my death to the offices of Marsh & Pruitt, Attorneys, Carrow.*

And below that, smaller, slanted hard against the edge of the paper as if written quickly and in anger:

*If I am dead before it is delivered, then someone has seen to it, and someone ought to ask why.*

* * *

Wendell sat down on a crate in the dust with the letter in her lap and did not open it.

She wanted to. Her thumbnail found the seal's edge without her permission — plain wax, unmarked, brittle with age — and she sat there feeling the tiny resistance of it, the whole hidden weight of whatever was folded inside pressing back against her thumb, and she thought about doors. About the workshop door, three nights ago, and the knock nobody answered. About the note pinned to the oilcloth, the letters marching in a furious cramped hand: *FORGET THIS FACE.* About footprints filling with snow.

Whoever had left the baby had left no other trace. But traces were like drowned bodies, her father said; they rose eventually, and usually when you least wanted them to.

The baby woke then — hungry, or cold, or simply offended by the silence — and began to cry, thin and furious, into the dusty dark of the loft, and Wendell realized with a lurch that sound carried down the stairwell into the drying shed, and from the shed into the yard, and from the yard to the quarters where Aldous Pike slept with a pistol under his pillow and a grudge already lodged against the child like a splinter working toward the heart.

She hushed her, rocked her, gave her a knuckle to grip while she got the sling loosened. The crying ebbed to hiccupping snuffles. And in the lamplight, mid-hiccup, the baby fixed her eyes on Wendell's face with that grave, absolute attention that infants have, as if memorizing her, and Wendell thought: *forget this face.* As if any soul on this earth could look at that small furious solemnity and simply close the book on it.

"No," she whispered to her, to the letter, to the listening dark. "I don't suppose anyone ever manages it."

She bundled the letters and the envelope into a fold of the softest sailcloth she'd found — fine linen-canvas, yacht-grade, too good for any cargo Halloway ever carried — and tucked the bundle inside her shirt, against her back, where the warmth of her body and the warmth of the child would meet over it. The ring she kept in her pocket. Her hand kept returning to it as she descended the ladder, checking it was real, the way you check a coin you're sure you've dreamed.

* * *

Back in the net loft she fed the baby by the shaded lamp and laid out the bundle on a flour-sack pallet and read, at last, by inches, with one ear cocked to the stairs.

The letters were love letters. She understood that within the first page, and understanding it changed the temperature of the room. They were not written in Elias Halloway's wire-fence hand — these were rounder, hurried, ink-blotted, a young man's hand, or at least a man writing young — and they were signed *E.* at the bottom of every one, and they spoke in the coded extravagant language of people who must not be understood if caught. *You know what I would give, if the ledger of my life were mine to balance.* *They watch the house, so I will write to you through the boy at the chandlery, and trust to his silence, which I have bought.* *Your mother writes that the beds are yours by law and always were. Let them prove otherwise. I will stand witness if it comes to that — I will stand anywhere you ask me to stand.*

The pearl beds. Wendell sat back on her heels. Everyone on the landing knew the story the way everyone knows the shape of their own doorframe: the upstream beds, richest in forty miles, claimed and worked for a generation by a family named — she searched her memory, and found the name surfacing slow, like something rising through green water — *Verey.* The Vereys, and their rights contested in the county courts year after year, and then not contested anymore, because there was no one left to contest them. A fever, the town said. A bad winter. Boats that went out and did not come back. And after, the beds in Halloway's ledgers as clean as if they'd grown there, and the divers' fees dropping, and the divers' debts growing, and her own father among them.

Her own father among them.

She looked down at the baby nursing sleepily against her wrist, and the thought arrived whole, fully dressed, unwelcome as a stranger at a funeral: *this child's mother was a Verey.* The letters' recipient. The woman whose ring lay cold in Wendell's pocket. The woman who had come, heavy with child or carrying one newly born, to hide her last papers in a sail loft above a drying shed — because where else? Because her father had dived these waters, and because somewhere in those eleven years of letters a diver had carried messages, ferried bundles, kept secrets at the bottom of his lungs the way he kept pearls in the bottom of the river. Wendell would never prove it. She knew it the way Maren Oduya knew weather coming. Some part of her father's life had passed through this room, and had left a sealed letter in a dead woman's handwriting, and had left a baby on a doorstep twenty paces from where Wendell slept.

The last letter in the bundle was different. Older paper, a woman's hand, elegant and very steady, dated nine years gone:

*They have taken the beds, the boats, and my mother's good name, and now they send men to ask after my health in a manner I do not like. If anything happens to me, know that I have hidden what matters where only a true hand of the river would think to look. Tell him — tell him the child is his responsibility whether he claims her or not. Blood does not drown. I have signed everything and lodged it where it can speak when I cannot.*

There was no signature. There was a burn mark at the corner, neat and circular, like a candle guttered onto the page.

Wendell read it three times. Then she wrapped everything back into the sailcloth with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect, and she sat in the lamplight with the sleeping child against her chest and understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, that she was no longer holding rags and old paper.

She was holding the reason someone had driven out through the snow three nights ago rather than let this child grow up asking questions. She was holding the reason a woman had burned a candle late over her last testament and hidden it in a loft and gone away — wherever women like that go — and not come back. She was holding, folded twice and tied with tea-colored ribbon, the difference between the Halloways of the world continuing forever and the Halloways of the world answering, at last, for an arithmetic written in other people's blood.

And she was holding it under the roof of the Halloway Trading Company, in the keeping of a girl whose contract they owned, whose bed they could search on a foreman's word, whose comings and goings Aldous Pike noted the way he noted missing rope.

In the morning, she thought. In the morning I'll take it to Maren. In daylight, down the lane, casual as a woman carrying fish. Nothing to see. Nobody notices Wendell Montrose; that has always been my one talent, and God forgive me, tonight it is my whole fortune.

She banked the lamp. The baby breathed against her collarbone, small tides, ebb and flow. Somewhere below, the river shifted its ice and groaned — a long, deep complaint, like a giant turning in a bed too short for him — and Wendell listened to it and thought: *soon.* The whole valley said so. The oldest men on the docks, who read the river the way clerks read ledgers, said the break would come early and come hard this year, and that when it did, half the landing's plans would go downstream with it.

She slept at last near dawn, the bundle warm against her spine, the ring in her pocket curled into her palm like a second pulse.

She woke to boots on the stairwell — heavy ones, unhurried, climbing — and Pike's voice calling up through the boards, mild as milk and twice as cold:

"Montrose. Company wants an inventory of this loft. Get dressed."
