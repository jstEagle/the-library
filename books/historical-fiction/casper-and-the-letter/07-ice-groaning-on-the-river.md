## 7. Ice Groaning on the River

The river began to speak in the last week of February, and by the first week of March it would not be quiet.

Wendell heard it before she understood it — a deep, grinding groan that came up through the frozen ground and into the soles of her boots, through the workshop's stone foundation and up the legs of her stool, so that she felt it in her spine while she sat mending ledgers by the window. It was a sound like a door too large for any house, opening somewhere beneath the world. She set down her pen and listened.

The ice on the Brannock had lain thick and silent since December, white and unbroken from bank to bank, solid enough that the pearl divers' children had skated it all January and the company drays had crossed it loaded with salt pork and nails. All winter it had been a road, a lid, a held breath. Now, in the grey light of a thawing morning, it groaned again — a long, shuddering complaint that rolled upstream like something walking.

*Early,* she thought. *Far too early.*

Old divers said you could set a farmer's whole year by the ice-break. Some years it held until the willows budded; some years it went out in a single roaring night and took the low docks with it. But this — the third week of February, with frost still furring the windows and the air cold enough to bite — this was the ice shifting in its sleep. Waking ahead of itself. Maren Oduya would know what it meant. Maren always knew what the river meant, though she gave her knowledge grudgingly, like a woman paying out line to someone she wasn't sure could hold the other end.

Wendell rose and pressed her palm flat against the cold window glass. Down at the waterfront, men had come out of the boathouses and stood along the bank in their caps and coats, looking at the ice the way people look at a sick relative — reading its color, its seams, the dark wet lines where pressure had begun to fracture it from below. One of them took off his hat. That frightened her more than the groaning did. A man who takes off his hat before a river is a man who has seen what rivers do.

Behind her, in the net loft above the workshop floor, Pearl slept.

Three weeks now, and the baby had grown from a bundle of oilcloth and desperation into a small, definite person — a person with opinions about being woken, a ferocious grip, and a cowlick of pale hair that stood straight up no matter how Wendell smoothed it. Three weeks of stolen milk, of muffled crying smothered against Wendell's shoulder during Pike's inspections, of sleeping in shifts with one ear open. Three weeks, and already Wendell could not remember the shape of her life before the knock at the door. That was the part no one warned you about, she thought. Not the danger. The way the heart simply redrew its map overnight and pretended the old borders had never existed.

The ice groaned again, longer this time, ending in a crack like a whip-crack somewhere far downstream. In the loft, Pearl stirred and made a small sound. Wendell counted to ten, waiting to see if it would become a cry. It didn't. Small mercies.

She sat back down and pulled the ledger toward her, because there was always work, and work was the shape her safety took. Figures in her neat, upright hand: burlap received, cordage issued, wages docked, debts carried forward. The Halloway Trading Company kept its accounts the way a spider keeps a web, everything touching everything else, every thread leading back to the center. She had learned the web from the inside. She knew which columns were lies and which were merely cruel.

Outside, the light strengthened, and the town woke up talking.

* * *

By midday the flood-talk was everywhere, running through Brannock's Landing faster than the thaw water ran down its gutters.

Wendell took it in at the company store, where she'd been sent for twine, and the dockmen argued in voices pitched high with something between fear and excitement. The winter had been strange all along — snow that fell wet and heavy in November, a January warm spell that rotted the riverbanks' snow cover early, then a hard snap that sealed everything under new ice thicker than anyone could remember. Thick ice holding back more meltwater than the channel could carry. Old Man Tebbets, who had fished the Brannock for fifty years, was saying it would go out "like a gun going off," and saying it loudly, to anyone who'd listen, and several people clearly wished he wouldn't.

"Eighty-one was nothing," Tebbets declared, slapping his hand on the twine counter. "Eighty-one was a gentleman's flood. This here ice is four inches over what my auger's ever cut, and it's got water underneath it with nowhere to go. When she lets go—" He drew his thumb across his throat and grinned, because old men who have survived many things enjoy alarming those who haven't.

"When she lets go, what?" asked the ferryman's wife, arms full of flour.

"Mill race first. Then the low wharf. Anything built below the grain elevator line." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd read a menu. "You mark me. The river's got a debt to collect this spring. Rivers keep books same as companies do."

Wendell paid for her twine and left them to it, but Tebbets's words walked home with her, turning over and over: *rivers keep books.* Everyone in Brannock's Landing owed somebody something — owed the company store, owed the boatyards, owed their indentures and their mortgages and their fathers' drowned debts. Even the river, apparently, ran a ledger. And when it called in accounts, it did not negotiate terms.

She let herself in through the workshop's side door, checked the loft — Pearl still sleeping, breath slow and even, one fist curled against her cheek — and had just gotten the kettle on when Aldous Pike's boots came down the outside stairs two at a time, which was unlike him. Pike descended stairs the way he did everything else, deliberately, as though the world might try to cheat him if he hurried.

"Wendell." His knuckles rapped the doorframe without waiting. "Workshop floor. All hands. Now."

All hands, in the case of the Halloway workshop, meant six people: Wendell, the two old net-menders named Coby and Ruth, the boilerman's boy Sam, the yardman, and Pike himself. They assembled in the long room among the hanging racks of drying nets, which filled the space with the smell of hemp and tar, and Pike stood them all up in front of the great iron stove with the bearing of a man delivering a verdict rather than news.

"You'll have heard the talk on the docks," he said. "I won't have gossip doing the company's informing. So hear it plain."

He unfolded a paper — not a notice, Wendell saw, but a letter, written in the upright, expensive hand she had learned to recognize on every invoice and contract that passed through the office. The hand that had signed her own life away, seven years ago. The hand that had written, though she hadn't known it then, across the bottom of an order to forget a child's face.

"The Halloway Company has entered agreement to sell this lot," Pike said. "Premises inclusive. Sale closes on the fifteenth of March. Possession transfers immediately thereafter."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Ruth, the older of the net-menders, said what they were all thinking: "Razed?"

"Demolished," Pike confirmed. "The buyer intends to clear the lot before construction. Spring building season." He folded the letter away with precise movements. "Your positions transfer to the main warehouse on Cordwainer Street. Effective the fifteenth."

"And the housing?" Coby asked. He and Ruth lived in the lean-to behind the drying shed; Wendell's cot was in the back office; Sam's family occupied the upstairs rooms. Company housing, company rules.

Pike's jaw worked. For just an instant — so brief Wendell would later doubt she'd seen it — something moved behind his eyes that was not satisfaction. "Housing ends with the lot," he said. "You'll make your arrangements through the warehouse steward."

"That's three weeks," Ruth said. "Less than."

"It's the company's arithmetic," Pike said, "not mine." And there it was again, that flicker, quickly shut away. Wendell watched him and thought: he didn't choose this. Or he chose it and doesn't like the taste. It was a dangerous thing to notice about a man like Aldous Pike, and she looked down at her hands and pretended she hadn't noticed it at all.

Questions came and were batted aside. What about the boats in the yard? Sold with the lot. What about the nets mid-repair? Complete them or lose the piecework. Sam wanted to know if his family could take the stove — it was theirs, his father had bought it — and Pike said he'd look into it, which everyone understood to mean no. Through it all, Wendell kept her eyes down and her face smooth, and inside her the fear grew quiet and hard, like ice forming.

Because she was not thinking of Cordwainer Street. She was thinking: *the net loft.*

The loft where Pearl slept was not on any inventory worth respecting, but a demolition crew does not respect anything. Razers pull down walls. They climb into spaces. They find things. On the fifteenth of March — less than three weeks — there would be strangers with crowbars in every corner of this building, and a baby's cry carries, and even if by some miracle the child stayed hidden until the walls came down, there was nowhere after that. No walls left to hide behind. The warehouse on Cordwainer Street had a foreman's office, a watchman, a schedule, and no net loft at all.

And the ice would break sometime around then, too. Tebbets said the river would go out "like a gun going off," and the river's calendar and the company's calendar had collided, and both dates were aimed at her like the two barrels of the same gun.

"Montrose." Pike's voice cut across her thoughts. "Anything from your department I should know about?"

"No, Mr. Pike," she said, in her flat, careful voice. "Ledgers are current."

He looked at her a moment longer than he needed to. Pike had been looking at her a moment longer than he needed to, ever since the morning three weeks ago when he'd found the baby and told her it must be gone by dawn, and she had stood in front of him with her heart hammering and said no. She had never said no to Aldous Pike before. She had expected to be turned out into the snow, or worse, turned over to the company office and the constable, and instead he had looked at the small red face over her shoulder for a long time and said, in a voice like gravel settling: *Not on the premises by daylight, Montrose. That's all I'm saying. That's the only thing I'm saying.* And had walked away, and had not mentioned it since, except in these looks she couldn't read.

"See that they stay that way," he said now, and dismissed them all.

* * *

She went to Maren at dusk, taking the river path because it was quicker and because she wanted to see the ice herself.

Maren Oduya lived in a low house of driftwood and tar-paper at the edge of the divers' settlement downstream of the wharves, where the pearling families kept their boats and their drying racks and their own counsel. Wendell had been coming here more often than was safe, drawn the way cold hands are drawn to a fire. Maren had known her father. Maren had taken one look at Pearl three weeks ago, lifted the baby out of Wendell's arms with hands scarred by forty years of cold water, and said, "Well. Here's a thing the river sent us," in a voice that closed the subject. Since then there had been goat's milk from the divers' common herd, and a cradle borrowed without being asked about, and a standing offer of shelter that Maren renewed each visit with a jerk of her chin toward the back room.

Tonight Maren was on her porch mending a dive-weight harness, and she saw Wendell coming before Wendell reached the gate, because Maren saw everything.

"You've got your father's walk when you're worried," she said. "Like the ground owes you money."

"The river's moving."

"It is." Maren set down the harness and listened. The groaning came again, fainter here but constant now, a sound like the earth clearing its throat. "Started day before yesterday. My knees told me before my ears did."

"Tebbets says it'll take the low wharf. Says it'll go out like a gunshot."

"Tebbets is usually right and always loud." Maren studied her. "That's not what brought you down the bank in this wind. Sit. There's tea."

Wendell sat on the porch step, close enough to feel the warmth leaking through the house's thin walls, and told her. The sale. The demolition date. The fifteenth of March. The transfer to Cordwainer Street, where there was no loft, no shadowed corners, no chance at all.

Maren poured the tea and drank hers slowly, looking out past the drying racks to where the river lay white and restless in the last light. The groans came at longer intervals now, deeper, like breathing in sleep.

"Fifteenth of March," she said at last. "And the ice?"

"Nobody knows. Early, everyone says. Tebbets says within days of the sale, maybe sooner."

"Then you're caught between two floods," Maren said. "Water and paper." She turned her dark, seamed face toward Wendell. "You know what my people say about the break-up. The river doesn't destroy. It *releases.* Everything it held all winter comes up at once, and whatever floats, floats, and whatever's tied down stays, and whatever's rotten goes to the bottom where it should have gone years ago." She sipped. "The trick is knowing which kind of thing you are before the ice moves."

"I'm trying to keep a baby floating," Wendell said, "in a town that wants to bury her."

"I know it." Maren set her cup down. "Listen to me, girl, because I'll only say this once. Fifteenth of March is not a deadline. It's a wall, and walls can be gotten around or gone through, but you don't decide how on the day you reach them. You decide now. Tonight. You decide whether you're hiding that child or moving her, because hiding has run out of road. And if you're moving her—" her eyes flicked meaningfully toward the river, the settlements upstream, the scattered camps where the law came rarely and questions came never— "then you need to know exactly what you're carrying and why someone thought she was worth forgetting. Because whoever left her at that door hasn't come back. That should frighten you worse than Pike does. People who leave babies and don't come back are people who are waiting. Patient people. And when they come, they won't come to thank you."

The groaning of the ice swelled, somewhere in the dark, into a long tearing sound — distant, immense, like furniture dragged across the floor of the world. Both women turned their heads. Downriver, faint in the twilight, Wendell could see men's lanterns gathering along the bank again, little points of flame watching the white expanse.

"Two deadlines," she said quietly. "The water and the wreckers."

"No," said Maren. "One deadline. You've just been counting it twice." She gathered her mending into her lap and began, with terrible calm, to work the needle again. "Go home. Feed the child. And tomorrow, start going through that workshop like a woman packing to leave — because you are. And if there's anything in that building that explains who she is, girl, you find it before the fifteenth. After that it'll be under rubble, and after the ice breaks it'll be under water."

Wendell walked back up the river path in the blue dark, lantern swinging, the ice talking to itself on her left the whole way home. She was halfway to the workshop, her mind churning over lofts and skiffs and dates, when she remembered why she had first gone searching the loft at all — the cold, the need for cloth, the cache she had found wedged behind the old cedar chest.

Letters. A woman's ring. And one sealed envelope, addressed in a hand she had seen that very afternoon, unfolded in Aldous Pike's fist.

She stopped walking. The lantern guttered in the wind off the river. Somewhere beneath her boots, four feet of ice groaned like something waking, and Wendell stood very still in the dark, understanding all at once that the answer to Maren's question had been sitting above her head for three weeks — and that tomorrow, while the wreckers counted down their days, she was going to open it.
