## 8. The Name in the Ledger

The ledgers of the Halloway Trading Company were kept in a hand so uniform it might have been printed rather than written — upright, unlovely, without a single wasted stroke. Wendell had learned that hand at thirteen, copying figures by lamplight while the company clerk dictated, and she had come to know its temper the way a sailor knows weather. When the ink thinned toward the bottom of a page, someone had been economizing. When a number sat slightly higher than its neighbors, someone had been hurried. And when an entry had been ruled through twice with a straightedge — not once, which was correction, but twice, which was intention — someone had wanted it forgotten.

She sat in the counting room long after the last lamp but hers had gone dark, the baby sleeping two floors above in the net loft with Maren's knitted shawl tucked around her, and she spread the ledgers open like a surgeon spreading a chest.

The letters lay beneath her elbow, wrapped again in the oilcloth from the cache in the loft wall. She had not yet broken the sealed envelope. She told herself it was because the seal was not hers to break, and this was true, but there was another truth underneath it, quieter and colder: as long as the envelope stayed shut, the name inside stayed a stranger's, and she could still, if she chose, put the whole bundle back into the wall and go on being a girl who mended nets and noticed nothing.

She was not sure, anymore, that she could choose that.

* * *

It had taken her three nights to find where to begin.

The cache had held eleven letters, a woman's ring worn thin at the band, a pressed sprig of river willow gone brown as rust, and the sealed envelope addressed in the ledger hand — the company hand, unmistakable, the capital H with its peculiar flat crossbar that appeared ten thousand times down ten thousand columns of Halloway accounts.

But the unsealed letters were written in a different hand entirely. It slanted leftward, quick and confident, with loops that climbed above their neighbors like swimmers raising their heads out of the water. The woman who wrote that hand had been educated, impatient, and fond of underlining — and she had been afraid. That last part came through even in the flourishes, in the way certain words were written smaller than the rest, as though the writer flinched from her own ink: *father*. *Lawyer*. *They know where I am*.

The letters were undated in the ordinary way. Instead of writing the date, the woman marked each one with a small drawing in the corner — a moon in one phase or another, sometimes with a star beside it, once with a line drawn through it that Wendell could not at first interpret.

It was Maren who solved it, on the second night, turning the oilcloth package over with fingers scarred smooth by thirty years of riverbottom silt.

"Shell record," she said. "Pearlers used it before they trusted paper calendars. Moon for the month, star for the quarter-days when the beds were closed. Line through it—" She paused, and her thumb moved once across the crossed-out moon. "Line means a death. Someone they'd lost."

"Who teaches that?" Wendell asked. "Who would know to read it?"

"River people," Maren said. "Your father did." And she would say no more than that, only closed Wendell's hand back over the letters and told her to mind the fire, the baby would wake cold otherwise.

So Wendell had carried the moons in her head to the counting room, and matched them against the dates ruled into the margins of the company ledgers, and begun — entry by entry, page by lamplit page — to build a life out of arithmetic.

* * *

The woman's name, when it first appeared, appeared as a debit.

**E. Voss — disbursements, March**, said the column, and beneath it a sum large enough to make Wendell sit back on her stool. Disbursement to whom, for what? The ledger did not say. Ledgers rarely did; that was their whole genius. A ledger could record the price of a woman's silence without ever recording the woman.

Wendell worked backward. E. Voss appeared first — earliest that she could find — the previous June, in a subsidiary book labeled simply **Correspondence & Claims**, a volume so dull-looking that no one had opened it, she guessed, since it was shelved. Claims against the company. She turned pages until her fingertips were grey with old dust, and the story assembled itself the way silt assembles into a shoal: invisibly, grain by grain, until a boat tore its bottom out on it.

Eleanor Voss. Of Voss Landing, upriver — a name Wendell half knew, the way you half know a word in a language you almost speak. There had been Vosses in Brannock's Landing once, big ones; the old men at the fish market still said *Voss dock* for the rotten pilings below the church stairs, out of habit, though the Voss dock had been sold off before Wendell was born. Sold to whom?

She found the sale. It took her most of the third night, cross-legged on the counting room floor with four volumes open around her like the points of a compass. The transfer was dated fourteen years back, executed by the estate's administrator, witnessed by two signatures she copied onto the corner of her apron without thinking about why. One witness was a name she didn't know. The other was **A. Halloway** — the founder's own signature, stiff and formal even in facsimile, the flat-crossbarred H like a gallows viewed from above.

Fourteen years ago, the Voss estate had sold its dock, its warehouse, its pearl-bed leases, and its town lots — everything — for a sum recorded here, and a sum recorded nowhere, and the difference between those two sums was the entire business of the Halloway Trading Company, or near enough.

And Eleanor Voss, who signed her letters with loops like lifted heads, had spent the last year of her life writing to the company asking for it back.

* * *

The letters made more sense now, and less bearable.

*I have the papers, or enough of them. My father kept copies, he always kept copies, he never trusted a man who shook your hand too warmly.*

*Mr. Halloway received me civilly. I want that recorded somewhere, that he was civil. He poured tea. He spoke of my father with affection. Then he showed me the estate accounts, all beautifully kept, all wrong, and explained that I would find no lawyer in this county willing to take the case, and I understood what the tea was for.*

*They have bought the court the way one buys a mule. I am going upriver to find one they haven't.*

That letter bore a moon with no star, late summer by its fatness. The next bore a thin crescent, autumn coming on, and the hand had changed — smaller now, the loops clipped, written fast, perhaps by candlelight, perhaps cold.

*I am with child. This is its own reason and has nothing to do with any of it, and yet everything has changed shape. Before, if they ruined me, they ruined me. Now I think about what name she will carry.* (There — *she*, underlined twice, the writer certain.) *I will not carry a stolen name to my daughter like a coat with someone else's blood on the lining.*

*I have engaged counsel in Stonebridge. They cannot buy everyone. Pray God they cannot buy Stonebridge.*

The last unsealed letter was dated by a moon with a line through it, and Wendell understood the line differently now. Not a death mourned. A death intended. A debt declared.

*If I do not come back from the Landing, the papers are safe where your man knows. He was kind to me on the boat and I have paid him nothing and he asked for nothing, which is more than any lawyer has done. Tell him I said the river keeps what it is given.*

*My daughter will come for these. She will be born at the turn of the year, under the last ice-moon. If I am dead, let whoever reads this understand: I was not sick, I was not sad, I was not careless. I was going to the Landing to ask for what was mine, and I went anyway, knowing.*

*Eleanor Voss*

Below the signature, in a different ink, added later and shakier: *born Feb. 3, both well. God keep her.*

Wendell read the date three times.

February the third. She looked at the ledger open beside her knee — the daily receipt book, the one she herself kept the minor entries in, her own small upright hand marching down the columns. She turned back through the winter pages, back past Christmas entries for lamp oil and salt pork, back into February, back to the third.

**Feb. 3 — female infant, landing stairs, 6 a.m. No claimant.**

No. That wasn't right; that was this winter, this child, the child asleep in the net loft upstairs. She was confusing herself. She turned further back — how far? Her finger moved along the columns, past January, and stopped.

There it was, one year prior, in the clerk's flawless hand:

**Feb. 4 — E. Voss, services rendered, settled in full. Burial, potter's field. £2 10s.**

Settled in full. Fourteen shillings and sixpence more than the company had ever paid its own drowned divers' widows. Wendell knew because she had copied those payments too, at thirteen, reading the sums aloud in her flat young voice while the clerk checked them against his list, and she remembered asking — once, only once — why Mrs. Cairn's widow's payment was smaller than the fee for hauling her husband's gear out of the mud, and being told that the gear had resale value.

Eleanor Voss had died on the fourth of February, and her daughter had been left on the workshop door on the night of the third, wrapped in oilcloth, with a note ordering the finder to forget her face.

Not abandoned. Delivered. Taken from her dying mother's side — or sent ahead, hidden, smuggled — and set down at the one door in Brannock's Landing where a certain kind of river person might find her. Your father did, Maren had said. He helped her mother once too. The river keeps what it is given.

Wendell sat very still in the dark of the counting room, and the grief arrived before the understanding did, the way floodwater arrives in a cellar before you hear the river change its voice.

She had never met Eleanor Voss. She knew her only as a slanting hand, a thin ring, a sprig of willow pressed flat as a coin. And yet the grief was specific, particular, heavy as wet wool: grief for a woman who had drunk civil tea across a table from the man stealing everything she owned, and thanked him, and gone upriver anyway to fight; who had given birth in hiding and died within a day, two streets at most from where her enemy counted his money; who had trusted her daughter to the river's keeping and the river had given her, of all things, to Wendell Montrose, whose father also knew what the water kept.

"She came here," Wendell said aloud, and the words sounded strange in the empty room, small and bright. "She came here to ask, and they buried her in the potter's field for two pounds ten, and then they put her daughter on my doorstep and told me to forget her face."

Forget this face. The note was still folded inside her bodice; she felt its corners against her skin like a held breath. As if a face could be forgotten because a piece of paper said so. As if the child upstairs, who gripped Wendell's finger in her sleep with a strength out of all proportion to her size, who had a temper already and a way of glaring at lamplight as though it owed her money, as if she could be unwritten by anyone's arithmetic.

The child was the heir. Not to debts — Eleanor's letters suggested there were none worth the name — but to the inheritance itself: the dock, the leases, the pearl beds, the lots. Everything Halloway stood on. Everything the company had bought for a fraction and ruled through twice.

A weapon made of ink, Maren had called such papers once, speaking of something else entirely, some old quarrel among the divers. Wendell hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, sitting among the ledgers with her hands flat on the pages, understanding it so completely that her hands trembled against the paper and she had to draw them back into her lap.

Because there was the terrible symmetry of it, laid out plain as a column of figures: the company had killed — or let die, which the law barely distinguished and conscience never could — a woman to keep this quiet. And the proof of the crime was sleeping upstairs in a knitted shawl, breathing in the small irregular rhythm that Wendell had memorized without meaning to, the way she had once memorized tide tables.

They would not stop at burning letters this time.

* * *

She put the ledgers away carefully. That was the discipline of being unnoticed, and she was grateful to it now: she reshelved every volume in its exact place, smoothed the pages, brushed the dust from her skirt so the count room would look by morning exactly as it always looked, as though nothing had ever been learned in it. She copied three things onto the scrap of paper she kept inside her cuff — the burial entry with its date and sum, the estate transfer with its two witnesses, and the words *Stonebridge, counsel* — folding the paper small as a seed.

Then she climbed the dark stairs to the net loft, moving quietly on the treads she knew by heart, the ones that groaned and the ones that kept silent.

The loft smelled of tar and hemp and, underneath, the warm milky smell of the child, which had in one week become to Wendell as necessary and ordinary as the smell of rain. Maren sat propped against the netting bundles, awake — she slept little and lightly, river habit — and looked up at Wendell's face and set down the netting needle she had been pretending to use.

"You found her," Maren said. Not a question.

"Eleanor Voss." Wendell sat down on the floorboards, close to the basket, close to the small sound of breathing. "Dead the day after her daughter was born. Buried by the company for two pounds ten. And the child—" Her voice caught and she was too tired to be ashamed of it. "Maren, the child owns the ground this workshop stands on. Owns the pearl leases. Owns half of what Halloway calls his."

Maren was silent a long moment. Out on the river the ice shifted, a long deep sound like a whale turning over in its sleep, and both of them listened to it without remarking, the way sailors listen to weather.

"And now you know," Maren said at last, "what you're carrying. Before it was a baby somebody didn't want. Now it's a baby somebody killed for." She reached out and adjusted the shawl over the sleeping face with two fingers, a gesture so gentle it seemed to belong to another woman entirely. "Knowing changes the weight, girl. Doesn't add to it. Just changes where you feel it."

"I keep thinking about her mother," Wendell said. "Writing those letters. Underlining things. She knew they'd kill her and she wrote *I went anyway*, like she was leaving instructions. Like courage could be handed down." Wendell looked at the small face in the basket, the frown of dream-concentration, the fist curled against a cheek. "I've spent six years learning to be invisible. Head down, eyes down, don't be interesting. And I'm good at it, Maren. I'm so good at it that a man stood on our doorstep in the snow and thought this was exactly the place to leave a baby no one would ask questions about."

"And?"

"And I don't want to be invisible anymore." It came out quieter than she meant, and steadier. "She can't sign anything. She can't even hold up her own head yet. Somebody has to hold her claim for her until she can. Somebody has to be visible." She heard herself say the next thing as though someone else were saying it, someone taller and older, someone her father might have recognized. "I'm going to find the Stonebridge lawyer. And the witness on the transfer — the one who isn't Halloway. Names leave trails. I've spent six years reading ledgers; I can read people the same way."

Maren studied her for a long time, firelight moving in the old woman's eyes. Then she nodded once, slow, the nod she gave when a diver went down and the depth was known and accepted.

"Then sleep," she said. "Because starting tomorrow, girl, you'd best learn to lie as well as you read. Because mark me—" and out on the river the ice groaned again, closer, like something waking— "whoever wrote *forget this face* is going to wonder sooner or later whether the finder obeyed. And when they wonder, they'll send someone polite to ask."

Downriver, far off, a dog began to bark, and would not stop.
