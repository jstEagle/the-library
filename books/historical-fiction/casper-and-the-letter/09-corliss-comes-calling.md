## 9. Corliss Comes Calling

The stranger arrived on a Tuesday, in the gray hour when the river fog had not yet decided whether to be weather or water.

Wendell saw him first as a shape at the end of the dock, hat brim dripping, boots striking the planks with the particular care of a man who wanted to be heard coming and not mistaken for anything sudden. That alone told her something. The men of Brannock's Landing walked like they owned their footsteps or like they'd forgotten them; this one counted his.

She was on the workshop porch with Pearl wrapped against her chest beneath her coat, the baby's warmth banked against her sternum like a coal carried in cupped hands. Three weeks now since the door, since the note, since the single line of footprints filling with snow. Three weeks of mending nets by day and mending a small life by night, of hiding Pearl in the net loft where the smell of tar and hemp covered the milk-and-lavender sweetness of her, of learning to sleep in half-measures, one ear open for Pike's boots on the stairs.

The company ledger lay open on her lap. She was entering cordage weights, because arithmetic was the safest thing in the world — numbers never asked questions — when the stranger's shadow fell across the page.

"Miss Montrose."

Not a question. He knew her name. The cold went through her coat, through the baby, through the bone.

She looked up slowly, the way she'd learned to look up at Pike — not eager, not afraid, just a girl with a pen behind her ear and work to finish. "Company business is handled inside," she said. "If you've an account to settle—"

"No account." He removed his hat and shook the damp from it, and that gave her time to study him: forty perhaps, weathered the way river men were weathered, but his hands were wrong for the water. Too clean at the nails. A man who worked indoors and came out only when someone paid him to. His face was arranged into pleasantness the way a shop window was dressed — everything positioned, nothing alive behind the glass.

"Wendell Montrose," he said again, gently, as if tasting it. "Daughter of Josiah Montrose, the diver. I knew your father. Slightly."

"You didn't."

It was out before she could weigh it, flat and certain, and something flickered behind the shop window of his face — surprise, then a kind of appreciation, the way a card player appreciates being called. "No," he admitted. "I didn't. Forgive me. It's a habit in my trade to open warm."

"What is your trade?"

"Finding things." He smiled. It was a good smile, constructed by a craftsman. "My name is Corliss. I've been engaged by parties who are trying to settle an old sorrow, and you may be able to help them close it kindly."

Behind her ribs, Pearl stirred. Wendell pressed her palm flat against her own coat, over the bundle, a motion that could pass for settling her sleeves. *Be still. Be stone. Be nothing.*

"I mend nets," she said. "I keep ledgers. I don't know what sorrows I could help anyone close."

"Perhaps none." He glanced past her, at the workshop — at the dark windows, the sagging porch, the net loft above with its one lit shutter. "It concerns a woman. A young woman who was in this town last autumn. She may have left things behind. Papers. Keepsakes. Small items of sentimental value to her family."

*Her family.* The word landed like a hook set deep. Wendell kept her eyes on his and thought about cordage. Three-strand hemp, breaking strength four hundred pounds. You did not test rope by pulling hard all at once. You watched where the fibers whispered.

"There are a great many women in Brannock's Landing," she said.

"There are." He produced, from inside his coat, an envelope — not the sealed letter sleeping upstairs under a floorboard, no, but a smaller thing, a photograph gone soft at the corners, and he held it out. "This was taken some years ago."

She looked. She made herself look the way a person looks at any stranger's photograph, mild and brief, and she thanked God and her father both that she had spent sixteen years learning to keep her face shut like a book closed in the rain. Because the woman in the photograph had Pearl's brow. Had the same small stubborn chin, the same arch of cheekbone, older now, wearing a coat too fine for Brannock's Landing and an expression that was not smiling and not asking either. Demanding. The mother's whole soul lived in that jaw.

"Pretty woman," Wendell said. "I don't know her."

"She stayed near here. Near this building." Corliss folded the photograph away with terrible patience. "Miss Montrose, my employers are not cruel people. They want only to gather what belongs to the family and see that she's — that her affairs are properly settled. Anything she left here would ease their grief considerably. And they're prepared to be generous. Generosity," he added, "is cheaper than grief."

He said it lightly. He said it the way a man says a thing he has said before, in other towns, to other keepers of other secrets, and Wendell felt the true sentence underneath the polite one rise through the fog like a drowned body rising through green water: *we will pay for it, or we will take it, and either way we will leave no one holding it.*

"How much?" she heard herself ask.

Corliss tilted his head. So did she, somewhere far back in her mind — why had she asked that? — and understood a heartbeat later: because a guilty girl refuses. A guilty girl goes white and says *nothing was left, I don't know what you mean,* and hands him the answer in her trembling. But a girl with nothing to hide hears an offer of money and does what any poor girl in Brannock's Landing does.

She considers it.

"Enough to clear your indenture," Corliss said. "I know what Halloway holds you for. Forty-one pounds, eleven shillings, against your father's debts. Enough to clear it twice over and put you in a room of your own with a stove that draws."

Forty-one pounds, eleven shillings. He knew the figure to the penny. Which meant he had been inside the company books, or stood close enough to a man who had, which meant Halloway itself was bent toward this like a reed toward wind, which meant there was nowhere in town the truth could be handed off and made someone else's problem. The knowledge should have broken her. Instead it settled over her like a net settling over a school of fish — enclosing, yes, but showing her the exact shape of her cage, bar by bar.

"That's real money," she said slowly. And then, because lying well meant lying in the direction of truth: "But I don't know what you think I have. This is a workshop full of nets and rope and other people's dust."

"May I look?"

"In the workshop?"

"Just a turn around the loft. Ten minutes. My employers would consider it a great kindness." He was already reaching into his coat again, and this time it was a purse, heavy, the leather worn shiny, and he set it on the railing between them with the soft clink that money makes when it wants to be noticed.

Wendell looked at the purse. She let herself look at it the way she would have looked at it three months ago — hunger, quick and shameful, the arithmetic of a girl who had eaten bread and drippings for supper every night since she was eleven. Then she looked back at his face.

"If there were something," she said, "it wouldn't be mine to sell, would it? Found property on company ground goes into the company's hands. Foreman Pike would have my hide for taking coin for it." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her everything she had; she felt it leave her body like blood. "Look, if you like. There's naught but nets. But if Pike sees a strange man in the loft, he'll want to know why, and I'll be the one answering for it. Come back Saturday. Payday. Half the yard will be drunk and the other half asleep, and no one will mark you."

It was perfect. It was flawless. It bought her days.

And Corliss smiled his crafted smile and did not move.

"The thing is," he said, still pleasantly, still softly, "my employers are anxious. Grief doesn't keep a calendar. I'll just have that ten minutes now, and we needn't speak of Saturdays."

He stepped past her onto the porch.

Every plan Wendell had made in three weeks of sleepless nights — the floorboard, the oilcloth, the loose slat in the loft wall where the letters could be slipped through into the river if it ever came to that — every plan assumed she would have warning. Not this. Not ten feet between this man and the stair, and between her chest and his eyes a living, breathing piece of the woman in the photograph.

Pearl chose that moment to wake.

It began as a shift, a small arching under the coat, and Wendell felt it with the whole surface of her skin the way you feel a rope begin to part strand by strand. Then a sound — not a cry yet, thank God, not yet, just the little grunting complaint of a baby surfacing from deep sleep, a sound that could, if God was merciful and fog was thick, be mistaken for a stomach.

Corliss stopped.

The fog hung between them, beaded on the rail, silvering the lamplight from the workshop window. He turned, and for the first time the shop-window pleasantness dropped, and Wendell saw the actual face beneath it, and it was worse than cruelty, it was *confusion*, a man looking at something his instructions hadn't prepared him for.

"You have a cold," he said.

"Aye," Wendell said. "Chest trouble. Comes on in the damp."

Another grunt from beneath her coat. Corliss took one step closer. Wendell did not retreat — retreat would open her coat's drape, would show him the curve of a blanket, the impossible smallness of what she carried — so she stood, and she met his eyes, and inside herself she went down into the place her father had taught her existed in everyone. *You can hold your breath longer than you think,* he used to tell her, crouched in the shallows when she was six, her braids dripping. *The panic tells you lies, chickadee. Panic says now, now, now. The body knows better. Go down quiet. Count your strokes.*

She counted.

One. Two. Corliss's gaze moved from her face to the front of her coat. Three. Four. The lamp in the window guttered slightly in the draft. Five. Six. Somewhere downriver a boat horn moaned, long and low, and under its cover Pearl gave one thin mewling note —

— and sneezed.

A tiny, furious, kitten-sized sneeze, muffled by wool and coat and the fog itself, and Wendell coughed at exactly the same instant, a deep theatrical chest-rattling bark that she summoned from somewhere in her boots. "Beg pardon," she rasped, wiping her mouth with the back of her wrist. "Told you. Damp gets in me like a tenant."

Corliss stood very still.

Then, slowly, the shop window redressed itself. The pleasantness came back, item by item, smile and tilt and softened brow. "You should see someone about that," he said. He picked up his purse from the railing. He put his hat back on, and the drip from the brim ran down past his temple like a tear that belonged to somebody else. "Saturday, then," he said. "I'll come for that ten minutes."

"I'll sweep the dust for you," said Wendell.

He went back down the dock the way he'd come, counting his footsteps, and the fog took him apart piece by piece — shoulders, hat, the shine of his hands — until there was nothing left but the river breathing.

* * *

She did not go inside right away.

She stood on the porch with her heart slamming and the baby pressed to her, and she shook — not the delicate tremble of fear in a novel but the full-body rattle of a wire fence in wind, her teeth knocking, her knees gone to water. Pearl had worked one hand free of the blanket and gripped a fistful of Wendell's shirt with the absolute conviction of the very small, and Wendell bent her head and breathed against that tiny knuckles-white hold until the shaking slowed.

"He knows my name," she whispered. "He knows my debt to the penny."

Pearl blew a bubble and regarded her with her father's — no. With her mother's eyes. Dark, unimpressed, waiting.

"You're right," Wendell said. "Feeling sorry for myself won't mend net."

Inside, she climbed to the loft by lamplight, pried up the floorboard, and drew out the packet: the letters in their faded ribbon, the woman's ring, the sealed envelope heavy as a struck bell. She sat among the hanging nets with the shapes of knots pressing shadows down around her and read the outside of the sealed envelope again, though she knew it by heart now. Her own name was not on it. No name was. Just a date, seven years old, and the hand — that upright, economical hand she copied out five days a week in the company ledgers. Halloway's hand. Old man Halloway himself, or whoever kept his pen.

*A weapon made of ink.* She'd thought it the first night, holding it up to the lamp. Now she knew it plainer: they will kill for this paper. Not a figure of speech, not the drama of a frightened girl. Corliss's hands were too clean for diving and his eyes had done arithmetic on her coat, on her cough, on the shape beneath the wool, and when the sum didn't come out he had retreated — but only to recalculate. *Grief doesn't keep a calendar.* Saturday was a lie he'd accepted the way a cat accepts a closed door: temporarily, and while planning the window.

Which meant Saturday would never come. Which meant the letters had to leave this building before any next visit, and not into the river — she wasn't ready to drown the only voice of a dead woman begging for her child. Which meant Maren. Which meant the divers, the pearl boats, the long cold run upstream to wherever truth could be witnessed instead of buried.

Which meant, said a voice in her that sounded like her father and was starting to sound like herself, that the time for hiding in plain sight was ending, and she would have to choose what she was willing to lose.

Wendell reburied the packet, all but the ring, which she slid into her pocket — small, portable, the mother's initial engraved inside — and sat a while longer in the net-smelling dark, listening to the river.

The ice was talking tonight. It had started two days ago, low groans rolling up through the pilings like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and the old men at the landing had begun saying *early this year, early*, with the particular satisfaction they took in disaster. The river was waking. The company was circling. A man who found things had found her.

Down in the workshop, Pike's lantern passed the window on his final round, paused — she held her breath — and moved on.

Wendell rose, trimmed the lamp, and began, with steady hands, to count her strokes.
