## 10. What Maren Dived For

Maren Oduya had not been down to the pearl beds in eleven years, and she told herself it was the cold that kept her away, though the river had never once cared what she told herself.

She stood on the shingle below the divers' camp, where the willows grew crooked from leaning into the wind off the water, and watched the ice shift in the gray light of a February morning. It moved the way old animals move — slowly, grudgingly, with groans that came up through the soles of her boots. The whole town of Brannock's Landing spoke about the ice as if it were weather, but Maren knew better. The ice was a ledger too. Everything the river took, the river kept accounts of, and every spring it balanced its books all at once.

Behind her, in her cabin, a baby slept in a fish crate lined with wool. Wendell Montrose's girl — no, not her girl, and that was precisely the trouble — slept with one fist curled against her cheek as if even in dreams she were holding something tight and would not let go.

Maren had promised the child nothing yet. She had promised Wendell only her help, and help was a word wide enough to drive a barge through. But she had known, from the moment Wendell came down the frozen path two nights ago with that bundle in her arms and her chin set the way her father's used to set, that help would not be enough. Help was for neighbors. What was coming for this child would require something older.

So Maren did what she always did when the water started talking to her. She went to the edge of it and listened.

* * *

In the year of the great pearl — 1876, when Maren was still a woman men wrote letters to and Elias Montrose was a young diver with shoulders like a yoke ox — the beds below Widow's Reef gave up a treasure the river had been keeping for a hundred years.

They had gone down together, she and Elias, working the deep channel where the current ran cold enough to make your teeth ache through your closed mouth. Diving for pearls was not romance. It was arithmetic done with lungs. You counted your breaths on the bank, learned exactly how many you owned, and then spent them like a miser, each one, down in the green dark where your fingers walked the bottom blind and your heart hammered so hard you feared it would crack you open like a geode before you ever found anything worth cracking open for.

Elias could hold his breath longer than any man or woman in the camp, and he did it not by stillness but by happiness. That was the strange truth of him. The other divers went down grim, wrestling the water, and the water always won the wrestle early. Elias went down grinning, and the river — Maren never found another way to say it — *liked* him. It let him stay. He'd come up after four minutes, sometimes five, spitting water and laughing, holding out some river-gift in his palm: a mussel heavy with pearls, a coin lost off a steamboat forty years prior, once a wedding ring with an inscription worn nearly smooth.

"River pays its divers," he liked to say, wringing out his hair. "Just don't ask what it wants back."

The year of the great pearl, the river wanted back more than either of them understood.

The find itself was ordinary at first — a bed of washboard mussels fat with seed pearls, worth a season's labor and no more. But beneath the bed, wedged in the clay where the current had scoured a hollow, Elias's hands found a leather packet wrapped in oilskin and tied with tarred cord. Even blind underwater he knew the shape of paper. He brought it up tucked against his chest, and when he surfaced, he wasn't laughing.

There were letters inside. Dozens of them. And they were not love letters, whatever the oilskin hoped you'd think. They were confessions, promises, receipts — the kind of papers that wealthy men write with one hand while the other hand signs the checks that make the writing disappear. There was a name repeated through them like a signature under a signature: Halloway. Augustus Halloway himself, in those days, young and hungry, buying up the landing lot by lot, the wharf rights, the warehouse leases, the courts if the courts could be bought, and the silence of men if the courts could not.

Elias read them by lamplight in Maren's cabin, and his face got older as he read, the way a face does when it learns how large the world's appetite is.

"He dropped them," Maren said. "Off the steamboat. Somebody's courier, drunk or careless."

"Somebody's courier running," Elias said. "These aren't papers you carry for honest reasons. Look here — dated, witnessed. A man named Finch signed as witness on half of them." He tapped the page. "You know what these are worth, Mare?"

"I know what they're worth to whoever threw them off that boat. Nothing. Less than nothing."

"And to everybody else?"

Maren remembered leaning over his shoulder, smelling the river still on him, cold mud and green water, and saying: "To everybody else? Trouble. Trouble with a price tag."

He laughed then, and folded the letters back into their oilskin, and she thought that was the end of it. She thought wrong. Two nights later he borrowed her dive weights without asking and was gone until dawn, and when he came back his lips were blue and his eyes were bright as coins.

"Where are they?" she asked.

"Safe," he said. "Bottom of the channel, under the reef shelf, where no dredge will ever drag and no honest man would ever look. River keeps its own vault, Mare. I've made my deposit." He grinned, but it wasn't his river-grin; there was iron under it. "Someday somebody's going to need those papers more than Augustus Halloway needs them buried. When that day comes, the river will hand them over. It likes me."

"You're gambling with another man's secrets."

"No," Elias said quietly, and here his voice changed, became the voice Maren would remember at odd hours for the rest of her life, "I'm keeping them for a person who can't keep them for herself yet. I don't know who she is. Maybe she isn't born. But papers like these are always somebody's inheritance, Mare. Somebody, someday, is going to come looking for what's hers. And I intend the river to be able to answer."

That was Elias Montrose entire, Maren thought now, standing on the shingle with the ice groaning like a dying animal. A man who hid other people's inheritances in the river's vault because his faith in the future was deeper than his fear of the present. A man who died three years later with a debt on his back and a daughter on his knee, drowned in six feet of water not a quarter mile from where he'd stowed the evidence of everything Halloway had stolen from everyone.

They called it an accident. Maren had never called it anything at all, out loud. But she had noticed, in the way of women who survive by noticing, that Elias began diving alone shortly before he died, and that he dove near Widow's Reef, and that the last time she saw him alive he asked her, casual as anything, whether she thought the river ever forgot where it put things.

"The river forgets nothing," she had told him. "It just waits for spring."

He laughed and said, "Then spring can't come soon enough," and went home to his daughter, and two weeks later the water closed over him like a book.

* * *

Maren turned from the shingle and climbed the path back to her cabin, her knees announcing every step the way old joints do, and stood a moment at the window before going in. The baby was awake. She lay in the crate watching the lamplight with the absolute seriousness of infancy, and when Maren's shadow crossed her, she made a sound — not a cry, not quite a coo, something between — that went through Maren like a fishhook going through water: small, silent, and impossible to take back out.

Wendell was asleep on the floor beside the crate, sitting up with her head on the mattress edge, one arm thrown over the crate wall. She had fallen asleep mid-watch again. She worked fourteen hours mending nets and keeping ledgers for the company that owned her years, and then she came here and kept a different kind of vigil, unpaid and unasked, because no one had ordered her to forget this face and she had decided she wouldn't.

Maren eased the door open. Cold air came in with her and the baby blinked but didn't fuss.

"Wendell," she said softly.

The girl woke the way indentured children wake — instantly, guiltily, already reaching for work. Then her eyes found the baby and settled.

"She's fine," Wendell said. "I was just—"

"Asleep. Good. You'll need it." Maren lowered herself onto her stool by the stove. Her hands were aching; they always ached before weather, before trouble, before things she couldn't name. She put them out toward the fire and looked at them — diver's hands, scarred and split, nails long gone to ridges — and made her decision the way she made most decisions, which was slowly and then all at once.

"There's something you should know about your father," she said.

Wendell went very still. People did, when you said his name. Elias Montrose had been dead eleven years and the town had folded his memory away like a flag, and here was his daughter, sixteen years old, holding a stranger's baby in a fish crate, wearing the same expression he used to wear before he said something reckless.

"What about him?"

"Everyone remembers him as the best diver on the river. That's true, and it's also the smallest thing about him." Maren fed a stick of wood to the stove. "Do you know why your father drowned, girl?"

"The ledgers say—"

"The ledgers say accident. Your father didn't have accidents. He had five minutes of air, a body the river loved, and more sense in his little finger than Pike has in his whole office. If he drowned, it's because he chose to go down somewhere he shouldn't, or someone made certain he stayed down." She said it plainly because Wendell deserved plainness; sugar-coating grief was a cruelty dressed as kindness. "I never proved anything. But I'll tell you what I do know, and then you'll understand what you're carrying in that basket besides a baby."

And Maren told her. About the year of the great pearl, and the oilskin packet, and the letters with Halloway's name threaded through them like a vein of ore. About the reef shelf and the tarred cord and the vault at the bottom of the channel. About Elias's faith — absurd, magnificent — that someday someone would come looking for what was theirs, and that the river should be ready to answer.

Wendell listened without moving. The stove ticked. Outside, the ice shifted and boomed, a sound like a door being forced somewhere far away.

"The mother," Wendell said finally, and her voice was strange, careful, like someone walking on river ice. "The woman who left her at the workshop. You said whoever left the child would come back for silence. How did you know that, Maren? How did you know so quickly?"

Maren met her eyes. There it was. The girl had inherited more than her father's chin; she'd inherited the noticing.

"Because I've met her," Maren said. "Or I met her, once, two summers ago. A young woman came down to the camp at dusk, well-dressed but dressed wrong — fine clothes worn like a disguise, like she'd taken them off a line somewhere. She was carrying her belly high and she was frightened in a way I recognized. Frightened like a creature that's already run and knows the running hasn't worked." Maren's hands remembered it: how cold the evening had been for June, how the woman's knuckles were white around a small canvas bag. "She asked for Elias Montrose."

Wendell's breath caught. "But Papa was dead by then. Six years dead."

"Yes. And when I told her so, she wept like the ground had opened. Said his name over and over — *Mr. Montrose, Mr. Montrose* — like a prayer she'd been saving. Then she said, 'He helped my mother once. He said if ever I needed hiding, or needed finding, the divers would know.'" Maren let the words sit in the cabin air. "Her mother. A woman who came to the beds in years past, buying pearls quiet-like, away from the company brokers. A woman your father ferried across the channel one night against his better judgment, because she paid him in questions instead of coin, and he liked her questions. I never knew her name. But I knew what she was afraid of, because everyone on this stretch of river was afraid of the same thing in those days."

"Halloway," Wendell whispered.

"Augustus Halloway's household," Maren corrected. "Which is a different animal than the man. The daughter-in-law, some said. The ward, others said. A young woman with a claim on property that kept getting misfiled, mislaid, misplaced — every document that proved her standing vanished like breath off glass, and everyone who might have held a copy found reasons to move upriver." She shook her head. "Your father hid things for people, Wendell. It wasn't only the letters under Widow's Reef. He kept a ring for a lady once, and a christening record, and I believe — though he never said it straight — a letter sealed in wax, written by a woman putting down on paper what had been done to her, so that even if nobody believed her living, the ink would go on telling it."

The baby stirred and made her small hooking sound, and both women looked at her. In the stove light her face was solemn and new, and Maren thought: *there is the answer to a question Elias asked the river thirty years before she was born.*

"When she left the child with you," Wendell said slowly, "she knew what she was doing. She didn't leave her anywhere. She left her at the *company workshop* — at the one place in Brannock's Landing where the Montrose girl works, where the daughter of Elias would find her."

"I've been turning that over since you came down the path," Maren admitted. "It's either a desperate woman's luck or a desperate woman's plan, and I've lived long enough to distrust luck. Either way, she chose you, girl. Out of everyone on this river, she put her child in your arms."

Wendell looked down at the baby, and Maren watched something settle in the young face — not peace, nothing so gentle, but purpose arriving like a keel settling into water. "The papers," Wendell said. "Papa's papers. Under Widow's Reef. Are they still—"

"Eleven years is a long time for a river," Maren said, and heard her own voice go careful. "Currents change. Dredges drag. But the shelf is limestone and the hollow behind it fills with silt slower than anywhere else in the channel, and if any spot on this river kept a promise, it's that one." She rose, joints cracking, and went to the chest at the foot of her bed. From under wool blankets and a bundle of old dive lines she drew a lantern and a coiled rope, and laid them on the table between them like cards.

Wendell stared at the rope, then at Maren, understanding dawning and dawning badly. "You're not serious. Not now — the ice—"

"The ice is exactly why it must be now," Maren said. "Come thaw, the channel runs brown and fast and no one dives it till summer, and by summer Corliss and whoever holds Corliss's leash will have burned or bulldozed or bought their way through everything softer than stone. We have a window, girl, and windows on this river close quick." She picked up the lantern. "I'm too old to make that dive. My lungs quit keeping accounts ten years ago. But I know the shelf by feel — I can put a hand on it blind, I've done it a thousand times — and I can hold the line."

"Maren, the water under that ice is barely above freezing. Four minutes. Five. If you cramp, if the line fouls—"

"If I drown," Maren said, not unkindly, "then you'll have inherited more than a duty, Wendell Montrose. You'll have inherited the whole of it — the vault, the secret, the legacy your father sank in trust for a child he'd never meet. And you'll make the dive yourself, come summer, and God keep you while you do." She looked at the baby, asleep again with her fist curled tight, and then back at the girl who had refused to forget her. "But I'd rather it went this way. Your father trusted the river. So do I."

Wendell stood. She was trembling, Maren saw — fear and fury and something larger than both, moving through her like current under ice. She reached out and touched the baby's head, one small stroke, the way you'd touch a charm for luck.

"All right," she said hoarsely. "All right. Tonight, before moonrise. Tell me where the shelf is. Tell me everything — every rock, every turn, everything he ever told you."

Maren was already pulling her oilskins from their peg. "Everything," she agreed. "Though I warn you — the last thing he ever told me was that spring can't come soon enough." She paused at the door, listening to the river boom and groan in the dark beyond it, shifting its ancient weight, balancing its books. "Well, girl. Let's go give it a reason to hurry."

* * *

They came down to the water an hour later, two shadows and a lantern, and the hole Maren chose sat in the lee of Widow's Reef where the ice was youngest and darkest. As she knelt to test it with her axe, the surface shuddered under her palms — and far downstream, faint as a heartbeat, she heard something else that froze her faster than the river ever had.

A boat horn. One short blast, then silence, then another — the signal pearlers used to mark a landing on an unfamiliar shore.

Somebody else, Maren thought, rising slowly, axe in hand, was on the ice tonight. And they were headed upriver — toward the reef, or toward the camp, or toward the child sleeping back in her cabin in a crate lined with wool.

"Get behind the willows," she breathed. "Now."
