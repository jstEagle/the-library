## 11. The Letter, Opened

The loft above the workshop held its breath with me that night.

I had waited three days to open it. Three days of feeding Pearl at odd hours, of mending nets under Pike's suspicious eye, of carrying the sealed envelope against my ribs like a coal banked in cloth — warm from my body, and dangerous in the same way. Three days is a long time to hold your breath. By Thursday I had begun to dream about it, and the dreams were worse than any thought I'd had awake, because in them the envelope spoke aloud, in my father's voice, and told me things I did not want to know.

So on Thursday night, when the workshop had gone quiet and the last lamp in the bunkhouse guttered out, I climbed to the net loft with a candle stub, a box of matches, and the letter.

Pearl slept in the crook of an old netting basket lined with flannel Maren had brought, her fists curled near her ears the way infants do, as if even in sleep they are bracing against the noise of the world. She had a milk-drunk heaviness to her breathing, that deep little bellows rhythm that means a child trusts the room she's in. I envied her that. I could not remember ever trusting a room.

I sat cross-legged on the floorboards with the cache spread before me: the bundle of letters tied in ribbon gone stiff with age, the woman's ring with its small pearl worn smooth, and the sealed envelope lying apart from the rest like a guest who has not yet been introduced.

I had read the loose letters twice through by then. They were from the mother — I was sure now it was the mother — to someone she called only *my dear confessor*, a name that struck me as odd until I remembered my father's habit of writing to men he trusted with half his soul and no part of his name. The letters were dated across two years, spring of 1889 through the winter just past. They began light, almost girlish, full of talk of river birds and a horse named Bucephalus who feared puddles. They darkened by degrees, the way the river darkens in October — not all at once but so gradually you cannot name the day the color changed.

*I have found my name in the company books,* she wrote in August. *Not my name — the other one, the one they invented for me. It is astonishing how easy theft becomes when you first rename the thing stolen.*

And later, in November: *Mr. Halloway smiled at me across the ledger desk today and asked after my health as though my mother had not died in his debt-house with her deeds locked in his strongroom. He has a beautiful smile. It is the kind of smile that should be entered into evidence.*

I had copied some of these phrases onto scrap paper, then burned the scraps, not knowing why except that it felt safer, the way you'd whisper a secret rather than say it plain. Now there was only the sealed one left. The last letter. Dated the eleventh of December, 1891 — nine days before the child appeared at our door wrapped in oilcloth.

My hands were not steady. I noted this the way Papa taught me to note the weather, without judgment, simply as fact: wind rising, take in sail. He used to say a diver's hands must be steady or she'll lose everything worth having down in the dark. He said it about pearls. I understood now he might as well have said it about everything.

I struck the match. The candle caught, and its light made a small gold room in the vast dark of the loft, and inside that gold room it was just me and the letter and the sleeping child and whatever truth had been folded up eight months ago by a woman's hand and sealed with a drop of wax the color of old blood.

The wax cracked under my thumbnail with a sound like a step on river ice.

* * *

The hand was different from the other letters. That was the first thing I saw. The earlier ones had slanted and hurried, alive; this one was set down slow and square, every letter standing upright like a soldier at inspection. A document, written by someone who knew it might be read by enemies. Who knew it might be read by lawyers.

It began without greeting.

*To whom it may concern, and to the courts of record of this county, and most especially to my daughter, if God grants her years enough to read it:*

My eyes snagged on *my daughter* and would not move past it for a long moment. Above me the rafters creaked, settling their old bones. Below, the river muttered against the pilings, that low constant conversation water holds with wood.

*I, Evangeline Rooksby, being of sound mind and failing body, do make this declaration freely, knowing it may cost me what remains of my life, and valuing my daughter's future above that remainder.*

Rooksby. I turned the name over. It was not in the company ledgers — I had searched it already, twice — but something in it rang against another name like one bell against another. Rooksby. And then I had it: *Rooksby & Hale, Salvage and Pearling Concern, est. 1868.* The firm whose assets Halloway Trading Company had absorbed, per the ledgers, "in settlement of outstanding obligations" in the winter of 1870. Absorbed. What a soft word for it. Like saying the river *absorbed* my father.

*My mother, Cordelia Rooksby, founded the Concern with her own capital and her own pearl beds, leased and licensed under her own signature, copies of which signatures exist and may be produced. Upon her death in December of 1870, Silas Halloway presented to the probate court a document styled as her will, which left everything to him. This document is false. My mother made her true will in September of that year, in my presence and in the presence of the witness named below. In it she left the Concern, the beds, and all properties to me, her daughter, with provision for my children and their children.*

*I have spent twenty-one years asking for what is mine in the manner permitted to women and to debtors, which is to say politely, and in writing, and to no effect. I attach a list of my petitions. Each was received. None was answered. Mr. Halloway's latest answer was to buy my debts from my creditors, so that he now holds the notes against my own house, and can turn me out of it whenever the inconvenience of my continued existence outweighs the poor appearance of doing so.*

There was more in that vein — dates, amounts, the patient terrible arithmetic of dispossession, each figure set down square and upright like a soldier — and I confess I skimmed some of it, because halfway down the second page the letter stopped being about property.

*But papers can be contested only by the living, and Mr. Halloway has grasped this better than any court. I write plainly, then, since plainness is all that remains to me: I believe he intends my death, and intends it to look like the debts catching up with me, which is a thing the river does often enough in this town that no one will ask twice. If I die suddenly, know this: I did not drown myself, and I did not fall, and my daughter —*

Here the ink blotched. Not a tear, I decided, studying it — a hand pressed too hard, a nib held too long in one place while the writer gathered herself. Then the upright soldier-hand resumed, tighter now.

*— my daughter, Cordelia, called Delia, born the fourth of June last, is the sole lawful heir to the whole of the Rooksby estate: the pearl beds, the salvage rights, the warehouse lots, and the company itself, which Silas Halloway stole and has traded under his own name these twenty-one years. Let this letter stand as testimony. Let it be witnessed. Let it be kept by someone who owes me nothing and therefore cannot be bought.*

*The witness is Tobias Finch, formerly clerk to my mother, who saw her sign and seal the true will and who signed beneath it. Mr. Finch is honest beyond his courage, which is a common condition among clerks, and I forgive him for it. If he yet lives, he will confirm all herein.*

Below, in a smaller, shakier hand than either of the others — a third hand, a clerk's hand, cramped from decades of entries — someone had written:

*Witnessed this 11th day of December, 1891, by me, Tobias Finch, who saw Evangeline Rooksby sign the foregoing of her own free will, and who attests that her statement herein accords with what he himself saw in September of 1870. God forgive us all our silence. T.F.*

And beneath that, Evangeline once more, one line only:

*Her name is Delia. Her face is the face of everything he took. Do not let him make the world forget it.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. The candle had burned down to a fat golden thumb of wax when I heard myself exhale, one long unspooling breath, like a diver surfacing after a dive that went too deep.

A weapon made of ink. That was the phrase that came to me, and it would not leave. I had never held a weapon in my life. I had held netting needles and ledger pens and my father's cold hands the morning they brought him up the bank, and none of those had changed anything. But this — this thin fold of paper, light as a moth wing, smelling faintly of lavender and lamp-oil and the sick-sweet ghost of laudanum (she had been taking it, then; the shakiness at the end was not only fear) — this could pull a company down. This could put a name on a deed where another name had squatted for twenty-one years. This could feed and school and dower a girl who was at that moment asleep in a netting basket three feet from my knee, her breath going in and out with the tide-rhythm of the river below.

This could also get us both killed. Both thoughts arrived together, holding hands, the way awe and horror do.

Because I understood now what I had only sensed before, in the shape of one set of footprints filling with snow, in Pike's too-quick eagerness to be rid of her. Whoever had carried Delia to our door in that oilcloth had not been abandoning her. They had been hiding her. There was a difference, and the difference was the length and breadth of this letter. You abandon what you don't want. You hide what must survive you. Someone — the father? a servant? Evangeline herself, in her last days of laudanum and failing breath? — had known the mother was dying or dead, had known the letter existed, had known that the child was the only living proof the letter was worth killing over, and had chosen our door. Of all doors in Brannock's Landing, ours. Perhaps at random. Perhaps not. Perhaps my father's old friendship with the divers ran deeper even than Maren had said, threads running under the town the way the pearl beds did, invisible and load-bearing.

And whoever had hidden the child might be found, and questioned, and the questioning done with lantern hooks and river ice. Which meant every hour Delia stayed in my keeping, I was not merely caring for her. I was standing between her and men whose arithmetic had no column for children.

I looked at the ring again, the pearl worn smooth. Worn by wear, I realized — not by polishing. Somebody had worn this ring for years, turning it, worrying it, the way I worried my father's memory. Evangeline, perhaps, wearing her mother's pearl the way the divers' wives wore theirs: not as ornament but as covenant. *What the river gives, the river keeps, unless somebody keeps it first.*

Papa's voice, then, clear as if he stood behind me in the loft: *A pearl's only worth what someone will swear it's worth, Wendell-girl. Everything else is ledger-talk.* He'd meant it as a joke about buyers in Brannock's Landing who lowballed the divers. Tonight it sounded like scripture. Here was a piece of paper swearing the worth of a child, witnessed and dated, and everything else — the ledgers, the deeds, the great gray weight of Halloway Trading Company — was ledger-talk.

I folded the letter along its creases. My hands were steady now. Wind fallen, I reported to my father silently. Sail trimmed. And underneath, in the place where I kept the things I did not say even to myself: *I am afraid, Papa. I have never in my life done anything that mattered, and I am afraid, and I am going to do it anyway.*

That was the moment I heard the stair boards.

Not the center boards — anyone walking the stairs honestly stepped center, where the treads were solid. This was the outside edge, the careful step, the step of someone climbing slowly in the dark so as not to be heard, which is itself a kind of announcement. I pinched the candle out. The loft dropped into darkness salted weakly by moonlight through the loading hatch. I slid the letter into the cache-box, shut the lid, pushed the basket with Pearl in it into the deepest shadow behind the net-hanging frame, and put myself between her and the stairs with a boat hook taken up off the wall, its wooden shaft smooth and familiar in my grip.

The steps came on. Slow. Counting them the way I'd count fathoms on a sounding line: four. Six. Eight. At ten they stopped, and a voice said quietly, reasonably, from the foot of the ladder —

"Don't trouble yourself, Miss Montrose. If I'd come to do harm, I'd not have let you hear me."

It was Corliss. The polite stranger with the river-stone eyes, the man who had offered me money three nights ago for "anything the mother may have left," and to whom I had lied with a steadiness that had surprised us both.

"I've been thinking," he went on, conversational, terrible, "about what a shame it would be. Fire season coming early this year, the way the thaw's run ahead of itself. Old buildings like this one — dry as tinder. A man wonders what a person would try to carry out of a fire, if she had warning." A pause, and I heard him shift his weight on the bottom stair. "I'll ask plainer, then. Has anything come into your hands lately that isn't yours?"

Behind me, in the dark, Pearl stirred and made a small sound — half sigh, half question — and Corliss heard it. I knew he heard it by the quality of his silence, which changed the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere in it.

"Well now," said Corliss softly. "That's a pity."
