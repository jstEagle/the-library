## 12. Pike's Bargain

The ledger room smelled of tallow and dust and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled by too many hands for too many years. It was the smell of money, Wendell thought — not the bright river-smell of coins passing palm to palm at the pearl market, but the closed-room smell of it, hoarded, counted, argued over in low voices while outside the ice groaned like something dying.

She had been copying figures since the noon bell. That was the punishment no one called a punishment: Aldous Pike had simply appeared at her elbow that morning with a stack of shipping manifests as thick as a hymnal and said, *You'll work inside this week, Montrose. My eyes need rest.* And because she was indentured, and because refusing was a word her mouth had never learned to shape, she had taken the manifests and sat down at the slanted desk beneath the one high window, where the light came down thin and gray as ditchwater.

It should have been a misfortune. It became an opportunity, and she hated herself a little for how quickly she recognized it.

Because the manifest stack sat beside the company's correspondence drawer, and the correspondence drawer had been left unlocked, and three nights ago she had read — in the loft, by candle stub, with Pearl asleep against her collarbone — a sealed letter whose handwriting matched the hand on these ledgers as surely as a child matches its mother's face. She only needed dates. Dates were patient things. Dates did not know they were evidence.

So while Pike sat at the far end of the room scratching his pen across a payroll sheet, his shoulders curved like a question mark, Wendell copied freight tonnage with her right hand and turned pages of the February ledger with her left, her eyes sliding sideways, sideways, always sideways, the way she had learned to look at everything in this place without ever appearing to look at all.

*February 14th, 1889.* The letter was dated February 11th. Three days. What had happened in those three days?

She found it in the disbursement column, halfway down the page, written small and unremarkable as weather: *Disb. — E.H. settlement, final — $400 — marked paid.* And below it, in the margin, in Pike's own cramped hand, a notation she had seen a hundred times before without ever seeing it: *closed per instruction.*

Settlement. Final. Marked paid.

But the woman who wrote that letter — the woman whose ring lay wrapped in oilcloth in the net loft upstairs, beside a sleeping child who would be one year old come thaw — that woman had never been paid. Wendell knew it the way you know a stone is cold: instantly, completely, in the marrow. A woman who received four hundred dollars did not write letters begging for what was hers. A woman marked *paid, closed* did not die in the charity ward at Miller's Bend with no name on the register except the one the nurse remembered giving: *she said her family name was Halloway, but I supposed she was proud, poor thing.*

Wendell's pen stopped. A drop of ink gathered at the nib, swelled, and fell — a black star blooming on the freight column.

"Clumsy," said Aldous Pike.

She had not heard him cross the room. She never heard him; he moved the way cats move through rooms that fear them, silent because everything else went still. He was standing over the desk now, and his eyes — pale, lashless, the color of watered milk — were not on the inkblot at all.

They were on the February ledger.

"You're on manifests," he said quietly.

"The figure wouldn't reconcile." Her voice surprised her. It was steady. Somewhere in the last month she had grown a second Wendell who spoke when the first one froze. "I thought I'd check the source."

Something shifted behind Pike's face — not anger. She had prepared herself for anger, had already composed the apology, the bowed head, the small correction of a girl who mends nets and knows her place. But what moved through his expression instead was older and more terrible than anger. It was recognition. He looked at her the way a man looks at a knot in a rope that he knows, from long bitter experience, is going to slip.

"Close the ledger," he said.

She closed it. Her hands did not shake. Later she would understand that this was the moment her hands stopped shaking for good.

Pike walked to the door. He turned the key in it — a small brass sound, absurdly gentle — and then he stood with his back against it, arms folded, and looked at her for a long time without speaking. Outside, the river made its low grinding noise, the sound it had made all week, ice plates shifting and settling, the whole frozen spine of the Brannock beginning to feel the spring coming up through it like a fever.

"I've been foreman of this company for nineteen years," Pike said at last. "In nineteen years I have watched men steal rope, steal nails, steal hours, steal each other's wages out of coat pockets. I know every kind of thief there is." He tilted his head. "You're not stealing money."

"No, sir."

"What is it, then? The letters?"

Wendell said nothing. Silence, her father used to say, is a net too — hold it loose or it tears.

Pike unfolded his arms. He crossed the room slowly, took the chair opposite the desk, and sat down in it with a heaviness that seemed to cost him something. Up close, in the flat winter light, she could see how old he had gotten. There was chalk-white scalp showing through his iron-gray hair, and the skin under his jaw hung soft, and there was a tremor in his left hand that he stilled by pressing it flat against his knee.

"How much do you know," he said, "about the mother?"

It was not the question she expected, and the surprise must have shown, because he nodded as if she'd answered.

"That's enough," he said. "That's answer enough."

"She died," Wendell said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "At Miller's Bend. In January of last year. She'd been writing to this company for eight months asking for what was hers, and someone in this room marked her account *paid* and *closed* while she was still alive to be lied about."

"Yes," Pike said. Simply. Without flinching, which was somehow worse than any denial. "Yes, that's very nearly the whole of it."

"And the child—"

"Is Edwina Halloway's granddaughter." He said the name the way a man says the name of deep water. "The daughter of the eldest son who died in the fall of '88, unmarried, inconveniently, after fathering a child on a woman from the upriver branch of the family. Edwina wanted the line erased. Not the child killed—" and here his eyes flickered, and Wendell understood that the distinction had perhaps not always held, "—only erased. Made nobody. You cannot inherit if you are nobody, Montrose. That is the entire architecture of the law, and Edwina Halloway built half of it herself."

The stove ticked in the corner. Somewhere above them, faint through the floorboards, a board creaked in the net loft, and Wendell's heart lurched toward it — *Pearl, the baby, my baby, ours* — and steadied itself again.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

Pike looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached into his breast pocket and drew out a folded paper — cream-colored, official, creased so many times the folds had gone furry at their edges — and laid it on the desk between them, face down, his flat hand resting on it like a man restraining something alive.

"Nineteen years," he said again. "Do you know what I was promised at the end of nineteen years? A house. A share. A name on the company rolls next to the Halloways'. Do you know what I got last month, when I asked?" His mouth twisted. "A bonus. Fifty dollars. *For continued diligence.* As if I were a dog that had learned a trick." He exhaled through his nose, slow. "I have spent my life keeping accounts for people who believe that everything can be closed. Every debt. Every person. Marked *paid*, marked *dead*, filed away." His hand tightened on the paper. "And now there's a letter somewhere in my workshop — in *my* loft, under my roof, though God knows it was never mine any more than I was theirs — a letter that opens every one of those accounts again. And a child who is either nobody or the richest heiress on this river, depending entirely on whether that piece of paper exists at sunrise on any given day."

He turned the document over.

It was her indenture contract. Her own name — *Wendell Montrose, aged fourteen years* — written in her father's careful hand at the bottom, beside the signature he'd signed with a broken finger, the winter the diving accident took his hearing in one ear and the company took the rest of him by installments. Four years of service rendered against seven owed. Three years still standing between her and freedom, like bars made of ink.

Beside it, Pike laid a second paper. This one was new, stamped with the company seal, and it needed no reading: *Certificate of Discharge — all obligations satisfied.*

Freedom papers.

The stove ticked. The river groaned. Wendell sat perfectly still and let herself want it — let the wanting move through her whole body the way cold moves through wet cloth. Three years. Three years of Pike's manifests and Pike's ledgers and Pike's voice saying *Montrose* in that flat tone that made her smaller. Three years of being furniture that could read and write. Gone. Finished. In exchange for—

For a dead woman's voice. For a confession witnessed and dated, naming Edwina Halloway's theft in language even a court could not pretend not to hear. For the only proof on God's green earth that a certain child was somebody.

"You'd give me this," Wendell said slowly, "for the letter."

"For the letter and everything with it. Ring, papers, all of it. Burned or surrendered, my choice." Pike's milk-pale eyes did not waver. "And in return, this conversation never happened, the child goes upriver to the divers where no one will trouble to find her, and you walk out of this company a free woman. Twenty-one years old, your own wages, your own name. No man holding a paper over you for the rest of your life." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost — almost — gentle. "Think, girl. I've watched you since you came here with your father's debts around your neck. You think like a bookkeeper. So bookkeep it. What is that letter worth to *you*? Nothing. It proves a stranger's claim. But your freedom—" he tapped the discharge certificate once, sharply, "—your freedom is worth everything, and you are trading it away for somebody else's war."

*Somebody else's.* Wendell looked down at the two documents lying side by side on the desk — her servitude and her liberty, offered and priced — and thought of the note that had come pinned to an oilcloth bundle on the coldest night of the winter. *Forget this face.*

Somebody had ordered the world to forget that face. Somebody had marked a living child *paid* and *closed*. And here was the machinery of that forgetting, sitting across the desk from her, offering to buy the last copy of her memory at the price of her own future.

"There's a flaw in your arithmetic, Mr. Pike," she said.

His eyebrow lifted.

"If I trade you the letter," Wendell said, "and Edwina Halloway learns tomorrow that it existed — that I existed, that I read it — then your freedom papers protect me exactly as long as it takes a hired man to row across the river some night. You know that better than I do. You've seen what happens to people who know too much. You marked the accounts yourself." She met his eyes. "My freedom isn't worth anything if I'm not alive to spend it. And the child isn't safe until the truth is *on record*, not hidden in a net loft waiting to burn. You taught me how this company thinks. Don't insult me by pretending otherwise."

For a moment — one long breath — something that might have been respect moved across Aldous Pike's face, followed immediately by something that might have been grief.

"No," he said quietly. "I don't suppose I can." He gathered the discharge certificate back, but he did not take the contract; he left it lying on the desk between them, face up, three years of her life written in faded brown ink. "Then we understand each other poorly, which may be the most honest arrangement on this river. You keep your letter and your war. I keep my fifty dollars and my shame." He rose, unlocked the door, and paused with his hand on the latch, not turning around.

"One thing," he said. "Since your arithmetic is so good. The wreckers are contracted. The lot clears before the thaw regardless of what burns and what doesn't. If you have business that needs finishing—" now he did turn, and the lamplight caught the tremor in his hand, "—finish it soon, Montrose. The river won't wait politely, and neither will Edwina Halloway. There's a man named Corliss who reports to her directly, and he stopped believing my reports three weeks ago. Whatever he tells her next, she'll act on it."

He opened the door and was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor, leaving the cold rushing in behind him.

Wendell sat alone with the ledger room's ticking stove and her own contract lying open on the desk, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had just refused her freedom — that she had held it in her hands, warm as a loaf, and given it back. She pressed her palms flat against the table and breathed, once, twice, and grieved for the girl she might have been, quick and quiet and free, walking out of Brannock's Landing with clean papers and no one's blood on her conscience.

Then she folded the contract, tucked it inside her shirt against her heart — *they will not have it to burn over me* — and climbed the stairs to the net loft two at a time, because Corliss had stopped believing, and the ice on the river had begun, that very hour, to sing.
