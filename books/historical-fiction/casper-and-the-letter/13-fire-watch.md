## 13. Fire Watch

The lamp oil came in tins, and the tins came from Halloway's own shelves, and that was the first thing Corliss noticed about the job — how tidy it was, how everything he needed had been sitting in plain sight for years, waiting for a man with the right orders to pick it up. He counted them out on the floor of his rented room above the cooperage: six tins, each stamped with the company mark, each heavy as a sleeping cat. Enough to eat a building. Enough to leave nothing but a black rectangle of ash where the net workshop had stood since before either of their grandfathers could remember.

He had done this kind of work before. That was the trouble with hiring men like him — you got a man who knew how, and a man who knew how was a man who had. Warehouses in two river towns downriver, a mill whose owner had been asking too many questions about timber rights, a granary fire that had taken a neighboring stable with it and a horse he still sometimes saw when he closed his eyes too long. He told himself the usual things. That buildings were only buildings. That the people inside would be paid out of insurance or pity or whatever passed for justice in Brannock's Landing. That a man who asked no names did no harm to anyone in particular.

But this time there was a name. This time there was a face.

* * *

The order had come the way they always came — not spoken, not written where a court could read it, but arranged. A note left at the ferry office in a hand he'd come to know: *The workshop is a liability. It should not survive the thaw. See that it doesn't.* And beneath that, in smaller letters, the part that had sat in his chest like a swallowed stone for three days now: *The child need not be found afterward.*

Not *the child must not be harmed.* Not even *let her go.* Just *need not be found.* The grammar of men who wanted a thing done without ever having said it.

Corliss had carried children before, in the course of other work. Carried them out of houses, once, when the fires had gone wrong — because they do go wrong, always, eventually, and a man who burns buildings learns quickly that walls hold heat like ovens and stairs fail like rotten teeth. He had never been ordered to make sure one stayed inside. He had simply never been ordered to make sure one got out, and the difference between those two things was a country wide, and he had spent thirty-nine years living in it without looking down.

Until the night he had carried her himself.

It had been an accident of timing, one of the few times he'd gotten close enough. The workshop door had stood open a hand's width — the girl Wendell airing the place out, careless, or tired, or both — and he had seen his chance to look inside, to count doors and windows the way his employer paid him to count them. But the baby had been crying. A thin, furious, exhausted sound, the sound of a small animal that has been cold too long and has decided, with all the authority of its eight months, that this was intolerable.

And then the girl had come around the corner of the drying shed with her arms full of firewood, and there had been nowhere for him to be except where he was, and no way to explain himself except the truth, which was that he had heard a child crying and come to see after her. Which was even true. Truth is useful that way; it fits so many hands.

The girl had looked at him the way a deer looks at a man standing very still near the treeline. She hadn't screamed. He remembered being surprised by that — most girls her age would have screamed, or run, or both. Instead she had shifted the wood onto one hip and said, in a voice flat as river ice, "If you've business with the company, the office is across the yard."

"I heard the little one," he'd said. "She's been crying a while."

"She's been teething." A pause, the length of a held breath. Then, in a different voice — quieter, almost unwilling, the voice of someone making a decision against her own judgment — "Do you want to hold her? I've got to get this wood in before my arms give out."

He should have said no. Every instinct honed over twenty years of this trade said *no, keep your distance, keep your hands empty, keep yourself a shadow.* But she had already been turning toward the door, trusting him or pretending to trust him or testing him, he still didn't know which, and the baby was there in the doorway wrapped in a blanket that had once been a flour sack, red-faced and furious, and some old buried thing in him — some thing older than the trade, older than the granary horse, older than every careful lie — had reached out its arms before he could stop it.

She had weighed almost nothing. That was what undid him, later, lying awake in the room above the cooperage. She had weighed almost nothing, and she had stopped crying the moment he picked her up, as if she could tell the difference between arms that meant harm and arms that didn't, as if the knowing were bred into her somewhere deeper than thought. She had grabbed his thumb with a hand the size of a walnut and held on with the serious, total grip of an infant, and she had looked up at him with eyes the color of strong tea and studied his face with an expression he could only describe as *assessment*, as if she were deciding something about him that would stand for years.

Then she had yawned, hugely, and fallen asleep against his coat, and the girl had come back and taken her, and thanked him — *thanked him*, coolly, watchfully, the way you thank a dog you haven't decided about yet — and shut the door.

He had walked back to the cooperage in the dark and stood outside it for a long time, listening to the river grind its ice, and he had understood, with the clarity that comes only after the fact, that he had just held the thing he was hired to erase.

* * *

Now it was the last week of February, and the ice on the river had begun to talk.

Everyone in Brannock's Landing talked about it — in the taverns, at the ferry office, in the queue at the company store. The ice was shifting early this year, groaning in the nights like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and the old men who read the river the way priests read scripture shook their heads and said *early break, bad break, water higher than any year since '71.* The town had begun, in its slow collective way, to prepare: boats dragged up above the high-water line, goods moved to upper floors, the low streets along the wharf marked with fresh chalk lines showing where the flood of '71 had reached.

And Halloway Trading Company had posted its notice on the workshop door, neat as a hymnal page: the lot sold, the structure condemned, demolition to commence upon the first safe day of thaw. Six days, the notice said. Six days, and then the wreckers' hammers, and then the razing, and then — if the fire did its work first — nothing at all but ash and a story about a stove that caught.

That was the design. His employer's design, communicated in that same bloodless hand: burn it before the wreckers come, let the thaw-flood scatter the ashes and drown the questions, let the river do what rivers do, which is take things and keep them. By the time anyone thought to ask what became of the indentured girl and the foundling, there would be no workshop, no ledger, no letter — if there was a letter — and no faces to connect to anything. Water and fire, the two oldest servants, working for the same master.

Corliss had burned buildings in February before. He knew the arithmetic of it: oil soaked into the sill timbers, set low where the flames climb instead of spread, lit on the windward side so the draft pulls it through. A drying shed full of hemp nets and canvas would go up like a struck match and carry the main roof with it. The net loft above — where he believed, with reasonable certainty, the girl kept the child hidden — would fill with smoke long before flame reached it. Smoke kills faster than fire and more quietly. A person asleep in a loft above a burning shed might never wake at all.

He knew all of this the way a surgeon knows anatomy, and for twenty years the knowledge had sat in him without weight, the way a hammer sits in a hand.

Now it had weight. Now, when he laid out the tins on the floor of his room and imagined the placement, he saw not a building but a loft, and in the loft a cradle made from a fish crate, and in the cradle a child with tea-colored eyes who gripped his thumb as though thumbs were promises.

* * *

On the third night of the six, he went to look at the workshop. He told himself it was reconnaissance — checking sightlines, confirming the shed's construction, noting where the watchman's rounds would put him. All true. None of it the reason.

The river was loud that night. The ice boomed and cracked out in the channel like artillery, and beneath the booming ran a continuous groan, deep and grinding, the sound of a frozen giant being slowly woken. Snow came down fine and dry, sifting through the lamplight from the company office window, and the workshop stood dark against it, its windows reflecting nothing, its eaves heavy with white.

He stood in the shadow of the drying shed and listened. From inside the main building, faintly, he heard singing. A woman's voice — the girl Wendell, he supposed — low and tuneless, half-hummed, the kind of sound a person makes without knowing they're making it, a lullaby worn smooth by repetition. And beneath it, softer, the small wet sounds of an infant settling toward sleep.

He had stood in the shadows of many buildings he was going to burn. He had never heard one sing.

He thought about the granary horse. It had been fourteen years ago, and the fire had spread the way fire does when the wind changes, and by the time he understood what was happening the stable wall was already glowing and there had been no way in that a man could live through. He had stood in the snow and listened to it scream. In the morning there had been a black smear where the building was and a story in the paper about a lightning strike in winter, rare but not unheard of, and he had taken his pay and moved upriver and told himself the usual things until the telling wore thin and the horse faded to a shape rather than a sound.

This would not fade to a shape. This would be a voice, humming, cut off mid-verse. This would be a weight in his arms that trusted him.

*Need not be found,* the note had said. He turned the phrase over the way you turn a coin you suspect is clipped. Whoever wrote it had never held her. Whoever wrote it thought of her as a line item, a risk to be struck from a ledger, the same arithmetic Foreman Pike applied to children and the company applied to everything — cost, benefit, remainder. Corliss had spent his whole life serving that arithmetic. He was good at it. That was the whole of what he was good at.

But arithmetic had a flaw, and the flaw was that it required the counter to feel nothing, and somewhere between the granary and the doorway of the net workshop, Corliss had discovered that he felt things whether he consented to or not. The horse had started it. The child had finished it.

* * *

On the fifth night, he made his decision, though he did not yet know what it was.

He went to the workshop again — openly this time, lamplight visible, hands in plain view, because he had learned that the safest disguise for a dangerous errand is honesty about everything except its purpose. The girl opened the door on the chain, saw him, and after a long moment unhooked it.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"If you've come about the child—"

"I've come about the firewood," he said. "Your pile's low. And the ice is talking. Cold nights ahead."

It was even mostly true. She studied him with those level gray eyes — she had her father's eyes, he'd learn later, though he didn't know it yet — and something passed over her face that was not trust but was adjacent to it, the wariness of someone who has decided to find out what a thing wants before deciding what it is.

She let him stack wood. He stacked it well, close under the eave where it would stay dry, and while he worked she stood in the doorway with the baby on her hip, watching him the whole time, and the baby watched him too, solemnly, over the girl's shoulder, with those tea-colored eyes.

At the end, he straightened, dusted the snow off his knees, and said — carefully, each word placed like a foot on river ice:

"This lot won't stand past the thaw. You know that. Whatever you're keeping here—" he nodded, slightly, at the bundle on her hip, and at the loft above, and let the sentence hang, "—it shouldn't be here when the wreckers come. Or before."

The girl went very still. The baby, feeling the stillness, made a small questioning sound.

"And why," she said quietly, "would you tell me that?"

Because I have burned four buildings and a stable and a man's livelihood twice over, he did not say. Because I was sent here to add this one to the list, and I stood in your yard last night and heard you sing. Because there is a horse screaming in a snowfield fourteen years gone and a weight the size of a loaf of bread asleep against my coat, and I cannot carry both anymore.

"Because the river's breaking early," he said instead. "And things that wait for the water usually lose to it."

She looked at him for a long time. Snow settled on both their shoulders. Somewhere out in the channel the ice boomed like a door slamming in a great house, and beneath it the groan went on and on, patient, enormous, inevitable.

"The night after tomorrow," she said at last, so quietly he had to lean in to catch it. "There's a boat. If you're the kind of man who tells people to leave before the fire, maybe you're the kind of man who rows."

He hadn't told her about the fire. He was certain he hadn't told her about the fire. He looked at her, and she looked back, and he understood that she had known — perhaps from the first night, perhaps from the woodpile, perhaps from the simple fact that men like him are known to women like her the way weather is known to sailors — and that she had let him stack her wood anyway, and that this was either the bravest thing he had ever witnessed or the most desperate, and that it hardly mattered which.

"The night after tomorrow," he said.

He walked back through the falling snow with the tins of oil still sitting in his room above the cooperage, unopened, and behind him the workshop glowed faintly at its windows, warm, alive, singing — and out on the river the ice groaned and shifted and began, at last, to come apart.
