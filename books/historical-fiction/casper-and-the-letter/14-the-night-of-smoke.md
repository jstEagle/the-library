## 14. The Night of Smoke

The night the fire came, I did not sleep so much as hover above sleep, the way a gull hangs over water without landing.

I had learned that trick from my father. On the pearl beds he would rest in the bottom of a skiff with his hat over his eyes and swear he heard every shift of the current beneath him, every groan of the river's bones. *A diver sleeps with one ear open, Wendell,* he told me when I was small enough to sit between his knees. *The river doesn't wait for you to wake before it changes its mind.*

So I was not asleep — not truly — when I smelled it.

Smoke has a hundred voices. Woodsmoke from the stove is patient, domestic, almost kind. Tobacco smoke drifts and lingers like gossip. But smoke that comes where no fire should be has a different voice entirely: it arrives flat and wrong, tasting of char and kerosene sweetness, and it does not drift. It climbs.

I sat up in my cot behind the ledger room with Pearl already against my chest before I understood why she was crying. She had woken first. Babies always know.

The drying shed stood at the workshop's western flank, a long low building where nets hung in tiers to cure and where we kept the lamp oil and the pitch. It should have been empty at three in the morning. It should have been cold. Instead, through the gap beneath my door, the light was moving — not steady as lamplight moves, but leaping, restless, alive.

I did not think. Thinking is for people who have time. I wrapped Pearl in the wool blanket Maren had brought, tucked her inside my coat against my heart the way divers carry their knives, and opened the door onto heat.

* * *

The shed was burning from the middle outward.

Flames had found the curing oil — of course they had; the whole building was soaked in it, season upon season, until its very timbers were made of fuel — and they climbed the hanging nets like men up rigging, orange hands grasping rope by rope. The heat came off the walls in waves I could feel through my coat. Somewhere inside, a tier of netting gave way with a sound like tearing cloth, and the sparks went up in a whirlwind, a blizzard of red going backward, falling upward into the dark throat of the chimney flue.

And the wind. That was the thing I understood only later, standing in the yard with my hair streaming back from my face: there should have been no wind that night. The air had hung dead and cold for two days while the ice groaned on the river. But a hard, deliberate draft was pulling through the shed's broken window — someone had broken the window — feeding the fire exactly what it wanted, aiming it, steering it east toward the workshop wall.

Toward the loft. Toward the letters. Toward us.

"Fire!" My voice came out cracked, too thin for the size of the thing. "FIRE!"

Doors began to open along the workroom. Old Pellings the netmender, gray-haired and half-dressed, his face gone slack. The Kessler brothers from the boatwright's bench, one still holding his boot in his hand. And down the lane, running through the snow with her skirts hauled up and her breath smoking like a locomotive, Maren Oduya, who had been sleeping on the *Maren's Luck* anchored off the company dock and who had seen the glow from the water.

"Wendell!" she shouted. "Wendell, the wall — get clear of the wall!"

But I wasn't watching the wall. I was watching the loft ladder.

The letters were up there. The ring. The sealed envelope with its careful, ledger-straight hand. Everything Edwina Halloway's family had killed a woman to bury, everything I had promised a dead stranger I would carry out of that building, and above me now the loft hatch was glowing at its edges like a coin left too long in a stove.

"Pellings!" I grabbed the old man's arm. "Bucket line — the cistern, now, or we lose the whole roof! Kesslers — axes, cut the ice off the troughs!" They stared at me for half a heartbeat, these men who had watched me mend their ledgers and keep my mouth shut for four years, and then something in them woke, because a voice that never asks for anything commands better than one that always does.

I went up the ladder.

* * *

The loft was an oven with a floor.

Smoke lay along the ceiling in a solid bank, black and boiling, and below it the light came through the floorboards in seams of orange, each board a brand waiting for its turn. I dropped low where the air was still breathable, where the old canvas sailcloth hung within reach, and I crawled.

My hands knew the way even when my eyes burned shut. Past the mending corner. Past the crate of cork floats. To the loose board under the third beam from the north wall, where I had buried the cache weeks ago wrapped in oilcloth — the same oilcloth, some mad part of my mind noted, that had once wrapped a baby left to be forgotten.

The board came up. The bundle came out. I shoved it inside my shirt against my spine, and Pearl, crushed between my body and my coat, screamed her fury directly into my collarbone.

"It's all right," I said, to her, to myself, to the woman whose ring was digging into my ribs. "It's all right, it's all right—"

The floor spoke.

Not creaked — *spoke*, a deep structural syllable of timber letting go somewhere beneath the eastern eaves, and through the gap between two boards a tongue of flame rose up like a snake scenting the air and found the hem of my skirt.

I have been afraid many times in my life. I was afraid at my father's funeral. I was afraid every day of my first year under Pike's thumb. But fear had always been a slow thing for me, a background hum, weather. This was different. This was fear with teeth in my actual flesh, and it did something no fear had ever done: it made me fast. I beat the flame out with my bare hand and felt the skin sear across my knuckles, and I didn't stop to look at it, I turned for the ladder—

—and the ladder was gone.

Where the top rungs had been there was now a hole full of light, and the light was climbing. Smoke poured up through it in a column, and beyond it, impossibly far below, faces — Pellings shouting, the Kesslers swinging axes at the shed's east wall to make a break, and Maren's voice cutting through everything, flat and certain as a lead line:

"HOLD THE CHILD AND JUMP, GIRL!"

Jump. Into a shaft of fire.

Pearl was screaming. The letter was against my spine. The smoke had begun, very gently, to feel like a friend — that is the treachery of it, the kindness it offers you at the end — and I remember thinking, with perfect clarity, *my father dove into black water where no light reached, and he came up with pearls, and I will not die on a floor.* 

I jumped.

* * *

I did not land in fire. I landed in darkness, and cold, and arms.

Maren had positioned herself beneath the ladder well with a wool boat tarpaulin stretched between her and the second Kessler brother, and when I hit it the world compressed to impact and coughing and Pearl's cry changing pitch — still angry, mercifully, still fighting, my furious little pearl — and then hands were hauling me across the frozen yard and the night air hit my scorched lungs like river water.

I tried to stand. My legs declined. Someone said my name. Someone else said *the bucket line's holding, the brick wall's taking it.* I said, "The letter," which came out as mostly ash, and Maren bent close and I rasped it again: "The letter. It's — I have it—"

"I know." Her face swam above me, silver-haired, streaked with soot, absolutely calm in the way of deep water. "I know, child. Breathe."

I breathed.

The last thing I saw clearly before the smoke took its toll was the bucket line working in silhouette against the blaze — Pellings, the Kesslers, the cooper's boy, half the dockside street who had come running with washtubs and milk pails — ordinary people throwing the river onto Halloway's fire, hour after hour, until dawn.

Then the sky tilted, and Maren caught me, and the night went soft and black.

* * *

I woke on the floor of Maren's cabin aboard the *Maren's Luck*, wrapped in blankets that smelled of tar and tea, with my burned hand bandaged in linen and Pearl asleep on my uninjured side, rising and falling with my breath like a small tide.

Dawn stood in the porthole. Real dawn, pale and clean, and beneath the boat's gentle rocking I could hear the river doing something it had not done all winter: moving. Chunks of ice knocked against the hull with a sound like crockery. The break was beginning.

"You slept eleven hours." Maren set a cup of tea by my elbow without asking whether I wanted it. She is not a woman who asks. "The doctor looked at your hand. You'll keep it. Your lungs are scoured but sound. The child ate porridge at six like a stevedore."

"The workshop?"

Her silence answered before she did.

"Shed's gone," she said at last. "Roof fell at four. But your bucket line held the east wall wet, and the wind died toward morning — God or the river taking pity, take your choice. Workshop stands. Scorched, stinking of smoke, half her windows burst, but she stands." Maren looked out the porthole at the ice-strewn water. "Pike's already down there this morning with his ledgers, counting what it'll cost him. He's calling it an accident. Lamp oil, spontaneous, nothing anyone could have done."

"Nothing anyone could have done." The words tasted like soot.

"Aye." Maren turned and looked at me straight, and I saw something in her face I had never seen there before: anger, banked and old, older than me. "That's the story he has to tell, because Halloway pays for that story. But you and I know what broke window looks like from the outside, girl. And I know what a man looks like when he's been watching a building. There was a watcher on the towpath two nights running. Tall fellow. Gray coat. Carried the child once, down at the dock — she cried, and he handed her back quick as if she'd burned *him*."

Corliss. The polite stranger with the soft voice and the money I had refused. So it hadn't been refusal after all — only delay. He had simply changed instruments. Money first. Fire second. What came after fire, I did not let myself imagine, because Pearl's breath moved against my arm, small and warm and utterly trusting.

"Maren." I sat up, and the cabin swam, and I held on. "They won't stop. Fire failed, or it half-failed, but they won't stop. And Pike will use this — a fire on company property, an indentured girl with no business saving anything, he'll say I nearly burned the place down myself if it suits the ledger. We have days. Maybe less, with the ice going."

"Yes."

"The letter can't stay hidden anymore. Hidden is what they expect. Hidden is where they'll keep looking." My voice steadied as I said it, the way a rope steadies when you put your whole weight on it. "It has to move. Upriver, to Finch's witness, before the channel closes or opens — either one, whichever comes first. And Pearl goes where the letter goes, because she *is* the letter, she's the living half of it, and I am done pretending those are two separate things I can protect apart."

Maren studied me for a long moment. Outside, the ice boomed — a great cracking report, like a cannon beneath the water, and the whole boat shivered, and I heard the crew of the next skiff shout with something between alarm and celebration. The river was waking. The deadline was no longer written on paper.

"You understand what you're saying," Maren said quietly. "Run the ice with a baby. Against whatever man Halloway sends next. That's not hiding, Wendell. There's no hiding after that. There's only open war, and the divers fight it their own way, and there's no coming back downriver to your cot and your lamplight and your quiet ledgers after."

I looked down at Pearl, who had slept through her own attempted murder and woken hungry and unimpressed. I thought of the note pinned to her oilcloth — *forget this face* — and of the woman who had died asking for what was hers, and of my father at the bottom of a bright green river, teaching me that the water does not wait.

"I was never any good at forgetting faces anyway," I said.

For the first time since I'd known her, Maren Oduya smiled with her whole face, and it made her look forty years young and dangerous as the thaw. She pulled a chart from beneath her bunk and spread it on the table, weighting the corners with teacups, and her finger came down on the thin blue thread of river running north out of Brannock's Landing.

"Then listen close," she said, "because the ice will give us one window, maybe two nights — and there's a man upriver we need to reach before Halloway's money does."
