## 15. Witnesses in the Net Loft

The divers' camp smelled of woodsmoke and wet rope, and for the first time in a season, Wendell breathed it without looking over her shoulder.

She had come to the river settlement at dusk with Pearl bundled against her chest inside her coat, the letter sewn flat against her ribs where Maren's sister had stitched a pocket into the lining of an old diving jacket. The firelight of the camp threw long orange shapes across the ice-stained mud, and everywhere she looked there were faces that did not flinch from her. Faces that had already decided.

That was the thing she could not get used to. In Brannock's Landing, being noticed had been the whole of her danger. At Halloway's workshop she had spent six years learning to be furniture — the girl who mended nets, who kept her eyes down, whose name the foreman only spoke when he wanted something carried or corrected. Unnoticed was safe. Unnoticed was survival. And now here were forty-odd people — divers, net-menders, children with river mud to their knees — turning toward her when she came up the plank walk, nodding, making room by the fire, one old woman pressing a bowl of fish stew into her hands before Wendell had said a word to anyone.

"They know," Maren said, coming to stand beside her. The old diver's gray braids were damp from the evening mist, and her hands, scarred thick as tree bark, closed briefly over Wendell's shoulder. "I told them what you carry. Not what it says — I don't know what it says, and neither do they. Only that you carry something worth killing for, and you carry a child worth more."

"Maren—"

"Hush. Eat." She steered Wendell to a bench near the fire. "You've been living on fear and cold tea since the shed burned. Fear keeps a body moving, but it doesn't keep it alive."

Pearl woke as Wendell sat, working one small fist free of the blanket, and the camp seemed to lean in around her. A girl of perhaps eight crouched at the edge of the firelight and made a slow, solemn face at the baby until Pearl laughed — a wet, startling sound, new as anything in this hard winter. The girl's grin split wide. Within minutes there were three children at the fire's edge playing the game, and Pearl was laughing at each of them in turn, gripping Wendell's coat button in one fierce little hand as though the world might try to take it.

*Forget this face*, the note had said. Wendell watched the firelight move across the baby's cheek and thought: *no one is forgetting anything ever again.*

* * *

They gave her the net loft.

It was not the workshop — nothing would be, not yet, maybe not ever — but it was high and dry above the drying racks, walled in cedar planks gone silver with weather, and it smelled of tar and hemp and the cold clean breath of the river through every gap. Maren's people spread straw and blankets for her without being asked. Someone brought a crate for a cradle and lined it with a sailcloth remnant. Someone else hung a lantern on a nail and left a tin of matches beside it, and Wendell stood in the middle of the small warm room holding her own child-not-child and understood that she was being kept. That these people had looked at her — a bonded girl with no family and no coin, carrying another woman's orphan — and had said *yes* out loud, all of them together, before she'd even asked.

"You're crying," Maren observed from the ladder hatch. She didn't sound surprised.

"The smoke," Wendell said. "From the fire. It gets in my eyes still."

"Of course it does." Maren climbed up and lowered herself onto the bench with the groan of a woman whose joints had spent fifty years underwater. For a while they listened to the river. The ice was talking again — it had been talking for days now, deep groans rolling up through the water like some vast animal turning over in its sleep, and everyone in the camp paused when it spoke, the way people pause for thunder.

"It'll break early," Maren said. "My knees say so, and my knees have never lied about the river. When it goes, it'll go fast and mean. Anything we mean to do, we do before then."

"I know." Wendell settled Pearl into the crate. The baby's eyes were already closing, lashes dark against cheeks still faintly smudged with soot that no amount of washing had fully lifted. "Maren — the letter. I still haven't opened it past the first fold. I read enough to know what it is. A confession. Names. Dates. It names her — names the child — as heir to everything Halloway took. But there's a part I can't make sense of. There are two signatures at the bottom besides the mother's. One I know. It's the hand from the company ledgers — Edwina Halloway's own, or someone writing as her. But the other—" She pulled the oilcloth packet from her jacket, unwrapped it with hands that had learned to be careful, and held the paper toward the lantern. "The other signature means nothing to me. *Aldous Pike* I'd believe capable of anything, but this name I've never seen. *Tobias Finch.* Witnessed, dated, eleven years back."

Maren went very still.

Wendell knew that stillness. She'd seen it in the workshop loft the night she'd first shown Maren the sealed envelope — the stillness of a woman recalculating the depth beneath her. "You know him."

"I know *of* him." Maren took the letter but didn't touch the signature, hovering her thumb above the ink like a diver testing water temperature. "Tobias Finch was company clerk in Brannock's Landing before I had gray in my braid. Kept the ledgers for old Manassas Halloway himself. Then one spring he wasn't there anymore. No scandal, no notice. Just gone. The company story was that he'd taken sick and gone upriver to his sister's to die quiet. Some said drink. Some said worse." Her thumb finally came down, light as a moth, beside the name. "But if this hand witnessed what I think it witnessed, then Finch didn't run from the company. He ran from *them*. From having seen too much and signed his name under it anyway."

"He'd be old now."

"He'd be sixty if he's breathing. And clerks who hide don't hide far. They hide where the records are, because a man who spent his life among papers trusts paper more than people." Maren looked up, and the lantern light caught the pale scars on her knuckles. "There's a records house at Graywater Bend. Forty miles upriver, where the county keeps its deeds and its deaths. Every year a box comes down from there with the tax rolls, and every year the same crabbed handwriting on the manifest. My brother-in-law hauls freight past it twice a month. He says there's an old man there who knows where every body in three counties is buried, because he filed the paperwork."

Hope rose in Wendell's chest like water in a diver's sight-glass — sudden, blinding, dangerous. "If he's alive. If he'll speak."

"If he's alive, he's the difference between a dead woman's letter and a living court's proof. A confession alone is ink. A confession with its witness standing in front of a magistrate — that's a weapon." Maren folded the letter back into its oilcloth with the reverence of a woman handling something that had been underwater a long time and remembered how to drown things. "But hear me, Wendell Montrose. If Tobias Finch signed that paper eleven years ago and has stayed hidden ever since, he isn't hiding from the Halloways out of shyness. He knows exactly what they do to loose ends. We won't be bringing him good news. We'll be bringing him the thing he's spent a decade pretending he never saw."

* * *

Finch came to them.

That was the part Wendell would tell wrong for years afterward — telling it as though they had gone to him, planned it, risked the road — when in truth the road came to their door, bent and coughing, three nights later.

He arrived with the last freight skiff before full thaw-lock, a narrow man wrapped in a coat too big for him, carrying a leather satchel clutched to his chest the way Pearl clutched her cloth. Maren's brother-in-law had mentioned at supper that a passenger had ridden up from Graywater Bend asking after "the diver woman Oduya," and the camp had gone quiet in that particular way Wendell was learning to read — not afraid, exactly, but gathered, the way a net gathers when something large moves beneath it.

So when the knock came at the ladder hatch, Wendell was not alone. Maren was below her, and two of the younger divers were idling by the fire with knives they were very casually sharpening, and Wendell herself had the letter within reach and her body between it and the door before she'd consciously decided either thing.

"Madam Oduya?" The voice was dry as ledger paper. "My name is Tobias Finch. I'm told — I'm told you have a document."

He climbed the ladder badly, one hand pressed to his side, and emerged into the lantern light a head shorter than Wendell had imagined, with a clerk's stoop and a face like a folded map, all creases going different directions. His eyes found the crate, found the sleeping child in it, and stopped there for a long moment with an expression Wendell couldn't name — grief and guilt and something older than both.

"She has her mother's chin," he said quietly. "Lily's chin. I used to watch her sign the petition forms. She'd press so hard the nib went through." He looked up at Wendell, and his eyes were wet and unashamed of it. "Eleven years I've told myself there was nothing I could do. Eleven years I've been wrong, and I've known it every day I filed another deed I knew was false. When word came downriver that a child had been left at a Halloway door with a note ordering silence — well. Old men travel poorly, madam, but guilt travels worse."

"You saw the note?" Maren asked sharply.

"I wrote half the company's correspondence for twenty years; I know Edwina Halloway's instructions when I see their shape, even secondhand." He set his satchel on the bench and opened it with fingers that trembled until they touched paper, and then went steady. "And I know what I signed. May I?"

Wendell brought the letter. She watched him read it — watched this man, who had presumably read ten thousand documents, read this one as though it were written in water and might vanish. When he reached the bottom, he laid his own hand flat beside the signature, matching it, and nodded once.

"That's mine. Dated and true. I witnessed Lily Vane's confession and her claim, and I copied the supporting deeds before Edwina Halloway's men burned the originals — or believed they burned them. Lily hid a set. I never knew where. I always feared nowhere." He looked up. "Where did you find this?"

"In the workshop loft. Hidden in the wall behind the net stores."

Finch laughed — a small, cracked sound, half sob. "Behind the net stores. I helped her carry those boards myself. We told the foreman we were checking for rot." He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "She trusted me to stay away and stay alive, and I did, and I have hated myself efficiently and thoroughly ever since."

"Then help us now," Wendell said. The words came out steadier than she felt. "The courts are slow. Halloway razes the workshop lot at ice-break — days from now. They've tried to burn us out once already. Whatever window exists, it's this one, and it closes with the thaw."

Finch drew a breath that shook going in and came out level. He was, Wendell realized, a man who had rehearsed this moment for eleven years in the privacy of his own cowardice, and now that it had arrived, the rehearsal was holding.

"Then we do it properly," he said, opening the satchel. Out came folders, neat as communion. "A confession needs its witness living and locatable — I am both, barely. It needs the corroborating deeds — which I copied, and which the county archive at Graywater holds under seal, because I filed them there myself nine years ago under a false estate name, where no Halloway clerk would think to look. And it needs a magistrate willing to hear it." He looked around the loft, at Maren, at Wendell, at the crate where Pearl slept on, oblivious, her small breaths keeping time with the groaning ice. "There is such a magistrate at Graywater. Judge Harrow. He was Lily Vane's father's friend before the Halloways bought every other bench in the county. He is old, and careful, and he owes the Vane name a debt he has never been able to pay."

"And the Halloways?" Maren asked. "They'll know the moment a filing touches their name. They have men for that. We've met one."

Something hardened in Finch's folded face. It was strange, Wendell thought, to watch a rabbit grow teeth.

"Edwina Halloway will know," he agreed. "Which is why nothing travels by post, nothing travels by the regular boats, and nothing waits for the thaw. Once the river breaks, the roads drown and the company's steamers own the water. We go upriver *now*, tonight, ahead of the ice — a freight skiff, a witness, a copy of the claim, and the original stays here, hidden, in case we don't come back." He met Wendell's eyes. "You should know what you'd be risking. If we're caught, they won't bother with magistrates for any of us. You especially. A bonded indentured girl has no standing, no protection, no name anyone is obliged to respect. To them you'd be stolen property, and they'd take you back and lose the paperwork."

Wendell looked at the crate. Pearl had turned in her sleep, one hand curled open on the blanket, palm up, the way children sleep when they have never once doubted the world will hold them.

Six months ago Wendell Montrose had been furniture. She had been the girl who fixed other people's nets and kept other people's ledgers and drowned quietly, like her father, in other people's debts. She had been taught by six hard years that the price of surviving was being small.

"I've been property," she said. "I know exactly what they do with it. That's rather the point."

Finch studied her for a long moment, and then he inclined his head — a clerk's bow, precise and formal, the first time in her life anyone had bowed to Wendell Montrose for anything.

"Then we leave with the dark tide," he said. "And God forgive me, but I find I hope the ice breaks *behind* us."

Outside, the river answered — a long, splitting crack rolled up from the downstream bends, loud as a shot, and every soul in the camp came out of every doorway to look at the water. The thaw was coming faster than any of them had planned for.

Whatever window existed, it was closing.
