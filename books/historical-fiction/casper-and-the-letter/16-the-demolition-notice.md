## 16. The Demolition Notice

The notice went up on a Tuesday morning, nailed to the company post outside the counting house while frost still furred the boards and the town's breath hung in pale columns above the market carts.

Wendell saw it before she meant to. She had gone out at first light to fetch lamp oil from the company store — a small errand, the kind of errand that kept her invisible, unremarkable, one more gray figure moving between buildings with her collar up and her eyes down. Six weeks ago she would have crossed the square without glancing at the post at all. But she was not the same girl who had mended nets by lamplight and asked nothing of the world but to be overlooked. She carried another person now, in a sling beneath her coat, a warm weight against her chest that breathed in small hitches and had opinions about cold air, all of which must be endured in silence.

So she stopped. And she read.

NOTICE OF DEMOLITION AND CLEARANCE

By order of the Halloway Trading Company, the workshop lot on Tanner's Slip, together with all structures, outbuildings, net-drying sheds, and appurtenances thereon, shall be cleared and razed commencing the twenty-ninth day of March.

All persons occupying said premises after that date do so at their own peril and without license or consent of the Company.

By order of E. Halloway.

*E. Halloway.* The initials sat there in bold type like two fingers pointed at her. Edwina. Corliss had given them the name eleven days ago, in the burned smell of the drying shed, and it had not stopped sounding since — a bell rung once and never quite done humming.

She counted on her fingers before she could stop herself. The twenty-ninth of March was six days off. And every soul in Brannock's Landing, from the fishmongers to the churchwarden to old men who'd never worked a day off the water, had been saying the same thing for a week now: *the break will come early this year.* The river had been talking in its sleep since midwinter — groaning, shifting, cracking its knuckles under the ice — and last night it had answered Finch's hope directly, splitting loud enough to wake the whole divers' camp. The thaw was coming fast. The demolition and the flood were going to arrive together, hand in hand, like twin bailiffs.

They did not mean to leave her time. That was the thing Wendell understood as she stood in the cold with the oil tin going numb against her palm and Pearl's small fist curled inside her coat. The notice wasn't a warning. It was an alibi. Paper posted publicly, dates declared, everything done in proper order so that afterward — when the workshop lay in splinters and whatever papers remained hidden in its bones had been carried off or crushed — no one could say the Company had acted in haste or secrecy. They were building themselves a lawful face even as they prepared a crime.

A cart rattled past behind her. She made herself walk on, unhurried, a girl with an errand, though her heart was beating in her throat.

*Six days.*

* * *

At the workshop, the others had already heard.

Maren stood in the doorway of the net loft with her arms folded, looking out across the yard at the wrecking company's stakes — they'd come in the night, someone had driven fresh survey stakes into the frozen mud, red-painted, each one a tooth marking where the teeth would bite. Tobias Finch sat at the trestle table with the letter open before him, though he wasn't reading it; he was staring at it the way a man stares at a map when the roads he planned have washed out.

"Twenty-ninth," Maren said, without turning. "I heard it from the fish boy. Six days."

"The ice won't wait six days," Finch said quietly. "It may not wait three. I've seen a break come down the valley like a stampede — took the lower bridge out whole and didn't slow down to do it."

"Then we go sooner."

"We're not ready." Finch spread his hands over the table, over the careful lists he'd been drafting — the witness upriver, the route, the tides, the names of what they'd need. "Mr. Halloran keeps his own hours. He's an old man with an old grudge and no telephone line to hurry him. We can't sign anything until we stand in front of him, and we can't stand in front of him until we make the crossing, and we can't make the crossing until—"

"Until the skiff is caulked and the moon is dark and the patrol boats are drunk," Maren finished. "I know what we're waiting on, clerk. I'm telling you the river doesn't care about our schedule."

Behind them, in the corner of the loft where the wool blankets made a nest, Pearl woke and fussed — a thin sound, quickly hushed when Wendell gathered her up. The baby had grown in the weeks since the door, since the oilcloth, since the note that said *forget this face*. She was heavier now, longer, with a fine dark fuzz of hair and a way of fixing her gaze on a person with grave, unhurried attention, as though taking their measure for some future account. Right now she fixed it on Wendell and subsided, one hand splayed against Wendell's collarbone.

Six days. Wendell looked around the loft — at the nets hanging in their patient rows, the tar barrels, the narrow stair, the cache in the wall where the letters had waited thirty years in the dark for someone to find them. This place had sheltered a crime and then, in its last season, had sheltered the proof. She had learned to walk quietly here, to be small here, to endure here. It seemed suddenly unbearable that it should end as rubble because Edwina Halloway wanted the walls silenced along with everyone who knew what they contained.

"They'll come at night," Maren was saying to Finch. "Demolition crews always start at night when they don't want witnesses — I've seen it done to diver camps on the coast. They'll bring lanterns and a magistrate's paper and forty men who don't ask questions."

"And we'll be gone," Wendell said.

Both of them looked at her.

"We'll be gone," she said again, and found her voice held steadier than she expected. "Before the twenty-ninth. Before the break if we can manage it, and ahead of the wreckers regardless. But—" and here was the thought that had been forming since the square, cold and practical, "we should know exactly when they're coming. Not the notice date. The real night. Because if we leave early and they're watching the river — and they are watching, Corliss says the patrols doubled after the fire — then we want our departure to look like nothing. A skiff going out to check lines. Not a flight. For that we need to know how much darkness we actually have."

Finch rubbed his eyes. "And how do you propose we learn that? Ask Mr. Pike?"

The name dropped into the room and sat there.

Pike. Foreman Aldous Pike, who had stood over her crib-side discovery that first freezing morning and demanded the child be gone by dawn, children being liabilities on the ledger. Pike, whose arithmetic had no column for mercy. Pike, who had nevertheless — Wendell thought of it now the way you probe a bruise — who had nevertheless been different since the fire. She had seen him in the days after, walking the yard with his hands behind his back, stopping at the scorched skeleton of the drying shed and standing there far longer than any foreman's business required. She had seen him look at her across the yard once, quickly, and look away. There had been something in his face she had not been able to name and had not trusted herself to try.

"Pike hates loose ends," she said slowly. "He hates waste. But he isn't the one who ordered the fire."

"No," Maren said. "Edwina Halloway ordered the fire. And Pike carries her orders like a dog carries a stick." Her voice was flat. She had no reason to love the man; none of them did.

"He stood at the shed for half an hour yesterday," Wendell said. "Just standing."

"He stood there because forty feet of company property is ash and his ledgers will carry the loss," Maren said. "Don't mistake bookkeeping for a soul, girl."

But Wendell kept turning it over, the way you turn a stone in your hand because one side of it is warmer than it should be.

* * *

The chance came that evening, and it came the way chances had been coming to her all winter: through work, through the ordinary machinery of the company, through being the sort of person nobody bothered to notice leaving a room.

Pike sent for her after supper. A boy delivered the message to the loft — "Foreman wants the Montrose girl, ledger room, nine o'clock" — and Maren's head came up sharply, and Finch's pen stopped, and Wendell felt the old fear move through her, familiar now, almost comfortable, the fear that had lived in her since childhood and which she had lately learned to use rather than obey.

She went.

The ledger room smelled of coal oil and ink and the particular dustiness of paper that has been handled for decades. One lamp burned on Pike's desk. He sat behind it with his sleeves rolled, and before him, instead of the usual columns, lay a single sheet — the demolition schedule, she realized, the working copy, with times and crew assignments penciled in a foreman's cramped hand. He covered it with his forearm as she came in. An instinct. Then, strangely, he didn't seem to know what to do with the gesture, and left his arm there anyway, like a man guarding a wound.

"Sit down, Montrose."

She sat. Pearl was with Maren, three hundred yards away and one locked door between them and the world, which was as safe as anything got these days. Wendell folded her hands and waited, because waiting was the oldest skill she had.

Pike looked at her for a long moment. Lamplight caught the deep lines around his mouth, the gray in his sideburns. He was not an old man, she realized; he was a man worn old by the company the same way stairs are worn old, by traffic, by years of the same feet.

"You've been with us six years," he said. "Never late to a shift. Never short in a column. Never once given cause." He said it like an accusation.

"I try to be accurate, sir."

"Hm." He looked down at the desk. His forearm still lay across the schedule. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, roughened, as though the words had to be pried loose from somewhere. "You know who burned my shed."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls the pipes ticked, carrying heat from a stove somewhere below, and beyond the shuttered window the river muttered to itself in the dark.

"I have an idea," Wendell said carefully.

"Aye. Well." He moved his hand at last — not to uncover the schedule but to rub at his eyes, hard, the gesture of a man fighting a headache or a conscience. "Thirty years I've worked for this company. I started on the docks when I was your age. I've kept their books straight through two owners and I never asked what the books were *for*, because asking questions is a good way to drown in this town." He lowered his hands. "But I didn't become a foreman to burn down buildings with people sleeping in them. You understand? There's a difference between razing a structure and murdering the tenants, and somebody in this company has lost track of that difference, and it isn't me."

"No, sir," Wendell said, barely breathing the words.

"I told them the fire watch would handle the shed. I told them the thaw would scatter what was left. I put that in writing because putting it in writing is the only way I know how to say I won't do it twice." His jaw worked. "I'm telling you this for no reason except that you're the only person in this building who does her job honestly, and I find I can't stomach lying to the honest ones anymore. It's a weakness. I expect it'll cost me."

The lamp hissed. Wendell's heart was beating so hard she could feel it in her wrists, and she made herself stay exactly where she was, exactly as calm, because the smallest movement might frighten this confession back into its hole.

"The notice says the twenty-ninth," Pike said.

"Yes, sir."

"The notice is for the town. Notices are public things. Public things get watched — reporters, magistrates' men, anyone with a grudge and a camera." His voice had gone very low now, and his eyes, when they met hers, were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "The actual crew comes the night of the twenty-sixth. Barge lands at the old tannery slip below your yard — they'll come up from the water side, not the road, so nobody on the street sees the equipment. Wrecking starts at two in the morning. They'll have the roof off before dawn." He paused. "Two nights from now."

Two nights. Not six. The floor seemed to tilt gently under her feet and right itself. She kept her face still. Six years of practice, and for the first time in her life she deployed all of it in service of a lie: that she was merely receiving information, that this changed nothing, that Aldous Pike was talking to a girl who would take her orders and mend her nets.

"Why tell me?" she asked quietly. "If they find out you warned—"

"Who'd believe it?" He almost smiled; it didn't take. "The Montrose girl. The quiet one. And besides—" He looked away, at the dark shuttered window, toward the river he'd served for thirty years. "There was a child crying in that shed the night it burned. I heard her after. In my sleep. A man gets to a certain age and finds he can't carry everything he's carried." He straightened, and the foreman came back into his spine, into his voice, the door of the confession swinging shut. "You'll keep the yard clear by the twenty-sixth, Montrose. That's a lawful order and you'll follow it. What you do with the rest of the information is between you and God."

"Yes, sir," she said. "Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me." He picked up his pen, bent over the ledger, and became abruptly, completely unavailable — a man closing a shop. She let herself out.

* * *

The cold outside hit her like clean water. She stood a moment in the yard, letting it steady her, and looked up at the sky where the clouds were breaking apart to show a scatter of indifferent stars, and at the black shape of the workshop against them, and at the river beyond, where the ice lay white under starlight and shifted, even now, with long internal sighs, like a sleeper about to wake.

Two nights.

She walked back through the dark, and it struck her — halfway across the yard, with her boots crunching the frozen ruts — that she had just watched a betrayal happen inside the enemy's house. Pike had spent his whole life carrying the company's orders like a good dog carries a stick, Maren had said, and tonight he had laid the stick down in the mud and turned his back on it. Not from love of her. Not from justice, perhaps, or not only. From a child's cry in a burning shed and thirty years of swallowed questions. People were stranger than ledgers. There was no column for what Aldous Pike had just done, and no way to total it yet either — whether it would save them or damn him.

In the loft, Maren read her face before Wendell got a word out. Finch set down his pen.

"Well?" Maren said.

"The twenty-sixth," Wendell said. "Two in the morning. Two nights from now. They land the barge at the tannery slip and come up from the water."

Silence. Finch took his pocket watch out, looked at it, put it away, as if time could be checked that simply. Maren began, without a word, rolling the charts she'd had drawn of the crossing.

"The moon's dark on the twenty-seventh," Finch said at last, half to himself, calculating aloud. "Patrols change at midnight, if Corliss's information holds. If we launch tomorrow evening on the ebb—"

"Tomorrow evening," Maren said. "We've no choice left to argue about. Good."

But Wendell stood in the doorway, looking out again at the river, and the joy she expected to feel — the relief, the *now we move* — wouldn't quite come. Instead she heard Pike's voice: *I find I can't stomach lying to the honest ones anymore. It's a weakness. I expect it'll cost me.*

He had handed them their lives, and the wreckers would come in two nights to a yard where the barge master would report — what? That the tenants had scattered? That would be reported upward, to the woman whose initials headed the notice, and the woman would understand immediately that someone had talked. And the first name anyone would think to ask was the foreman who walked the fire's ashes every afternoon like a mourner.

"Finch," Wendell said slowly, "when we send word to Corliss about the launch — we send it coded, and we send it tonight. Because if we're gone before the wreckers land, the first question Edwina Halloway asks won't be *where did they go.*"

Maren paused, chart in hand, and looked at her.

"It'll be *who told them when.*" Wendell pulled her coat closer, though the cold was not the thing chilling her. "And there's one man in this company whose answer she'll guess before anyone speaks it."

Outside, far downriver, the ice gave a long groan and cracked — a sound like a door opening somewhere in the dark, too far away to see, coming closer all night long.
