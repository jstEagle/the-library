## 17. Corliss Turns His Coat

The order came on a Tuesday, folded inside a bank draft that would have bought Corliss a farm.

He sat in the back room of the Ferryman's Rest with the paper open on his knee and the lamp turned low, because he had learned in twenty years of hired work that men read dangerous things better by half-light. The handwriting was the same as always — small, precise, unhurried, the hand of someone who had never once needed to hurry. *The lot must be clear before the wreckers come. The woman, if she interferes. The child, without question. The paper burned unopened or the whole arrangement is void and your fee forfeit.*

*Without question.*

Corliss had carried out orders for eleven years. He had frightened witnesses off trains, bought silence from clerks, broken a printer's fingers in a town so small it had only the one press. He had told himself, in the way hired men tell themselves things, that he had never done the worst of it — that there was always a line, and he had always known where his lay. But he had never been handed an infant's name on a bank draft before. He had never been asked to write *without question* into his own hands and call it work.

He looked at the draft again. Three hundred dollars. Enough to disappear west. Enough to stop being the man who did this kind of errand.

Then he thought of the net loft, and the night he had carried the child down from it wrapped in his coat while smoke still hung in the rafters, and how she had stopped crying the moment she felt a heartbeat under her ear that wasn't her own fear. She had gripped his lapel with one fist, small as a sparrow's foot, and held on like he was something worth holding.

Nobody had held onto him like that in thirty-one years.

Corliss folded the order along its creases, put the draft in his pocket, and went to find Maren Oduya, because he knew that Wendell Montrose would not meet him anywhere he chose, and he did not blame her.

* * *

Maren found him waiting at the edge of the divers' camp at dusk, standing well out from the firelight like a man who understood he was weather — something to be waited out, not welcomed. She came toward him slowly, an old woman with river-gray braids and a diver's rolling gait, and she stopped just beyond arm's reach, which told him everything about how this meeting would go.

"You sent word you had something to trade," she said. "We don't trade with your kind."

"I know." He kept his voice level. He had practiced the words all afternoon and they still came out wrong, too plain, stripped of the smoothness he usually wore like a coat. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to look at what I brought."

"Show me."

He took the folded order from inside his jacket and held it out flat on his palm, the way a man offers meat to a dog he doesn't know. Maren didn't take it. She read it where it lay, her lips moving once over *without question*, and when she looked up her eyes were the color of deep water over stone.

"And the draft?"

"In my pocket. I haven't cashed it." He hesitated, then drew it out and held it beside the order. "I want you to understand what I'm giving up. Not because I want pity. Because I want you to know I can't be bought back. There's no second price coming."

"There's always a second price," Maren said. "Men like you are built in two pieces, Corliss. The piece that takes the coin and the piece that hates itself after. You've just decided which piece gets to live."

He had no answer to that. It was truer than anything he'd planned to say.

"Maren." Wendell's voice came from behind her, from the shadow of the mending tent, and Corliss's chest tightened the way it did before hard crossings. She stepped into the firelight with the baby on her hip — Pearl, they were calling her now, though the name wasn't yet spoken in front of strangers — and she looked at him the way she must have looked at the ice on the river: measuring how much weight it would bear. "You said you had proof. Show me all of it. Then you'll leave, and we'll decide what we believe without you standing there."

"That's fair," he said.

"It isn't," she said. "But it's what you get."

* * *

They let him as far as the door of the mending tent, no farther. He stood at the threshold with his hat in his hands — he could not remember the last time he had taken his hat off indoors for anyone — while Wendell sat across the room with the baby against her shoulder and the lamp between them, and Maren leaned against the tent pole with her arms crossed, positioned exactly where she could reach him in two steps.

Wendell Montrose was sixteen years old and she had the flattest, steadiest eyes he had ever seen on a living person. He had watched her for weeks through the winter — that was his work, watching — and he had catalogued her the way he catalogued everyone: girl, indentured, careful, forgettable. He had been wrong. Forgettable people did not lie to him the way she had lied to him in the workshop office, smooth and sweet, sending him away empty-handed while the letter he'd been hunting sat ten feet above his head in a net loft. Forgettable people did not carry babies out of burning buildings and come back for ledgers.

"I'll start at the beginning," Corliss said. "And I'll say all of it, because if I leave anything out and you find it later, everything else becomes worthless."

"Start," said Maren.

"The woman who left the baby at your door. Her name was Lillian Halloway. She was Edwina Halloway's niece — the old man's brother's daughter, raised in the house on the hill like family until she wasn't. She got with child by a man the family disapproved of, and worse than that, she got it into her head to claim what her father had left her, which was a share in the company that old Halloway had already quietly moved into his own name years before. She wrote letters. She threatened to go to a lawyer in Carverton. Then she died — fever, they said, though I've heard enough fevers described to know when a story's been dressed for burial. The baby was born alive, and whoever attended the birth couldn't stomach the next part. That person is the one who left her at your door. I don't know their name. I was hired six weeks later."

"By whom?" Wendell asked, though her arms had already tightened around the child, and Corliss saw that she knew — had maybe known since the fire, since the orders kept coming with the precision of a family's private knowledge.

"Not the old man. He's failing; half his mind went last autumn. The orders come from—" He stopped. This was the sentence he had rehearsed forty ways and every one of them sounded like treason, because it was. "The orders come from Edwina Halloway herself. Her hand signs them. I've seen it on company letterhead and on plain paper both. She runs the company now in everything but the signature blocks, and she intends to run it after the demolition too, with the lot cleared and any paper that proves Lillian Halloway ever existed burned or drowned or buried under new pilings."

The lamp flame bent in a draft. Nobody spoke. On Wendell's shoulder, the baby made a small sound and settled again, one hand curled against Wendell's collarbone, and Corliss found he could not look away from that small hand, and hated himself a little for the softness of it.

"You said proof," Maren said finally. "Not stories. Proof."

He set his satchel on the floor and opened it with deliberate slowness, letting them see everything he drew out. "Four orders in Edwina Halloway's hand. The first, hiring me in December — 'watch the workshop, report any sign of the child.' The second, after the fire — 'the lot must be clear.' The third, naming the woman and the child together, which is the one that matters, because it puts Edwina Halloway's own pen within a week of Lillian Halloway's death and asks me to account for 'any surviving issue.'" He laid them one by one on the table between them, squaring the edges like a clerk, because his hands wanted something orderly to do. "And the fourth." He tapped the newest one. "Dated three days ago. You've seen it."

Wendell reached forward and turned the papers toward the lamp, reading each one twice, and Corliss watched her face do what faces did when a person's whole future rearranged itself: nothing at all, then everything. When she looked up, her voice was quiet.

"Why?"

It was the question he deserved and the one he least wanted. He gave her the truest answer he had.

"She cried when I carried her out of the smoke. Gripped my coat and held on. And I have spent thirty-one years being paid to be the thing people hold on to last, right before I hand them over to something worse." He swallowed. "I'm tired of being last. That's all the why there is. If you're waiting for a nobler reason, there isn't one."

Maren uncrossed her arms. It was a small motion, but Corliss had spent a lifetime reading small motions, and he knew it for what it was: a door opening the width of a knife blade.

"If we take these papers," Maren said, "and Finch sends word upriver to his witness, and the witness comes down to testify — those orders hang Edwina Halloway. They also hang you. Conspiracy, arson, whatever the law decides to call the rest. You understand that."

"I understand that."

"You'd be testifying against yourself to save a child who will never remember your name."

"Yes," Corliss said. And then, because it seemed suddenly important: "She'll have other names around her. That's more than I had."

Wendell rose. She shifted the baby to her other hip and walked to the table, and for a long moment she stood over the four sheets of paper with her jaw set, and Corliss realized she was not deciding whether to believe him. She had believed him almost at once — he could see it in the way she'd squared the pages, the way she handled evidence, ledger-trained, precise. What she was deciding was whether to accept what accepting cost: that the man who had hunted her all winter was now the only shield between Edwina Halloway and everything Wendell loved.

"We leave the night after tomorrow," she said at last. "Before the ice breaks, before the wreckers come. Upstream, to Finch's witness. If you come with us, you row, you keep watch, and you do what I say when I say it. And if at any point I think you've turned—"

"You won't have to think long," Corliss said. "I'll make it obvious."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one might someday be built. She gathered the orders into a stack, folded them into the oilcloth the baby had been abandoned in, all those months ago, and tied it with a length of net twine. Evidence wrapped in evidence. History folding over on itself.

"Edwina Halloway," she said, testing the name aloud, and her voice did not shake. "She signed her own name to all of it."

"She never imagined anyone would live long enough to show it around," Corliss said. "That's the trouble with powerful families. They stop believing paper can hurt them."

Outside, far downriver, the ice groaned — a long, deep sound like a giant turning in its sleep, the sound the whole town had been listening to for a week, the sound that meant the river was done waiting. Maren cocked her head toward it, then looked at Corliss with those deep-water eyes.

"Get some sleep," she said. "You row first watch."

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat outside the mending tent with his back against a stack of crab pots and watched the moon come up over the water, and near midnight Wendell came out with a blanket around her shoulders and the baby drowsing against her, and she stood beside him without sitting, looking at the river.

"The note," she said. "The one left with her. *Forget this face.*"

"Yes."

"Do you know who wrote it? Truly?"

"I have a guess." He turned the draft over in his pocket, the one he hadn't cashed, feeling its edges. "Same hand as the orders. Edwina's. She wanted the child gone and untraceable, but she couldn't quite bring herself to have it done plainly — not then, not with the birth attendant still alive and talkative. So she wrote an instruction instead of an order. Left herself a door marked mercy." He shook his head slowly. "People like that always leave themselves a door. They think it makes them decent."

Wendell was quiet a long time. The baby stirred, and she rocked her without thinking, an easy motion she'd learned somewhere in the winter without noticing.

"Well," she said finally. "She forgot the face. We kept it." She looked down at the sleeping child, then at Corliss, and her eyes in the moonlight were not warm — warmth would take longer than that, maybe years — but they were no longer measuring him for ice. They had decided he would bear weight. "Two nights, Corliss. Then the river decides everything."

Downstream, the ice groaned again, louder, closer, a sound like the world's oldest lock beginning to give way.
