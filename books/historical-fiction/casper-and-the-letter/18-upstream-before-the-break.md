## 18. Upstream Before the Break

The last night at the workshop came down like a held breath.

Wendell had thought she would spend it packing, but there was almost nothing to pack. A wool blanket. The letter, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the lining of her coat where it lay against her ribs like a second heart. A tin of milk-powder Maren had brought wrapped in sacking. Pearl's spare clout, a spare stocking, the carved bone whistle that had come in the cache with the letters — Tobias Finch had sworn it belonged to the child's mother, and Wendell could not leave it behind, though she could not have said why, except that some things deserved to be carried out of a burning house even when the house was not yet burning.

Everything else fit inside a bread tin.

She sat on the net loft's edge with the tin between her boots and listened to the river. Everyone in Brannock's Landing knew the sound of ice going rotten — the deep complaint of it, the long groan that started somewhere below the shallows and climbed through the pilings into the floorboards and up through the soles of your feet until your teeth felt it too. But this year the ice had learned new language. It cracked now in short, sharp reports like distant rifle fire, and it sighed, actually sighed, long exhales of pressure releasing through the black water, and once, just past midnight, it *screamed* — a high metal shriek that sent every dog in the town barking and left Wendell with her arms locked around Pearl and her heart hammering against the sewn letter.

Pearl slept through it. Babies, Wendell had learned, trusted the arms that held them more than they feared the world. It seemed to her a kind of holy foolishness, and she envied it, and she resolved, silently, kneeling there in the dark with her cheek on the downy head, that she would spend whatever years God gave her making sure this particular foolishness never got punished for itself.

Below her, in the workshop's gutted main room, Corliss moved among the shadows checking the shutters one last time. He did it quietly, the way he did everything, with a economy of motion that still unsettled her — a man built for entering rooms without being heard in them, now using the same craft to guard one. He had not asked again whether he was coming. After he'd given them the name — *Edwina Halloway*, said flat as a nail driven into a board — he had simply begun preparing, as if his inclusion were already decided, and perhaps it was; Maren had looked at him once, long, the way she looked at river water before trusting it to carry her, and said only: "You row, then. I've got hands older than your grudge."

* * *

They launched at the hour Wendell's father used to call the blue nothing — after moonset, before any honest gray had touched the east, when the river ran invisible beneath its rotting lid of ice and the whole world was the color of a bruise healing.

Maren chose the moment the way she chose dive times, by something in her body older than instruments. She stood at the foot of the company dock — the old pearling dock downstream of town, half-collapsed, its pilings leaning like tired men — and put her bare hand flat on the nearest piling and held it there, eyes closed, reading the water's tremor through the wood.

"Now," she said. "It'll hold till it won't. Get in."

The skiff was a pearling boat proper, shallow-drafted, tarred black, built by divers to sit light over the beds and slip through gaps no cargo man would attempt. Wendell had grown up in boats like it, before the drowning, before the debt, before the ledger with her name in it, and when she stepped down into the hull and felt the water's give beneath her feet, something in her chest unlocked that she had not known was locked. Muscle memory rose in her like a fish to feed: here is where your weight goes, here is how you kneel so you do not rock the gunwale, here is how you hold a child against your body so her warmth becomes part of yours and neither of you shivers.

She tucked Pearl into the space between her ribs and the forward thwart, swaddled double, under the canvas flap they'd rigged. The baby sighed and worked her small fist free and gripped Wendell's finger, and held on the whole night, and never let go.

Corliss took the oars without being told. Maren took the stern. Wendell knelt amidships with her daughter — because that was what the word meant now, whatever paper existed or didn't — and cast off the line, and Brannock's Landing began, foot by silent foot, to become a place she used to live.

* * *

The river was a road made of breaking glass.

Ice floes came down around them all night — plates and rafts of it, some thin as windowpane and some thick as doors, grinding past in the dark current with sounds like millstones, like bones, like slow conversation in a language nobody living spoke anymore. Corliss worked the oars constantly, feathering, backing water, nudging the bow away from one floe and swinging it down the seam between two others, and he did it with a fluency that surprised her until she remembered: a hired man learns water somewhere, everyone does, on the river nobody is born knowing ledgers.

"Port," Maren would say, quiet as prayer. "Port. Hold. Starboard, easy." And the skiff would thread the needle.

Once a floe the size of the workshop roof came shouldering down out of the dark directly ahead, taller than their gunwales, and Wendell had time to think with perfect calm, *so this is how it ends, we will be crushed by winter itself,* and then Maren said, "Hard to port, boy, *now*," and Corliss threw his whole body into the oars and the skiff leapt sideways like a trout and the great slab went by close enough to touch, shedding a cold spray across the canvas, and behind them the wake of its passage rolled under their keel and lifted them and set them down again gently, almost kindly, as if the river were sorry.

Nobody spoke for a while after that.

Then Pearl stirred and whimpered, and Wendell fed her under the canvas with her own body curved around the small warmth like a wall against the wind, and the men kept their eyes forward, and the river went on talking to itself below the ice, low and dark and patient.

"You knew this water," Maren said at last, softly, to Wendell. It wasn't a question.

"My father's beds were past the Narrows."

"Mm." Maren watched the dark ahead. "He'd have liked this. Bad launch, bad ice, running freight worth more than pearls." She paused. "He'd have liked the girl most."

Wendell bent her head over Pearl and did not answer, because if she opened her mouth she was afraid the thing that came out would be a sound she'd been swallowing since she was eleven years old, and there would be time for that later, maybe, when the letter was signed and the ice was gone and the stove in a rebuilt workshop warmed a different winter. Later. Everything later. That was the bargain she was making with the dark: let us through tonight, and I will grieve properly tomorrow.

* * *

They smelled the town before they saw it — woodsmoke and wet rope and the sourness of the tannery — and then Brannock's Landing assembled itself out of the darkness in pieces: the lamplit windows along Front Street, the warehouse roofs furred with frost, the Halloway clock tower black against the lesser black of the sky. The ice below the docks had pulled away from the pilings in a wide black channel, water open and running fast, and the whole waterfront groaned like a ship in weather.

"Down oars," Maren breathed. "Drift."

They drifted. Past the end of Front Street, past the dark hulk of the Halloway warehouse with its painted name flaking, past the workshop — *their* workshop, small and broken-backed and standing, its windows like closed eyes — and Wendell looked at it sliding by and understood that she was looking at the outside of her whole life, every night of it, every lamp-lit ledger and mended net, and that she might be seeing it for the last time, and that if the wreckers took it in six days she would rather remember it like this: whole, and hers, and behind her.

That was when they heard the other boat.

Not an oar-sound — an engine, or the ghost of one, a chugging cough carrying flat across the water from upstream of the docks, from the company moorage. Then a lantern swung a wild arc at the water's edge, and a voice shouted something swallowed by distance, and another voice answered, sharper, and a second lantern joined the first.

"They've seen us," Corliss said. No fear in it. Inventory.

"No," Maren said. "They've seen *a* boat. There's a difference, and we're about to make it." She pointed her chin upriver, toward the black seam where the Narrows pinched the channel between stone. "Company men launch heavy. They'll take the main channel because they don't know better and because their boat draws too much to try anything else. We go where the ice is thickest and the water is thinnest and only a diver's hull has any business being."

"And if we meet a floe in the Narrows?" Wendell asked.

"Then we meet it," Maren said, "together, politely."

The engine behind them found its rhythm — *chug, chug, chug* — and a searchlight bloomed at the moorage, sweeping the water in long white strokes. The beam passed over them once while they lay flat in the shadow of a grounded floe, Wendell folded over Pearl, Corliss flat across the thwarts, Maren's hand on the painter holding them snug against the ice like a mother pressing a child's shoulder into a doorway. The light crawled up the floe beside them, turned its face white and blind, slid across the canvas an inch above Wendell's head —

— and went on.

"Chug-chug," Maren whispered, mockingly, when the searchlight had swung away. "A steam launch in broken ice. They'll be aground on the middle bar before midnight, calling each other fools."

But her hands, when she shipped the painter, were not entirely steady, and Wendell saw it, and loved her the more for it, and said nothing.

* * *

The Narrows took the rest of the night.

It was water the way a fist is a hand: clenched. Black stone walls rose on either side, close enough some places to trail a palm along, and the channel threaded between them in S-curves the flood had carved and the ice had polished, and the current ran so hard through the throat of it that the skiff needed no oars at all — only steering, only Corliss leaning on the sweeps and Maren calling the seams, and Wendell wedged over the baby doing the work of ballast and prayer both. Ice came down the Narrows with them, funneling, faster than the open river, shrieking against the walls, and twice they shot gaps between floes that Wendell genuinely did not see until they were already through them, and afterward her knees would not stop shaking, and she was nearly certain it was cold.

Dawn found them through. Behind, far downstream, faint as memory, the engine still chugged and stuttered — hung up, Maren judged, on the bar, or afraid of the Narrows, or waiting for light to see by. Ahead the valley opened gray and silver, mist standing on the water like the river's own breath, and the banks went soft with early willow, and somewhere beyond the first bend Tobias Finch's witness waited with a pen and a conscience.

Pearl woke into the mist and looked at it, and looked at Wendell, and produced — loudly, imperiously, in the face of the entire fleeing dawn — a single delighted squeal.

"Hush," Wendell begged, laughing despite herself, folding the canvas back over her. "Hush, my heart. Not yet. When we're safe you can sing the whole river down."

"She's got a diver's lungs," Maren said, with unmistakable pride. "Good. She'll need them."

Corliss rowed. The mist thickened, and hid them, and the current carried them upriver at the speed of escape and the pace of mercy, and Wendell put her hand inside her coat and felt the letter's small stitched rectangle against her ribs and made it a promise without words: *you will be read.*

Behind them, muffled by miles and mist, the ice below Brannock's Landing gave way at last with a sound like the world clearing its throat — and the flood came on.
