## 19. The Ice Breaks

The river spoke first.

We had been running the dark channel for an hour, Maren at the sweep, Corliss bailing with a tin pail, Pearl strapped against my chest beneath my coat where her small heartbeat knocked against mine like a second clock. The ice that had held all winter lay in broken plates along the banks, grinding and sighing, and above that sound — above the creak of the skiff's timbers and the hiss of water along the hull — came a groan so deep I felt it in my teeth.

"There she goes," Maren said quietly, as if greeting an old friend. "Upstream first. Listen."

I listened. The groan became a crack, a crack became a report like a rifle fired somewhere in the dark hills, and then another, and another, a rolling volley traveling downriver toward us faster than any boat could row. The whole world seemed to be unbuttoning itself.

"Is it coming for us?" I asked. My voice sounded very small.

"The break always comes for everything," Maren said. "Question is whether you're riding it or under it." She glanced back over her shoulder, toward the black water behind us, and I followed her look.

Lanterns. Two of them, maybe three, bobbing low on the current a half mile back — Halloway's men, launched from the company dock within minutes of our slipping past the net stakes. They had a steam launch, someone had said; Corliss had named it, the *Maggie B*, with six men and a hired pilot who knew the channel. Against our pearling skiff, old and shallow and slow, they should have caught us before midnight.

But the river had other arithmetic.

* * *

I want to set down what that night was truly like, because people afterward only ever asked about the letter, or the trial, or Edwina Halloway's face when the verdict was read. Nobody asks about the cold. Nobody asks about the way fear tastes after four hours of it — like copper coins, like river mud, like the inside of your own cheek where you've been biting it without knowing.

The cold came up through the floorboards and into my bones, and Pearl felt it too, because she stirred against my chest and made a small sound, not quite a cry, and I pressed my lips to the top of her head and breathed the warm smell of her until she settled. That was the whole of my religion that night: keep her warm, keep her quiet, keep moving.

"Finch's man will have a fire lit at Grayling Bend," Corliss said between bails of water. He'd told us twice already, as much to steady himself as us. "Tobias wired him from the telegraph office before we left. The witness — Alderman Voss — he'll take the deposition at dawn. He said—" Corliss stopped, worked the pail again, started over. "He said if the paper is what Finch believes it is, he'll sign it into record before the circuit judge arrives, so no one can buy it or burn it in the meantime."

"And if Halloway's men reach us first?" I asked.

Corliss didn't answer, which was its own answer. He had turned his coat two nights before, had laid Edwina Halloway's own instructions on Maren's table in his careful clerk's hand — *let the thaw hide the ashes* — and still, when he looked back at those lanterns, I saw something move across his face that was not fear exactly. It was older than fear. It was the look of a man watching consequences come up the river after him, patient as ice.

"They won't reach us," Maren said. She said it the way she said everything, flat and certain, a woman who had spent forty years reading water. But then she did something I had never seen her do. She took one hand off the sweep and touched the little iron charm at her throat — her father's diving charm, the one she wore for luck under the ice — and pressed it once, hard, before returning both hands to the oar.

The river rose around us as we ran. I could feel it rising, the way you feel a fever climbing in a child: not all at once but steadily, the current quickening under the hull, carrying chunks of ice downstream past us like white leaves, some small as plates, some broad as doors. The channel narrowed where the bluffs leaned in, and the water there moved fast and smooth as poured glass, and Maren steered us straight down the throat of it without a word, trusting the dark, trusting thirty years of memory.

Then the sky behind us turned wrong.

* * *

It began as a sound I had no name for — a rushing, a roaring, like wind through a canyon, except there was no wind. Then Maren said, very calmly, "Hold onto that child and hold onto yourself," and swept the skiff hard toward the northern bank, out of the main channel, into the slack water behind a gravel bar.

"What is it? What's happening?"

"Ice jam's let go up at the narrows." Her voice carried no panic at all, and somehow that frightened me more than anything else that night. "Whole winter's worth coming down at once. Flood crest. We ride the edge of it or we're kindling."

The roar grew. The lanterns behind us — I kept checking them, couldn't stop checking them — seemed to falter, to swing wildly, and then one by one they went out. Not extinguished. Swallowed. The *Maggie B*, with her deep draft and her heavy boiler and her six men who had never loved anything enough to learn this river, was in the main channel when the crest came down.

I heard it before I understood it: a grinding crash, wood on ice, ice on wood, and then human voices — thin, high, torn apart by the water almost as soon as they were raised. A lantern light leapt up, impossibly bright, reflected off a wall of moving ice taller than a man, and then the wall rolled over it and the light was gone and the voices were gone and there was only the flood, vast and indifferent, carrying its cargo of shattered winter down toward Brannock's Landing.

"Maren." My voice cracked. "The men—"

"Nothing we can do." She didn't soften it. She never softened anything. "Pray if you pray. Some of them will wash out on the bars below town. The river takes what it takes." And then, quieter, almost to herself, in a language I didn't know — her mother's tongue, I think — she said something short and grave, a word for the drowned, and touched her charm again.

Pearl woke at the noise and cried — one long, wailing cry that tore out over the water — and I pulled her fully against me, inside my coat, and rocked her, and whispered nonsense into her hair, and after a long time she slept again, her fist curled around my collarbone as though holding on were something she'd been born knowing how to do.

The skiff rode the edge of the flood the way a leaf rides a gutter. Maren read the water like print, easing us out of the main torrent into the eddies, using the whirlpools themselves to carry us along, and hour by hour the gray began to come into the east, and hour by hour the banks changed — the bluffs falling away, the pines giving over to bare willow flats, the country opening toward Grayling Bend where, according to Tobias Finch's wire, a man named Alderman Voss sat waiting with a lamp in his window and a pen already dipped.

Dawn found us gray and shaking and alive.

* * *

Grayling Bend was nothing — a shelf of gravel, a leaning boathouse, a two-room house with smoke standing straight up from its chimney into the windless morning. A man stood on the bank as we came in, tall and stooped at once, wrapped in a wool coat too big for him, and beside him a boy of perhaps twelve holding a lantern that the daylight had made useless but that nobody had thought to put out.

"You'll be the ones Finch wired," the man called, and his voice was the voice of a courtroom, dry and precise even shouted over water. "Voss. Get that child by the fire before you do anything else. Papers can wait ten minutes. Children can't."

I remember loving him a little, right then, for saying that first.

Inside, the house smelled of coffee and lamp oil and old paper. Voss cleared his table with one sweep of his arm — ledgers and letters sliding to the floor, unimportant now, every paper in the world unimportant except the one I carried — and I unwrapped the oilcloth packet from inside my shirt where it had ridden against my skin all night, warming itself with my warmth, and laid it on the bare boards.

My hands were shaking so badly I could barely work the knot. Corliss stepped forward to help and I flinched away before I could stop myself — old habit, the habit of a girl who had learned that every hand reaching toward her meant a debt — and then I caught myself, and looked at him, and nodded, and together we opened the packet while Maren stood at the door with her arms folded, watching the river, watching the road, watching everything.

The letter came out into the lamplight. The confession. The witness signatures, dated and sealed, naming Elias Halloway's theft of Lavinia Marsh's inheritance — the pearl beds, the waterfront lots, the whole stolen fortune — and naming, in a line that still made my breath catch every time I read it, *the infant Lavinia bore in the winter of her distress, sole surviving heir to all herein described.*

Voss read it twice. He read it a third time with a magnifying glass, tilting it toward the window as the light strengthened. Then he sat down heavily, took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and looked at me over the top of them.

"Miss Montrose," he said. "Do you understand what you've been carrying?"

"A weapon made of ink," I said. Maren had said it first, weeks ago, and the words had lived in me since.

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth — not quite a smile. "Alderman Finch writes that you are indentured to the Halloway Company. Is that true?"

"It was."

"It is or it isn't, miss. The law is particular."

"It is," I said. "For another four years."

He nodded slowly, and wrote something on a slip of paper, and signed the deposition in a hand as dry and precise as his voice, and had Corliss sign as witness to the finding, and Maren, and then me — Wendell Montrose, my own name written in my own hand on a legal record for the first time in my life, and my hand shook so badly the W came out crooked, and I have never in my life been prouder of a crooked letter.

When it was done, Voss blotted the page, set it in an iron document box, and locked it, and put the key on a string around his own neck.

"Now," he said, "we talk about what happens next. Because I promise you, Miss Montrose, that woman will not surrender to a piece of paper. She'll fight it in every court she can buy, and she can buy several. The law is slow. The law is slow on purpose — it was built slow, by men who wanted it slow." He looked at each of us in turn, and last and longest at Pearl, who was sitting on the floor by the stove chewing the wooden handle of the boy's lantern with perfect contentment. "So the question before this room is: what do we do while the law is being slow?"

Maren answered before anyone else could. She came away from the door, and her voice was quiet and absolutely level.

"We do what divers do," she said. "We go down after what's ours, and we bring it up, and we don't ask the water's permission."

* * *

We heard about Brannock's Landing three days later, at the diver camp upriver, and the news came to us the way news comes to river people — in fragments, by boat, by shouting across the water, growing stranger and larger with every telling.

The flood crest had hit the town at midmorning, two hours after we slipped past it in the dark. It had taken the lower wharf entire. It had taken the fish market, the net sheds, the saloon, the church steps. It had taken the Halloway warehouse, whose foundations, the tellers swore, had been rotted through by years of the company's own diverted drainage — and when the warehouse went, it had torn open the company's counting room, and the river had carried the ledgers out through the broken doors and scattered them across the flooded streets like autumn leaves, and by afternoon the townspeople were wading waist-deep through their own history, pulling up pages that recorded, in Elias Halloway's neat accounting hand, every debt, every dodge, every theft the confession had named.

You cannot burn what the river has already taken. You cannot buy back what half the town has already read.

They said Edwina Halloway stood on the high ground above the ruins with her skirts gathered in her fists, watching the water carry her father's empire past the church, past the mill, past the last of the pilings, out into the open current where no one would ever collect it again. They said she did not cry. They said she did not speak. They said that when someone shouted something up at her — I can guess what — she only turned and walked back toward the house on the hill, and that her back was very straight, the way a person walks when they know the ground is going out from under them and have decided to pretend otherwise.

But the part of the story that reached our camp last, and stayed with me longest, was the smallest part.

The wreckers' crew — the men hired to raze our workshop before the thaw — never came. Their boat had been the *Maggie B*'s tender, and the tender had swamped at the narrows with the rest, and the men had crawled out on the gravel bars downstream and walked home through the floodwater, and somewhere on that long wet walk they had apparently decided, all of them, together, without discussion, that they were done being Halloway's hands. One of them delivered this message to the workshop door himself, where Tobias Finch stood guard with a shotgun and a company seal he had no authority to use and used anyway: *Tell the diver woman and the girl the lot is theirs by old river-right. Salvage law. Ask any waterman. Ask the water.*

Maren laughed when she heard it — a real laugh, deep in her chest, the first I'd heard from her since the night the child arrived. "Salvage law," she repeated, savoring it. "Forty years I've dived by that law. Never thought I'd see it walk up on land and start handing out deeds."

That evening I sat on the dock at the camp with Pearl asleep in my lap, watching the last light go gold and then copper and then gray on the flood-swollen river, and I took out the crooked-W deposition copy that Voss had made for me, and read it once more by the last of the light. Somewhere downriver, in a locked box, on a signed record no court could quietly lose, a child's claim to her name and her inheritance existed now as a fact of ink. Somewhere upriver, a workshop full of nets and lamplight and the ghost of my father's hands waited to be claimed by the people who had actually kept it standing.

And somewhere south, Edwina Halloway was writing letters of her own. I could feel it the way Maren felt weather in her knees. The river had ruled for us — the river, and a crooked W, and a child who slept through her own vindication — but rivers recede, and courts convene, and women with straight backs and empty ledgers do not forgive.

Maren came and sat beside me, and put one weathered hand flat on Pearl's small back, feeling her breathe.

"Rest tonight," she said. "Tomorrow we go home and take what's ours."

Below the dock, the ice went by in pieces, melting as it traveled, becoming at last only water.
