## 21. Burning the Contract

The paper came on a Tuesday, carried up from the company office by a boy too young to understand what he was delivering.

Wendell took the envelope from him at the workshop door—the same door where she had found a bundle of oilcloth and a note ordering her to forget—and turned it over in her hands. Heavy stock. The Halloway crest pressed into the wax, the scales of the trading house rendered in gold that caught the morning light like something alive. It smelled of cedar and money.

She did not open it right away. She stood on the threshold a long moment, listening to the river.

The break had come and gone two weeks past, and the water was running high and brown and loud, carrying the last of the winter's wreckage down toward the sea—fences torn loose, whole trees rolled end over end, somebody's rowboat spinning like a leaf. The flood had done its damage and moved on, but it had left the town changed. Left *them* changed. There were men in Brannock's Landing now who touched their hats to Maren Oduya when they passed her on the plank road. There were men who would not meet Wendell's eye at all.

Inside the workshop, the stove ticked as it cooled from the morning fire. That same stove. It had kept a child warm through the worst winter of their lives, and it sat there now in the corner of the mending room, squat and black and ordinary, its pipe running up through the ceiling into the loft where Pearl had slept for six months in a nest of netting.

Tobias Finch was at the long table with his spectacles down his nose, copying depositions in his careful clerk's hand. He glanced up when she came in. "Bad news travels heavy," he said. "That envelope's heavy enough to sink a skiff."

"Good news is heavier," Wendell said. "It has to carry you somewhere."

She broke the seal with her thumbnail.

*To the former ward of this company,* it began, which was one way of saying what she was, and inside, in language wound tight as net-cord, an offer. The scandal had made things awkward, it allowed. The courts being what they were, and public feeling running high, the Halloway Trading Company wished to make amends to any parties inconvenienced by recent irregularities. Miss Wendell Montrose's indenture, it said, could be restored in full—with honors, the word underlined twice by some anxious pen—with wages retroactive, with a position of trust in the counting house, with her name entered in the company ledger in letters of credit instead of debt.

All she had to do was sign. All she had to do was come back.

She read it twice. Finch read it three times, holding it close to the lamp though it was broad daylight.

"They're frightened," he said at last. "A frightened company is a generous company, for about a month." He set the letter down and polished his spectacles on his sleeve. "What will you tell them?"

Wendell looked at the stove.

"I'll tell them nothing," she said. "I'll show them."

* * *

She waited until evening, when the light came low and amber through the west windows and the mending room was empty except for the ones who belonged there.

Maren came up from the dock with the smell of river water in her clothes and Pearl riding her hip like a sack of flour, one small fist knotted in the old woman's hair. Corliss followed, carrying split birch for the stove—he still did that, brought wood as if paying a debt by the armful—and behind them came three of the divers, Oduya kin and near-kin, wet to the knees, drawn up the bank by whatever quiet signal had passed along the dock that afternoon. They filled the doorway and then the room, and nobody spoke above the sound of the fire being built.

Wendell got her father's contract out of the tin box under her bunk.

She had kept it through everything—the fire, the flood, the nights when Corliss's footsteps had circled the workshop like a slow animal. She had kept it because keeping things was what she knew how to do. Her whole life had been an exercise in holding still and holding on: the debt after her father drowned, the ledgers, the nets, the small hours by lamplight when she had told herself that endurance was a kind of virtue. Endurance was just what you did when no one handed you anything better. She understood that now. She had crossed a frozen river to learn it.

The paper was soft with handling, four years of folding and unfolding, the ink gone brown at the creases. *This Indenture Made this ninth day of October...* Her father's mark beside the company seal, a wobbly X pressed so hard the pen had torn through. He had signed it dying, she'd learned later. Signed it in the counting house with river water still in his lungs, or near enough—brought up from the pearl beds too late, propped in a chair while Aldous Pike read him terms he could barely see. A diver's debts passed to his daughter. A daughter was worth more in a ledger than in a boat. That was the company's arithmetic, and it had never once counted a person as anything but a number that could drown.

"You don't have to say anything," Maren said softly, settling onto the bench with Pearl. "Some fires want silence."

"No," Wendell said. "This one wants words. I've been saving them four years."

She crouched at the stove. The birch had caught properly now, flames standing up orange and steady, heat pressing against her face. Through the little iron window of the firebox she could see the coals beginning to bloom, white-edged, hungry.

"My father's name was Elias Montrose," she said, to the fire, to the room, to the girl on Maren's hip who would not remember any of this and would be told anyway, every year, until it was hers. "He dove the beds below Miller's Bend for nineteen years and he never once came up empty-handed. He could hold his breath longer than any man on the river, and he taught me that the trick isn't the lungs. The trick is being willing to stay down in the dark a moment past fear."

Her voice held. She had expected it to shake. It didn't, and that surprised her, and then it didn't surprise her, because these words had been sitting in her chest so long they had grown roots, and roots hold.

"The company told me he owed them. Fourteen pounds, eleven shillings. I know now that he didn't—that the books were written crooked the way they wrote everyone's books crooked—but I was nine years old and the paper said what it said, and I went to work in this room and I stayed in this room and I made myself small enough to fit inside their arithmetic." She unfolded the contract. The heat made the old ink shine. "I have been small for four years. I mended nets and I kept my eyes down and I thought if I was quiet enough, careful enough, useful enough, someday they would let me be a person again."

Corliss had stopped moving entirely, a stick of birch still in his hands. One of the divers had taken off her cap. Even Pearl was still, watching the firelight move on the walls with her round dark eyes.

"Then someone put a baby on this doorstep," Wendell said, "and ordered me to forget her face. And I looked at her, and I understood that I had been given a choice my father never got. I could keep being small. Or I could be worth more than the ledger says."

She opened the firebox door. The heat rolled out against her knuckles, and for a moment she just held the contract there at the threshold, letting it feel what she had felt all those winters—the warmth that had kept a child alive, offered freely, asking nothing.

"This is fourteen pounds, eleven shillings," she said. "This is my father's death sold twice. This is every night I slept on a rope bed with my shoes on in case they called me before dawn." And then, quieter, the thing underneath all the rest, the thing she had never once said aloud in four years: "I miss him. I miss him every day, and I never got to say goodbye, and I have been angry at him for leaving me to this, and I'm done being angry. Papa, look. I'm coming out of the dark."

She fed the paper to the fire.

It resisted at first, curling back on itself the way paper does, the way living things do, and then the flame found the dry center of it and it went up all at once—a bloom of orange, the seal wax hissing, Elias Montrose's crooked X burning brightest because the pen had pressed hardest there. Ash lifted up the flue in a slow grey ribbon. Wendell watched until the last corner whitened and folded into nothing, watched the firebox swallow the whole architecture of her smallness, and felt—not triumph, nothing so simple—felt the strange lightness of a body setting down weight it had forgotten it was carrying.

Maren began to laugh, low in her chest, the way she did, and the divers laughed with her, and Corliss put the birch down and cleared his throat like a man who had something in his eye.

Pearl reached both arms toward the firelight and said, clear as a bell, her second word ever: "*More.*"

"No, little fish," Wendell said, wiping her face with the back of her wrist. "That's enough of that. We're full."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone down to the dock and the stove had settled to a red murmur, Wendell sat at the long table with a fresh sheet of paper and the good pen, the one Finch had fished out of the wreckage of the counting house along with two inkwells and a brass rule.

Finch had drawn up the papers himself, of course. He could not stop being a clerk any more than the river could stop being a river, and she loved him for it. The salvage claim sat before her now, drafted in the old forms of river-right, older than the company, older than the town: the workshop lot and dock claimed by the diving community under rights of recovery, held in common against all comers until such time as the law—which had finally, grudgingly, begun to crawl toward the truth about Edwina Halloway and the inheritance she had smothered in a crib-sized fraud—caught up with what the river already knew.

At the bottom, a line waited for her name.

*Keeper of the Workshop,* Finch had titled her. Not ward. Not debtor. Keeper.

She dipped the pen. Outside, the high brown water talked against the pilings, and somewhere downstream the rebuilt crane groaned as the night shift loaded the first pearling boats of the season. Spring had come on fast after the flood—willow green along the banks, mud smelling of everything beginning—and the dock below the workshop, half-rebuilt by forty pairs of diving hands in three weeks, already looked like it had always been theirs.

Wendell signed. Then, beneath her name, in the space Finch had left for remarks, she added a line of her own in her small neat ledger-hand, the hand they had trained her in and could never take back:

*Entered into record this day: one contract, burned. One debt, discharged by fire and witness. One family, found.*

She blotted it and set the pen down and sat a while in the warmth of the stove, listening to the river run.

There was a knock at the door—two knocks, hesitant—and when she opened it, Corliss stood on the step with his hat in his hands, looking older and younger at once, the way men did when they were trying to be honest and finding the fit unfamiliar.

"Got a letter today myself," he said. "From upriver. From the lawyers for—" He stopped, jaw working. "For *her*. Edwina Halloway. They're calling witnesses. Formal inquiry at Miller's Bend, week from Thursday, before the circuit judge." He looked up, and there was something raw in his face, something that had been cracking open since the night he stood outside a burning shed with a coal-oil can and couldn't make his feet move. "They're going to call me, Wendell. And I'm going to stand up in front of God and the circuit court and tell them whose orders I carried. But there's things I know that I haven't told even you. Things about that night the mother came to the workshop. Things she gave your father to hide at the bottom of the river—because Maren's not the only one who knows what Elias Montrose dove down with besides pearls."

The river rose and fell against the pilings. In the loft above, faintly, Pearl stirred in her sleep.

"Sit down," Wendell said, pulling out the chair beside the warm stove. "Start at the beginning."
