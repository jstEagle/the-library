## 1. The Weight of a Lamp

The clockwork wanted forty-one turns. Vesper Zhou knew this the way he knew his own pulse, because for six years he had counted them every night at half past eleven, and before that his predecessor had counted them for thirty years more, and somewhere in the tower's stone throat there was a groove worn into the brass plate where a hundred thousand winds of the crank had polished the metal to a mirror shine. He set his palm against it now, felt the cold, and began.

One. Two. Three.

The mechanism took up its complaint in the walls around him, gears the size of cartwheels turning beneath the floor, chains sliding through their channels with a sound like slow rain. Above him the great lens floated on its bath of mercury, four tons of glass and brass balanced so perfectly that a child's finger could set it revolving. It turned above him now as it always turned, sweeping its beam out over the black water, and Vesper wound, and counted, and did not think about anything at all.

That was the trick of it. He had learned the trick from the lamp itself.

* * *

His wife had taught him about portable things, though she never used the word. In the last winter of her life — the winter they had spent on the mainland, in a rented room that smelled of camphor and other people's cooking — Maren had been stripped down by degrees to almost nothing. The doctors took her strength first, then her appetite, then her hair, then the use of her left hand, and Vesper had watched her measure what remained and decide, with the same clear-eyed practicality she brought to everything, what was worth carrying.

"The ring," she said one night, working it over her knuckle, which had gone thin enough that it slid off whole. "You keep the ring. Not because I'll want it where I'm going, but because you'll want something of me that fits in your hand."

She had made him inventory the rest like a quartermaster. Her mother's Bible: too heavy, leave it. The photograph of their wedding day: light enough, but paper curled in damp and the island was nothing but damp, so look at it now, she said, look long, and then put it away in your memory where the weather can't reach it. The blue enamel brooch: yes. The letters from their courtship: no, burn those, they were never meant for anyone's eyes but theirs and hers were going first.

"You'll want a rule," she told him, propped against her pillows with her hands folded on the quilt like a woman at a church meeting. "When a life gets small, you can't afford anything without a weight to it. Everything you own should be either useful or beloved, and beloved things should be few, and everything — everything — should be something you could carry out of a burning house in one trip."

He had laughed, then. She had not.

"I mean it, Vesper. I've watched you these months, hauling in sentiment like fish, every little thing too heavy to lift. Learn to travel light. Promise me."

He promised. It was the only promise in those final weeks she had asked of him that he kept without fail, and he sometimes thought — winding the clockwork, trimming the wick, polishing the lens with a cloth that had once been one of her aprons — that she had known exactly what she was doing. That she had been preparing him for a grief she could see coming and he could not, arming him with a discipline that would hold him together when she was gone, the way a keel holds a hull. Grief was water. It came into every available space. A man who traveled heavy would sink in it.

So: the lighthouse. Forty-one turns. Three lamps in rotation, two lit. Eleven minutes for the full winding if his shoulder behaved, fourteen if it didn't. Oil measured nightly to the dram and logged in a ledger whose columns were straighter than any sermon. His possessions, catalogued annually on the back page of that same ledger in his small square hand:

One coat, wool, grey. One spare coat, older. Boots, two pair. Tools of the trade, complete. The blue brooch. The ring, worn on a cord beneath his shirt. Books, nine, all beloved. Teapot, Maren's. And Nell, who was not portable at all, and who was the only thing in the world he could not carry and could not lose.

* * *

The turn of the crank reached forty. He paused, as he always paused, letting the mainspring take its tension, listening to the tower settle around the stored energy like a horse accepting the bridle. Then the forty-first, and done. The lens would rotate true until tomorrow night at this hour, sweeping its white arm across the water every seven and a half seconds — he had timed it with Maren's pocket watch, which was also on the inventory, listed under *beloved, fragile*.

He climbed the iron stairs to the lamp room. The fog had come in off the sea an hour after sundown, the way it did in late autumn, and now the island sat inside it like a stone inside a closed fist. The beam went out and was eaten, went out and was eaten, and Vesper stood at the glass and watched the light fight for every yard of air it won.

Fog was the honest season. In summer the sea could lie to you — show you glitter where there was nothing, hide rocks behind pretty weather — but fog told the truth: the world ends at your fingertips, and everything beyond is faith. He had come to prefer it, in the way of men who make peace with difficult things rather than beautiful ones. On clear nights the water reminded him of Maren's eyes and he had to stand somewhere else. In fog there was only work.

Below, in the keeper's cottage bolted to the tower's base like a barnacle to a hull, his daughter was asleep. He knew this because he had checked — not, he would have told you, because he doubted her, but because checking was part of the order of things, the way the forty-one turns were part of the night. Nell, ten years old next spring, asleep with the dog-eared book of sea stories open on her chest more often than not, one arm flung out for a dog they did not have. He had stopped standing over her a year ago; now he stood in her doorway and listened to her breathe, which was a compromise between love and dignity that he suspected every father alive had negotiated.

Her breathing was even. He moved on.

In the kitchen he poured himself tea from the pot — Maren's pot, blue willow pattern, chipped at the lip in a place his own mouth always found — and sat at the table with the logbook and did what he did every night of his life now, which was to write down the day as if someone would read it.

*Oct. 14th. Wind SW freshening. Fog since 8 bells. Lamp wound 11:30. Oil consumed, per measure. Ferry due tomorrow, last crossing before bridge closes for the season. Provisions adequate. All well.*

He looked at *all well*. It was the standard phrase, used by keepers everywhere, meaning nothing and everything: no ship wrecked, no lamp failed, no family member dead. He wrote it most nights. Some nights the pen hesitated over it, and tonight was such a night, though he could not have said why. He let it stand. The truth was that all *was* well, in the countable sense. The oil was sufficient. The roof had held through the equinox gales. Nell had eaten her supper without negotiation. The fishing families down in the harbor village had hauled their boats above the tideline for winter, and the smoke rising from their chimneys at dusk had been ordinary smoke, in ordinary quantities.

And yet the pen had hovered.

He finished the tea, washed the cup, dried it, set it on its hook. He banked the kitchen fire, shuttered the parlor, turned the key in the storeroom where the oil drums stood ranked like soldiers. Every lock, every latch, every lid accounted for. This was the architecture of his days and he built it faithfully, course upon course, because he had learned in the rented mainland room that structure was the only thing that stood when everything else fell.

At the door of Nell's room he paused again, longer than usual. The girl had kicked her blanket to the foot of the bed in her sleep, and he went in and drew it up over her shoulders, careful as a man handling rigging in a blow. Her hair was Maren's color exactly — that dark red the islanders called *storm-copper*, rare here, common nowhere — and in sleep her face went slack and young and utterly undefended, and Vesper stood over her in the dark and felt the full unportable weight of her, which he carried anyway, every day, in both hands.

"Goodnight," he whispered, because saying it aloud was a superstition he allowed himself, one of the few items in his inventory that served no purpose and weighed nothing and could not be burned.

* * *

He woke before dawn, as he always did, to the tail end of the foghorn's schedule — two blasts every ninety seconds, automated now by a clockwork striker as old as the tower, faithful as a heartbeat. Outside the window the world was grey wool. He dressed in the dark by touch, each garment in its place, and went up to relieve the night's watch over machinery that needed no watching, and this contradiction — the unnecessary vigilance, the ritual attendance — was, he understood, simply what prayer looked like when it was rebuilt by a practical man.

Morning brought no clearing. The fog lay on the island like a second island, deeper than the first, and the sound of the harbor was muffled to a rumor: a block and creak, a gull's argument, the far-off hammering of Osei's boys repairing a skiff. Vesper cooked porridge, woke Nell, supervised the washing of her face with the resigned severity of a man who had lost this battle daily for years.

"Papa." She said it around her bread, mouth half full, eyes on the window. "Is the ferry still coming today?"

"It comes today."

"And then not again till spring?"

"Not again till spring. The bridge closes at noon tide, and after that we're on our own until the storms pass and the causeway road opens."

Nell considered this, chewing. Ten years old and born on the island; she had never known a winter any other way. The closing of the bridge was to her what the shortening of days was — a fact of the season, like fog, like gales, like the boats coming out of the water. "Then we should have gotten more sugar," she said.

"We have sugar."

"We have *some* sugar." She gave him a look that was so precisely her mother's look — one eyebrow, level, unhurried — that Vesper had to turn to the stove and tend a fire that needed no tending. "Widow Osei says Magistrate Corren bought up half the winter stores on the mainland side already. She says prices at the company store will be wicked by Christmas."

"Widow Osei says many things."

"She's usually right, though." Nell swallowed, licked crumbs from her thumb, and Vesper noted — filed, in the ledger of his attention — that this was true. Osei was usually right. She had been right about the roof beams, right about the timing of the herring, right about Maren's illness before anyone else on the island would say the word aloud. A man ignored her at the cost of learning later how much cheaper it would have been to listen.

"After breakfast," Nell said, "can I go to the orchard tree? There was a dog there yesterday, a yellow one, all ribs and ears. Tam's brother said it came off a boat but no boat's claimed it. I'm going to name it."

"Name it what?"

"I don't know yet." She said this with total confidence, as though names arrived on their own schedule and were not to be rushed. "Something will fit."

The orchard tree. It stood alone on the headland slope below the tower — a stray apple tree, nobody's planting, bearing fruit so sour the gulls mostly left it. Some long-dead settler's seed, perhaps, blown in with a bird, or dropped from some mainland picnic fifty years gone. It had no business surviving this far north, exposed to every wind the sea could throw, and yet it survived, crooked and stubborn, blooming extravagantly every spring as if defying the climate to do something about it. Maren had loved it. *The island's only optimist,* she called it, and had gathered its hard little apples every autumn and made a jelly that puckered your whole face and that she served, grinning, to everyone who visited, insisting it was delicious.

Vesper had not made the jelly in three years. The apples fell and rotted where they landed, feeding the crows, and he walked past the tree every day with his head steady and his eyes forward, because it was not on the inventory, because it was far too heavy to carry, because some things a man must simply refuse to pick up.

"Mind the cliffs," he said, which was what he said.

"Mind the cliffs," Nell repeated, with the fondness of a child quoting ritual, and kissed his cheek, and went out into the fog with her hood up, and he watched her from the doorway until the grey closed over her, counting the seconds until he heard her boots on the path again — which he did, fainter and fainter, going down toward the harbor road.

* * *

The rest of the morning was oil and brass. He filtered and filled the reservoirs, tested the spare burner, cleaned the fog-salt from the lantern's vent panes — salt crept in everywhere, a fine crust on every surface, the sea's patient signature — and entered each task in the log with dates and measures. At half past eleven he glanced at Maren's watch out of pure ceremony. The clockwork had eleven hours and fifty-nine minutes left in its spring. Everything in its time.

It was near noon, when the fog thinned to rags along the clifftops, that he saw the ferry come in — or thought he did. From the gallery rail, with the glasses raised, he found the channel markers and then, beyond them, the low dark shape of the ferry *Kestrel* wallowing up the strait toward the bridge, her funnel smoke hanging flat in the dead air. Ordinary sight. He had watched her make this crossing a hundred times.

But she was early, and she was flying no flag, and as he watched she slowed oddly in mid-channel, wallowed, held station for a long minute while a small boat put off from her side — and then resumed, creeping toward the island like something reluctant.

Vesper lowered the glasses. His shoulder ached where it always ached. Down in the harbor, barely audible across the water, a bell had begun to ring — not the church bell, not the school bell, but the short urgent pattern of the harbor bell, the one that meant *come down, everyone, come down now.*

He stood at the rail with the wind moving his collar and the fog dripping from the gallery above him, and he felt the pen hesitate again, the way it had hesitated over *all well.* Something was arriving on this last ferry besides flour and kerosene and the year's mail. You could not have proven it by the glasses or the logbook. But a man who lived by measures knew when a number was wrong.

Forty-one turns. He counted everything. And something in the tally of the morning had come out uneven.

He shut the lamp room door behind him and went down to answer the bell.
