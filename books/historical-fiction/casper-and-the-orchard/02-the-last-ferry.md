## 2. The Last Ferry

The fog came in off the water the way grief came into a house — slowly at first, then all at once, until you couldn't remember what the rooms had looked like without it. Vesper Zhou stood at the rail of the ferry landing with his hands in the pockets of his oilskin coat and counted the things he could carry.

It was a habit he had never managed to break, this measuring of his life in portable things. It had begun in the winter of Mira's illness, when the doctors in the mainland town had given her a year and then quietly revised it downward, and he had learned to look at every object in their home and ask it a single question: *if we must go, can you come with us?* The clockwork mechanism of the lamp — no. The good brass telescope — yes. Her wedding ring — always. The photograph of them on the pier at Halloway, young and squinting into sun that seemed, even then, like something borrowed — yes, wrapped in wool, in the bottom of the sea chest. He had made lists in those months, small careful inventories written in pencil on the backs of oil receipts, and when she died he had found one of the lists tucked into her prayer book, in her own hand, and understood that she had been doing the same arithmetic all along.

Now the habit persisted like an old scar that ached before rain. He stood on the landing and thought: *one lantern, one ledger of my own accounts, Nell's coat, the tin of tea.* That was the whole of what mattered. Everything else on the island — the lighthouse itself, ninety feet of whitewashed stone he had tended for eleven years — could burn or crumble or be taken by the magistrate's men, and he would grieve it, but he would not be destroyed by its loss. This was not resilience. He knew that. It was just another way of bracing for the blow.

The ferry *Kestrel* sat low in the grey water, taking on its last cargo of the season. Around him the landing was crowded in the way it only ever was twice a year: at the opening of the bridge season, when the mainland sent up its inspectors and its seed merchants and its hopeful young teachers who lasted a winter and left; and now, at the closing, when everything went back the other way and the island contracted to its bones. Crates of salted herring. A piano, absurdly, roped under canvas, belonging to the schoolteacher who had decided the mainland needed her more. Barrels of oil for the lighthouse — his oil, his responsibility, stacked and stenciled and counted twice.

"Zhou." The ferryman, Tam Broadbelt, came down the gangplank wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before anyone could remember. "You'll want to sign for that oil before I load it proper. Paperwork's gotten particular this year."

"Has it." Vesper took the proffered pencil and signed where Tam's cracked finger indicated. "Particular how?"

Tam glanced toward the customs shed at the end of the pier, where two of Magistrate Corren's men stood under the eaves with a ledger open between them, writing. They wrote a great deal these days. They wrote at the checkpoint on the causeway road; they wrote at the fish market; they had appeared, last month, at Widow Osei's door asking after her late husband's debts as though Osei himself might answer from the grave.

"Particular like a man counting another man's spoons," Tam said, low. He moved closer, so that his shoulder blocked the view of the customs shed, and his voice dropped into the register he used for weather warnings. "You heard what Corren's been doing over in Millhaven?"

"I don't hear much of anything. I hear the foghorn."

"Course you do." Tam almost smiled. "That's why I'm telling you. Corren's agents have been buying debts. Fishing debts, store debts, funeral debts — anything owed by anybody on this coast. Buying them cheap, mind. A man owes forty crowns, they pay the creditor twelve and call it square, and then the paper belongs to Corren. And a debt in Corren's hand isn't a debt anymore. It's a hook."

Vesper signed the second line and did not look up. "People can pay what they owe."

"Aye. If there were fish to catch and a market to sell them at that wasn't already Corren's cousin's. You know what the herring run did this year. Half the fleet is mortgaged to its masts already." Tam took back the rag and folded it, unfolded it, folded it again — a man with something still caught behind his teeth. "There's talk the bridge closing early this year weren't weather."

Vesper looked at him then. The bridge — the great timber span across the narrows, the island's single thread to the mainland — was scheduled to close on the first of November, when the storms made the crossing suicidal. It was the twenty-third of October. The water this morning was flat as poured pewter.

"The surveyor said the pilings were sound," Vesper said.

"The surveyor said what he was paid to say, maybe." Tam shrugged, and the shrug was too elaborate to be casual. "I only know I've been running this crossing nineteen years and I've never been ordered off it while the birds are still coming south. And I know that when the bridge shuts, whatever papers a man hasn't got in order — deeds, receipts, proofs — he can't get them corrected till spring. Five months. A lot can happen to a piece of paper in five months."

The foghorn sounded from the lighthouse behind them, one long note rolling out over the water, and Vesper felt it in his chest the way you feel a knock at a door you'd rather not answer. He kept his face still. Eleven years of keeping a light had taught him that most of his job was looking calm while the sea tried to frighten him.

"You're telling me to get my papers in order," he said.

"I'm telling you the wind's changed." Tam stepped back, raised his voice to its ordinary gruffness. "Oil goes aboard now. You'll want to check the seals yourself, Zhou, no offense meant."

"No offense taken." But as the dockhands rolled the barrels up the plank, Vesper walked the length of the line and checked each seal, and he was aware of the two men under the eaves watching him do it, and aware that he was being entered into some ledger of his own — *lighthouse keeper, Zhou, V., oil purchased, disposition noted* — and that he did not know what column his name was being written into.

* * *

Nell was not at the landing when he finished, which was unlike her, so he climbed the path toward the lighthouse with the particular tightness in his throat that fathers learn to mistake for patience. She was where he should have known she'd be: sitting cross-legged in the grass beneath the orchard tree, with a dog the size of a folded sailcloth lying against her knees.

The tree stood alone on the rise between the lighthouse and the dunes — an apple tree, half-wild, nobody's planting that anyone remembered, dropping sour little fruit each autumn that the gulls fought over and the children dared each other to eat. In blossom it was beautiful enough to make passing fishermen slow their boats. Now it was going gold and bare, leaves coming loose in the wind like letters nobody claimed.

The dog was new.

"Nell," Vesper said.

"Papa." She didn't get up. She had her mother's way of not getting up when she knew she was in the right, which was most of the time. "He followed me from the landing. He doesn't belong to anybody. I asked everybody."

"He looks like he belongs to somebody."

"He looks like he belongs to nobody, that's what I said." She scratched the animal behind one ear with the authority of long practice, though she had clearly known him less than an hour. He was a brown-and-grey thing, ribs showing under a coat stiff with salt, one ear torn as if by a net-knife or worse. He regarded Vesper with the weary diplomacy of a creature who has been thrown off many boats. "I gave him half my bread."

"There's a reason we don't feed strays. They stay."

"I know." She said it simply, without guile, and Vesper felt the sentence land somewhere below his ribs, because he had fed a stray once too — a frightened girl of seven with her mother's dark eyes — and she had stayed eleven years, and he would have carried her anywhere in the world if the world had required carrying. "His name is Casper."

"It is not."

"It is now. He told me."

"Dogs don't tell names."

"This one did." She lifted her chin. "Anyway, you named me after a grandmother I never met, and I didn't get a vote either."

He wanted to argue and found he had nothing to argue with. The dog — Casper, apparently — thumped his tail once against the grass, sealing the arrangement, and Vesper crouched despite himself and let the animal smell his hand. There was intelligence in the face, and damage, and the specific patience of things that have survived by being useful.

"He'll want feeding all winter," Vesper said. "And he'll sleep somewhere warm, and that will be the kitchen, and within a month you'll be telling me he was always part of the family."

"Yes," Nell said, pleased, as though he had agreed to something instead of lost.

They walked up to the lighthouse together, dog trailing, and Vesper went through the evening rounds with the mechanical comfort of ritual: the clockwork wound, the mercury bath checked, the wicks trimmed, the lens turned by hand until the weights caught and the great light began its patient rotation above the darkening water. Below, in the kitchen, Nell set out a saucer of fish stew and pretended she hadn't, and the dog ate it in the doorway, unwilling yet to come fully inside, and Vesper watched him over the top of his account book and thought about debts bought cheap and bridges closed early and ledgers full of other people's names.

After Nell was asleep he climbed the tower and stood in the lamp room with the wind worrying the glass, and he looked down at the island laid out below him — the crooked lanes, the smoking chimneys, the dark shapes of boats hauled up beyond the tide line — and he thought of Tam's voice saying *a lot can happen to a piece of paper in five months*, and of the two men under the eaves writing, writing, writing.

Somewhere out in the fog a ship's bell rang twice and went silent, and the light swept over the water, and swept again, faithful and blind, seeing nothing of what was coming and lighting the way regardless.

In the morning, he decided, he would row to the mainland store himself for the winter oil order, and see with his own eyes what the checkpoint on the causeway road had become.

Below him, in the kitchen, the stray dog lifted his head at the sound of the clockwork turning, listened a long moment, and slept.
