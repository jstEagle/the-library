## 3. A Clerk in the Dunes

The customs house at Duncarrow kept its records in a room with no windows, and Casper had come to think of that as deliberate. A room without windows could not be looked into. It could not offer anyone outside a view of what was done within. Whatever happened among the ledgers and the ink stayed among them, sealed like a jar, and the fog that pressed against the island's walls all winter pressed against this room hardest of all, finding no seam to enter by.

He worked at the second desk, the one nearest the lamp, because his eyes were the youngest in the building and Magistrate Corren believed — or said he believed — that fine work required good light. The other clerks, Pell and old Master Dunmore, had desks in the darker corners where they entered shipping manifests and duty payments, honest arithmetic if such a thing still existed on this island. Casper's work was different. Casper's work was the seizure orders.

Tonight there were nine of them.

He copied them out in his small, even hand, the hand that had won him the position three years ago when he'd arrived from the mainland with a letter of recommendation and a trunk full of books and the belief — God help him, he could still feel the shape of that belief, the way you can feel the socket of a tooth that's been pulled — that a clerk's virtue was precision. That the law was a machine and the man who kept its gears oiled served something larger than himself. He had been twenty-three. He was twenty-six now, and the machine had shown him what it ground.

*By order of the Magistracy of the Northern Isle,* he wrote, *the vessel* Merrow's Daughter *and appurtenances thereof are forfeit to the Crown's provision, the debt of* — and here the figure, forty pounds, eleven shillings, a sum invented two desks away by a man named Harl who kept Corren's private accounts and laughed too easily — *being six quarters in arrears.*

Six quarters in arrears. Tam Meade had fished the* Merrow's Daughter *for nineteen years. Tam Meade had paid every levy ever laid against him until the new levies came, the ones nobody had voted on, the ones assessed so suddenly and so steeply that no fishing family on the island could have met them if they'd sold their nets, their pots, their roofs. Six quarters in arrears meant nothing except that someone had written it down, and what was written down became true. That was the whole discovery of Casper's life, the thing he had learned in this windowless room: writing did not record the world. Writing replaced it.

His pen scratched on. The lamp hissed. Outside, the great storm that the fishermen had been watching for three days was still assembling itself somewhere over the northern water, and the walls of the customs house ticked and shifted with the pressure of it, like the ribs of a sleeping animal dreaming uneasily.

"Casper." Pell's voice from the dark corner, low. "The Meade one. You've got the arrears at six quarters. Harl's sheet says four."

Casper did not look up. "Harl corrected it this afternoon."

"He corrected it upward?"

"Four quarters wouldn't meet the forfeiture threshold under the maritime provision. Six does."

There was a silence. In it Casper could hear everything: the wind finding the eaves, the sea working itself up beyond the seawall, Dunmore's wheezy breathing as the old man pretended to study a manifest he had read an hour ago. Pell was perhaps thirty, with a young wife in the fishing quarter and a way of not asking questions that Casper had once mistaken for stupidity and now understood to be the most expensive discipline on the island.

"Right," Pell said, and went back to his manifests, and that was the end of it, and Casper hated him a little for letting it be the end of it, and hated himself more for being grateful.

* * *

He finished the ninth order near midnight. His hand had gone stiff with cold — they let the fire die after ten o'clock, another economy that somehow never touched the magistrate's house on the hill — and the last lines came out thinner than the first, so that the page began confident and ended in something close to a whisper, which felt right, which felt like the truth of the document finally leaking through his pen whether he willed it or not.

He set the copies in the drying rack and gathered the originals for return to Harl's cabinet, and that was when he saw it.

It should not have been there. He understood that immediately, and the understanding arrived not as thought but as temperature — a cold dropping through him faster than the room's cold, a cold with a direction, pointing at the thing itself. Beneath the nine fraudulent orders, at the bottom of Harl's folio, lay a ledger he had never seen before. Small. Bound in grey cloth gone soft at the corners. Unlabeled.

Every ledger in the customs house was labeled. That was the first law of Harl's kingdom, the only rule enforced with any consistency: everything named, everything shelved, everything findable. An unlabeled ledger was either contraband or a mistake, and Harl did not make mistakes with paper. Paper was the one thing he loved.

Casper stood with the folio in his hands and the lamp hissing beside him and listened to the building breathe.

Open it, said something in him. Don't open it, said something else, older and more practiced, the voice that had governed three years of nights: *you copy, you do not read; you carry, you do not know; a clerk who knows things is a clerk who can hang.* He had built that voice sentence by sentence, plank by plank, the way a man builds a wall against rising water, and for three years it had held. But walls get tired. Walls get tired exactly as the man inside them gets tired, and Casper was tired in a way that sleep did not touch anymore. He woke each morning with his jaw clenched and the taste of coins in his mouth, and last week, signing the Osei seizure preliminary — the widow Osei, whose kitchen had fed half the island through the hungry spring, whose name even Corren spoke carefully — he had found that his eyes were wet and could not remember their beginning, and Pell had been at the far desk, and no one had seen, and that no-one-had-seen had frightened him worse than the tears.

He opened the ledger.

It was Harl's own hand inside, which somehow made it worse; Harl's neat, cheerful figures, the figures of a man doing sums he enjoyed. And what the figures recorded was the manufacture itself. Not debts incurred and pursued — debts *created*, line by line, dated back months and years, entries posted to accounts that had never held money, interest calculated on sums never lent, and beside each fabricated entry a notation in the margin: *Corren approved. Corren approved. C.A., 12 March. C.A., 30 May.* Forty families so far. Forty names, and beside each name the true history of their account — the payments actually made, the levies actually met — kept in parallel, kept honestly, as though some part of Harl needed one clean record the way a man who lies all day needs one room in his house where he never lies.

Or as insurance, said the colder voice. As leverage. Harl was not sentimental. If this was insurance, then Harl knew what it was worth and would kill to keep it, and here it was, in Casper's hands, in a room with two witnesses pretending to work.

He turned the pages slowly. The wind rose outside and the lamp guttered and steadied. Tam Meade was on the fourth page, his real payments marching down the left column like soldiers, the invented debt climbing the right column like ivy over a wall. On the sixth page, Vesper Zhou — the lighthouse keeper, the widower; Casper knew the name the way everyone did, had seen the man once at the autumn market buying rope with a little girl trailing him like a small determined shadow. Zhou's account was clean. Completely clean. And beside it, in Harl's margin: *No debt possible. Lighthouse exempt by charter. Watch him anyway — he sees the traffic.*

*He sees the traffic.* Casper sat with that a long moment. The lighthouse stood above the causeway and the anchorage both; its keeper watched every boat that moved at night, including, presumably, the boats that ran unmanifested cargo between Corren's cellar and the mainland buyers whose names Casper had copied onto requisition forms without ever being told why a magistrate needed mainland goods delivered by night. Of course they watched him. Of course the light itself — the faithful, blind, sweeping light he had steered home by in his first island winter, before he'd taken this post — was a problem to be managed.

Something closed in his chest, quiet and total, like a door shutting in a distant room. Later he would understand that it was the sound of the wall coming down — not collapsing, not dramatically, just quietly deciding it had held long enough — but in the moment he felt only calm, the terrible administrative calm of a man who has stopped arguing with himself because the argument is over.

Dunmore coughed. Pell's pen scratched. Neither of them looked up.

Casper closed the grey ledger. He took the nine fraudulent orders from the drying rack, folded them into the folio, and laid the folio on Harl's desk to await morning collection. Then he removed his coat from the peg by the door, put it on, and as he shrugged the shoulders settled — a movement no larger than settling a coat — the ledger went inside the lining, against his ribs, where his heartbeat would keep it company.

He did not allow himself to hurry. Hurrying was how men were caught; he had processed enough smuggling cases to know that the guilty moved fast and the innocent moved like tired men going home to bed. So he banked the lamp, wound the clock over the manifest cabinet, wished Pell a flat goodnight and received a flat goodnight in return, and stepped out into the fog with the storm coming on behind him like a tide.

The dunes took him the long way, the path past the marram grass where the sand hissed dry even in rain, because the direct road passed the magistrate's house and the watch post, and a man with a stolen book against his ribs learns very quickly to love detours. The fog was thickening as the pressure dropped; the island was contracting to the length of his arms. Somewhere off to the north the sea was already breaking heavy — he could feel it through the ground, a deep irregular drumming, the storm arriving ahead of itself. Overhead, invisible, the lighthouse beam swung past, and for half a second the fog around him glowed like paper held behind a flame, and he thought: *that's what I am now. Held behind a flame.*

Where would he take it? The question caught up with him in the hollow below the dunes, and he made himself stand still and face it, because sentiment without a plan was just suicide wearing a better coat. Not the mainland — the bridge closed within the week, and the checkpoint already logged departures; a clerk leaving the island days after touching an unlabeled ledger would be a story with one ending. Not Harl — whatever the ledger was to Harl, it was not a gift Casper could survive giving. Not the constable, who was Corren's. Not the priest, who was frightened.

Which left the islanders themselves. Which left the names in the book.

He thought of the fisherman's daughter he had seen once from the customs house window, walking the causeway road with her dog, and of the widow whose seizure he had signed, and of the lighthouse keeper buying rope with careful hands. People who owed the magistrate nothing but their ruin. People who lit lamps and mended nets and did the unglamorous arithmetic of staying alive, and who outnumbered the men in the windowless room by a hundred to one, if they ever knew.

If they knew. Everything came down to that. Paper only replaced the world if people believed the paper.

Rain reached the dunes then, the first hard slanting strokes of the storm's leading edge, and Casper pulled his collar up and turned toward the point, toward the light he could no longer see but could still feel sweeping somewhere above the fog. His ribs ached where the grey ledger rode against them, warm now, warmed through, almost alive.

In the morning, Magistrate Corren would discover the ledger missing, and Harl would turn the customs house inside out, and the search would begin that would follow Casper to the edge of everything. He did not know that yet. He knew only that his feet had chosen a direction while his mind was still deliberating, and that for the first time in three years the two parts of him — the clerk who copies and the man who reads — were walking together, in step, through the rain, toward the lighthouse.

Behind him the storm came on. Ahead of him, faint through the fog, a dog began to bark.
