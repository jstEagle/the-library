## 4. Storm Signs

Nell Zhou knew the island's moods better than she knew her letters, and on the morning the boats stopped going out, she felt the change in her teeth before she understood it in her ears.

She was sitting on the sea-wall below the fishing sheds, legs swinging over water that looked like hammered pewter, when Tam Yuen's boat *Sweet Alice* came around the point under oars instead of sail. That was wrong for three reasons. First, there was wind enough to carry a church roof — it had been combing the grass flat all night, combers of it, long silver waves running up the hillside toward the lighthouse where Papa kept his lamp. Second, Tam loved his sails the way other men loved their children; he furled them grudgingly even in the worst weather. Third, a man rowing into harbor that slowly, that carefully, with his hat pulled down and his shoulders rounded like a question mark, was not coming home from fishing.

There were no fish in his hold. Nell could see that from fifty yards. The boat sat too high in the water, light as an eggshell.

She slid off the wall and followed the boat in, the way you follow anything strange — not because you should, but because your feet decide before your head is consulted.

* * *

The sheds smelled of tar and herring and old rope, a smell Nell loved so much she sometimes stood at the shed door just breathing it, the way other people smelled bread. This morning the smell had company: too many boots, too little talking. Half the fishing families were crowded inside Tam's shed, and they were standing in a half-circle the way people stand around a grave, and in the middle of the half-circle sat a table that did not belong to anyone's shed.

At the table sat a thin man with ink-stained fingers and a face like a folded letter. Behind him stood two of Magistrate Corren's men in their gray coats, arms crossed, watching the fishermen the way cats watch a hole in the wall.

Nell knew the thin man slightly. He was the magistrate's clerk. She had seen him once or twice on the ferry steps with his leather case held against his chest like something warm he didn't want to catch cold. Papa said his name was Casper and that he had mainland manners but island eyes, which Papa said without explaining, because Papa believed some things explained themselves if you watched long enough.

Nell watched now, from behind a stack of creels, small enough and quiet enough that grown men forgot a child could see them.

Tam Yuen put his hand flat on the table. It was a big hand, rope-scarred, the hand of a man who had hauled nets since before Corren had ever seen the island. It lay there among the papers like something landed by accident.

"Read it to me again," Tam said. His voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when shouting would break something he couldn't afford to break. "Slow."

The clerk picked up the paper. "By order of the magistrate's court," he read, "the debt of Tam Yuen, being found lawful and owing in the sum of—" he paused, wet his lips, "—being found lawful and owing, the vessel *Sweet Alice* is entered as surety until such sum is discharged."

"Surety," Tam said. "That's a fine word. What's it mean?"

"It means the boat isn't taken," the clerk said. "It's held."

"Held where? In my slip? Under my tarpaulin? Who holds it?"

"The court holds it. In law." The clerk's voice was very soft. Nell noticed his hands were not steady. "Mr. Yuen, I'll be plain with you, since plainness costs me nothing today. There are figures against your name that you never signed for. I know it and you know it. But the figures are in the book, and the book is in the court, and the court is the magistrate, and until someone can show otherwise—" He stopped himself, as though he'd walked to the edge of a cliff and looked down.

"Until someone can show otherwise," Tam repeated. His wife made a sound behind her teeth, small and terrible, like a gull. "And how would a man show otherwise? If the book says what the book says, and the man who keeps the book is that one's clerk—" he tipped his chin at the gray coats, "—where does a man go to say his piece?"

"Nowhere," said one of the gray coats pleasantly. "That's the beauty of it."

Nobody laughed. Nobody breathed, hardly. Tam Yuen looked at his boat through the open door — Nell could see the *Sweet Alice*'s nose poking out of the water beyond him, bobbing gently, innocent as a sleeping dog — and then he did the thing Nell would remember all her life, the thing she would think about years later when she understood what it had cost him. He nodded. Slowly, twice, like a man agreeing to his own funeral arrangements.

"Then she's yours till spring," he said. "But hear me, boy." He leaned close to the clerk, so close Nell could barely hear what came next, though she caught every word anyway, words travel strangely in a silent room. "I know what you write down. I've watched you at the ferry steps. Your hand shakes when you write lies and it doesn't shake when you write truth. A man might notice such a thing. A man might find it useful someday."

For one heartbeat the clerk's eyes came up — gray eyes, fever-bright, frightened and fierce at once — and met Tam's. And then they flicked sideways, to the creels, to the shadow where Nell crouched, and stopped.

He'd seen her. She was sure of it, absolutely sure, though he gave no sign except a small settling of his shoulders, as if a coat had been put back on. He gathered his papers, wished the shed a courteous good morning in his mainland voice, and walked out past her hiding place so close she could smell the ink on him, sharp as pine, sour as pennies.

The gray coats followed. Outside, gulls screamed and wheeled over the *Sweet Alice*, already arguing about who owned her now.

* * *

Afterward Nell did not go home. Home meant Papa, and Papa on a morning like this would ask questions with his eyebrows while pretending to wind the clockwork, and she wasn't ready to be seen yet. Instead she went where she always went when the world got too full of adults doing arithmetic with people's lives: up the hill, past the second fence, to the orchard.

It wasn't much of an orchard anymore. Once — so Widow Osei said — it had been a proper orchard, planted by the first keeper's wife, apples that made a cider strong enough to strip paint. Now only one tree remained, growing crooked out of a fold of ground where the wind couldn't get its full grip, alone in the rough grass with nobody to keep it company but the lighthouse above and the sea below. Its apples were small and hard and more acid than sweet, and the birds took most of them, and nobody minded.

Nell sat down in its shade — thin shade, more suggestion than shelter — and hugged her knees.

They were selling everything, she thought. Not selling. Trading. Mrs. Prynne's skiff for a debt Mrs. Prynne had never heard of until last month. Old Hollis's two boats for a figure on a page. The Deng family's whole livelihood, gone into a ledger like coins into a pocket, and none of them had ever seen the coin. Debts you couldn't see, debts you couldn't count, debts that grew in the dark like mushrooms — that was what the grown-ups wouldn't say out loud but what all their faces said instead. Papa said the islanders' trouble was that they trusted the shape of things: a deed with a seal on it must be true, a book kept by an official must be honest. Mama, when she was alive, used to laugh at Papa for the same faith in shapes. *Vesper, my love, a box is only as honest as what's inside it.*

Nell missed her mother most at moments like this, when something funny and true occurred to her and turned to give it to somebody, and there was no somebody. She said it aloud to the tree instead, because the tree was what she had.

"A box is only as honest as what's inside it," she told it. "So what's inside you, then?"

The tree moved its branches. Wind, probably. But it felt like an answer.

She thought about the clerk. Casper. His eyes had found her in the dark of the shed, and instead of pointing her out — instead of saying *there, a listener, a spy, drag her out* — he had done nothing. Pulled his coat back on. Walked on. Why? Grown-ups did things without reasons, mostly, but this felt like a reason-shaped hole. He had written lies with a shaking hand. Tam had said so, right out loud, in front of everyone, which meant everyone had known it already; it takes a whole island a long time to know a thing and about five minutes to start saying it once it's known. And a man whose hand shook at lying, sitting all day in a room full of lies, writing lie after lie after lie—

Nell tried to imagine it and couldn't, quite. It would be like being made to breathe seawater all day. Your body would fight it. Your hands would shake.

"You're sad," she told the tree. Then, correcting herself, because the tree hadn't been the sad one: "*He's* sad. The ink man."

And then it happened, the way it happens in childhood — a word arriving fully formed, wearing its own name-tag, no ancestry, no explanation, simply *there*. She looked at the crooked tree in its fold of hillside, holding on against the wind the way the fishing families held on against the magistrate, alone and stubborn and still making fruit, and she thought, with total certainty, the way you know your own name:

*Casper.*

"Casper," she said out loud, testing it. The tree shivered in the wind and did not object. "All right. You're Casper now."

Why that name, for that tree? She had no reason she could have given anyone. It wasn't clever, wasn't a joke, wasn't like her usual naming system (which ran mostly to descriptive honesty: the rock by the jetty was Big Ugly, the gull with the crooked wing was Captain Limp). Something about the tree and something about the man had touched somewhere deep in her, two lonely stubborn things in gray weather, and fused. If Papa asked why — and Papa would not ask, Papa respected names the way he respected weather, as things that arrived uninvited and had to be lived with — she would have said *I don't know,* and meant it, completely.

She stood, wiped her palms on her skirt, and patted the bark the way you'd pat a horse's neck. "Don't tell," she advised it. "Names are secret until they aren't."

Down in the harbor, another boat was being rowed in under oars.

* * *

She went down again — of course she went down again; she was nine years old and the harbor was the only theater on the island — and this time it was the Prynnes' skiff, and this time there was no table, no clerk, just a gray coat with a list, ticking boats off like parcels delivered. Mrs. Prynne stood on the slipway with her sons flanking her, three women's worth of fury packed into one small body, while a stranger measured her boat with a knotted string.

"That's my husband's boat," Mrs. Prynne said, in a voice that carried across the water clear as a bell. "My dead husband's boat. He built her with these two boys hauling wood for him when they were smaller than that dog. And you're telling me she belongs to a debt no one ever showed us, signed by no one living, witnessed by no one living, held in a court that sits on the mainland eleven months of the year?"

"I'm telling you the entry stands," the gray coat said. He sounded bored. Boredom, Nell decided, watching him, was the worst expression a face could make. Anger at least admitted the other person existed.

"Then your entry," said Mrs. Prynne, "is a lie with a seal on it."

The words went through Nell like a fishhook. *A lie with a seal on it.* Her mother's box, inverted. So that was the whole trick then — not magic, not devilry, just lies dressed in the clothes of truth, and everyone too respectful of the clothes to check underneath. The gray coats wore gray. The clerk wrote in a fair hand. The seals were red wax, properly round. Everything on this island that was stealing them blind looked exactly like the things they'd been taught to trust.

Mrs. Prynne's sons took the oars out of the skiff. Just took them, quietly, and carried them home. It was not in any way the law allowed. It was entirely what the law should have allowed. The gray coat wrote something down.

Widow Osei appeared at the top of the slipway — she appeared places the way weather appears, suddenly and everywhere at once — and drew Mrs. Prynne's arm through her own and steered her away, talking loudly about flour prices, and Nell understood without being told that this was rescue, that Osei's chatter was a blanket thrown over a fire before it could spread. Osei's eyes swept the crowd as she passed and rested on Nell for half a second, weighing her, filing her. Nothing escaped Osei. Nothing ever had.

Nell turned and climbed the hill home with the wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, hurrying her along, or warning her, or both.

* * *

Papa was winding the clockwork when she came in. Of course he was. Papa wound the clockwork the way other men prayed — at fixed hours, with both hands, eyes somewhere else. The great lens above them turned in its slow eternal circuit, sweeping its blade of light around the empty dayroom, once, twice, patient as tide.

"Tam Yuen's boat is held," Nell said, before he could ask. "Surety. Same for the Prynnes' skiff and both of Hollis's. The ink man read it out of a book." She hesitated on the edge of the next part, then said it anyway, because keeping things from Papa made them heavier, not lighter. "Tam told the clerk his hand shakes when he writes lies. Papa. What happens to a man like that? A man who knows what he's writing?"

The clockwork took its last quarter-turn and clicked home. Papa stood very still, looking at the lens turning, and Nell watched his face do the thing it did when grief and thinking occupied the same room and neither would leave — a tightening around the eyes, a settling, like a house in wind.

"A man like that," Papa said slowly, "is carrying something heavy. And heavy things want setting down. The only question is where." He looked at her then, and something in his look made her think he was answering a different question than the one she'd asked, one he was asking himself. "Where a man sets a thing down matters, Nellie. It matters more than almost anything."

Outside, the wind had changed its voice. All day it had been the ordinary combing wind, the island's everyday breath. Now there was a new note under it, lower, longer, the sound the sea makes before it decides to become weather. Papa heard it too; she saw him tilt his head like a bird, and his jaw set, and he began checking oil gauges that did not need checking, because that was what Papa did with dread — he filed it under maintenance.

"Storm coming?" Nell asked.

"Soon," Papa said. "Days, maybe less. The glass is falling faster than I like." He glanced at the barometer, then out the seaward window at the horizon, where the water had gone that particular bruised color that meant the Atlantic was rolling its sleeves up. "When it comes, it will come hard. And the magistrate's men don't like storms. Storms close the bridge."

"Why do they not like that?"

"I don't know," said Papa. But he said it in the voice he kept for things he knew perfectly well, and Nell, who had inherited her mother's ear for boxes, heard the falseness of the contents and let it lie.

That night she dreamed of the ink man. He was sitting at a table in the dunes with the sea running behind him, writing and writing, and his hand shook so badly the letters flew off the page like startled birds, and each bird became a boat, and the boats flew low over black water looking for a light, and there was no light, the horizon was bare, and Nell woke with her heart banging and lay listening to the real wind rising outside the real glass, higher and steadier than any wind of October she could remember.

Below her window, at the foot of the lighthouse, the crooked tree stood in the dark getting the storm's first taste of it, bending but not breaking, holding on.

"Casper," she whispered to it through the glass. "Hold on."

Out on the causeway road, hours later, in the rain and the rising wind, a man with a shaking hand and a leather case pressed to his chest would come stumbling up the hill toward the light — but that, Nell could not know yet, however clearly she seemed to see it in the dark.
