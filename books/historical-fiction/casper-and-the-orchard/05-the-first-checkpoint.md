## 5. The First Checkpoint

The oil came from the mainland store twice a season, and Vesper had made the crossing so many times in eleven years that his body knew the ferry's rhythm better than his own heartbeat. He rose before the gulls did, banked the lamp down to its night watch, wound the clockwork an extra quarter-turn to buy himself the hours, and left Nell sleeping with the dog — she had named it Bracken, though it answered to nothing but the smell of bread — curled at the foot of her bed like a second blanket.

He wrote the note anyway. *Gone for oil. Back by dark. Feed yourself and the dog, not the other way round.* He had written some version of that note since Nell was four years old, back when Maren was still alive to read it aloud to her, tracing each word with a finger as if teaching herself the alphabet of their small life. Now he pinned it to the doorframe where he always pinned it, and stood a moment longer than the errand required, looking at his own handwriting as though it might tell him something new.

It didn't. It never did. That was the comfort of it.

The fog was thinning when he reached the causeway road with the empty oil tins rattling on the handcart, forty pounds of iron and tin that sang a different song empty than full. The island lay behind him in grey layers: the lighthouse white against the pale sky, the fishing roofs below it patched with whatever the storms had spared, the orchard tree standing alone on its rise above the dunes with its crooked branches bare. Casper, Nell called it. She would not say why. Children name things the way weather names days — without permission and without explanation.

The bridge itself was old timber and older iron, half a mile of planking over the tidal flats, built by men who had assumed the island would always want to be connected. At its far end stood the mainland store, the customs shed, and — new since autumn — a table.

Vesper saw the table before he saw the men behind it.

* * *

There had been no table in October. There had been no men in Corren's grey coats in October either, or if there had, they had kept to the magistrate's house and let the island pretend they were guests. Now there were three of them at the bridgehead, and the table between them and the mainland was draped in oiled canvas against the damp, and on the table sat a ledger bound in black leather, a stoppered inkwell, two pens, and a lantern burning against the morning murk though the day was well up.

"Name," said the nearest one. He was young, with the soft jaw of a man who had never missed a meal, and he held his pen the way schoolboys hold them — too tight, afraid of being wrong.

"Zhou. Vesper Zhou." Vesper set the handcart's handles down. "Lighthouse keeper."

"You live on the island side."

"I've lived on the island side for eleven years."

"Purpose of crossing."

"Oil. For the lamp." He tipped his head at the tins. "You'd rather I ran short?"

The young man did not smile. Behind him, the second guard — older, greyer, with a face like a folded map — turned a page in the black ledger and wrote without looking up: *Zhou, V. Lighthouse. Oil. Out.* The pen scratched like a beetle in a wall.

"When do you return?" the map-faced one asked.

"Before dark. Sooner if the wind holds."

"Return through this point," the man said. "Both directions are logged now. Magistrate's order." He said it evenly, without apology, the way a tide chart says *high water at six*. "Anything you carry out gets listed. Anything you carry back gets listed. Sign here."

He turned the ledger around. Vesper looked at the column of names above the blank line. He knew every one of them. Halvard Ness, who fished the north shoals. The Osei brothers, coming over with wool, going back with flour. Old Tam Wicker, who crossed every Tuesday to sit with his sister on the mainland and had done so for nine years without anyone asking him to sign his life into a book. Eleven names on the outbound page already, and the morning not half spent.

Vesper signed. His hand did the thing his hands always did — steady, economical, the hand of a man who had learned during Maren's illness that trembling wasted candle-oil — and the young guard waved him through as if waving through a cart of turnips.

But Vesper felt it, walking the long planks toward the mainland, the way a dog feels a fence go up. Not the table itself. Tables could be burned. It was the *page*. Someone, somewhere behind that folded-map face, would read tonight that Vesper Zhou had crossed out with five empty tins and would expect him to cross back with five full ones, and would know the hour of it, and the weight of it, and would write it all down again in a book that did not belong to the island.

An island was a cage only if someone kept count of the door.

* * *

The mainland store smelled of paraffin and rope and the particular dust of places that sell to people who cannot afford to browse. Old Sef, the storekeeper, had Vesper's tins filled before the pleasantries were done — he was efficient that way, and deaf enough that efficiency cost him nothing in conversation.

"Bridge table's new," Vesper said, while Sef pumped.

Sef grunted. Pumped. Filled the first tin to the gauge line and moved to the second.

"They logging everyone?"

Another grunt, but this one had a shape to it. Sef set the pump handle down, wiped his hands on his apron, and leaned across the counter far enough that Vesper could smell the coffee on his breath.

"Corren's been buying paper," Sef said quietly. "Debts. Notes of hand. Half the fishing families on your island owe money to somebody, and come winter, somebody's going to call those notes in — and the somebody will be Corren, or men wearing Corren's coat. He's buying cheap because he knows something the fishermen don't." Sef straightened up. "He knows the winter's going to be bad. Storms coming off the north water like nobody's seen in twenty years. Boats won't go out. Fish won't be caught. Debts won't be paid. And then the deeds get called due, and the magistrate holds the paper, and your island stops belonging to the people who were born on it."

"That's speculation," Vesper said. But even as he said it, he thought of Halvard Ness's face at the autumn market, the way Ness had sold his second boat for less than the wood was worth, and how Ness had looked at the water afterward — not grieving, exactly. Calculating. A man doing sums he didn't like.

"It's arithmetic," Sef said. "I sell to both shores, keeper. I hear the numbers from both sides." He resumed pumping, louder than before, as if the noise itself were a kind of discretion. "Sign your book. Keep your head down. Winter ends, the bridge opens proper, and maybe the man buys different paper by spring."

Maybe. Vesper carried the word out with the tins, and it weighed more than the oil.

* * *

He allowed himself one indulgence on the return leg, because the wind was at his back and the tins were heavy and a man can carry only so much caution in the same arms as forty pounds of paraffin. He stopped at the customs shed — the island's own little annex, a plank building with a stove and a stove-warmed clerk named Pimm who had held the post since before Corren arrived and intended, with the passive genius of the very dull, to hold it after.

Pimm was feeding the stove shavings. He looked up when Vesper came in, and something crossed his face — quick, gone, like a fish under ice.

"Cold day for hauling," Pimm said.

"Cold day for everything till spring." Vesper set the tins by the door. "Thought I'd warm my hands. Bridge men have got me signing my own shadow away."

"Yes, I heard." Pimm fed the stove another shaving. His eyes went, briefly, to the cabinet behind his desk — the records cabinet, green-painted iron, where the island's deeds and titles and tax rolls lived in their neat folders. It was locked. It was always locked. But Pimm glanced at it the way a man glances at a sleeping animal he is not sure is friendly.

"Everything all right here?" Vesper asked.

"Why wouldn't it be?" Pimm said, too fast, and then smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Vesper had seen all day, because Pimm had never once in eleven years smiled at him. "Magistrate's been asking for copies of things. Routine. Audits." He fed the fire another handful, as if stoking it could burn the sentence out of the air. "Routine," he said again, softer, and Vesper understood that the word was not for him at all. It was Pimm talking himself down, the way you talk a dog down from a growl.

Vesper warmed his hands, thanked him, and left. Outside, the fog had thickened again, rolling in off the flats in slow grey ropes, and the bridge ahead of him had become a suggestion rather than a structure — planks appearing and vanishing, the checkpoint table materializing out of the murk like something dreamed rather than built.

He thought about what Sef had said. He thought about Pimm's glance at the green cabinet. He thought about the column of names in the black ledger, and how a list is a gentle thing right up until the moment someone decides what the list is *for*.

Then he put his head down and hauled.

* * *

At the table, the map-faced guard was waiting with the book already open to the inbound page.

"Tins," the man said.

Vesper tipped one. The oil sloshed, heavy and honest.

"Five tins, lamp oil, mainland store," the guard recited, writing. "Return time, quarter past two." He looked up, and for the first time his eyes did more than record — they measured. "You keep the light alone out there? Just you and the girl?"

The question was ordinary. Every question on the island was ordinary; that was what made the cage fit so well. Vesper heard himself answer it the way he answered everything: "Just us."

"Must be quiet work."

"It's work."

The guard wrote something else — a small notation, a tick or a dash, Vesper couldn't see which — and closed the book, and waved him onto the island as though the island were a room and Vesper were being shown back into it.

The planks rang under the cart wheels all the half-mile home. By the time the fog swallowed the bridge behind him, Vesper had catalogued the morning the way he catalogued everything, in portable units: one table, three men, one black book, eleven names outbound, his own name twice, one question about his daughter, one glance at a green cabinet, one word — *routine* — repeated by a dull man who was frightened.

None of it was proof of anything. That was the trouble with cages. They were assembled from ordinary pieces, each one legal, each one explicable, and you only recognized the shape of the whole when the latch clicked.

* * *

Nell met him at the dune path with Bracken orbiting her knees, and she talked the whole walk up — about the dog, about the widow Osei's ginger cakes, about a boy at school whose father had sold a boat and who now walked past the harbor with his eyes on the ground — and Vesper listened with half his mind and gave the other half to the lighthouse rising ahead of them, white and patient, its lamp dark in the daylight the way a heart is dark between beats.

He banked the fire, filled the reservoirs, trimmed the wicks. Ordinary work. Sacred in its ordinariness. While his hands moved through the ritual, the rest of him kept turning the day over like a stone, looking underneath.

And underneath, near the bottom, was the thing he had not let himself think on the bridge, because thinking it there, in front of the table and the book and the pens, would have shown in his face the way weather shows in glass.

The magistrate was collecting debts. The clerk was copying papers. The records were being audited. And every family on the island was being written into a book they could not read, in a hand they could not check, by men who answered to a man who smiled too easily.

Winter was coming. The storms were coming. And the island, which had always been poor and had never once been trapped, was beginning to feel like a trap.

That evening the wind swung north and the barometer fell so fast the needle seemed ashamed of itself. Vesper climbed the tower at dusk and lit the lamp, and its beam went out over the blackening water, steady as a promise, touching nothing it could save.

Far down the causeway road, small in the last grey light, he saw a figure coming across the flats toward the island — a single man, walking fast, stumbling once, getting up, walking on. No cart. No lantern. No business any sane person had being out with the weather turning.

Vesper watched him until the fog took him, and stood a long moment in the cold gallery with the lamp turning at his back, its light sweeping the dark like a slow search.

Then he went down and barred the door — not against the storm.
