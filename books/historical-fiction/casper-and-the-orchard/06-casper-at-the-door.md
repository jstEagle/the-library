## 6. Casper at the Door

The road out of Corren's town had been made for carts and certainty, and Casper Aldous had neither.

He walked with his left arm pressed to his ribs like a man carrying something breakable, which he was — though what was breakable was not his arm, or not only. The ledger rode against his chest inside his coat, bound in oilcloth, tied with the green string he'd stolen from the customs house drawer because it seemed right that a thing meant to save people should be tied in the color of growing things. He had laughed at that thought, alone on the causeway in the failing light, and the laugh had turned into coughing, and the coughing had bent him nearly double in the wet sand of the flats, and when he straightened there was blood on his glove. Not much. Enough.

The tide was coming in behind him. He could hear it — a long, patient sound, like a ledger being closed page by page. He did not look back. Looking back was a habit he had given up three days ago, in the moment when Harl Voss's boot had found him twice in the same place, below the ribs on the left, where the magistrate's men knew to put their boots because it made a man compliant without making him conspicuous. *We don't want you dead, clerk,* Voss had said, crouching over him in the records room with the lamp guttering. *We want you sensible.* Then he had shown Casper the list of names for next month's seizures, and the Osei name had been third from the top, and something in Casper had come apart quietly, the way wet paper comes apart — no tearing, just a soft surrender of all its structure at once.

*Sensible.* He had been sensible for eleven years. He had copied fraudulent orders in a fair hand and sealed them with the magistrate's wax and told himself that the alternative was worse, that a man who stayed near the machine could sometimes slow it, that the widow Osei would keep her boat one more season because of some delay he had engineered, some misfiled notice, some ink that dried too faintly to be read and had to be redone. He had kept a private arithmetic of these mercies, and the arithmetic had grown smaller every year until it was mostly zeroes, and zeroes, he understood now, lying to himself in columns as neat as any he had ever ruled.

He stumbled on the causeway stones and went down on his knees. The water was close now, licking at the road's edge, silver-grey in the last light. Somewhere ahead the foghorn spoke — a long low voice from the lighthouse, patient as a father calling a child home across a field. Casper had never thought much about lighthouses. They were entries in ledgers: oil, wick, salary, maintenance. A line item. Now, crawling back onto his feet, he understood that the line item was a promise someone kept every single night whether or not anyone was watching, and he found himself weeping — not from the pain, or not only — because he had spent eleven years doing the opposite of that, keeping nothing, promising nothing, watching.

The island rose ahead of him out of the fog like the back of a sleeping animal. On its shoulder stood the lighthouse, white and certain, and beneath it — he could just make it out through the grey — a crooked tree, bare-limbed, leaning away from the prevailing wind like a listener straining toward some other conversation.

He aimed himself at the light and walked.

* * *

The fever had been with him since the second day, riding the pain in his side like a passenger riding a ferry, occasionally standing up to look at the scenery. By the time he reached the island's single lane it had begun to speak to him in the voices of the people whose names he had copied.

*Elias Maren,* said his mother's voice, gentle as Sunday. *Boat and deed, seized October of last year, debt manufactured from a repair loan that was forgiven twice on paper and once in truth.*

*I know,* Casper said aloud, to the empty lane, to the gulls. *I know. I'm carrying it.*

*The Torvald girl,* said his old schoolmaster. *Fourteen years old. Her family's net rights signed over to cover a debt of nine crowns that appears nowhere in the original register.*

*I know.* He was walking faster now, though faster meant swaying, though swaying meant the world tilted like a ship's deck. *That's why I'm here. That's why—*

*You are here because you are afraid,* said the voice of Magistrate Corren, and this voice was the worst one, because it was reasonable, because it never raised itself, because it sounded like a man explaining weather to a visitor. *You are a clerk, Aldous. You have always been a clerk. Paper does not stop men like me. Paper is what men like me write on.*

Casper stopped in the lane, in the fog, in the rising wind, and put his good hand flat against the stone wall that bordered the path, and breathed.

The wall was old. Older than the magistrate, older than the customs house, older than the whole rotten apparatus of debts and deeds and seizures. His fingers found the grooves of it, the lichen, the small stubborn plants holding on in the cracks, and he thought: *the wall doesn't argue with the storm. It just stands where it stands.* And then he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like joy: *but I am not a wall. I am paper. And paper moves.*

He pushed off the wall and went on.

* * *

The lighthouse door was barred. Of course it was. He stood before it a long moment, swaying, listening to the sea come up the flats behind him, and raised his fist to knock — and then paused, because he heard, on the other side of the oak, the particular silence of a man listening. Not an empty house. A held breath.

"Keeper," Casper said. His voice came out cracked, half-swallowed by the wind. He tried again, louder, and heard how strange the words must sound through two inches of oak to a man who had spent the evening barring his door against exactly this kind of arrival. "Keeper. My name is Casper Aldous. I am — I was — the magistrate's clerk."

Nothing. The wind. The horn above him, sweeping its slow voice across the water.

"I have something for you." He pressed his palm against the door, leaned his forehead against the cold wood. The fever was climbing again; he could feel sweat starting at his hairline even in the cold. "Not for you. For the island. For — whoever. Anyone but him." A wave broke somewhere behind him, closer than the last. "I don't have time to convince you. I don't have time for anything. Please."

A long silence. Then a voice, low and careful, through the wood: "How do I know you're not one of his?"

It was a fair question, and Casper almost smiled, because it deserved better than desperation and he had nothing else to give. "You don't," he said. "I'd have written you a letter proving my good intentions, but I've spent eleven years writing letters for that man, and I'll not write another one. Open the door or don't. But if you don't, open it tomorrow for whoever finds me on your doorstep, because I will still be here, and the tide comes up over this lane twice a night, and I'd rather the ledger didn't drown with me."

Another silence — longer. And then the sound of the bar being lifted, wood scraping wood, deliberate as a held breath released.

* * *

Vesper Zhou was not what Casper had expected, though he could not have said what he had expected. Tall, spare, with the kind of face that keeps its own counsel; hands that moved with the economy of a man who has learned that everything he owns must be portable, everything he loves must be liftable and carried. His eyes went to Casper's arm first, then his face, then the door behind him, reading the lane, the weather, the absence of anyone else.

"You're hurt," Vesper said.

"Yes," Casper said. "Also dying, possibly, though I've been wrong about that twice today already." He got his boots over the threshold somehow — he would remember, later, the keeper steadying his elbow without being asked, the small humiliation and mercy of it — and the door closed behind him, and the bar went back down, and the wind became a thing outside instead of a thing everywhere.

The tower's base was clean and ordered in a way that hurt to look at, everything squared and shelved and labeled, and Casper understood it immediately for what it was: grief wearing the costume of habit. He had inventoried enough houses in his years of seizures to recognize a life arranged against loss.

"There's a chair," Vesper said. It wasn't a question or an offer, quite. It was a statement of fact about the location of chairs.

"The ledger first." Casper's legs were going; he could feel them going, the way one feels a tide turn, gradually and then entirely. He fumbled at the green string with numb fingers, failed, and then simply pulled the oilcloth bundle out with both hands and held it out into the lamplight between them, the way one holds out bread, or a child, or evidence. "Every seizure order for three years. The originals and his copies, side by side, in his own hand where they differ. The debts were manufactured, Keeper. Invented. Written after the fact to match whatever he wanted taken. There are forty-one families on this island and he means to hold paper against thirty-eight of them by spring."

Vesper did not take the bundle at once. He looked at it the way a man looks at a live coal — with respect, with fear, with the calculation of what it will burn. "Why bring it to me?"

"Because you keep a light," Casper said, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back. "Because you're not one of his. Because Widow Osei's kitchen is watched and her sons are known and every strong back on this island is already carrying too much. Because—" and here the fever took the wheel briefly, and honesty poured out of him unmeasured, "—because I need it to be someone, Keeper. Eleven years of copying those orders and I need it, finally, to be someone. I can't carry it to the mainland myself. Look at me. I couldn't carry it to your gate if your gate were further than your door."

And Vesper Zhou looked at him — a long, level look, the look of a man measuring not the bundle but the bearer — and then he took it. His hands were steady. That steadiness undid Casper more thoroughly than the fever had; he sat down hard in the chair without deciding to, and the room tilted, and the keeper's hand was on his shoulder, and Casper Aldous, who had not wept in eleven years of complicity, put his face down toward his own knees and shook.

"The tide," he managed, after a while. "Check the tide. If anyone followed—"

"No one followed. I watched the causeway for an hour before I barred the door." A pause. "There's soup. It's fish. Nell caught most of it."

"Nell?"

"My daughter." The word carried a weight Casper recognized, a whole life balanced on four letters. "She's asleep upstairs. She'll want to know you in the morning, so try to be someone worth knowing."

Casper laughed, and the laugh cost him, and he paid it gladly.

* * *

Later — how much later he could not say; the fever had begun folding time like linen — Vesper built up the fire and set a basin of water near it, and cut away the coat from Casper's ruined side with a sailor's knife, quick and unsentimental, and did not flinch at what he found. Casper watched the man work. There was a photograph on the mantel, a woman with grave dark eyes and a smile she seemed to be withholding from the camera out of politeness, and beside it a smaller frame, empty, and Casper thought: *there is a story in that empty frame, and it is not mine to ask after.*

"The ledger," Casper said. His voice was far away now, coming down a long corridor. "Page forty. The Osei entry. The margin — I wrote the true figures in the margin, in pencil, small. Pencil can be denied. Ink cannot."

"I saw."

"And Keeper—" The firelight swam. He gripped the arm of the chair. There were things that needed saying and the corridor was getting longer and he could feel the tide of it coming in behind his eyes, patient, page by page. "If I don't wake — and I want to be honest with you, I've been honest for approximately four hours now and I find I like it — if I don't wake, the pages matter more than the body. Bury me shallow if you have to. Under the tree, if it can be done. I've always liked that tree. Don't ask me why. I don't know why. Some things you know the way Nell knows—"

"Hush," Vesper said, not unkindly, pressing a cloth soaked in cool water to Casper's forehead, and Casper hushed.

But one more thing surfaced before the water closed over him, and he said it with his eyes already shut, in the voice of a man handing over not a ledger but his whole unredeemed life:

"There's a second copy. Customs house. Behind the third shelf in the map cabinet, wrapped in chart paper. He doesn't know about that one." A pause; the fire popped; the great lamp overhead turned and turned, its beam sliding across the shuttered windows like a slow search. "He means to burn the customs house at season's end. Everything. Every record, every deed, every scrap of the truth, and then the fog rolls in and the bridge opens and it's all perfectly legal forever."

The last thing Casper felt before sleep — or something like sleep — took him was Vesper Zhou going very still beside the fire, the stillness of a man doing sums he does not want to do, and then rising, quietly, to stand at the window with the ledger in his hands, looking out at the black water where the tide was coming in, and the storm behind it, and behind the storm, somewhere, spring.

Outside, under the leaning tree, the wind moved through the bare branches like a page being turned.
