## 7. What the Dead Ask

The ground took the spade grudgingly.

Vesper dug by lantern light beneath the orchard tree — the wild one that grew crooked out of the cliff-side soil where nothing else volunteered to grow, its branches bent inland as if leaning away from the sea. He had chosen the spot before he had decided anything else. The body could not stay in the lighthouse. The body could not go to the churchyard, because Casper had no name on this island that anyone would speak aloud at a graveside, and a grave without mourners was an invitation to questions Vesper could not answer. But the earth beneath the tree was soft with old leaf-rot and no one came here. No one but Nell and her dog, and the gulls, and the wind that came up from the water smelling of iron and cold.

He dug until his shoulders burned, and the whole time the ledger sat against his chest where Casper had pressed it, inside his coat, over his heart, like something transplanted there that would either take root or kill him.

*You keep it,* the clerk had said. Or Vesper remembered it that way now — memory already rearranging itself around the weight of what had been asked. *You're the one who keeps things lit.*

Casper had been thirty years old if he was a day, thin as a winter reed, fever burning out of him like heat off a stove-top, and he had laughed once — badly, wetly — when Vesper had said his name. *Like the tree?* he'd asked. And Nell, who had been eavesdropping from the stairs the way children eavesdrop as if it were their trade, had called down: *That's what we call it. Casper. The tree.*

So the man and the tree shared a name for one hour, and then the man stopped breathing somewhere between the third watch and the fourth, quietly, in the chair by the cold stove, with his hands folded in his lap like a man who had finished his work and knew it.

Vesper laid him in the ground with his coat buttoned to the throat because there was no one to wash him properly and Vesper would not leave him bare to the dark. He said the words he remembered from his wife's burial, the ones about the sea giving up its dead, though he stumbled twice and had to start again, and the wind kept pulling the sound out of his mouth and scattering it over the water toward the mainland, where Corren's lamps were burning in the customs house like patient yellow eyes.

He filled the grave. He set a flat stone at the head, unmarked. Then he sat down against the trunk of the tree, and he did not go inside, and this is how the night began: a widower, a ledger, and an argument he could not close like a door.

* * *

The sensible thing — and Vesper was, above all else, a sensible man; grief had taught him sensibility the way the sea teaches a hull its lines — the sensible thing was to walk to the magistrate's house in the morning and put the ledger on Corren's desk.

He turned it over the way he turned all problems: methodically, weighing each side like ballast.

If he gave it up, he lived. That was the beginning and nearly the end of it. He would say — what? That the clerk had come to him dying, delirious, pressing papers on him, and that Vesper, a simple keeper, a man with no politics and no learning worth the name, had taken them only to be rid of them. Corren might even believe it. There was nothing in Vesper's history to suggest otherwise: eleven years on this rock, no visitors, no letters, no opinions offered at the few gatherings where opinions were exchanged. A man who measured his life in portable things. A man whose wife had died upstairs while he wound the clockwork because the clockwork did not care that Marta was dying, and someone had to keep the light for the herring boats, and the dead do not need lamplight. He had made himself into exactly the kind of man who could not be suspected of courage. It had felt, at the time, like prudence. Now it looked like preparation for surrender.

And what would Corren give him for it? Safety, yes. Perhaps the mainland posting — the ferryman had said Corren bought men cheap, and a lighthouse keeper with a fatherless daughter and no income past his government stipend was very cheap indeed. Nell would have a school with more than nine pupils. Nell would have a doctor within reach, and shoes that fit, and a life that did not require her to barter eggs for lamp oil.

Against that: the fishing families. The Okafor brothers, who had mended Vesper's roofline after two storms without ever being asked and had refused payment with a shrug that closed the matter forever. Widow Osei, who sent soup up the hill every Friday as if Friday were law. The Pell family, whose boy had drowned two springs back and whose boat still went out every morning because the sea does not observe mourning. Fourteen families. He tried to count them honestly and got fourteen every time, which troubled him, because he wanted the number to be smaller. If it had been four families, perhaps. If it had been none he knew by name.

But it was fourteen, and the ledger in his coat said, in a clerk's careful hand, that their deeds were being taken through debts that had never existed — that the paper was manufactured in the customs house the way fog is manufactured by the meeting of warm water and cold air, naturally, invisibly, and in service of nothing but itself.

*Hand it over,* said the sensible voice, *and you are safe. Safe is not nothing. You have been safe since Marta died and it has kept Nell fed.*

*Safe,* answered another voice, one he did not recognize as his own, though it came from his own chest, *is what Corren is selling. Ask yourself who else is buying.*

* * *

Somewhere past midnight the moon went behind cloud and the island became a rumor of itself. Vesper stayed under the tree because going inside meant passing the chair where Casper had died, and he was not ready to pass the chair. Instead he listened to the sea working the rocks below the cliff, patient as a jeweler, and he thought about his wife.

Marta had been ill for three years, and in those three years Vesper had learned to carry everything that mattered in a manner that could be moved within an hour. Her medicines in one case. Their papers in another. Photographs flat against the bottom of the seabag so they wouldn't warp. He had done it without letting himself understand why, and it was only after she died that he understood: he had been practicing leaving. Some part of him had known she would go and had prepared for flight rather than grief, because flight was a problem you could solve with lists and grief was not. He had spent her last months organizing their belongings for an escape that was never coming, packing for a journey that had no destination, and when she died he had unpacked everything except the habit.

Now the habit had found its destination after all. Eleven years late, the packed bag finally had somewhere to go, and it was carrying a dead man's ledger instead of a living woman's photographs.

He almost laughed at that. He didn't, because laughing alone under a tree over a fresh grave was the kind of thing that, once started, might not stop.

The argument shifted ground, the way arguments do in the small hours, from *what should I do* to *who am I*, which is the same question wearing different clothes.

Because here was the thing he could not say to anyone, the thing that sat beneath the sensible arithmetic like a stone beneath topsoil: he wanted to give it to Corren. Not for safety. For absolution. If he handed the ledger over, then the whole terrible machinery of it would belong to someone else — Corren's fraud, Corren's ledgers, Corren's sin — and Vesper Zhou could return to being a man who maintained a light and asked nothing and owed nothing and saw nothing. Handing it over was not cowardice exactly. It was a kind of blindness, deliberately purchased, and God help him, he could afford it. He had saved for it his whole life.

But he had watched Casper die in that chair, and Casper had not been blind. Casper had copied the fraudulent orders with his own hand, night after night, and had grown sick with it — sick in the body too, though whether the fever killed him or the conscience loosened some knot the fever needed, no doctor would ever say. And at the end, dying, he had not asked to be forgiven. He had asked to be *useful*. He had carried those pages through the dunes with soldiers behind him and a bullet-graze along his ribs, straight up the hill to the only man on the island who kept a fixed light, because in his last hour Casper had needed to believe that some things were still aimed at preservation and not at plunder.

*You keep it. You're the one who keeps things lit.*

A man hands you his last act and calls it yours now. You can refuse it. Refusing is legal. Refusing may even be wise. But you will spend the rest of your life knowing that a dying man reached the top of your stairs, and you were home.

* * *

Toward the coldest hour, Vesper did what he always did when thinking failed him: he worked.

He went inside — past the chair, which he looked at directly, refusing to flinch, and which was only a chair, only wood and woven seat and the small impression a thin man leaves even after an hour — and he climbed the tower and checked the lamp. He trimmed the wick. He wound the clockwork mechanism that rotated the lens, listening to it take up the tension with its familiar ratcheted sigh, and he stood a moment watching the great glass turn, throwing its beam seaward once every eight seconds. Eight seconds. All night, every night, eleven years. The light did not know about magistrates. The light did not distinguish between the herring boat of a man whose deed was being stolen and the revenue cutter of the man stealing it. It swept them both home. It had never once asked whether the people in the boats deserved saving.

He had thought, once, that this indifference was the light's limitation. Standing there with a stolen ledger against his ribs, he understood it differently. The light was not indifferent. The light was simply faithful — faithful ahead of any question of merit, faithful the way rain is faithful, the way the tide is faithful. It did not decide nightly whom to serve. It had decided once, in its building, and then it simply served.

*That,* said the unfamiliar voice in his chest, *is the choice. Not tonight's choice. The one underneath it. Decide what you are, and the rest is maintenance.*

He thought of Nell asleep below, her breath audible through the floorboards if he stood in the right place, which he did, and waited until he heard it. Nine years old. She had named a tree today — named it after a stranger she'd met once, dying, in her kitchen — and when he'd asked why, she had shrugged and said, *He looked like he didn't have anything of his own. Now the tree is his.* As if that were obvious. As if naming things for the dispossessed were a thing children simply did, before the world taught them the price of claiming anything.

Nell would inherit whatever he chose tonight. Not the ledger — the choosing. She would grow up in the house his decision built, and she would learn from it whether safety was the highest thing a man could hold, or merely the lowest thing that held a man together.

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, the way dawn comes in fog season, arriving less as event than as concession. Vesper went down and made tea he did not want and sat across from the chair and drank it anyway, and when the light was strong enough he took the ledger from his coat and set it on the table and opened it and read it properly for the first time, page by page, the way you read a confession.

It was worse than he had thought, and better. Worse, because the fraud was systematic, patient, elegant — debts recorded against boats that had never been mortgaged, signatures traced and retraced until they blurred, dates adjusted backward to catch families in arrears they had never incurred. Better, because Casper had annotated it. In the margins, in a small precise hand that trembled only near the end: *false — see original, customs house, second copy retained.* Over and over. The man had not only stolen proof. He had built a map of the crime, corner to corner, and left it pointing at its own twin.

Vesper closed the book. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, something had finished its arguing — not resolved, exactly, but exhausted, the way a storm exhausts, leaving behind a flat calm in which decisions stand up plainly and can be seen.

He would not give it to Corren.

He would not, either, pretend to heroism about it. He was afraid. He would be afraid tomorrow and the day after and at every checkpoint and in every shadowed doorway between here and the bridge. Fear was going to sit in his chest like a second heart, pumping its black blood alongside the red, and he would simply have to function as a man with two hearts functions: poorly, and anyway.

But the ledger would go out. To the mainland inspector, to whoever in the provincial capital had jurisdiction over a magistrate gone rotten — someone, anyone, beyond the reach of Corren's yellow lamps. And if the cost was the posting, the safety, the tidy blindness he had been saving for — then the cost was the cost. Casper had paid more for less.

He wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, the way he wrapped spare wicks, and he considered where to hide it, and rejected the loft and the oil store and the loose stone by the hearth, all the places a searching man looks first, and finally — because the tree already bore the name, and graves keep secrets better than houses do — he thought of the marker stone, and put the thought aside unfinished, unfinished thoughts being the only kind that keep.

Outside, the fog had lifted just enough to show him the mainland, a dark smudge under the morning, and the bridge, and — small, moving, wrong — a mounted figure coming up the coast road toward the light, unhurried, at an hour when no one honest travels.

Vesper stood in the doorway of his own house and felt the two hearts begin to beat.

Behind him, on the table, the chair sat empty. Down the hill, already awake and running toward him through the grass with the dog behind her and her hair flying, Nell was shouting something he couldn't yet hear.
