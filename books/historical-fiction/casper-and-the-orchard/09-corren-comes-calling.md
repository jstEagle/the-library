## 9. Corren Comes Calling

Magistrate Aldous Corren did not care for islands. They were, in his long experience, places where men forgot their size — where the sea made them feel important and the smallness of their affairs made them feel sovereign. A man who could not leave an island for six months of the year began to believe the island needed him, and Corren had built a career on the knowledge that no one was indispensable anywhere, least of all to themselves.

But he dressed well for the crossing anyway, because dressing well was its own kind of argument.

The boat put him ashore below the lighthouse just after eleven, when the fog had thinned to a grey gauze that let the sun through as a pale disc, directionless. He climbed the path with his walking stick finding the stones precisely, unhurried, two of his men trailing at a distance he had instructed them to keep — near enough to be seen, far enough to be deniable. The stick had a silver ferule shaped like a heron's head, worn smooth by twenty years of tapping out his patience on courthouse floors. He had bought it the year of his first appointment and had outlived three superiors and one investigation since.

The lighthouse rose out of the fog in increments — first the base, white-washed and streaked with salt, then the gallery, then the lantern room with its black cap, the whole of it standing with the particular loneliness of things built to be looked at from far away. Corren had never cared for lighthouses either. They suggested, wrongly, that the world's dangers could be answered with a single steady flame, and that duty was its own reward. He had found neither to be true.

The orchard tree stood off to the left of the path, a stray apple gone semi-wild, half its fruit fallen into the grass where it sweetened and rotted. A dog lay beneath it — some mongrel of no breeding, tawny, one ear folded — and it lifted its head as he passed but did not bark. Corren noted the dog. He noted everything. That was the whole of his method: other men collected grievances or loyalties; he collected details, and details spent better than either.

He knocked at the door with the heron's head.

It took longer than courtesy required. He heard movement inside — the soft industrial sound of clockwork being wound, a ratchet clicking somewhere above like a slow insect — and then footsteps, and the door opened on Vesper Zhou.

Corren had prepared himself for grief. It was the widow's natural condition, and grief made men either pliable or flinty, and one needed only to determine which before choosing the instrument. What he had not entirely prepared for was the orderliness of the man. Zhou stood in his doorway the way a mechanism stands in its housing — fitted, oiled, nothing wasted. His shirtsleeves were rolled to the elbow with a precision that seemed less vanity than discipline. His eyes went to Corren's face, then briefly past his shoulder to the two men on the path, then back. Counting, Corren thought. Good. A man who counted was a man who understood arithmetic, and arithmetic could always be renegotiated.

"Magistrate," Zhou said. Not *sir*. Not *your worship*. Just the title, set down between them like a coin on a table.

"Warden Zhou." Corren smiled, which he knew from long practice read as warmth. "I hope you'll forgive an unannounced visit. I find that announcements turn visits into ceremonies, and ceremonies into lies. I wanted simply to call on the keeper of the island's most essential institution."

Something moved behind Zhou's eyes — not suspicion exactly; suspicion would come later, Corren expected it and respected it — something more like fatigue. "The lamp's lit every night. You can verify that from the mainland."

"I can. And do. Every evening, in fact, I look out my window and see your flame, and think: there is a man who understands what is asked of him." He let the compliment sit a moment, unwatered, then added, "May I come in? The fog is climbing up my coat."

* * *

The interior of the lighthouse surprised him, though he kept the surprise off his face, which was another thing twenty years had taught him. He had expected poverty dressed as tidiness. Instead he found a kind of severe beauty — the spiral stair worn pale at its center by a hundred years of climbing, the brass fittings polished until they gave back the grey light doubled, everything squared and shelved and labeled in a small precise hand. On the mantel stood a clock, and beside the clock a woman's photograph in a frame that had been repainted rather than replaced, and beside the photograph a jar of apples, cut and dried, arranged in concentric rings.

A child lived here too. He saw the evidence at once — a slate propped against the wall covered in a girl's careful figures, sums done twice in different colored chalk; a pair of boots too small for anyone else, lined up with their laces tucked in. Nell. The daughter. He turned this fact over gently, the way one turns a key one intends later to use.

"You keep a fine house," Corren said, running a finger along a shelf and examining it. Clean. Of course clean.

"It's not a house. It's a machine I live inside."

"An honest answer." Corren took the chair Zhou indicated without being invited to take it — a small usurpation, invisible unless watched for. "I'll be honest in return, then, since we're establishing terms. I've come about Casper."

There it was. The name landed in the room and Zhou did not touch it, did not move at all except to fold his hands, and Corren privately revised his estimate of the man upward. Most people flinched at names. This one filed them.

"The clerk," Zhou said.

"The clerk. A tragedy. A young man of considerable gifts, undone by — well, by the coast, I suppose. The damp gets into the lungs of newcomers. I'd known him only professionally, but I confess the loss sat poorly with me. He died, I understand, out here. In your dunes."

"In my dunes," Zhou agreed. Neutral as ballast.

"And was buried—" Corren let the sentence open like a hand, "—I'm told, under your tree. Which I confess I find touching. The islanders have taken to calling the tree by his name, I hear. Casper. As if a tree could hold a man's memory better than stone. Sentiment is a strange crop; it grows wherever it isn't planted."

Zhou said nothing. Outside, the dog barked once, at nothing, and stopped.

Corren leaned back and let the silence work for him. Silence was the tool most magistrates mishandled — they filled it with threats, or with offers made too soon. But silence properly deployed made the other man's mind begin to furnish the room with possibilities, and furnished rooms were always more convincing than described ones.

"I'll tell you why I've come, Warden, because I believe in saying difficult things plainly. When Casper died, he was — I must be delicate here — he was not in good standing. There are irregularities in the customs accounts. Discrepancies. A clerk in his position, underpaid and far from supervision, has opportunities, and I regret to say the audit suggests he took them. Nothing proven. He's dead, and I don't prosecute the dead; it profits no one and looks vindictive. But there may be papers. Working papers, drafts, notes in his hand. Things that would explain the discrepancies and let me close the file cleanly. For the record's sake. For his family's sake, if he has any."

"Has he?"

"No," Corren said softly. "Which is why it falls to strangers to be kind to his memory."

He watched Zhou receive this. The man's face was a lighthouse face — built to show a steady beam regardless of weather — but Corren had spent two decades reading faces that didn't wish to be read, and he caught the flicker at the phrase *close the file*. Fear? No. Calculation. Zhou was calculating, and a calculating man was a reachable man.

"Nobody brought me any papers," Zhou said. "Casper came to my door dying. I did what one does. I buried him."

"One does," Corren agreed warmly. "And I have no doubt it was a kindness. But Warden — may I call you Vesper?" He did not wait for permission; the name was already in his mouth. "Vesper. Let me describe to you the year you're going to have, because I'd rather you hear it from a friend than from weather."

He rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind him, studying the fog-bound water as if inspecting his own holdings.

"This season will be hard. The storms coming are worse than the ones your generation remembers — the pressure's wrong, the fishermen say so themselves, and the fishermen are rarely wrong about weather and rarely right about anything else. The bridge will close within the fortnight. The island will be on its own rationing, its own accounting, its own law — my law, in practical terms, since appeals take a season to drown. Now. A man alone on an island in a hard winter, with a daughter to feed and oil to buy, needs friends. And I am offering friendship of a very specific and durable kind."

He turned. Zhou had not risen, had not shifted; the folded hands might have been carved.

"There's a post on the mainland," Corren continued. "Harbormaster's assistant at West Quay. Salaried. Housed — there's a cottage attached, two bedrooms, a garden, and I mean a real garden, Vesper, soil you can grow a family in, not this gravel with delusions. Your girl would go to the town school, a good one, with other children instead of a slate and a dog. The lamp here would pass to an assistant I'd appoint, someone with mainland experience, and the island loses nothing and gains a magistrate who sleeps better knowing the light is competently kept." He smiled again, softer now. "All I ask in return is housekeeping. Any papers the clerk may have left. Anything at all in his hand — a receipt, a note, a scribbled sum. Bring them to me, and Monday next you're on the ferry as a salaried man of the mainland service. No winter. No rationing. No watching your daughter count calories on a slate."

He said *calories* deliberately. He had noticed the slate.

* * *

For a long moment the only sound in the tower was the clockwork overhead, the great weight descending through its gears, patient as gravity. Corren let it tick. He had learned long ago that the space after an offer belonged to the offered, and that rushing into it was like reaching to catch one's own echo.

"You've thought about this," Zhou said at last. Not a question. An observation delivered flat, almost admiring in its coldness.

"I make a profession of thinking about other men's winters."

"And if I told you there were no papers?"

"Then I'd believe you'd looked." Corren held the smile. "And I'd sleep soundly, and you'd still get the posting, because I reward candor, Vesper, even when candor is inconvenient. But I'd ask you to look again. Men who are dying do strange things with their last strength. They write letters. They settle scores. They entrust things to whoever happens to be standing nearest when death arrives — and in this case, that appears to have been you. It's a burden, being trusted by the dead. I know. I carry a few such trusts myself." He let that hang, ambiguous, a door left ajar onto darkness. "I'd like to relieve you of yours."

"And if I refuse the posting?"

"Refuse?" Corren laughed, genuinely, and the laugh was the truest thing he'd offered all morning, which was why it worked. "My dear man, it's not an offer one refuses. It's a tide. One doesn't refuse a tide; one notices whether one is floating or standing in front of it." He retrieved his hat from the table and settled it with two fingers, precise. "Think until Thursday. I'll send the boat Thursday, and whichever answer is ready can come aboard with me. If it's the papers, bring them wrapped. If it's only yourself, bring yourself. Either way, we part as friends, and I'd very much like us to part as friends, because winters are long and I hate beginning one with an enemy I haven't finished needing."

At the door he paused, as if struck by an afterthought, and turned back with an expression of avuncular concern.

"The girl — Nell, is it? Eleven? Remarkable arithmetic on that slate. My own schooling failed me at figures, so I notice it in others." He adjusted the brim of his hat a degree. "Children grow toward whatever shore we point them at. Worth pointing carefully."

He went down the path with his stick ticking the stones, the heron's head flashing dull silver, and the mongrel dog rose from under the tree and followed him exactly as far as the boundary of the yard and no farther, walking stiff-legged, silent, its eyes fixed on his back the entire way down to the boat.

Corren did not look around. He noted the dog anyway. Everything got noted; nothing got wasted.

In the boat, cutting through the grey water with the lighthouse shrinking astern, his senior man finally spoke. "He'll burn the papers, sir. That type. They burn them."

"Of course he'll consider burning them," Corren agreed pleasantly, watching the tower stand off in the fog like a candle in milk. "That's why I mentioned the girl. A man will burn his own options, Mr. Hale, but he will not often burn his daughter's future to spite me. By Thursday he'll have looked at that ledger — oh, yes, there's a ledger, there's always a ledger, dead clerks travel heavy with paper — and he'll discover he can't read it without deciding whose side of the arithmetic he stands on. And then he'll bring it to me. Or he'll try to use it, in which case I'll learn where it is from how he moves." He settled back against the thwart and closed his eyes against the thin light, entirely content. "Either way, the paper comes home. It always comes home. Paper is patient, Mr. Hale. People merely think they're the ones carrying it."

Behind them, high in the fog, the lamp turned once, unseen, over water where no ship yet needed it — and below, in the drowned light of the kitchen, Vesper Zhou stood at the window a long time, watching the boat diminish, one hand resting flat against the glass as though testing it for a break that hadn't come yet.

And in the grass by the grave marker under Casper's tree, half-hidden where the roots buckled the earth, the corner of a page had worked itself loose in the night wind — showing white for a moment each time the breeze lifted it, like something breathing, like something waiting to be found by small hands.
