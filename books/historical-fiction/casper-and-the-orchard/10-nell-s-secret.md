## 10. Nell's Secret

Nell Zhou had learned to count in secrets the way other children counted in sevens and tens.

One secret was the dog — the yellow-eyed stray with salt-stiffened fur who had appeared at the orchard tree in the first cold week and never left, whom she fed scraps from her father's table when Papa wasn't looking and called Bracken because his coat reminded her of the ferns behind the schoolhouse. Two was the loose stone in the lighthouse stair, third from the landing, that rocked underfoot if you stepped on it just so, which she did every single time out of a feeling she couldn't name, something between apology and greeting. Three was Mrs. Tull's limp, which everyone pretended didn't exist and which Nell had noticed on her very first day of lessons. Four —

Four was Casper's grave.

She had not meant to find it. That was the thing about secrets; they found you more often than you found them, the way Bracken had found her, appearing at her heel without ceremony as though he'd always been there. She had been carrying water for the widow Osei's kitchen — a chore she'd traded two hours of mending for, because Widow Osei let her talk while she worked and never told her that a girl her age ought to have quieter hands — when the rain had come sideways off the sea and she'd ducked beneath the orchard tree, the one they called Casper now, though nobody quite remembered who had named it first or why it had stuck the way names stick to islands: like barnacles, without anyone deciding.

The grave marker wasn't a proper stone. It was a slab of weathered granite that Vesper had hauled from the tideline, and he had carved no dates into it because he said he didn't know them, only a name, and even the name had been a guess offered by a dying man through cracked lips. The slab sat tilted at the tree's roots like a door left ajar. And when the rain softened the ground and the frost of early winter had heaved at it in the nights, the slab had shifted — shifted enough that Nell, sheltering against it with her shoulders, felt it move under her back and heard, unmistakably, the dry whisper of paper.

She should have put it down. She knew that even then, standing in the rain with her heart going like a gull's wings. Papa had come home from burying the clerk with a face like the fog itself, and he hadn't spoken for a day and a half, and when he finally had, his voice had been scraped thin: *Whatever comes of this, Nellie, whatever you see or hear, you leave it be.* He hadn't said what *it* was. He hadn't needed to. Children on the island learned early that the things adults refused to name were the heaviest things of all, and that their weight could be measured by the silence around them.

But the slab had moved, and the paper had whispered, and Nell Zhou was eleven years old and curious the way the tide is patient — endlessly, and against all instruction.

She waited until the rain passed. Then she waited three more days, because three was also the number of times she made herself walk past the orchard and *not* look, testing her own resolve like probing ice with one foot. On the fourth day, a grey still morning when Papa slept after his watch and Bracken trotted at her heels like an accomplice, she knelt in the wet grass and worked her fingers into the gap beside the slab and pulled.

The oilcloth packet came out heavier than she expected. Heavier than paper had any right to be, as though it carried not just ink but consequence. She unwrapped it right there in the grass, breath held, and looked at columns of figures in a small precise hand, at names she knew — Osei, Tull, Bren, the ferryman Halvard whose boat she'd ridden exactly twice — and beside each name a number, and beside each number a stamp in violet ink that she recognized because it sat on her father's own landing papers: the magistrate's seal.

She couldn't read what it meant. Not truly. But she understood its shape the way she understood storms by the ache in her ears before the wind arrived. These were debts. Everyone on the island whispered about debts now, at the well and the fish shed and outside the chapel after service, voices dropping whenever boots passed. Magistrate Corren was buying debts, Halvard had said on the last ferry before the bridge closed, and nobody had asked him to stop saying it, but nobody had said it loudly either.

Nell wrapped the packet again, careful as wrapping a bird's egg, and slid it back into the earth, and pressed the slab down with her whole weight until the gap closed to a hairline, and sat back on her heels with her secret counted and held.

Five.

* * *

The trouble with a secret, Nell discovered, is that it wants company the way a wound wants air.

She carried it for nine days. She carried it through arithmetic and through mending and through supper while Papa talked about oil deliveries in the careful flat voice he used when he was talking around something. She carried it to the point where it grew so heavy she thought it might pull her through the floorboards, down through the rock of the island itself, out into the dark water where all the drowned weights went.

And then Tam Wrenn asked her what she was smiling about.

They were sitting on the sea wall behind the schoolhouse, sharing a heel of bread, watching the gray water work at the causeway stones. Tam was her best friend in the way island friendships are best friends — total, unexamined, and older than either of them could account for, since their mothers had shared a washing line before either was born. Tam had a chipped front tooth and a habit of listening with his whole body turned toward you, which made lying to him feel like lying to a mirror.

"Nothing," Nell said, which was the first lie, and it came out wrong, too quick, the way first lies do.

"You've been smiling at your slate for an hour like it told you a joke."

"It's nothing." Second lie. Worse.

Tam waited. He was good at waiting. His brother Dario was the same way, and later — much later, when everything had already gone wrong — Nell would think about how she should have known, how the whole Wrenn family had that quality of patience that other people mistook for kindness, patience that was really just a kind of hunting.

"I know something," she said.

The words left her like birds off a wire, all at once, impossible to call back.

"What kind of something?"

"The grown-up kind."

Tam's eyes went wide and bright. "About the magistrate?"

"How do you know about the magistrate?"

"Everyone knows about the magistrate." He said it with the breezy confidence of someone who knew far less than everyone. "Dario says Corren's going to be sent away soon. Dario says the mainland will sort him. Dario works at the customs house now, he hears things."

Nell hesitated. And here was the moment, she would understand it afterward, here was the exact hinge on which everything swung — the moment small and dull and ordinary as a dropped pin, the moment she would replay a hundred times on a hundred sleepless nights, searching for the version of herself that kept her mouth shut and finding instead, always, the girl who opened it.

"Dario works for *Corren*," she said slowly.

"He works for the *island*. It's the same office." Tam shrugged. "He says the office doesn't change when the magistrate does. He's just a clerk like the last clerk."

Like the last clerk. Something cold walked over Nell's shoulder at that — the memory of the man beneath the orchard tree, the dying stranger her father had carried in from the dunes, the name carved in granite. A clerk. Just a clerk. And look what had become of him.

But Tam was watching her, and Bracken had laid his chin on her knee, and the sea was making its endless argument with the stones, and the secret was so heavy, and Tam's face was so open.

"There's a paper," she heard herself say. "A true paper. It proves the debts aren't real debts. They were made up. All of them — the Oseis', the Tulls', maybe yours." She hadn't checked for Wrenn. She realized it only now, saying it aloud, and the omission stung strangely. "It's hidden somewhere safe and when the time comes it will fix everything."

Tam stared at her. Then, slowly, he grinned, the chipped tooth catching the light, and Nell felt the warm flood of having impressed someone, the sweetest and most dangerous drink there is, and she drank it down.

"That's the biggest secret on the whole island," Tam breathed. "Bigger than the Fenn boy running away. Bigger than anything."

"Bigger than anything," Nell agreed, glowing.

"You won't tell me where?"

"Papa said—" She stopped. Papa had said to leave it be, and she had already broken that twice over. "No. I can't. Not even you."

"Not even me," Tam agreed easily, and stood, and dusted off his trousers, and offered her the last of the bread, which she took.

That was the thing she would remember longest, afterward. That he took it so easily. That he didn't beg or sulk or press, the way she would have pressed, the way any honest friend hungry for a secret would have. He simply folded it away and said goodbye and walked home along the cliff path with his hands in his pockets, whistling.

Children keep secrets badly. But children give them away beautifully — without even knowing they've done it.

* * *

The Wrenns' house stood at the crook of the harbor road, and Dario Wrenn came home to it at dusk, smelling of lamp oil and official paper.

He was twenty-two and careful, was Dario, the eldest of four, a young man who had learned early that the island's economy ran on two currencies — fish and favor — and that he had no boat and therefore must trade in the second. The customs house post was not a good job. It was a job that kept his mother's deed in her own name, mostly, for now, and kept bread on the table in a winter when bread had begun to require explanations, and Dario told himself every evening as he walked home past the shuttered fish sheds that he was not Corren's man, he was simply a man employed where Corren was, the way a nail is not the hammer.

That evening Tam was bursting. Dario saw it before he got his boots off — the youngest of the middle children vibrating at the table like a struck tuning fork, waiting for the room to empty. Dario ate slowly. He kissed his mother's cheek. He carried the washwater out. And only then, in the cold lean-to where the wood was stacked, did he turn to his brother and say, "Well?"

"I know where the paper is," Tam said.

Dario's hands went still on the axe handle. "What paper?"

"The paper. The true ledger. Nell Zhou knows where her father hid it — the clerk's paper, the one that proves everything. She told me at the sea wall. She wouldn't say where, but she knows, and her father knows, and—"

"Stop." Dario set the axe down very carefully, the way he did everything, and looked at his brother for a long moment. Through the wall he could hear their mother singing to the little ones, a low looping tune about a herring and a moon. "Tam. Listen to me. Did you tell anyone else?"

"No. Only you."

"And she told you where it is?"

"She didn't have to." Tam's chest swelled. "I figured it out after. She goes to the orchard tree every day. Every day, Dario. She sits by the grave and talks to it like it can hear. Where else would you hide a paper than where nobody would dig — under a *grave*? I bet you anything it's in the marker stone. It moved this winter, she said so herself, the frost heaved it. She said the frost *heaved it*, Dario, why would she say that unless—"

"Stop," Dario said again, softer this time, and the softness frightened Tam into silence, because Dario was never soft. He crouched down to his brother's height, and his face in the lean-to's shadow was gray and old. "Do you know what happens to families on this island right now, Tam, if the magistrate thinks they know things? Do you know how the Brens lost their boat? Not to a storm. To a stamp. Violet ink and a stamp, and Mr. Bren stood in his own kitchen while men carried his livelihood out the door, and there was nothing — *nothing* — he could do, because the paper said what the paper said."

"But this paper says the opposite—"

"This paper," Dario said, "if it exists, is the most dangerous thing on this island. More dangerous than the storm coming. And now my eleven-year-old brother knows where it is, and I am a man who works for the magistrate, and if anyone ever learns that I knew — if anyone ever learns that this family knew — then we are not safe, Tam. We are not neutral. There is no neutral anymore."

Tam's eyes had gone glassy. "I only wanted—"

"I know what you wanted." Dario closed his eyes. He saw it all with terrible clarity, the way he saw ledgers: entries and consequences, debits flowing downhill. If he said nothing, and the thing surfaced some other way, and it emerged that the Wrenns had known — his mother's deed, the little ones, everything burned for a child's bragging. And if he spoke to Corren — his stomach turned over, actually turned, a slow sick roll — but if he spoke first, came forward, offered the information as loyalty rather than surrendering it as evidence, then perhaps. Perhaps his family stayed safe whichever way the season broke. Corren rewarded those who came to him early. Everyone in the customs house knew that. Everyone pretended not to.

He thought: *I am not Corren's man.*

He thought: *the nail is not the hammer.*

He stood, and put his hand briefly on his brother's head, and said, "Don't speak of this again. Not to anyone. Go inside."

That night Dario did not sleep. At some black hour past the turning of the tide he rose and dressed and stood at the window looking out at the lighthouse, its beam swinging steady across the water, patient as conscience, marking every quarter hour of the dark. He watched it a long time. Then he turned from the window, and the light swept the room and left it, and left him in shadow, and in the morning the harbor fog lay thick on the water, and Dario Wrenn put on his good coat, and walked up the hill toward the magistrate's white house with his collar up and his heart knocking, rehearsing under his breath the words *I've come to offer something*, and behind him, unaware, the island went about its morning — nets mended, kettles filled, and at the foot of the orchard tree a girl knelt in the frost-bright grass talking softly to a granite slab that held, beneath its tilted weight, the only true thing left.

By noon, the magistrate knew.

And in the lighthouse, Vesper Zhou trimmed his wicks and wound his clockwork and measured out his portable life, knowing none of it — not yet, not yet — while down the coast, past the closing bridges and the rising barometer, the first of the great storms gathered itself over the open water like a fist.
