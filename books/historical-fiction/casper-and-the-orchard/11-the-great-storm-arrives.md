## 11. The Great Storm Arrives

The barometer had been falling for two days, the needle sliding down its dial like a man descending stairs he already knew were rotten, and by the morning of the third day Vesper Zhou stopped pretending it was anything other than what it was. He had kept lights through storms before — through the great blow of '21 that had torn the roof off the coal store and dropped half a fishing skiff into the yard as if the sea were returning borrowed goods — but this one arrived with a weight in it, a slow gray pressure that sat on the island like a hand laid flat over a child's mouth.

He measured his preparations the way his wife had taught him, without ever meaning to teach him: in portable things. During her illness, their whole life had contracted to what could be carried from room to room — the medicine bottle, the thin blanket, the photograph of Nell at two years old standing in the orchard with her arms around nothing, hugging air because she'd been told to smile at the camera. When Marguerite died, Vesper had found he could not stop the habit. He inventoried. He counted. He knew exactly how many steps it took to move everything he loved out of harm's way, and he knew, too, the colder arithmetic underneath that number: that almost nothing he loved could be moved at all.

So he did what could be done. He lashed the oil store's shutters with new rope, double-knotted, the knots seated so hard they sang when you plucked them. He carried the spare lamp glass up into the tower itself, wrapped in flannel, because glass was the one thing the sea could not forgive him for losing. He filled every vessel in the house with water — not for drinking, though that too, but because during the '21 storm the cistern pipe had split and he had spent nine hours bailing by hand with a light burning above him that would not wait. He checked the clockwork, wound it to its full tension until the mainspring resisted his fingers like a living thing objecting to confinement, and then he went down to the kitchen where Nell was feeding the dog.

The dog had come with the autumn, appearing out of the fog near the orchard tree the islanders had begun calling Casper, and it had simply declined to leave. It was a rough-coated thing, brindled, one ear folded permanently wrong, and it had appointed itself Nell's shadow with the solemnity of an animal who understood appointment. Now it lay under the table with its chin on her foot, watching the bread disappear with the patience of a monk.

"Storm coming," Nell said. She said it the way children state weather, as fact rather than fear, but her eyes came up to his face and stayed there, reading him the way she read everything now, hungrier for information than she had been before Casper died in their guest room and was buried before she fully understood what dying meant.

"There is," Vesper said.

"The fishermen pulled all the boats up past the second dune. Widow Osei says it will be the worst in ten years. She said the last one like it, men died."

"Widow Osei is usually right about large things."

Nell turned the loaf knife over in her hands, not cutting, just turning it. "Will you be in the light all night?"

"All night. Probably two nights."

"I'll keep the fire going."

"You'll keep yourself inside with the door barred," Vesper said, "and the dog with you, and if anyone knocks who isn't me or Mrs. Osei, you don't open it. You don't even speak through it."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he saw her assemble the sentence she wanted and decide against it. She had been deciding against sentences since the burial. That frightened him more than the barometer.

* * *

The storm made landfall an hour after dusk, and it did not arrive so much as take possession.

Vesper stood in the lantern room and watched it come across the water as a single unbroken wall of darker dark, and then the rain hit the glass like gravel thrown by an enormous deliberate hand, and the wind followed it with a sound he had no honest name for — not howling, howling was too musical; this was the sound of a thing vast and indifferent changing its mind about whether the island should exist. The tower swayed. It always swayed; the masons who built it had intended the give, and Vesper had lived inside that sway for eleven years until it felt less like motion than like breathing. Tonight it breathed harder. Tonight the whole structure groaned on the upswings like an old man rising from a low chair.

He set the lamp. He trimmed the wick to the precise height the flame wanted — too tall and it smoked the glass, too short and the light fell short of the shoal — and he set the clockwork running, and the great lens began its rotation, gathering his single flame and hurling it outward in spokes of white that wheeled across the churning sea like the spokes of a wheel turning beneath a falling cart.

Out there, he knew, were ships. The season's last merchantmen running for harbor before the bridge closed; the herring boats that had put out despite the warnings because debts do not care about weather, and Magistrate Corren had been buying those debts cheap all autumn. The thought of Corren arrived in his mind the way a splinter arrives — small, sideways, buried before he noticed it going in — and he pushed it back out. There was no room tonight for Corren. There was only the light. The light was the oldest contract he had: keep me, and the sea keeps them. Break me, and whatever happens after belongs to you forever.

At some black hour — the clock in the service room said two, but clocks lie in storms, or perhaps time does — he heard the boat.

He heard it before he understood it, a knocking far below, arrhythmic, insistent, and his first insane instinct was that it was the shutters, except the shutters were lashed and the knocking came from the door, the front door, and no shutter knocked four times and paused and knocked four times again. Human hands. Hands knocking in a pattern, which meant hands that expected an answer.

He went down the spiral stair with the storm screaming through the vents above him, taking the iron rails two-handed because the tower was swaying wide enough now that the descent was a matter of timing, of stepping with the building and not against it. In the kitchen Nell was already awake, pressed against the wall with the dog rigid beside her, both of them staring at the door as if it might breathe.

"Papa," she whispered.

"I know." He took the whale-oil lantern from its hook and lit it, and his hands were steadier than he expected them to be. "Behind the stove. You know where."

She knew where. They had rehearsed it in October without naming why, the way families rehearse disasters — she folded herself into the gap behind the iron range with the dog, and the dark took her, and Vesper stood a moment listening to her breathing go quiet, and then he opened the door.

Four men pushed in with the wind at their backs, and the wind entered with them, taking the kitchen apart in the instant of admission — the fire gutted sideways, papers lifting off the table, the dog erupting into a frenzy of barking behind the stove until Nell's voice, very low, cut it silent. The men wore oilskins with the magistrate's seal stenciled at the shoulder, and the leader was a broad-faced man Vesper knew by sight from the bridge checkpoint, a sergeant named Hallow whose talent was for looking bored while doing damage.

"Keeper Zhou." Hallow did not remove his hood. Water ran off him onto the floorboards Vesper had planed himself. "Order of the magistrate. Premises to be searched."

"Searched." Vesper kept his voice level and slightly stupid, the voice of a tired workman. "In this?"

"In this."

"For what? No one's crossed the bridge in three days. Nothing washes ashore in this but driftwood and dead gulls."

Hallow looked at him with the flat patience of a man who has heard every kind of refusal and found them all equally tedious. "The clerk," he said. "Casper Lindqvist. He left the customs house nine days ago. He hasn't been accounted for."

"He's dead," Vesper said. "You know that. Your magistrate's own physician signed the paper. He's buried under my orchard tree, and I dug the grave myself, and you're welcome to look at it."

"Oh, we'll look at it." Hallow's gaze traveled past Vesper, inventorying the kitchen with the unhurried insolence of an auctioneer. "We'll look at everything. Storm's your problem, keeper. Light's your problem. This is ours. We can be quiet about it or we can be loud. Loud tends to frighten children."

Somewhere behind the stove, Vesper heard Nell breathing, and prayed to no one in particular that the storm would cover it, that the wind would lean on the house hard enough to make its own noises, its own excuses.

* * *

They searched the way occupying armies search — thoroughly and disrespectfully, in that order. Two of them went through the ground floor, drawers turned out onto floors, mattress slashed along its seam with a knife not to find anything but to announce that knives could be used. The fourth climbed to the sleeping loft, and Vesper stood in the kitchen with Hallow and listened to his daughter's few possessions being handled: her books thumped down, her tin of colored pencils rattled across the boards, the lid of her keepsake box — Marguerite's music box, actually, given to her last spring — opening and closing with its small broken tinkling.

He kept his eyes on the wall. He kept the ledger out of his mind entirely, because minds are readable, he believed, in the way that houses are — not through doors but through windows, and a man who thinks frantically about a hidden thing points at it like a signpost. So he thought about the light instead. Deliberately, mechanically, he thought about the light: the wick height, the oil feed, the clockwork's remaining tension, the way the lens balanced on its bath of mercury and how in a truly violent blow the mercury could slosh enough to tilt the whole apparatus a quarter-degree, which at three miles was the difference between clearing the shoal and finding it.

"The light's burning?" Hallow asked.

"It's always burning."

"Even now."

"Especially now. That's rather the point of it."

Hallow nodded slowly, and Vesper understood that the question had not been a question. It had been a reminder. *We know where you are, keeper. We know you must stay at your post. A man who must stay somewhere is a man who cannot run.* The search was not only a search. It was also a demonstration of geometry: they owned the door, and he owned the tower, and neither of those facts was negotiable tonight.

The men from the loft came down shaking their heads. The men from the cellar — he hadn't seen them go, but their boots told him where they'd been — came up with wet knees and empty hands, and something in Hallow's jaw tightened with genuine irritation, the first honest emotion he'd shown. He had come expecting to find a dead clerk's papers. He had found a poor man's poor house, wind-battered and clean and hiding nothing, and it offended him the way an empty trap offends a trapper.

"The grave," Hallow said.

They went out into it together, all five of them plus Vesper, into a violence of rain that made the lanterns useless blobs of yellow haze. The orchard tree thrashed overhead, its bare branches whipping like a woman tearing her hair, and the grave marker — a simple fieldstone Vesper had dressed himself, because Casper had no family to send marble — stood at its foot with the earth before it lying dark and undisturbed.

Hallow looked at the stone for a long moment. Rain sheeted off his hood. Then he gestured, and one of his men drove a spade into the ground.

"They buried him deep?" Hallow shouted over the wind.

"Deep enough," Vesper said. "Ground's clay below the loam. You'll want the spade sharpened."

He watched them dig. That was the discipline of it, the whole discipline: he stood in the freezing rain with his hands at his sides and watched strangers exhume the man who had died in his guest bed with his hand gripping Vesper's wrist, and he let his face do nothing at all. Grief, he had learned at Marguerite's bedside, is survivable if you refuse it an audience. The spade rang on something — the shroud boards, crude ones he'd nailed himself from packing crates — and the men exchanged a look and levered them up, and the rain did the rest, filling the hole faster than they could clear it, the island itself conspiring in the dead man's privacy. They saw what there was to see, which was a shrouded shape returned to the condition of soil, and no papers, no oilskin packet, no ledger. Hallow stared into the pit a long time, water pouring from his chin.

"Fill it in," he said finally.

"You'll fill it in," Vesper said. "You dug it."

For one naked second, in the swing of the lantern light, Hallow considered him — considered the old man in the storm with his dead friend's grave open between them — and whatever he found there made him laugh, short and ugly, and turn away. His men refilled the grave badly, tamping the mud with the flat of the spade, leaving it looking like a scabbed wound, and then they tramped back toward the bridge checkpoint with their lanterns guttering, and the storm closed behind them like water behind a swimmer.

* * *

Vesper went back up.

That was all. There was no ceremony in it, no moment of trembling at the base of the stairs; there was a light that required him, and so he climbed. But partway up the spiral, with the wind hammering the masonry and the whole tower leaning into each gust like a ship, he stopped and put his forehead against the cold stone and allowed himself, for exactly ten heartbeats, to feel everything he had refused to feel in the yard: the fury, the fear, the obscene violation of strangers' spades in Casper's resting place, and underneath it all, colder than any of it, the arithmetic. They had come for the ledger. They would come again. And they had gone to the grave first — which meant someone had told them the grave mattered first.

Only four people alive knew where the ledger was hidden. Himself. Widow Osei. And Nell, who had watched him seal it into the hollowed core of the replacement marker stone the week after the burial, and who had been told, in the plain words Vesper used for important things, *this is a secret that can hurt people.*

He climbed the rest of the way with that thought riding his shoulders like ballast.

In the lantern room the world was all motion and noise, the lens wheeling, the flame bending in the draft despite every baffle, the sea below showing itself only in flashes — great hills of black water rearing up and collapsing with a concussion he felt through the floor. He checked the mercury bath. He checked the wick. He wound the clockwork, which had burned down to half tension, and as the mainspring tightened under his hands he looked out through the streaming glass toward the mainland shore, invisible now, and toward the customs house beyond it, invisible too, and he thought of what else might be written in that building besides frauds and seizures — manifests, debt rolls, every record Corren had spent three seasons manufacturing — and he thought of the order Hallow carried in his breast pocket, and the season ending, and the word that had been moving through the fishing families all week in whispers: *burn.* When the records were no longer needed, they would not be filed. Records that could hang a magistrate do not get archived.

A light blinked out there in the black — not a star, stars did not show in this — a single desperate point rising and falling with the swell, and Vesper's chest tightened around itself. He threw the shutter levers, widening the arc, and fed the wick a hair taller, and the beam reached further, and the little light rose once more on the hill of water and held.

*Keep,* he told it. *Keep.*

And below, in the drowned dark of the island, Nell lay awake behind the stove with the dog's heartbeat against her ribs, thinking about a hollow stone, and a secret that could hurt people, and a schoolmate named Pila who had begged her, three days ago in the schoolyard, to tell her just one true thing about the night the clerk died.
