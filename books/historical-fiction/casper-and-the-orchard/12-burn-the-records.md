## 12. Burn the Records

The journal was bound in grey sailcloth, the kind the fishing families used for their storm sails, and it had been hidden where only a man who knew the Widow Osei's habits would think to look — sewn into the ticking of her spare mattress, beneath the window that faced the orchard tree. She found it on a morning of hard rain in the last week of the storm season, when her hands were aching from wringing out sheets and she had stripped the bed to air it against the damp. Her fingers found the seam's irregularity before her eyes did. Sailcloth is honest cloth; it does not forgive lumps.

She read it by the one window, standing, because sitting down would have meant admitting how long she meant to stay there. The hand inside was small and careful, a clerk's hand, the letters upright as fence posts, though toward the end it wandered and the ink thinned where the pen had been pressed too long in one place, waiting for words the writer could not yet afford.

This is what Casper left behind.

* * *

**The ninth day after I took the posting.**

I have been a keeper of other men's lies for eleven years and thought myself immune to them, the way a ferryman is supposed to be immune to drowning. Tonight I copied forty-one seizure notices and my hands are steady and I hate them for it.

The work is simple, which is what makes it terrible. A fisherman borrows against his boat — or is said to have borrowed; the signature page comes to me already signed, the amounts already entered, the interest already compounded at a rate no mainland lender would dare. My task is to make it beautiful. Fair copy. Even margins. The red wax seal applied while still soft so it bleeds slightly at the edges like a wound under a bandage. Corren says the people trust documents that look dignified. He says this without irony, eating his supper.

Forty-one tonight. That is most of the eastern fleet. When the bridge closes they will not be able to carry their grievances anywhere, and by the time it opens, the boats will be his.

I write this in the loft above the customs house because I cannot sleep in the room where I worked. I have begun sleeping badly the way other men begin coughing — a symptom, a herald.

**The twenty-third day.**

Today a woman came to contest an entry. Zhou — no relation to the keeper, or perhaps every relation, everyone here is braided together the way roots are. She had brought her receipt. An actual receipt, kept in oilcloth, for a loan repaid in full two winters ago. She laid it on my desk with both hands, the way you set down something breakable, and asked me — politely, oh so politely, as if politeness were armor — which of us was mistaken.

I looked at the master ledger. The entry was there: borrowed, unpaid, defaulted. In fair copy. In my fair copy, made from figures handed to me by a man named Dree who smells of the magistrate's kitchen.

I told her there must have been a clerical error and that I would investigate. She thanked me. That was the worst of it. She thanked me, and I sat in the empty office afterward with her receipt in my hand and understood that I had a choice, and that I had already failed to make it, and that the failure had a shape and a weight exactly like a ledger.

I did not return the receipt to her. It is beneath the loose brick under the stove. If anyone asks, I am keeping evidence. If no one asks, then I am keeping it because I cannot bear to hand it back and watch it disappear into the machine.

**The thirty-first day.**

Corren came to the customs house today and stood over my shoulder while I worked, which he has never done. He smelled of bay rum and cold. He said, "You're thorough, Casper. That's rare." Then, after a silence long enough to fall into: "Thorough men last. Careful ones don't."

I do not know which he counted me as. I have been turning the sentence over all evening like a stone with something underneath it.

He mentioned the storms. He said this would be a heavy season, that the records room would want protecting from the damp, that perhaps everything should be consolidated before the bridge closes. Consolidated. He said the word the way a man says *pruned*, meaning cut away.

I understand now why he hired a clerk from the mainland instead of training one of the islanders. An islander would have cousins. I have only a conscience, and he believes he has purchased it.

Perhaps he is right. Perhaps I am writing this journal so that someday I can point to a page and say: see, here, I noticed, I objected, I felt. Feeling is not action. I know the difference professionally; my whole trade is making one thing stand in for another.

But tonight I made the second copy.

It took four hours. I waited until Dree left for his supper and the guard at the door went to warm his hands, and I worked with the true ledger open on my knees under the desk, transcribing by feel more than sight, my pen moving in the dark like a blind man's finger across a face he loves. Every debt that exists only on paper. Every boat signed over twice. The dates altered on nine entries — I measured them against the harbor register, which no one thinks to falsify because no one reads it, and the harbor register contradicts the seizure orders on nine boats, and contradiction is proof, and proof is the only weapon this island will ever be given.

The copy is done. Forty pages, folded flat, wrapped in waxed canvas. I have not yet decided where it goes, and I am aware that deciding is itself a kind of dying, because once it has a hiding place it also has a discoverer, and I would rather it had a carrier. But there is no carrier. There is only me, and I am a clerk, and clerks are not permitted to leave the island once the season turns.

I will decide tomorrow. Tonight I will sleep, or lie near sleep, with the package under my shirt against my chest like a second heart.

**The fortieth day.**

I am sick. Or I am poisoned; I can't tell the difference and no longer much care. Fever since Tuesday. Dree brought me medicine and watched me drink half and take the cup back, and his eyes were very kind, and kindness from Dree frightens me more than anything Corren has said aloud.

I have hidden the copy. Not well enough to satisfy me, well enough to satisfy urgency. But I have realized — fever clarifies, it burns off everything but structure — that a hidden document is a buried body: it proves nothing until someone digs. What I need is a second copy in a place so obvious it becomes invisible, a place guarded precisely because it is guarded, where destruction would require an act so public the whole island would witness it.

The answer has been sitting above my head for forty days. The customs house archive. The sealed strong-room where the original deeds repose under official lock, protected by the very law Corren is bending. He cannot burn the strong-room without burning the deeds themselves — the real ones, the ones the mainland registry mirrors. Unless he means to destroy those too. Unless the fire is the whole point.

I went back through the consolidation order tonight, the one Corren drafted himself and had me fair-copy in August, and I read it properly for the first time, the way you finally hear a sentence that has been spoken over you all winter. *All island records to be moved to secure mainland storage at season's end.* At season's end. Before the bridge opens. Before any inspector might come asking. Everything — the forged ledgers, the true deeds, my copies, the evidence of every crime — gathered into one building, one room, one night's work with a lamp and a can of oil.

He is going to burn it all. The fraud and the proof of the fraud, together, in the same fire, so that ash cannot testify to which was which.

And the island will stand in spring with no boats, no deeds, no papers, and no way to prove they ever owned anything at all except the salt under their nails.

**The forty-first day.**

Fever again. I am writing this propped against the wall because lying flat makes the coughing worse, and because I have begun to suspect that if I lie down I will not negotiate my way back up.

I tried to reach the bridge checkpoint today. I got as far as the dunes. The guard knew my face and waved, friendly, and I understood with perfect clarity that friendliness was the leash: I am permitted to move within the island because I am believed to be owned by it. A clerk who flees is a clerk who talks. They would catch me at the first plank of the bridge, and whatever I carried would be taken, and I would be taken, and nothing would change except the speed of the ending.

So: the keeper.

Zhou. Vesper Zhou. I have watched him from the customs house window all season, lighting the tower at dusk, winding his clockwork at dawn, a man made entirely of routine — and routine, I realize, is just grief wearing its work clothes. They say his wife died two springs ago and he has kept the light ever since as though she might navigate home by it. A man like that knows about carrying things. A man like that carries obligations past all reason, across seasons, up stairs, through weather. He is the opposite of a clerk. Where I record what happened, he keeps something alive.

If I bring him the truth, he will carry it. I believe this the way I have never believed an entry in any ledger.

But I am afraid. Write it plainly, since this book is the only place I have ever been plain: I am afraid to walk out to that lighthouse, and I am more afraid of dying in this loft with the knowledge still folded in waxed canvas, useless as a deed for a burned boat. Between those fears I choose the longer road, the one that goes through the fog and the dunes and whatever is wrong with my blood.

Tomorrow night, when the light goes up. A lit lamp is an invitation; that must be what widows and keepers understand and magistrates never learn.

To whoever finds this — and I have sewn it where only one pair of hands on this island is old enough and patient enough and angry enough to go looking — know these things:

There is a second copy of the true ledger, forty pages, in the customs house. As for where: I will not write the place itself, in case this book is found before it is used. The place is a question whose answer is *the harbor register*. Anyone who has read this far can solve it; I have left the arithmetic in these pages deliberately, the way I was taught to leave instructions in a fair copy — legible only to the diligent.

Know also that Corren intends to burn the customs house at season's end, before the bridge opens. Every record, true and false alike. After the fire there will be nothing to prove and no one alive to prove it. Whatever is to be done must be done before the last great storm clears, while the checkpoints are still watching each other instead of the water.

And know this last thing, which is not evidence and cannot be entered into any record, but which I ask you to weigh anyway: the people on those forty-one notices are innocent. Not of debt — of owing it. Their receipts exist, some of them, folded in oilcloth, kept the way poor people keep paper, which is to say perfectly. The island does not owe Corren. Corren owes the island, and the debt has been compounding all season, and I was its clerk.

I was its clerk. Let that line stand in fair copy.

The light is up now. I can see it from the loft window, swinging its slow arm across the fog, patient as anything that has decided to outlast you.

Time to go.

* * *

The Widow Osei closed the book and did not open it again that day. She sat with both hands flat upon the cover, feeling the grain of the sailcloth, and outside the rain kept on, and beyond the rain, somewhere out in the grey, the lighthouse turned its arm — though whether Vesper Zhou still slept beneath it, or what the magistrate's men had carried out of his house in the night, she could not yet know.

What she knew was arithmetic, and the arithmetic was this: a copy hidden in the customs house, a fire planned for season's end, and between the two of them a narrow interval in which a burned-handed keeper, if he still breathed, would have to become a thief of his own country's records.

She put the journal inside her bodice, against her heart, the way Casper had carried his own copy, and went out into the rain to count who on this island could still be trusted — and how far, and for how long, and at what price.

The last great storm was three days out. She could smell the iron in it coming.
