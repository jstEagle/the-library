## 13. The Second Checkpoint

The coat smelled of lamp oil and another man's sweat.

Vesper had taken it from the hook behind Widow Osei's kitchen door without asking whose it was, and she had answered the question anyway, stirring her pot as if she were speaking to the fire. "It belonged to Tam Brellan. He ran oil to the customs house for eleven years before his lungs gave out. Nobody on this island will look twice at that coat." She had paused then, wooden spoon dripping, and looked at him with those eyes that seemed to weigh a man the way a fishmonger weighs a catch — by the eye first, and never kindly. "They'll look at your face, though. So keep it down. Keep your collar up. And Vesper —" she had said his name the way she said everything, flat and final, like a stone set on a grave — "if they stop you, be dull. Dull men live long lives."

He was counting on it.

The ledger rode against his chest now, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the lining of the coat itself, because Osei's forger — a bent-backed woman named Maren who had once copied shipping manifests for the mainland trade — had insisted that pockets could be turned out but seams were sacred. "A man searching you will take your knife, your coin, your tobacco," Maren had said, her needle flashing in the lamplight of Osei's back room. "He will not unpick a stranger's coat at midnight in the rain. Men are lazy about other men's clothing. It is one of God's small mercies."

So the ledger lived in the seam now, a stiff ridge against his sternum like a second breastbone, and every time Vesper breathed deeply he felt it press back, as if the dead clerk were still trying to say something through all that paper and thread.

* * *

The storm had passed two days before, but the sea had not finished with the island. The causeway between the fishing quarter and the customs house point — a spine of fitted stone that the islanders had laid, so the story went, three hundred years ago, when the mainland still bothered to send masons instead of magistrates — lay under four feet of green water. At low tide the water dropped to knee height, churning, and for perhaps ninety minutes the crossing was possible to a man willing to gamble his footing against the current.

Osei had wanted him to wait. Everyone had wanted him to wait.

"Corren burns the records at season's end," Vesper had told her, standing in her kitchen while rain hammered the shutters like a fist demanding entry. "Casper's journal says so. That gives us weeks at most, and every week Corren's men carry more pages out of that building to the burn pile behind the guardhouse. I've seen the smoke. You've seen the smoke. We are watching our own history go up in pieces, Osei, one barrel at a time."

"And if you drown in the causeway carrying the only true copy we have left?" She had not raised her voice. She never raised her voice. That was what made her frightening. "Then Corren wins not because he was clever but because you were proud, and my granddaughter grows up on an island where men's deeds belong to whatever magistrate holds the bridge."

"I won't drown."

"No," she agreed, and turned back to her pot. "You won't. Because Nell needs you, and because the dead don't get to choose their errands twice." She had ladled fish stew into a bowl and pushed it across the table at him. "Eat. A drowned man is no use to anyone, and a hungry one drowns faster."

Now, in the grey hour before dawn, with the tide at its lowest ebb and the wind come around to the northwest, Vesper stood at the head of the causeway with a yoke of empty oil cans balanced across his shoulders and let the cold walk into him like a tenant taking possession.

The disguise was simple because simplicity was its armor: the oil courier's coat, a canvas hat pulled low, boots two sizes too large stuffed with sacking at the toe. The cans were real — Osei had procured them from the fishing families, who had filled them with enough oil to make them honest weight. If anyone asked, he was running oil down to the customs house lamps, which was work that happened every few days even under Corren's rule, because Corren's men liked their lamps lit even when they let the lighthouse go dark. There was a bitter symmetry in that which Vesper did not have the leisure to appreciate.

He thought of Nell instead, the way he always did before danger, the way a man touches a talisman. She was at Osei's, sleeping in the loft above the kitchen with the widow's granddaughter, and she believed — because Osei had decided she should believe — that her father was making a routine delivery. He had kissed her forehead while she slept and she had murmured something in her dream, half a word, and he had stood there in the dark longer than was safe or sensible, memorizing the sound.

*Come back,* he told himself, in her voice, in the voice she used when she was being reasonable with him, which was more often than he deserved. *Come back or the tree has no one to talk to.*

He stepped off the causeway's head onto the first submerged stones.

* * *

The water took him to mid-thigh within ten paces and the cold was total, absolute, a white noise that erased thought. He had crossed this causeway a hundred times in dry weather, walking it with Nell on summer evenings when the stones showed their old faces and the gulls wheeled overhead screaming their opinions. He knew the causeway the way he knew the stairs of his own lighthouse. But knowing a thing dry and knowing it underwater were different knowings, and the sea did not care which one he carried.

The current came at him from his left, pushing toward the open channel between the island and the black teeth of the outer rocks. It was not violent — not yet, not at slack water — but it was patient, and it found every gap between his boots and the stones, every place where his balance was a guess rather than a fact. He learned quickly to walk sideways, presenting his body to the push, planting each foot with a fisherman's deliberation he had inherited from a father-in-law he had never much liked and now, absurdly, thanked.

Twenty paces. Thirty. The customs house point rose ahead of him out of the dark, a low hump of rock and slate roof, its windows shuttered, one guard lamp burning yellow at the landward door. Forty paces. The oil cans swayed on their yoke with each step, knocking together softly, and he made himself stop flinching at the sound. A real courier would not flinch. A real courier would be thinking about his supper and his feet and nothing else in the world.

Be dull, Osei had said. He tried. He thought about oil. He thought about wicks. He thought about the price of whale tallow versus rapeseed, a subject of such magnificent tedium that it should have been able to carry him across the Atlantic, let alone forty yards of flooded stone.

At fifty paces the bottom changed.

He felt it before he understood it — the stone beneath his right boot shifting, tilting, a slab the size of a coffin lid rocking on some buried fulcrum the storm had loosened. His foot slid into the gap. The water, which had been holding him at the waist, suddenly had no argument with taking him whole.

The world inverted. Cold closed over his head like a door.

The yoke tore free of his shoulders — or he let it go; afterward he could never decide — and the oil cans went spinning away into the dark, two pale shapes tumbling in the green murk like moons knocked from their orbits. The current had him now, truly had him, dragging him along the causeway's flank the way a dog drags a stick, and his boots, his enormous stupid sacking-stuffed boots, filled and dragged and became anchors tied to his own legs.

He did not panic. That surprised him later, that he could remember being surprised at all in that moment, some small clerk of the mind still keeping books while the rest of him fought for air. He curled, got his knees under him, found the stone with his hands — barnacled, savage, it opened his palms and he barely registered the pain — and pushed upward.

His head broke the surface six feet downstream of where he'd gone under. He gasped, swallowed half the channel, coughed, went under again, came up again. The current was walking him toward the outer edge of the causeway, past which the bottom fell away into the channel proper, past which there was no standing, no swimming against the ebb, only the long grey ride out to the rocks and the colder ride after that.

*Not tonight,* he thought, with a clarity that felt borrowed from someone braver. *I have an appointment with a fire.*

He got a hand onto the causeway's seaward lip. The stone there was old work, dressed and fitted, and the joint between blocks gave his fingers just enough of a hold. He locked his arm straight and hung there, cheek against the wet granite, breathing in ragged gulps, while the current peeled at him like a hand trying to take a letter from a fist.

One block. Then the next. He moved the way he wound the lighthouse clockwork — not fast, never fast, but without pause, each motion complete before the next began, because speed was how men died on this coast and patience was how they grew old. His arms burned. His opened hands left pink ghosts on the stone. Twice a swell lifted him and slammed him gently, almost apologetically, against the causeway's side, and twice he held.

The last twenty feet the water shallowed, and he crawled rather than walked, on hands and knees through the churn, until the stone rose under him and the sea gave him up with what felt, in his ruined state, like reluctance.

He lay on the customs house point among the wet boulders, streaming, shaking, and laughed — a single bark of sound that he strangled immediately, pressing his burned-then-frozen palm over his own mouth. Above him, thirty yards up the slope, the guard lamp burned on, indifferent. Somewhere behind the shuttered windows, a man coughed in his sleep.

Vesper had lost the oil cans. He had lost his hat. His palms were shredded, his left shoulder sang with a deep bruise where the yoke had torn away, and the ledger — he pressed his hand to his sternum, to the stiff ridge beneath the soaked wool — the ledger was still there, sewn into the seam, dry inside its oilcloth, patient as a heart.

* * *

There was no way to approach the customs house door as an oil courier now. The cans were gone, and a courier without oil was a riddle, and riddles got men searched. So Vesper crouched in the rocks below the point and made himself think like the building instead of like the man who needed to get inside it.

He had delivered oil here for years, back when the customs house had been merely the customs house, before Corren and his requisitions and his men with their ledgers of invented debts. He knew its bones. The main door faced the landward road, watched by the guard lamp. The loading dock faced the harbor side, chained in winter. And on the harbor side, below the dock, there was the old coal chute — a slot of rotted timber at the foundation line, built for the days when the building burned coal instead of oil, disused for a decade at least, remembered by no one who mattered because it had never mattered to anyone.

Casper had hidden the second ledger somewhere a clerk could reach alone, at night, without explanation. Not the record rooms — those were guarded, inventoried, watched. Casper's journal had been maddeningly brief on the point, written in his cramped dying hand: *the second copy sleeps where the fire cannot be bothered to look. Where I slept, the first winter, when the quarters froze. Under the floor that remembers the coal.*

Under the floor that remembers the coal. Vesper had turned the phrase over for two days like a stone in his pocket, and now, staring up at the dark bulk of the building, he finally saw it. The old coal cellar. Sealed off when the heating was converted, its hatch probably still there beneath the harbor-side storeroom's floorboards, forgotten by everyone except a freezing young clerk who had once slept beside its residual warmth and never entirely stopped thinking of it as his.

And the coal chute was how a man reached it without opening a single guarded door.

Vesper climbed the rocks to the harbor side, moving between swells, and found the chute exactly where memory said it would be: a horizontal slot, waist-high, its timber hatch swollen with rot and salt. The nails had rusted to lace. He worked his fingers into the gap — his opened palms screamed, and he let them, silently, the way he had learned to let grief scream — and pulled. The wood gave all at once, soft as bread, and he was looking into a throat of darkness that smelled of coal dust and forty years of dead fires.

He went in headfirst, the way a man goes into anything he cannot face going in feetfirst.

Inside, the chute narrowed, then opened into the old cellar, and the darkness there was total in a way the night outside had not been. He knelt in the black and listened. Water dripped somewhere. Timber ticked. Overhead, faint through the floorboards, a guard's boots crossed the storeroom and paused — paused — directly above him, and Vesper held his breath until his vision sparked, until the boots moved on, until his heart resumed its work.

He found the hatch by touch: a square of boards set into the cellar ceiling, newer than the rest, replaced sometime in the last decade. Someone had cut through into the storeroom above. Someone had needed a private way up.

Casper's floor. Casper's winter. Casper's hiding place, chosen by a dying man who had known exactly which rooms in this building would burn and which would be overlooked, because he had drawn the burn orders himself.

Vesper pressed his ruined palms flat against the hatch and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to a dead man that the dead hadn't already paid for. Then he set his shoulder to the boards and lifted.

The storeroom above was dim, lit by the guard lamp's glow leaking under the far door. Barrels in rows. Rope coils. And there, against the wall where the journal had promised, a stack of crates marked in faded chalk — and beneath the third crate, when he lifted it with arms that no longer belonged to him, a loose floorboard, and beneath the board, wrapped in the same careful oilcloth as its twin, a ledger as thick as a psalter.

He took it. He sewed it — no, he had no needle here; he bound it to his body with a length of rope coil cut from the nearest spool, cinched tight across his chest beneath the coat, riding above the first ledger like a second heart. Two hearts. He was a man with two hearts now, and both of them belonged to Casper.

He was lowering himself back toward the coal hatch when the light under the far door brightened.

Voices. More than one. Boots on the corridor flags, and among them a voice he knew — smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man who had sat in Vesper's own kitchen drinking tea and admiring the view while measuring the room for a coffin. Corren. Here, at this hour, in the storm's wet aftermath, at the customs house where the records waited for the season's end and the fire.

"—no, leave the manifests, they're worthless. The seizure rolls and the debt registers go to the guardhouse tonight. All of them. I want nothing left in this building that a match could make interesting."

A pause. Then, lighter, almost amused: "And check the old coal cellar while you're at it. Our late clerk was fond of cellars."

Vesper stood in the dark of the storeroom with two stolen hearts against his chest and nowhere left to go but down.
