## 14. A Traitor's Bargain

The Widow Osei had learned long ago that a house tells you everything, if you let it. Her kitchen told her the weather three days out — the way the mortar between the stones wept salt before a storm, the way her kettle sang flat when the pressure dropped. It had told her about her husband's illness months before the doctor found words for it, in the way he began leaving his boots by the wrong door, small disordered things that meant a man was no longer sure he'd need them again.

And tonight it told her that Tammas Vey had come to betray them.

She knew it from the moment she heard his knock — three raps, then a pause too long, then a fourth, apologetic, as though the knuckles themselves had lost their nerve. Tammas had been coming to her kitchen since he was a boy tall enough to reach her shelf of dried herring. He knocked like family or didn't knock at all. This careful, rehearsed knocking was a stranger's knock.

"Come in before you freeze, then," she called, and did not rise from her chair by the hearth. She kept her hands folded over her apron, over the ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath it, and watched the door.

He came in sideways, the way a man enters when part of him is already measuring the exit. He was twenty-six, broad through the shoulders from hauling nets, with his mother's soft chin and his father's habit of looking at the floor when lying, which was why he looked very hard at her face now — overcorrecting, always overcorrecting, her mother would have said.

"Mistress Osei. I — my sister said I might find you awake."

"Pip says a great many things to a great many people lately." She let that sit a moment, saw the flicker cross his eyes, and knew. *Pip.* Eleven years old, gap-toothed, devoted to Nell Zhou, and incapable of keeping a secret bigger than a sweet. Of course. Of course the child had talked, and the talk had traveled up the hill to the one boy in the family whose wages came from the magistrate's guardhouse.

Tammas hung his coat on the peg and missed it twice. That, too, told her things.

"I'll take some tea," he said, and then, immediately, "no — thank you — I shouldn't be long. I only came to—" He stopped. Started again. "There's talk at the guardhouse."

"There's always talk at the guardhouse."

"Not like this." His hands were working at each other, knuckle over knuckle. "They know about the courier. The oil-man route through the causeway. Corren's put a man on the second checkpoint starting tomorrow, and orders are — whoever comes through flooded causeway after dark is to be taken, not questioned first. Taken." He swallowed. "I thought you should know. Because of your people. Because of what you're all—"

"What I'm all mixed up in," Osei finished gently. "Yes."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wind had begun its low rehearsal for the great storms, dragging its bow across the chimney. She studied this boy whom she had fed through two famines of the heart — his father's drowning, his mother's long gray decline — and felt the old arithmetic begin in her chest, cold and patient as the sea itself.

Because here was the truth she could not say aloud: Tammas Vey had not come to warn her. Not really. A warning given out of loyalty does not knock four times. A warning given out of love arrives sweating and shameless and stays until dawn. What had come to her kitchen tonight was a transaction wearing a warning's clothes. He wanted her to know that he *could* have sold them and hadn't — yet. He wanted her gratitude, which he intended to hold up later like a lantern against whatever accusation came. Or worse: he wanted her to understand that his silence had a price, and that his family's deed — the Vey plot above Harrow Gut, eleven acres, mortgaged twice now to a debt that Corren's clerks had invented out of thin ink — was sitting on the seizure rolls like an egg on a ledge.

He wanted her to buy him.

"Sit down," she said.

"Mistress Osei, I really can't—"

"*Sit,* Tammas."

He sat. Twenty-six years old and he sat like he was nine, hands trapped under his thighs, and something in her ached even as the colder thing in her went on counting.

*Silence him.* The thought arrived without being invited, wearing the voice of practical men. He knows the route. He knows about the ledgers — not their contents perhaps, but their existence, and existence is enough to hang a man on this island now. Tomorrow morning, if his nerve returns or his fear deepens, he walks into the guardhouse and gives up Vesper Zhou with a sentence, and every name Osei has spent three weeks stitching into a web of cellars and spare coats and forged passes goes into Corren's fire along with the paper. Silencing him needn't mean violence. It could mean a word in the right ear — Tammas Vey consorts with smugglers, put him somewhere far — or it could mean the other thing, the thing her grandmother's generation had done once, quietly, to a collaborator on the south shore, the story everyone knew and nobody told. The island was full of dark water. A man could go into it easily enough.

*Forgive him,* said another voice, smaller and harder to hear, the voice that sounded like her husband reading scripture badly, stumbling over the prophets but refusing to skip them. *Forgiveness is also a strategy. The strongest one left.*

The trouble, she thought, watching Tammas's miserable hands, is that both voices were right, and neither was kind.

* * *

She made the tea anyway, because her mother had taught her that no decision worth making should be made without something warm in the room, and set the cup before him. He drank it in gulps like a child taking medicine.

"You want to know what I think?" she said at last. "I think you've been standing at the guardhouse window for three days watching them write your family's name into a book, and every night you've told yourself you'd do something about it, and every morning you haven't. And last night Pip came home talking about a grave marker and a secret, the way children do, and you realized the danger wasn't out there anymore. It was sleeping in the next room." She held up a hand before he could speak. "Don't deny any of it. Denial wastes my patience and your time."

His chin went down. His father's son at last.

"They have my deed, mistress. They have it in a drawer with my mother's name on it. If I'm marked as unreliable, they pull it out and burn it, and then my mother dies landless, and I—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hate it. "You don't know what that is. To be the last one holding a thing and feel it going."

"Don't I." She said it very quietly, and he had the grace to flinch, because everyone on the island knew about the Osei house — how the magistrate's own predecessor had seized it forty years ago, how she had spent nine years and every coin of her widowhood buying back what had never rightly been taken, deed by deed, room by room, until the law itself had grown embarrassed and restored the whole. "I know exactly what it is, Tammas. I bought my house back with my knees. So don't stand in my kitchen and teach me about holding on."

The wind rose. In the rafters, something shifted and settled.

"But here is what you need to understand," she went on, "and you will listen, because this is the only time I will say it. What Corren offers you is not safety. It is the *shape* of safety. You give him me, and Vesper, and the ferryman, and the girl — and your deed stays in that drawer, Tammas. It does not come home. Men like Corren keep the leash; they do not give away the collar. Your mother will die in a rented room on the mainland while you carry his papers, and one day you will be of no more use than the late clerk was, and you will learn what happens to instruments when the music ends."

"That's not—"

"Casper Aldane," she said. "Say his name in your head. A clever young man, better position than yours, closer to the magistrate than you will ever be. He tried to save himself by being useful, and when usefulness turned to conscience, they broke him on the dunes. The difference between Casper and you, Tammas, is that Casper understood too late. I am offering you the chance to understand early."

Tammas stared into his empty cup. When he finally spoke, his voice was barely above the fire. "Then what am I supposed to do? Tell me what a man is supposed to do."

It was, Osei thought, the most honest question anyone had asked her in weeks. She rose, joints announcing themselves the way they did now, and went to the dresser, and took down the small tin box where she kept what passed, these days, for hope.

* * *

What she gave him was not forgiveness, quite. Forgiveness would come later, if it was earned, and she was honest enough with herself to know she was not offering grace so much as making a purchase — the corruption survival demands, her husband would have called it, except he'd have said it sadly. She was buying a traitor's loyalty with the only currency that reliably beat fear: a better fear.

"The inspector," she said. "Mainland Revenue. The one Corren dines with twice a season and lies to beautifully. We have written to him. He has answered. Do you know what that means?"

Tammas shook his head slowly.

"It means that in spring — likely sooner, if the storms close the bridge and force the mainland's hand — there will be a reckoning with the weight of the mainland behind it. And when it comes, the first question the inspector will ask is not *who helped Corren.* It will be *who warned the island, who stood at the checkpoints and saw the lists, who can testify to the order in which the frauds were written.*" She leaned across the table, close enough to smell the fear-salt on him. "You work in that guardhouse, Tammas Vey. You copy those registers with your own hand. There is not a soul on this island better placed to be on the right side of spring — and no soul quicker condemned if he chooses the other one. Corren holds your deed for a season. The inspector would hold your freedom for life, either direction. Choose your master by who keeps his promises longer."

"And if I choose—" he wet his lips "—if I choose yours. What do I do?"

"Nothing dramatic." She smiled, and knew the smile was terrible. "That's the beauty of it. You do nothing. You say nothing. When they ask whether you've heard anything of a courier, you look stupid — you've had practice, don't glare at me — and you forget to mention the causeway, and you misplace one page, just one, of the guardhouse duty roster, the page covering tomorrow night. Can you do that much? Lose a piece of paper?"

"I can lose a piece of paper," he whispered.

"Good. Because tomorrow night a man in an oil-courier's coat goes down the flooded causeway toward the customs house, and everything depends on the guardhouse being confused about when and where. You won't have betrayed Corren for us, do you see? You'll simply have been careless for us. Carelessness is forgivable. Even Corren forgives carelessness — once." She took the cup from his hands and set it aside, and held both his rough hands in hers, the way she had when he was small and his father's boat came back without his father. "And Tammas. Listen to the old woman now. After spring, whatever happens — you come to this kitchen, and we will talk about what you almost did, and I will feed you herring, and we will decide together whether it can ever be spoken of again. But it cannot be spoken of before. Not to Pip, not to your mother, not to God if you value His opinion of timing. Do you understand?"

He nodded. Then, because he was his mother's son too, and honest underneath everything: "Mistress Osei — I nearly ruined all of you. Why are you being kind to me?"

She almost laughed. Kindness. If he only knew what kindness cost, what it weighed in the hand compared to a knife, how much heavier it was to lift.

"Because," she said, releasing his hands, "an island is a small boat, Tammas. Everybody bails, or everybody drowns. There's no third thing, however it looks from the shore." She stood, signaling the audience closed. "Now get home before the watch changes, and remember — lose the page."

* * *

After he was gone she sat a long while by the banked fire, doing the sums she had not allowed herself to do in front of him.

One traitor, half-converted. One child's loose tongue, unfixable now. One route compromised in ways she could only pray were patched by a single missing roster page. Against it: one ledger hidden in a grave marker that half the island's children could draw a map to, one clerk's journal pointing to a second copy in the customs house, one magistrate who had ordered the burning of every record on the island — *tonight*, Vesper had heard him say, or within days — and one keeper with burned hands and a known face carrying both ledgers toward a building that was about to become a bonfire.

It was not a plan anymore. It was a wager dressed as a plan, and she had bet the whole island on a reformed coward's missing page and a dying man's stubbornness with paper.

She unpinned the oilcloth bundle from under her apron and set it on the table — her own copies, her lists of names, the forged pass in its final form, smelling faintly of the herring brine she'd aged it in. Outside, the wind found its full voice at last, and far off toward the harbor she heard — or imagined she heard, and would swear forever after that she hadn't — the first shout of night watchmen, and beneath it, thinner, rising, the sound of something that was not yet flame but soon would be.

Osei closed her hand around the forged pass and began, with steady fingers, to snuff the candles.
