## 15. The Customs House Fire

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and in its place came a fog so thick that Vesper could not see his own boots on the causeway stones. He moved by memory and by the sound of the water — the flooded causeway sang differently where the stones were sound and differently again where they had cracked, and he had learned its song over three nights of walking it with oil barrels balanced on his shoulders like a man carrying his own coffin.

He was not carrying oil tonight. He was carrying nothing but himself, dressed as the courier he had pretended to be for three nights running — Osei's grey coat, stiff with tar at the collar to hide the lighthouse keeper's clean smell, a canvas satchel slung across his chest that held, instead of lamp oil, a folded note from Widow Osei written in a hand so small it might have been stitched. *The second ledger is in the customs house, iron cabinet, third drawer from the bottom, behind the survey maps. Casper's journal does not lie. Burn this after reading.*

He had burned it. The ash was still under his fingernails.

The customs house sat at the island's eastern shoulder, a squat granite building that had once been proud — Corren's predecessor had built it when the herring fleets still came, and its lintel bore a carved fish leaping through a carved wave, though the fish's mouth had been chipped away by some vandal years ago so that it now leapt open-mouthed and silent, screaming without sound. Vesper had passed it a thousand times in his life on the island and never once thought about what went on inside. Now he thought of nothing else.

A lantern burned in the window. That was wrong. The customs house kept no night hours; the clerks worked days, and Corren's men drank their evenings in the Anchor Room above the chandlery. A lantern at this hour meant either a careless clerk or a careful one, and Vesper crouched in the lee of a beached dinghy for a long time, letting the fog decide for him.

It decided nothing. The lantern stayed.

*You can turn back,* said the voice in him that always argued for turning back — the voice that had sounded all night while Casper lay dying, that had sounded all day while Casper lay buried beneath the orchard tree, that had sounded every hour since. *You can walk back along the causeway, wind the clockwork, light the lamp, and no one will ever know you came this far. Corren offered you a mainland posting. Men have lived whole lives on smaller mercies.*

But the other voice — Nell's voice, or maybe Casper's, or maybe his wife Maren's, who had spent her last year teaching him that a thing carried is a thing kept — said only: *Third drawer from the bottom. Behind the survey maps.*

He crossed the yard low and fast, the way he imagined a crab would do it, and pressed himself against the cold granite below the window. Inside, he heard movement. Not footsteps exactly — the drag-and-settle of a heavy body shifting weight, and the scratch of a pen, and then, unmistakable even through stone, a cough. He knew that cough. It belonged to Hobb, one of Corren's checkpoint men, a broad-faced fellow who had once, before Corren came, sold Vesper mackerel off his own boat and always threw in the smallest fish for free.

Hobb was alone. Hobb was writing something. And Hobb, if the drag-and-settle rhythm meant anything, was half asleep.

Vesper went around to the seaward door. It was locked, but the lock was old, older than Corren's appointment, and the frame had swollen with three days of storm rain until the door stood proud of its jamb by the width of a knife blade. He worked his knife into the gap and leaned his weight against it, slowly, the way you lean against a stubborn animal, and the door gave with a groan he felt in his teeth more than heard.

Inside, the customs house smelled of ink and mildew and, faintly, of smoke.

That last smell stopped him with his hand still on the door. Smoke, in a building with no fire lit. He stood in the dark of the entry hall and let his nose do what his eyes could not, and the smoke smell resolved itself: not hearth-smoke, not pipe-smoke. Lamp-oil smoke. The sharp, mineral reek of oil poured out and left standing, waiting.

They were going to burn it tonight. Not at season's end, as Casper's journal had promised — tonight. Something had hurried them. Perhaps the storm damage, perhaps the inspector rumored to be crossing from the mainland when the weather broke, perhaps simply Corren's appetite for finishing things. Whatever the reason, the oil was already down, and Vesper was already inside it.

Above him, Hobb coughed again, and a chair scraped, and there came the slow tread of boots crossing the floor toward the stair.

Vesper did the arithmetic of the room in the dark. Entry hall. Stair up to the counting room, right side. The record vault — Casper's journal had been precise about this too — down three steps, left side, behind the iron-banded door that was never locked because the customs men were the only ones who ever entered and locks, Corren had reportedly said, were for people with something to fear.

Vesper had something to fear. But the vault door stood open anyway, black as a well mouth, and he went down the three steps and into it and pulled the door nearly shut behind him, leaving a finger's width of dark.

The boots came down the stairs. Lantern light swung through the finger's width of dark and laid a hot gold line across the vault floor, across Vesper's boot, and he drew his foot back into the deeper shadow and held his breath until his chest burned.

Hobb paused at the top of the vault steps. Vesper heard him sniff — the same nose-work Vesper had done, reading the air — and heard him mutter, half to himself, "Bloody oil. Reek'll take a week to shift." Then the boots moved on, back up, and a door closed overhead, and the gold line died.

Vesper counted to two hundred before he moved. It was a habit from the lamp room, where counting was the difference between patience and panic, and it served him now: by two hundred his hands had stopped shaking enough to work by feel.

The vault was not large. Shelves lined both walls, and he walked them with his fingertips, reading spines the way a blind man reads a face. Shipping manifests. Tide surveys. Tax rolls going back forty years, each year fatter than the last with paper nobody read. Third drawer from the bottom — but drawers of what? His fingers found the cabinet against the end wall, iron-banded like the door, and he knelt and began to count downward. One. Two. Three.

Locked. Of course. Corren locked what mattered and left the rest open, and this drawer mattered.

Vesper knelt in the dark with the knife he'd used on the door and felt along the seam of the drawer, and found — as he had hoped, as Osei's forger Tam had told him to hope — that the lock was a cheap brass thing newer than the cabinet, screwed into old wood. He set the blade against the screws and turned them, slowly, each quarter-turn a small scream of metal that he masked by scraping his boot on the floor in time with it, and one by one the screws backed out and the lock plate came free in his hand and the drawer slid open onto the smell of old paper and lamp oil.

Survey maps. He lifted them out in a bundle and set them on the floor beside his knee, and there, flat beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth tied with string, was a ledger bound in blue board with a brass clasp.

His hands knew it before his eyes could. Same binding, same clasp as the ledger Casper had pressed into his arms in the dunes, bleeding and burning with fever, saying *there are two, there are two, I copied everything twice because I didn't trust myself not to fail.* This was the twin. This was the testimony that made the first ledger not one man's word but a pattern, a system, a machine of fraud with Corren's name on every gear.

He untied the string just enough to confirm — pages dense with Casper's small upright hand, dates, sums, names of fishing families beside names of debts that had never existed — and retied it, and slid the ledger inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay over his heart like a second heart.

And that was when he smelled the match.

* * *

Smoke has a grammar, and Vesper had learned it in thirty years of keeping a flame alive in a tower of wood and pitch. First the sulfur bite of the strike. Then the thin grey thread of kindling catching. Then the long inhale of a building deciding whether to live or die. He was somewhere between the second and third when he came up the vault steps with the survey maps abandoned on the floor and the ledger hard against his ribs.

Overhead, through the floorboards, orange light flickered where no light should be. He heard laughter — two voices, young and loose with drink — and the slosh of something being poured, and a voice saying, clear as a bell through the boards: "Corren wants it looking like the damp got into the records. Damp and carelessness. So mind you don't torch the whole wall, you great ox, just the shelving."

"Then why pour oil on the floor?"

"Because the floor's where the damp is, idiot. Come on. We're due at the bridge checkpoint by four."

Boots. Moving toward the seaward door. Toward the door Vesper had jimmied and left standing proud of its jamb by the width of a knife blade.

There was no time to be clever. There was barely time to be anything. Vesper took the stairs two at a time into the counting room, where the lamplight Hobb had left still burned and the smoke was already threading down from the shelving at the far end where someone had stacked oiled rags against the tax rolls and struck a match and walked away whistling. Fire had taken the bottom shelf. It was climbing the tax rolls with the calm appetite of a thing that has all night.

The seaward door was opening. Vesper looked at it, looked at the window opposite — shuttered, bolted from within — and chose the window, because a door opens onto faces and a window opens onto fog.

He hit the shutter with his shoulder and the bolt tore partway out of rotten wood and the shutter banged wide and cold air and fog poured in and he was through it, dropping into the yard, rolling, coming up running — and behind him the two men shouted, and a lantern swung out the broken window and caught him full in the face.

Not his silhouette. Not his back. His *face*. Fog or no fog, lantern-light or no lantern-light, the beam found him square as a signature, and he saw the younger of the two men squint after him and heard him say, in a voice pitched to carry: "Grey coat! Grey coat and a satchel — that's the oil courier, that's the one from the causeway checkpoint, stop him, *stop him!*"

Vesper ran.

He ran the way he had not run since he was a boy on this island, down the shingle and along the tideline where the stones rolled under his boots and tried to take his ankles, and behind him the shouts multiplied — the whole eastern shoulder waking, doors banging, dogs starting up, the checkpoint bell beginning its ugly clangor. The fog that had hidden him all night now hid his pursuers too, which cut both ways; he ran blind, guided by the sea-sound on his left, and the ledger thumped against his chest with every stride like a fist knocking to be let in.

At the bend where the tideline met the fish-drying racks he made his mistake. He looked back.

One glance, half a second, the instinct of a man who has spent thirty years checking whether the light is still burning. In that half second his boot found the gap between two stones and turned, and he went down hard on the shingle, and his hands — flung out to save his face, the way hands do — came down on the wet stones and took the whole falling weight of him.

Something in his right wrist crunched. Pain went up his arm like a struck bell. And the ledger, thrown by the impact, skidded from his coat across the stones into the dark.

He crawled for it. There was no dignity in it and no time to find any; he crawled on his forearms with his ruined wrist curled to his chest, patting the shingle blind, and the shouts were closer now, and the bell was still ringing, and his fingers found string — oilcloth — brass clasp — and he had it, he had it, he stuffed it back inside his coat and forced himself upright using a drying rack for a crutch, and he saw, twenty yards ahead through the shredding fog, the black shape of the overturned dinghy he had crouched behind hours ago, and beyond it, the causeway.

They would watch the causeway. Of course they would watch the causeway; it was the only way off the eastern shoulder except the water, and the water was running high and black with the storm's leftover swell.

So Vesper gave them the causeway. He staggered to the causeway mouth in plain view of the shouting shapes behind him, let them see him commit to it, let them turn and run to raise the bridge checkpoint — and then, ten steps onto the causeway, he stepped off its northern edge into the shallows and waded.

The water took him to the waist and then to the chest. It was colder than any water he had known, cold enough to stop the breath in his throat, cold enough to make the crunch in his wrist sing a second note. He turned north along the causeway's outer face, keeping the stones at his left shoulder, moving with the swell rather than against it, and the ledger inside his coat soaked the cold through to his skin and lay against his heart like a stone that had chosen to be carried.

Twice the swell knocked him against the stones and he thought, calmly, the way one thinks in the lamp room during the worst weather: *this is how it ends, drowned a hundred yards from safety, holding a dead man's book.* Twice he found his feet again. The third time, the fog opened ahead of him and showed him the silhouette of the old net-shed at the causeway's midpoint, Osei's territory, and a single candle burning in its seaward window like a very small lighthouse.

He hauled himself up the shed's slip-stones with one arm and knocked, and the door opened before the second knock, because Osei had posted Tam there every night this week, and Tam took one look at him — the grey coat streaming, the wrist bent wrong, the face white as the foam — and pulled him inside without a word.

* * *

They splinted the wrist with a herring batten and strapping cut from a sailcloth scrap, and Osei herself arrived within the hour, moving through the fog with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, and she sat him down on a barrel and unwrapped his hands to look at them and made a sound low in her throat.

The right hand was the worst — the wrist, and two fingers already swelling purple. But the left had taken the shingle too, skinned raw across the palm and fingers, and when she cleaned it with spirit the pain made him gasp and then, absurdly, laugh.

"What's funny," Osei said. It was not a question.

"I keep thinking," Vesper said, "that I've spent thirty years protecting my hands. Winding the clockwork, trimming the wicks, polishing the lens. A keeper's hands are the whole trade. Maren used to say my hands were worth more than the lamp." He looked at them lying in his lap, bandaged, useless, trembling. "And now they're ruined, and the lamp's still burning, and the book is safe, and I can't decide whether I've failed at everything or managed the only thing that matters."

Osei finished the strapping and tied it off with her teeth and her good hand, and for a moment she said nothing, and the candle in the net-shed guttered and steadied.

"You know what they're saying at the checkpoint already?" she said at last. "Tam heard the relay. *The courier's face was seen. Grey coat, satchel, keeper's build.* They've sent a description to Corren's house." She looked at him steadily. "Your face is known now, Vesper. By morning every man on this island who serves Corren will know it. You can't go back to the lighthouse as yourself. You can't stand at your own checkpoint. You can't—"

"Nell," Vesper said.

The name dropped into the shed like a stone into a well, and the silence after it went all the way down.

"She's at Osei's house," Osei said quickly. "She's safe, she's sleeping, she thinks you're on the mainland buying oil. But Vesper—" and here the old woman's voice changed, took on a weight he had not heard in it before, the weight of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and outlived every magistrate this island had thrown at her, "—Corren knows the courier was seen. And Corren knows whose build the courier had. And there is a girl on this island with your chin and your stubbornness, and men like Corren do not sit long with a piece of knowledge like that. They use it."

Outside, far off across the water, the customs house fire had grown visible even through the fog — a dull orange bloom low on the eastern sky, eating forty years of tax rolls and manifests and, somewhere in its heart, whatever true records had survived Casper's copying. Let it eat them, Vesper thought. Let Corren believe the burning settled the matter. Let him sleep soundly tonight with his forged ledgers and his bought men and his velvet voice.

Because tomorrow — Osei was already talking tomorrow, quietly, rapidly, naming names and boats and tides — tomorrow Corren would learn that the courier's face belonged to the lighthouse keeper. And Vesper Zhou, with his ruined hands and his known face and two ledgers' worth of truth sewn into the lining of a dead man's argument, would have to decide what a man trades when the only thing he has left to trade is himself.

Osei put her hand, gentle as gull-down, on his bandaged ones.

"Rest," she said. "Tonight you rest. Tomorrow we find out what kind of man Corren is when he stops pretending."

Across the water, the fire bloomed and faded, bloomed and faded, like a lamp signaling in a language Vesper was only beginning to read.
