## 16. Taken

Nell knew something had changed before anyone said a word. She knew it the way she knew weather — not by seeing the storm but by the way the gulls went quiet, by the way her father's hands moved that morning, slower over the kettle, slower over the wick of the small lamp they kept in the kitchen because the big one above needed him all night now. He had come back from the mainland side smelling of smoke. His bandages were new, whiter than the old ones, and he would not let her look at what was underneath.

"Papa," she had said, "your hands."

"Bread, Nell. Eat your bread."

But his voice had gone thin at the edges, like paper left out in rain, and when Widow Osei came to the door and spoke to him in the low tone adults believed children couldn't hear, Nell heard every word. *Known now. No hiding it. The dog — where's the dog? Good. Keep the dog close.*

She took a slice of bread with honey and went out into the grey morning to find Casper-the-tree instead of Casper-the-dog, because the tree never ran off, and she needed something that stayed.

* * *

The orchard stood at the bend of the path between the lighthouse and the village, six trees in a ragged line, five of them old and bent like fishermen listening for bells under the water, and one — hers — younger, crooked, stubborn, grown from some seed a bird had dropped years before anyone on the island could remember. She had named it Casper back in the autumn, for no reason she could explain then and no reason she could explain now, except that names came to her sometimes whole, like stones turned up by a plough, and this one had simply arrived.

She put her palm against its bark. The bark was cold and slightly damp, and beneath her hand she felt the faint tick of sap, or imagined she did, the way you could feel a sleeping cat's heart if you held still long enough.

"You know things," she told the tree. "Trees know things. Papa says that's nonsense but Papa says a lot of things are nonsense."

The wind came off the water in a long breath, and the five old trees leaned together and whispered about it, and Casper-the-tree stood straighter than it should have for something so young, which Nell had always privately considered brave.

Behind her, down the path, boots.

Not her father's boots. Her father's boots were soft-heeled, mended twice, and made a sound like an apology. These boots made a sound like doors closing.

Three men in the magistrate's grey-and-blue came around the bend, and the one in front was not a soldier at all. Nell knew him from market days and proclamation-boards: Magistrate Corren himself, in a good wool coat with the collar turned up, his gloves tucked in one hand as though the cold were a thing that happened to other people. He was smiling. Nell's mother had died when she was seven, but she remembered enough of grown-ups to know that this smile was the kind that worked hardest when the face behind it was doing arithmetic.

"There she is," Corren said. "Nell Zhou. I've heard a great deal about you."

Nell did not move from the tree. "I haven't done anything."

"No one said you had." He crouched, which was somehow worse than standing; it put his face level with hers, and his eyes were pale grey like the sea before ice. "I'll tell you what has happened, because I believe children deserve the truth. Your father has been careless with some important papers. Papers that belong to the island — to all of us, really. Until he returns them, the island can't be safe. And until the island is safe, a girl shouldn't be alone out here in the wind." He straightened, gestured gently at the two men behind him. "You'll come and stay with me. As my guest. The magistrate's house has a fire that actually burns, and Mrs. Tann makes biscuits on Thursdays. Today is Thursday."

"I have school."

"It's Thursday," Corren repeated, as though that settled everything, and perhaps it did, because the two men were already moving, one on each side of her, not touching her, never touching her — she understood even then that not-touching was part of the design — and the four of them walked down toward the village while Nell looked back once, only once, at the crooked young tree that bore her dead friend's name.

*Be brave,* she thought at it. *I'll be brave too.*

Casper-the-tree bent in the wind and said nothing, the way trees do, which is either nothing at all or everything.

* * *

The magistrate's house sat on the high ground above the bridge, and it was the largest building on the island after the customs house, with windows that caught the grey light and gave nothing back. Inside it smelled of lemon oil and cold stone. Mrs. Tann, the housekeeper, showed Nell to a room on the second floor with a real bed and a washstand and a window facing the water — facing, Nell realized within a minute of being shut in, the lighthouse.

They hadn't locked the door. That was the first thing she tested. It turned easily in her hand, and downstairs she could hear Corren talking with someone, low and pleasant, and she understood: locks were for prisoners, and she was not a prisoner. She was a guest. Guests stayed because leaving was impossible, which was a kind of lock that didn't need a key.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass.

The lighthouse stood on its black headland half a mile away, white in the wrong places where gulls had left their mark, and the great lantern room at the top caught the morning light like an empty eye. Her father would be up there, or asleep below it, his hands wrapped in their white bands. She tried to imagine what he'd felt when he came home and found the kitchen empty and the door swinging. She couldn't. It was too large a feeling to fit inside her, so she set it down outside herself, the way you set down something heavy, and picked up the smaller thing instead, the thing she could carry: *Don't give them the papers, Papa. Whatever they do. Don't.*

Because she had read enough of the ledger — one night in October, curious, when it lay hidden in the grave marker and she'd lifted the slate just to touch the secret of it — to know what those pages meant. Names. Her neighbors' names. The Osei name. The Pell family. Old Anders who repaired nets and sang off-key. Column after column of debts that had never been owed, manufactured in a clerk's careful hand so that a man in a good coat could take boats and houses and a way of life without ever once picking up a weapon.

She had told Tomas. That was the sin she carried now, sitting on a stranger's bed in a stranger's house. She had told Tomas because he was her friend and because secrets are heavy for ten-year-olds in a way adults forget, and Tomas had told his brother, and his brother worked here, in this house, polishing brass and carrying messages, and now she was here and the papers were not.

A knock. Mrs. Tann with a tray: bread, butter, milk in a cup with a chip in the rim.

"I'm not hungry," Nell said.

"Eat it anyway." Mrs. Tann set the tray down and, in the same motion, the same breath, the same ordinary movement of a woman making up a bed, whispered: "Window latch is broken. Rattles in the wind. Terrible nuisance." Then she was gone, and Nell sat looking at the window, and then at the door, and understood that the island was larger than the magistrate thought, that it had more rooms than his house, and that some of them were hidden inside plain words.

* * *

Corren came himself at dusk.

He knocked — of course he knocked — and entered with a plate of Thursday biscuits and an air of a man arriving at his own fireside. He sat on the chair by the window, uninvited, and arranged his coat across his knees.

"Are you comfortable?"

Nell said nothing. Silence was a thing she'd learned from her father, who could hold it the way other men held hammers.

"Your father burned down the customs annex last night," Corren said pleasantly. "Or helped to. Or knows who did. You'd know better than I, I expect." He let the silence sit a moment, then continued as though she'd answered. "Here is what will happen, Nell. Nothing. Nothing will happen to you at all. You will sleep in that bed, eat Mrs. Tann's cooking, walk in the garden at stated hours, and be treated with every courtesy, for exactly as long as it takes your father to be sensible. He loves you very much — any fool can see that. A man who loves his daughter will do the reasonable thing. Reasonable men always do, eventually." He smiled the arithmetic smile. "You may consider yourself an argument. The best kind. The kind that can't be shouted down."

"My papa isn't reasonable," Nell said. It surprised her, coming out of her mouth. "He keeps a light burning all night for strangers in ships he'll never meet. He buried a man he barely knew under a tree because nobody else would. He's not reasonable at all."

Something crossed Corren's face then — quick, quickly smoothed away, but she saw it, and filed it away the way her father filed observations about weather. It was doubt. Just a sliver. Just the width of a knife going in.

"No," Corren agreed softly, standing. "No, I'm beginning to think he isn't." At the door he paused. "Biscuits are almond. Your favorite, I'm told."

He closed the door gently, and Nell sat in the gathering dark and did not cry, because crying was loud and she had work to do. When the house settled — Mrs. Tann's footsteps below, the soldier at the gate stamping against the cold, the clock in the hall clearing its throat — she went to the window and worked the broken latch until it opened with a rattle the wind obligingly covered.

Cold air poured in. Below, the garden, the wall, the drop to the cliff path. Beyond, the black water, and on the far headland, the lighthouse — and even as she watched, the great lamp woke, sweeping its beam across the strait, steady, patient, her father's heartbeat made visible.

*I'm here,* she told him across the water. *Don't be sensible. Be like you.*

Then she did the thing she'd been turning over all afternoon, the thing Mrs. Tann's whisper had unlocked. She went to the washstand, found the little stub of pencil beside the jug, and on the back of the biscuit-plate's paper napkin she wrote, in her careful school hand:

*I am well. They are kind because kindness is the trap. The window rattles. Tell the widow the tree remembers where things are buried. — N*

She folded it small, smaller, smallest, and waited for the dog.

Because Casper-the-dog had followed the cart, she'd seen him from the window at noon, grey ghost slipping along the garden wall before a soldier shooed him off — and a dog that could find her across half an island could find a napkin thrown from a second-story window, if the wind was right and the dog was clever and the girl was patient. She had named the tree after him. She had, in some way she couldn't explain, named herself partly his ever since. Dogs kept faith. It was the whole of their religion.

An hour passed. The lamp swept and swept. Then, below the window, in the shadow of the garden wall, two ears rose like small moons.

Nell's heart banged once, hard, and went quiet. She leaned out into the cold, checked the soldier at the gate — turned away, stamping, collar up — and dropped the napkin. It sailed sideways on the wind, a white moth, and the grey shape below flowed out of the shadows and snapped it from the air almost politely, and then was gone, over the wall, swallowed by the island's dark, carrying her small true words toward the lighthouse the way he had once, she was certain, carried something larger and heavier through three checkpoints sewn into a coat.

She closed the window. She ate one biscuit — almond, annoyingly delicious — and got into the stranger's bed in the stranger's house, and lay awake listing what she had that they could not take: her name, the tree's name, the dog, the window, the widow's whisper, the memory of a slate marker lifted by moonlight.

In the morning, Corren would receive a message from the ferryman's skiff, and his pleasant face would change for the first time in front of witnesses, because her father was coming — everyone on the island would see him come, walking the causeway road with his bandaged hands and both ledgers, giving himself up — and Nell intended to be at that window, wide awake, watching the trap she had helped to salt begin to close.
