## 18. The Exchange

The storm had been building all day, and Corren thought it a fine omen. Weather like this made men grateful for any authority that promised them shelter, and gratitude was the raw material of his trade. He had built eleven years of office on it — on debts forgiven just often enough to be remembered, on seizures postponed until the postponement itself felt like a gift. By spring he intended to be a man of the mainland, with a posting in the provincial capital and a house above the harbor fog, and this island would be a story he told at dinners: the place where he had begun, the rock he had turned to paper.

The paper was the whole of it now. Two ledgers, or so the intelligence claimed — the clerk Casper's original, hidden somewhere in the lighthouse since the man's burial, and a second copy in the customs house that Corren had ordered burned before it could be inventoried. The fire had done its work, mostly. What mattered was the girl.

He stood at the window of the customs house and watched the pier lash itself in the rising sea. Beyond it, the lighthouse beam swung through the rain like a slow arm, patient, indifferent, sweeping the water and finding nothing. He had always disliked that light. It asked nothing of anyone. It simply shone, night after night, on the strength of one man's stubbornness, and there was no leverage in it, no debt, no want. You could not buy a man who needed nothing but his own duty.

But you could buy him with a daughter. Corren had learned long ago that every person carried one thing they would not negotiate, and the trick of governance was finding it without seeming to search. In Vesper Zhou it had taken no searching at all. The man had walked into the customs house that morning, hat in his bandaged hands, and said the word *Nell* as though laying it on the counter like currency — which, Corren reflected, was exactly what it was. He did not despise Zhou for it. Love that could be spent was still love; it merely confirmed what Corren had always known about the arithmetic of people.

"Your guest is asking after her father again," said Brann, his sergeant, from the doorway. Rain ran off the man's oilskins and pooled at his boots.

"She may ask. She'll be fed, kept warm, and returned by nightfall." Corren drew on his gloves, finger by finger. "That's the arrangement, and I keep arrangements. It's why people deal with me at all."

"And the ledgers?"

"Genuine or not, they leave the island tonight." He said it lightly, but the words had weight he'd been carrying for weeks. The mainland inspector's letter sat locked in his desk — *an inquiry into land registrations, northern district* — and though such inquiries died more often than they lived, this one had an auditor's name signed beneath it, and auditors were a different animal from inspectors. An inspector could be entertained. An auditor counted.

He went down to where the girl waited.

They had given her a chair by the stove in the guardroom, and she sat in it the way children sit in borrowed places — upright, hands folded, taking up as little of the world as possible. Her dog lay across her feet, a ragged yellow animal with salt-stiff fur that raised its head when Corren entered and fixed him with a look he chose not to interpret. Dogs always disliked him. He had stopped minding.

"Nell," he said, warmly. He had practiced the warmth. "Your father will be here within the hour."

She looked at his hands rather than his face, which he found interesting. Children read hands; they hadn't yet learned to trust the face instead. "You'll give me back?"

"On the pier, into his own arms, before dark." He crouched to her height, careful to keep his distance — near enough for sincerity, far enough for dignity. "I want you to understand something, Nell. This isn't punishment. Your father has been carrying papers that don't belong to him — work documents, records of my office — and a record left my office is a record that can't protect anyone. Not the fishing families. Not you." He let that settle. "I'm going to look after those papers properly, and your father is going to stop carrying burdens too heavy for him. That's all this is."

"You burned the customs house," she said.

"The fire spread from a lamp. Accidents are common in storms."

"Casper said you'd say that."

The name went through him like a splinter finding its depth. Casper — dead three weeks, buried under a stray apple tree at the edge of a keeper's garden — and still speaking. Corren straightened. "Casper," he said evenly, "was a sick man who took things that confused him."

"He wasn't confused." The girl's voice didn't rise at all. "He used to come to the tree and talk to it. Before he was dead. I heard him once. He said the tree knew how to keep things." Her eyes came up to Corren's face at last, and they were gray and calm and entirely unafraid, and he understood suddenly why the keeper had crossed a flooded causeway with burned hands rather than surrender anything to do with this child. Some innocence is not ignorance. It is clarity that hasn't yet agreed to be lied to.

"The tree," Corren repeated. "Well. Trees keep apples, usually." He turned to Brann. "Bring her along when the bell rings. Coat and hood."

Outside, the wind had climbed into its full voice. He crossed the yard to the pier with his collar up and his mind working the problem one last time, the way a man counts money twice. Two ledgers recovered. The girl returned. Zhou discredited — a grieving widower, a smuggler of stolen documents, a man whose testimony would arrive wrapped in suspicion. And if the true pages surfaced later, they would surface as copies of copies, deniable, orphaned. Paper without a witness was only paper. He had outlived better evidence than this.

He almost believed it by the time he reached the pier.

* * *

The exchange point was the pier's midsection, where the planking gave onto a stone apron slick with spray. Corren placed four men along the rail with lanterns hooded against the wind and put Brann at his shoulder. The ferry lay off the pier's end, riding its moorings in the swell, and Vesper Zhou stood at the bow of the ferryman's dory as it came grinding through the surf toward the stones.

Corren watched him come and catalogued him as he had taught himself to catalogue every man on this island. Hands bandaged, so the burns were real. Face drawn, so the fear was real — or the resolve; the two wore the same expression at this distance. Coat heavy at the breast, so the ledgers were on him, sewn or strapped, exactly as promised. A man keeping his end of a bargain. The rarest thing on the northern coast, and Corren intended to honor it in precisely the measure it served him.

The dory's keel bit stone. Zhou stepped down into the wash without waiting for the line, and the ferryman — Tam, the cowardly one, the one who had logged every departure at the checkpoints and then wept about it in the tavern — held the boat steady with an oar and did not look at anyone. Corren noted that too. Men who had been broken once stayed broken. That was the comfort of breaking them.

"Keeper." He raised his voice over the wind. "You came alone."

"I came as agreed."

"The papers first, then. Fair dealing both ways." He extended his hand, palm open, letting the gesture be seen by the lanterns and by Brann's watching men. Procedure witnessed was procedure that held.

Zhou reached into his coat. There was a moment — Corren would remember it afterward, would turn it over on many sleepless nights looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the trap had been stitched shut while he watched — there was a moment when the keeper's bandaged hands fumbled at his breast, and Corren felt the ordinary triumph of a transaction closing. Then two bound volumes came out, sodden at their edges, swollen with damp, and were laid into his palms with a care that struck him, briefly and absurdly, as funerary.

He weighed them. Heavy. Complete-looking. He opened the first to its middle and saw columns in Casper's cramped hand — names, plots, sums, dates — and the familiar architecture of his own design looking back at him, page after page. The second ledger matched. His signature appeared where his signature should appear. The fraudulent valuations were all there, the manufactured debts, the seizure orders dressed as mercy loans.

"Both of them," Zhou said. "As they were given to me. Now my daughter."

"In a moment." Corren closed the ledgers and held them against his chest, and the feeling that moved through him then he would never admit to anyone, because it was very close to tenderness. Eleven years of work. The whole island, folded and sewn. "Sergeant, fetch the girl."

Brann went. The surf boomed against the apron. Zhou stood in the streaming rain with his ruined hands hanging at his sides, and Corren studied him, waiting for the collapse, the sob, the curse — some human spillage he could file away. But the keeper only watched the guardroom door, and his face had the terrible patience of a man who has already survived the worst thing the sea can do and does not expect mercy from weather again.

Then the door opened, and Nell came out under Brann's hand, hooded, the yellow dog pressed to her side.

"Father," she said, and it was not loud, but Zhou crossed the wet stones as if the wind had blown him there, and gathered her in, and Corren looked away from the embrace with professional detachment and one flicker of something else — envy, perhaps, filed and forgotten.

"The boat is yours," Corren called. "The bridge opens in spring regardless. Tell the island I dealt fairly. It costs nothing to say it, and it will make your winters easier."

Zhou turned, the girl's hand swallowed in his bandaged one. For a moment the lighthouse beam swept past behind him, and his face lit white and then fell back into rain-dark, and he said, quietly, in a voice that carried anyway, the way certain voices do against wind:

"You should have burned both ledgers, Magistrate. If they were yours to burn."

And they were pulling for the ferry, and the dog leapt into the dory's stern, shaking spray, and Corren stood with the two volumes clutched to his chest and felt the first cold thread of doubt go through the fabric of the evening — thin as wire, easy to ignore.

* * *

In the customs house, with the storm hammering the shutters, he dried the ledgers page by page before the stove, and it was there, an hour past midnight, that the doubt finished becoming knowledge.

Osei's forger was good. That was the first thing Corren understood, and understanding it was like hearing a lock click shut in another room. The pages were right — the ink aged, the hand convincing, the sums consistent. They were right the way a portrait is right, and wrong the way a portrait is wrong: no page was a copy of any document he had ever signed.

He checked the registry numbers against his memory and found numbers he had never issued, in sequences his office had never run. He found dates that predated the scheme by months, debts recorded as existing before the men who owed them had ever borrowed. Whoever had salted these volumes had not tried to erase his fraud. They had done something far worse. They had filled his fraud's house with furniture that belonged to someone else — so that any auditor who opened these ledgers would find not Corren's crimes, neatly documented, but a chaos of impossible entries that would lead the counting backward, outward, into every registry the province maintained. These pages did not hide the truth. They were bait. They were a trail — and Corren followed it sitting alone at his stove, page after page, his hands going colder as the room grew warmer, and at the bottom of the final volume he found the entry that stopped his breath.

A single line, in Casper's hand — the true hand, unmistakable, the hand of a dying man given one last forgery of his own:

*The magistrate keeps what he believes is whole. What is whole is elsewhere.*

Corren sat back. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the sea detonated against the pier piles, and he thought: *elsewhere.* Thought: the girl on the pier, calm, hands folded, reading his hands instead of his face. Thought: the dog — the wretched yellow dog that had watched him with its flat coastal eyes while he made his little speech about papers protecting no one.

He was moving before he finished the thought, shouting for Brann, dragging on his coat, and even then, even running through the rain toward the ferryman's cottage with the false ledgers under his arm, some cold and precise part of him was already doing the arithmetic he would spend the rest of the winter denying: the exchange had gone exactly as he had demanded, every condition met, every courtesy observed — which meant that from the moment he had set the terms, he had never once been the one setting them.

The dory was gone from its moorings. Of course it was. And on the door of the ferryman's cottage, nailed fast against the wind, hung a single sheet of oilpaper, and beneath it, in Widow Osei's upright schoolroom hand, a message meant for morning:

*Come to the bridge, Magistrate. Bring your books. The island will bring its own.*
