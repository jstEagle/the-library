## 19. The Light Goes Out

The lighthouse had never been a prison before. Vesper had lived inside its walls for eleven years and had thought of it as a spine — the island's spine, his spine — something that held a body upright against the weather. Now the door was barred from the outside, and the same walls that had sheltered him felt like the inside of a lung that had forgotten how to breathe.

They had taken him at dawn, two days after the fire. His hands were wrapped in linen stiff with tallow and the salve Widow Osei had pressed on him at the water's edge, and the bandages made his fingers into something borrowed, something he operated rather than inhabited. The pain came in waves, and he had learned to count them the way he counted the turns of the clockwork — one hundred and twelve, one hundred and thirteen — as though pain were a mechanism he could wind down if only he kept proper time with it.

Corren's men had not beaten him. That was the strange courtesy of it. They had walked him up the spiral stairs with a lantern held ahead of him like an usher at a wedding, and they had locked him in the lamp room itself, with a cot, a bucket, a jug of water, and a window on the whole grey world. *You'll be comfortable, keeper,* the sergeant had said, not unkindly, the way a man speaks to a horse he has been ordered to shoot. *The magistrate wants you where you can watch.*

Vesper had not understood what that meant until the second night.

* * *

The storm came up from the southwest the way the great ones always did — not arriving but *assembling*, gathering itself out of the sea in stages, first a long low swell that made the fishing boats strain at their moorings, then a sky the color of wet slate, then the wind, which began as a whisper in the rigging of the world and ended as a shout. Vesper knew this storm. He had known storms like it all his life on this coast, and he knew what they asked of a keeper: everything, and then more.

He stood at the glass with his bandaged hands pressed flat against the cold pane and watched it come, and behind him the great lens sat in its cradle like a sleeping animal, dark for the first night in thirty-one years. Thirty-one years — his father's service and his own — and in all that time the lamp had gone cold only twice, both times for less than an hour, both times with a boy on the stairs holding a spare wick and his heart in his throat.

Now it was dark because a magistrate wanted it dark.

They had come at dusk, four of them, with a written order Vesper was not permitted to read. They had worked the mechanism with the clumsy efficiency of men who understood engines but not what engines were *for*, and the last thing before the light died was the smell of the wick being pinched — a smell like a snuffed candle in a sickroom, like the end of a vigil. Vesper had heard it through the door they would not open for him, and the sound had gone through him like a fishhook.

*For the duration of the emergency,* the sergeant had said, not meeting his eyes. *The magistrate's orders. There's no traffic in this weather anyway. No one's fool enough to sail.*

Vesper had pressed his forehead to the glass and thought: *Everyone is fool enough to sail. That is what the sea is for.*

* * *

The first hours of the storm were only noise. He measured them in the old way, by the clockwork that no longer needed winding, by the barometer he could see through the open door of the instrument case — falling, falling, the needle sagging like a tired shoulder. Twenty-nine inches and still dropping. He had seen that reading twice in his life, and both times there had been wrecks, and both times he had been awake for three days afterward, and both times the light had burned.

The wind found its voice around midnight. It came at the tower in long shoving gusts, then in a sustained roar that made the whole structure hum like a struck bell, and the rain came sideways, and the sea came over the rocks in sheets that whitened even the darkness. Vesper sat on the cot with his hands in his lap and listened to his life's work being silenced, and he thought about Casper.

He thought about Casper more often now than he thought about Maren, which was its own kind of grief, a grief he had not expected and did not know how to carry. Maren had been his for twenty years; her loss was a country he had learned to farm. Casper had been his for one night — one fevered, bleeding night on the kitchen floor, one whispered *the customs house, the second copy, the fire at season's end* — and then a shallow grave beneath the orchard tree, and a name Nell had already given to the tree itself, so that the man and the tree had become one thing in the child's mind, something with roots and branches and apples that no one ate.

*You kept the light for them,* Casper had said, in the last lucid hour, meaning the fishing families, meaning everyone. *You keep it and you never ask what they do with the seeing.*

Vesper had not understood that then. Sitting in the dark tower with the storm building, he understood it now. The light was not his. It had never been his. He had only been the hands it borrowed, and a man who locks up the hands has not locked up the light — the light is in the people who look for it, who steer by the memory of it, who trust it the way Nell trusted the dog, the way the island had trusted him.

And Corren had put it out.

* * *

He heard the ship before he saw it — or rather, he heard the *absence* that meant a ship, the way a shepherd hears a missing bell. The storm had its own music, and into that music, faint and wrong, came a new sound: a steam whistle, blown in the pattern of distress, three short blasts, over and over, thin and desperate against the wind.

Vesper was on his feet before he knew it, both palms against the glass, his bandages leaving pale smears on the pane.

The fog was gone — the storm had scoured the air clean — but the rain was a grey curtain, and through it, out past the shoal markers, he caught it: a light. Not his light. A running light, green to starboard, red to port, swinging wildly, and beneath it the black suggestion of a hull. A coaster, by her size. A small steamer or a heavy schooner with an auxiliary engine, working up the coast in weather she should never have attempted, or weather she had thought she could attempt because — and Vesper felt the thought land in him like a stone dropped down a well — because the light on this headland had burned without fail for thirty-one years, and every master on this coast knew it the way they knew their own names, and a man will risk a crossing he should not risk if the one fixed thing in his mathematics is truly fixed.

*She thinks we're lit,* Vesper said aloud, to no one, to the glass, to Corren wherever Corren was warm and dry. *She's making her bearing on a light that isn't there.*

The whistle blew again. Three short. Three short. The hull swung, and he could see her problem written in her movement: she was standing in toward the headland to take her bearing off the light, the way a hundred masters had done in a hundred storms, and the light was not there, and the rocks were.

He did the arithmetic he had done a thousand times, the arithmetic that lived in his hands more than his head. The shoal lay a half-mile northeast of the tower. The coaster was making perhaps six knots against the sea. He had — they all had — minutes. Less than minutes.

And there was nothing in his power. No lamp to trim. No wick to light. The oil store was locked and guarded; the mechanism was cold; the door was barred; his hands were ruined. He pressed his bandaged fists against the glass as though he could push the light out through his own body, as though grief and fury were fuels, and the ship came on, and the whistle blew, and the storm roared down like a judgment.

* * *

What happened next, Vesper saw entire, because the sea lit it for him.

A wave lifted the coaster — he saw her whole, keel to truck, a grey ghost standing on a grey hill of water — and set her down inside the shoal line, and the rocks took her. Not all at once. The sea is not merciful enough for that and not cruel enough either; it simply grinds. There was a sound that reached the tower through the wind, a sound like a door being torn off a house, and then the green light was gone, and then the red light was gone, and then there was only the storm and the white water boiling over the shoal, and — small, terrible, unmistakable — a cluster of lights on the wreck's afterdeck, lanterns, the kind men light when the ship is lost and the boats are being lowered.

He counted them. Six lanterns. He would count them for the rest of his life. Six lanterns, rising and falling in the white, and then fewer, and then the storm took even the lanterns, and Vesper stood with his ruined hands against the glass and made the sound that a man makes when there is nothing left in him that words can carry.

He did not know how long he stood there. The clock in his chest, the one that had measured oil and wicks and turns of the mechanism for thirty-one years, had stopped. When he came back to himself he was on his knees on the lamp room floor, and the barometer had fallen again, and the storm was at its height, and somewhere below him, in the barred and darkened tower, the great lens sat in its cradle like a dead man's eye.

*Six lanterns,* he thought. *Corren will say the weather did it. Corren will say no light could have saved them. Corren will stand at a window somewhere dry and say the sea is a hard mistress, gentlemen, and pour another glass.*

And then a second thought came, and it came not like grief but like a coal, glowing, and Vesper held it in both bandaged hands and did not let it go.

*But the island saw.*

* * *

They came at first light to bring him water, and he made them look at him.

The sergeant — a broad, dull-faced man from the mainland garrison, the same one who had ushered him upstairs — set the jug inside the door and would have left, and Vesper crossed the room and stood in front of him, close, so close the man could not look anywhere else.

"You were on the wall last night," Vesper said. His voice was wrecked from the smoke of the customs house fire, a gravel-and-glass voice, and it made the sergeant flinch. "You heard the whistle."

"Keeper, I have my orders—"

"Six lanterns." Vesper held up his bandaged hand and spread the fingers, and the gesture was so like a man counting that the sergeant's eyes went to it and could not leave. "I stood at that glass and I counted six lanterns going down on the shoal, and you stood on the wall and you heard the same whistle I heard, and when they pull bodies off the rocks this morning — and they *will*, the sea always gives them back — every one of those men was a man your magistrate drowned with a written order and a pinched wick."

"The weather—" the sergeant began.

"The weather is mine," Vesper said. "I have kept this light through weather that would strip the flesh off your bones. I know its temper like my wife's. The weather did not put out that lamp. A man did. A man with a name, and a seal, and a fear of paper." He stepped closer still. "And when the island asks who — and the island *will* ask, sergeant, because the widow Osei is already asking, and that woman has never once asked a question she did not already know the answer to — what will you say? Will you say you were only following orders? Or will you say you stood on a wall in a storm and heard six men die for a magistrate's signature?"

The sergeant looked at him for a long moment. Rain still streamed down the lamp room glass behind Vesper; the light through it was the color of pewter. And in the sergeant's face, dull as it was, something moved — not conscience, perhaps, not yet, but the animal unease of a man who has begun to understand which way the ground is tilting.

"I bring you water," the sergeant said at last, and set the jug down, and left. But he left the door unbarred behind him for the length of one breath, one breath in which Vesper could see the stairs going down, and the grey morning, and the rocks where the sea was already giving up its dead — and then the bar clanked home, and the breath was gone.

It was enough. It was everything. A door that opens for one breath is a door that has remembered it is a door.

* * *

By noon the island knew.

Vesper watched it happen from the lamp room, the way he had watched a hundred storms and a hundred homecomings. The news went along the shore road the way news always went on the island — on foot, from kitchen to kitchen, from net-loft to net-loft, carried in the faces of the women who went down to the rocks with baskets as if baskets could matter, and came back up with the baskets empty and their shawls soaked and their faces set in a line he had never seen on them before.

They came to the shoal in twos and threes. Old Tam with his white beard, who had taught Vesper's father to read the swell. The Widow Osei, upright as a mast, standing on the black rock at the shoal's edge with her skirt whipping in the wind, not searching — she was past searching — but *witnessing*, her eyes going from the wreck to the dark tower and back again, and Vesper knew that she was not blaming him. He knew it the way he knew the weather. But he also knew that she was counting, as he had counted, and that her arithmetic would become the island's arithmetic, and that Corren had made a mistake no forgery could mend.

Because here was the thing Corren had never understood, the thing Vesper had only learned in the last dark days: the light had never been the lamp. The lamp was glass and brass and oil. The light was the *agreement* — thirty-one years of a fixed thing on a moving coast, an agreement between the keeper and every soul who put out to sea, an agreement so old and so kept that men had stopped thinking of it as an agreement at all and had come to think of it as the way the world was. Corren had broken the world in front of the whole island, and he had done it with a written order, and the order was in his own hand, and the hand could be proved.

Down on the rocks, the Widow Osei turned from the sea. She looked up at the dark lantern room, and even at this distance, even through the streaming glass, Vesper saw her find him — saw her eyes fix on the one lit window in the dark tower, the window where a prisoner stood — and she raised one hand. Not a wave. Something older than a wave. The gesture a helmsman makes: *hold your course.*

Vesper pressed his bandaged hand to the glass in answer.

And then, far off, at the landward end of the causeway, he saw something that made his heart turn over: figures at the bridge checkpoint. More figures than the checkpoint had ever held. Fishermen, net-menders, boys, old Tam, the ferryman Brand with his oilskin streaming — gathered, not dispersing, gathering the way water gathers behind a failing weir. And at their center, unmistakable even at this distance, the Widow Osei had arrived, and the crowd closed around her like a hand closing around a coal.

The storm was still blowing. The wreck still lay on the shoal with its dead beneath it. The lamp above Vesper's head was cold, and would stay cold until hands freer than his lit it again.

But the island had seen. And what the island had seen, the island was now, at last, in the grey light of the morning after, beginning to *do*.

Down at the checkpoint, a torch was lit against the rain. Then another.
