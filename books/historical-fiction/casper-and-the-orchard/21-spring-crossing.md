## 21. Spring Crossing

The bridge opened on a Tuesday, with the ice going out of the channel in great groaning plates that ground against the pilings like an old man turning over in bed. Vesper Zhou heard it from the lighthouse gallery before he heard anything else — that low tectonic complaint of winter surrendering — and he stood for a long moment with his bandaged hands resting on the rail, careful even now not to press too hard, and listened to the sound the world made when it decided to go on.

He had not slept. None of them had slept, really, since Adeyemi's notebook came out on the bridge and Widow Maren had said *I have been robbed* in a voice that made ninety years of patience sound like a drawn blade. But the insomnia of the past weeks had been a watchman's insomnia, all wire and waiting. This morning's was something gentler and stranger: the restlessness of a man standing in a room whose walls have just been taken away.

Below him, the island was already moving. He could see the fishing families coming up from the harbor in their Sunday clothes and their working boots, because they had not been able to decide which the day required and so had worn both. Old Ostergaard boys carried the returned deeds before them on a plank like a wedding cake — the mainland inspector had insisted they be handed back publicly, all forty-one of them, in the order Corren's office had taken them, and someone had found ink to re-enter each name in a fair hand. Vesper could not read the names from this distance. He didn't need to. He knew the shape of them, the way you know the shape of your own harbor in the dark.

The magistrate's men were already gone. They had been put on the morning boat with their collars turned up, and no one had spat at them or thrown anything, which Vesper thought was somehow more terrible than if they had. The island had simply watched them go the way water watches a stone it has decided not to carry.

Down the spiral stair, one careful step at a time — his boots seemed very loud in the stairwell, loud as they had been the night the soldiers searched his kitchen while he kept the light above their heads — Vesper let himself out into the morning.

The air smelled of thaw and salt and, faintly, of blossom.

He stopped in the lee of the tower and looked at the orchard tree. Casper, Nell called it; had named it for reasons she never explained and he had stopped asking after, because some names arrive the way weather arrives, without permission and entirely correctly. It stood where it had always stood, crooked and unregarded at the edge of the lighthouse ground, half wild thing and half monument. All winter it had held its bare branches up against the gales like a man keeping his arms raised against a sky that would not listen. And now, absurdly, impossibly, it was covered in white.

Not snow. The season for that was over. Blossom — early, reckless, pushed out by some private calendar the tree kept regardless of storms or magistrates or dying clerks. The petals trembled in the wind off the channel, and every few moments the wind took a handful and carried them out over the water, a slow white procession heading for the mainland whether anyone escorted it or not.

Vesper stood before it the way he had once stood before his wife's chair in the months after, unable to sit and unable to leave.

"You're early," he told it. His voice cracked on the second word, and he let it.

* * *

The ceremony on the bridge was brief, because the islanders did not have much appetite for speeches and Adeyemi, to his credit, sensed it. He was a broad, patient man with the unhurried manner of someone who had spent twenty years reading other men's ledgers and finding the same rot in all of them, and he read the seizure orders aloud in the flat voice of a court record while the wind pulled at his notes. When he reached the entry for the Halloran boat — the first deed taken, two winters ago, from a family that had fished those waters for four generations — a sound went through the crowd that was not quite a cheer. It was lower than that. It was the sound of a long-held breath finally being let out, and it moved down the line of people like current moving under ice.

Corren himself had gone before them, in the custody of two inspectors from the capital, in a boat better than any fisherman on the island had ever been allowed to charter. Vesper had watched him from the gallery, as he had watched everything that winter from the gallery. The magistrate had stepped into the boat neatly, without resistance, adjusting his coat against the spray, and for one moment he had looked up at the light — Vesper's light, the light that had burned without him for three weeks now, kept by rotation, Osei's people climbing the tower in pairs — and something had passed across his face that Vesper chose, deliberately, never to name. Not triumph. Not even anger. Recognition, perhaps, of the arithmetic finally done: that power is only ever a loan, and the interest comes due in spring.

The deeds were distributed from a plank table set up at the bridge's landward end. Widow Osei stood beside the table, and it was she who called each name, her voice carrying the way it always carried, level and warm at once, a hearth-fire voice that had organized hideouts and forged passes and forgiven betrayals within the space of one hard winter. When she called "Halloran," the whole Halloran family came forward together, and Adeyemi handed the deed to the eldest daughter rather than the father, and Vesper saw Osei's mouth move very slightly — approving, unsurprised.

His own name was not called. He hadn't expected it to be. A lighthouse keeper's post was not a deed; it was a duty, and duties had changed hands this spring in a way no ledger recorded. When the last name had been read and the plank table was empty, Osei looked across the crowd and found him — she always found him; she had a talent for locating quiet men in crowds — and did not call out or wave. She simply inclined her head, a small precise bow, keeper to keeper. He returned it.

Nell found him afterward, pushing through legs and skirts with the dog flowing behind her like brown water. She was eleven now, or insisted she was, and she had her mother's way of looking at him when she wanted to know something he would not volunteer.

"Are you going to the tree?" she said.

"I am."

"With your hands?"

He looked down at the bandages. The burns had healed better than the mainland doctor had promised and worse than Vesper had hoped; the left hand obeyed him again, mostly. The right still spoke its own language, a stiff grammar of scar tissue, and the doctor had been firm about what it should not be asked to do. Digging was on the list. Digging was near the top of the list.

"With my hands," Vesper said.

Nell considered this. Then she turned to the dog and said, "Wait here," in the tone she used when she meant *follow me at a discreet distance*, and fell into step beside him.

They walked the shore path together without speaking, which was their oldest arrangement and needed no renewal. The tide was out far enough to show the black teeth of the rocks where, in the dark of February, a coal barge had come apart in the night while the lamp stood dark — the night Corren's men had extinguished it, the night the island learned exactly what his rule cost in hulls and men. Two of the crew had lived by swimming. One had not. There was a cross now, rough-hewn by Halloran hands, and someone freshened its paint each week though nobody admitted to it. Vesper nodded to it as they passed, the small nod of one keeper acknowledging another's dead.

At the foot of the orchard tree the ground was soft with thaw, and the seedlings were waiting.

Casper had planted them — Vesper had pieced this together slowly, from the journal Osei had found, from the clerk's own hand written in a script that grew shakier as the entries grew shorter. Six apple seedlings, started in tins in the customs house window, carried out at night and heeled into the sheltered lee below the tree's root line, there to wait for a spring their planter had privately doubted he would see. *If anyone reads this*, the last entry ran, *they are not mine. I took nothing that was mine. Let them go wherever they are planted next.*

Vesper set down the spade and the sack he had brought — sacking, boiled dinner scraps for the roots, the small farmer's kit he had assembled from memory of his father-in-law's habits — and knelt, and the kneeling itself was a negotiation with his hands.

"Tell me which ones," Nell said.

"The strongest two." His voice was steadier now. "The rest stay. A tree shouldn't give up everything it has."

She was down on her knees beside him before he finished, sleeves pushed up, reading the six small stems with the absolute seriousness she brought to ships' lights and stray animals. She rejected the first on sight — "too thin, look" — and the fourth — "the wind bent it, see how it learned wrong" — and settled at last on a pair near the wall side, sturdy as thumbs, their buds already swelling the same reckless white as the parent tree's.

Vesper took up the spade.

His right hand closed around the haft and the scars pulled, and he adjusted, and they pulled again, and he found after a moment that if he held it lower, nearer the blade, taking the strain through the wrist instead of the fingers, the tool did most of what he asked of it. He cut the first circle of sod. The earth came up dark and cold-smelling, full of worms turning lazily away from the light, and he worked the spade with the patience he had spent nine years learning at the top of a tower: not strength but repetition, repetition until repetition became result.

It hurt. He let it. There was a rightness to the pain that he did not examine too closely — the burns earned in a fire he had entered on purpose, carrying pages out of a building that men were paid to make certain no page ever left. His wife had died of an illness no one could have argued with, a sickness that came and took and offered no one a choice. These hands had chosen. He found he was grateful for the difference in a way he suspected he would spend years failing to explain to anyone, including himself.

"Roots," Nell warned, peering into the hole. "You're near the big roots."

"I know. That's why here. They'll hold onto the old roots, and the old roots will teach them where the water is." He lifted the first seedling, cradled in its plug of earth, and Nell received it into the sack with both hands, solemn as an acolyte. "Trees are like people that way. They do better transplanted with something familiar."

"Is that why you're taking them? Because we're going?"

Vesper rested on the spade handle and looked at his daughter. Her hair was coming loose from its braid the way it always did, and there was dirt already on her chin, and behind her the orchard tree shed another slow drift of white onto the ground that held a clerk's bones.

"No," he said. And then, because lying to Nell had never once worked and he was out of practice anyway: "Partly. Your mother used to say belonging wasn't a place, it was a cargo. You load it and carry it. But—" He worked the spade again, cutting the second hole, deeper this time. "But a cargo wants a destination. And I find I can't look at this island anymore without seeing every yard of it at once. The checkpoint. The causeway. The spot in the water where I nearly went under. Grief needs a smaller window, maybe. Or a newer one."

Nell absorbed this with the gravity of a harbormaster. "So we'll see the light from over there?"

"From the point, on clear nights. Osei says the rotation will keep it lit every night, all year. She's got the Hallorans trained on the clockwork already."

"It won't be the same."

"No," Vesper agreed. "It'll be different. Different isn't the same as worse." He paused, wiping his forearm across his face. "Most of the time."

The second seedling came free more easily than the first, its roots already exploratory, feeling along the edges of its tin for a world bigger than the tin. Nell carried both to the sack and arranged them with blankets of dock leaves, and the dog — who had ignored the instruction to wait, as instructed — came and put his nose briefly against the sacking and then sat back on his haunches with the satisfaction of an official sealing a document.

* * *

They filled the holes and tamped the earth and watered it from the kettle Vesper had brought, and by then the sun was high enough that the channel glittered like hammered tin, and it was time.

The ferry made its spring crossing at the turn of the tide, and half the island had gathered at the slip to see the Zhous off — or to see the seedlings off, which amounted to the same thing and was discussed in those terms by everyone, loudly, so that nobody would have to say anything about the man with the bandaged hands. Widow Osei embraced Nell for a long count and Vesper for a short one, which was exactly correct. She pressed something into his palm as they parted: a folded paper, sealed.

"For the first night," she said. "When the light comes up over the water and you're standing there like a man at his own funeral. You'll want something to read besides your own thoughts. My advice: don't read it till then."

"What is it?"

"A list," said Osei, "of every keeper on the rotation, with their nights marked. Forty-one families, Vesper. The lamp won't so much as flicker for want of you. That's not a consolation. It's an order."

The ferryman — who had once, in a winter he would never discuss again, chosen caution over courage at his own jetty, and who had since ferried two ledgers and one wounded conscience past three checkpoints without charging so much as a copper — cast off the lines with unnecessary ceremony and called the crossing open.

Vesper stood at the stern rail with the sack between his feet and watched the island assemble itself behind them: the tower, white-capped against a sky rinsed clean; the orchard tree in full reckless bloom, throwing petals into the wind like a bride's thrown coins; the roofs, the drying nets, the cross on the rocks with its new paint catching the sun. Nell leaned beside him, and the dog leaned against Nell, and the wake drew a long pale seam between them and everything that had broken them and remade them.

Halfway across, the light station passed behind the orchard tree's crown, and for a moment — only a moment — the blossoms seemed to stand in for the lamp itself, a white fire burning in daylight, unattended, unbudgeted, entirely sufficient.

"There," Nell said suddenly, pointing. "You can already see where the point is. Where we'll live. You can see it *from here*."

And she was right. On the mainland shore, due west, the headland they had chosen showed clear in the spring light — a fold of green ground above the water, near enough that on a clear night a lamp burning anywhere along it would share its horizon, its whole sky, with the light on the island. Close enough, Veper thought, that the two places would lean toward each other across the channel the way the seedlings would lean toward the old roots underground. Carried, not cut free. Planted forward, not buried behind.

The wind shifted, came off the island astern of them, and delivered the last of it: a single white petal that had ridden the air all the way out from the orchard and now settled, without ceremony, on the mouth of the sack. Nell reached to brush it away and Vesper caught her wrist, gently, with his bad hand — and it surprised him, how well it obeyed — and shook his head.

"Let it travel," he said.

The ferry pressed on toward the mainland, and behind them, faint over the water, came the sound of the islanders cheering something at the slip — the crossing, the spring, the deeds, the mere fact of motion after a winter of standing still. Vesper did not turn to look. He kept his eyes fixed ahead on the green fold of shore rising slowly out of the haze, the spade across his knees, the seedlings breathing softly in their sacking at his feet, and in his coat pocket, sealed and waiting for nightfall, the list of forty-one keepers who would hold the light over the water behind him — every night, all year, whether he watched or not.
