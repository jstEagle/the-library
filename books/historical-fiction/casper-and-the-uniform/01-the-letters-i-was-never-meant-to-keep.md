## 1. The Letters I Was Never Meant to Keep

The first thing anyone learned about Elodie Blackwood was that she could drive. The second thing, if they bothered to learn anything at all, was that she drove for Miss Verity Ashcombe, which meant she was either very brave or very foolish, and no one wasted much time deciding which.

That suited her fine. Invisibility was a skill like any other, and Elodie had practised it the way other women practised piano — daily, diligently, with an eye toward eventual mastery. A chauffeur in cap and goggles is furniture with an engine. People said things in front of furniture. People handed furniture envelopes sealed with wax and marked *private* and never once wondered whether the furniture could read.

Elodie could read beautifully.

* * *

Tuesday began, as Tuesdays did, with frost on the windscreen of the Hupmobile and Verity emerging from the Russell Square house at seven sharp, coat buttoned to the throat, a sheaf of correspondence under one arm and a look on her face that meant Parliament had disappointed her again before breakfast.

"Kensington first," Verity said, folding herself into the passenger seat. "Then the Post Office. Then whatever Mrs. Pankhurst's people have left in my letterbox overnight."

"Very good, miss."

"I wish you'd stop calling me miss."

"I wish you'd stop getting arrested," Elodie said, and cranked the engine.

Verity laughed despite herself. That was the shape of them: eleven years apart and a whole world apart in consequence, Verity Ashcombe whose name appeared in newspapers under headlines like MILITANT SPEAKER RELEASED FROM HOLLOWAY, and Elodie, who appeared in photographs only as a blur of cap at the edge of the frame. She had stopped minding. Mostly. She told herself she had stopped minding, which in the practice of invisibility counted as advanced coursework.

She read the mail at stops. Not all of it — she had rules, though the rules grew more flexible each month. Nothing addressed by hand to Verity personally; that felt like eavesdropping on a friendship. But circulars, committee minutes, donations wrapped in notes of encouragement from ladies in Manchester and Brighton — those she read at kerbsides while Verity argued inside someone's drawing room, and she read them the way a starving person eats crumbs, without shame and without stopping.

It was at the third stop, outside a Kensington house with geraniums gasping in the cold, that she found the letter that undid everything.

No return address. The handwriting cramped and hurried, ink blotted as though written standing up or in fear. Inside, a single page.

*Miss Ashcombe — Forgive the anonymity; you will understand why. I was clerk to a supply regiment stationed near the Colonel's command in '76 and again in '80. There are men alive who saw what happened after the engagement at — but no. Names in ink are dangerous things. Ask instead why Colonel Fairhurst's dispatches from that winter were never published entire. Ask why the survivors of the column were dispersed to postings so remote they might as well be buried. Ask what his family pays yearly to keep quiet. He will stand for Parliament in the spring. If he stands unopposed by truth, then God help us all, and God forgive me for writing no more than this.*

There was no signature. There was a faint smear at the bottom right corner where a name had been written and wetted away with a fingertip.

Elodie read it three times. The engine ticked. Frost crept up the glass. Somewhere down the street a costermonger cried his wares into the grey morning, and she sat in the driving seat of a car full of history and understood, with a strange lurch, that she was holding history — actual history, not the kind in books with gilded spines that happened to dukes and dead men, but the raw wet thing itself, still bleeding through the envelope.

Colonel Fairhurst. She knew the name the way everyone did: from illustrated papers, from the jubilee honours lists, from a famous photograph of him on a white horse with medals on his chest like a breastplate. A war hero. A national treasure. A man about to be elected to govern the very country his legend had been manufactured to serve.

Someone wanted him unmade. Someone was too frightened to do it themselves, so they'd posted the task to Verity Ashcombe, who collected lost causes the way other ladies collected fans.

Elodie folded the letter along its original creases and put it back in the envelope, and her hands were not entirely steady, and she noticed that about herself and filed it away as evidence against her own composure.

When Verity came out forty minutes later, flushed with small victory over some Kensington hostess, Elodie handed over the day's post with the Fairhurst letter buried in the middle of the stack, and watched from the corner of her eye as Verity read it at the next kerb and went very still, and then very quiet, and said nothing for six miles.

That night it rained.

* * *

The house in Russell Square slept badly when it slept at all. There were always women coming and going — typists, refugees from worse lodgings, one memorable fortnight a lady just out of prison who played the violin at midnight and wept between movements. Elodie kept a room at the top, narrow as a train compartment, and she liked it precisely because nobody knocked.

She was awake past one, reading — she was always reading — when she heard the knocker.

Not the confident double-rap of a colleague. A single knock, soft, almost apologetic, then the sound of feet moving quickly away down the area steps and out into the wet street.

She went down in her dressing gown with the gas turned low and stood in the hall listening. Verity was in Manchester; the typists slept like the dead; the house exhaled around her. Elodie opened the door on the chain, then all the way.

Nothing. Rain silvering the pavement. A lamp flickering at the square's far side. And then, at the foot of the steps, something moved.

A child. Six years old perhaps, maybe seven, standing perfectly still in the rain-soaked dark as if he'd been placed there rather than arrived, the way parcels are placed. He wore a soldier's greatcoat, a real one, officer's quality wool, so large on him that the hem dragged the wet stone and the sleeves had been rolled and rolled again into thick cuffs at his wrists. His face was pale and composed and utterly unreadable, the face of a child who has been instructed to be brave and has decided compliance is simpler than argument.

In his fist, held out toward her, sodden already, a note.

"Casper," the boy said, when she crouched to his level. His voice was small and careful. "I'm meant to say my name is Casper."

"Meant to?"

"That's all I'm meant to say."

She took the note. The paper was good stock, expensive, the handwriting elegant and unhurried — nothing like the cramped anonymous scrawl from the Kensington letter. Five words only:

*Forget this face. Forget tonight.*

The boy watched her read it. Rain ran off the coat's collar in little streams. Behind his eyes something was being held very carefully in place, the way you carry water in your cupped hands, knowing most of it will go but trying anyway.

"Are you going to forget?" he asked.

And here is the thing Elodie would return to for the rest of her life, the hinge on which everything afterward swung: she could have done it. It would have been easy. Take the child inside, warm him, feed him, let the morning absorb him into some workhouse queue or parish ledger, burn the note, tell no one, be furniture again. Furniture survived. Furniture lived long comfortable lives.

Instead she looked at the note, and the elegant handwriting, and the boy in the dead soldier's coat, and she thought of the letter smouldering in Verity's bag somewhere on a train from Manchester, and she thought: *someone wrote this. Someone powerful wrote five words tonight and sent a child to deliver them, because children cannot testify.*

"No," she said, and stepped back to let him pass. "Come inside. You're freezing."

She got him milk. She got him dry socks from the rag bag, and set the greatcoat to dry by the kitchen range, and while he drank with both hands around the cup she should have thrown the note into the coals. It would have taken a second. The fire was right there, obliging, orange, hungry.

Instead she smoothed the wet paper flat on the deal table and looked at it a long moment — *forget this face, forget tonight* — and then she folded it small, smaller, and slipped it into the pocket of her dressing gown, where it lay against her hip like a coal that hadn't caught yet.

Complicity, she thought. Or evidence. She suspected, even then, that the two looked identical from the inside.

The boy fell asleep in the kitchen chair with his mouth open. Elodie covered him with her own blanket and sat across from him until the sky went the colour of dishwater, and when the others came down at dawn and found them — driver and foundling, keeper and kept — she had only one explanation ready, and it was true, and it changed nothing and everything:

"He's ours now. Somebody left him, and I read the note."

Verity would be home by evening. There would be questions with teeth in them.

Elodie touched the folded paper in her pocket and waited for morning to arrive.
