## 3. What the Victors Burn

The copying took four nights.

I want to be honest about how it was done, because honesty is the whole point of what follows: I did not steal originals. I stole something better. Originals can be denied, disowned, mourned as lost. Copies, made carefully and kept in many places, are a hydra — cut off one head and two more rise up hissing your name.

The Fairhurst letters lived in a deed box at Halloway & Pruitt, solicitors, and I learned their location the way I had learned everything worth knowing in three years of driving for Verity Ashcombe: by listening. The senior partner, Mr. Pruitt himself, kept the box in a cabinet behind his clerk's room, and on Thursday evenings the clerks went to the pub at half past six and did not return until the gas lamps were lit. On the first Thursday I sat outside in the car with my collar up and my heart going like a sewing machine, and when the last clerk ambled off whistling *Pack Up Your Troubles*, I walked into the office of one of London's oldest legal firms wearing my most respectable driving coat, and I asked the elderly porter if I might wait indoors for Mrs. Ashcombe, who was due any minute. He knew Verity's name. Everyone knew Verity's name that year. He fetched me tea.

I was alone with the cabinet for eleven minutes. I know because the clock on the mantel counted every one of them aloud, like a witness.

I copied fast. My shorthand was good enough for the movement's minutes and it would have to be good enough for this. The first letter was dated the eleventh of October, 1906, from Colonel Edmund Fairhurst to his brother Gerald, and it began, *You will destroy this as you destroyed the others,* and went on to describe — in the flat, tidy language of a man discussing drainage — an action at a place called Kessab Ridge, where a village that had already surrendered was put to the torch with its people inside, and where the official report recorded a gallant engagement against armed insurgents instead. There were nine letters. Three concerned the burning. Four concerned a woman named Sarah Casper, a laundress at Fairhurst's garrison town, and a son born to her in 1904 whom the colonel acknowledged in writing and then paid, through these very solicitors, to forget. The last letter instructed Gerald — now Lord Fairhurst, now a man with a seat in the Lords and a career to protect — to ensure that *if the boy ever surfaces, he is returned to obscurity before he can be produced as evidence.*

*Produced as evidence.* They had said it themselves. A child, filed between a burned village and a falsified report, in the same tidy hand.

I copied all nine letters into my shorthand book, and then I copied them again onto tissue paper in pencil, and I folded the tissue into the linings of three different parcels in three different delivery bags. By the time the porter knocked on the clerk's door to see whether the lady wanted more tea, I was sitting by the window reading yesterday's newspaper like a woman with nothing in her but patience.

* * *

Hiding the copies was the easy part; I had been hiding things in delivery rounds for years. A parcel of pamphlets looks like a parcel of pamphlets. A hatbox bound for a milliner in Pimlico can hold a great deal beneath its ribbon. Over four days I distributed the evidence across London the way other women hid suffrage literature under floorboards: one set of pages sewn into the hem of a dress waiting at Mrs. Okafor's dressmaking establishment in Camberwell; one set inside the hollowed spine of a hymnal destined for a chapel that owed Verity a favour; the third set I kept on me, because someone had to, and because I no longer entirely trusted anyone else to.

It was on the fourth day that I noticed the man in the grey bowler.

He was good, I will grant him that. He never stood twice in the same spot, never let me catch him at a corner twice running. But I had spent three years learning to see without being seen, and I recognised the craft because it was mine. He appeared outside Halloway & Pruitt the morning after my first visit, reading a newspaper he held slightly too high. He was across the street from the chapel in Bermondsey. He was at the end of the mews behind our house at dusk, smoking a cigarette down to its cork filter with the deliberation of a man being paid by the hour.

Mr. Pruitt's man. Or Lord Fairhurst's. It hardly mattered which; they were the same hand now.

On Friday I drove Verity to a deputation at Westminster, and the grey bowler followed us the whole way at a distance so precise I could have measured it with a tape. Verity saw him too. She watched him in the wing mirror for a long moment, her jaw set in that way that meant she was doing arithmetic about consequences.

"He knows," she said.

"He suspects," I said. "There's a difference. Suspicion can be starved."

"Elodie." She turned to look at me properly, and I saw how tired she was, how much this year had cost her. "If they take those letters from you, they will not stop at the letters."

"I know."

"And if they take *you*—"

"They won't." I heard how certain I sounded, and I was grateful to whatever part of me had manufactured it. "I've been invisible for three years, Verity. It turns out invisibility is a skill."

She almost smiled. Then the crowd around Parliament Square swallowed us, and there was no more time for anything but the work.

* * *

They arrested her on Saturday.

I should have known — the Home Office had been circling her for weeks over the window-smashing campaign, and she had refused, flatly and magnificently, to moderate a single word. But knowing a storm is coming is not the same as being ready when it breaks. The summons came while she was speaking on the green at Bethnal Green, and she went with the police quietly, chin up, telling the constables exactly what she thought of a government that jailed women for asking to be citizens. The crowd roared. The newspapers would have it in Monday's editions under a headline about disorder.

I drove home alone with the empty seat beside me feeling like a wound.

Casper was in the kitchen with Mrs. Dunn, our neighbour who came in to mind him, and when I came through the door he read my face the way children do — instantly, totally, without mercy.

"She's been taken," he said. Not asked.

"Yes."

"Are they going to take me too?"

And there it was, the whole terror of the thing, spoken plainly by a boy of nine in an apron too big for him, butter still on his fingers. I knelt down in front of his chair and took his sticky hands in mine and told him the truth, because I had decided somewhere on the drive home that this child was finished being lied to by adults.

"No one is taking you," I said. "Do you hear me? Not the Fairhursts' solicitor, not their man in the bowler, not anyone. You are not a parcel to be delivered or a problem to be buried. You are a person, and what was done to you and to your mother is going to go into the record, in ink, where nobody can burn it."

"What record?"

"The kind that survives."

He studied me with those grave eyes, and then he nodded once, the way soldiers nod, and went back to his bread, and I understood that I had just made a promise in front of a witness.

* * *

That night I could not sleep. I sat at the kitchen table with the shorthand book and the tissue copies spread before me, and I heard the clock counting, the way the clock at Halloway & Pruitt had counted, eleven minutes at a time. Somewhere out in the dark the grey bowler was smoking his hourly cigarette at the mouth of the mews, patient as debt.

I thought about everything I had ever read and never signed. Three years of other people's correspondence passing through my hands — love letters, death notices, Cabinet memoranda, the raw unedited material of history — and always I had told myself the same soothing story: that I was a courier, and couriers are not responsible for the cargo. That history was written by the people whose names went at the bottom, and my job was only to carry it from them to the printer intact.

But Verity was in Holloway. The movement's hands were full. The inquiry into the conduct of the war — the one Lord Fairhurst's friends had carefully scoped so that it examined everything except Kessab Ridge — was sitting in closed session, and I had learned from a leaked schedule that it would receive final submissions within the fortnight and then fold itself up like a tablecloth. After that, the letters meant nothing. After that, the victors' version stood forever, and a burned village stayed a gallant engagement, and a boy named Casper stayed a face everyone had agreed to forget.

Terror, I discovered, sitting alone at that table with the gas turned low, is a fuel like any other. It burns hot if you feed it. I fed it.

I took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote at the top, in my clearest hand: *Statement of Elodie Blackwood, of Clarendon Mews, respecting documents in her possession.* Then I stopped, because I realised I was smiling, and I could not remember having smiled since the arrest. It was not happiness. It was something older than happiness — the feeling of a door opening after years of standing in corridors.

They thought they were hunting a driver. Let them hunt a driver. Drivers are invisible. But come the day the inquiry sat, I would walk into that room and stand up in front of counsel and peers and press, and I would say: *my name is Elodie Blackwood, and I am here to enter these letters into the record.* And there was nothing in any solicitor's bag of threats for a woman who had decided to become evidence herself.

I worked until dawn, drafting, copying, distributing. The hymnal had gone to Bermondsey. The dress was in Camberwell. The third set stayed against my ribs, warm as a second heartbeat.

On Monday morning the morning paper arrived, and Mrs. Dunn shrieked, and Casper ran in from the yard with his sleeves flapping. I read the headline twice to be sure. The inquiry had announced, abruptly and without explanation, that it would sit in public for one additional session — a single day, at the end of the month, for *newly raised matters*. Somebody inside had grown a conscience, or lost one, or been leaned upon by persons unknown.

One day. One slot on the calendar, and Lord Fairhurst's people would fill the room with counsel and calumny, and I would have to fill it instead with the truth.

I looked at Casper, watching me from the doorway with the whole of his future in his face, and I picked up my driving gloves.

"Well then," I said. "We'd better get ready."
