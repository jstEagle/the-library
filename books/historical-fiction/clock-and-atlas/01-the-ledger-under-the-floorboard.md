## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

There were forty-one beds in the convalescent ward of the garrison hospital at Verenmoor, and by the middle of January Oscar Amberdale knew every one of them the way a mother knows her children — which ones complained, which ones lied about their pain, which ones hoarded bread under their pillows as though the war might come back for them specifically.

Bed thirty-one had been empty for six days. That was where Private Ansel Kray should have been.

Oscar stood at the washroom window with her hands in a basin of water too hot to be comfortable, because comfort was a thing she distrusted in small doses. Outside, the river ran grey between its white banks, patient as a clerk waiting for a form to be filled in. Somewhere upriver, men were already talking about ice. They always talked about ice from November onward, the way old women talked about other old women's health: with relish, and a certain professional envy.

"Your water's gone cold, Sister," said Corporal Denny from bed nine, who was not blind and not stupid and had learned that the way to be noticed in a ward of forty men was to narrate.

"The water is exactly as hot as your attitude deserves," Oscar said, and carried it over anyway.

She had been twelve years at Verenmoor. She had arrived with one trunk, a nurse's certificate that had cost her three teeth's worth of worry to obtain, and a face that people trusted immediately and then forgot, which she had discovered was the most useful combination of qualities a woman could own. In twelve years she had buried three head matrons, outlasted two commandants, survived an epidemic of typhoid, a fire in the laundry, and the appointment of Matron Hessel, who believed — sincerely, piously, insufferably — that a ward ran on discipline, whereas Oscar knew it ran on small bribes, selective deafness, and knowing which orderly drank.

The men called her Sister because they had to. Privately they called her the Magpie, because she collected things. She collected extra blankets from stores by claiming damp. She collected sugar by trading her tea ration to a cook who preferred tea. She collected names.

That last part nobody knew about.

* * *

The official record said that Private Ansel Kray, 3rd Sappers, was *unaccounted*. Two words, typed on the sixth of December, filed somewhere in a room Oscar would never see, by a hand that had never held his wrist while he died.

He had died on the fourth of December, in the corridor outside the surgical theatre, because there had been no bed and no time and because Sergeant Brell, who was supposed to fetch the stretcher-bearers, had instead gone to find a lieutenant to complain to about the stretcher-bearers. Oscar had sat with him for eleven minutes. She knew it was eleven minutes because the ward clock was loud and honest in a way most people weren't.

Ansel Kray had been nineteen. He had a scar through one eyebrow from falling off a hay wagon at fourteen — he'd told her this while she held a bowl for him, apropos of nothing, the way dying boys distributed their biographies to whoever happened to be near. He had a girl named Liv in a town he described as "nothing, absolutely nothing, two streets and a church," and he had made Oscar promise — he had gripped her sleeve with the shocking strength of the nearly gone and made her promise — to write and tell Liv it had been quick.

Oscar had promised. She always promised. It was a clinical intervention, like morphine; you administered it and did not delude yourself about the cure.

Then the paperwork had come around like weather, and some clerk with better handwriting than conscience had written *unaccounted*, and the space where Ansel Kray had been closed over smoothly, like water behind a passing barge. No family to notify, apparently. No Liv on file. A man could spend nineteen years assembling a whole self — scar, sweetheart, hay wagon, church — and the army would misplace him between two lines of a ledger.

Well. Oscar thought, drying her hands on her apron. Between two lines of *a* ledger.

* * *

Her room was in the east wing, shared with the linen store on one side and the dispensary on the other, a location she had requested on the grounds of "being near supplies" and maintained on the grounds that nobody else wanted it. The floorboards were original to the building, which meant they were uneven, which meant that beneath the third board from the window — the one that lifted with the flat of a knife if you knew the angle — there was a cavity full of dust, one dead mouse of historical interest, and a ledger.

It was not a beautiful object. She had bought it herself, from the stationer in town, paying cash and saying it was for inventory. It was a plain ruled book, quarter-bound, the sort used for tracking coal or boots. In its pages, in her neat, upright, deliberately unremarkable hand, were four hundred and eleven names.

Not all dead. Some were simply gone — deserters the reports listed as fallen, so that no one would go looking; civilians recorded as enemy casualties so that a colonel's arithmetic would balance; a sergeant struck from the rolls for something that had actually been done by a captain. Four hundred and eleven entries, each dated, each with whatever she knew: a wound, a word, a last sentence, a scar from a hay wagon.

Twelve years of them. She sometimes tried to remember when it had started, and could only ever get back to a feeling rather than a date: standing over the first list she was ordered to burn, watching the paper curl, understanding with perfect clarity that the fire was not destroying anything at all, merely moving the names from one kind of keeping to another. The ash kept nothing. So somebody would have to.

She waited until the ward bell rang for evening medications, until she had done her rounds, argued with the pharmacist about a mislabelled bottle, told Corporal Denny that no, he could not have brandy, it was medicinal the same way a horse was a songbird, and checked twice that Matron Hessel had gone to her supper. Then she went to her room, shut the door, put the chair against it — habit, pure habit; the door locked, but the chair didn't know that — and knelt on the cold boards with the knife.

The third board came up with its familiar sigh.

Here is what she would have said, if anyone had ever asked her why: that a name written nowhere is a name that never existed, and existence is a debt we owe each other. But nobody asked, and besides, she didn't entirely believe in reasons anymore. Reasons implied a choice made once. This was not a choice made once. This was a thing she did the way she washed her hands — before she could think, because thinking came after, and after was where the dread lived.

She opened the ledger to the next blank line and wrote:

*Kray, Ansel. Pvt., 3rd Sappers. d. 4 Dec., corridor, surg. theatre. 19 yrs. Scar, L. eyebrow (hay cart). Sweetheart: Liv, town unnamed, "two streets and a church." Promised letter.*

She looked at the last words a long moment. The nib had left a small blot, like a period insisting on itself.

She had not written the letter. Twelve years of nursing taught you which promises were medicine and which were debts, and she had filed this one — she admitted it now, kneeling on the floor with her knees aching, she had filed it under *medicine*, because the boy had needed it more than Liv ever would. Except that now he was in the ledger, and the ledger was where debts went to be counted, and counted debts wanted paying.

"Two streets and a church," she murmured. "That narrows it to half the country."

But she tore a corner of scrap paper anyway and wrote: *find Liv.* She slipped it inside the front cover, where three other slips of paper lived — other unfinished business, other ghosts on layaway. Then she put the ledger back, pressed the board down until it seated with a soft knock, swept the dust from her apron, and stood.

Her reflection hung in the dark window: a woman of forty-odd, greying sensibly, dressed in sensible wool, holding a knife for lifting floorboards. If you saw her on the street you would think: hospital matron, or colonel's widow, or someone's dependable aunt. You would not think: keeper of four hundred and twelve names. That was the point. She had built the disguise out of twelve years of being exactly what she appeared, until the appearance and the woman had fused like ice over running water, and some nights, lying awake, she could not say which was the surface and which was the current underneath.

The dread, when it came, came quietly, as it always did — not the fear of discovery, though that lived in the house too, but the older fear: that she had become a person whose truest work happened alone, on her knees, in a hole in the floor.

She washed her hands. She went down to check the ward one last time.

* * *

Bed thirty-one had been given, temporarily, to a transfer from the fever hut, a gunner with a broken leg who snored in B flat. Oscar adjusted his blanket without waking him and paused, as she always did, at the little rack of bed-cards by the door. Names, ranks, complaints. The army's version of a soul: four lines and a number.

Matron Hessel found her there, buttoned into her coat, smelling faintly of the supper she had clearly enjoyed more than any meal served to the staff.

"Ward's tidy," Hessel said, in the tone of a woman reporting a miracle.

"It usually is."

"Hm." Hessel drew on her second glove. "Commandant wants a list of long-stay patients by Friday. And you'll be pleased to hear we're getting a proper admission tomorrow. Colonel Vasseur, down from the divisional staff. Pneumonia, they say. They want him kept quiet."

The name arrived in Oscar's chest like a dropped surgical instrument — cold, metal, familiar. She kept her face doing what faces did, which was nothing.

"A colonel," she said. "In my ward."

"He'll have the end bay, screened off. And Amberdale—" Hessel paused, and for once looked briefly less like a monument to discipline and more like a tired woman in a bad coat. "Whatever the man was at the front, he's a sick old officer now. Mind your bedside manner."

"I don't have one," Oscar said. "I have a routine."

Hessel left. Oscar stood a moment longer by the bed-cards, listening to the ward breathe — forty-one chests rising and falling, the clock clearing its throat on the hour, the wind testing the bridge chains down at the crossing with a sound like someone learning to whistle.

Vasseur. Here. In her ward, coughing, helpless, alive.

She thought of the ledger upstairs under the floorboard, warm in its hole like a heart in a chest. She thought of how many pages his name threaded through — not written plainly, never that, but there in the dates, the gaps, the entries marked *transf., Kolvik area* that stopped mid-list like a rope cut with a knife.

"Well," she said aloud, to the gunner, to the clock, to nobody. "Two streets and a church."

She turned down the lamp, and went up to her room, and did not sleep.
