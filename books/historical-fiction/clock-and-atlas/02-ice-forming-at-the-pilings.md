## 2. Ice Forming at the Pilings

The news arrived the way all important news arrived in Vrenholm: carried by a boy who had been paid not to carry it, and therefore told everyone within four minutes of crossing the bridge.

Oscar heard it from Marta, who heard it from the laundry line, where the garrison's sheets dried in a wind that could have carried them to the far bank unassisted if anyone had thought to ask.

"Bridge closes Friday," Marta said, pinning a pillowcase with the violence of a woman who had opinions about pillowcases. "Ice-master says the river will lock up by Sunday after. Last cart crossed this morning — a grain shipment, if you can believe it. They sent grain ahead of their own escape."

"The river doesn't need to escape," Oscar said, folding bandages. "It's been planning this all year."

"Don't joke. Half the town's already hoarding lamp oil." Marta shook out a sheet and glared at the horizon as though the frost were a personal enemy of long standing, which, in a sense, it was. Everything that came over the bridge in summer left in winter, and everything that stayed got smaller. "You'll want anything posted to go out tomorrow at the latest. After that it sits in the commandant's mailroom until April, and you know what happens to things in the commandant's mailroom."

"They get read."

"They get *annotated*," said Marta, with the contempt of a professional. "There is nothing so innocent that man cannot make it suspicious by writing in the margin."

Oscar folded another bandage, and her hands did not hurry, because twelve years of practice had taught her hands never to hurry. But something behind her ribs leaned forward, attentive, like a dog hearing its name spoken in another room.

Friday. The last post cart went out Thursday morning, if it went at all; the drivers liked to beat the ice by a full day and spend Wednesday night drinking to their own cleverness at the Ferryman's Rest. That gave her — she counted without letting herself count — five days.

Five days to decide whether the ledger left Vrenholm or wintered under her floorboard for a fourth consecutive year, gathering dust and courage by turns.

* * *

The ward was quiet that afternoon, which was itself a kind of weather. Six men convalescing, two orderlies playing cards with the solemnity of bishops, and Private Anders by the window teaching himself, with great patience, to hold a spoon again. The surgeons were pleased with Private Anders. His record said shrapnel. His record was wrong, but then, most records were, and he had asked her herself not to correct it, so she hadn't.

"Matron," he said, when she passed with the water jug. "Is it true about the bridge?"

"It's true."

"My wife will think I've died. I wrote her in October that I'd be home before the frost."

"You'll write her today," Oscar said, "and tell her the frost arrived early and you're alive despite it. She'll prefer the second part."

He grinned, lopsided, the way his whole face worked now. "You always say the practical thing, Matron."

"Somebody has to. It's an unfilled position."

She filled the jug at the tap in the corridor, and while the water ran she let herself do the arithmetic she had been refusing since breakfast. The ledger weighed perhaps three pounds wrapped in oilcloth. Small. A parcel of that size, addressed to her cousin Anneli in the capital, containing — on its face — a volume of sermons she had promised Anneli for two years, would pass through the commandant's mailroom without comment. Sermons were the one category of literature no censor had ever loved enough to read.

That was the plan. It had been the plan for three winters now. Each winter the parcel had gone into her coat pocket, and each winter she had stood at the post window on the last morning, and each winter some small, reasonable voice had observed that once the ledger left her hands she could no longer protect what was written in it, nor the people named there, nor — the voice was always honest about this last, being a small reasonable coward — herself.

And each winter she had put it back under the floorboard and called the decision caution, which is what fear calls itself when it wants to be invited back.

This year the voice had begun adding arguments. Ilse of the burning rosters was still alive somewhere. So was Captain Dree, whose signature sat on the second page like a stain. Men came over that bridge every autumn on rotation. If the ledger surfaced carelessly, it would not surface as truth; it would surface as provocation, and provocations were answered in Vrenholm by men with forms and handcuffs and, in the old days, worse.

But.

But the colonel was coming Thursday.

She had known it since Monday, when the requisition list crossed her desk: one private room prepared to senior-officer standard, extra blankets, the good china moved from stores to the officers' wing. *Col. E. Vasseur, en route to the capital for medical board, detained by medical necessity.* She had read the name twice, set down the paper, walked to the window, watched the river for a quarter hour doing nothing at all, and returned to work.

Twelve years, and the army proposed to deliver him to her ward as casually as a crate of dressings.

* * *

Wednesday evening the frost came down off the hills early, silvering the pilings where the river ran slowest. Oscar walked to the bank after supper, as half the town had, because watching a river prepare to close was the closest thing Vrenholm had to theater. Ice formed first at the pilings, thin plates of it spreading from the black timber like breath on glass, and the boatmen stood along the rail of the ferry landing making predictions they clearly hoped to lose.

"Sunday," one said. "Not before."

"Friday night," said another. "My knees haven't lied to me yet."

"Your knees told you your wife would come back," said a third, and there was laughter, the flat warm laughter of people who intended to be locked in a room together for five months and had decided to begin practicing goodwill now.

Oscar stood a little apart, hands in her coat pockets, and felt the shape of the parcel she had carried there all day like a stone. She had actually done it — wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, bound it in string, buried it inside the sermons, written *Anneli Iversen, Weiler Street, capital* on the front in her least memorable hand. It had taken her forty minutes and cost her, by her own estimate, a year of her pulse.

All day it had sat in her pocket growing heavier according to a physics she did not teach.

"Evening, Matron." The ferryman's boy, red-eared, breath steaming. "You posting something? Cart goes tomorrow, first light, if you've a mind."

"This?" She looked down as though surprised to find her own hand in her pocket. "I'd forgotten it was there."

Which was a lie, and a poor one, and the boy accepted it the way boys accept poor lies from their elders, with courtesy and total disbelief, and ran off along the rail to kick at the new ice.

Oscar looked at the river. Under the spreading plates it still moved, dark and enormous and patient, carrying everything it carried toward a sea she had never seen. Somewhere upstream, days away, Kolvik Bridge stood in its twelfth winter, rebuilt twice, photographed once, mourned officially never. The river remembered. Rivers kept better records than historians; they simply declined to testify until spring, and when they testified they drowned somebody, which limited their usefulness to the profession.

Thursday morning, then. First light. The cart, the driver, the road south, the mails, Anneli's shelf, and — eventually, impossibly, if the world consented — daylight.

Or Thursday morning, the floorboard, and Vasseur upstairs by noon, coughing in a room she had made ready with her own hands, and whatever he knew, and whatever he suspected she knew, sitting between them like a loaded thing on the tea tray.

She had rehearsed, over the years, what she would say to him if she ever saw him again. Most of the rehearsals ended with her saying nothing at all, which she had come to recognize as her signature move. Once, in a weaker December, she had rehearsed asking him why. She had abandoned that draft on the grounds that men like Vasseur answered questions of *why* with questions of *what business was it of yours*, and she had not survived twelve years by asking men like Vasseur anything.

But documents changed the arithmetic. Documents meant he had not merely obeyed at Kolvik; he had kept proof of having disobeyed upward, warned, objected — or worse, kept proof of having arranged it. Either way, a man who carried papers toward a medical board was a man afraid of dying before he could use them or destroy them. Frightened men talked. Frightened men, in her experience, talked most freely to women whose silence they had already purchased once and found reliable.

Her silence had been reliable. That was the word for it. Twelve years, perfectly reliable, and here came the invoice.

"Matron!" One of the orderlies, breathless from the hospital path, coat unbuttoned as if decency were a summer garment. "Message from the commandant's office. Your senior officer arrives tomorrow — they've moved it up, roads getting bad in the pass. He'll be at the garrison by midday."

"Tomorrow," Oscar said.

"Midday. They want the private room warmed tonight and hot water standing by from six o'clock." He pulled his coat straight, recovering his dignity by degrees. "Colonel Vasseur. Big name, isn't it? There'll be decorations in the obituary column someday. Imagine nursing a man like that."

"Imagine," said Oscar Amberdale.

The boy went. The river ground its new ice against the pilings, a sound like slow teeth. In her coat pocket the parcel pressed against her palm, three pounds of other people's names, and Oscar understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the chance to act sensibly has expired, that she could not send it out tomorrow. Not with him arriving over the same road, past the same cart, possibly waving to the driver. Not while the question of what Vasseur kept was still an open door she might yet walk through.

She turned from the bank and started back along the path toward the hospital, unhurried, a matron with duties, a woman with a parcel she had simply decided to keep a little longer.

Under her feet the frost creaked. Upstairs in the officers' wing, a fire needed lighting and good china needed arranging, and by midday tomorrow the man whose name headed the fourth page of her ledger would be lying in a bed she had made, close enough to touch, close enough to talk, coughing his secrets into the air of her clean white ward — and she would be standing beside him with a basin and twelve years of silence, waiting to see which of them broke first.
