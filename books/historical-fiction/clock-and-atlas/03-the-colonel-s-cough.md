## 3. The Colonel's Cough

The winter after the war — the war nobody at the garrison called by its name, the way you might avoid stepping on a grave — the first hard cold came down the river valley in the second week of November, and with it came the coughing.

Oscar Amberdale heard it before she saw him. She was in the linen closet, counting blankets with the particular resentment of a woman who had counted the same blankets for twelve years, when the sound came up the stairwell: a deep, wet, authoritative cough, the cough of a man who had spent a career making other people jump and saw no reason to stop now, even while drowning slightly in his own lungs.

She knew that cough. You do not forget a cough like that. It had carried across a field hospital at Kolvik Bridge like a church bell, ordering men into columns, ordering columns onto boats, ordering boats away from a shore still crowded with people who were not soldiers and therefore, in the arithmetic of that day, did not count.

"Bed seven," said Nurse Brandt, appearing with the admission slip pinned to her apron like a decoration for valor. "Colonel. Pneumonia, the doctor thinks. Brought up from the officers' wing because the officers' wing is full of officers who won't share rooms, and this one can't be left alone tonight."

Oscar took the slip. Her hands did not shake. Twelve years of practice had seen to that; her hands had not shaken since the winter she was nineteen, and she had sometimes wondered whether that was composure or simply a part of her that had been carried off with the boats.

"Name?" she asked, though she had read it already.

"Vasseur. Colonel Étienne Vasseur." Brandt lowered her voice with the reverence the garrison reserved for medals. "Two crosses, Oscar. They say he held the ford at—"

"I know where he held the ford," Oscar said.

She said it mildly. She always said things mildly. That was the trick of her, the thing people praised without knowing they were praising a wound: *Such a calm woman, Sister Amberdale. Nothing rattles her.* Nothing rattled her because everything that could rattle had rattled her once, all at once, on a gray afternoon in October twelve years ago, and she had gathered the pieces and glued them into the shape of a competent hospital sister, and the glue had held.

She walked down the ward with the admission slip and the blankets, and she made herself look at him.

He was smaller than she remembered. That was the first obscenity of it. At Kolvik he had seemed built of oak and authority, a man whose shadow arrived before he did. Now he was a gray bundle against the pillows, sixty-some years of him folded up like a tent left out in the rain, his mustache — still magnificent, she noted with a flicker of unwilling respect, the mustache alone had survived the war intact — yellowed at the edges. His breathing made a sound like a saw working through green wood.

He turned his head. His eyes found her, and for one unguarded moment they were the eyes of any frightened old man in a strange bed.

Then they sharpened.

"You," he said.

"Good evening, Colonel," said Oscar, in the voice she used for fevers and for grief. "You're in the convalescent ward. I'm Sister Amberdale. The doctor will see you in the morning."

"I know you." He tried to sit up, and the cough took him, a long terrible business that bent him double, and Brandt moved to help him and he waved her off with a hand that still expected obedience and, infuriatingly, got it. When he surfaced again he was looking at Oscar with an expression she had spent twelve years not thinking about. "The field hospital. Kolvik."

"Many hospitals look alike," Oscar said.

"You were the one with the lists." His voice dropped. The ward was quiet — nine sleeping men, one snoring in a manner that would have embarrassed a smaller engine — but he dropped his voice anyway, as though the walls at Kolvik had followed him here. "The young one. The one who kept the lists."

"I keep many lists," said Oscar. "It's a hospital. Lists are the whole of it, really. Lists of linens, lists of doses, lists of men who complain about the porridge. You'll be on the third one by Thursday; the cook takes it personally."

Brandt made the small sound she made when she suspected a joke was happening and wanted no part of it, and went off to fetch the doctor's orders. Oscar unfolded a blanket and laid it over the Colonel with the impersonal tenderness of her trade, tucking the corners the way she tucked every corner, and hated herself a little for how well she did it.

He caught her wrist. His grip was nothing. It was like being held by a bundle of kindling. But his eyes were the same eyes, and they said: *I know what you did.*

And hers said back, steady as a held note: *And I know what you did, and one of us has been sleeping well for twelve years, and it is not me.*

"Water," he said, releasing her.

She gave him water. She measured his temperature and wrote it on the chart. She propped him up at the angle the doctor would have approved of, and she stood a moment at the foot of the bed, in the pool of lamplight, a calm woman in a clean apron, and looked at the man whose order had sent the boats out empty.

"Sleep, Colonel," she said. "You're safe here. That's more than some people got."

It was not like her to say such a thing. She heard it leave her mouth the way one hears a glass tip over — too late to catch it, interested to see what breaks.

Vasseur's face did something complicated. The mustache quivered. "Yes," he said finally. "I imagine you'd say that."

* * *

She did not sleep well that night, which was unremarkable; she had not slept well any night that month, what with the ice groaning out at the pilings like an old man complaining about his joints, and the bridge due to close within the week, and the ledger under her floorboard growing by one name and weighing, in her private accounting, by roughly a ton.

But tonight she lay awake composing conversations.

This was an old habit. In the early years she had rehearsed confrontations the way other women rehearsed prayers — *if he ever comes through my ward, I will say this; if he ever lies dying in front of me, I will lean close and say that* — and the things she had planned to say had been magnificent, annihilating, the sort of speeches that end novels. She had imagined herself cold. She had imagined herself weeping. She had imagined, on the worst nights, saying nothing at all, merely standing at his bedside while he begged, and turning away.

What she had never imagined was that he would arrive coughing, gray, and frightened, and that her first act would be to tuck his blanket in with genuine professional excellence.

*You're getting soft*, she told the ceiling.

*You're getting careful*, the ceiling replied, which was worse.

The truth — the truth she admitted to no one, not even the ledger — was that she had spent twelve years despising Vasseur the way you despise weather. Vastly, impersonally, without hope of appeal. He was not a man to her; he was the name she had given to the whole machinery: the order, the boats, the officer with the list, the match she had held to the paper while a woman named Marta's husband stood on the far shore. Despising the machinery had been survivable. Despising a man in bed seven, who said *thank you, Sister* when she gave him his medicine, and whose hand shook lifting the cup — that was a different industry entirely.

* * *

In the morning the doctor came, pronounced it pneumonia of the left lung, "and a constitution that intends to fight it, more's the pity," and ordered steam, poultices, and quiet. The garrison, learning that a decorated colonel lay ill in the convalescent ward, began a ritual Oscar knew well: officers drifting through on "inspection," standing at a respectful distance from bed seven, murmuring. By noon the Colonel had been visited by four captains, a chaplain, and the mess steward, who brought a pudding that Vasseur eyed with the suspicion of a man who had survived worse things than custard.

Oscar moved through it all with her tray and her chart, and each time she entered the ward, his eyes came to her. Not accusing. Worse than accusing: *confiding.* There was something in the look of a man who has recognized the one other person in the room who knows, and who is too tired and too sick and too full of custard-suspicion to keep the knowledge behind his teeth.

On the second evening, the ward was down to its lamps and its snorers, and Brandt had gone to her supper, and Oscar was grinding through the medicine round when Vasseur said, quietly, "Sister."

"Colonel."

"Amberdale." He said her name carefully, tasting it. "You kept your name."

"Did you expect me to lose it?"

"I expected—" The cough interrupted, and she waited it out with her arms folded, and when he had finished he lay back, spent, and looked at the ceiling. "I expected everyone from that shore to be scattered. Erased. That was rather the intention."

"Yes," said Oscar. "It was."

A long silence. The stove ticked. Out at the pilings the ice groaned, a long low complaint, like the river clearing its throat before saying something it had been holding all winter.

"You hated me," he said. It was not a question, and he did not seem to require an answer, which was fortunate, because Oscar's throat had closed around several answers and was refusing to release any of them. "Good. I want to tell you something, and I'd rather you hated me while you heard it. It's cleaner that way."

"You're feverish," Oscar said. "You should sleep."

"I have been sleeping for twelve years, Sister. It's the waking that's killing me." He turned his head on the pillow with an effort, and the lamplight caught his eyes, and she saw that they were wet, and she looked away, because a man like Vasseur weeping was a thing the world had no shelf for. "You were there. You know what was on that shore when the order came."

"I know what was on that shore," Oscar said, "when the order came."

"And you burned the list."

The ward went very quiet. Even the snorer in bed two paused, as though the sentence had passed through his dreams and he had stopped to listen.

Oscar stood with the medicine tray in her hands and felt the floorboard in her room, three floors up, the loose one, the one under the trunk, where a ledger slept with a hundred and forty names in it, and she thought: *he knows, or he guesses, or he is delirious, and either way the next five minutes are a door, and I have been standing in front of doors my whole life, and this one is opening whether I knock or not.*

"I burned a list," she said carefully. "I was ordered to. I was nineteen, and the alternative was a firing squad, or so I was told, and I have had twelve years to decide whether I believe it."

"Do you?"

"Some nights I believe it. Some nights I believe I was a coward. Some nights I believe both, and those are the worst nights, because at least on the other nights one of them is a lie."

Vasseur made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could rust. "Nineteen," he said. "God. They gave the matches to a girl of nineteen." He closed his eyes. "And you kept a name. I remember — you hesitated. Before the paper caught. You hesitated over one line, and the officer shouted at you, and you fed it to the flame like all the rest. But you hesitated first. I have thought about that hesitation for twelve years. Isn't that a joke? A man can order three hundred civilians off a bridge and sleep like a brick, and one girl's half-second of hesitation will sit on his chest every night of his life like a cat."

"Three hundred and eleven," Oscar said.

His eyes opened.

"Three hundred and eleven civilians were on the Kolvik shore when the boats left," she said. Her voice was level. Somewhere very far away, the part of her that had kept the ledger was standing up and counting. "The official record says two hundred and forty. I was there. I counted them off the boats that came back, and I counted the ones that didn't, and I have spent twelve years keeping the difference somewhere it can't be burned."

The stove ticked. The ice groaned. Vasseur looked at her for a long time, and whatever he saw in her face made him close his eyes again, and when he spoke, his voice had gone small, a colonel's voice shrunk down to the size of the man.

"Where?" he whispered.

"That," said Oscar, "is not a question I answer in a ward with nine sleeping men."

"No." He breathed. The saw in his chest worked. "No, of course. Of course not. You were always — even then, you were the careful one. I used to watch you carry those lists, and I thought, there is a woman who understands what paper is worth." He opened his eyes and fixed them on her, and there was something new in them now, something urgent riding on top of the fever. "Sister. Sister Amberdale. I have something you need to see. Something I have carried longer than you have carried your counting, and I am — the doctor says lungs are honest, and mine has stopped lying to me. When I'm stronger. Tomorrow, perhaps. Or the day after. There is a case. A dispatch case, locked, and I will give you the key, and you will do with it what I could never make myself do."

Oscar set down the medicine tray. Her hands, those famously steady hands, were not entirely steady now, and she folded them into her apron where no one could bill her for it.

"Colonel," she said, "you are feverish, and you are talking in riddles, and I am going to give you your dose, and you are going to sleep."

"Yes," he said, and took the cup from her like a child, and drank, and lay back. But as she turned to go, his hand came out and caught her sleeve, gently, the way you catch someone in a dream.

"Sister," he said. "The bridge closes this week, doesn't it."

"It closes within the week. Yes."

"Then we haven't much time," said Colonel Vasseur, "and I have been a coward for twelve years, and I find I don't care to make it thirteen."

Out at the pilings, the ice groaned again — long, low, patient — as though the river had been listening at the window all along, and was willing, for now, to wait.
