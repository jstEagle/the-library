## 5. The Bridge Closes

The morning the bridge was to close, the whole town turned out to watch it happen, which struck Oscar as a peculiar national trait: the celebration of an inconvenience. In her experience people did not gather in the cold to applaud the shutting of doors. But this was Rennberg, and Rennberg took its rituals where it found them.

She had the day off — Matron had insisted, in the tone of a woman who would not survive one more nurse hovering over the pneumonia ward with that particular hungry attention — and so Oscar stood among the crowd on the near bank, wool coat buttoned to the throat, dispatch case flat against her ribs like a second sternum she had not been issued at birth.

*You will know what to do with it,* Vasseur had said, two nights ago, between coughs that sounded like someone tearing heavy cloth. *Or you will not. Either way it is no longer mine to carry.*

He had said it the way men gave away burdens they were too tired to hate properly. She had taken the case because refusing would have required a conversation, and conversations with dying colonels about complicity and evidence were not among her skills. So now she was a custodian. Twelve years a keeper of names in a ledger under a floorboard, and apparently promotion came eventually, even in nursing.

"Mind the rail!" someone shouted, unnecessarily, at the last cart.

It was a farm cart driven by old Halvard Sorensen, whose turnips were famous within a radius of perhaps four kilometers and whose opinions were famous considerably further. He had loaded the cart with everything he wished stored on the far side until spring — barrels, a trunk, a crate of hens who objected to travel in voices like rusted hinges — and he drove at the pace of a man who understood, correctly, that the entire garrison would wait for him rather than let him miss his moment.

"They'll chain it behind him," said Fru Brandt the baker's widow beside Oscar, with the relish of someone narrating an execution. "Chains and a posted notice. My late husband remembered them doing it in the year of the flood. Armed sentry on the near end all winter."

"How festive," Oscar said.

"Well, it's something to look at." Fru Brandt adjusted her shawl. "Winter here is mostly looking at things."

This was true. The river had begun its slow argument with the season; ice sheets fanned out from the banks each night and broke up by noon, re-forming thicker, the water between the pilings gone slushy and sullen. In another fortnight the river itself would lock, and then the town would be an island of lamplight and gossip in a white country, connected to the rest of the world only by the telegraph line and by whatever news arrived on the weekly mail sledge from the east.

The mail sledge. Oscar had thought about the mail sledge for three days the way a gambler thinks about a marked deck.

Her plan — if a thing assembled at four in the morning over cold tea deserved the word — had been simple. Address a parcel to her cousin Astrid in the southern capital, a cousin who existed, more or less, in the sense that everyone has a cousin Astrid somewhere, a woman of formidable dullness who collected recipes and would open any parcel without curiosity. Inside, wrapped in an old shawl among some innocuous knitting, the ledger. Out before the bridge closed, out before anyone knew there was anything to find, and then whatever happened next would happen to paper in a safe drawer in another province instead of paper under a floorboard beneath her bed.

Except.

Except that a parcel leaving the garrison hospital post was logged. Except that she had watched Sergeant Dahl, who ran the mail room, develop over the autumn a new habit of weighing things and looking at handwriting, because someone senior had told him to look, though nobody would say who or why. Except that the commandant's office had grown in six weeks from merely officious to quietly vigilant, the way a house grows damp — invisibly, and then everywhere at once.

And except, most of all, that the case against her ribs was heavier than any ledger, and could not go to Astrid, because Astrid would open it, because Astrid opened everything, and Astrid could not unsee what was inside it, and neither, having seen it, could be expected to survive knowing it in a small southern apartment full of recipe cards.

So the parcel sat unwrapped in the bottom of her wardrobe under the winter petticoats, and the window closed anyway, the way windows do: not slammed, just eased shut while you were busy.

Halvard's cart reached mid-span. The crowd made the low collective sound crowds make at bridges — half anxiety, half appetite for catastrophe — and Halvard, hearing it, raised his whip in acknowledgment like a man saluting his own funeral procession.

"There's the engineers coming out," said Fru Brandt. "See, with the chains."

Two soldiers and a corporal walked onto the span behind the cart, carrying coils of chain with the self-conscious theater of men who knew they were the afternoon's entertainment. One of them was young Eklund, who had spent a week in the ward in October with a badly set finger and who waved at Oscar with his good hand. She waved back. It occurred to her, not for the first time and with no more comfort than usual, that she liked these people. That after twelve years the garrison was not a posting but a home, that the orderlies teased her, that Fru Brandt saved her the end pieces of the honey loaf, and that every single person she was fond of was, by profession or proximity, employed in the maintenance of a version of events that had erased forty-one names she could recite alphabetically in the dark.

Not forty-one. Forty-two. She had added Private Anders Lysgaard in October, recorded in the regimental book as *unaccounted*, which was the army's way of saying *we looked nowhere*. The ledger did not say *unaccounted*. The ledger said what the laundry woman Nelly had told her, and what Nelly had heard from the stretcher party, and what the stretcher party had half-remembered, and where memory failed the ledger said *here the record is silent*, which was at least honest about being a lie.

"You've gone somewhere gloomy," Fru Brandt observed.

"I'm watching a bridge close."

"So is everyone. Gloomy is optional."

The cart cleared the far abutment. Halvard turned once at the top of the rise, touched his cap to the crowd — to the crowd, mind, not to any individual in it; Halvard distributed his courtesies the way he distributed turnips, wholesale — and disappeared down the eastern road toward his winter pasture of silence and card games.

Then the soldiers went to work, and the noise died out of the crowd, and Oscar felt the change in her own chest before she understood it in hers.

Chains do not sound like much. That was the cruelty of it. All winter, all those months, represented by a sound like a dropped toolbox: chain paying out through iron stanchions, link over link, the corporal calling numbers, a padlock the size of a fist clicking home through the last links with a neatness that seemed almost apologetic. Then the posting — a board nailed to the near rail, whitewashed, stenciled:

**BRIDGE CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE GARRISON COMMANDANT. NO CROSSING UNTIL OFFICIAL REOPENING IN SPRING. BY ORDER.**

*By order.* The war had been fought on those two words, Oscar thought. Whole villages had died of them. And now they hung off a nail over a river, keeping farmers from shortcuts.

The crowd, having gotten its spectacle, began to dissolve into small conversational groups drifting homeward toward supper, already converting the event into anecdote, improving it in transit. Within a week the chains would be reported thicker and the wind colder. By February, Halvard's hens would have escaped the crate on the bridge itself, in front of witnesses.

Oscar did not move.

She stayed at the rail long after Fru Brandt had been claimed by a neighbor, long after the engineers tramped back past her with their empty coils, Eklund giving her a cheerful nod that she returned with what she hoped was cheer and suspected was more like the face of a woman doing arithmetic. The light went from pewter to slate. Lamps came up along the embankment road, yellow and domestic, absurdly cozy, as if the town were tucking itself in around a locked door.

She made herself look at the facts the way she made patients look at their bandage changes: quickly, honestly, all at once.

Fact: the bridge was closed. No cart, no rider, no walker would cross legally until the engineers judged the ice-out safe, which might be March and might be May. Fact: the road south was a week's walk through forest that the army described, in its maps, as *impassable in winter*, a description she had good reason to believe was accurate, since she had helped write part of it herself, transcribing a scout's testimony while he shook with fever. Fact: the telegraph office read outgoing messages aloud to the operator's superior as a matter of course; she had learned that in her first month here, when a love letter sent as a telegram had caused a scandal involving a farrier. Fact: the mail sledge left Tuesday, and Sergeant Dahl weighed parcels now.

Fact: she had in her coat a locked dispatch case containing orders signed in a hand she recognized, rosters in a format she recognized, and photographs she had not been able to make herself look at twice, and every hour it remained on her body or under her floorboard it moved from being evidence to being evidence *found on her*, and the difference between those two conditions was, in practical terms, the difference between a witness and an accomplice.

And fact: the man who had given it to her was lying in Ward B producing, at intervals, sounds like a sawmill drowning, and saying things in his fevers. She had heard him say Kolvik twice through the screen. Orderlies had heard him say it. Word traveled through a garrison hospital faster than through the telegraph office, and with less discretion.

She was trapped. Not dramatically, not with soldiers at the door — but trapped the way the town was trapped, thoroughly, seasonally, administratively. Locked in for the winter with her evidence, her conscience, her enemies, and four hundred people who would be very surprised to learn which category she belonged to.

A laugh came out of her, short and unassisted, and a passing soldier glanced over. She converted it into a cough, twelve years of practice making the conversion seamless.

Because here was the other fact, the one she kept beneath the others like the ledger beneath the floorboard: a small, shameful, persistent relief. For twelve years the truth had sat in the dark asking her a question she could not answer — *what will you do with us?* — and she had answered with silence, and the silence had grown so heavy she had mistaken it for her own weight. Now the question had been handed to her by the worst possible courier, in the worst possible season, with a deadline. But it was a question with edges again. Something to grip.

If the choice was going to be forced, at least it was finally going to be forced.

"You'll catch your death standing there," said a voice behind her, and there was Corporal Eklund again, off duty now, collar up, hands in pockets. "Bridge won't run away. It hasn't got legs."

"No," Oscar agreed. "It's the leaving that's got legs."

Eklund considered this, decided it was nurse humor, and grinned. "They say the historians come before the hard snow. From the capital. To write the campaign record — you know, the official one." He lowered his voice with the importance of a man delivering intelligence. "Big people. My corporal says they're staying at the garrison all winter. Going through everything. Every report, every witness."

The river said nothing. The chains said nothing. Somewhere behind her ribs, the dispatch case lay against her heart like a hand.

"Every witness," Oscar repeated.

"That's what he said." Eklund stomped his feet against the cold, pleased with his news. "Anyway. Supper. Goodnight, Sister Iversen."

She watched him go up the embankment road, a young man hurrying toward warmth, and she stood a moment longer at the rail with the ice groaning out along the pilings, sheet by sheet, the river closing its own door under the army's chained one.

Historians. Witnesses. Everything.

Well, she thought, turning at last toward the lamplit streets, the case a cold flat certainty against her side — well. They would want the ward records. They would want material witnesses. They would come to the hospital with their questions and their forms, and she would pour their tea and answer carefully and keep her voice level, and underneath the level voice the ledger would wait under its floorboard with its forty-two names, patient as seed.

She only hoped, walking home through the blue dusk with the first flakes starting down, that whoever they sent was a fool. A thorough, pompous, blinkered fool who would ask exactly the questions in the regulations and no others.

Anyone curious, she thought, would be dangerous — though she could not yet have said to whom.

* * *

At the hospital, the night orderly met her in the corridor with his cap in his hands and his face doing something complicated.

"Sister. You'd better come to Ward B." He hesitated. "The colonel's asking for you. Been asking an hour. Says—" he winced— "says you'll know why. Says you're the only one who knows what he did at Kolvik Bridge."

The lamps hissed. Down the corridor, behind the screen, the coughing went on, tearing and regular, like work being done.

Oscar stood very still, the case warm now from her body, the cold seeping in everywhere else, and understood that the door had not finished shutting after all. It had only changed hinges.
