## 6. The Historians Arrive

The morning the historians came, I was elbow-deep in a basin of linens and arguing with Corporal Fenwick about whether a man with a fractured femur could be permitted a second egg.

"He's a major," Fenwick said, as though rank digested differently.

"He's a man who will vomit that egg onto my shoes," I said, "and then he'll still be a major, and my shoes will still be ruined."

This was the level of discourse the garrison hospital had sunk to by midwinter — eggs and shoes — and I confess I loved it. After twelve years of tending convalescents, I had learned that the smallness of a quarrel is directly proportional to the peace it buys you. Nobody argues about eggs during a catastrophe. If we were arguing about eggs, then the war was properly dead and buried, and all that remained was laundry.

Fenwick opened his mouth to renew the campaign when the door banged open and Orderly Brandt came in with snow on his shoulders and his eyes lit like festival lamps.

"They're here," he said.

"Who's here?" I wrung out a sheet. "If it's the supply wagon, tell them we're short on everything and grateful for nothing."

"Historians." He said the word the way other men said *bride* or *inheritance*. "From the Commission. Two of them, come by sledge from the capital. The commandant wants the ward scrubbed and everyone in clean shirts by noon."

"Why?" asked the major from his bed. "Are they going to inspect us or eat us?"

"Both, probably," I said, but quietly, because something cold had slid down my spine and settled at the base of it, where it intended to stay.

Historians. Of course. The bridge would close within the week — the whole town had been talking of nothing else, carters rushing their last loads across, the ferryman drinking to his own unemployment — and what better moment for the victors to send their pen-men? While the river still ran, while the record could still be *fixed*. I thought of the ledger beneath the floorboard under my cot, forty-one names in my own careful hand, and I thought: *they have come to write their book, and mine is not in it, and if they find it, it never will be.*

"Clean shirts," I repeated aloud, and handed Fenwick the wet sheet. "Well. You heard the empire."

* * *

The garrison spent the morning grooming itself like a cat expecting company. Snow was swept where snow could not reasonably be swept. The commandant's adjutant ran up the stairs twice for no reason anyone could determine. In the officers' mess someone polished silver that had not been silver since before the armistice.

I kept to the ward, because I had decided — in the way one decides things at four in the morning, lying awake with a floorboard pressing thoughts into one's back — that I would not go and look at them. Let them arrive. Let them unpack their fountain pens and their official envelopes stamped with the crest of the Commission on Historical Records. I had survived a war and eleven winters of this town; I could survive two scholars.

At half past noon, curiosity won, as it always does. It is the least dignified of my qualities and the most reliable.

They arrived at the commandant's house just as I crossed the yard with a basket of dressings, and so I had no choice but to stand in the falling snow and watch the sledge pull up — and I am glad now that I did, though I didn't know it yet.

The first historian descended like an act of government. He was perhaps fifty, gray in the deliberate way of men who choose their tailors carefully, and he surveyed the garrison — the walls, the flag, the commandant hurrying out to meet him — with the mild disappointment of a man who has been promised a meal and served a photograph of one. His fur collar alone must have cost more than my annual wages. He did not look at me. Men like him never do; we are scenery.

"Doctor Brandt tells me you've come to write the campaign history," the commandant was saying, pumping the man's hand. "An honor, Senior Historian Kessler. A real honor."

"A duty," said Kessler, in the tone of a man who finds duties fattening.

And then the second figure climbed down from the sledge, and I confess I made an error of assessment, because I took her at first for a secretary. She was young — late twenties, perhaps, though she carried herself older — in a plain wool coat that had been mended at both cuffs with more patience than money. She wore spectacles that fogged immediately in the cold, and while Kessler shook hands and discussed accommodations, she stood looking up at the garrison wall with an expression I recognized before I could name it.

It was the expression of a person reading.

Not glancing. Reading. Her eyes moved along the stone the way mine moved down a page, and when she reached the memorial plaque the town had put up three years ago — nineteen names, chiseled by a stonemason who had run out of room and squeezed the last two into the border like a secret — she stepped off the packed path into the snow to get closer, and stood there reading every name, in the wind, with her bare hands going red around the strap of her case.

Then she noticed the last two names, the squeezed ones, and tilted her head. And she smiled. Not a diplomatic smile. The private, crooked smile of a woman who has caught a document doing something interesting.

I stood in the yard with my basket and felt something I had not felt in twelve years and distrusted instantly.

Hope has terrible manners. It arrives uninvited, wearing the enemy's uniform, carrying its little leather case, and it asks questions.

* * *

By evening the garrison knew everything there was to know, which is to say it had invented most of it. The historians were installed in the guest rooms above the mess. Kessler had requested hot water bottles, a desk facing south, and "access to personnel for structured testimony" — a phrase the adjutant repeated all evening with relish, having decided it meant something scandalous.

"The tall one's writing a book about the general," Brandt told us over supper, authoritatively. "The great flanking maneuver. They'll want the hospital's part in it."

"There was no hospital part in any flanking maneuver," I said. "We patched legs and boiled linen. History can leave us out; we shan't complain."

But I went back to the ward after supper, ostensibly to check on the major's egg-related recovery, actually to think, and found Fenwick lighting the lamps.

"Nurse Iversen," he said, "the young one came by. While you were at supper."

"By the ward? Why?"

"To ask about our dead. Politely. There's a list she wanted — men who died here since the armistice, names and dates. She said the Commission wants the record complete." He shrugged. "I gave her the register. She copied it standing up at the desk, never sat once, wrote faster than the doctor does. Then she thanked me. Twice." He frowned at a lamp wick. "Odd one. Polite like a foreigner, if you take my meaning."

"She's from the capital, Fenwick. The capital is abroad, as far as I'm concerned."

"No — I mean polite like it costs her something. Like she's paying attention to being polite." He got the wick right at last. "Kessler doesn't thank anyone. He asked me whether the men ever spoke of the campaign, and when I said they mostly spoke of food, he wrote that down too. *Mostly spoke of food.* Like it was evidence."

"It is evidence," I said. "Of the only thing that matters."

But I did not sleep well, and it wasn't the major's snoring. A historian who thanks orderlies twice. A historian who reads memorial plaques in a freezing wind. A historian who wants the names of our dead — the ones the reports forgot, the ones nobody counts because counting them profits nobody.

Forty-one names under my floorboard. Forty-one people who existed, which is apparently now a political position.

* * *

She came to the ward herself the next morning.

I saw her before she saw me — she paused in the doorway, cap in hand, taking in the room the way she'd taken in the wall: methodically, left to right, the way one reads. Twelve beds, nine occupied, one window, one stove, one nurse pretending to be very busy with a chart.

"Nurse Iversen?" Her accent was the flat, educated capital accent, every syllable ironed. "Ilse Brenner. The Historical Commission. May I impose on you?"

"You may impose on nurses," I said, "it's practically the definition of the profession."

The corner of her mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the same country as the one at the plaque. "I'm compiling testimony for the official record of the winter campaign. Senior Historian Kessler conducts the formal interviews. But there are always gaps. Small things. The kind of things only the people who were actually in a room notice." She held up a notebook. "May I ask you some questions? Nothing formal. I'd rather talk to you than to a schedule."

"And yet the Commission sent you here with one."

"The schedule," she said, "is Senior Historian Kessler's. The gaps are mine."

There it was again — that crooked honesty, offered like a card laid face-up on the table. I should have refused her. I want that understood, by whoever reads this, whenever this is read: I knew what she was. She was the victors' clerk, the empire's memory with a fountain pen, hope dressed in the wrong coat — and I let her in anyway, because after twelve years of keeping names in a floorboard like a woman hiding love letters from a dead man, someone had finally asked me a question I wanted to answer.

"Sit," I said. "Mind the major. He bites when he loses at cards."

She sat precisely, knees together, notebook open, pencil sharpened to an aggressive point. Then she looked at me over her spectacles and asked, not first but early enough to matter:

"Were you at Kolvik?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed. I felt the cold spot at the base of my spine, the old tenant, shift in its chair.

"I've been at this garrison twelve years," I said. "Before that I moved about. Field hospitals, mostly."

"I know," she said mildly. "Your name appears in the transfer records. Oscar Iversen, nursing corps, attached to the Third Column hospital detachment — Kolvik Bridge, October." She turned a page, unhurried. "It's strange. The rosters for the Third Column list ninety-six civilian personnel attached to the medical detachment at Kolvik. But the withdrawal manifests from the following week list only sixty-one accounted for. Thirty-five people simply stop appearing in the paperwork. No transfers. No casualty reports. Nothing." She looked up. "Paperwork hates a gap. It itches. I've been scratching at this one for a month."

Every instinct I had, honed through eleven winters and one war, said the same thing: *give her weather, give her soup, give her anything but the truth.* And so of course I heard myself say:

"Rosters are written by tired people, Miss Brenner. Tired people make mistakes, and proud people don't correct them."

"Oh, absolutely," she agreed, writing it down. "*Rosters written by tired people* — may I quote you? Good." Another page. "Though thirty-five simultaneous mistakes is rather a lot of tired. Unless—" and she looked at me with those clear, unfrightened eyes, "—the tiredness was organized."

Down the ward, the major snored on, dreaming of eggs. The stove ticked. Ilse Brenner waited, pencil poised, patient as ice forming — and I realized, with a sensation between dread and vertigo, that I liked her. That I wanted to tell her. That twelve years of silence had not cured me of the disease of speaking, but only driven it underground, and here it came now, rising.

"Miss Brenner," I said, "what exactly does the Commission intend to do with its record? When it's finished?"

"Print it, seal it, lodge it in the national archive," she recited, clearly from the mandate itself. "The campaign history becomes definitive. Nothing enters afterward without ministerial approval."

"And nothing leaves?"

"Nothing leaves." A pause. Her pencil lowered a fraction. "Why do you ask, Nurse Iversen?"

Because I am trapped behind a bridge that closes in four days with thirty-five missing people folded under my floorboard, and you have just described, cheerfully, the lid of the coffin — but I said:

"Because I keep records too. Hospital records. One learns that what goes into them matters. And what stays out of them."

"Yes," she said softly, and for a moment she was not a Commission clerk at all but a young woman with mended cuffs who read plaques in the wind. "That's rather the whole profession, isn't it. What stays out."

She thanked me — twice — and left. At the door she paused.

"I interview the laundry staff tomorrow," she said. "And the kitchen. The Commission wants 'a complete picture of garrison life.'" A beat. "Complete pictures take a long time. I intend to be thorough."

And she was gone, boots ringing on the frozen lane, leaving me standing in the ward with my heart going like a trapped bird and one thought repeating itself, absurd and dangerous and warm as a coal in a cold hand:

*Thorough. She intends to be thorough.*

Under the floorboard, forty-one names lay waiting in the dark. Four days until the bridge chained shut. And for the first time in twelve years, I began — God help me — to consider letting one of them out.
