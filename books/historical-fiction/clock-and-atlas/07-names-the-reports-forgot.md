## 7. Names the Reports Forgot

Ilse Brandt had been a historian for six years, and in that time she had learned three things: that archives smelled the same everywhere—dust, glue, and someone's old lunch; that senior men said *the record* the way priests said *God*, with a small pause of ownership before it; and that the truth, when it finally agreed to appear, almost never wore its own face.

She had not yet learned the fourth thing, which was that it sometimes wore an apron and brought you tea.

The interview room at the garrison hospital was really a linen closet promoted beyond its station. There was a table, two chairs, one window looking out on the frozen river, and a smell of starch so aggressive it felt like a personality. Ilse had set up her papers with the neatness she reserved for occasions that mattered: the official campaign rosters, the casualty annexes, a fresh pen, and the standard questionnaire stamped by the Historical Commission in letters so pompous they seemed to be shouting.

Her instructions from Herr Doktor Reinholt, senior historian of the commission, had been simple. "Take the witness statements from the support staff. Medical personnel, laundry, kitchen. Routine corroboration only. Don't wander." He had said *don't wander* the way other men said *don't touch the stove*, and Ilse, who was twenty-nine and had been wandering professionally since she could walk, had smiled and promised nothing.

The nurse who came in at ten o'clock precisely was not what the roster of garrison personnel had led Ilse to expect. The file said *Amberdale, O., ward supervisor, convalescent*. It did not say that the woman would carry herself like someone who had been quietly running a small country for years and had decided against telling anyone. She was perhaps fifty, gray-eyed, with hands that looked as though they had done everything at least twice.

"You're the historian," the woman said. Not a question.

"Fräulein Brandt. Historical Commission." Ilse stood, offered her hand, and watched the woman take it with a grip that was friendly the way a well-made lock is friendly. "Thank you for coming, Miss Amberdale. I know the ward doesn't run itself."

"It runs better without me for an hour," Oscar Amberdale said, sitting down. "Sister Kessel has wanted my job since 1919. I like to give her occasional opportunities."

Ilse laughed before she could stop herself, and noted, in the private ledger she kept behind her face, that the nurse had made her laugh on purpose. A technique. Or a kindness. With this sort of person it was often impossible to tell the difference, which was the whole trouble with them.

"I'll try to keep this short," Ilse said. "You were at the field hospitals during the campaign?"

"Kolvik. Then north. Then here when they built the garrison." The woman folded her hands. "Twelve years ago. It's all in my service record."

"Your service record is exactly why we're speaking." Ilse opened the questionnaire. "The Commission is compiling the official history of the campaign. Everything cross-checked, everything witnessed. You'd be listed as a material witness."

"A material witness," Amberdale repeated, tasting the phrase like a spoonful of something possibly spoiled. "And what materials do you imagine I'm made of?"

Ilse looked up. The gray eyes were perfectly pleasant.

"Your observations," Ilse said. "What you saw. What you recorded."

"Mm," said the nurse, in the tone of a woman agreeing to the existence of weather.

* * *

The first half hour went according to form. Yes, the field hospital at Kolvik had been established in October. Yes, conditions were difficult. Yes, supplies were short. No, she had no complaints about the medical officers. Each answer arrived complete, correct, and hermetically sealed, like tinned rations. Ilse filled in the boxes and understood that she was learning nothing whatsoever, and that the woman across the table knew she was learning nothing, and that both of them were being extremely polite about it.

It should have ended there. Routine corroboration only. Don't wander.

But Ilse had spent the previous evening going through the casualty annexes for the river sector, because she could not help herself, and there was a thing in those pages that had kept her awake past midnight with the lamp turned low so Reinholt wouldn't knock.

"The rosters," she said now, setting down her pen. "May I ask you something off the form?"

The nurse's eyebrows rose a fraction. "That depends entirely on the question, Fräulein Brandt."

"The casualty lists for the last week of October. The final consolidated version—I assume you've seen it? They issued copies to the garrisons."

"I've seen it."

"There are four hundred and eleven names on the civilian annex. Displaced persons processed through the Kolvik crossing." Ilse turned her papers around so the numbers faced the woman. "But the field hospital intake log for that same period—which survives in fragments in your garrison records—shows admissions alone exceeding that figure. Just admissions. Not counting anyone treated in the open, not counting the chapel, not counting—" She stopped, because the sentence was carrying her somewhere she hadn't fully mapped.

The silence in the linen closet changed quality. It did not become hostile. It became *attentive*, the way a room changes when a cat comes into it.

"Logs burn," Miss Amberdale said mildly. "Paper is very fragile. Especially in field conditions. There were stoves."

"Were there stoves in October?"

"Everywhere," said the nurse. "It was cold."

Ilse sat back. Somewhere outside, a cart creaked across the ice-road below the bridge, and the sound came through the window thin and far away.

"Four hundred and eleven," she said. "That's the number that went into the annex. That's the number that will go into the official history. And if the true count was different—"

"Then some clerk in the rear wrote a tidier number than God gave him, and everyone signed it, and here we are, twelve years later, two women drinking tea over arithmetic." Amberdale glanced at the teapot. "More tea?"

"No, thank you."

"You should eat more," the nurse said, with sudden maternal authority. "Historians. You all get thin and strange. We had a chaplain like that at Kolvik, counted everything, weighed nothing."

"Counted everything," Ilse repeated. "Weighed nothing. Is that how you'd put the official record?"

There was a pause. Ilse had the distinct sensation of standing at a door on which someone had knocked from the *other* side.

"I'd say," Amberdale said slowly, "that a number written by a man who wasn't there is a portrait painted by a man who never saw the face. It may be a fine portrait. It may hang in a fine gallery." She poured her own tea. "But it isn't Aunt Marta."

Ilse wrote that down. She couldn't have said why; it wasn't evidence of anything. But her hand moved before her caution did, and when she looked up, the nurse was watching the pen move with an expression Ilse couldn't read at all—and Ilse prided herself, professionally, on reading expressions.

"That name," Ilse said. "Marta. Is that someone you knew?"

"Somebody everybody knew, eventually." A small, dry smile. "You'll want the laundry staff for your interviews. She's down there. Marta Lindqvist. She was at Kolvik too."

*Was*. Ilse heard the word settle. The annex listed displaced persons *processed through the crossing*. Processed. As though civilians were flour.

"The last week of October," Ilse said carefully. "The bridge order. The withdrawal of the covering units across the river. The official account says the evacuation of the east bank was 'substantially complete' before the demolition preparations began."

The nurse drank her tea. Outside, the light off the river came up through the window and laid a pale bar across the table between them, and Ilse thought absurdly of a lamp lit under a cloth—the glow of something you could see but not name, warming the air between their two sets of hands.

"Substantially complete," Amberdale echoed.

"Yes."

"What a lovely phrase." The nurse set down her cup. "Did you write it?"

"Heavens, no. I was nine years old."

"Mm. Someone wrote it. Someone with good handwriting and a warm office." She looked out at the river for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had not changed register at all, which was somehow worse. "I can tell you about substantially complete, Fräulein Brandt. On the twenty-ninth of October, the field hospital at Kolvik held two hundred and change wounded and rather more civilians than beds, because civilians don't require beds, officially. They require a ditch and a blanket, and even those were short. On the thirtieth, the order came down to redeploy the transport. All of it. There is a great deal of paperwork involved in moving ambulances away from a hospital, and every sheet of it was filled out correctly, and every signature on it was legitimate, and the transport left."

Ilse's pen had stopped. She made it start again.

"And the civilians?"

"Were told the crossing would be reopened within the week." The gray eyes came back around, level and unblinking. "That's what they were told. By officers with legitimate signatures. Now—you're the historian. In your professional opinion, Fräulein Brandt, is that statement corroborated by the official record?"

Everything in Ilse wanted to say *no*, loudly, immediately, the way she had wanted to say it in seminar rooms at nineteen. But she had been in the commission's employ long enough to learn that the first loud *no* was the cheapest coin in the building, and that this woman across the table had survived twelve years in a garrison full of the men whose signatures those were, and had done it by knowing exactly which coins to spend.

So instead she said: "It's corroborated only if you trust the signatures. Do you trust the signatures?"

Something moved behind the nurse's eyes—quick, quickly hidden, but Ilse caught it. Approval, or disappointment, or both; a fisherman's flicker when the line goes taut and you don't yet know what's on it.

"Careful girl," Amberdale said softly. Almost to herself.

"I beg your pardon?"

"I said, that remains to be seen." The nurse rose, smoothing her apron, the interview apparently concluded by unilateral decree. "You'll find Sister Kessel very cooperative. She remembers everything about linens and nothing about anything else. I recommend her highly."

"At the risk of keeping you," Ilse said, also rising, "I haven't finished the form."

"The form will keep. It's official; officials are immortal." At the door, the nurse paused, hand on the frame, and looked back with an expression that had thawed perhaps two degrees. "The intake logs you mentioned. The fragments in the garrison records. Where exactly did you find them?"

"In the auxiliary archive. The cellar of the commandant's offices. Why?"

"Curiosity," said Miss Amberdale. "It's a dangerous habit. Guard it carefully."

And she was gone, footsteps receding down the corridor with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had somewhere to be and no intention of telling anyone what it was.

* * *

That evening the historians' quarters smelled of Reinholt's pipe and the pickled herring the cook served four nights out of seven on the theory that victors' historians should suffer like everyone else. Reinholt sat by the stove with his proofs, making small contented noises, a large soft man assembled mostly of tweed and self-regard.

"Well?" he asked, without looking up. "The witnesses?"

"Cooperative," Ilse said. "Mostly."

"Good. Keep them that way. The campaign volume goes to print when the ice breaks and not one day after; the general wants his foreword typeset by March, and I want to be home by April." He turned a page. "Anything irregular?"

Ilse considered the question with the whole of her attention while appearing to consider none of it. This, too, was a skill the commission had taught her, though it had never appeared on any syllabus.

"Arithmetic," she said. "The civilian annex for the river sector. Four hundred eleven processed through the Kolvik crossing, but the supporting figures don't reconcile. Intake fragments suggest more. Possibly several hundred more."

Reinholt's pipe slowed. For a moment he looked, not up, but into his proofs, and Ilse watched a door close behind his eyes with tremendous administrative efficiency.

"Fogures from field conditions," he said comfortably. "Unreliable. Duplicates. Civilians drift, Brandt—they pass through three crossings and get counted at each. The annex was compiled by Colonel Weiss's section, and Weiss was thorough."

"Thorough men don't leave totals that don't add."

"Thorough men leave totals that don't *matter*." He closed the folder and patted it, the way one pats a dog. "The number in the annex is the number, because the number in the annex is what was approved. That is what an official record is, my dear. Not what happened. What was approved to have happened. You'll learn."

"I thought we were supposed to find out what happened."

"We were sent to write down what happened *correctly*," Reinholt said, with the mild patience of a man explaining rain to a child. "There is a general alive and decorated because of that week, and a country that needs him, and a book that will be finished when the ice breaks. Four hundred eleven is a sad number and a closed number. Leave it closed. Take your linen lists and your laundresses and give me corroboration, not curiosity."

He returned to his proofs. Ilse ate her herring and said nothing further, and smiled at the correct moments, and went to bed at the correct hour, and lay in the dark listening to the wind come off the river with the patience of something enormous waiting for spring.

Closed number, she thought. Approved to have happened.

She got up, struck a match, and by its light copied out, in her smallest hand, the discrepancy she had shown no one: *411 approved / intake fragments ≥ 700 (est.)*. Then she folded the paper twice and slid it into the lining of her glove, where it made the glove fit slightly wrong, and where she would feel it every time she pulled them on, like a splinter, like a pulse.

Because there had been one more thing in that linen closet today—one thing she had not written down anywhere, and could not have explained to Reinholt if she tried.

When Ilse had asked *do you trust the signatures*, the nurse had looked at her for a moment too long, and then, gathering her things, she had said, quite quietly, as if remarking on the weather:

"Come to the ward sometime, Fräulein Brandt. Tuesday evenings are quiet. The men sleep early." A beat. "Some of us stay awake."

It might have been kindness. It might have been an invitation. It might, Ilse knew, have been a trap being baited by a woman who had spent twelve years learning exactly how much truth a person could carry without rattling.

She lay in the dark with her gloved hand resting on the chair beside her bed, feeling the small folded weight of the paper inside it, and thought: Tuesday.

Tuesday was two days away.
