## 8. The Widow from Kolvik

The laundry of the garrison hospital occupied the basement of the east wing, a long low room where the windows sat at ground level and let in a stripe of gray winter light that never quite reached the washing machines. The air was always wet and always smelled of lye soap and boiled linen, and after twelve years Oscar had come to think of the smell as the true smell of the building — more honest than the carbolic upstairs, which pretended everything was clean when really it was only disinfected.

She came down on Thursday mornings with the ward's linens because it was the one errand nobody else wanted and therefore the one she could keep to herself. Twelve years of practice had taught her that the best hiding places were not dark corners but dull chores. Nobody watched a woman carry sheets.

Marta Lindqvist worked the second mangle, the big one with the cracked roller that sang a thin note when it ran hot. She was fifty or thereabouts, broad-shouldered, with forearms like a dockworker's and a face that gave away nothing except tiredness, and even the tiredness seemed rationed out carefully, a coin at a time. She had come to the garrison three winters ago with a reference from a relief society and no history anyone asked about twice. She washed soldiers' sheets and bandage wrappings and the officers' tablecloths, and she did it better than anyone, and she spoke perhaps forty words a day.

Oscar liked her enormously and had never once let herself wonder why.

On this particular Thursday the laundry was short-handed — the girl with the chilblains had been sent to the infirmary, and the boy who fed the boiler had overslept into a sulk — so Marta was running both mangles alone, moving between them with the economy of a woman who had learned that pity for oneself was a luxury billed at compound interest.

"Sit," Marta said, without looking up. "You hover. Hovering wrinkles nothing."

"I'm supervising."

"You're hiding." She fed a sheet into the rollers with two efficient slaps. "There's a difference. Supervisors stand where the light is."

Oscar set down her basket and, because there was nothing else to do with her hands, began folding the dried things off the rack. Towels first. Then the pillowcases, which she matched by their faded stamps — the garrison stamped its linens with a small crown and a number, and every number had a history she could have recited if anyone had ever thought to ask.

They worked in the comfortable silence of two women who had run out of small talk years ago and found they hadn't needed it. The mangle sang. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above them a ward door opened and shut, and boots went down the corridor, and neither of them looked up, because boots going somewhere were no business of theirs.

It was Marta who broke the silence, in the way she sometimes did — sideways, apropos of nothing, as though the thought had been sitting in her pocket all morning waiting for a gap in the noise.

"The colonel's dying," she said.

"He's holding," Oscar said. "Pneumonia is a patient disease. It can afford to wait longer than he can."

"Good." She said it flatly, without heat, the way one says *good* about weather that will be convenient for drying clothes. Then, feeding another sheet: "You've been carrying something heavy lately. Under your coat. I see you walk past the window every morning with your shoulder down on one side, like a woman with a bad hip or a secret."

Oscar's hands kept folding. This was the discipline of twelve years: the hands keep working, the face keeps mild, the heart does whatever it likes behind the ribs where no one can audit it.

"Coal," she said. "I keep a little stove in my room. The ward gets cold by four."

"Mm." Marta turned the sheet, slapped it flat again. "I also keep a stove. My coal comes in sacks. Sacks are round. Whatever you're carrying is square, and it has corners, and you check on it the way a hen checks an egg."

The mangle sang. Oscar folded a towel that was already folded.

"Twelve years I've worked here," Marta went on, conversational, terrible. "Three in this laundry. You know what I've learned about this place? Everybody carries something square. The commandant carries his career. The chaplain carries his doubts, poor man, they must weigh as much as yours. The orderlies carry gambling debts. And you—" she glanced over, finally, and her eyes were the color of dishwater and just as hard to see through, "—you carry the heaviest thing in the building, and you carry it politely, and everyone is grateful to you for being polite, because polite people make it easy to pretend the weight isn't there."

"You should write sermons," Oscar said. "The chaplain would retire in shame."

"I wrote enough letters in my life." Something moved behind her face then, briefly, like a fish under ice. "None of them got answers."

Oscar knew — had known since Marta's second week, when she'd seen the woman's name on the duty roster and felt the floor tilt — that Marta Lindqvist had come from Kolvik. It was in none of the records; the records said she came from the relief society. But Oscar had spent twelve years learning to read what documents didn't say, and Marta's silences had a geography to them. They clustered around certain words. *River.* *Bridge.* *November.* She had never once said the town's name aloud in Oscar's hearing, and Oscar had never once offered her the mercy of saying it either, because some mercies are indistinguishable from wounds.

Now, in the steam and the singing rollers, the wound came looking for her.

Because that was the moment the orderly arrived with the colonel's things.

He was a cheerful boy named Pell, seventeen, with the untroubled conscience of the very young, and he came clattering down the stairs with a canvas sack over his shoulder. "Beg pardon, ma'am — Matron says the colonel's personal effects need doing, he's soaked through the bedclothes again and the doctor wants everything boiled. There's his kit bag too, she says go through it careful, there's papers."

"Papers don't get boiled," Oscar said. "Give me those separately."

"Right you are." He dumped the sack on the sorting table with the graceless enthusiasm of youth presented with other people's belongings, and among the shirts and the shaving kit and the spare boots, wrapped in an oilcloth against the damp, was the dispatch case.

Oscar saw it and felt the whole basement tilt.

She had kept it for six days now — under the floorboard by day, in her coat at night, against her body while she slept as though proximity could digest it. She had told herself she would find a way to send it out before the bridge closed, and then the bridge had closed, and she had told herself that keeping it hidden *was* the work, that custody was its own kind of action, and she had almost believed it. But she had made one mistake, the mistake of a woman who had grown too good at hiding things: she had stopped expecting them to surface.

"Those go straight back," she said, reaching for the case. "Officer's effects. Nothing of ours."

But Pell had already turned away, whistling, and Marta had come around the end of the mangle, wiping her hands on her apron, and she was standing very still, looking at the case.

It was a standard staff dispatch case, black leather gone green-gray at the corners, brass fittings dulled, the lock hanging open where Vasseur's delirium had left it. On the lid, embossed in the leather and picked out once in silver that time had eaten to a ghost, was the seal of the Third Army Corps — the crossed bridge and star.

Oscar had looked at that seal every day for six days until it had become furniture. She had forgotten what it was to someone else.

Marta's hand came out and touched it. Just the fingertips, resting on the embossing, the way you touch a gravestone to be sure it's real.

"That's the corps seal," she said. Her voice had changed entirely — all the dryness gone out of it, all the wit, leaving something raw underneath like meat. "Third Corps. That was the seal on the orders at Kolvik."

"Marta—"

"They posted them on the church door." She wasn't looking at Oscar anymore. She was looking through the case, through the wall, through twelve years. "A captain nailed them up with four nails because three wouldn't hold. Withdrawal of protective forces, effective such-and-such hour, by order of — I read the name until the ink wore off in my memory. Everyone stood in the square reading it and nobody understood it, because we weren't soldiers, we were *civilians*, the word was supposed to mean something. My husband read it twice and then he laughed, do you know? He laughed and said, well, they'll send someone else. Someone will come. The word civilian means something." Her fingers were still on the seal. "Eleven days later the bridge went and the river took four hundred and eleven people and the word meant exactly what they wanted it to mean, which was nothing."

The mangle had run empty. Its rollers turned against each other with a hollow clacking sound, and nobody stopped them.

"Four hundred and eleven," Oscar said quietly.

"Four hundred and ten and my son, who was fourteen and insisted on counting himself separately from me." Marta turned. Her eyes were dry, which was somehow worse than the alternative — dry the way ground is dry after a fire, past any use for tears. "Where did you get this case?"

"It was given to me. By its owner."

"Which owner? Who owns a thing like that?"

"You know who owns a thing like that."

For a moment the only sound was the empty mangle clacking, and the radiator ticking, and above them the ordinary footsteps of the living going about their morning.

Then Marta hit her.

Not hard — she was a strong woman and she pulled it, Oscar would understand that later, the blow landed more like a shove than a strike, catching Oscar's shoulder and turning her half around against the sorting table. But it carried twelve years in it, and Oscar took it the way you take a wave, letting it move her rather than trying to stand against it.

"You *knew*," Marta said. "All this time. Three winters I've washed this floor next to you. You knew what I was and you never said a word, and the whole time you were carrying—" her voice cracked, not with grief but with rage, which is grief that has found employment, "—you were carrying the *proof*, tucked under your arm like a hymnal, and you let me fold your sheets."

"Yes," Oscar said.

It was, she would think afterward, the first completely honest word she had spoken in the building. It cost her everything and it cost her nothing, and it stopped Marta cold the way only agreement can stop an angry person.

"*Yes*," Marta repeated, disgusted. "Just yes. No explanation. No begging forgiveness."

"Would it help?"

"No. Yes. I don't know." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and for a moment the great stillness broke and Oscar saw the woman underneath, the one from the church square, the one who had counted herself separately from her son. "Why?" she said, muffled. "Why carry it and say nothing? Why hide it from *me*?"

"Because I hid it from everyone. Because hiding it was the only thing I knew how to do anymore." Oscar steadied herself against the table. The old reflex rose in her — the soft deflection, the joke, the mild face — and she put it down deliberately, like setting down a heavy case. "I'll tell you all of it. But not standing up in a laundry with Pell coming back in five minutes. Can you get away tonight?"

"I live in a room the size of a coffin. So do you."

"My room, then. After the ward settles. Nine o'clock."

Marta lowered her hands. She looked older and younger at once, scrubbed raw. "If you're lying to me—"

"I'm done lying. It's exhausting work and I've been at it twelve years and I'm tired right down to the bone." She heard her own voice and was surprised by it — there was iron in there somewhere, rusted but real. "Nine o'clock. Bring your anger. You'll want it."

* * *

She spent the afternoon in a state she could only describe as rehearsed terror. She checked the floorboard twice — once at noon, once at four — and both times the case lay where she had left it, wrapped in its oilcloth under the loose board beneath her cot, surrounded by the false archaeology of an unremarkable life: spare stockings, a box of stationery, a novel she had never finished. She caught herself talking to it. *Soon,* she told it. *Soon you'll be somebody else's problem.* It did not answer, but then, it never had. That was the trouble with evidence. It just lay there, being true, waiting for someone brave enough to repeat it.

At nine o'clock Marta knocked — two raps, pause, one rap, the knock of a woman who had learned caution the way other people learned prayers — and Oscar let her in and bolted the door and lit the second lamp, because this was not a conversation to have in shadows.

The room was small and clean and held nothing personal except the ledger, and Marta found it within thirty seconds, because of course she did. She was standing by the bookshelf and her eye went to the gap where the books leaned slightly wrong, and she crouched, and her fingers found the board's edge, and she looked up at Oscar with an expression that was almost admiring.

"Under the floor. In your own room. Twelve years?"

"Eleven and a half. I started it the spring after."

Marta lifted the board and brought out the ledger itself — the plain account book, cloth-bound, its spine labeled in Oscar's careful hand as *Ward Stores, 1919–1931*, the dullest possible title for the most dangerous object in the province. She set it on the cot and opened it, and the lamplight fell across the pages, and she stopped breathing.

Page after page. Names in ink, in pencil, in the brown fading iron-gall of the early entries. Names with ranks, names with no ranks, names with only descriptions where the name had been lost — *woman, gray coat, carried an infant, infant survived, mother did not.* Dates. Places. Cross-references to official reports, each discrepancy noted in a small precise hand: *recorded as desertion; witnessed otherwise.* Recorded as unaccounted; recorded as drowned, no body recovered; recorded as enemy casualties, actual identity civilian.

Marta turned the pages slowly, with wet hands she kept wiping on her apron, as though the names might smudge, as though they were alive and could be hurt.

"There are so many," she whispered.

"Two hundred and six," Oscar said. "That I could verify. There are others I couldn't, and I wouldn't put a guess in a book like this. A guess is just a lie with good intentions."

Marta's finger traveled down a page and stopped. "Kolvik section," she read. "There's a *section*."

"Pages forty-one to sixty-three. Chronological, then alphabetical. Four hundred and eleven names. I was there. I kept the field register until they ordered it burned, and I burned the register, and then I went home and started writing down everything I could remember, and I've been adding to it ever since. Every survivor I could find. Every widow who wrote a letter that got no answer." She paused. "Your letters are in there. All seven of them. You wrote to the War Office, the Relief Commission, and the Bishop. None of them answered. I copied every word."

Marta sat down on the cot very suddenly, as though her knees had been dismissed without notice. She held the open book in her lap the way you hold a sleeping child.

"Why?" she said at last. "Why do it? Why burn the real list and then spend twelve years rebuilding it in secret? What did it buy you?"

Oscar considered the question honestly, because Marta had earned an honest answer and because she had never managed to give herself one.

"Guilt buys strange things," she said. "At first I think I did it to punish myself. Keeping the names was a sentence I sentenced myself to — every evening, an hour with the dead, no parole. And then somewhere along the way it stopped being punishment and became... work. Real work. The kind that matters. Somebody had to remember them properly, with spelling correct, and it turned out the somebody was me." She smiled, and it was a crooked, tired smile. "You asked what it bought me. It bought me a purpose, which is the most expensive thing there is, because once you have one you can't put it down."

"And the case? Vasseur's case?"

"Orders. Rosters. Photographs. Proof that the withdrawal wasn't negligence — it was sanctioned. Decided above him, signed, filed. He gave it to me six days ago because he's dying and he wanted the burden off his soul and onto mine, which I consider a thoroughly typical officer's maneuver — delegating." The bitterness surprised her with its freshness. Twelve years and it still had teeth.

Marta closed the ledger and held it flat against her chest. When she spoke again her voice had settled into something new — not the dry wit, not the raw grief, but a third thing, level and deliberate, like water finding its course.

"I spent three years deciding you were a good woman who minded her own business," she said. "I was half right. You're a good woman who minded everybody's business and told no one, which is worse, or better, I genuinely cannot decide." She looked up. "Here is what I need you to understand, Oscar Amberdale. I have been quiet for twelve years. I raised quiet into an art form. I came to this frozen garrison at the end of the world and I washed other women's sons' bloodstains out of sheets, and I told myself it was survival, and it was, and I regret nothing about surviving. But I am fifty-one years old, and I am tired, and my son has been a statistic with the wrong number attached to him for twelve years, and I have just learned that the right numbers have been sitting under your floorboard the entire time I've been here."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the case goes to someone. The historians — the ones in the east wing, the ones asking questions. I've seen the young one watching you. She watches you the way I used to watch the ferryman, wondering if today he'd take me across." Marta stood, and tucked the ledger back under the floorboard herself, gently, and replaced the board, and pressed it down with her palm as though sealing a promise. "And I'm saying that whatever you decide, you decide it knowing that I know. No more polite weight-carrying between us. If you send it out, I help. If you bury it, you'll have to look at me across the mangle every morning for the rest of our lives, and I promise you I can outlast you. I'm very good at lasting. It's the one thing Kolvik taught me."

Oscar looked at her — this woman she had liked for three years and never let herself wonder why, this woman whose letters she had copied by lamplight with her throat tight, this ally she had acquired without meaning to and could not now return.

"You understand what you're asking," she said quietly. "If this comes out, everyone who helped is exposed. You. Me. Anyone who stands with us. The commandant was there, Marta. At Kolvik. Men like him don't forget faces."

"I've been forgotten by better men than him." Marta pulled her shawl straight, and for the first time all evening, she smiled — a small, fierce, unlovely smile, the smile of a woman picking up a tool she'd sworn off years ago and finding her hands still remembered the grip. "Nine o'clock tomorrow. We start making copies. Your handwriting, my memory, and God's patience, if He's speaking to either of us."

She was at the door when the knock came — three quick raps, official, unfamiliar — and through the panel, the voice of the night orderly, apologetic and breathless:

"Beg pardon, Miss Iversen? Matron's compliments — the commandant's ordered a inventory of all storerooms and private quarters, first thing tomorrow. Documents, he says. There's documents gone missing somewhere in the garrison, and they're searching every room on the roster."

In the silence after his boots faded down the corridor, Marta and Oscar looked at each other across the small lamp-lit room, at the floorboard, at the case, at the twelve years balanced on top of them.

"Well," said Marta. "We'd best not wait for nine o'clock."
