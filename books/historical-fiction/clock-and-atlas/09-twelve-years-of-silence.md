## 9. Twelve Years of Silence

The floorboard came up easier in those days, or perhaps her knees had been younger and simply hadn't told her yet. Oscar knelt on the cold boards of her room above the laundry and did not take out the ledger. Not tonight. Tonight the ledger could wait, because tonight — as on every third or fourth night for twelve years — she was going back to Kolvik.

She had learned that memory was like the river: you could not stop it, only choose which bank to stand on while it went past.

* * *

The field hospital at Kolvik had been three tents and a barn, and the barn was the good ward because the roof held. It was October, the second October of the war, and the war had learned by then to be efficient. The wounded came up from the bridgehead in carts that smelled of straw and iodine and something under both that no one named. Oscar was twenty-six and had been a nurse for four years and had already stopped flinching at amputation saws, though she still flinched at the sound of geese, because geese sounded like the men who'd been gassed at Marlen Ford, and there was nothing to be done about that either.

Her superior was Sister Brandt, a woman built like a church pew and about as sympathetic, who ran the ward on tea, discipline, and an unshakable conviction that God kept the books so nurses didn't have to. Oscar loved her the way one loves a wall that is holding up the building.

"You've ink on your nose again," Sister Brandt said that morning, not looking up from the ration ledger. "You write like a blacksmith."

"I write legibly," said Oscar.

"You write *a lot*. There's a difference. A list should be a list, Amberdale, not a novel." She tapped the page. "Name, unit, wound, disposition. Four columns. Anything else is vanity."

Oscar had been adding a fifth column. She hadn't shown anyone. In the fifth column she wrote small things: *sings in his sleep. Asked twice if his mother wrote. Left boot missing, right foot fine.* Things the reports would never want. She told herself it was for the patients' sake — easier to know a man if you knew he sang — but even then, at twenty-six, some honest cellar of her mind knew better. She was keeping them. That was the word she avoided. Keeping.

The civilians had begun arriving the same week. Refugees from the east side of the river, walking out of a countryside that had decided against them. They camped in the orchard below the hospital because the military authority would not let them cross the bridge into the town — the bridge, everyone said, was needed for supply. So they waited in the orchard, four hundred souls by Sister Brandt's count, in tents made of blankets, cooking over fires made of fence rails, and every morning more of them came down the road with their bundles and their children and their terrible patience.

Oscar's ward took their sick. Measles, dysentery, a child with a burned arm, an old man whose heart was simply tired of the whole arrangement. She wrote them in a separate book, because Sister Brandt said mixing military and civilian records would cause "an argument above our pay," and in this book too she kept her fifth column. *Girl, 6, red mittens, answers to Liska. Man, farmer, coughs like a bellows, wife dead at Marlen, he thinks. Woman who has given her coat away twice.*

"Four columns," Sister Brandt said, catching her at it.

"This is my own book," Oscar said. "For my own sins."

Sister Brandt looked at her a long moment. "Then keep it where your sins can't be read," she said, which was, Oscar understood much later, the closest thing to a blessing the woman ever issued.

* * *

The order came on a Thursday, which felt like an insult to Thursdays.

Captain Reiner Hoss arrived at the barn at dusk with two escorts and a face like a closed drawer. He was not old — thirty at most — but he had the voice officers acquire when they have stopped listening to themselves: flat, pre-recorded, a gramophone of a man.

"The bridgehead is being consolidated," he announced to the assembled staff. "Effective tomorrow, oh-six-hundred, all non-essential personnel withdraw across the river. The hospital relocates to Varnholt."

"And the civilians?" Sister Brandt asked.

Hoss consulted his paper, as though the answer might be written there in someone else's handwriting. "The civilian population remains in place pending further disposition."

"In the orchard," Sister Brandt said. "In October. Without the hospital."

"The disposition is under review."

"By whom?"

"By the appropriate authority." He said it the way a man says *by the weather*, meaning: I did not make the rain, do not shout at me about the rain.

There were four hundred people in that orchard. There were measles in tent two and a boy with a perforated bowel whom Oscar had sat up with for two nights and who would die without surgery inside the day. There was a woman nine months pregnant who had chosen the orchard's one dry corner with the instinct of a cat and defended it accordingly.

"The appropriate authority," Sister Brandt repeated, in the tone she used for bedsores found early.

"Colonel Vasseur's headquarters," Hoss said, and for the first time his gramophone voice skipped, just slightly. "I have my orders, Sister. You have yours. Pack."

He stayed to watch them pack. That was the part Oscar remembered most, afterward — not the order itself, orders were weather, but that Hoss stood in the barn doorway while they folded cots and counted morphine tablets, watching them with the expression of a man memorizing his own alibi.

It was later that night, in the barn office lit by one storm lantern, that he produced the list.

Not her list. A roster — the official register of civilians treated at Kolvik since September, compiled at headquarters' request, names and ages and dispositions, four columns, no vanity. Someone at HQ wanted to know exactly who was in that orchard. Oscar understood, in the slow cold way one understands things at midnight, why a staff officer might want such a document before doing what was going to be done in that orchard, and what one did with documents afterward.

"Burn it," Hoss said.

"It's a copy," Oscar said. "The original is at—"

"There is no original. As of tonight there was never a register. Burn it." He set the lantern closer to her, courteous as a waiter. "You're the record-keeper. Records are your responsibility. That's rather the point of you."

She looked at the pages. Two hundred and six names. She had written most of them herself, in her careful blacksmith's hand. *Liska, 6, red mittens.* Somewhere on the third page, the farmer who coughed like a bellows. Somewhere near him, the woman who gave her coat away.

"If I refuse," she said, "you'll burn it yourself and find another nurse to sign the inventory."

"Yes," Hoss said. He did not enjoy saying it. That was somehow worse. "And then I'd have to report the refusal, and the report would go where reports go, and none of it would help anyone in that orchard. You know how this works. You've known how this works longer than I have."

She hated him for being right, and she hated more that he seemed to hate being right, as if decency were a coat he'd been issued and couldn't return.

So she took the pages to the stove. And here is the thing she told no one for twelve years, the thing that lived under the floorboard of her chest along with everything else: she did not burn all of it.

The stove was hot and the paper went quickly, and Hoss stood watching until the first several pages were ash — enough that he was satisfied, enough that the ritual was complete and witnessed. Then Sister Brandt called him to the door — a summons about transport manifests, perfectly genuine, perfectly timed; Oscar never learned whether it was accident or the pew-built woman's one act of rebellion — and in the forty seconds he was gone, Oscar slid the last leaf from the stack and pushed it deep into the coals' cold edge, beneath the grate, where it blackened at the corners and died quietly instead of loudly.

One leaf. Perhaps thirty names. It was the bottom of the register — the last arrivals, the ones she'd entered latest, in her worst handwriting, smudged. Among them: *Marta Voss, 31, husband Erik Voss, 34, laborer, orchard camp, wife expectant.*

She pressed the scorched leaf flat between the pages of her own book, her sin-book, her fifth-column book, and when Hoss returned she was feeding the rest of the ash to the fire and complaining, truthfully, about the draft.

"Good," Hoss said. He almost managed gratitude. "For what it's worth—" and then he stopped, because for what it was worth was nothing, and they both knew the exchange rate.

He left before dawn with the withdrawing column. The hospital went east to Varnholt in eight carts. Oscar rode in the last one, facing backward, watching the orchard shrink: the blanket-tents, the fence-rail fires, the red mittens of a child chasing a dog between the apple trees. The boy with the perforated bowel had died at noon, mercifully ahead of schedule. The pregnant woman had waved at the carts. Oscar had waved back and felt her own smile lie through its teeth.

What happened at Kolvik Bridge happened eleven days later, and Oscar learned of it in fragments, the way one learns of distant earthquakes — a rumor, a number, a correction downward, a correction upward. The official account used the phrase *tragic necessity* within the month. The unofficial accounts used other words and were not printed. Sister Brandt, who had gone with a volunteer party to the west bank in December under a flag of parley, came back gray and silent and resigned her commission in January, and when Oscar asked her — once, stupidly, wanting comfort — *was it as bad as they say*, Sister Brandt had looked at her with the first and only kindness of their acquaintance and said:

"You kept a book, didn't you."

It wasn't a question. Sisters always know which novices are hiding sweets.

"Then keep it," she said. "Keep it well. Names are the only currency the dead can spend."

She died the following winter, of her heart, which had carried too much. She left Oscar her tea tin. It contained, along with the tea, a slip of paper: *Four columns are for the living. Write the fifth column for God, and hide it from His clerks.*

* * *

That was the year the ledger began. Not as resistance — she was too tired, too frightened, too ordinary for resistance. It began as bookkeeping. The war was ending everywhere except in the paperwork, and the paperwork was winning. Men who had died at Kolvik appeared on rolls as *transferred*. Civilians appeared nowhere at all. Deserters shot behind the granary at Dolsk were recorded as *unaccounted*, a phrase Oscar came to loathe with a purity of feeling she otherwise reserved for very few things. Unaccounted. As though the universe had misplaced them. As though no one had counted.

So she counted. Quietly, in pencil first, then ink, in a plain school exercise book bought from a stationer in Varnholt who assumed she was a teacher. Name, age, unit or village, date, manner of death where she knew it, source. She copied the scorched leaf entire, every smudged name, before the paper could crumble further, and filed the original between waxed cloth. Marta Voss. Erik Voss. Thirty names off one saved fragment, and the other hundred and seventy-six she gathered over years, from refugees, from letters, from a laundry-woman's memory, from a priest's buried register, from a corporal who talked too much after two glasses of plum brandy and wept into the third.

Some nights the work felt holy. Most nights it felt like mopping a floor during rain. Once, early on, she showed a page to a doctor she nearly trusted, and he read it, went pale, and said, "You must destroy this, you understand. For your sake. For all of ours." And she had said yes, sister, thank you, sister, and destroyed nothing, and trusted no one again for years. Secrecy, she discovered, is a skill like any other: practice makes permanent. By the fifth year she could lie about the ledger's existence with her pulse slowing. By the tenth she could lie about nearly anything, which she tried not to examine too closely, examination being dangerous to a load-bearing wall.

The habit had grown over her like bark over wire. She was aware of it the way one is aware of a limp in cold weather. She was, she admitted on the bad nights, no longer entirely sure whether she kept the ledger to give the dead their due or because she no longer knew how to stop — whether it was a lamp or a wound she kept reopening to check the healing. Both, probably. Sister Brandt would have said both, and then made her a cup of tea strong enough to strip varnish.

* * *

Now, kneeling on the floorboards of her room above the laundry, twelve years and one winter later, Oscar let the board settle back into place and sat in the dark with her hands flat on her knees.

Tomorrow — today, rather; the clock downstairs had given up its ghost with a wheeze around midnight — Colonel Vasseur lay in her ward with pneumonia in both lungs, and he had looked at her across the ward noise with recognition, and worse than recognition: with relief. *Amberdale,* he'd said, as if she were the answer to a prayer he'd been ashamed to make. She had despised the man for twelve years by proxy, by association, by the simple arithmetic of Kolvik, and now he was going to cough his way toward death twenty feet from her desk, and she was going to nurse him competently, because that was the job, and competence was the only revenge available to women like her.

But he had said her name like a man holding something heavy. And officers who held things heavy near Oscar Amberdale had a historical tendency to hand them to her.

She got into bed and pulled the blanket to her chin and stared at the ceiling, where the frost made maps of countries that didn't exist and never would, being made only of cold and glass.

"Four columns," she informed the ceiling, in Sister Brandt's voice, "are for the living."

Outside, the river was beginning to talk in its new winter voice, ice forming at the pilings with small cracking sounds, like knuckles, like a lock turning. Within the week the bridge would close, and whatever was meant to cross it — ledgers, cases, confessions, truths — had better cross soon.

She fell asleep at last with the scorched leaf's names running through her head like a rosary, and woke to the bell of the ward, and to the news, passed orderly to orderly with the morning's bread, that the colonel's fever had climbed in the night, and that he was asking for Nurse Amberdale by name, and would not stop.
