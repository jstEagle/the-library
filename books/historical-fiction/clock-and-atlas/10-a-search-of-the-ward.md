## 10. A Search of the Ward

The dead man's name was Sorel.

He had come into the convalescent ward eleven days earlier with a bullet wound in his thigh that had been healing beautifully right up until the moment it wasn't, and now he lay in the corner bed by the window with a sheet drawn up to his chin and his boots — polished, absurdly, by his own hand two days before he died — placed neatly beneath it, toes pointing at the door, as though he intended to step back into them on his way out.

Oscar had liked him. He was the sort of patient who thanked you for the bedpan as if you'd brought him flowers. He had a wife in Tarnow and a habit of describing her cooking at such length and in such loving detail that the whole row of beds would lie awake at night groaning with hunger. He had died on a Tuesday, quietly, between one round of broth and the next, and the paperwork of his leaving was still working its way through the garrison's machinery, which moved with the speed of a glacier and roughly the same warmth.

Which meant that Private Sorel's belongings were still here. Packed, tagged, inventoried, waiting for the transport clerk to find a wagon going toward Tarnow — which none would, now, until spring.

Oscar stood at the foot of his bed on Thursday morning, holding a cup of coffee she had no intention of drinking, and looked at those belongings the way a gambler looks at a bad hand.

* * *

It had begun, as catastrophes in garrisons always do, with a rumor wearing a uniform.

She had heard it first from Orderly Beck, who heard it from the cook, who heard it from the wife of the adjutant, who heard it — this part was spoken with great reverence, as though tracing the word of God down through the prophets — *from the adjutant himself*. Documents were circulating. Improper documents. Someone at headquarters had used the phrase "seditious papers," and someone else had used the phrase "a thorough accounting," and by the time the rumor reached the kitchen it had grown a commandant, a search, and a firing squad, in that order.

By ten o'clock it had shrunk back down to its actual size: the commandant had ordered the hospital buildings searched, beginning with the convalescent ward, at noon.

"Beginning with," Beck repeated to Oscar in the linen room, wringing a towel as though it owed him money. "You know what that means. We're not an afterthought, Sister. We're the overture."

"Thank you, Beck. The towels."

"The point is—"

"You're strangling that towel."

He stopped. Looked at the towel. Set it down gently, like something wounded.

"Sister Amberdale," he said, lowering his voice to the register men use when they are about to be brave on someone else's behalf, "if there's anything in this building that shouldn't be found, I could look the other way for five minutes. I'm very good at it. It's practically a medical skill."

And there it was: the moment the floor opened under her feet, quiet as a trapdoor in a well-run house. Because Beck had noticed *something*. Not everything, thank God — not the ledger, never the ledger; the ledger lived under the floorboard of her own quarters across the yard, wrapped in oilcloth, twelve years of names sleeping in the dark — but the dispatch case. The case Colonel Vasseur had pressed on her six days ago with hands hot as bread ovens, the case full of orders and rosters and photographs of Kolvik Bridge, the case she had been carrying from hiding place to hiding place like a woman smuggling her own heart.

Currently it lived behind the loose panel in the dispensary, behind the bottles of iodine nobody used because the new surgeon preferred something modern that smelled worse.

Beck knew about the panel. Everyone knew about the panel. It was where you hid your gin.

"No," Oscar said. "Nothing is hidden anywhere. That's the point of nothing being hidden."

He looked at her the way dogs look at a whistle only they can hear. Then he nodded, once, and picked the towel back up, and said no more about it, which was its own kind of loyalty, and which she would remember.

* * *

The trouble with the dispensary panel was precisely that everyone knew about it. A search that found nothing in the obvious places would not stay a search long. Commandants who smell smoke kick over stoves.

So Oscar spent the hours before noon doing arithmetic.

The case was too big for her person. She had measured this against herself in the dark, twice, with the grim thoroughness of a dressmaker: strapped to her thigh beneath skirts it changed her walk; against her spine it announced itself like a hunchback. And a search of a hospital would include, without question, the nurses. She had been searched before — twelve years ago, at Kolvik, by a nervous lieutenant with cold hands — and she remembered the particular humiliation of standing still while a stranger decided what she was allowed to be.

No. The case could not be on her body or in any of the places bodies kept things.

It could not be in the dispensary. It could not be in the linen room, the store room, the chapel — the chapel would be searched *first*, chapels always were, the army's idea of a joke — and it could not go out of doors, because the yard was a bowl of white silence in which every footprint was a confession.

Which left, when she finally let her mind go there, the corner bed by the window.

Private Sorel's trunk sat at the foot of his bed, locked, tagged, inventoried. Inside it, per the inventory she had witnessed and signed — she signed these things; it was part of the terrible ordinary courtesy of hospitals — were: one winter coat, damaged; two shirts; one photograph, framed; letters, bundle; pipe; tobacco tin; playing cards, incomplete; bootlaces; a knitted scarf, blue, made by the wife in Tarnow, which he had worn indoors like a bishop and which had made the orderlies laugh and then, somehow, stop laughing and start knitting requests for their own mothers.

And the inventory, Oscar thought, was the beautiful part. The inventory was a lock of its own. Because no searching officer, confronted with a dead private's sealed effects, would open that trunk — not because he couldn't, but because opening it meant *counting*. Counting meant writing. Writing meant signing. And somewhere down the chain of command, some clerk would compare the count, and if one item were missing — one pipe, one incomplete deck of cards — then some sergeant would be answering questions about theft from the dead, and sergeants did not forgive that kind of arithmetic.

A dead man's trunk was not a hiding place. It was a *bureaucratic instrument*. Nothing on earth was harder to open.

She only had to put the case inside it, and the case became a dead soldier's shirt.

* * *

She waited until the ward was empty at half past eleven — patients at physiotherapy, orderlies at dinner, Vasseur upstairs in the fever ward delirious and calling for her, which was a wound in itself she had decided not to bleed from until evening — and then she did it.

The trunk's lock was a joke; Sorel had lost the key months ago and opened it with a knife handle. The dispatch case slid in beneath the shirts, flat against the bottom, and she arranged the folded linen over it with the tenderness she'd have used on him, and shut the lid, and turned the little hasp, and stood there with her hand resting on the cool wood.

"I'm sorry," she told him. She meant it for the wife in Tarnow too, though she couldn't have said what exactly she was apologizing for. For borrowing him. For making him carry, posthumously, more than his share. Twelve years of war had taught her that the dead did most of the hauling.

Then she went and washed her hands, because there was a ward to run, and terror is no excuse for unmade beds.

* * *

They came at noon precisely, which surprised her; she had expected the army to be late even to its own paranoia.

There were six of them: a lieutenant with a clipboard and the unfortunate authority of youth, four military policemen with the patient faces of men who had done this to barracks and brothels alike and saw no reason a hospital should be different, and — her stomach dropped a full floor — the commandant himself.

Major Halloran was not a large man, but he had the gift, common to certain officers, of making rooms feel under-staffed. He stood in the doorway of the convalescent ward in his greatcoat, snow melting off the shoulders onto the clean floor, and looked down the row of empty beds the way a man looks at a bill of lading.

"Sister Amberdale." He did not remove his cap. "Thank you for your cooperation in advance."

"You haven't asked for anything yet, Major."

"I'm asking now. My men will conduct a search of these premises. There is reason to believe certain documents are circulating within the garrison — correspondence of an unofficial nature. You will understand that I can't be more specific."

"Circulating," Oscar said. "Like a magazine?"

The lieutenant's pen paused. One of the MPs developed an urgent interest in the ceiling.

Halloran's face didn't move, which was how she knew she'd scored. "Like papers that ought to be in official custody and are not," he said. "You'll assemble your staff, please. They will remain in the dayroom during the search. You will remain with me."

"Because I'm the danger, Major, or the tour guide?"

"Because," he said, moving down the ward, unhurried, reading bed tags as he went, "you've been here longest. Twelve years, the records say. You'll know better than anyone what belongs in this building and what doesn't." He stopped at the corner bed. He looked at the sheet, the still shape of it — they had taken Sorel to the mortuary that morning, so it was only the mattress's memory of him now — and then down at the trunk, and Oscar felt her pulse perform one enormous drumbeat and resume as though nothing had happened.

"Poor devil," Halloran said. "Sorel. The thigh?"

"The thigh."

"Healing well, I heard."

"They generally are, Major, right up until they aren't. That's rather the nature of it."

He crouched — actually crouched, joints cracking, a man past the age at which crouching came free — and laid one gloved hand flat on the trunk's lid. Oscar watched him the way she had once watched a doctor's face over a child's chart.

Then he straightened, wincing, and said, "Effects?"

"Inventoried and tagged. Waiting on transport to Tarnow. Transport closes with the bridge." She held out the clipboard with the inventory sheet, already signed, already witnessed, already triple-locked in triplicate. "You're welcome to verify the contents, of course. I'll need to note the seal break, and you'll need to countersign, and the effects officer at Tarnow will need to be wired, since the count won't match his manifest otherwise. It's about an hour of paperwork, all told. More, if we have to re-inventory."

She said it lightly. She said it the way you discuss laundry schedules. And she watched the arithmetic happen behind the major's eyes — one hour, two signatures, a wire, a discrepancy, a question from some captain in Tarnow whose name would end up on Halloran's desk — and she thought, with a ferocity that frightened her a little: *count it. Go on. Open the trunk of a dead private over some papers you can't even name, and explain to the effects officer, and explain to the widow, and explain to the audit.*

Halloran glanced at the inventory. His eyes moved down the list — coat, shirts, photograph, letters, pipe, cards, scarf — items so humble they were practically a eulogy.

"No need," he said. "Dead men keep their own counsel." And he moved on.

Oscar discovered she had been holding her breath long enough to feel faint, and let it out slowly through her nose, like a lady.

* * *

The search took two hours and found, officially, nothing.

Unofficially it found: a bottle of cherry brandy behind the chapel organ (chapel first, as predicted; Oscar nearly wept with vindication), a dog (live, indignant, belonging to no roster), forty-one cigarettes' worth of contraband tobacco distributed among three orderlies, and a cache of letters under a mattress in the surgical annex that turned out to be love letters, which the lieutenant read for longer than procedure strictly required, blushing the entire time, before tagging them as evidence anyway, thereby ensuring their eventual destruction and the permanent enmity of Corporal Vasik, whose handwriting they were.

Oscar stood beside Halloran through all of it with her hands folded, offering commentary in the mild voice of a museum docent, and inside she was somewhere else entirely: standing very still in the corner of her own chest, next to a trunk, listening.

Once — only once — the search came near. An MP, working methodically down the window side, paused at Sorel's bed, lifted the mattress, checked underneath, and moved on. He did not touch the trunk. Nobody touched the trunk. The trunk sat with its tags fluttering slightly in the draft from the door, guarding its guest, keeping its count.

At half past two Halloran called his men off, accepted the cherry brandy with a sigh that suggested it was the least surprising thing in his week, and put his cap back on.

"Thank you for your cooperation, Sister."

"You're welcome for my cooperation, Major."

He paused at the door. Snow had stopped melting off him; he'd dried at the shoulders, gone white at the edges, like a man being drawn in chalk. "They say the historians arrive within the week," he remarked, to the doorway more than to her. "Before the bridge closes. Two of them, from the capital. Compiling the campaign record." A beat. "Official history is a great comfort to the bereaved, I've found. It tells them exactly what happened. All of it. In the proper order."

"How restful for them," Oscar said.

"Yes," said Halloran. "Isn't it."

And he left, and the ward filled back up with its ordinary noises — Beck's laugh from the dayroom, a spoon against a basin, the river talking to itself under the ice — and Oscar walked, unhurried, a nurse with rounds to make, to the corner bed by the window, and laid her palm flat on the trunk lid for just a moment, feeling the small warmth of the day's terror still radiating out of the wood.

"Held," she murmured. "Good soldier."

That evening Vasseur's fever broke, briefly, and he woke clear-eyed and asked whether the case was safe, and she lied to him, and told him yes, and he slept like a man acquitted.

And two mornings later, before the frost had given up its grip on the windows, the sledge bells sounded in the lane, and Beck came through at a run with the extraordinary news that the historians had beaten the ice after all — two of them from the capital, arrived on the last passable road, and been installed in the officers' wing — and that the young one, the female one, had already sent down to the hospital asking, of all things, for the admission registers.

Asking. For records. Of Oscar's ward. By name.

Oscar set down her pen mid-entry and looked out the frosted glass at the white lane, where two figures were climbing down from a sledge wrapped in furs, one tall, one slight, and felt the peculiar sensation — familiar from old battlefields, unwelcome everywhere — of hope arriving dressed for the other side.
