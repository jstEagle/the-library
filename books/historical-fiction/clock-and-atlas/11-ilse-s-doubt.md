## 11. Ilse's Doubt

The Historical Commission worked, in theory, according to method. Method was the word Dr. Kesselheim loved best — he said it the way other men said *brandy* or *darling*, letting it roll around his mouth as though it had a flavor. Method, he insisted, distinguished the historian from the memoirist, the archivist from the gossip, the servant of truth from the mere collector of anecdotes.

Ilse Brandt had come to the garrison believing this completely. She had packed her reference cards in alphabetical order, her ink in unbreakable bottles, her conviction in a compartment of her chest she assumed was watertight.

That was before she began transcribing the interviews.

The commission's routine was this: each morning, witnesses were brought to the commandeered schoolroom — soldiers mostly, a few civilians, once a laundress who stared at the floor as though it owed her money — and their testimony was taken down by Ilse in shorthand. Each afternoon, the fair copy was made, and each evening Dr. Kesselheim reviewed the day's pages, initialed them, and locked them in the commission's dispatch box, from which they would travel, when the ice broke, to the capital and into the official campaign record, there to be bound, shelved, cited, and believed for approximately forever.

It was on a Tuesday, with snow ticking against the schoolroom windows like a polite but persistent visitor, that Ilse noticed the discrepancy.

She had transcribed the same sergeant's account twice — once three weeks ago, when the commission arrived, and again yesterday, because Kesselheim wanted "confirmation of details." Confirmation of details was one of his phrases. In the first version, Sergeant Brack had said that on the third day of the retreat his company passed through the burning village of Alden and found no one alive. In the second version, he said the same thing, in nearly the same words, because memory is loyal that way.

But the two fair copies did not match.

In the first, Brack's account ran eleven pages and included, near the end, a paragraph about an artillery captain who had ordered a farmhouse cleared for a gun position while its cellar still held wounded men. The paragraph was dry, factual, terrible in the way only facts can be. Ilse remembered copying it and having to stop, twice, to look out the window at the ordinary white world.

In the second, the paragraph was gone. Not struck through — gone, lifted cleanly, the page renumbered so that nothing showed. And where it had been, someone had inserted a new passage, in Kesselheim's small upright hand, describing how the captain — now named, now decorated, now a general — had *"personally supervised the evacuation of non-combatants with exemplary care."*

Exemplary care. Ilse read the phrase four times. It did not improve with repetition.

She looked across the room at Kesselheim, who was dictating to himself as he wrote, a habit of his, murmuring his own sentences like a man feeding pigeons. He was fifty-three, pink-cheeked, fond of his pipe and his jokes about the garrison coffee, and he had once, over cards, taught her a song about a tax collector's donkey that she still hummed while washing. She liked him. That was precisely the problem, she realized. Liking him had been doing quiet work all these weeks, standing between her and questions she ought to have asked in October.

She asked one now, mildly, the way you'd ask about the weather.

"Dr. Kesselheim — in Brack's second deposition, I seem to have lost a passage. The Alden farmhouse."

Kesselheim's pen continued moving for a moment, finishing a sentence, which was somehow worse than if he'd stopped.

"Redundant material," he said pleasantly. "Brack repeated himself. The record benefits from concision."

"Did he repeat himself? I have both transcripts. He didn't mention the general at all in the first one. He mentioned a captain."

"A promotion occurred between the events and our arrival. Titles must be kept current; otherwise the record confuses its readers." He smiled at her over his spectacles, warm as a stove. "You'll learn, Fräulein Brandt: history is not what happened. History is what is written down carefully enough that nobody checks."

He said it as a joke. She was almost certain he meant it as a joke. She laughed, because not laughing would have required an explanation, and explanations were doors she wasn't ready to walk through in daylight.

But that evening, alone in the small room she shared with the commission's crates, she took out her own notebook — the one she kept under her mattress, technically against regulations, technically containing nothing but drafts and reminders — and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in letters smaller than usual:

*Alden farmhouse. First version: 11 pp., para. 9. Second version: para. 9 replaced.*

Then she sat with her chin in her hands and considered what she had just done, and what it made her.

* * *

An institution's employee, Ilse reflected, is a person who has agreed to be part of a machine in exchange for the machine agreeing not to notice her. She had signed that agreement at twenty-two, gratefully. The archive in the capital was warm in winter, cool in summer, and smelled gloriously of paper and floor wax. Her colleagues were serious people who believed, or had once believed, that they were building a cathedral of fact, stone by verified stone. Nobody there thought of themselves as capable of removing a paragraph. Removals happened to other institutions, less careful ones, foreign ones.

And yet here was the arithmetic, laid out plainly on her lap: a paragraph about wounded men in a cellar had been exchanged for a sentence about exemplary care, and the exchange had been initialed by a man who sang about donkeys.

She spent a bad night cataloguing excuses for him. Fatigue — he was fifty-three and the light was poor. Pressure — the commission had a mandate and a deadline and superiors above superiors. Innocence — perhaps the general's staff had sent corrections, and Kesselheim was merely the postman. This last excuse held up longest, until she remembered that corrections arrive in writing, and that she filed the commission's correspondence, and that no correction had ever crossed her desk.

By morning she had exhausted mercy and arrived somewhere colder and more useful: curiosity. What else had been exchanged? She was, after all, the person closest to the machine's gears. Every transcript passed through her hands twice — once as shorthand, once as fair copy. If anyone in this frozen garrison could compare the before and after, it was her.

She began that afternoon, quietly, between duties. It was tedious work and terrifying work, and it would have been impossible without the garrison's gift for bureaucratic redundancy. Everything was copied; everything was filed; everything was stamped. The army trusted paper more than people, and Ilse had come to share the opinion.

She found the pattern within four days.

Small things first. A lieutenant's description of looting in a captured depot softened to "requisition of materiel." A civilian woman's account of soldiers taking her livestock gained, in fair copy, a clause about compensation received — a clause the woman had never spoken, and which Ilse knew for fiction because the woman had wept about the cow. Then larger things. A whole deposition from a corporal who had served under General Reinhalt — the general of the *exemplary care*, the general whose campaign this record would crown — reduced from nine pages to four, the missing five concerning, among other matters, an order given at a place called Kolvik.

Kolvik. The word surfaced in documents the way a body surfaces in a river: reluctantly, briefly, always face down. It appeared in supply requisitions. It appeared once in a road report. It appeared in Brack's first transcript too — a crossing, a bridge, a crowd — and then, in the second, did not appear at all, as though the village had been unmade, decreed out of existence by whoever held the pen.

Ilse sat very still in the schoolroom after everyone had gone, holding Brack's two versions side by side, and felt something shift in her chest with an audible click, like a lock accepting a key it had been waiting years for.

She had assumed, until now, that she was guarding the truth for other people — the dead, the distant public, posterity, that patient shareholder. She understood suddenly that this was vanity dressed as duty. Nobody had asked her to guard anything. The question was simpler and much more frightening: what was *she* going to do?

* * *

Copying a suppressed page was absurdly easy, which was its own indictment of everything she'd believed about careful institutions.

On Sunday the commission rested — officially. Kesselheim played cards with the commandant's adjutant and lost cheerfully and at length. The dispatch box stayed in the schoolroom, unlocked, because the lock had rusted in November and nobody had reported it, because reporting things in a garrison generated forms, and forms generated work, and work was the true enemy of every military organization ever assembled.

Ilse came back after supper with a plausible errand — her gloves, forgotten, a lie so small it barely qualified — and stood for a full minute in the cold room listening to the stove tick, giving her conscience every last chance to object.

Her conscience, traitorously, said nothing. So she did it.

She copied the Kolvik paragraphs by hand into her notebook, in her smallest script, working fast, her breath loud in the empty room. The corporal's words were plain and awful: the order to clear the bridge, the crowd already on it, the timing chosen for the withdrawal, the numbers the corporal had heard shouted — and then the sentence that stopped her pen mid-stroke:

*Nobody gave the order to wait.*

Five words. She copied them twice, involuntarily, the way you touch a bruise.

She put the originals back exactly as they'd lain, locked nothing, told no one, walked back through the blue evening snow to her room with the notebook flat against her ribs like a second heart, and lay on her bed staring at the ceiling beams.

She should take it to Kesselheim. Confront him, give him the chance — perhaps the exchanges had orders behind them, perhaps he was squeezed between mandate and conscience, perhaps there was a version of this in which he was another trapped man, another person surviving a winter. She composed the conversation three times, complete with his likely answers, and got nowhere, because every version ended the same way: he would apologize gently, explain reasonably, and by Thursday the paragraph — and her notebook, and probably Ilse herself — would be dealt with. Institutions were kind to employees who brought them problems and merciless with employees who *were* problems. She had watched it happen twice in the capital, to better people than she.

Should she go outside the commission? To the commandant? The commandant whose adjutant played cards with Kesselheim, whose garrison's honor the record would burnish? She thought of the commandant's smooth, unhurried voice at dinner and discarded the idea before it finished forming.

To the witnesses, then. To the people on the other side of her table all these weeks — the sergeant with his careful repetitions, the laundress who hated the floor, the hospital workers who came in with eyes that had seen things and voices that had decided, long ago, to stop mentioning them.

She turned onto her side. There was the nurse. Sister Amberdale, the ward matron — a woman Ilse had interviewed twice and liked in a wary, unresolved way, the way you like a door you suspect leads somewhere you're not ready to go. Oscar Amberdale listened the way few people listen: entirely, without preparing her reply. And twice, when Ilse had asked routine questions from the standard schedule — *dates, units, numbers present* — the sister had answered correctly, fluently, and then paused half a beat too long afterward, as though checking whether the answer had cost anything.

Numbers. Ilse had the sudden, total recollection that Sister Amberdale had known, without notes, exactly how many wounded had passed through the field hospital at Kolvik in the week before the evacuation. Had volunteered it, even, quietly correcting Ilse's summary figure downward by eleven, and had said, when Ilse thanked her: *"Eleven matters. Somebody counted them once."*

Somebody counted them once.

Ilse lay in the dark with the stove dying and the river groaning somewhere below the town — it did that at night lately, a deep complaining sound, ice under strain — and understood that she had crossed a line and could not find it behind her anymore. An hour ago she had been an employee of an institution. The institution had a mandate that expired when the ice broke; after that, sealed, bound, believed, forever. She had maybe six weeks. She had a copied page that proved a living general had been flattered over the graves of people nobody would count.

She was not brave. She wanted that recorded too, somewhere, honestly: she was frightened, she liked her warm archive, she missed her mother, she had never in her life done anything that might get her sent anywhere colder than a filing room. But the employee, she discovered, was a costume, and she had grown out of it sometime around the moment she copied those five words twice.

*Nobody gave the order to wait.* Well. Somebody could refuse to stop counting.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would find a reason to visit the hospital ward — her cough was conveniently real, the garrison air a genuine offense — and sit by Sister Amberdale's stove, and ask one small question, and watch the pause that followed it.

She blew out her candle. Outside, the river groaned again, longer this time, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to wake.

The notebook lay under her mattress, over her heart, getting warm.
