## 12. The Colonel Dies

He died the way he had lived — badly timed, stubbornly loud about it, and in the way of everyone else's sleep.

I had known for three nights that it was coming. You learn to read dying the way farmers read weather: not from any single sign but from a pressure in the room, a smell under the antiseptic, a quality to the breathing that makes you stop mid-sentence and listen. Private Hollis had it on Tuesday; by Thursday morning we had moved his bed to the corner by the window where the light was kind, and by Friday afternoon the light was all he had left of anything. Colonel Vasseur had it now, and being Vasseur, he was not going to let a little thing like pneumonia take him quietly when he could make an occasion of it.

"Oscar." His voice came out of the dark end of the ward, cracked down the middle like river ice. "Oscar Amberdale."

Not *Sister*. Not *Nurse*. He had never once called me either, not at Kolvik and not here, and I felt the old anger rise up in me, familiar as my own hands. Twelve years, and the man could still find the nerve I'd buried him without.

I crossed the ward. The orderlies had banked his stove too high; the room smelled of coal smoke and camphor and the particular sweetness of a body working its way toward its own ending. His eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling, tracking something up there that only he could see. Perhaps the ceiling of the field hospital at Kolvik. Perhaps nothing at all.

"You should be sleeping," I said, because it was easier than everything else I might have said.

"I slept in '09," he said. "It was overrated."

Despite myself — despite twelve years of practicing despising this man in the careful, economical way one practices shorthand or bread-baking — something in my chest did a small treacherous hop. He had been a witty man once. That was the unforgivable part, if I am honest, which I am attempting to be in these pages precisely because I have spent so long not being it. It is easy to hate a stupid man who does monstrous things. Vasseur had been clever, charming even, in the way of men who have never once had to sit in a room with the consequences of their cleverness.

"You're burning up," I said. I put my hand on his wrist without thinking, professional habit, counting what I could no longer fix. The pulse ran quick and shallow, a bird's pulse, a sparrow caught in a barn.

"The numbers," he said.

And there it was. The word fell into the room between us like a coin dropped down a well, and we both listened for it to hit bottom.

"What numbers, Colonel?" I said it evenly. An orderly, young Pell, was folding linens at the far end of the ward, close enough to hear the shape of our voices but I hoped not the words. The whole garrison knew the colonel was dying; half of them drifted past his door on invented errands, hungry for a last anecdote to carry to the mess hall. A dying colonel is the winter's only theatre.

"The arithmetic," Vasseur whispered. "Nobody ever does the arithmetic."

I pulled the chair closer and sat, and arranged my face into the expression I had worn for twelve years at bedsides: attentive, neutral, a face like a closed ledger. "Then rest," I said. "You can do sums in the morning."

But he laughed — a terrible sound, half cough, and I held the basin for him while his whole thin frame shook — and when it passed he gripped my sleeve with fingers that had lost their argument with gravity, and said, "You were there. You know them. Four hundred and six, Amberdale. Do you still say four hundred and six?"

The ward tilted. Twelve years fell away with the rude speed of a floor giving way, and I was twenty-six again, standing in a tent that smelled of iodine and snow, watching a column of civilians walk back out of the wire into the dark because a decorated officer on a bicycle had decided the bridge could not be reopened, not for *them*, not tonight, and someone had to tell them, and it would not be him, and it could not be the junior lieutenant whose hands shook so badly he couldn't hold the lantern steady, so it had been me. Me with the list. Me with the lantern. Me saying, *the bridge will reopen at first light, keep together, keep warm,* knowing — knowing — that first light would bring the artillery and the ice would bring the river, and four hundred and six souls walked out of my lamplight into both.

"Not here," I said. My voice came out low and strange, someone else's voice. "Colonel, not here."

"Where, then?" He turned his head, and his eyes found me, and they were clear — horribly, briefly clear, the fever having burned away whatever comfortable fog most men die inside. "There was nowhere then and there is nowhere now, and you know it, which is why you're sitting in my ward at four in the morning looking like a woman doing sums herself."

* * *

Let me say plainly what I could not say to him: I hated him, and I hated that he was dying badly, and I hated most of all that some sliver of me had spent twelve years waiting for exactly this — for the man to come to the end of himself and hand me the truth like a man settling a debt — and now that it had arrived I could not decide whether I wanted to catch it or throw it back in the river.

He dozed after that, or something near it, and I sat with him because it was my shift anyway and because leaving felt like a confession. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the corridor Matron Brundt dropped a tray and swore with the fluency of thirty years' service, and Private Hollis slept on by his window, and the garrison clock counted the small hours with the smug patience of a machine that has never once been asked to hurry.

At half past five he began again.

This time it wasn't to me. This time the fever had him properly, and he was talking to the room — to the orderlies, to Pell, to young Sorensen who had come on early and stood wringing a bedpan cloth as though it might give counsel, to the stove, to the ceiling, to Kolvik.

"Hold the bridge until dawn," he said, in the parade-ground voice that forty years of command had bolted onto a man who had probably once been a boy who liked mathematics. "Hold the bridge. No. Countermand. Countermand the countermand." A pause, wet breathing. "Four hundred and six crossed at dusk. Four hundred and six went out. Count them, Amberdale, count them out, you count everything, count them—" and then, softer, in a voice like a child's, "—it was the arithmetic that failed. Not the bridge. The arithmetic."

Pell had stopped folding. Sorensen had stopped wringing. They stood in the grey pre-dawn light looking at their dying colonel the way people look at weather they don't understand — not frightened exactly, but aware that something large was passing overhead and that the wise thing was to stand very still.

"Numbers, sir?" Pell asked me, kindly, helpfully, wanting to write it off. "Delirium takes them all sorts of ways, my mum said her father counted horses at the end."

"He was at Kolvik," Sorensen said, in the hushed tone of a man citing scripture or scandal. "My uncle served under him. Kolvik Bridge. They don't teach it, but everyone's heard."

"There's nothing to hear," I said, and heard how it sounded — the exact voice, God help me, of every officer who had ever said *nothing to see, move along* — and Pell gave me an odd look, and I busied my hands with the water jug as though hydration were the colonel's most pressing concern.

Dawn came up grey behind the frost-ferned windows. The river made its sounds under the ice, that long groaning language it speaks all winter, and Vasseur spoke with it — numbers, always numbers now, fragments, *two hundred in the first hour, the ice took the middle span* — and then, at seven minutes past six by the ward clock, he stopped speaking altogether in the middle of a sentence, and the sentence was:

"Tell them I counted."

Matron Brundt pronounced him at quarter past. Pneumonia, lobar, terminal. She wrote it in the ward book in her round schoolroom hand while Pell and Sorensen straightened the blankets over his chest with the tender clumsiness of boys who had mostly folded flags before this.

And then, within the hour — within the *hour*, I want that recorded somewhere, within the time it takes to boil a kettle and drink two cups of it — the lie arrived, fully grown, wearing its boots.

* * *

Commandant Rell came himself. That was the first sign. Commandants do not come to wards for privates; privates die like leaves fall, noticed collectively, mourned individually somewhere else. But for a colonel with a decoration and a name that would look well in the campaign history, the commandant put on his good coat and came down the corridor with two adjutants and an expression of solemnity he kept polished for exactly these occasions.

"Sister Amberdale." He inclined his head. He had never learned my actual title and had settled years ago on the grander one, which I had long since stopped correcting, correction being a currency I could not afford. "A great loss. A great soldier."

"He was a very ill man," I said, which was true, and let him hear only the truth he wanted in it.

"Pneumonia," he said to the adjutant, who wrote it down. "Contracted in the honorable discharge of his duties. A service record beyond reproach." He looked around the ward — at Hollis sleeping, at the frost flowers, at me — with the proprietary satisfaction of a man inspecting a stage set. "Kolvik," he said, and my heart performed a manoeuvre the army would have classified as unauthorized. "They'll ask about Kolvik, of course. The historians. He was there. We shall say — and it is the truth, Sister, mark me — we shall say he carried the burdens of that night with characteristic fortitude."

The burdens. I looked at the bed. Under the blankets, Vasseur weighed almost nothing now; I had watched the number of him decline for three weeks the way I had once watched other numbers decline. *Carried the burdens.* The man in the bed had carried nothing for twelve years except the dispatch case I now knew sat under my floorboard, and the arithmetic he had refused to do until the fever did it for him.

"Fortitude," I repeated.

"You'll provide the clinical particulars," the commandant said. It was not quite a question. "Date of onset, course of treatment, cause of death. The historians will want precision. Precision is your department, I believe." Something moved behind his eyes when he said it — a flicker, a fish turning under ice — and I understood, with the cold unhurried certainty of a woman who has hidden documents for twelve years, that the commandant was not merely performing grief. He was laying flagstones. Building the road he wanted the story to travel down, and inviting me, courteously, to pave it.

"Cause of death was pneumonia," I said. "That part requires no embellishment."

"Of course it doesn't." He smiled. "None of it does. That is what makes a good record, Sister — the truth, properly arranged." He clicked his heels at the bed, an odd little gesture, half salute and half apology, and swept out with his adjutants, and I stood in the ward listening to his boots diminish down the corridor and understanding that I had just watched a lie be born, and that it had come into the world easily, healthy, full-term, already walking, already wearing its father's coat.

* * *

Grief is a strange country, and nobody warns you about its borders.

I had hated Vasseur. Let the record show — my record, the true one, the one under the floorboard — that I hated him, cleanly and on principle, for twelve years, the way one hates a bad law or a cold wind. But hatred, it turns out, is a form of attention, and attention is a form of attachment, and when they wheeled the screen around his empty bed I found myself standing in the linen closet with a folded sheet against my face, crying for a man I would not have crossed a street to save.

Not for him, precisely. For the boy who liked mathematics, whom I never met. For the version of him that might have stood on the bridge at dusk and said *open it* instead of *close it*, and become a footnote instead of a hero. For the fact that he had died whispering *tell them I counted* — which was, I suspected, the only true sentence he had spoken in a dozen years, and which the official record would bury under *fortitude* before supper.

Or perhaps — and here is the thought I could not put down, the splinter that worked deeper the more I worried it — perhaps I cried because he had done the thing I had never dared. He had said the numbers. Out loud. To a room of orderlies, in delirium, past caring, and the words had gone into other ears — Pell's ears, Sorensen's ears — and could not be entirely recalled. It had taken a fever to loose his tongue, but it had loosed it. Whereas mine, mine had stayed shut through twelve winters, and I had built a life on the shutting of it, and the ledger under my floorboard was the monument to my silence the way his medals were the monument to his.

"Matron says you're to eat something." Marta stood in the doorway of the linen closet with a roll and hard butter on a plate, and she had been crying too — I could see it in the reddened rims of her eyes, though whether for the colonel or for Kolvik, or for the strange kinship of survivors attending the death of one of their makers, I could not tell. She set the plate on the shelf beside the bleach. "Pell says he was talking numbers at the end. Kolvik numbers."

"Pell says a great deal," I said.

"He said four hundred and six." She said it flatly, watching my face the way you watch ice to see if it will bear weight. "That's the right number, isn't it. I've always wondered if anyone else counted. I counted. I got four hundred and nine, but I counted late, after some had already gone onto the ice, and I may have counted some twice, they were moving, in the dark—"

"Marta." My voice failed on the second word and I had to begin again. "Yes. It's the right number."

She nodded slowly, and set the plate down more firmly than it needed setting, and looked at me with an expression I would spend the next days learning to read — fury and hope braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which.

"And now he's dead of pneumonia," she said, "a hero, and the historians are coming on Tuesday, and the bridge closes Saturday, and nobody counted us but us." She turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the doorframe. "Unless somebody in this hospital keeps better records than the army does."

And she looked, for one long moment, directly at the floor beneath the linen closet — not at my floorboard, she couldn't have known, it was three rooms away, but at floors generally, at the idea of floors, at everything a woman might hide underneath one — and then she went out, and left me alone with the butter, the bleach, and the growing certainty that the lie downstairs was going to need company, and that I would have to decide, before Saturday, whether I was going to introduce it to the truth.
