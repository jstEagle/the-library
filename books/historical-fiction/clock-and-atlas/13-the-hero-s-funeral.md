## 13. The Hero's Funeral

The morning of the funeral, the garrison's bugler came down with a cold, and so the honors were rendered by a corporal who could play exactly four notes without splitting them, which gave the ceremony the air of a wounded bird being honored by another, sadder bird.

Oscar stood at the edge of the parade ground with the rest of the hospital staff, her hands folded, her face arranged into the particular expression she had spent twelve years perfecting: attentive, neutral, faintly sorrowful. It was a mask so well worn that she sometimes caught it in mirrors when no ceremony was happening at all — over breakfast, over a patient's chart, over the ledger at night. She had begun to suspect the mask was the face now, and the face underneath something small and startled, like an animal discovered in a wall.

They buried Colonel Aurel Vasseur under gray skies with full honors. Six pallbearers from his old regiment. A folded flag. The commandant in his best greatcoat, his breath steaming like a locomotive at rest. Wreaths from three ministries. Someone had even procured flowers in February, which cost more than the average soldier's yearly pay, and Oscar thought of Marta in the laundry, whose husband's grave at Kolvik had never gotten so much as a stone.

The coffin itself she knew intimately. She had helped dress the body that morning, because there was no one else, and because some stubborn part of her insisted that a man should not be made presentable by strangers who had never heard him weep. He had weighed almost nothing at the end. Pneumonia had eaten him down to suggestions. But the undertaker's art, such as it was in a river garrison, had restored the shape of a colonel: jaw set, hands crossed, medals aligned on the breast like a row of small brass arguments against everything he'd whispered at dawn.

*Four hundred and eleven,* he had said. *I counted them myself. I wanted to know what I owed.*

And here was the debt being settled: a gun salute, three volleys into the white sky, startling crows out of the pines.

Oscar did not cry. She had expected to feel nothing but contempt, and was disturbed to find grief wedged in among it, awkward as a large man in a small pew. Twelve years of hating a man does a strange thing to you when he dies. Hate needs its object the way a lock needs its key. Without Vasseur, some part of her had nowhere to go.

*He died a hero,* the flags said. *He died of pneumonia,* the medical record said. *He died whispering numbers,* Oscar said, to no one, forever.

The commandant stepped up to the podium — a plain wooden affair borrowed from the drill hall — and cleared his throat with the confidence of a man who had never once doubted a sentence he was about to finish.

"We gather," he began, "to honor Colonel Aurel Vasseur, soldier, father of this garrison, servant of the nation."

Beside Oscar, Nurse Brakke sniffled. Brakke cried at funerals, weddings, satisfactory blood counts, and once, memorably, at a particularly well-made rice pudding. Her handkerchief was already deployed.

"Colonel Vasseur's career spanned three campaigns," the commandant continued. "But it will be remembered above all for one day. The day at Kolvik Bridge."

Oscar felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth.

"We have heard careless voices," the commandant said, and here his gaze lifted from his notes and traveled slowly across the assembled ranks, "speak of Kolvik as though it were a crime. As though hard decisions, made under fire, in service of the many, can be judged by those who never stood where heroes stand." He paused. Snow ticked down, fine as flour. "History requires courage of two kinds. The courage to act. And the courage to say plainly, afterward, that what was done was necessary."

There it is, Oscar thought. Watch closely, everyone. This is how it is made. Not forged in secret, not smuggled in the night — manufactured right here, in daylight, in front of four hundred witnesses and God, with wreaths for trimmings. A massacre becoming a necessity in real time, the way ice becomes a bridge: quietly, gradually, and then all at once, and then you can drive carts across it.

"He saved the army that day," the commandant said. "Let no one in this garrison ever speak otherwise. Let the record show it. Let your children learn it."

*Let the record show it.* Oscar nearly laughed, a dangerous laugh, the kind that comes out wrong. The record. If only he knew what records she kept, and where she kept them, and what they showed.

She looked down the line of mourners and found Marta.

Marta stood among the laundry women, who had been marched over in their aprons for the sake of numbers, her hands bare in the cold, her face absolutely still. Stillness, Oscar had learned, was not calm. Stillness was what happened when every other option had been exhausted too quickly.

The commandant finished — "earth to earth, honor to honor, and may history be kind to all brave men" — which struck Oscar as a prayer asking history to commit perjury. The volley cracked. The bugle corporal produced his four notes, two of them split. And the crowd began, with the terrible fluidity of crowds, to flow toward the graveside, heads bowed, murmuring the sorts of things people murmur: *a good man, a decorated man, they don't make them like that anymore.*

Marta turned and walked the other way, toward the laundry, fast.

Oscar followed.

* * *

From her place near the rear of the assembly, Ilse Brandt watched the eulogy with her notebook open inside her glove, writing nothing.

This was itself a kind of note. Three weeks compiling the official campaign record had taught her the grammar of ceremonies: what got written down was rarely what mattered, and what mattered was never written down. Major Reinholt, her senior, stood front and center, and she had watched him during the eulogy the way one watches a translator — checking whether the spoken version matched the document version. It did. Almost word for word. *Tragic necessity.* The phrase had appeared in the draft chapter on Kolvik twice; now it had a uniform and a grave.

Ilse had come north expecting archives, dust, the honest arithmetic of paperwork. What she had found instead was a town holding its breath around something in the middle of the room that nobody would look at directly. The rosters didn't add up. The civilian casualty figures for Kolvik read like a clerk had given up halfway through a difficult sum and written *insignificant* with a shaking pen. And the hospital matron, Petra Iversen — Oscar, the orderlies called her, just Oscar, as if she needed no other name — answered questions with the precision of a woman defusing something.

"You're not recording the eulogy?" whispered Lieutenant Ansel beside her, a young officer assigned to assist the historical delegation, chiefly by standing near things.

"I have it memorized," Ilse said. "It's very short. Necessity, courage, children. All the load-bearing words."

Ansel blinked at her. He was twenty-two and still believed the war had ended the way wars end in schoolbooks, with signatures and relief. She envied him the way one envies someone sleeping through weather.

The crowd dispersed toward the officers' mess, where the wake would consist of the good brandy and lies of increasing size. Ilse lingered. She watched Matron Iversen peel away from the hospital staff and follow one of the laundry women — the tall one with the burn-scarred hands, the one whose file Ilse had requested twice and been refused twice, *personnel matter, not campaign-relevant.*

Interesting, Ilse thought, tucking her notebook away. Everything interesting in this garrison seemed to flow through that woman in sensible boots. The rosters bent toward her. The dying colonel had asked for her specifically, according to the night orderly, who would answer any question for the price of a cigarette and had long since stopped noticing what he was answering.

Ilse decided, with the mild ruthlessness that surprised people who mistook her round face and quiet voice for softness, that she would get to know the matron better. Properly. Not as a material witness to be interviewed and filed, but as whatever she actually was.

She glanced once more at the fresh grave — the wreaths, the flag, the brass medals catching what little light February offered — and thought: *You are the most expensive lie I have ever seen buried.* Then she went, unhurried, toward the laundry.

* * *

Marta threw open the laundry door and stood in the steam, shaking.

Not crying. Marta did not cry where machines could hear her; she had told Oscar once that tears were a currency and she was done spending. But her whole body trembled like a wet dog's, and when Oscar came in behind her and shut the door against the cold, Marta rounded on her with a look that had waited twelve years to be delivered.

"Necessity," Marta said. The word came out flat and terrible. "Necessity. They put it on a podium. They let him say it out loud, in front of everyone, and everyone nodded. Did you see them nod?"

"I saw."

"They nodded, Oscar. The same people who buy their soap from me. The major's wife who I boil her husband's collars for — she nodded. Like it was weather. Like Kolvik was weather."

Marta seized a basket of sheets and began slamming them into the mangle with unnecessary force, feeding the rollers as though feeding people into them, and Oscar, watching, thought: yes. That is exactly what it looks like from the inside. That is what the word *necessary* looks like to the people it was necessary to discard.

"My Erik is under the ice at Kolvik," Marta said to the mangle. "No stone. No name. Not even a number in their book — *insignificant*, that's what their book says, I've heard it repeated, insignificant. And that man gets medals on his chest and flowers in February and a speech. Flowers. In February." She laughed, one hard syllable, nothing funny in it. "Do you know what I could do with flower money? Do you know how much bread that buys?"

"Marta—"

"And I stood there. I stood there in my apron like a good woman and I let them say it." She stopped the mangle. The rollers squealed and quit. In the sudden quiet, her voice dropped to something worse than shouting. "I spat, you know. When they lowered him down. I turned my head so Brakke wouldn't see and I spat in the snow. Small thing. Nobody noticed. The snow eats everything anyway."

"Somebody noticed," Oscar said gently. "Me. It was the truest thing at that funeral."

Marta looked at her then, really looked, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and asking a question she had been asking for weeks, ever since the night she'd recognized the dispatch case's seal on Oscar's shelf and everything between them had changed shape.

"You have it all," Marta said. "Names. Orders. The photographs. You have the truth sitting under a floorboard like contraband tobacco while they bury him as a hero and write the book that my grandchildren will be taught from. Tell me why. Tell me one reason that isn't fear."

And there it was — the question Oscar had been avoiding in the bath, in the dispensary, in the dark behind her own eyes. Why indeed. Fear was the respectable answer. Fear of the commandant, who had been at Kolvik and knew it; fear of arrest, of the transport east, of the small unremarkable deaths that happened to inconvenient witnesses in winter. But underneath the fear sat something older and uglier: habit. Twelve years of carrying names carefully, secretly, alone — until secrecy had grown over the purpose like bone over a fracture, until she could no longer tell whether she was protecting the dead or simply hoarding them.

"The historians," Oscar said instead. "The young one. Ilse. She's not like the other one. She's been pulling threads — the rosters, the civilian figures. She interviewed me twice and took notes I would swear she didn't show her superior."

"So?"

"So the mandate ends when the ice breaks. After that, the record seals. Whatever's in it when the river opens — that's the truth, forever, stamped and archived and taught to children." Oscar heard her own voice steadying as it committed. "We have weeks. Perhaps less."

Marta pulled a sheet from the basket, snapped it flat, and began folding it with the violent precision of a woman who needed her hands to have an opinion. Steam curled between them.

"Then hear me," Marta said. "Erik goes in that record. His name, spelled right, the way his mother spelled it. Not 'civilian losses, insignificant.' Him. Even if it costs us everything — even if your floorboard gives you up — he goes in. I have been polite for twelve years, Oscar. Politeness is how they win. I am finished being a survivor they can be proud of. I want to be a widow they have to account for."

Outside, distantly, the wake's laughter carried from the officers' mess across the snow — the sound of history being toasted into its final form.

Oscar reached out and closed her hand over Marta's, stilling the folding. "Then we'd better decide tonight," she said, "which page he goes on. Because I'm not giving them the ledger, Marta. I'm going to give it to someone who'll enter every name it holds — his first."

From the doorway came the soft click of boots on the step, and Ilse Brandt's voice, apologetic and utterly unembarrassed: "Forgive me. The door was open, and I believe I'm the someone you're discussing."
