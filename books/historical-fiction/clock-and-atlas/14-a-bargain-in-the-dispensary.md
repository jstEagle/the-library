## 14. A Bargain in the Dispensary

Oscar had decided, in the particular way she decided things — slowly, over days, until the decision seemed to have always existed and she had merely noticed it — that the dispensary after ten o'clock at night was the only room in the garrison where a lie could not easily follow you.

It was not sentimentality. It was chemistry. The room smelled so aggressively of iodine, camphor, and the garrison's entire strategic reserve of cod-liver oil that no one lingered who did not have to. The orderlies found reasons to be elsewhere. The lamp burned behind green glass, which made everyone look slightly seasick and therefore honest. And the shelves of bottles stood in their ranks like witnesses who could not be bribed.

She had the page with her. She had carried it for two days folded inside her apron, where it lay against her ribs like a coal that would not cool.

The floorboard came up easier now than it had twelve years ago; the wood had learned the trick of it. But taking out *one page* — leaving the ledger whole beneath the boards, taking only Kolvik's first night, seventeen names in her own careful hand — that had felt like pulling a single thread from a hem and trusting the coat not to fall apart in public.

Footsteps in the corridor. Measured ones. She knew Ilse Brenner's step by now: the walk of a woman who had been taught that hurrying was a form of confession.

"Frau Iversen." Ilse stood in the doorway in her grey archivist's coat, hat still on, which meant she had come from somewhere official and wanted it known. "I hope I'm not disturbing—"

"You are," Oscar said pleasantly. "Sit down. That's rather the point."

Ilse blinked. Then she took off her hat, hung it on the peg with the precision of someone filing it, and sat on the dispensary stool as though it were a witness stand, which, Oscar thought, was closer to the truth than the girl knew.

"I received your note," Ilse said. "'Come to the dispensary at ten. Bring nothing. Say nothing to Dr. Rehm.' It was very terse."

"It was very clear."

"It was also, technically, an improper use of the hospital's internal post."

Oscar looked at her over the green lamp. Twelve years of tending soldiers had given her a full catalogue of human silences — the silence of men pretending not to be afraid, the silence of officers pretending not to be ashamed, the silence of the dying arranging their faces. Ilse Brenner's silence just then was none of these. It was the silence of a young woman delighted against her will.

"You find this funny," Oscar said.

"I find it *notable*," Ilse said primly. "Everything is notable. That is the whole of my profession. Noting things." She folded her hands. "You asked me here. I noted that too."

"Yes." Oscar's hands, on the counter, were steadier than she had any right to expect. "Then let me note something for you, Fräulein Brenner. You've interviewed me twice. You've called me a material witness, which is a phrase meaning a person whose memory is useful but whose person is not. You've asked me about supply ledgers, troop rotations, the winter roads. Very good questions. I answered them all truthfully."

"Within limits," Ilse said quietly.

"Within limits," Oscar agreed. "And each time we talked, I watched you notice my limits. You never pressed. Do you know what that told me? Either you are too careful to be trusted — or you are exactly careful enough."

* * *

Ilse had rehearsed nothing for this evening, which was itself unusual; she rehearsed everything, including conversations with her landlady about the boiler. And so when Frau Iversen reached into her apron — the gesture unhurried, almost domestic, a woman producing a clothespin or a recipe — Ilse felt her professional mind go entirely, gloriously blank.

The paper was old. Cream-colored, soft at the folds, ruled in a hand so even it might have been printed: seventeen lines, seventeen names, each followed by small notations in a cramped, disciplined shorthand. Ages. Descriptions. A word or two beside some of them — *the cooper's boy, sang; held his brother's hand* — that had no business whatsoever in any ledger Ilse had ever catalogued.

"This is not an official document," Ilse said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"No," said Frau Iversen. "Official documents were rather the problem."

Ilse read the names. Her lips moved without her permission; she had never outgrown the childhood habit of reading aloud inside her mouth, and these names seemed to demand it, syllable by syllable, as if each one were a small door that would not open unless knocked upon properly.

"Kolvik," she said at last. "These are civilians."

"These are the first page of the second night," Frau Iversen said. "There were more pages before it. There were many pages after."

The camphor smell pressed in. The green lamp hummed. Ilse became aware that she was holding the paper too tightly and that her thumbs had left damp crescents at the margins, and she set it down on the counter with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that has begun to burn.

"You understand what you've done," she said. "By showing me this."

"I'm beginning to."

"If my mandate were different — if Senior Historian Weiss were a different man — this paper could put you in a cell by morning. Concealment of evidence relevant to the historical record. It's in the statute; I've read the statute; I read everything, it is my one talent."

"Is it?" said Frau Iversen. "I'd assumed your one talent was drinking terrible coffee without flinching. I've seen you at the ward's urn. It's heroic."

Despite herself — despite everything — Ilse laughed. It escaped her like a bird from a shed, and she saw the older woman file the sound away with evident satisfaction.

"That's the bargain, isn't it?" Ilse said, when she had herself back. "That's why I'm sitting in a room that smells like a drowned pharmacy at ten o'clock at night. You're deciding whether I'm trustworthy, and you've chosen a method that leaves you nowhere to hide. If I report this, you lose everything. So you've handed me the knife handle-first."

"Do they teach you metaphors at the archive?"

"They teach us footnotes. Metaphors are insubordination." Ilse turned the page toward herself again, gently now. "Why the seventeenth of the month? Why this name—" her finger stopped at the fourth line, *Lindqvist, Anselm, cooper, 44* — "why show me this one?"

For a moment Ilse thought Frau Iversen would not answer. Then the nurse said, in a voice stripped of all its warmth and humor, "Because Anselm Lindqvist had a wife who survived, and she scrubs the garrison's sheets six days a week, and last Tuesday she mended the collar of Senior Historian Weiss's coat without being asked, because that is the kind of woman she is. And because Weiss's official history, chapter nine, records Kolvik as a regrettable incident involving an unquantified number of displaced persons. Unquantified. As though they were rain."

The lamp hissed softly. Neither woman spoke.

"I can't fix chapter nine," Ilse said finally. "Not alone. Perhaps not at all. You should understand the machinery before you trust me to it. Our commission sits under a dual mandate — compile and certify. While we compile, testimony can be entered, amended, contested. Anything. But certification closes the record. After that, amendments require a ministerial order, which requires, in practice, that a minister care, which requires—" she made a small hopeless gesture— "history behaving better than it does."

"When does certification close?"

"The ice," Ilse said.

Frau Iversen frowned. "Say that plainly."

"The bridge reopens when the river breaks. Our commission is recalled within the week following — the schedule is tied to the thaw, absurdly, because the general staff plans everything around the movement of armies and nothing around the movement of truth. When the ice breaks, the record seals. Whatever is in it becomes the past forever. Whatever isn't in it—" she spread her hands— "was never anywhere at all."

* * *

So there it was, Oscar thought. The door she had felt closing since autumn had a date on it now, and the date was written in water.

She watched the girl across the counter — twenty-six perhaps, chapped hands, ink stain on her cuff shaped like a small country nobody had invaded yet — and performed the arithmetic she had been performing for two days. Trust was not a feeling. Trust was a wager placed with imperfect information, and she had been placing them all her life: the private she'd recorded as unaccounted, the lie she'd told at Kolvik, the ledger under the floorboard that had slowly become the load-bearing wall of her soul.

"How long," she said, "until the ice breaks?"

"Six weeks. Seven. The old boatmen say it's late this year; the cold went down deep." Ilse hesitated. "They say when it goes, it goes all at once. A week of cracking, then the whole river moves."

"A week," Oscar repeated. "Of cracking."

"And you should know the rest of it, since we are apparently trading in knives." Ilse's chin lifted. "Weiss intends to close early. He's petitioned the commandant to certify before the thaw — 'while conditions permit secure transport of the record.' He wants it sealed while the road home is still open. He told me this over dinner, cheerfully, between the fish course and the pudding."

Oscar closed her eyes briefly. Of course. Of course the man would do his tidying in winter, while the town was a locked box and every sledge leaving it was searched at the barrier. She thought of Marta's face at the funeral, of the spit melting into snow, of the dispatch case's weight in her coat those weeks ago on the bank. The walls of the trap were familiar now; she had nearly stopped bumping into them.

"Then we have less time than the river," she said.

"*We*," Ilse repeated.

Oscar opened her eyes. The girl was watching her with a steady, frightened, luminous attention, and Oscar understood that the word had done its work whether she had meant it to or not. She had said *we*, and the saying had made it true, the way writing a name in a ledger makes a death true.

"You may still walk out of this room," Oscar said. "Report me. Keep your position. Chapter nine stays unquantified, and you go home in the spring with a commendation and a clean coat. I want you to hear the offer plainly, because I intend to make it only once, and I don't make offers to children."

"I'm not a child."

"No," Oscar agreed. "Children sleep well. Now — the other side of the scale. If you stay, you will spend the coming weeks doing small, quiet, unlawful things. Copying pages. Asking questions in the archive that will make Weiss uncomfortable. Possibly hiding documents in places I haven't thought of yet, because you are twenty-six and I am forty-nine and your imagination is better. And when it ends — however it ends — you may lose your position, your career, possibly your liberty. And the most you can honestly hope for is that some of the names get into the record before it shuts, and that nobody thanks you, ever, because thanks leave traces."

The green light made Ilse's face unreadable, almost. Almost. Oscar had spent twelve years reading faces across fevered pillows; she saw the fear arrive, and pass through, and come out the other side wearing the girl's stubbornness like a borrowed coat.

"I have one condition," Ilse said.

"One."

"Anselm Lindqvist." Her finger rested on the fourth line. "His name goes in first. Before anything else, before strategy, before safety — he goes into the record, and beside his name it says what he was. Cooper. Father of three. Not *displaced person*. Not *unquantified*. If I'm going to burn my career, I want it to be for a man with a trade."

For a moment Oscar could not speak. She thought of Marta, and of the conversation she would soon have to have, the one she had been dreading and dodging in equal measure. She thought of the officer at Kolvik saying *burn it* in his bored voice, and of the match trembling in her own fingers, twelve years ago tonight, it might have been.

"He'll be first," she said. "I give you my word, and you may note, Fräulein Brenner, that I am a woman whose entire life consists of keeping written promises."

"Then I have another condition," said Ilse, and the ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Terrible coffee. Yours, not mine. If we are conspiring, we conspire properly. My landlady's boiler has been broken since November and I have been drinking hot water with regret in it."

Oscar found herself laughing — really laughing, the first time in longer than she could account for — and reached up to the shelf for the private tin she kept above the quinine, the one no orderly had ever been permitted to notice.

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamp had burned low and the two of them had spoken in fragments and half-names until the plan had a shape, crude but standing, like a table built in a hurry — Ilse pulled on her coat by the door and paused.

"There's one thing you should know," she said. "Weiss keeps a working copy of all testimony in his quarters. Including yours. Both interviews. And he annotates them." She hesitated. "I've seen your file, Frau Iversen. In the margin of your second interview, beside your account of the field hospital's evacuation, he wrote one word."

"What word?"

"*Persistent.*"

Oscar considered this. "From Weiss, I imagine that's either a threat or a compliment."

"With him it's usually both," said Ilse, and put on her hat, and went out into the dark, where the river lay under its ice like a held breath, waiting.
