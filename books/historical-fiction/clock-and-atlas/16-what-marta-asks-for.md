## 16. What Marta Asks For

The laundry steam had a way of getting into a person's bones and staying there, which was why Marta Lindqvist folded sheets the way she did everything now — quickly, before her hands could think about what they were doing. Fold, stack, next. Twelve years of practice at not thinking. She had gotten so good at it that she sometimes fooled herself, and those were the best hours of her week.

She was not fooling herself this morning. This morning she had seen the seal.

She'd been carrying the colonel's linens down from the ward — they gave her the officer's wing because she was fast and because nobody senior wanted the walk — and Oscar Amberdale had come out of the linen room with her coat hanging wrong, the kind of wrong a coat hangs when there is something square and heavy in the pocket, and for half a second, as she shifted it to close a button, Marta had seen the wax.

Black wax. A stamped eagle with its wings folded down, pressed into a disc the size of a coin.

She had stood in a room with that seal once before. It had been on the door of the headquarters building at Kolvik, the night the trucks left without them. She had looked at it for a long time then too, though for a different reason.

Fold, stack, next. But her hands had stopped.

* * *

She found Oscar in the dispensary at the end of shift, counting out iodine tablets with the concentration of a woman defusing something. Marta shut the door behind her and leaned against it, which was a liberty, and she knew it was a liberty, and she took it anyway. Twelve years of being careful had thinned down to almost nothing, like soap.

"The dispatch case," Marta said. "The one you're carrying around like a hot brick. I saw the seal."

Oscar's hands went still over the tablets. Not a flinch, exactly. More like a clock deciding whether to keep ticking.

"You were mistaken," Oscar said. "It's a hot water bottle."

"I've seen that eagle before," Marta said. "On a door. In Kolvik. The night of the eleventh of December."

The clock decided. Oscar set down the bottle of tablets very carefully, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw, and looked up, and Marta saw twelve years pass across her face like weather.

"Sit down," Oscar said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Suit yourself." Oscar rubbed her eyes. When she lowered her hand she was older-looking and very tired and somehow more honest than Marta had ever seen her, which was saying something, because Oscar Amberdale was honest the way some people were religious — privately, and never where it might cost them.

"There's a case," Oscar said. "It belonged to Colonel Vasseur. It contains documents. Orders, rosters. Photographs." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb was pressing a groove into the countertop, back and forth, back and forth. "It proves what happened at the bridge was sanctioned. Ordered from above. Written down by men who signed their names and went home to decorations."

Marta's knees informed her they would like to sit after all. She pulled up the stool by the scales, the one used for weighing the orderlies' dignity when they came in claiming fever, and sat.

"And the ledger," Marta said. "There's a ledger too. Don't look surprised. You think I haven't watched you for twelve years? A woman who writes letters home every Sunday to nobody — I've seen the addresses, they're all to the same dead aunt in three different towns. A woman who knows the name of every private who ever died in this building, including ones who arrived already unconscious. You keep names, Oscar Amberdale. You keep them somewhere, and you keep them hidden, and you've been keeping them since before I got here."

For a long moment the only sound was the boiler, ticking away down the corridor like a patient heart.

"Under the third floorboard from the window," Oscar said at last, "in the ward storeroom. Under the spare blankets nobody counts because I do the counting." She smiled, briefly and without much humor. "You're right. I keep names. Four hundred and six of them, as of Tuesday. Soldiers the reports list as unaccounted, civilians the reports list as regrettable, deserters the reports don't list at all. Every one of them real. Every one of them somebody's Tuesday."

"Is Anders in it?"

"Marta—"

"Is my husband *in it?*"

"Yes," Oscar said quietly. "Page nineteen. Anders Lindqvist, ferryman, Kolvik. Listed by the commission as drowned in the course of an unspecified civil disturbance. I have him as shot at the eastern rail of the bridge at approximately twenty-two hundred hours, along with eleven other men, by soldiers of the Third Column, under written order." She recited it flatly, like a pharmacist reading a label, and Marta understood that this was how Oscar survived it — she had turned the whole thing into labels, and read them daily, and never let herself taste them.

Marta sat with it. Anders at the eastern rail. Twenty-two hundred. She had always pictured him in the water, because the water had been easier to imagine than the guns; the water meant he hadn't suffered, or told herself so, for twelve years. Now here was a woman handing her a different picture, precise as a receipt, and Marta found — this was the strange part, the part she would turn over for years afterward — that she wasn't shattered. She was *furious*, but not at Oscar. Furious at the tidiness of it. Someone had known the hour. Someone had written it down. All this time the truth had existed, in ink, in a drawer, while she folded sheets.

"Then we give it to the historians," Marta said.

"No."

"No?" Marta stood. The stool scraped. Somewhere down the hall a door opened and shut; they both waited until the footsteps faded. "Explain to me, slowly, why we have spent — how long have you spent on that ledger?"

"Twelve years."

"Twelve years keeping the names of the dead, and now, when two historians are sitting in the officers' mess drinking the good coffee and asking for testimony, you say no?"

"Because the commandant knows something is circulating," Oscar said. "Because Vasseur gave me that case three days before he died and by the end of the week he'll have searched every floorboard in this garrison. Because Ilse Brandt is genuine, I believe that, I've tested her, but she is one junior archivist inside an institution whose job is to make the war mean whatever the victors need it to mean, and if we hand her everything and she loses control of it for one afternoon — one afternoon, Marta — then the case is seized, the ledger is burned, and every name in it dies twice. And the people who helped us—" She stopped.

"Go on," Marta said. "Say it. The people who helped us get shot at the eastern rail of something. I can bear it. I've had practice."

"That isn't fair."

"None of this is fair. That is the entire point." Marta heard her own voice go low and steady, which frightened her more than shouting would have, because shouting was ordinary and this was something else. "Do you know what I did for twelve years, Oscar? I was quiet. I took the laundry job because it was quiet. I never spoke about Kolvik, not to the priest, not to the women, not to myself if I could help it. I was safe. Do you know what safe got me? Safe got me twelve years of folding other people's sheets while my husband lay in the commission's files under the words *unspecified civil disturbance.* Unspecified! He specified plenty, I promise you. He had opinions on everything. He had opinions about the correct way to hang laundry, which I ignored for nine years of marriage out of principle."

Despite everything, something in Oscar's mouth twitched.

"He'd have hated that ledger," Marta went on, softer now. "Not the having of it. The hiding of it. Anders believed a thing written down and put away was a thing buried alive. He used to say it about his ferry accounts. *A record kept in a drawer is just gossip with good handwriting,* that's what he said. It has to go out into the daylight and be argued with. That's how it stays true."

"Anders," Oscar said gently, "is not the one who will be arrested."

The words landed exactly where she meant them to. Marta sat back down.

"There are thirty-one people in this garrison," Oscar continued, "who have helped me in small ways and large ways and ways they don't even know. Sister Anselm, who misfiles things on purpose. Young Piet in the stables, who has carried that case under the hay twice. The laundress Halvi, who asked me no questions, which was itself an answer. If the commandant moves, he doesn't move against me alone. He moves against everyone whose silence can be bought and everyone whose silence can't. I will not hand him a list of the brave ones. I've spent twelve years making lists of the dead. I refuse to make another."

"And I," Marta said, "have spent twelve years being protected. By my own silence first, and then by yours. Do you know how it feels, Oscar, to be protected? It feels like being a name on page nineteen. Somebody else holds the pen. Somebody else decides what the risk is worth. Well." She leaned forward and put both hands flat on the counter, palms down, the way Anders used to when he was about to say something he considered final. "I am done being a name on your page. My husband's name goes in the record. Not in a private book under a floorboard. In the record. With the date, and the hour, and the word *shot* instead of the word *unspecified,* and if it costs us all our comfortable positions and possibly our necks, then I will say what I have noticed about necks, which is that they are given to us for the purpose of sticking them out."

The boiler ticked. Oscar looked at her for a long time, and Marta watched the calculation move behind her eyes — not coldness, never coldness, but the arithmetic of a woman who had been counting costs since she was young enough that counting them should have been someone else's job.

"You sound like him," Oscar said finally. "Anders. Sister Anselm told me about him. She was at Kolvik too, before she took orders — did you know that? She remembers him arguing with a lieutenant about the ferry manifests while the shells were coming in. She said he was the only calm person on the riverbank."

"He was calm," Marta said. "It was infuriating. Marry a calm man and spend your whole life wanting to shake him." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it. There. Let it crack in front of Oscar Amberdale, keeper of four hundred and six names, and add hers to the collection: Marta Lindqvist, widow, still furious, still here.

Oscar came around the counter. She didn't embrace her — neither of them was built for that, or not yet — but she stood close enough that Marta could see her hands, which were shaking slightly, and take some comfort from it. It was good to learn that the steady ones shook too.

"Here is what I can offer," Oscar said. "Not your name in the sealed record, not yet. But Ilse Brandt comes to the dispensary tomorrow. I've promised her one page of the ledger — one, as proof it exists. I had planned to give her something safe. A soldier's entry. No witnesses, no living relatives, nothing anyone could be punished for."

"Give her Anders."

"If I give her Anders, and anything goes wrong, you are implicated before the ice breaks. You understand that. Your name will be in her notes, and her notes can be seized same as anything else."

"My name," Marta said, "has been in worse places for less. Put it in."

Oscar closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had changed in her face — some load redistributed, some wall taken down and the timber saved for a bridge instead. Marta thought: there it is. That's what surrender looks like when the person doing it is glad.

"All right," Oscar said. "Anders goes in. Page nineteen, in full. And Marta — if we're going to do this properly, then it can't be one page and a prayer. Ilse needs testimony, and the ledger needs copying, because the original stays hidden no matter what happens to any of us. Three nights of work, maybe four. Lamplight, in the dispensary, after lockup. Can you write?"

"I kept ferry accounts for nine years," Marta said. "My figures were famous. Anders himself called them unimprovable, and he did not hand out compliments, the calm wretch."

"Then tomorrow night," Oscar said, "you start on pages one through ten, and God help us both."

They stood a moment longer in the little room with its bottles and its scales, two women who had circled each other for twelve years and finally stopped circling. Then Marta did something she had not planned. She reached out and touched the counter where the dispatch case had rested — just touched the wood, lightly, the way you touch a church rail.

"For Anders," she said.

"For Anders," Oscar agreed. "And for the other four hundred and five."

Marta went back down the corridor toward the laundry, past the boiler with its faithful tick, and she noticed that her hands had stopped wanting to fold anything. They wanted to hold a pen. It had been twelve years since they'd wanted that, and she walked a little faster, thinking of ink.

She was halfway to the stair when she heard the bell at the gate — the heavy bell, rung twice, which meant official visitors — and saw, through the frost-furred window, a sledge pulling up with garrison colors on its side, and an officer climbing down from it who was not an officer she knew, and behind him two soldiers carrying between them a long wooden crate the shape of a coffin, or a filing cabinet, and she thought, with the terrible clarity of women who have seen seals on doors before:

He's brought somewhere to put what he finds.
