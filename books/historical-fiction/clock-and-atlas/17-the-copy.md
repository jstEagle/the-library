## 17. The Copy

Ilse had stolen documents before. She wanted that on the record, if there ever was a record — she had done it once, in the archives at home, when she was twenty-two and still believed that clerical courage was a small, forgivable sin. She had slipped a suppressed casualty report into her handbag, walked it past the door guard with her heart hammering like a fist on wood, taken it home, typed a copy by lamplight, and returned the original before morning.

She had been caught, of course. Not arrested — merely noticed. Her supervisor had looked at her over his spectacles for a long, terrible moment and said, "Ambition takes strange forms in the young," and she had never known whether he meant it as warning or blessing.

This time there would be no returning the original. This time the theft was not one page but a whole testimony, and the typewriter she used was not her own, and the hour was two in the morning, and the only witness was the garrison cat, who had installed himself on the radiator beside her desk as though hired for the purpose of companionship and was failing at every duty except warmth.

The carbon paper was the difficult part. Ilse had requisitioned it honestly enough — *for duplication of standard interview forms*, she had written, and no quartermaster questions a historian's hunger for forms — but using it felt different from owning it. She fed the sheet through with the care of a woman handling a live coal. Then she began to type.

Her fingers knew the testimony by then. She had transcribed it twice already: once in shorthand, once in fair copy for Brandt's folder, where it now sat awaiting his red pencil. She typed it a third time, slowly, because speed made mistakes and mistakes could not be corrected here. A smudged page raised questions. Questions were the enemy.

Outside, the river groaned. It had begun doing this at night — long, tectonic complaints, as though the ice were trying to turn over in its sleep and could not quite manage it. Ilse had grown fond of the sound. It meant the world was still under construction.

"Kolvik Bridge," she read aloud, very quietly, typing it. "Fourteenth of October." The keys struck. The carbon held. Two copies existed now where one had existed before, and one of them would belong to history and the other to whoever was brave enough to carry it there.

The cat yawned.

"Don't judge me," Ilse told him.

* * *

The copying of the ledger was Oscar's idea, and like all of Oscar's ideas it arrived without fanfare, folded inside an ordinary remark, so that you did not realize you had agreed to something enormous until you were already halfway into it.

They met in the dispensary, because the dispensary was the one room in the hospital wing with a lock that worked and a smell strong enough to explain any oddity of behavior. Anyone finding the three of them there at midnight could plausibly be told they were taking inventory. Nobody inventories anything at midnight, but lies do not need to be perfect; they need only to be boring enough that nobody wants them.

Oscar came first, the ledger wrapped in a linen towel as though it were a loaf of bread she'd smuggled from the kitchen. Marta came second, carrying three candle stubs, a bottle of ink, and an expression that suggested she had been arguing with God on the way over and had won on points.

"Well," Marta said, setting the candles down. "Three of us, forty pages, and however many nights our luck holds out. I've copied out funeral notices my whole life. Names, dates, names, dates. This will be no different, except that nobody will frame these on a mantelpiece."

"Somebody might," Oscar said.

"I hope so," said Marta. "I hope somebody frames every cursed one."

Ilse had brought paper — good paper, requisitioned in the same dull quantities historians use for dull purposes, a few sheets at a time across two weeks so that no tally in the quartermaster's book would ever add up to anything. She laid the sheets out now in neat stacks and felt, absurdly, like a girl setting a table for guests who might never come.

"You understand what this is," Oscar said. It was not quite a question. She stood with the towel-wrapped ledger against her chest, and in the candlelight her face had the stillness Ilse had learned to read as feeling rather than its absence. "Once it's copied, it can't be uncopied. Every one of us carries it then. Every one of us can be ruined by it."

"Yes," Ilse said.

"And you're certain your mandate ends when the ice breaks."

"Certain as anyone is of anything in this building. When the river opens, the commission closes. Whatever record exists on that day becomes the record. Forever. There's a phrase in the instructions — 'the final compilation of the campaign.' Brandt says it the way other men say grace."

Marta snorted. "Grace," she repeated. "I'll give him grace."

"Marta," Oscar said, mildly, and Marta subsided the way a kettle subsides — still boiling underneath, only quieter about it.

So they began.

It went slower than any of them had guessed. The problem was not the copying itself — Marta's hand was swift and sure, trained by decades of parish registers, and Oscar wrote like a woman who had done nothing else her whole life. The problem was that neither of them would simply write. They read each entry aloud first. All of them.

"Private Anselm Roth, age nineteen, Kolvik, unaccounted — recorded as desertion, witnessed otherwise by O.A., fourteenth October."

"His mother sent a petition," Marta said, dipping her pen. "I remember. She walked to the provincial office three winters running. They kept telling her the file was being reviewed."

"It was being reviewed," Oscar said. "It's being reviewed right now. By us."

That was the rhythm of the first night: name, story, pen scratching. Ilse sat with her own notebook and took down what she heard, because although the ledger was the spine of the thing, testimony was the flesh, and she was discovering that the two women remembered differently. Marta remembered people — faces, habits, the way a man had whistled. Oscar remembered facts, arranged like surgical instruments. Between them, a whole town reassembled itself in a locked dispensary at midnight, out of ink and candle smoke and stubbornness.

At some point past two, Marta stopped mid-entry and pressed the heel of her hand against her eye.

"Erik Halvorsen," she said. "Fisherman. He carried our boy on his shoulders at the harvest fair so he could see over the crowd. And here he is in half a line. 'E.H., Kolvik, drowned.' Drowned, as if the water did it on its own initiative."

Ilse reached across the table and turned her own notebook around. She had written, in the margin: *carried his son on shoulders at fair, per M.*

Marta stared at it for a long moment.

"Well," she said finally, and her voice had gone rough and bright. "Now it's true twice."

* * *

By the third night they had a system, and the system had jokes, which Ilse privately considered the truest sign that human beings had accepted a situation. Oscar read; Marta wrote; Ilse verified entries against the rosters she had memorized and flagged every discrepancy with a small code mark that would mean nothing to anyone who seized her notes and everything to the three women in the room.

The discrepancies were the whole argument, and Oscar knew it. That was why she insisted Ilse check everything.

"There," Oscar said on the third night, tapping an entry. "Lieutenant Brandt's roster lists forty-one civilians at the eastern approach. My ledger has fifty-three. Explain the difference to me as a historian."

"Twelve people erased between the field count and the official return," Ilse said. "Either the counting officer couldn't count, or someone decided twelve fewer bodies made a tidier report."

"Tidier," Oscar repeated. The word sat in her mouth like a stone she was deciding whether to swallow or spit. "Yes. That was always the word for it. Tidy."

The candles burned low. Outside, the river spoke in its sleep — a cracking, then a long silence, then a cracking deeper than before, and all three of them paused to listen the way farmers pause to listen for rain.

"It's early," Marta said quietly. "The ice usually holds till well into April."

"It's been an early winter too," Oscar said. "Everything's ahead of schedule."

Ilse wrote that down. She didn't know yet why; she only knew that Oscar's voice had changed when she said it, gone flat and careful, the way it did when dread put on the costume of observation.

But then the work resumed, and the dread receded, because the work would not let it stay. There was something in the act of writing a name by hand, Ilse thought, that no typewriter quite managed. The machine reproduced; the hand affirmed. When Marta wrote *Halvorsen, Erik, fisherman,* she was not transcribing a fact. She was insisting on a man. Each letter was a small act of refusal — refusal of *drowned*, refusal of *unaccounted*, refusal of the great tidy blank that the war had left where a town used to be.

Somewhere past three o'clock, Marta set down her pen, shook out her cramping hand, and announced: "When this is over, I'm going to sleep for a week, and then I'm going to bake something, and everyone in this garrison is going to eat it whether they've behaved or not."

"That sounds like a threat," Ilse said.

"It is a threat."

"What kind of something?"

"Plum cake," said Marta, with dignity. "My mother's recipe. It has ruined better people than the commandant."

And Oscar — Oscar laughed. Ilse had been at the garrison six weeks and had heard Oscar Amberdale produce every variety of non-laugh: the polite exhalation, the dry single note, the smile that did the work of laughter so the real thing wouldn't have to be risked. But this was the actual article, brief and startled out of her, and it transformed her face so completely that Ilse had to look down at her notebook until she could trust her own expression again.

Twelve years of silence, Ilse thought, writing it down anyway, because she was what she was. Twelve years, broken over plum cake.

They finished the last page as the sky behind the frost-furred window went from black to iron-gray. Forty pages, three copies of the ledger now in existence — Oscar's original, Marta's fair copy, and the pages Ilse would carry, interleaved with her typed testimony, in the false bottom of her document case where the commission's seals could keep them company.

Oscar gathered the towels and candle stubs with the economy of a woman who had hidden things for so long that concealment had become a kind of housekeeping. At the door she paused.

"Thursday," she said. "We finish the cross-checks Thursday night. After that—" She stopped. Ilse watched her decide how much future she was willing to promise herself. "After that we wait for the ice."

"After that we eat plum cake," Marta said.

"After that," Oscar agreed, and almost smiled, and the three of them separated into the blue dark of the corridor like conspirators — which, Ilse supposed, was exactly what they now were, whatever gentler word they preferred.

Ilse walked back to her room past the sleeping ward, past the night orderly dozing over his newspaper, past the window where the river lay white and muttering under the stars. Her document case bumped lightly against her hip with every step, heavier than paper had any right to be.

She did not see the orderly at the end of the hall, who had been awake longer than any of them, and who had eyes, and who had also, as it happened, debts.

But the river saw. The river cracked once more, far out near the pilings — a sharp, reporting sound, like a door being tested in its frame — and then settled into silence to wait.
