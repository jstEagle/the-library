## 18. Betrayal

The betrayal, when it came, came through the laundry chute.

I want to say I was surprised. I want to tell you that after twelve years of keeping secrets in a garrison full of people whose chief occupation was noticing things, I had some better defense than a young orderly named Pimm who liked money and disliked thinking. But the truth is simpler and sadder: I had trusted him because he was cheerful. Cheerfulness is not a credential. It is a costume anyone can buy.

Pimm carried the dispatch case for me exactly four times — from the ward to the dispensary, from the dispensary to the linen room — each trip arranged so that he believed he was smuggling nothing more scandalous than a locked box of the colonel's private correspondence. He was paid in small coins and large smiles. On the fifth trip he was paid by someone else.

I learned this later. What I learned first, that gray Thursday morning, was the sound of boots on the ward floor in a rhythm I had come to dread: two men, unhurried, which is always worse than running. Hurried boots mean an emergency, and emergencies pass. Unhurried boots mean a decision has already been made and someone is simply delivering it.

* * *

I was changing the dressing on Corporal Enke's foot when they came. Enke had lost three toes to frostbite and his sense of humor to no one; he kept it in fine working order despite everything.

"Sister," he said, watching my face change as the boots stopped behind me, "am I about to receive very good news or very bad news?"

"That depends," I said, tying off the bandage, "on whether you've been hiding anything under your mattress."

"Only my opinions."

"Then you're safe. Nobody searches for those."

The commandant's adjutant did not find this funny. Adjutants rarely do; humor requires imagination and their training removes it early, like tonsils. He read from a paper in a flat voice — *by order of the garrison commandant, a search of the hospital premises is authorized pursuant to reports of unauthorized documents in circulation* — and I stood there with Enke's dirty bandage in my hand and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *Pimm.*

They found the dispatch case where I had hidden it, which was nowhere clever at all, because there are no clever places left in a building that has been searched twice already. It was inside the footlocker of Private Hallem, dead nine days and awaiting transport home, wrapped in his spare shirt on the theory that grief is the best camouflage in the world. I had counted on the searchers' reluctance to rummage a dead man's belongings. I had forgotten that bureaucracy feels no reluctance about anything. The adjutant lifted the case out with gloved hands as if it were ordinary contraband — tobacco, letters, a stolen watch — and perhaps to him it was. He could not have known he was holding Kolvik Bridge in a leather box.

"Oscar Amberdale," he said, reading my name from his paper rather than looking at me, which I appreciated. Eye contact would have required him to admit I was a person. "You will accompany us to the commandant's office at your earliest convenience."

"My earliest convenience," I repeated. "How generous."

"Sister Amberdale," said Enke from his cot, "is needed here. I've got a foot."

"You've got most of one," the adjutant said.

"And she's the only one who knows how to keep it."

For a moment — I will love Enke for this until I die — the entire ward went quiet in that particular way patients have when they decide, collectively and without a word spoken, to be an obstacle. Sixteen men in beds found urgent reasons to require things. One asked for water he had not wanted in a week. Another developed a sudden interest in his own pulse. A third, a farmer who had never in his life addressed me above a mumble, demanded to know whether the hospital now permitted arrests during visiting hours, and if not, what time visiting hours ended, because he intended to have family in until then.

It bought me ten minutes. Ten minutes is nothing and it is everything; it let me cross the corridor to the linen room and press my palm once against the loose board behind the calico shelf where the ledger lay, feeling through the wood the small solid weight of every name I had ever promised to keep, and to know that they had not found it. They had found the colonel's case. They had not found mine. Whether that was luck or merely slowness I couldn't say, but I walked to the commandant's office with my heart going like a cart on a rough road and my face arranged into the mild, competent blankness of a woman who has spent twelve years being unremarkable on purpose.

* * *

The commandant's office smelled of stove iron and ink. He sat behind his desk with the dispatch case open before him, and I watched him look at the contents the way a man looks at a well he suspects is poisoned: carefully, without touching.

"Sister Amberdale." He gestured to a chair. I remained standing. "Do you know what this is?"

"It appears to be the late Colonel Vasseur's personal effects. I catalogued them myself."

"Do you know what's in it?"

"His effects," I said. "Orders, rosters. Photographs. Men keep photographs."

He looked up at that. For a moment something almost human crossed his face — recognition, maybe, or its cousin, exhaustion. "You were at Kolvik," he said.

"I was at a great many places during the war. So were you, I believe."

The silence between us had furniture in it. He rose, poured two glasses of water from the pitcher — a small courtesy, and I understood it as such, the way you understand a handshake from a man who intends to have you flogged — and set one near my hand.

"Here is what happens next," he said. "You will be confined to quarters pending inquiry. When the ice breaks, you will be transported downriver for trial. Unauthorized possession of military documents. Concealment. Possibly sedition, depending on how the tribunal wakes up that morning." He paused. "Or."

"Or," I said.

"Or you surrender whatever else you have — and we both know there is something else — and you take the transfer I offered you in the autumn. A pension. A quiet posting far from this river. Some histories are written by other people, Sister. It is not a crime to let them."

"There are names in that case," I said. "Civilians. Women. Children. Men who surrendered. There is an order signed and dated, and rosters proving the units involved received it, and photographs—" My voice caught, and I hated it, and pushed on. "There are one hundred and forty reasons in that box, Commandant, and you're standing between them and daylight."

"One hundred and forty-one," he said quietly.

And I understood, all at once, why he had poured the water. Why he had offered the pension twice. Why he had never once been able to look at me the way the adjutant did, as paperwork. He had been at Kolvik. He had perhaps even counted.

"You could testify," I said. "You could put it in yourself. You'd be believed. You have the uniform for it."

"I have the uniform for surviving," he said. "It is a different garment." He rang the little bell on his desk, and that was the end of our conversation, and I was escorted down the corridor to a storeroom with a cot in it, and the door was locked, and I sat on the cot and laughed — actually laughed, alone, until my eyes watered — because twelve years of careful silence and it had all come down to a cheerful boy in the laundry and a man too cowardly to be brave even when bravery was sitting right there offering itself in a glass of water.

* * *

They let me have visitors, because I was not a prisoner, you understand; I was a matter pending. Marta came first, with clean linens I didn't need, and stood in the doorway of the storeroom radiating the fury of a woman restrained by furniture.

"Pimm," she said.

"I know."

"He sold you for thirty marks. Thirty! I pay him extra to bring the hot water first and he can't even do *that* reliably."

"Marta—"

"The case?"

"Gone. Seized. In the commandant's office, probably already boxed for transport."

"The ledger?"

"Safe." I tapped the floor with my heel, twice, our old signal. "Under the boards where it has always been."

She closed the door behind her, which orderlies are not supposed to allow and Pimm, notably, was elsewhere. "Ilse," she said. "Does she know?"

"I don't know. She should hear it from us before she hears it from the commandant's office. She'll be suspected just for knowing me."

"She *is* suspected. Everyone with eyes has suspected her since December. The senior one, Brandt — he watches her like a man watching milk he believes has turned."

That evening — I was permitted supervised walks to the chapel, a mercy I attributed to Enke's campaign of bureaucratic nuisance — I saw Ilse across the corridor and managed forty seconds beside her at the window while her escort consulted his watch.

"They took the case," I murmured.

"I heard." Her hands were folded very still, the way she held them whenever she was frightened, which was how I knew she had known since morning. "Oscar. The ledger—"

"Safe."

"Then listen to me, because there won't be another window." Her voice dropped to almost nothing, and I had to watch her lips. "Brandt is drafting the closing summary. Everything goes into the record when the ice breaks — including the case, if he reaches it first. He'll classify it. Lost, destroyed, unverifiable. It will vanish the way things vanish, and no one will ever be able to prove it existed." She breathed. "Unless it's already *in* the record before he touches it. Testimony entered is testimony sealed. Even he can't remove an entry — only annotate it. And annotations leave fingerprints."

"How long?"

"Days. Maybe less. The ice groans at night now; I hear it from my room." She stepped back as her escort looked up, and became again entirely the junior archivist, pen and notebook in hand, harmless as stationery. But as she turned away she said, clearly enough for the escort to hear, in her official voice: "Sister, I'll need your formal statement regarding the Vasseur estate. Tomorrow, ten o'clock."

"I'll be here," I said, "pending."

* * *

She did not come to the ten o'clock meeting. At eleven, Marta came instead, and her face told me before her mouth opened.

"Burned," she said. "Her notes. All of them. Last night, in the dispensary stove — the cook swears she saw smoke and Ilse claimed she was burning outdated requisitions. Which, incidentally, is the single worst lie I have ever heard anyone tell with a straight face. Requisitions? No one burns requisitions. No one has *ever* burned requisitions."

"What happened?"

"Brandt searched her rooms this morning. Found ash and wire notebook spirals. He had her stripped of her access before noon — relieved of duty, records sealed, waiting on transport downriver with the first thaw. He stood in the corridor outside the archive and announced it to anyone who happened to be passing, which was everyone, because Brandt announces things the way weather does." Marta sat down heavily on the cot beside me. "He asked her directly, in front of witnesses: what did you destroy and why. And do you know what she said?"

"What?"

"She said: 'Drafts, sir. Of paragraphs that weren't true.' And then she thanked him for the opportunity to clarify, saluted, and walked out. Marta Kolvik, who has spat in the snow of a colonel's funeral, tells you honestly: I have never in my life seen anything so brave or so useless."

But it wasn't useless. That was the thing I sat turning over on the storeroom cot while the light went thin and the river began, faintly, in the distance, to make the sound it makes at night now — a deep grudging groan, like a giant objecting in his sleep. Ilse had burned her notes to leave nothing that could incriminate me, yes. But she had also burned them for a second reason, and I saw it only slowly, the way you see the pattern in a frost window: everything she had copied, everything that mattered, she had copied *twice*. She was the archivist. Duplication was not her sin; it was her sacrament.

Somewhere in this garrison — in a stove flue, in a mattress, in the false bottom of her trunk, in the one place Brandt's search had not gone because Brandt's imagination had limits — there was a second testimony, typed, dated, entered-ready. And somewhere under my floorboard there was a ledger with a hundred and forty names, and now, thanks to three nights of lamplight and cramped hands, a fair copy in Marta's strong round script and mine.

They had the case. Let them have it. A locked box is evidence only if no one is left to open it properly.

The trouble was the clock. The commandant meant to move against me formally before the ice broke — I had heard it in his voice, *transported downriver for trial*, and trials travel slowly but tribunals convened quickly can beat any thaw. Once I was on a barge, I was a rumor. Rumors don't testify.

Marta left at dusk. At the door she paused, and for once did not argue with me about anything, which frightened me more than the arrest did.

"The closing session," she said. "Day after tomorrow. The whole garrison attends; Brandt reads the summary; the record closes." She looked back at me. "What are you going to do, Oscar?"

I told her.

She listened all the way to the end without interrupting, which for Marta was an act of heroic discipline, and then she said only: "I'll need to press my good dress."

* * *

That night the ice spoke for hours. I lay on the storeroom cot listening to it — the long cracking groans, the sharp reports like rifles under the floor of the world — and I thought about names. Pimm's name, which I would add to no ledger, because the ledger was for people the record forgot, and Pimm would be remembered forever, in the smallest possible way, as the man who was paid twice and served neither side. Vasseur's name, dying at dawn with numbers on his lips while orderlies wrote *pneumonia* in the margin. The commandant's name, the one hundred and forty-first, worn privately all these years like a stone in the boot.

And mine. Still missing. Still waiting.

The lock turned at dawn. Not the adjutant — Pimm, pale as porridge, holding a tray of tea nobody had ordered and a piece of paper he could not quite meet my eyes to deliver.

"From Miss Ilse," he muttered. "Through the cook. Who owes me. Don't ask."

The note was six lines long, in her precise archivist's hand:

*Closing session moved forward — tomorrow, nine o'clock, Brandt insists, before any thaw delays the record. Commandant intends to announce your arrest publicly at its close. Second copy is where we agreed. Bring yours. Do not bring fear; there is no room for it in the transcript.*

I read it twice. Then I looked up at Pimm, who was staring very hard at the tea.

"Thirty marks," I said.

He flinched. "Fifty, actually."

"Was it worth it?"

He thought about it, which I hadn't expected. "No," he said finally. "It turns out I'm not suited to it. I'm sorry, Sister. Truly." And then, because he was still, at bottom, a decent boy who had made a cheap and ordinary mistake: "The guard on your door tonight is Brenner. He sleeps sitting up, but he snores like the end of the world, and he takes his boots off first either way."

Tomorrow at nine o'clock, then. The record would close over Kolvik like water over a drowned village — unless a nurse with no standing, no permission, and no remaining options stood up in front of the whole garrison and refused to sit down.

I finished the tea. Under the floorboard, the ledger waited, patient as it had been for twelve years, for the one name still missing from the first page.
