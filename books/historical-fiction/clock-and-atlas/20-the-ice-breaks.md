## 20. The Ice Breaks

Ilse had read in her training manuals that a river breaking up in spring made a sound like cannon fire. The manuals were wrong. It was nothing like cannon fire. Cannon fire was over in an instant; this went on all night — a long, groaning conversation between the ice and the water beneath it, the way an old house talks to itself when no one is listening. She lay awake in her narrow room above the garrison library and listened to it, and thought: *this is the sound of something that has been holding for a very long time deciding to stop.*

Her copied page was folded inside her shirt, against her skin. Her typed duplicate testimony was in the false bottom of her document case, under three years of innocuous correspondence about paper allowances and filing protocols. She had not slept more than two hours at a stretch since the arrest attempt, since Brandt's men had taken the dispatch case, since she had stood in the corridor outside the commandant's office with her heart hammering and watched Oscar Amberdale walk out of it un-arrested, unbroken, and entirely unreadable.

That had been four days ago. Since then, nothing. The garrison held its breath along with everyone else. Orderlies whispered in stairwells. The laundry ran hot and silent. Somewhere in the officers' wing sat a locked dispatch case whose contents could hang half a dozen careers, waiting — Ilse assumed — for someone to decide what to do with it, which meant waiting for someone to decide what to do with *her*, because she was fairly sure she was on whatever list existed now.

And then, at ten minutes past five in the morning, the river solved the problem for everybody by simply ending winter.

* * *

The crack came just before dawn — one enormous report from the far bank, like the sky clearing its throat — and after that the groaning began in earnest, sheets of ice grinding against the pilings, heaving up in gray-green slabs, splitting along seams nobody could have predicted. Ilse dressed in the dark, put her document case under her arm as casually as a lunch basket, and went down to the water.

Half the garrison was already there. That was the thing nobody told you about rivers breaking: it was the one event in a garrison town that drew every rank together without orders. Privates and corporals and laundresses and two lieutenants who should have known better, all standing shoulder to shoulder in the blue morning cold, watching a mile of ice come apart like a dinner plate dropped on stone. Somebody's dog barked joyfully at nothing. A cook passed around a jug of something warm that Ilse chose not to identify.

Marta was there, from the laundry, arms crossed, face turned toward the bridge. When the first full slab reared up and folded over with a sound like a door slamming in God's hallway, Marta said, quietly, to no one and therefore possibly to Ilse: "It took my husband in November. Frozen solid by December. I used to think I hated the winter." She did not finish the thought. She didn't need to.

By seven o'clock the channel was open — a ragged, churning black seam of open water running bank to bank — and by eight the garrison engineers were down on the near span, striking the chains off the bridge, posting the notice that the crossing was open to military and civilian traffic effective immediately.

Which meant, Ilse thought, standing at the rail with her breath steaming, that the historians' mandate was live again too. Travel resumed. Communication resumed. The commission's sealed record would go south with the next dispatch boat or the first convoy, and once it was sealed in the capital archive, no testimony entered after the fact would carry weight. Whatever was going to be in that record had to be put there *today*.

She looked down at her document case. Inside it: a typed duplicate of Oscar Amberdale's testimony, forty-one pages, witnessed and dated. And against her skin, folded into quarters, the single suppressed page she had copied from Senior Historian Reineke's working draft — the page where General Aldringen's withdrawal order at Kolvik had been quietly transformed, in the official prose, from "ordered" into "regretfully necessitated by circumstances beyond local command."

Regretfully necessitated. Ilse had read that phrase perhaps four hundred times in her life, in a hundred archives, and had always found it merely tedious. Now it made her feel slightly ill.

"You look," said a voice behind her, "like a woman doing arithmetic."

Oscar Amberdale stood beside her in her hospital coat, collar up, gray hair escaping its pins in the wind off the water. There was a bruise-yellow shadow under one eye that hadn't been there last week, and Ilse decided, deliberately, not to ask about it.

"I'm calculating how fast a resignation travels downstream," Ilse said. "If I resign this morning, does my access to the record close before or after noon?"

Oscar considered this the way she considered everything — as though the question were a patient who might yet be saved. "Faster than you'd like. Bureaucracy is only slow when it's protecting someone. When it's punishing someone, it moves like fire."

"That's what I thought." Ilse turned to face the water. A slab the size of a barn roof ground past the pilings, groaning. "So I shouldn't resign first."

"No," Oscar said. "You should enter everything first, and resign second, and let them spend the rest of their lives arguing about whether they can un-ring a bell that's already been archived under seal." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "I've spent twelve years learning how records work, Mrs. Voss. From the wrong side of them. Believe me: a document in the archive is a great deal harder to kill than a person."

* * *

The commission's workroom occupied the long room above the garrison library, and at nine o'clock it contained exactly two people: Senior Historian Reineke, who was shaving with the unhurried dignity of a man who believed history was something that happened to other people's paperwork, and Ilse, who had been arranging and rearranging the same stack of folios for twenty minutes while her pulse did arithmetic of its own.

Reineke had been kind to her, in his way. He had recommended her for the commission. He had taught her the citation formats. He kept biscuits in his desk drawer and offered them to junior staff with genuine warmth. This was the part Ilse had never anticipated about complicity — that it came wearing comfortable shoes and remembered your birthday.

"Voss." He didn't look up from the mirror. "You're up early."

"The ice broke."

"So I hear. Bridge is open. We can finally get the Kolvik annexes sent south and be shut of this place." He rinsed the razor, examining his jaw with the satisfaction of a man concluding another year's honest service. "I'll have the final draft collated by Thursday. You'll want to check your sections before sealing."

"My sections are checked," Ilse said. "That's rather what I want to discuss."

He paused, razor halfway to its case. In eleven days of shared workrooms, Ilse had learned to read Reineke's silences, and this one had a warning in it, faint as the smell of smoke before you see flames.

"I've prepared supplementary testimony," she said, keeping her voice level, almost bored — the voice of pure procedure, the most powerful voice in any bureaucracy. "A material witness statement from the hospital, corroborated by ward staff. Plus supporting documents. Under Article Nine of the commission's charter, any testimony received prior to sealing must be entered into the record."

The silence this time was longer. "Article Nine," Reineke repeated.

"Paragraph four."

"And these documents arrived... when?"

"They're arriving now," Ilse said. "This morning. Before sealing. Which makes them timely, which makes them admissible, which makes them — " she allowed herself the smallest pause — "mandatory."

He set the razor down very carefully, the way a man sets down something he'd like to throw. "Frau Voss. Sit down."

"I'd rather stand, thank you."

"*Sit down.*"

Ilse sat. It seemed politic.

For a moment Reineke said nothing at all. He stood at the window with his back to her, watching the broken river, and when he finally spoke his voice had shifted — softened, even, into the register of a man giving friendly advice to a promising junior colleague he genuinely did not wish to see destroyed.

"Do you know why we are here? Truly here?" He didn't wait for an answer. "We are here because after a war, people need a version of events they can live beside. That is not cynicism, Voss, it is *public health*. If we write down every ugly particular, every private's grudge, every widow's grief, we will produce a record so heavy no one can lift it, and a peace so bitter no one can swallow it. Aldringen is a living general with a living family and a division of living veterans who worship him. What you propose to write into that record will not hurt him — men like him don't fit in records, they stand on top of them. It will hurt the widows. It will hurt the veterans' mothers. It will hurt *you*." He turned around. "I have seen what happens to archivists who mistake themselves for judges. They end up cataloguing seed inventories in the provinces. Take the day. Think about your career."

Ilse thought about her career. She thought about it quite seriously, because honesty required her to admit that Reineke was offering her a real thing: thirty years of quiet, respected, biscuit-adjacent work among the dead, instead of a lifetime of being a name on somebody's list of problems.

Then she thought about the phrase *regretfully necessitated*. About Marta at the rail that morning, saying *I used to think I hated the winter*. About a ledger hidden under a floorboard for twelve years, filled with names written by hand because no machine would write them.

"With respect, sir," Ilse said, rising, "I have thought about it. Article Nine, paragraph four. Here is the testimony. Here are the supporting documents, receipted. I am entering them into the record under my section authority as of nine-forty this morning, and the log entry will reflect that time precisely." She set the folder on the table between them, squared its edges — a small, absurd, comforting gesture — and took out her pen. "You may countersign or decline. Declining is also recorded."

Something flickered across Reineke's face — anger, certainly, but underneath it, briefly, something that looked almost like respect, or possibly indigestion; with senior officials the two were often difficult to distinguish.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that this ends your position on this commission."

"Not immediately," Ilse said. "Immediately it ends *your* discretion over the record. My resignation follows the entry. In that order." She uncapped the pen. "I learned that sequencing from you, actually. Procedure first, sentiment later. It's in your lecture notes."

She wrote the log entry in a hand that surprised her by not shaking at all:

*9:41 a.m. Received and entered per Art. 9 §4: sworn testimony of O. Amberdale (matron, garrison hospital), 41 pp., witnessed; supplementary depositions, ward staff, 6 pp.; documentary exhibits A through F, including rosters, orders, and photographic plates pertaining to events at Kolvik Bridge, November [war year]; consolidated name-list, 214 entries, submitted for entry in full.*

Two hundred and fourteen names. She had counted them three times by lamplight, because some things deserved exactness.

Reineke did not countersign. He also did not stop her, which — Ilse understood, with the strange clarity of the moment — was itself a decision recorded nowhere and worth everything. Perhaps he saw which way the wind was blowing. Perhaps he simply lacked the appetite for a public fight over Article Nine with witnesses already in the corridor. Or perhaps — and this was the possibility Ilse would think about for years afterward — some small unfired part of him was relieved to be ordered by procedure instead of conscience, relieved to let the machine do the moral labor machines were built for.

She signed. She dated it. She placed the folder in the commission's strongbox, locked it, and entered the key number in the log.

"There," she said, and her voice cracked only slightly on the word. "Now it exists."

Reineke looked at the strongbox for a long moment. "You know they'll try to reopen it. Challenge the entry. Claim duress, forgery, insanity."

"Let them try," Ilse said. "Challenging an entry means reading it. Reading it aloud. In a room. With the names in it." She smiled — actually smiled, for the first time in eleven days. "You taught me that too, sir. The record defends itself by being read."

* * *

The resignation letter took her four minutes to write, most of which she spent on the opening line, which she rewrote six times before settling on the magnificent bureaucratic plainness of: *"I hereby resign my position on the Historical Commission, effective upon completion of the sealing inventory."* No reasons given. Reasons were for people who expected to be argued with.

She was carrying it toward the commandant's office when the corridor filled with noise — boots, voices, the specific commotion of soldiers uncertain whether to be ceremonial or alarmed — and Sergeant Brandt appeared at the far end, hat in hands, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he hasn't finished disbelieving.

"Frau Voss." He caught up to her, breathing hard. "You'd better come to the river. And bring—" he glanced left and right, lowered his voice— "bring whoever keeps your papers in order. The commandant's men opened the dispatch case this morning, per orders, to inventory the contents before sending it south."

"Yes?"

"It was empty," Brandt said. "Except," and here he hesitated, visibly choosing between loyalty and truth and landing, to his own surprise, on truth, "except a laundry list. Forty items. Marked *Kolvik, wash batch nine*."

Ilse stood very still. Somewhere below, faint through the stone, the river went on talking to itself — the great slabs turning over, the channel widening, the whole frozen weight of the season coming apart in the sunshine.

Someone had emptied the case. Someone had moved the documents. And there was exactly one person in this garrison who had spent twelve years learning to hide things in plain sight, in mislabeled bundles, under other people's names, in the boring white noise of ordinary paperwork — and who had, Ilse realized, been asking oddly precise questions about the commission's sealing procedures for the better part of a week.

"Mrs. Voss?" Brandt prompted.

"I think," Ilse said, tucking her resignation letter under her arm and heading for the stairs at a pace just short of unseemly, "you'll find that the wash batch has been delivered. Come along, Sergeant. History is being filed."
