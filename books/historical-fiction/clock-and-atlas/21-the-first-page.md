## 21. The First Page

The order came down on a Tuesday, the way most cowardly things do.

Not a tribunal. Not a cell. Not even a reprimand with an official stamp that Marta could frame and hang above the laundry mangle. Just a memorandum, typed on thin paper, informing the garrison that Commandant Brecker had been relieved of his post "for reasons of health" and would be departing for the capital "to pursue treatment." Effective immediately. Replacement pending. No further questions would be entertained, and indeed no questions were asked, because everyone in that town had learned long ago that questions were the sort of animal you did not feed unless you wanted it living in your house forever.

Oscar read the notice where it was posted beside the duty roster, between a reminder about boil-washing the ward linens and an advertisement for a lost dog named Sergeant who was, according to the description, "friendly but dishonest about food."

"Well," she said to Sergeant's owner, a farrier's boy who had given up hope weeks ago, "at least someone in this town is being honest about their appetite."

The boy laughed. Oscar did not, quite. She kept looking at the memorandum, at the phrase *reasons of health*, and thought: there it is again. The great soft eraser of the age. Not fire, not arrest — just a quiet retirement, a pension, a house in the capital with geraniums, and a man who had threatened her with arrest in March walking free in April because trying him would mean asking questions, and questions were the animal nobody fed.

She had known this was how it would end before it ended. Ilse had warned her, in her flat archival way, folding her hands on the table of the dispensary like a woman laying out instruments: "They will not try him. Trying him makes the record curious, and the record must never be curious. They will thank him for his service and lose his forwarding address."

"And that's justice?" Oscar had asked.

"That's what justice looks like when it has to fit through a filing system," Ilse said. "It isn't everything. But the names stand. Don't let the shape of the defeat hide the shape of the win."

*The names stand.* Oscar held onto that through the whole ridiculous farewell. Because Brecker got one — a farewell, small and mortified, on the parade ground, the garrison drawn up in ranks while he shook hands with officers who could not look at him and saluted soldiers who did not know enough not to look at him. He climbed into a staff car that had come across the ice-road's last solid week, and as it pulled away he turned, once, and found Oscar standing at the edge of the parade ground with her hands in her coat pockets.

She did not glare. She had spent twelve years learning not to glare; it was practically her trade. She simply looked at him, the way she looked at a fever chart when the numbers told her something she already knew. And she raised one hand — not a salute, nothing so dignified — just a small wave, the kind you give the milk cart.

His face did something complicated. The car carried it away.

* * *

Spring came on slowly that year, the way honest things do.

The river broke in the third week of April — Ilse heard it first from her window at the historians' quarters and woke half the garrison stumbling into the yard in nightclothes to watch — but the bridge itself took longer. The flood had taken two spans and left them "leaning with intent," in the words of the engineer, a cheerful man named Polt who talked about timber damage the way other men talked about horses. For three more weeks the town crossed by ferry, paying a penny and grumbling about it, and Oscar rode the ferry each morning to the hospital and back, and every crossing she looked down at the water — brown now, and busy, carrying sticks and fence rails and one entire henhouse past the pilings — and thought about how a river keeps nothing and carries everything, and how she had spent twelve years being the opposite kind of water.

Then, on a bright cold morning smelling of thawed mud and cut lumber, Polt declared the bridge sound, the last plank was nailed, and the chains came off.

There should have been a ceremony. There wasn't, because ceremonies cost money, so instead there was simply a moment when a boy from the toll house unhooked the chain, coiled it over his shoulder, and stood aside, and the first cart of spring rolled across — potatoes, as it happened, three sacks of them, driven by a woman who had been waiting since January and had opinions about the delay that she shared generously with the whole span.

Oscar watched all of this from the near bank, holding a bundle of flowers wrapped in newspaper.

Marta had pressed them on her at the laundry door an hour earlier, thrusting them at her chest with the tenderness of a woman loading a musket. Crocuses and the first stubborn daffodils from the strip of ground behind the wash-house where Marta had planted bulbs in the autumn without telling anyone, without knowing if she'd still be employed by spring, without any assurance at all that the world would be worth flowers in it. That was Marta all over. She planted against the evidence.

"You'll take them to the far side," Marta had said. It was not a request. Marta's requests came out as statements; it saved time.

"Why the far side?"

"Because I can't cross yet." Marta said it plainly, wiping her hands on her apron. Her hands had gone still, which was how Oscar knew it was costing her. "One day I'll cross. Not today. But you'll walk it for me today, and you'll carry something living to the other bank, and that will be — " she searched, and settled on, " — correct."

So Oscar walked the repaired bridge on its opening morning carrying flowers like a mourner at a wedding.

The planks rang faintly underfoot. New wood among old, pale among grey, and Polt had sawn the joints so neatly that the seam was almost invisible unless you knew to look — and once you knew to look you saw it everywhere, the whole span a ledger of what had broken and been mended. Halfway across she stopped, because halfway across was where the water ran deepest, and leaned on the rail, and looked down at the current going somewhere with everything it found.

Twelve years ago she had stood on a bank not thirty miles upstream and watched smoke and listened to orders and written names on a list she was told to burn, and had burned some of it, and had lied about the rest, and had gone home and begun the ledger under the floorboard because silence was no longer something she could do quietly. She had thought, for years, that the ledger was guilt. Then she had thought it was duty. Standing on the new bridge, she understood it had been neither, or both: it was simply the part of her that refused to become water that keeps nothing. She was the other kind. She carried things to the far bank. That was the whole of her profession, in the end. Nurse, witness — same job description. You keep the patient until they arrive somewhere.

A cart rattled past her, the driver tipping his hat. A soldier on leave jogged across, laughing at the novelty of it. The bridge held. Of course it held; Polt had built it to hold, and Polt was the sort of man whose pride you could stand on.

On the far bank, Oscar found a spot where the grass had already greened, above the ferry landing, and knelt, and put the flowers in a tin that had once held boot polish. Crocuses purple and daffodils yellow against the brown earth, ridiculous and defiant, exactly as intended. She did not say anything over them. Marta hadn't asked her to say anything, and anyway the names had been said now — all of them, aloud, in a room full of historians, entered into the record, printed, filed, sealed, permanent. There are things that outrank speeches. A name in a book outranks nearly everything.

She stayed a while. The sun came through. Somewhere behind her the town went about its business, arguing about potato prices, and the river carried its cargo of winter southward, and none of it — none of it — felt like victory. That was the honest shape of the thing, and Oscar had promised herself, somewhere around the night she'd read the Kolvik names aloud to a hall of strangers, that she would never again lie about the shape of a thing, not even to comfort herself. The commandant would grow geraniums. Vasseur lay under a marble-ish stone that said HERO. The general whose flattery had bent three chapters of the campaign history would probably be promoted. And forty-one names stood in the official record where before there had been the phrase *civilian losses incidental to withdrawal*, a phrase so bloodless it could have been typed by a machine, and possibly was.

Imperfect. Honestly rendered. It would have to do, because it was what there was, and she had learned — finally, at fifty-one, slowly, the way she learned everything — that *it will have to do* is not resignation. Sometimes it is just the truth, standing up straight.

* * *

On her way back across, she met Ilse coming the other way with a document case and an expression that meant trouble of the interesting kind.

They had fallen into a habit since the closing session: meeting on Wednesdays in the dispensary, ostensibly to review Oscar's witness statement, actually to argue, which both of them enjoyed more than either would admit. Ilse had resigned — formally, in writing, over the objection of her senior historian, who had objected at length and to no effect, since Ilse's resignation letter was itself now a matter of record and therefore beyond his power to lose — but she had stayed in town, cataloguing. She had accepted a position with a private archive being established in the capital by people who had read the testimony and decided that the victors' version of history could use a rival, and she was spending her last weeks at the garrison doing something she described only as "making sure nothing here gets tidied."

"Tidied," Oscar said now, falling into step with her on the near span. "You use that word the way surgeons use *debrided*."

"I use it accurately. I found four pages of the garrison log missing for November of the war year. Four pages, cut out with a razor and the stubs initialled as if nothing were absent. Someone *tidied*." She said it with real feeling. Ilse's outrage had always been administrative in nature, which made it more durable than most. "But that's not why I was looking for you. Two things. One trivial, one not."

"Give me the trivia first. Build my strength."

"Your farrier's boy found his dog. Sergeant was living at the ferry landing, eating the earnings off three different fishermen. As predicted: friendly, dishonest about food."

"A happy ending with a criminal at the center of it," Oscar said. "Fitting for our times. And the other thing?"

Ilse stopped walking, which was unusual; Ilse generally moved through the world at the pace of a clerk late for a train. She set her hand on the bridge rail and looked out at the water.

"The archive in the capital wants to acquire your ledger," she said. "Formally. Under acquisition protocols, with a catalogue number, conservation treatment, the whole apparatus. It would sit beside the testimony. Researchers would find them together." A pause, precisely measured, the way Ilse measured most things. "And they want it with the first page filled in. Provenance, they call it. Who kept it, who wrote it, under what circumstances. A ledger of names without a keeper's name reads like rumor. With one, it reads like evidence."

Oscar looked at her hands on the rail.

She had known this was coming too. Ilse had mentioned it twice before, gently, the way one mentions a dose of medicine to a patient who hates doses, and each time Oscar had changed the subject to linens or weather or the scandalous price of coal. Because the first page had been blank for twelve years, and the blankness had been load-bearing. As long as the ledger began with other people's names and not her own, it remained what it had always been: an act of hiding that looked like an act of keeping. The moment her own name sat at the front — *Amberdale, Oscar, civilian nursing sister, ward attendant, witness* — it stopped being a secret and became a claim. Anyone who wished her ill would know exactly where to point.

Except, she thought, looking at the water, there was hardly anyone left to point at her. The commandant was retired. Vasseur was dead and buried under his lie. The officer who had made her burn the list — she had written his name in the ledger too, years ago, in ink she'd mixed herself from soot and glue because proper ink was rationed and secrets don't wait — that name stood in the record now as well, and men like that did not come for nurses. They came for archives, and archives had catalogue numbers and conservation treatment, and were harder to burn than floorboards.

"I'll need a good pen," she said.

Ilse blinked. In twelve years of knowing her — well, six weeks of knowing her, though it felt like twelve — Oscar had never once agreed to anything on the first ask, and Ilse had clearly budgeted for three more attempts. "That's — yes. I have pens. Good ones. Steel nibs, from the capital."

"Don't gloat, Ilse. It's unbefitting an archivist."

"I'm not gloating. This is documentation of satisfaction." But she was smiling, briefly and badly, the way people smile when they've won something they're not sure they had the right to want. Then, quieter: "You don't have to write more than the name. If the rest is too much. Name, rank, date. The account can stay as fragments. Fragments are admissible. I checked."

"No," Oscar said. "I'll write the whole of it. All of it, including the parts where I obeyed, including the list I burned, including the name I lied to save. If the page is going to exist, it's going to tell the truth, or there's no earthly reason for it to exist at all." She started walking again, and the boards rang under them, new wood and old. "Besides. Twelve years under a floorboard teaches you one thing thoroughly: hiding is exhausting. I'm fifty-one years old and I would like, for once, to put something down *on purpose*, in daylight, where people can argue with it."

"People will argue with it."

"Good. Arguments mean it's alive."

At the bridge's end they parted, Ilse toward the historians' quarters and her razor-cut mysteries, Oscar toward the hospital and the ward, the evening shift, the fevers and the linens and the ordinary machinery of keeping people alive until they arrived somewhere. At the corner she paused and looked back once. Ilse had also paused, and lifted her hand — not a wave, nothing so casual. A small salute, archivist to witness, keeper to keeper.

Oscar laughed and waved back properly, milk-cart style, and went in to work.

That night, after the ward slept and the lamp was trimmed low, she took up the loose floorboard one last time — not to hide anything, but because the space needed cleaning before the ledger left it forever — and she sat on the cold floor with the ledger open on her knees and the good steel-nibbed pen beside her and the first page blank in front of her, white as March ice, waiting.

She dipped the pen. Her hand, she noticed, was entirely steady.

She wrote: *Oscar Amberdale.*

And then, beneath it, because the truth deserved a fair start and she had always been one to give a patient the full chart: *Nursing sister. Witness. Keeper of these names. And here is how it began —*

The pen moved. Outside, the river ran on, carrying everything, keeping nothing. Inside, under a lamp, in her own hand, the keeping had just begun.
