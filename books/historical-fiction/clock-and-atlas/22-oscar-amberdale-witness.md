## 22. Oscar Amberdale, Witness

The ink froze on the sill before it dried.

That was the trouble with spring in the river country — it arrived like a guest who promises to come and then takes another month about it, and when it finally knocks it has already tracked mud across the whole doorstep. Oscar Amberdale had lived through forty-one of these springs in the town of Vellmark, or at least she had been present for them; whether a person who hides half herself under a floorboard can be said to fully live through anything was a question she had only lately begun to allow into the house.

She sat now at the window of her small room above the laundry, the ledger open on her knees to its first page.

For twelve years that page had been blank. She had always known why, though she had never said so aloud, not even to Marta, not even to Ilse. The blank page was a door she kept shut with her own body. As long as her name wasn't in the ledger, she was only the keeper — a clerk of other people's griefs, a woman who wrote down what happened to everyone else and remained, officially, nobody. Nobody could be arrested for nobody. Nobody could be tried, transported, shot at dawn, erased. She had built her safety out of an absence, the way the garrison built its walls out of other men's stones.

But absence, she had learned, is just silence wearing a tidy dress. And she had promised herself, standing on the repaired bridge in March with Marta's flowers going brown in her hands, that she was finished with silence.

So: the first page.

She dipped the pen again and held it over the paper, and her hand did the thing it had done three times already that morning, which was stop. A drop gathered at the nib, swelled, and fell onto the margin — a small black moon.

"Blast," she said, to the room, to the drop, to twelve years of her own cowardice dressed up as prudence.

From below came the thump-thump-thump of Marta wringing sheets, and then Marta's voice, carrying up through the floorboards with the ease of a voice that had survived Kolvik Bridge and therefore feared nothing so minor as a ceiling. "What have you ruined now?"

"A page."

"Is it your page?"

"It is."

"Then ruin it properly," Marta called back. "You've been ruining it politely since breakfast. I can hear you thinking from down here. It sounds like a mouse trying to lift a piano."

Oscar laughed despite herself, and the laugh loosened something, and that, she supposed, was what Marta was for. She blotted the little ink moon, took a breath the length of a full sentence, and wrote:

*Amberdale, Oscar Elin. Head Nurse, Third Field Hospital, Kolvik. Keeper of this ledger from the winter of the war's end to the spring of the ice-break. Present at Kolvik Bridge on the night of the abandonment. Witness to all names herein.*

Then, because the truth does not like to be written in installments, she kept going — the account Ilse had asked for twice, gently, and which Oscar had promised once, in the dispensary, with lamplight between them. She wrote about the field hospital and the smell of the stoves. She wrote about Colonel Brandt putting the first list into her hands and saying *burn this, nurse, that's an order,* and how she had burned one page and hidden the rest inside her boot where they rode against her ankle for eleven days like a second pulse. She wrote the name of the boy she had saved by lying — Corporal Anders Vik, recorded dead of fever when he was alive and walking south under a borrowed coat — because a lie told for mercy deserved, after twelve years, to be entered honestly into the record as a lie. She wrote about the order to withdraw. She wrote about the civilians on the bridge, and the ice, and the sound.

The sound she wrote last, and briefly. Some things go into a ledger best in few words, the way a wound goes into a bandage.

When she finished, the light had moved from the sill to the floor, and her hand ached, and she sat for a while doing nothing at all, which was a luxury she intended to practice until she got good at it.

* * *

Ilse's archive occupied three rooms above the customs house in the capital, which Ilse described, with the fierce pride of a woman who had fought a general's aide over a filing cabinet, as "temporary accommodations," and which everyone else understood to be permanent ones, since the customs house had been promising to reclaim the rooms for eleven years and no one at the customs house could remember why.

Oscar traveled there in April, taking the morning boat — the bridge was for carts and walkers now, and she still crossed it slowly, out of respect rather than fear, but the river felt different from a deck, kinder, and she allowed herself kindnesses these days the way a reformed miser allows himself a second sugar.

Marta had come with her as far as the quay, ostensibly to see her off and actually to inspect the boat captain for signs of unreliability. "Write if the archivist gives you any trouble," Marta said. "I'll come up on the next boat and fold her into a cupboard."

"She's five foot two and carries a pistol she's never fired."

"I've folded bigger."

The city was loud in the way cities are loud after a winter in a garrison town — everything talking at once, hooves and gulls and a man selling something hot and unidentifiable — and Oscar walked through it with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, feeling the weight of it the way one feels a sleeping cat: present, warm, faintly alarming.

Three floors up, a bell that didn't work announced her anyway, because Ilse heard the stairs and was at the door before the third landing.

"You came by boat," Ilse said. It was not a question. Ilse had a habit of stating facts as greetings, which Oscar had decided was less coldness than armor worn on the wrong side.

"The bridge seemed too easy," Oscar said. "After everything, I wanted the river to have to work for it."

Ilse's office — the middle room, the good light — was a country made entirely of paper. Boxes stood in ranks along the walls like patient soldiers. There was a map on one wall pinned through with colored flags in a pattern that meant something only to Ilse, and possibly not even to Ilse anymore. On the desk, squared off with archival precision, lay a green folder labeled in Ilse's upright hand: *Kolvik — Testimony & Ledger — Final Deposition Pending.*

"Pending," Oscar said, reading it upside down.

"Pending your signature," Ilse said. "And pending tea. I have both ready. Well — I've had the tea ready since Tuesday. The signature I was prepared to wait for."

They sat, and the tea was strong and slightly stewed, which was exactly right, and for a moment neither woman spoke, because some friendships are conducted mostly in comfortable silences with occasional documents.

"You know," Ilse said at last, turning her cup, "when you stood up at that closing session and began reading the names, I counted them. Professionally. I couldn't help it. One hundred and fourteen, and you didn't miss one." She looked up. "I've never told you that. That I counted."

"You were the only one in the room counting," Oscar said. "Everyone else was holding their breath."

"Yes, well. Someone has to count. That's rather the whole point of me." Ilse set down her cup and drew the green folder toward her, but did not open it. Her fingers rested on it the way a doctor's fingers rest on a wrist. "There's something you should know before you sign. The commandant."

"What about him?"

"He was retired with honors, as you know. Full pension. The tribunal declined to pursue the Vasseur correspondence — insufficient institutional interest, they called it." Ilse's mouth did something complicated. "Last month he wrote to me. A long letter. He wanted to know, formally, whether his name appears anywhere in my holdings."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I couldn't confirm or deny the contents of an active collection." Ilse permitted herself the smallest smile Oscar had ever seen on her — a smile like a coin found in an old coat. "Which is technically true until you sign today, and false forever afterward. His name is in the ledger, Oscar. Page thirty-one, I believe. He was at Kolvik, and the record says so, and the record is no longer anyone's to edit."

Oscar thought about the commandant — the smooth voice, the offer of a transfer and a pension, the terrible gentleness of a man pricing her survival — and found, somewhat to her surprise, that she felt almost nothing. Not forgiveness; she wasn't in the business of manufacturing that. Just the particular lightness of a debt no longer carried.

"Good," she said simply.

* * *

The signing itself took four minutes and felt like four years.

There was a form, of course — there is always a form; Ilse's profession ran on forms the way the garrison had run on rumor — and Oscar read every line of it, because she had spent a lifetime watching people sign things without reading and knew precisely what that cost. *Depositor.* That was the word for her now. Not witness-in-waiting, not keeper, not the woman under the floorboard. Depositor: one who hands over.

At the bottom, beneath the line reading *Name of depositor,* her pen hesitated one last time.

"Not this again," she murmured.

"Pardon?" said Ilse.

"Nothing. Talking to my hand." And she signed — *O. E. Amberdale* — in the same hand that had kept the ledger through frost and search and betrayal, and it was the same name and it was an entirely different name, and both things were true, which was, she was coming to understand, the ordinary condition of true things.

Ilse carried the ledger to a shelf herself. She would not let the assistant touch it; she had explained earlier that the assistant was seventeen and enthusiastic and had once dropped a box of court transcripts down a stairwell, and that some collections earned the privilege of clumsiness being forbidden. The shelf was oak, plain, at shoulder height — reachable, visible, nothing like a floorboard.

"There," Ilse said, stepping back. "It will be catalogued by Friday. Anyone may read it. Anywhere. Forever."

"Forever is a strong word for an institution that loses boxes down stairwells."

"Forever is a strong word," Ilse agreed, "but paper is stronger. Paper outlives every general, every tribunal, every man who writes asking whether his name appears. I've staked my whole dull life on it." She turned, and for a moment the young archivist who had arrived by sledge wearing the enemy's uniform stood in her face again — the curiosity, the care, the stubbornness that had copied a suppressed page and shown it to no one until the showing mattered. "Twelve years you kept those names alive alone. Now the state keeps them. That's not a small thing, Oscar. You should be proud."

"I am," Oscar said, and found it was true, and found also that pride, unpracticed, sits awkwardly, like a coat cut for someone else. "Though I confess I keep waiting for someone to arrest me. Twelve years of waiting leaves a groove."

"The groove fills," Ilse said. "It takes a season or two. I'm told."

* * *

She stayed in the capital three days, at Marta's insistence ("you'll come back sunburnt and full of ideas and I'll hear about none of them unless you stay"), and on the second day she did something she had not planned.

She went back to the archive alone, in the late afternoon, and asked the young assistant — carefully, away from the shelves — whether she might simply sit with the ledger a while. Reading room privileges, Ilse had decreed, extended to depositors, and the assistant, awed by anyone whose name was already in the catalogue, waved her through with the reverence of a sacristan.

The reading room was empty. Light came slantwise through tall windows, and dust moved in it with no particular ambition.

Oscar opened the ledger not to the first page but to the middle, to the names entered in the winter after the war, in her own careful hand — privates and laundresses and a ferryman and two children recorded only as *girl, approx. 6* and *boy, infant, unnamed.* She had given the boy a name eventually, in the ledger: *Karl, son of Marta.* Marta had chosen it herself, one night over mending, and Oscar had written it down, and now here it was in a government archive under glass-free daylight, permanent as stone and soft as cloth.

A girl of six. An infant named Karl. A colonel who died whispering numbers. A commandant who wanted to know if history had noticed him.

And at the front of the book, in ink that was now merely old instead of impossible: her own name, rank, and account.

She closed the ledger gently and sat with her hands flat on the cover, and understood — finally, plainly, the way one understands a thing one has been circling for a chapter and a half of one's life — that she had never been only the keeper of other people's names. Keeping them had *made* her. Every entry had been an answer to the question the war kept asking, *who are you when no one is recording?* — and the answer, entered nightly in a hidden hand, had been: *the one who records.* The silence had been her disguise, but the ledger had been her self, all along, written in the only language she trusted.

She was not handing over a burden today. She was publishing a biography — twelve years long, one hundred and fourteen names plus one, bound in boards and hidden under a floorboard by a woman who had mistaken hiding for living, and who had, thanks to a widow's fury and an archivist's stubbornness and a dying enemy's guilt, unlearned the mistake in time.

Oscar picked up the pen the reading room provided, and on the slip of paper visitors used to request volumes, she wrote a note for the catalogue, because Ilse had mentioned the cataloguer was stuck on how to describe the collection:

*Not a record of the dead only. A record of the one who refused to let them be forgotten. Handle accordingly, and often.*

She left it on top of the ledger, put on her coat, and went down the stairs past the broken bell, out into the loud forgiving evening, where somewhere across the river Marta was waiting with opinions and supper, and the ice was long gone, and the water — she could hear it even here, even in the city — was running bright and unstoppable toward the sea.
