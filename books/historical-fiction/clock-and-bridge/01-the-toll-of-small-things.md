## 1. The Toll of Small Things

The river woke before I did, the way it always did in March — groaning under its load of snowmelt, pushing at the pilings of the toll bridge like a debtor who wanted to be let out early. I heard it through the wall of my room above the counting house, and for a moment, half-asleep, I thought it was my father's voice. Then I was awake, and it was just water, and that was fine. Water was fine. Water was what we had instead of bells or roosters or anything kind.

I dressed in the dark so I wouldn't have to see the ledgers stacked on my desk. They'd still be there when the light came. That was the one reliable thing about Marrow & Vane: everything stayed where you put it, if you put it down hard enough and signed your name beside it.

Downstairs, the counting house smelled of tallow and cold iron. Brannigan was already at his high desk by the window — he lived to be there before me, to have his coat off and his ink mixed while I was still on the stairs, so he could look up with that expression like a man finding a wet dog in his kitchen.

"You're late," he said. The clock over the safe said otherwise, but the clock did not have opinions worth having in this room.

"The clock disagrees."

"The clock counts for the company." He went back to his columns. "Same as you."

There was nothing to say to that, which is why he said it. Brannigan had been clerk of Marrow & Vane since before I could write, and he had the particular cruelty of small men in small rooms, which is to say he never raised his voice and never wasted a cut. He called me *the diver's girl*, though my father had been dead six years and my mother hadn't been near the counting house since the day they read us the indenture papers aloud. He said it the way other men spat.

I sat at my low desk by the back wall — the one with the leg shimmed with a folded invoice from 1893 — and opened the day's ledger. The Pearl Account. Divers' debts, boat liens, advance payments against future catch. My father's name was in it on the first page I turned, because my father's name was in nearly every page of it somewhere, the way rivers are in maps.

*Almeida, R. — pearl boat *Santa Luzia* — lien held M&V — balance outstanding.*

I ruled my lines. I carried figures. I added a column twice because the first time it disagreed with itself, and the second time it agreed with me, which is how arithmetic works when you do it honestly: eventually everything confesses.

By mid-morning the divers started coming up from the flats to argue their accounts, as they did every Thursday before the flood season shut the bridge and the packets stopped running. I knew most of them by their debts before I knew them by their faces. Old Marta Iriarte owed eleven pounds and a boat. Her nephew Tomas, who was seventeen and built like a capstan, owed four pounds against next season's catch and stood behind her now, arms crossed, glaring at Brannigan's window the way he glared at everything that had a roof.

"Interest," Marta said, flat, tapping the page I turned toward her. "Again. Every season, interest, and the balance never goes down. Explain that to me, girl, since you hold the pen."

I explained it. I was good at explaining it; I'd had practice explaining it to myself. The interest compounded on the anniversary of the lien, the payments were applied first to penalties, the penalties accrued monthly — I laid it all out like a recipe, flour and fat and blood, and watched her face go the way faces went when the arithmetic was correct but unbearable. Correctness was no comfort to anyone in this town except the men upstairs.

"That's not right," Tomas said quietly.

"It's accurate," I said.

"Those aren't the same thing."

Brannigan's pen stopped. In the counting house, a stopped pen was a shout. "Something to add, Iriarte? Take it up with the owners, if you like. They're aboard the *Meridian* this week, taking refreshment." He smiled at some private joke about refreshment. "They'll be sailing soon enough. Then it'll just be us again, thank God."

Tomas looked at me when he left — one long look, the kind divers give a stretch of water they don't trust — and then they were gone and the door was shut and Brannigan sniffed like a man clearing smoke from a room.

"Watch yourself with that lot," he said. "You're company now. Best remember whose side of the counter you stand on."

I remembered. That was the whole trouble with my life; I remembered everything, and none of it changed anything.

* * *

At noon I carried the post bag across the bridge — my one errand outside, my daily ration of sky. The bridge was the only reason this town existed, and everyone knew it, and everyone pretended not to. Two hundred yards of timber and iron spanning the narrows, owned by Marrow & Vane, tolling everything that crossed: carts, cattle, coffins. Every coin dropped into the toll chest made a sound like a very small promise being kept. Old Sef, the keeper, sat in his toll house wrapped in three coats despite the season, and lifted two fingers at me in greeting, which from Sef was a speech.

"Flood's coming," he said, looking at the water going brown and muscular between the piers. "Early this year."

"They always say that."

"They're always right, eventually. Being right eventually is the river's whole trade." He glanced at me sideways. He was the only person in this town who didn't call me the diver's girl. He'd known my father; they'd drunk together in the years when my father still came off the boats with pearls in his teeth and salt drying white on his shoulders. "Your mother well?"

"Well enough."

"Give her my regards."

I said I would. We both knew I wouldn't, because Sef's regards were a language from before, and my mother had stopped speaking it. But it was polite, and politeness was one of the few things the bridge gave away free.

From the middle of the span I could see the whole town laid out like something a child had built and got bored of: the counting house, the warehouse, the chandlery, the mudflat shore below the bluff where the diver families kept their houses leaning against each other for comfort, and out in the roads, the *Meridian* riding her anchor, black-hulled, patient. The Vane cousins had come up on her a week ago from the coast, and the town had gone stiff and formal the way it always did when ownership arrived in person. They dined at the big house. They inspected the warehouse. They walked the bridge once, in good coats, and paid their own tolls in exact coin, which everyone agreed was either generosity or an insult, depending on where you stood.

Tonight, Brannigan told me, there'd be a dinner at the counting house for them — the last one before the tide. I'd serve. I always served at the dinners, because my hands were clean and my mouth stayed shut, and those were, as far as I could tell, the two qualifications for my entire existence.

* * *

My mother's house was the third from the waterline, which meant it flooded knee-deep every spring and dried out smelling of river until June. She was mending a net when I came in, though she hadn't been on a boat in nine years, because the net belonged to the Iriartes and she took their mending in exchange for oil and meal. She never took anything from me but news, and she took that reluctantly, like a woman accepting coins she suspected were clipped.

"You're thin," she said, by way of hello.

"I'm the same weight I've been for two years. You weigh me every visit."

"And you're thinner every time." She bit thread, tied off. Her hands were quick and bitter and beautiful, my mother's hands, hands that had gutted fish and hauled lines and once, people said, been the fastest on the *Santa Luzia*'s deck. "Did you eat?"

"Yes."

"Liar. There's soup."

There was soup. It tasted mostly of the pot it was cooked in, but it was hot, and I ate it at her table while she worked, and neither of us spoke for a while, which was our way of speaking.

"They're sailing tomorrow," she said finally. "The Vanes. Tide's at dawn."

"So Brannigan says."

"Before the floods close the road." She pulled the netting needle through and through. "Convenient, isn't it. Always leaving before the water comes. Like gulls."

"It's the flood season, Mamãe. Everyone leaves before it if they can. The magistrate's coming by river next week and he can't even get here after — "

"I know when the magistrate comes." Sharp, then softening, because sharpness cost her more than it used to. She looked at me across the table, and my mother had eyes the exact color of deep water, and looking into them was like looking into a place where things sank and stayed. "You balance their books, Wendell. You know better than anyone what leaves this town on those ships."

"Pearls. Rubber. What the divers owe."

"What the divers owe," she repeated, and something in how she said it made me look up. But she only shook her head and bent back to the net. "Nothing. Eat your soup. Come Sunday if the water lets you."

I walked back along the flats in the last light. Behind me the houses leaned together; ahead, the bridge stood black against a bruised sky, and beneath it the river ran high and fast, carrying sticks, leaves, a drowned hat, everything loose it could find, out toward the sea. On the *Meridian*, lamps were coming up one by one, little gold points, orderly as ledger entries. Tomorrow at dawn they'd be gone, and the town would settle back into its long gray sleep, and the bridge would close for the season, and nothing here would change.

That was what I believed, walking home with my skirts hemmed in river-mud. I believed it the way you believe in weather, or debt, or your own name — completely, without ever once examining it.

In the counting house, Brannigan had left the dinner preparations half-done and gone home. The ledgers waited on my desk for tomorrow's entries, and among them the Pearl Account lay open to the page where I'd stopped, my ruling pencil still resting in the gutter like a bookmark.

I should have closed it. I always closed it.

But the lamp was lit anyway, and the page was open anyway, and my eye fell anyway on the entry two lines from the bottom — a boat lien, dated the twelfth of November, six years past — and above it, in faded brown ink, a signature.

*R. Almeida.*

My father's name, in my father's hand.

Dated three weeks after they pulled his body out of the narrows.
