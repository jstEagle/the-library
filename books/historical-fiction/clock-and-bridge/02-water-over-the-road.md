## 2. Water Over the Road

The river announced itself before dawn.

Wendell Almeida heard it from her cot in the loft above the counting house — a low, grinding sound, like a millstone turning somewhere out in the dark. By the time she dressed and climbed down the narrow stair, the windows along Water Street had gone the color of old pewter, and the men standing in the doorways all wore the same expression: not fear, exactly, but the flat readiness of people who had done this before and would do it again.

"Flood's come," said the cooper's boy, who was eleven and considered this the finest news of the year. "Bridge closes by noon, they're saying."

Wendell pulled her coat close and walked toward the water.

The Marrow River in flood season was a different animal than the brown, patient stream she'd known all her life. It came down from the high country carrying whole trees, fences, a chicken coop with its roof still on, moving at a speed that made the eye uncertain. The pilings of the toll bridge — the bridge, the reason the town existed at all, the thin wooden spine that connected the pearl grounds and the mudflat settlements to everything east — stood in churning water already up to their collars.

Old Sef was on the near abutment when she arrived, leaning on the rail with his pipe unlit, watching the current carry past what looked like an entire barn door.

"Morning, Miss Wendell."

"Sef."

"Close today." He said it without looking at her. "You can tell it by the sound under the boards. When she talks like that, we shut her up and let her say what she wants to nobody."

The toll house was a small square room at the bridge's center, and Sef its keeper for thirty years. When the bridge closed, Sef closed with it; his job became sitting out the season in the toll house with a stove and a lamp and whatever books he'd hoarded, keeping watch over a road no one could use. Wendell had always thought it must be the loneliest work in town. Sef seemed to think it was the best.

"They'll want the last entries before noon," he said. "Company traffic. You'd best bring the book up before the water does."

That was Wendell's errand, then. She collected the toll ledger from the company clerk's desk and carried it back through streets gone busy with the particular industry of closing: shutters coming down, boats being hauled above the waterline, the fishmonger arguing with his neighbor about whose fault the chicken coop was.

* * *

Marrow & Vane's counting house occupied the best corner in town, two stories of grey timber with the company name painted across the lintel in letters that had faded from black to a tired charcoal. Inside smelled always of ink, lamp oil, and the pickled herring Brannigan ate at his desk despite three separate instructions to stop.

Brannigan was there when she arrived, as he was always there, a broad soft man with small clean hands and the permanent aggrieved look of someone who believed the world had promised him better.

"You're late."

"The bridge is closing. I brought the toll book."

"I can see what you brought." He didn't reach for it. "Mr. Vane wants the quarterly abstracts on his desk by four o'clock, and he wants them *correct*, which is more than I can say for the last lot you gave him. Sit down and don't touch anything until you've washed your hands."

Wendell set the toll ledger on its shelf and took her stool at the low desk by the window — the junior desk, hers since she was twelve years old, when the company had converted her father's outstanding debt into her indenture with the smooth efficiency of a mill converting grain to flour. Six years now. Four remaining.

She worked. That was the whole of her talent, or the whole of the one they paid for, which amounted to the same thing. She could total columns faster than Brannigan and with fewer errors; she could read a hand of shipping manifests in three languages; she could keep six accounts in her head at once and never once confuse them. What she could not do, and had long ago stopped trying to do, was make Brannigan see her as anything other than what the ledgers said she was: property, entry number 4471, indentured to Marrow & Vane Trading Company, term of fourteen years, consideration being the settlement of debts incurred by Joaquim Almeida, pearl diver, deceased.

Her father had been dead eight years. The debt, somehow, was not.

By noon the bridge was closed. She watched it happen from the counting-house window: Sef walking the length of the span one last time, checking each board with his boot, then swinging the great timber barrier across the near approach and dropping the bar into its brackets. A small crowd watched him do it, the way crowds watch anything, and then drifted away, and the bridge stood empty over the flooded river like a sentence no one would finish.

The last packet boat of the season went downriver at half past two, riding high and overloaded with the mail, the season's pearl consignments, and everyone in town who had anywhere else to be. Wendell stood at the window and watched it go. After it, the only vessel left on the water was the *Corvina* — Marrow & Vane's own ship, black-hulled, riding at anchor a quarter mile off the company wharf, waiting out the flood tide like a cat outside a mousehole.

The Vane cousins had been aboard the *Corvina* for nine days.

Nobody in town had met them before this visit, though their names were stitched through every ledger Wendell had ever kept. Ambrose Vane was the elder — sixty, silver-bearded, with a traveler's weathered face and a collector's habit of touching things without picking them up. His cousin Silas was younger and quieter and did most of the actual looking. They had arrived, so far as anyone knew, to inspect their investment: the pearl fleet, the counting house, the toll bridge. They dined aboard the ship. They came ashore in the afternoons in a shore boat with brass trim, walked the wharf, touched things. And every evening the shore boat carried a leather document case back out to the ship, and every morning it carried another one back.

Four o'clock found Wendell climbing the wharf stairs to the owners' rented rooms above the harbor office, abstracts under her arm. Ambrose Vane received her in a parlor furnished entirely in objects that had clearly been bought elsewhere: rugs, instruments, a case of birds' eggs from some country she couldn't have named.

"The girl keeps our books," he said to his cousin, not quite as introduction. "Sit down. Let's see if she can read her own figures."

She laid the abstracts on the table. Silas Vane turned pages without appearing to read them, and asked questions that weren't questions, and Ambrose talked over both of them about the river, the flood of '89, a bridge in Singapore built by a man who later drowned. Wendell answered what was asked of her and kept her eyes on the middle distance and thought about nothing at all, which was a skill too.

At the end, Ambrose tapped the topmost page and smiled at her with genuine warmth.

"Good. You'll bring the diver rolls tomorrow morning, first thing. All of them. Boat registrations, debt assignments, signatures. We'll want the full picture before we sail."

"Before you sail, sir?"

"When the tide serves. Flood season ends when it ends." He waved a hand at the brown water. "We'll run downriver ahead of the last of it, catch the coastal packet at Marsh Landing. So. Everything in order by tomorrow, and you'll have done your part."

She gathered the abstracts. At the door, Silas Vane spoke for the first time directly to her.

"How long have you kept the diver rolls?"

"Six years, sir. Since before my indenture was formalized."

Something crossed his face — not suspicion; assessment. "Then you'll know them better than anyone living. Good night."

* * *

The diver rolls lived in the archive room behind Brannigan's office, a locked closet of shelves that smelled of mildew and mouse. Wendell had the key because someone had to have the key and Brannigan considered archive dust beneath his dignity.

She meant only to put the rolls in order, as instructed. That was all. It was evening by then; Brannigan had gone home to his wife and his herring; the counting house below was dark, and the loft where she slept was quiet overhead, and the river ground against the pilings like something patient working at a knot.

She began at the current year and worked backward, as she'd done a hundred times, checking registrations against assignments. Boat *Salley Ann*, owned Tomas Iriarte's uncle, debt assigned, signature present. Boat *Fair Weather*, owned Rensalu Cruz, debt assigned, signature present. She moved down the shelf year by year, and the routine of it held her the way routine always had, safe and small and warm as a coat —

Until she reached the third volume back, and found her father.

Joaquim Almeida. Boat *Petrel*. Entry in her own fair hand, copied forward from the volume before it, and before that one, the record running back through the years like a rope through rings. She knew every line of it. She had entered half of those lines herself.

But the volume she held open was dated the year *after* his drowning, and there on the assignment page, in ink she did not remember, sat her father's signature.

Wendell did not move for a long time.

She knew his hand the way she knew her own face. The heavy downstroke on the J. The q that trailed off unfinished because he never lifted the pen cleanly. She had seen him sign the crew manifest a dozen times on the wharf, tongue between his teeth, patient with his own slowness. This signature was perfect. Too perfect. Her father had written like a man thinking in two languages, and whoever had written this had written it in one smooth motion, fluent in a dead man's name.

Dated March. He had drowned in February. She had stood on the mudflats in February, sixteen years old and already keeping books, and watched the company boat bring up his gear bag, and signed for it herself, itemized, in her own hand.

She set the volume down very carefully, as if it might go off.

Then she pulled the other volumes off the shelf, all of them, and spread them across the floor, and worked by lamplight with her heart going hard and strange. Diver by diver. Year by year. Every dead man's boat, assigned, signed. Cruz, who drowned off the shoal the same spring as her father. Old Man Tavares, dead of the lung sickness, his boat taken within the month. Signature upon signature upon signature, all of them written by the same steady fluent hand, none of them written by any hand that had ever held a pen alive.

Outside, the river rose and ground against the bridge, and the *Corvina* rode at anchor with its cousins aboard, its document cases packed, waiting for the tide that would carry everything — the fleet, the boats, the proof, the whole paper history of the town — downriver and out of the world.

Wendell sat back on her heels among the opened volumes, and the smallness she had made her peace with broke apart all at once, quiet as wet paper.

History, she understood now, had never happened elsewhere. It happened here, on this floor, in this ink. And her father had signed his name eight months after he died.

She reached for fresh paper, and began to copy.
