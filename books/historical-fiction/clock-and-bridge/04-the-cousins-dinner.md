## 4. The Cousins' Dinner

The invitation went out on Tuesday, written in Mr. Vane's own hand on cream stock: *Dinner aboard the *Coriander*, seven o'clock, for the gentlemen of the town.* Wendell read it three times before she understood that she was not invited but conscripted — a line at the bottom in Brannigan's blocky script: *Girl to serve. Full livery. Be punctual.*

Full livery meant the black dress with the company's pearl-button collar, the one sewn for her when she was fourteen and had since let out twice. It meant white gloves she would have to wash herself in the counting house basin afterward, because there was no one else to wash them. It meant standing for four hours behind six chairs while men who owed their fortunes to her arithmetic drank wine she could not pronounce and laughed at jokes she was not permitted to hear.

She pressed the dress with the flatiron until the creases held an edge like a ledger rule.

* * *

The *Coriander* rode at anchor a half-mile off the company wharf, a three-masted barque riding high and empty, waiting on cargo and tide both. A launch ferried the guests out in two trips — the harbor master, the customs man, two aldermen whose names appeared in the ledgers under *advances, personal, forgiven*. Wendell went out with the second trip, holding a basket of covered dishes against the river wind, watching the ship grow from a shadow into a wall of black timber and brass.

Everything aboard smelled of fresh paint and salt. The cabin they'd converted to a dining saloon still had sawdust in its corners; the Vanes had refitted it in a hurry, which told her something. Ships that plan to stay a long time are fitted slowly. Ships that mean to leave are made comfortable fast.

There were two cousins, though everyone said *the Vanes* as if they were one animal with two heads. Ambrose Vane was the elder — sixty, maybe, silver-bearded, with the patient voice of a man who had never once raised it because he'd never needed to. He ran the money. The younger, Cyrus, ran everything else: the boats, the divers, the wharf gangs, the fear. Cyrus was forty and built like a capstan, and he had a habit of looking at people the way he looked at cargo — estimating weight, guessing what the market would bear.

Wendell had served them before, at the annual supper in the counting house. Tonight was different. Tonight there were no divers' wives in the room, no town worth performing for. Only six guests who all already knew whatever the Vanes wanted known, and so the talk came loose and easy, the way talk does among men who believe the furniture cannot hear.

She poured the first wine and learned the first thing.

"The magistrate," the harbor master was saying, "arrives Thursday next. By river, they say. Old fellow. Hard of hearing, my mother says."

"Hard of hearing is useful," Cyrus said, "in a magistrate."

Ambrose lifted a hand — a small motion, barely a finger's width, and Cyrus subsided as neatly as a dog called to heel. "Mr. Ferro will find nothing here but honest trade and grateful labor," Ambrose said mildly. "We have nothing to hide."

He said it the way Wendell wrote *received in full* across a paid account: smoothly, finally, with the pen already moving past it.

* * *

The soup was oyster, brought up from the mudflats that morning. Wendell ladled it and kept her eyes down and her ears open, and discovered that serving was a kind of invisibility. Men did not lower their voices for servants. They lowered them for each other. She moved around the table's long circumference and caught the conversation in pieces, the way you catch fish through ice — one hole at a time.

"—the manifest lists machinery," the customs man said. "Crates marked *machinery* get less attention than crates marked—"

"Then we shall have machinery," Ambrose said. "A great deal of machinery. Forty-one crates of it."

Forty-one. Wendell set down the ladle without a tremble. She had spent Sunday copying the shipping schedule into the fair book. The *Coriander*'s hold was listed at thirty-eight crates, and every one of them contained pearl shell, dried fish, and company papers bound for a factor in a port whose name she'd had to look up twice. Three crates unaccounted for. Or rather — accounted for differently, depending on which paper a magistrate happened to see.

She carried plates. She filled glasses. Between the fish course and the meat, the talk turned to the bridge.

"Flood won't break for ten days yet," the harbor master said. "Maybe twelve. You'll be gone before the road opens."

"We sail on the morning tide," Cyrus said. "Day after tomorrow."

Wendell's hand stopped on the decanter. She resumed pouring before anyone could mark it, but inside her something had gone very loud and very quiet at once, the way the river goes before it rises.

Day after tomorrow. The magistrate came Thursday next. That left eight days between the truth leaving and the truth arriving, and the truth was going to be sitting in a hold full of crates marked *machinery*, halfway to a jurisdiction where no one in this town could reach it.

"Such haste," the harbor master said. "You'll miss the season's last pearls."

"Pearls keep," Ambrose said. "Paper keeps better, if it's kept in the right place." He smiled down the table at his guests, and it was a warm smile, a generous one, the smile of a man sharing a confidence he expected them to be too polite to examine. "A company's records are its soul, gentlemen. We take ours where they'll be safe. Fire, flood, litigation — a prudent man keeps his soul somewhere dry."

Litigation. He'd said it lightly, a word among words, but Wendell heard the weight in it. Somewhere in the last week, someone had asked questions. Someone official enough that Ambrose Vane had decided the counting house was no longer the safest place for his soul.

The inquiry. Her father's name on a contract dated after his drowning. She had thought she'd found a thread; it seemed instead she'd found the hem of something much larger, and the whole garment was being packed into crates while she stood behind a chair in borrowed gloves.

* * *

Between courses she was sent below to the galley for the sauce, and passing along the companionway she heard Cyrus's voice through a half-open door — not the table voice, the other one, low and flat.

"—Brannigan says the girl's been in the archive room. Twice this month."

"And?" Ambrose again, unhurried.

"And I say we put her ashore tonight and be done with it. She's a diver's brat. Nobody asks where diver brats go."

"Cyrus." One word, and Wendell heard the older man set down his glass. "We do not make martyrs over a clerk. If the girl has been reading, she has been reading paper. Paper proves nothing unless it is spoken aloud to the right ears, and the right ears leave on Thursday next — after we do." A pause. "Watch her. Note what she touches. Nothing more."

Wendell stood in the dark of the companionway with the sauce cooling in her hands and her heart going like a pump in a flooded hold. *If the girl has been reading.* They knew. Not everything — not the copies, not Sef, not the toll chest — but they knew she had been in the archive, and Brannigan had told them, which meant Brannigan had been watching her longer than she'd known.

She made herself walk back into the light. She served the sauce. She smiled the small blank smile of furniture, and all the while Cyrus Vane watched her across the candle flames with the expression of a man doing sums.

* * *

The dinner broke up near midnight. The launch took the guests back in relays, and Wendell stayed to clear the table because clearing it meant staying aboard, and staying aboard meant hearing the last of it — the talk men do when the guests are gone and the crew is dismissed.

She stacked plates in the pantry and listened through the bulkhead.

"Thursday's too late," Cyrus said. "I don't like it. I don't like any of it. Burn the duplicates tonight and let's be clean."

"Burn nothing." Ambrose's voice had the mildness of deep water. "Burned paper is evidence of paper. We take everything, Cyrus. Every page, every book, every scrap with a signature on it. Let the magistrate arrive to an empty office and a tidy story. There is nothing so innocent as a shelf with nothing on it."

"And the girl?"

"The girl is sixteen and indentured. Indentured girls do not testify; they scrub floors. But—" a pause, and Wendell heard ice move in a glass, "—if anything unfortunate should happen to delay us, we simply sail later. The tide decides, not us. Tides are very convenient that way."

Wendell finished stacking the plates. She thanked the cook, a fat, kind Dutchman who pressed a heel of bread into her hand for the row home. On deck, the night was black and the river breathed against the hull, and the anchor chain hung down into all that dark water like a question nobody aboard intended to answer.

She rowed back to the wharf alone, the bread in her lap, the current pulling at the oars. Behind her the *Coriander* sat at anchor with its lamps lit, patient as a spider. Ahead the town lay dark except for the counting house windows, where — even now, near one in the morning — a lamp still burned. Brannigan, working late. Or Brannigan, waiting up.

She tied off the boat and walked the length of the wharf with her heart in her throat, and the counting house door opened as she passed it, spilling yellow light across the boards.

"Late night," Brannigan said. He stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, suspenders hanging, a pen still tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil. His eyes went to the bread, the wet hem of her dress, her face. "Good service?"

"Adequate, sir."

"They tip the help aboard ships. Did they tip you?"

"No, sir."

"No," he said softly. "They wouldn't." He didn't move from the doorway. The lamplight made his shadow stretch all the way to her feet. "You were up there four hours, Miss Almeida. Four hours listening at elbows. I wonder what a clever girl hears at elbows."

"Soup orders, mostly, sir."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not belief, not quite suspicion either. Something more careful, the look of a man deciding how much of a thing to write down. "Mind your sums tomorrow," he said. "And mind where you walk. Flooded streets drown the careless." He stepped back inside and shut the door, and the lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Wendell stood in the dark on the wharf, the bread cold in her hands, and did the only arithmetic that mattered.

The Vanes sailed on the morning tide, day after tomorrow, carrying every record the company owned. The magistrate arrived eight days too late. And the one man positioned to notice what she touched had just told her, in the language men like him used, that he was watching.

Two tides. She had two tides to turn paper into testimony, and a town full of divers who hated her for the collar she wore, and an old bridge keeper who might or might not be brave, and a plan that existed so far only as a cold knot in her stomach.

She ate the bread walking home, because she did not know when she would eat next, and because her hands needed something to do besides shake.

At dawn she would go to the mudflats. She would knock on Tomas Iriarte's door, the angriest boy on the flats, the one whose family boat sat in the company yard with a forged bill of sale nailed to its mast — and she would ask him to trust the girl from the counting house.

It was, she thought, either the beginning of something or the end of her, and the tide would decide which.
