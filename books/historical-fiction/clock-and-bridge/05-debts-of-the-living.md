## 5. Debts of the Living

The Iriarte house leaned toward the river the way old men lean toward a fire. It was built on stilts of blackened mangrove, and the mud around it stank of low tide, and a girl was beating a rug against a rail with the rhythm of someone who expected the rug to fight back.

Tomas Iriarte was not the girl. Tomas was on the roof, re-thatching, and he did not come down when Wendell called his name. He came down when she said *your uncle's signature is a lie*.

He dropped from the eaves and landed in the mud in his bare feet, and he stood there looking at her the way you look at weather that has finally turned.

"You're the ledger girl," he said.

"I'm Wendell Almeida."

"I know who you are." He wiped his hands on his trousers, leaving mud on mud. "Your father dove with mine. Before."

"Before," she said, and let it sit.

He was taller than she remembered, thinner too, with rope burns across one palm that had healed badly. Sixteen like her, or near enough — but where her smallness had made her quiet, his had made him loud. She had seen him at company auctions shouting numbers back at the criers until Brannigan had him removed by the arms.

"I have something to show you," she said. "Not here. Somewhere with walls."

"My walls are fine."

"Your walls have ears. Your neighbors owe the company money. I don't know which of them reads Brannigan's letters for coin, and neither do you."

That got through. He looked up at the roofline, at the girl still beating the rug, and something in his jaw worked.

"The salt shed," he said. "The old one. Nobody uses it since the pilings went."

* * *

WENDELL

The salt shed smelled of rot and iodine, and light came through the gaps in the boards in thin bright blades that cut the dark into slices. Wendell laid the ledger page flat on an upturned barrel and weighted its corners with two shells and a rusted bolt.

Tomas read it the way illiterate men read — slowly, with his finger under each word, moving his lips. His uncle Joaquín had been dead four years. Drowned off the Cabeza shoals with a lung full of blood and a hold full of oysters nobody ever collected. The contract in front of Tomas was dated eleven months after that. At the bottom, in ink gone brown at the edges, was Joaquín Iriarte's signature: the looping J, the heavy cross-stroke, unmistakable to anyone who had seen the man sign a delivery chit a hundred times.

Except Joaquín had been in the river when this pen touched paper.

"That's his hand," Tomas said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"He signed over the *Salomé*. My mother's boat. My family's boat, three generations." His finger stayed on the name, pressing hard enough that Wendell worried for the paper. "Who wrote this?"

"Someone in the counting house. I don't know who. The ink matches company stock. The hand matches Brannigan's — look at the way the *r* hooks. But matching isn't proving. Anyone can copy a hand if they practice."

"Then what do we do?"

She took a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed all night, standing at her window watching the bridge lamps burn above the floodwater.

"We prove it properly," she said. "There's a magistrate coming by river within the week. We give him sworn testimony — yours, your mother's, mine. We show him the ledgers next to parish burial records next to delivery chits your uncle actually signed. We build it so heavy no bought man can lift it."

"Bought," Tomas repeated. "You think the magistrate's bought."

"I think everyone can be bought. I'm hoping truth weighs more than coin."

Tomas laughed once, a short ugly sound, and kicked the barrel, and the shells jumped and the page slid, and he caught it before it hit the floor with a diver's reflexes — fast hands, careful hands, hands made for holding things that die if you drop them.

"You know what I did when they took the *Salomé*?" he said. "I sat on the quay all night. In the rain. Because if I went home my mother would see I'd been crying and she'd start, and then we'd both drown right there on dry land." He set the page down gently, which somehow was worse than if he'd thrown it. "And you're telling me patience. Paper. A man who might already be paid."

"I'm telling you the difference between being right and being able to say so."

* * *

TOMAS

Here was the thing Tomas Iriarte knew about ledger girls: they were the soft hands of the same fist. His father had said so. Every debt note, every seizure order, every auction notice nailed to a mast came out of that counting house smelling of lamp oil and clean paper, and the people who kept those papers neat and dry never once got their lungs full of river.

So when Wendell Almeida stood in the salt shed with her careful page and her careful plan, some animal part of him wanted to put his fist through the barrel.

But another part of him — the part that had watched her catch the sliding page without thinking, the part that noticed she'd walked through the flats alone at dawn to come to *him*, the angriest boy, because apparently she'd decided anger was a tool worth picking up — that part was listening.

"They'll sail," she was saying. "The Vanes. Tomorrow morning's tide, maybe the next one. Everything goes aboard with them — the ledgers, the contracts, the proof. After that, the truth lives in another country, in English courts, in languages your mother doesn't speak. We get one window."

"And if the magistrate's bought? You said it yourself."

"Then we've lost nothing but time we didn't have anyway. But if he isn't — if even one officer of the court hears it spoken aloud, sworn, witnessed — then it exists somewhere the Vanes can't buy it back. Fire destroys paper, Tomas. It doesn't destroy testimony. Testimony lives in heads, and heads are harder to burn."

He stared at her. "You've thought about burning things."

"I've thought about everything. Burning the counting house keeps me awake." She said it flatly, without shame, and he felt something shift in his chest — respect, maybe, arriving sideways. "But here's what fire gets us: ash, and a town that starves faster because its only trade burned down, and the Vanes sailing away anyway with every original record safe aboard their ship. We'd be criminals *and* wrong. They'd write the history. Men like them always write the history."

"Then what does writing it get us?"

"A record. Copies. Sworn words from living mouths about dead men's forged names." She leaned forward over the barrel. "You want your uncle's boat back. Fine. But boats are things. What they took first was the story — they made it so Joaquín Iriarte *sold* the *Salomé*, so your family lost it fair, so any man who asks is told your uncle was a gambler or a drunk or a fool. They stole the truth and left you the shame. I want to steal the truth back."

Tomas was quiet a long moment. Out past the wall boards, the river muttered against the pilings.

"If we do it your way," he said slowly, "and it fails — they'll know who came to the shed. They'll know it was us."

"Yes."

"And you're a company girl. Indentured. They own your years."

"They owned my father's life," Wendell said. "They own a debt with his forged name on it too. I checked last night. Fourteen months after they pulled him out of the channel." Her voice didn't break. It just went very level, the way water goes level right before it goes over the edge. "So understand me, Tomas: I am not doing this as a company girl. I'm doing it as a debtor's daughter who happens to hold the pen."

* * *

WENDELL

They talked until the blades of light shifted westward. Terms, mostly — who would speak to which families, who would carry word to Old Sef at the bridge, how many copies could be made in one night with how many hands. Tomas knew the flats the way she knew the ledgers; between them they began to assemble a map of grievance, household by household, boat by stolen boat.

It was fragile. She could feel it, the old resentments running under everything like the river under the bridge. When he mentioned his mother, Tomas's voice hardened; when she mentioned the counting house, he flinched as if she'd named a jailer. Twice he accused her of going easy on Brannigan because she worked under him. Twice she accused him of wanting a fire more than a victory. Neither of them apologized. Neither of them left.

Somewhere in the third hour he said, "Why come to me first?"

Because your uncle's forgery is the cleanest, she almost said. Because your anger frightens people into moving. Instead she heard herself answer:

"Because when my father drowned, your father was one of the divers who went down looking for him. My mother told me. Four hours in the cold water, and Joaquín Iriarte was the last one up." She looked at her hands. "I never thanked anyone for that. There wasn't a form for it in the counting house, so I filed it nowhere. I'm filing it now."

Tomas didn't say anything for long enough that she thought she'd broken something. Then he nodded, once, the way divers nod before going under.

"All right, ledger girl," he said. "We build your paper mountain. But I want it understood—" and here he stepped close, and up close she could see he was afraid, properly afraid, and hiding it the way she hid hers, "—if your magistrate is bought, if this fails, if they come for my mother's house — I will burn something. Maybe not your counting house. Maybe something smaller and closer. So make it work."

"It'll work," Wendell said, because someone had to say it, and lying felt less dangerous than doubting.

* * *

TOMAS

Walking her back along the ridge path above the flats, Tomas counted boats in the company yard out of habit — the *Salomé* at her mooring among the seized, masts bare, hulls going soft. Fourteen of them now. Fourteen families. He tried to imagine fourteen sworn statements lined up like pages in one of her ledgers, and found he couldn't yet, but could almost.

Halfway down the path Wendell stopped walking.

"Brannigan's shutters," she said.

Tomas followed her gaze. Across the water, in the counting house windows on the second floor, the shutters stood open and lamplight moved behind them — lamplight moving in the wrong rhythm, back and forth, a man pacing.

"He's working late," Wendell murmured. "He hasn't worked late since the Vanes arrived. Unless—"

The lamp went out. One heartbeat. Two. Then a shutter closed, quick and quiet, like an eye deciding it had seen enough.

"—unless he's watching the flats," she finished.

They stood very still on the path, two silhouettes against the dusk, and Tomas felt the fragile thing they'd built that day test its own weight and hold — barely.

"Tomorrow night," Wendell said quietly. "We start copying tomorrow night. And Tomas — whatever you see, whatever moves out there after dark — you come to the bridge and tell Sef first. Not me. Sef."

"Why Sef?"

She was already moving down the path, fast, coat snapping in the river wind.

"Because tomorrow morning," she said, "I'm going to find out whose side the bridge keeper is on."
