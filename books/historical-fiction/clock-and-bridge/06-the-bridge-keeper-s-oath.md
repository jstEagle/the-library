## 6. The Bridge Keeper's Oath

The trouble with keeping a bridge is that you see everyone who crosses, and no one crosses back to see you.

Sef had held the toll house for thirty-one years. He knew the sound of every foot that struck the planks — the quick patter of the fishwives at dawn, the heavy tread of company men with their boots heeled hard like they were stamping the river into obedience, the soft shuffle of divers coming up from the mudflats to sell what they'd bled for. He'd heard Wendell Almeida's steps a thousand times. Light ones. Careful ones. A girl who walked like she was apologizing to the boards.

Tonight she had crossed at dusk going toward town, and now, near nine bells, she was coming back. But she wasn't walking past the toll window. She was standing in it.

"You're still awake," she said.

"I'm always awake." It was mostly true. Sleep came to him these days in shallow pulls, like a man surfacing for air and diving again. "You look like a girl carrying something heavier than books."

She didn't smile. She set her satchel on the toll ledge, and her hands were trembling in a way she clearly thought she was hiding. "Mr. Sef. When my father's boat was sold — after the drowning. Did you hear anything? Anything at all?"

The river moved beneath them, fat and brown with the flood, dragging whole trees along like straws. Sef looked at it a long moment.

"Come inside," he said.

* * *

He poured her tea he was ashamed of — the leaves used twice already — and sat her on the stool by the stove while he took the chair, because his knees had opinions about the floor. The toll house was one room and a loft, crowded with thirty-one years of small necessary things: coil of tarred rope, signal flags gone stiff with salt, a tin of biscuits he kept for children who cried at the toll. He had never charged a crying child. That was one thing. He clung to it.

"You asked if I heard anything," Sef said. "I'll tell you what I saw instead. Seeing is worse."

Wendell waited. She had her father's patience, he thought. The patience of a man who spent hours under water waiting for oysters to be where they were going to be anyway.

"Six years ago," Sef said. "Winter. The river was down, low as I've seen it. Your father was alive then — you know this part, you lived it. What you don't know is what happened in the counting house three days before the *Peregrine* went out."

Her breath caught. "Before?"

"Before. I'd been called over to witness a document. Old habit — Vane liked a bridge keeper at the witnessing table, made the paper look honest. A man of the town, a public face. There were four signatures wanted that evening. Three divers came themselves, signed their debt renewals, drank the company's rum, and left happy enough. The fourth name was Almeida."

The stove ticked. Downriver, somewhere, a ship's bell rang once.

"He wasn't there," Wendell said quietly.

"No."

"But his name was on the page."

"There was a page," Sef said, "and there was a pen, and Brannigan held the pen, and Vane the elder stood at his shoulder saying, *just so, just so* — guiding the hand like a man steering a skiff. And I stood in the corner holding my hat and watching a dead man's boat change owners before its owner was dead. Because that was the plan, girl. You understand? The signature wasn't stealing from your father. It was *preparing* him. They sign the paper first, then the river does the rest, and afterward everything is tidy. Boat to the company, widow to charity, questions to nobody."

Wendell's face had gone very white, but her voice stayed level, and that frightened Sef more than weeping would have. "Why did you say nothing?"

There it was. The question he'd been turning over for six years like a stone in the pocket, worn smooth.

"Because they paid me," he said. "Not much. Enough. Thirty shillings a quarter, marked in the company ledger as *bridge maintenance supplement*, and it was more than the bridge itself earned me, and my wife was sick that winter and the winters after, and medicine is not free anywhere in this world. And because the first time is impossible and every time after is easy. You watch a man's hand being guided once, and you think: next quarter I'll speak. Then the next. Then your wife dies and the speaking has curdled into confessing, and confessing means losing everything you traded for the silence, all of it wasted, and an old man will do remarkable arithmetic to avoid finding out his life was wasted." He looked at his own hands, spotted and rope-scarred. "That's the whole of it. No villain in it. Just a coward with a pension."

For a long moment Wendell said nothing. Then: "You could have said no."

"Yes," Sef said. "That's exactly the size of it. I could have said no."

* * *

He slept, or half slept, and dreamed of the river at low water, showing all its bones — the pilings, the drowned crates, the shape of a hull he recognized — and woke before dawn with his mind fixed like a knot pulled tight.

He dressed in his good coat, the one from his wife's funeral, and took the key off the hook behind the signal flags. It was a small ugly key, brass gone green, and it had hung there untouched for four years, and every morning he had seen it and every morning it had accused him.

When Wendell crossed at first light, he was waiting at the window.

"Where are you going?" she asked, though she must have seen the good coat.

"With you. As far as the flats, then further if they'll have me." He set the key on the ledge between them. "Company archive room. Back of the counting house, past the strong room. Every contract, every renewal, every bill of sale since Marrow & Vane opened its doors. Brannigan thinks I don't know it exists because I'm old. Being old is mostly being underestimated; I've made a career of it."

Wendell stared at the key. "How do you have this?"

"Cut it myself, twenty years ago, when Vane the elder decided the bridge keeper ought to have a spare to 'the storeroom' and gave me one to the wrong door by accident. I kept it. I told myself I kept it out of prudence." His mouth twisted. "I told myself a great many things."

"They'll know it was you."

"They'll know someone opened that door, yes. And when they ask who, I intend to be standing somewhere signing my name to it. In front of witnesses. My own hand, my true name, Sebastian Ferro, bridge keeper, thirty-one years' service." He drew a folded paper from inside his coat — he had written it at four in the morning, fair copy, the rough draft burned in the stove. "My statement. Everything I saw that night six years ago, everything since. Dated, sworn format, room for a magistrate's seal. I've never given testimony in my life. I'd hoped never to need to."

Wendell took it carefully, the way she took everything, as though paper could bruise. She read it through once, fast, then again slow, and when she looked up her eyes were bright and hard at once.

"This says you watched the forgery of my father's signature."

"It says more than that. It names the other two nights. The Corvo boy's contract, the year before the storm. Iriarte's brother-in-law." He counted them off on fingers gone stiff. "Fourteen names, girl. Fourteen boats. I stood in that corner fourteen times wearing my hat."

"And you're giving this to me."

"I'm putting it *on the record*. There's a difference and your whole future depends on understanding it." He leaned forward, knees cracking. "Listen. Paper obeys whoever holds it — I told you that before and I meant it as a warning. But testimony spoken before a magistrate, witnessed, sealed — that's the one kind of paper even money can't easily unmake. The Vanes can burn ledgers. They cannot burn a magistrate's ears."

"The magistrate may be bought."

"He may. But bought men still hate surprises, and we are going to surprise him with fourteen names and a living witness who watched the ink dry." Sef stood, slowly, and took his hat off the peg — his church hat, his funeral hat, the only respectable thing he owned besides the truth. "I was silent when silence was worth thirty shillings a quarter. I find the price has changed."

They went out together onto the bridge. The flood ran beneath, high and furious, and downstream the Vane ship rode at anchor, masts swaying against a grey sky, black smoke already curling from her funnel — getting up steam, days early, as if impatient.

Sef stopped mid-span, at the exact center, where he had stood ten thousand mornings, and looked down at the water that had taken Almeida and might yet take everything else.

"When I cross this bridge tonight," he said, "I'll either be an honest man or a ruined one. Thirty-one years I've been neither." He settled the hat on his head. "Come along, girl. Bring your pen. We've got copying to do, and I suspect we'll want more hands than yours."

Behind them, at the far end of the span, Brannigan's office lamp flickered on — early, too early — and a silhouette bent over a desk, sorting papers into a leather case.
