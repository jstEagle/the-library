## 7. Night Copying

We met at the foot of the bridge stairs at half past eleven, when the counting house lamps went from two to one.

Tomas came first, hood up, moving like a man walking into weather that hadn't arrived yet. Behind him, the two he'd sworn to trust: his cousin Rosa Iriarte, who mended nets and could copy a knot pattern after one look, and a boy called Fenn Halloway, sixteen like me, whose father's boat the company had taken three years ago on a signature nobody had ever seen him make. Fenn didn't say anything the whole climb. His hands shook, and I decided not to look at them again, because if I kept looking I would start shaking too.

Old Sef was waiting in the toll house with the archive key in his palm, held out flat, as if it were something that might bite.

"Third door past the strongroom," he said. "The lock wants you to lift before you turn. And girl—" he caught my sleeve, "—Brannigan keeps irregular hours this week. He's been working the shipping manifests till all hours. If he stays past midnight, you'll have light under his door and no way out but the window ledge."

"The window ledge," Tomas said, "is four inches wide."

"Then don't be heavy-footed." Sef closed my fingers over the key. His hand was cold as river stone. Whatever he had carried for nine years, he was carrying it up those stairs with us tonight, whether or not his legs made the climb.

* * *

The archive room smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old paper going brittle, which until that night I had loved without complication. We worked by one shielded lamp, its shade angled toward the wall, and we set ourselves in a row along the long table like children kept late for punishment: Rosa copying names and dates, Fenn copying sums, Tomas cutting quills because his handwriting was the worst of ours and he knew it. I took the contracts themselves — the pages where the dead had signed.

That is a strange thing to write, and stranger to have done. To sit beneath a lamp with your father's name an inch from your thumb, knowing his hand never touched that ink, knowing the man who held the pen is upstairs somewhere now, adding columns by his own small flame.

I copied the date first. Every time. Date, then name, then the mark of the witness — always Brannigan's initials, J.B., neat as stitching — then the sum, then the clause transferring title of the boat to Marrow & Vane. After the fourth page I stopped reading them as documents. They became a litany. *Aurelio Almeida, drowned March '94, signed June '94. Mateo Iriarte, lost with his net, signed the following spring. Old Paulo, lungs full of river, signed twice, once alive and once after.* That last one nearly broke me. A man so poor in death the company made him sell his boat twice.

Below us, through the floorboards, we could hear Brannigan: the scrape of his chair, the clink of the inkwell when he refilled it, once a long cough that went through the whole building like a fish bone caught sideways. Every sound froze us. When his footsteps crossed toward the stairs, Rosa pinched out the lamp and we stood in total dark, four people breathing through their mouths, while he climbed halfway up, stopped at the landing, and went back down. I learned something about fear that night: it doesn't fade. It just changes shape. First you're afraid of being caught. Then you're afraid of being afraid, because terror makes noise, and noise is the only thing that can undo you.

Somewhere past two o'clock, Fenn spoke for the first time.

"This is my father's." He held a page up to the lamplight without copying it yet. "This is the one."

"Then do it slow," Tomas said. "Slowest of all."

Fenn nodded and bent over the paper, and I watched a boy copy his own grief letter by letter, and understood that what we were building wasn't evidence. Evidence is for magistrates. This was something else — proof that could be held, that could travel, that existed outside the company's walls for the first time since the company had built walls. The truth, living in more than one pair of hands. Sef had said it best, the night before, standing at his bridge rail: *Paper obeys whoever holds it. So hold more of it than they do.*

* * *

Brannigan left at ten past three. We heard the bolt, the door, his boots diminishing down the wet street — and then a worse silence, because silence meant nothing between us and the dark but our own nerve. Rosa laughed once, high and shocked, and clapped her hand over her mouth. Tomas blew the tension out through his lips like breath on a ember.

We finished as the window went grey. Forty-one pages. I checked each against the original twice, then had Rosa check me, because exhaustion lies and a lie in these pages would hang us all. My wrist ached in a way I would feel for a week. The copies went inside Tomas's shirt, wrapped in oilcloth; the originals — the true forged pages themselves — I took, because it fell to me, and because I had asked Sef for the hiding place and he had given it with a strange look, half warning, half blessing.

The toll chest sat under the toll house floor, a strongbox older than the bridge, where decades of crossing-fees had slept before being carried to the bank. Sef's trick was simple: a false bottom above the coin drawer, deep enough for a sheaf of papers, shallow enough that anyone lifting coins would never think to look higher. We knelt on the cold boards while I fitted the pages in, and I want to record that my hands were steady, because they were — steadier than they had been all night. There is a particular calm that comes from having passed the point where turning back is possible. You stop being afraid of falling when you are already in the air.

Sef lowered the lid himself and turned the key.

"Dawn in an hour," he said. "Go home by the water path. All of you. Sleep if sleep will come."

It wouldn't, for me. I walked home along the mudflats as the sky went from charcoal to slate to the color of unbleached linen, and the river ran high and brown and fast beneath a world that did not know what it was holding. The Vanes' ship rode at anchor downstream, lamps burning at her rail, waiting on the morning tide. Waiting to carry every secret I had spent the night unmaking.

I thought of my mother asleep in her bed, of my father somewhere under that same brown water, of forty-one pages in a boy's shirt and a dead man's forged name locked under a bridge floor. I felt terrified and luminous at once, as though I had swallowed a lamp.

At my door, Tomas caught up to me, breathing hard.

"Wendell." He glanced back the way he'd come. "The ship. She's taking on water and stores tonight — I saw lights, heard the boats going out. They're loading for dawn. Not tomorrow's tide. This one."

The east was going pale behind him. Downriver, faint on the still air, came the rattle of a derrick and the cry of men working cargo in the dark.

They had found something missing. Or they were simply leaving early, ahead of rumor, ahead of everything. Either way, the tide would turn within hours, and with it, if I did nothing, the last original proof in the world would sail out of reach of any magistrate who ever set foot here.

I looked at the river, and the river looked back, patient as a ledger.
