## 8. Fire on the Water

I never trusted paper. Paper was what took our boat — a page with my uncle's name on it, a stamp, a clerk's dry voice saying *the company holds title now*. So when Wendell Almeida put the copied pages in my hands and said, *this is how we fight*, I held them like something that might bite.

She'd been right about one thing: fire would have felt good for exactly one night. Then the Vanes would own the ashes too.

So we copied instead. All night in the archive room while Brannigan scratched at his desk below, and I learned what it costs to write quietly. By dawn my hand ached worse than after a day on the lines. We finished as the light went grey over the water, and Wendell locked the originals in the toll chest under the bridge house, and I thought — foolishly, I see now — that the hard part was done.

The hard part had only just noticed us.

* * *

They came an hour after sundown the next day. I was at the mudflats mending nets when Old Sef's boy ran the whole way from the bridge, shouting my name before he even reached me.

"Men at the counting house. Four of them. They went up the stairs to the archive."

Wendell.

I didn't think. I ran. The flooded streets turned me east and west and east again, and all the while the river roared under everything like a crowd waiting to be told who to hate. The Vane cousins' ship sat at anchor off the quay, lamps lit along her rail like a string of watching eyes.

Brannigan stood at the counting house door when I got there, and behind him two men I knew by sight — Vane men, dock enforcers, the kind whose wages are paid in silence. Brannigan's face had gone past anger into something colder. He held a page in his hand. One of ours. A copy, ink still fresh enough to smear.

"The girl," he said. "Where is she?"

"I don't know what you're talking about."

He looked at me the way you look at a knot you intend to cut. "Your family owes us eleven years, Iriarte. Shall I add to it? Interest accrues nightly."

That's the thing about debt. It isn't just money. It's a leash they keep coiled even when they're not holding it.

"Wendell Almeida," Brannigan said, to the enforcers, not to me. "Find her. She'll be at the bridge or the flats or the old woman Corvo's. Bring her and bring whatever she's carrying. Gently if possible." A pause. "It hardly matters anymore."

They went. I stood in the doorway hating him, and he let me stand there, because letting me hate him was its own small humiliation, and men like Brannigan collect those too.

Then I did the only smart thing I did that night. I ran for the bridge ahead of them.

* * *

Sef was already awake. He was always awake; I think the man slept in pieces, like a watch dog. When I came pounding up the toll house steps he had his lamp lit and his coat half on, and I told it to him fast — the copies found, the men out looking, Wendell in danger.

He listened the way old men listen, which is slowly, as if translating.

"They'll burn whatever she copied," I said. "If they catch her—"

"They won't burn it." Sef picked up his keys from the nail by the door. His hands were steady. "Burned paper is easy to explain. A girl who disappears is not. They'll want her quiet, not gone. There's a difference, and it matters to her." He looked at me. "You run to the flats. Get her mother, get the divers, get every soul who signed nothing and knows it. Bring them to the magistrate's landing at first light. Witnesses are the one thing Vane money can't unmake."

"And you?"

"I mind my post." He said it simply, the way he'd said it thirty years. "The toll chest stays with me."

I want to tell you I argued. I want to tell you I understood what he meant. I understood neither. I was nineteen and the night was full of running and I left an old man standing in his lamp light beside a chest full of the only true words this town had ever owned.

* * *

I found Wendell at her mother's, already packing pages into oilcloth, already three moves ahead of everyone. Her mother watched us from the doorway with the flat calm of a woman who has drowned once already and refuses to do it again.

"They found a copy," I told her. "Brannigan has men out. They know it's you."

She didn't panic. That frightened me more than panic would have. She tied the oilcloth with a sailor's knot and said, "Then we go to Sef. The originals can't stay under the toll house now — he needs to move them, and we need to be gone before first light. The magistrate's barge—"

"Magistrate's landing. I know. Sef said witnesses."

Something crossed her face then — not fear. Recognition, almost. As if a ledger line had finally balanced and she wished it hadn't. "Sef stayed at the bridge," she said.

"He said it was his post."

"Yes," she said quietly. "That's what he would say."

We went together through the flooded lanes, and the rain started somewhere above the town and came down sideways off the river, and by the time we saw the bridge through it, there was light where no light should have been.

Not lamplight. Not lantern light.

Firelight — under the toll house eaves, orange against the black water, growing.

* * *

Here is what I pieced together afterward, in the weeks when piecing things together was all any of us could do. The enforcers found Wendell's trail and followed it wrong, toward the flats, and doubled back past the bridge. There was a scuffle at the toll house — Sef's boy swore to it later, hiding in the pilings. One of the Vane men carried a lantern because the rain had stopped for an hour, because the night wanted this, because small carelessness is all catastrophe ever needs. A shove. A swing. The lantern went into the wall of the toll house, into forty years of dry timber and oiled canvas and lamp stores.

Whether they meant it, I cannot say. Whether it mattered, I cannot say either. What I can tell you is what I saw.

The toll house burning like a struck match. Sef coming out of the smoke with the toll chest on his shoulder, an old man carrying a weight built to take two dockhands, walking down the burning stairs one step at a time because there was no other way down. The chest going into the water at the end of a rope, splashing once, sinking toward the mud where we could find it again.

And Sef turning back. Turning back for the second thing he would not leave — I never learned what it was, whether papers, whether his oath book, whether only the habit of a man who saves what is his — and the stair giving way beneath him with a sound like a bone breaking.

The fire didn't kill him quickly, and I won't describe it. Some things belong to him alone. What belongs to the rest of us is this: he held his post.

The flames got into the timbers of the bridge itself, and then the bridge burned above the flood — the only road out of this town, the span every fortune here hangs on, going up plank by plank while the river rose beneath it and could do nothing, being made of the wrong element entirely. People came out of their houses to watch their livelihood crackle. Nobody shouted. It was too big for shouting. The whole town stood in the rain-rot dark and watched the fire eat the crossing, and somewhere in that crowd, I knew, the Vane cousins were watching too, from their lit deck, deciding what version of this story they would tell.

Wendell grabbed my arm so hard it left marks. "The chest," she said. "Tomas. The chest went in the water."

"We'll get it."

"It has to be us. Before them." And then, absurdly, horribly, she laughed — one short breath of a laugh with no joy in it anywhere. "Paper obeys whoever holds it. He told me that. He was right about everything."

* * *

They arrested us at the waterline.

Four divers and a ledger girl hauling a dripping chest out of the shallows by lantern light while a bridge burned behind us — it made a picture, and pictures make prisoners. The harbor watch came with boat hooks and chains and the particular energy of men relieved to finally arrest someone who wasn't paying their wages.

Wendell didn't fight. I did, briefly, and earned a split lip for it. She spoke to the watch captain in a voice I'd only heard her use over ledgers — level, precise, unbribable.

"The chest contains sworn statements and original company records. Forged signatures. If anything happens to it, or to us, the magistrate arrives next week to a town full of living witnesses and nothing else to look at but ash. Write that down, captain. In your report. Word for word."

He wrote it down. Men like order, even when order is aimed at them.

They took the chest between us — evidence now, which meant it had to exist, which meant it was safe in the exact way a hostage is safe. They took Wendell, and then, because she would not give them any reason to separate us and every reason to include us, they took me, and old Corvo, and the Ferro brothers. A cell under the quay, stone and damp, the smell of the river coming through the walls like a reminder.

Through the high barred window we could see the bridge still burning. Embers went out over the water and died, and the ship at anchor sat motionless among them, patient as a spider.

"They'll say we set it," I said. My voice sounded younger than I wanted it to. "The fire. They'll swear to it. They'll hang it on us and sail on the morning tide and—"

"Tides need channels," Wendell said. She was sitting with her back straight against the stone, eyes closed, doing sums in her head — I could tell by her lips. "And we just watched half a bridge fall into theirs. Let them try to sail over their own bonfire."

"You don't know that stops them."

"No," she said. "But it slows them. And slow is all we've ever needed." She opened her eyes and looked at the ember-light moving on the ceiling. "Sef gave his statement before all this. Sworn. It's in the chest with everything else. He knew, Tomas. He's known since the first forgery, and tonight he decided." Her voice caught, just once, and repaired itself. "Old men decide late. But he decided."

In the dark of that cell, with the bridge dying above us and the tide turning somewhere out beyond the point, Wendell Almeida reached through the bars of my sleeve and gripped my wrist — the diver's grip, the grip you use to tell someone underwater that you have them and you will not let go.

"When the magistrate comes tomorrow," she said, "everything depends on what we can prove and who we can prove it against. So sleep if you can. Because starting at sunrise, I am going to talk until someone official writes it all down."

Above us, the last span groaned, shifted, and fell into the river, and the sound rolled through the stones of our cell like a bell counting us toward morning.
