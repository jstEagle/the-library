## 9. The Magistrate's Morning

They gave me a cell with a window, which I took at first for mercy and later understood as strategy. A girl who can see the river will spend all her hours watching it, and a girl watching the river is not talking to anyone.

So I watched. Through the bars and the frost-furred glass I saw the *Resolute Adelia* riding at anchor, lamps burning on her quarterdeck all night like a house that refuses to sleep. I saw the smoke of the toll house still rising at dawn, thin and gray, the way a body smokes after the fire has taken everything worth taking. And I saw the debris — God, the debris. The bridge had come down into the channel in burning sections, and now charred beams and twisted chain rode the flood tide in a slow orbit around the anchorage, knocking against hulls, snagging mooring lines, drifting out and drifting back as if the river itself had decided to keep a piece of everything Marrow & Vane owned.

The channel was fouled. Any pilot could see it. The ship could not sail until the wreckage was cleared or the tide made some new arrangement of it, and neither of those things would happen by morning.

I did not know yet whether that was luck or Sef's last joke.

* * *

They came for me at eight o'clock: Brannigan and a company man I didn't know, with a set of keys and a face like a closed ledger. Brannigan wouldn't look at me. He'd worked late below us while we copied pages overhead; he'd frozen by his own lamp the same night we froze by ours. I wondered what he thought about when he thought about that.

"Magistrate Corr arrives on the morning packet," the stranger said, to Brannigan rather than to me. "She'll be brought before him."

"I'm right here," I said. "You can say *she'll hear*."

Neither of them smiled. The cell door opened and I walked out between them, past the desk where the night sergeant sat very carefully not reading anything, down the steps, into the front room of the jailhouse, which someone had swept and furnished overnight with a table, three chairs, and a basin of water gone cloudy. Someone had also put a pitcher of water and one cup on the table. Three chairs, one cup. I understood the arithmetic of that arrangement before anyone explained it to me.

Magistrate Corr arrived an hour later, and he looked exactly like what he was: a tired man of fifty-odd who had traveled two days by river in November to find a burned bridge, a drowned channel, a trading company in a state of barely managed panic, and a sixteen-year-old girl in jail. He wore a coat too heavy for the season and carried his own bag, which told me something. Bought men don't carry their own bags. Bought men have people for that.

"You are Wendell Almeida," he said. Not a question.

"Yes, sir."

"You are indentured to Marrow & Vane Trading Company." He unfolded a paper. "The company charges you with theft of documents, conspiracy, and — this is the part they're proudest of — incitement to arson. They've submitted a statement of loss exceeding four thousand pounds."

"That last one is convenient for them," I said. "Since their toll house burned the same night they discovered their ledgers had been read."

Brannigan, standing by the door, made a small sound like a man swallowing his own name.

Corr looked at me for a long moment. Then he moved the single cup aside and laid both hands flat on the table. "Miss Almeida. I will tell you how this looks from where I sit, because you strike me as someone who prefers to know her position. A bridge burns. Hours before, documents go missing from the company whose bridge it was. The company says the girl stole papers and burned the bridge to cover it. The girl says—" He gestured at me, palm up. "What does the girl say?"

"The girl says the company bought dead men's boats with dead men's signatures." I heard my own voice come out steady and was grateful to it. "Three years of ledgers, sir. Every diver who drowned, his boat reappears on the company's rolls within the month, transferred by contract, signed in his hand. My father has been signing contracts for four years, sir. He's been at the bottom of the river the whole time."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a beam knocked against a hull, patient as a clock.

"Where are these ledgers?" Corr said.

"The originals are somewhere the Vanes haven't found them. I won't say where. There are copies in other hands besides mine. If anything happens to me, the copies speak. That isn't a threat, Magistrate. It's just bookkeeping."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. "You understand your situation. You're charged. The company holds your indenture, which means until this is resolved they decide where you sleep and what you eat and whether—"

"My indenture is void," I said. "Or it will be. That's my price."

Brannigan stepped forward. "You're in no position to set prices—"

"Then let me be clear about the position." I turned to Corr and folded my hands the way the Vane cousins folded theirs at dinner, and I watched the gesture land. "You came here expecting to weigh one girl against a company. Here is the actual scale. On one side: money, and a ship, and two owners who will be at sea by Thursday with every record of what they did locked in their cabin. On the other side: sworn testimony from me, from Tomas Iriarte, from Old Sef's statements given before he died carrying your evidence out of a fire — yes, we know about the toll chest, Magistrate, the whole mudflat knows — plus original documents bearing forgeries so crude a clerk's first-year apprentice could prove them. Words against money, spoken aloud on your record, in your proceeding. Or silence, and a cleared channel, and a ship gone by Thursday. Those are the weights. You do the arithmetic. You came here with your own bag; I assume you do your own sums."

The cloudy water in the basin trembled faintly. Outside, the packet boat was coming upriver, slow and black against the grey water.

Corr sat back. He picked up the single cup, considered it, and set it down again. "Bring in the company's counsel," he said. "And send someone to the harbor master. Nobody clears that channel without my order. Nothing sails."

* * *

They argued for two hours. It was remarkable to watch, once I stopped being afraid of it. Mr. Ambrose Vane arrived with his brother Rufus, and the brothers performed outrage the way musicians perform a piece they've played a hundred times — technically flawless, emotionally vacant. Theft! Sedition! A servant girl poisoning the well of commerce! Ambrose's voice climbed and climbed, and Corr let him climb, writing nothing, nodding at intervals, until Ambrose ran out of altitude and stood there breathing.

"Mr. Vane," Corr said mildly, "your ship is trapped in your own debris. Your channel is closed by my order. Your records are subject to seizure under the inquiry statute, which applies to any commercial concern operating a public toll — and your bridge, I note, being burned, cannot currently collect tolls, which rather weakens your claim to be operating one. Sit down, sir."

Rufus sat. Ambrose remained standing a moment longer, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I had done, and arrive at the same sum, and hate it.

The bargain, when it finally settled onto the table like a bird too exhausted to fly elsewhere, was this: my testimony and whatever originals I produced, given openly, entered into the record, in exchange for charges against me and the Iriarte boy held in abeyance pending the inquiry's outcome. The forgery counts against the company would be heard first, on Corr's insistence, because — he said, looking directly at Ambrose Vane — "a court must establish the character of the parties before it weighs their complaints."

"We want the originals *before* she testifies," Rufus said.

"No," I said, at the same moment Corr said, "Absolutely not," and for once the magistrate and I were in perfect agreement.

It was while they were drafting the order that the harbor master's runner burst in, hatless, dripping, and delivered the news sideways, the way bad news travels — addressed to no one, meant for everyone:

"She's trying to move, sir. The *Adelia*. Warping off the anchorage on her own cable, half the crew at the capstan, and there's a beam across her bow the size of a church."

Everyone stood at once. Corr said, "Can she make it?"

The runner shook his head. "Pilot says no, not on this tide, not with the wreckage drifting. But sir — she's trying."

I looked at the window. Down on the grey river, the great ship had come alive against all sense, hauling herself toward open water by brute force, chains groaning, her wake pushing burnt planks aside in a bow wave of ruin. For one absurd moment she seemed to be making way. Then a charred section of decking, still held together by its iron straps, swung slowly across her hawse and lay there like a hand closing.

The capstan stopped. The ship swung back with the tide, lamps lit at midday, going nowhere.

"They knew," Tomas said beside me. He'd been brought in an hour ago, still smelling of river mud, and had heard everything in silence. "The runners told them the magistrate ordered the channel closed. They tried to run *before* the order reached the harbor. That's guilt, isn't it? Trying like that? You can write that down?"

Corr, at the window, watching the stranded ship, said quietly: "Mr. Iriarte, it can be written down that the company attempted to depart while under inquiry, destroying nothing and obeying no order, because no order had yet reached them — a distinction lawyers will enjoy for years. But juries are not lawyers." He turned from the window. "Miss Almeida. Your testimony begins tomorrow at nine o'clock. I suggest you spend tonight deciding which parts of the truth you intend to tell, because tomorrow you will tell all of it or none of it has value."

* * *

They took me back to the cell with the window, and by unspoken agreement nobody barred the door hard.

Evening came down on the water. The *Adelia* rode her anchor with her sails crossed and furled, and the debris circled her, patient, keeping its hold. I thought about my father's signature sitting in a ledger somewhere below the Vane cabin, four years of a dead man's handwriting, and I thought about the toll chest under what remained of the stair, holding the true pages, holding Sef's oath in his own careful script — *I saw it done and I said nothing, and hereafter I say otherwise.*

Tomorrow I would stand in a room with one cup and three chairs and speak my father's name out loud on the record, and the record, unlike a ledger, unlike a bridge, unlike a promise made to a drowning man, cannot burn.

Except that at dusk, when the lamplighter's ladder scraped against the jailhouse wall, a different sound came up from the harbor: hammering. Persistent, rhythmic, industrial. I pressed to the bars and looked down and saw lanterns strung along the *Adelia*'s deck and her boats lowered into the water, and men — dozens of them, company men, working through the dark — dragging the wreckage of the bridge toward the shore.

Clearing the channel. Working through the night, against tide and cold and sense.

Not to flee.

To leave before the inquiry ended — legally, openly, with cargo aboard.

With my father's forged signatures sealed in the captain's strongbox, sailing for a country where no magistrate of ours had any power at all.
