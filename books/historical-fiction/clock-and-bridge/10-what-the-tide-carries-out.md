## 10. What the Tide Carries Out

The ship left on a Tuesday, on the last of the ebb, and half the town stood on the mudflats to watch it go.

The *Corvane* sat high in the water — higher than she had any right to, three weeks out from port and carrying nothing but her ballast and her shame. The magistrate's men had gone through her hold for two days, hauling crates into the light while the Vane cousins stood on their own deck like passengers at someone else's funeral. Whatever they'd meant to bury abroad stayed in Port Merrow now, stacked in evidence under a tarpaulin that leaked when it rained. Wendell knew because she'd counted the crates herself. Old habits. The ledgers were in her blood whether she wanted them or not.

She stood apart from the crowd, near the waterline where the mud took her boots to the ankle, and watched the cousins avoid each other's eyes across the width of their own quarterdeck. Aldous Vane had shaved for the occasion. That was the detail she kept: he had shaved, and put on his good coat, and stood very straight, as if posture were a kind of innocence a man could wear out of port. His cousin Roderick did not come up from below until the anchor was up. When he did, he did not look at the town at all.

No one cheered. That surprised her. She had thought — she had spent three weeks in a cell thinking — that there would be shouting, maybe stones. But the diver families just stood with their arms crossed, and the shopkeepers stood beside them, and the silence went out over the water like something thrown, and the *Corvane* caught the tide and leaned into it and was smaller, and smaller, and then a shape, and then not.

"They'll be back," Tomas said. He had come to stand beside her without her noticing; he moved quiet for a big man still growing into himself. "Investigations end. Money waits."

"Then we write faster," Wendell said.

He looked at her sideways. He'd been doing that since the cell door opened — looking at her as if recalculating something, a ledger entry he'd gotten wrong. Ledger-keeper, ally, arson suspect, witness. She supposed she was hard to categorize. She was having trouble doing it herself.

* * *

Her indenture died by paperwork, which felt correct. All those years bound by ink, unbound by ink.

Magistrate Corren did it in the customs house with the shutters open, reading the articles aloud in a dry voice while Brannigan stood against the wall with his hat in his hands. Brannigan had turned Crown's evidence within a day of Sef's statement entering the record — turned fast and thoroughly, naming every order he'd ever carried out, every signature he'd ever copied, though never once meeting Wendell's eyes while he did it. She found she didn't hate him anymore. Hate required thinking about him, and she was done spending attention on small men.

"The indenture of Wendell Almeida," Corren read, "executed by Marrow & Vane Trading Company, is hereby declared void on grounds of fraudulent execution, the company having entered the agreement under false premises and maintained it through falsified accounts." He set down the page. "You're also free of the charges. For what it's worth."

"For what it's worth," Wendell repeated.

"It isn't much worth," Corren said, and for the first time she saw something tired and honest under the magistrate's face. "You were charged because charging you was cheaper than charging them. You're cleared because clearing you embarrasses them. None of it is justice, Miss Almeida. It's arithmetic." He closed the file. "But you'll find arithmetic is most of what passes for justice in this world. The remainder you have to make yourself."

She walked out of the customs house into ordinary daylight and stopped in the middle of the street because she didn't know what to do with a morning. Six years of mornings had belonged to the counting house. Six years of bells, of Brannigan's cough, of ink ground before dawn. She stood there until a cart went around her, and then she walked down to the flats because the flats were where her mother was, and her mother was where home had always been, whatever else burned.

* * *

They buried Sef on the Thursday, on the rise above the bridge pilings, in ground the divers' families had used for a hundred years because it flooded last and dried first.

Half the town came. The toll house was still standing then — half of it, one blackened wall with the window frame holding nothing, the glass gone to heat — and the mourners faced away from it, out to the river, because Sef had spent forty years facing the river and it seemed wrong to make him look at anything else now.

Tomas spoke. Not a eulogy; Tomas didn't have it in him to dress things up. He told the story everyone already knew: Sef keeping the bridge through four flood seasons, opening the gate for the first packet boat of every spring, teaching boys to read the current off the surface. Then he told the part they hadn't known until the record opened: Sef watching the first forgery happen, thirty-one years old and needing his pension, saying nothing for eleven years.

"He wasn't a brave man," Tomas said. "He said so himself, in writing, which is more than most cowards manage. But he decided which side of the page to be on. Late. Too late for the toll house, too late for him. Not too late for us." He looked along the row of faces. "That counts. I've decided it counts, and I'm the one carrying his chest up this hill."

Wendell put her father's pearl — the small grey one, the only thing of his that wasn't paper — into the grave before the first spade of earth. Her mother put a river stone. Tomas put the key to the archive room, which no longer needed keeping.

Afterward, walking down through the graves, her mother said: "Your father would have signed anything they put in front of him, you know. If he'd lived. That's what frightens me about all this. You saved the men who couldn't be saved from paper. Who saves you?"

"I do," Wendell said. "One page at a time."

Her mother laughed — short, unconvinced, loving. "Write small, then. So it doesn't eat you."

* * *

The question of the bridge came to a vote in the fish hall on a hot evening with the doors propped open and moths coming in off the river.

Marrow & Vane's charter was dead; the company's assets were in receivership, which meant the bridge — or the char and ironwork of it — technically belonged to whoever the courts sold it to next. The town had a choice, Corren explained from the head table: let a new buyer rebuild and toll it, or pool everything, petition for a municipal charter, and build it themselves, slowly, badly, together.

"Slowly and badly," said the chandler Oakes, "versus quickly and owned by strangers again."

"We've had forty years of quickly," said Mama Iriarte, and that more or less ended the argument, though it took two more hours of everyone saying so.

Wendell attended as a spectator and left as a clerk. They needed minutes kept, subscriptions recorded, a charter petition drafted in a fair hand — no one trusted a hired scrivener after everything, and someone said, not looking at her, that the girl who caught the forgery might check their sums. She said yes before she'd decided to. Wages: eight coppers a week and a share of the tolls once there were tolls.

Eight coppers. Less than a tenth of what the counting house had paid her, and hers, all hers, owed to nobody. She walked home in the dark doing sums she didn't need to do, just to feel them sit in her own hand.

* * *

She bought the ink on a Saturday.

There was enough money left after the ink for bread, and not much else, and her mother raised an eyebrow at the bottle — good ink, imported, the black kind that doesn't fade — but said nothing, because her mother had watched her copy forged pages by lamplight with men on the stairs and understood, finally, what the ink was for.

She had a table now. It was a door laid across two barrels, in the corner of the room that used to be Tomas's uncle's net store, which the Iriartes had lent her until the family boat came out of receivership, which would be spring. The table wobbled. She shimmied the near barrel until it didn't.

Then she sat, and dipped the pen, and for a long time wrote nothing at all.

Here was the problem no ledger had prepared her for: where to begin. A ledger begins with balances. A history — she was beginning to understand — begins with a decision about what matters, and every decision after that is an argument. If she began with the flood, someone would say the truth started earlier. If she began with her father, someone would say this was not his story. If she began with the bridge —

She began with the bridge.

*The bridge at Port Merrow was built by men who did not live here,* she wrote, *toll it how they liked, and for forty years the town paid for the privilege of leaving itself.*

The nib scratched. The river ran below the window, going about its business, carrying silt and small boats and, somewhere far downstream, a disgraced ship full of nothing. She could hear Tomas below, hammering at the boat, fitting new planking where the receivers had stripped the old; he hammered the way he did everything, as if arguing with the wood and winning slowly.

She wrote through the afternoon. The counting house. Brannigan's contempt, described fairly, which cost her something. The page where her father's name sat on top of a date after his death — she wrote that scene twice, tore it up twice, and the third time wrote it plainly, without ornament, because plainness was the only honest register for it. The ledgers obeyed whoever held them, Sef had said. Fine. She was holding them now. Every sentence was a transfer of custody.

By dusk she had nine pages and a cramp in her hand and she understood, finally, what freedom was for. Not rest. Rest could be had inside an indenture, in the gaps. Freedom was for this: for choosing what to carry and carrying it on purpose.

She blotted the ninth page and weighted it under the inkstone and went down to help Tomas hold a plank against the hull while he nailed it, and the river went dark, and the lights of the fish hall came on across the water where the town was drafting its charter, and none of it was finished. The Vanes would return with lawyers. The inquiry would drag. Half the seized boats were still in receivership limbo and would rot before the courts moved, unless people pushed. There was so much left to push.

But the ship had sailed lighter than it came, and she had seen it go, and tomorrow she would buy more paper.

That night she added one line at the bottom of page nine, small, almost an afterthought, the way true things often get written:

*This account is continued by its author, who intends, this time, to finish what she starts.*
