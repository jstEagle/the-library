## 1. The Ledger Under the Linen

The cold came through the walls of the dressing station the way water comes through a hull—steadily, from everywhere at once. Mara Montrose had stopped fighting it by December. Now, in the third week of January, she simply worked inside it, her fingers moving through the ritual of the day: strip, clean, pack, bind. Strip the wound. Clean it with the carbolic that arrived in bottles smaller than hope. Pack it with lint she boiled herself because the orderlies boiled nothing. Bind it, and move on.

"Next."

The barn had been a barn once. You could still smell the hay under the blood and the iodine, a sweetness gone wrong. Forty-two men lay on straw pallets in rows, and the straw was thinner every week because the requisitions came up short and nobody upstream seemed to wonder why. Mara wondered. That was her trouble, Sister Agnes had told her once. You wonder too loud.

The man on the next pallet was a private of the 33rd, twenty years old if that, with a ball through his thigh that had missed the artery by the width of a prayer. He'd been crying without sound for an hour—the tears just running, his mouth busy with something else.

"Don't talk," Mara said, cutting away his trouser leg. "Save your breath for bleeding, which you're doing enough of already."

"It's my brother," he said anyway. "Same company. Daniel Wace. They said—" His throat worked. "They said he's posted missing. But Pimm saw him go down at the bridge. Pimm saw it."

"Then he'll come up on the rolls when they reconcile them." She said it the way you say anything to a wounded man: gently, and without committing to its truth. The reconciliation of rolls was a fiction she had stopped believing in around the time she started keeping her own account of it.

"He won't though." The boy's eyes found hers, fever-bright and terribly clear. "Will he?"

Mara tied off the bandage, tucking the end flat so it wouldn't catch when they moved him. "I'll tell you what I tell everyone. Names get lost in the post out here. It doesn't mean the man is lost."

It didn't mean he wasn't, either. She took the boy's pulse, found it steady enough, and moved down the row to the next man, and the next, until the light through the cracked barn boards went from white to grey to nothing, and the surgeon-major called the day closed as if it were a shop.

* * *

Nights were hers.

Not entirely—there were always men calling out, always the dying who needed someone to sit with them so they didn't go alone into whatever waited—but between one bell and the next there was an hour when the station slept or pretended to, and that hour belonged to the ledger.

She kept it in the linen trunk at the back of the store room, beneath the bandage rolls and the spare sheets, wrapped in a pillowcase because the leather had begun to crack and she couldn't bear the sound it made opening. It had started two winters ago as nothing. A memorandum book, army issue, salvaged from a dead paymaster whose effects no one claimed. She had taken it for notes on dressings, and then one night in November she had written, instead:

*Pvt. H. Loomis, 33rd Foot — died of fever, 14 Nov. Not entered on regimental roll. Sgt. Cleaver says the roll was sent before he died.*

That was all. A fact, set down where facts could be found. But facts, she had learned, were like sparks in dry straw. One led to another. By spring she had eleven names. By this winter she had filled forty pages, small and close-written, names and dates and causes, each entry annotated in her tidy schoolroom hand: *not on roll*, *struck as deserter*, *posted missing, witnesses say killed*, *effects burned by order*.

She never took a name she hadn't verified. That was the discipline. Grief invented ghosts; she had seen women at home mourn men who'd deserted them rather than died for them, and she would not build her ledger out of other people's wishes. Two witnesses, or a document, or her own eyes. Otherwise the name went in pencil, in a separate list at the back, marked *unconfirmed*, and stayed there until something made it true.

Tonight she had three entries to make.

She worked by candle stub, shielded behind the trunk's raised lid, copying from scraps she'd collected through the day in her apron pocket—a bit of wrapper, a corner of a return, the back of a laundry list. Her breath fogged in the candlelight. Somewhere down the ward a man called for his mother in a language the orderlies didn't speak, and then went quiet, and she noted the hour automatically, the way she noted everything now, because everything might someday need proving.

*Pvt. R. Kessel, 44th Foot — gangrene, left arm. Died 19 Jan., approx. 4 in the morning. Witnessed by self.*

*Kitchen woman Anka Berl, civilian contractor — dysentery. Died 17 Jan. No record anywhere. She has a daughter in Thorn Mill.*

And the third scrap she held longer before writing. It was a slip of paper she had taken off a sergeant-major's desk while signing for stores, an internal memorandum in Colonel's-staff hand: *Wace, D., Pte., 33rd — struck from strength w.e.f. 9 Jan. Reason: missing, presumed deserted.*

Presumed. She had heard Pimm himself, that morning, sobbing out the details to anyone who'd listen: Wace shot down on the bridge at Vosk, seen clearly, seen falling, no more deserted than the stone under his feet.

Mara dipped her pen. Her hand did not shake; it never shook anymore, which frightened her sometimes more than shaking would have.

*D. Wace, Pte., 33rd Foot — struck from rolls 9 Jan. as "missing, presumed deserted." Reported killed at Vosk Bridge by Cpl. Pimm, witnessed. Brother admitted here 21 Jan., wounded. Brother not informed of entry on roll.*

She read it over. Added: *Brother expects reconciliation. There will be none.*

Then she blew out the candle, wrapped the ledger in its pillowcase, laid it flat beneath the boiled sheets, and piled the bandage rolls on top until it was exactly as invisible as a thing could be while still existing.

* * *

The officer came on a Thursday.

She knew his type before he'd finished ducking through the door—a staff captain, gloves uncracked by work, carrying himself with the particular stiffness of a man performing his own importance in a place designed to make importance look ridiculous. Captain Something-or-other, attached to the Colonel's staff at division. Behind him hovered Sergeant Cleaver, who carried the rolls and carried, Mara suspected, a growing suspicion about her, though he had never yet found anything to carry it with.

"You're the senior nurse?" the captain said, looking past her at the ward as though inspecting a stable.

"I'm the only one with seniority who hasn't died," Mara said. "Sir."

His eyes came back to her, tested the answer for insolence, found the surface smooth, and moved on. "There was a man through here. Wace. Thirty-third."

"Daniel Wace. Died on the ninth. Gunshot, bridge action. We had him two days." She kept her voice level and informative, the voice of furniture.

"His effects." The captain produced a folded paper, an order with signatures on it, and let it hang in his gloved hand rather than offering it. "There's been an error in documentation. The man is recorded absent without leave. His effects are therefore to be disposed of. Burned. You'll witness it done and sign the disposition."

Mara looked at the paper without reaching for it. "Burned, sir? Usually effects go home to next of kin."

"Not in cases of desertion." He said the word cleanly, without flinching, and she understood that this was the whole trick of it: say the word often enough, smoothly enough, and it stops being an accusation requiring proof and becomes a category requiring paperwork. "You'll have them packed. Sergeant Cleaver will supervise the burning at noon."

"Yes, sir."

"And nurse." He paused at the door, and for a moment something moved behind the officiousness—not quite conscience, more like a man checking his boot for what he'd stepped in. "The brother. The wounded one. He isn't to be told the manner of the entry. If he asks, the records are under reconciliation."

"Yes, sir," said Mara, who had spent two years becoming excellent at saying those words while meaning none of them.

* * *

Wace's effects fit into a canvas kit-bag not much bigger than a bread loaf. Mara unpacked them alone in the store room at noon-eve, laying each item on the table the way she laid out instruments: orderly, deliberate, giving each its moment.

A razor, horn-handled, worn down to a crescent. A housewife sewing kit, the thread all used but the black. Three letters from home, softened at the folds from rereading. A tin whistle, dented. A pair of mittens, half-knitted, the yarn still attached to the needles as if he'd meant to finish them when he got back to them. Soldiers always meant to finish things. That was the arithmetic of war—you borrowed time against intentions.

And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a small portrait: a woman with a baby, done in charcoal by some camp artist for three francs, and on the back in the dead boy's hand: *Sarah + Tom, born Sept. Do not let them forget me.*

Mara stood over the kit-bag for a long moment. Then she took out her memorandum book—the small everyday one, the decoy—and copied, quickly, the address from the top letter. *Mrs. S. Wace, Mill Lane, Dunsford.* And beneath it, in the ledger that evening, after the trunk lid had risen and fallen twice:

*Effects of D. Wace ordered burned 23 Jan. by Capt. Ellery, div. staff. Order cites desertion entry of 9 Jan. Contents inventoried: razor, sewing kit, 3 letters, tin whistle, unfinished mittens, portrait (wife Sarah; child Thomas, b. Sept.). Address obtained: Mrs. S. Wace, Mill Lane, Dunsford. Note: I intend to write to her myself.*

She hesitated over that last line. Writing to the families was against every regulation and several good instincts—it invited questions, created threads a clever investigator could pull. She wrote it anyway. Some omissions were worse than danger.

At noon the kit-bag went into the brazier behind the kitchens, and Cleaver watched her watch it burn, and she signed the disposition form in her round, unremarkable hand, and the smoke went up thin and grey into a sky the color of dishwater. Inside her sleeve, folded small, was the portrait. She had decided the charcoal would not be missed among the ash. Let the army keep its razor and its whistle. She would keep the face.

* * *

Sister Agnes found her at dusk, standing at the window of the store room with the portrait tucked back into her apron, watching the snow come on.

"You signed it, then." Agnes was seventy if she was a day, Belgian by birth, and had survived three armies by perfecting a facial expression that communicated nothing. "The burning."

"I signed it."

"And you'll sleep tonight."

"I sleep every night."

Agnes looked at her the way old women look at young ones who are lying competently—with respect, and worry, and no intention of intervening. "There's talk," she said finally. "Down at division. Talk about paper. About returns that don't match what the chaplains bury. Cleaver's been asking which nurses can write."

"I can wash linens and hold a man's legs still," Mara said. "That's what I'm known for."

"Mm." Agnes gathered her shawl. "Known for, and known by, are different animals. Mind which one you're feeding."

She left. Mara stood a while longer at the window, listening to the wind work at the barn boards, thinking about the boy down the ward with the hole in his thigh who believed, because she had told him so, that names got lost in the post. She had four letters from families at home unanswered in her trunk because answering meant either lying or confessing, and she had not yet decided which cruelty she preferred.

Then she lifted the trunk lid, moved the bandages, and opened the ledger to a fresh page, because there was one more entry the day owed her.

*23 Jan. — Effects of the struck-out are now being burned by direct order. This is new. Previously they were merely forgotten. Forgetting is neglect. Burning is policy. Someone has realized that paper survives, and is afraid of it.*

She sat with her pen above the line for a moment, then added the sentence she had been circling for two years without writing, the way you circle a wound before probing it:

*I am the paper that survives.*

Outside, across the frozen parade ground, a rider came up the road at speed, throwing snow from his horse's heels, and did not slow at the sentry line—did not even check—which meant either a dispatch of enormous urgency or a man being pursued, and the sentries, when they finally challenged him, would discover which.

Mara closed the ledger. Some part of her, the part that had learned to hear trouble coming the way farmers hear weather, was already certain it was both.
