## 2. A Stranger at the Sentry Line

The sentry fired at dusk, and the sound came through the canvas walls of the dressing station like a knuckle rapping on a coffin lid.

Mara set down the bowl of water she'd been carrying and did not flinch. That was a discipline older than her hands—flinching cost time, and time was the only medicine they had enough of. She looked instead at Private Corliss, who had both legs bandaged to the knee and who had gone white at the shot.

"It's just Perkins," she said. "He fires at shadows when the light goes. It saves him having to admit he's frightened."

"Are you frightened?" Corliss asked.

"Constantly," Mara said, and went out into the cold.

The perimeter of the dressing station was a suggestion rather than a fact—a ring of wagons, a snowbank thrown up by the last storm, two sentries who rotated so often through the front lines that neither could be said to guard anything but his own endurance. Beyond it the plain ran white to a treeline that was more rumor than forest. Snow had begun again, the fine dry kind that didn't fall so much as arrive, already everywhere.

Perkins stood at the snowbank with his musket shouldered, pointing at nothing.

"There," he said. "There was something there."

Mara followed his barrel with her eyes. At first she saw what he was supposed to see: snow, wind, the gray seam of evening. Then the nothing moved.

It resolved slowly, the way things do in heavy weather—not a man walking so much as a shape the wind had failed to erase. It took three more steps and then its legs simply quit, and it went down into the snow without drama, the way exhausted things go down, folding at the middle as if the ground had been waiting for it all day.

"He's alive," Mara said. "Help me."

"He might be French."

"He might be. He also might freeze before we decide." She was already moving. "And if he's French and wounded, he's still my patient. Bring the stretcher."

Perkins muttered something about regulations, which was how soldiers prayed, and came after her.

* * *

The man weighed less than he should have. That was her first professional observation, made while they hauled him over the snowbank and onto the canvas—weight loss past the point where the body begins consuming its own dignity. His coat was civilian wool, gray once, now gray forever, stiff with ice at the shoulders where sweat had frozen and thawed and frozen again. No greatcoat. In this winter, no greatcoat meant either remarkable stupidity or a journey begun somewhere warmer in too much of a hurry.

His face told her the rest. Thirty, perhaps thirty-five. Hollowed at the cheeks. Lips cracked and split from wind. Frost-white at the tips of his ears and the first knuckle of every finger—the body's ledger of what the cold had taken first. His eyes were open. That interested her. Men who collapsed usually closed their eyes and surrendered to it; this one was fighting to keep his, tracking her hands as she cut away his coat sleeve.

"Satchel," he said.

His voice was wrecked, a whisper dragged over gravel.

"You can have your satchel back when I know it isn't full of gunpowder."

"No—" One hand came up, trembling, and gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both. His fingers were iron for two seconds and then spent. "Don't... it doesn't leave my sight. Please."

"Everything leaves your sight when you're unconscious," Mara said. "Which you nearly are. What's your name?"

"Elias. Vane." Each word purchased separately.

"What's your rank, Elias Vane?"

A pause. The eyes went careful, which in her experience meant the man behind them was doing arithmetic.

"Clerk," he said. "Paymaster's clerk. Fourth Division train."

"Well, Clerk Fourth Division Train, you have frostbite in six fingers and hypothermia in everything else, and you will die tonight unless you do exactly what I say." She pried his hand off her wrist, gently, and held it a moment longer than necessary. "Your satchel stays within arm's reach of your bed. That is the best offer on the table."

He studied her. Even half-dead, he studied her, weighing the offer the way a man weighs rope before trusting it.

"Arm's reach," he repeated.

"I don't renege. Ask anyone I've ever cheated, if you can find one."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth that in a healthier face would have been a smile. Then his eyes rolled back and he went under entirely, and the negotiation was concluded by default.

* * *

They carried him into the ward, and the ward took an interest immediately, because a new arrival was the only entertainment available and dying men are connoisseurs of other men's conditions.

Mara worked him the way she worked everyone: boots off, wet clothes off, skin to warm skin under blankets because there was no other fuel left to burn. She had done it with drowning boys from the pontoon trains and with a Russian prisoner last month whose shivering had shaken the cot frame. She did not ask permission anymore; modesty was a luxury item, requisitioned like everything else, and rarely in stock.

It was when she pulled off his boots that the arithmetic changed.

They were army boots. Not bought-from-a-convoy boots, not the mismatched footwear of a man who'd traded his way across a province—but issue boots, right and left, same pattern as the ones rotting off the feet of the men in her ward, stamped with the same contractor's mark on the heel she saw forty times a day. Worn, yes. Resoled badly, with stitches that were a soldier's stitches, the ugly functional kind learned out of necessity. But army. A paymaster's clerk attached to the divisional train would ride a wagon. His feet would know leather and ink, not forced marches.

She turned one boot over in her lap and looked at it for perhaps four seconds longer than she needed to.

Then she took his hand to check the frostbitten fingertips, and found the second answer waiting in his palm. Calluses—not the writer's ridge on the middle finger that every clerk wears like a badge of trade, though he had that too, faintly, beneath newer history. These were across the palm's heel and the base of each finger. Rifle calluses. The thickened skin of a man who had gripped a musket through long drills or long marches until his hand gave up arguing and grew armor instead.

Clerk, she thought. Yes. And something else underneath, the way a grave is under grass.

She said nothing. She wrapped his hands in linen soaked with cool water—slow warming, never fast, fast killed—and set the satchel exactly where she'd promised: on the ground beside his cot, close enough that his nearest hand, if it woke and wanted, could fall upon it.

Then she sat down on the stool between him and Corliss, and treated the rest of the ward, and waited for him to come back.

* * *

He surfaced near midnight, in stages, the way men do—first the shivering, violent and good, a sign the furnace still drew; then the mumbling; then, abruptly, full wakefulness, his eyes snapping open and going straight to the floor beside the cot.

Finding the satchel, they closed again, briefly. Relief is unmistakable in any language.

"Drink," Mara said, holding a tin cup to his lips. Broth, thin, saved from the officers' kitchen by means she declined to document even internally. He drank like a man who had been rationing himself for weeks and knew better than to gulp.

"The satchel," he said, when the cup was empty.

"Is where I said it would be." She refilled it from the small pot on the coals. "You're welcome, by the way."

"For what?"

"Generally. It accumulates." She watched him over the steam. "You gave a name and a trade and neither one fits together properly, Mr. Vane."

His stillness was total. Even his shivering paused, as if his body understood that something important was being decided.

"Your boots," she went on, conversationally, stirring the broth as though discussing the weather. "Division issue, contractor's mark of Halloran and Sons, resoled with a soldier's stitch, not a clerk's. Your hands have rifle calluses over the top of pen calluses, which means you carried a musket long enough for your palms to form an opinion about it. And you walked here from the east road in the teeth of a blizzard without a greatcoat, which no man does for pay. Clerks follow pay." She looked up. "So. Shall we try the truth this time, or shall we let me keep guessing? I warn you, I've had a dull winter and a vivid imagination."

For a long moment he lay looking at the ridgepole, and she could see him running the sums—how sick he was, how far the door was, whether the woman with the cup was a danger or merely a problem.

"You counted my boots," he said finally. His voice was coming back, rougher but real. "Most people count a man's face."

"I haven't time for faces. Faces lie. Boots are honest."

That got the almost-smile again, stronger this time, before he banked it. "If I told you the truth," he said, "you'd wish I hadn't. And then you'd have a choice to make, and I wouldn't wish that on you. You look like a woman who's made enough choices for one war."

"I look like whatever's useful," Mara said. "Everyone here looks through nurses. It's the single advantage of the profession. We're furniture that boils water." She set the cup down. "I'll tell you what I told myself the night I decided to stop being furniture, Mr. Vane: a choice you refuse to make is still a choice. It's just the worst one."

He turned his head and looked at her properly for the first time—not at the nurse, not at the hands, but at her, with the assessing attention of a man cataloguing assets. She let him. She had been looked at by surgeons and colonels and one very drunk general, and none of them had ever actually seen her; she had stopped expecting it and started using it.

"You have a ward full of witnesses," he said quietly. "Any one of whom Hargreave's provosts would squeeze like a rag if they thought you'd heard something. Why would you take that on for a stranger?"

"Hargreave," Mara repeated. She kept her voice level, and noted—with the particular cold satisfaction of a woman who keeps ledgers of her own—that he realized his mistake one syllable too late. The name had come out of him unbidden, urgent, the way a wound announces itself. "Colonel Hargreave. Now why would a paymaster's clerk be worried about the colonel's provosts?"

Elias Vane closed his eyes. When he opened them again, something had settled in them—the arithmetic finished, the sum arrived at.

"Because I'm not what I said I am," he said. "And because there are twelve men in a barn twenty miles east of here who are going to die for a crime they didn't commit, unless—" His gaze dropped to the satchel. "Unless what's in there gets where it's going."

Mara sat very still.

Twelve men. The number landed in her chest with the specific weight of a thing recognized. She thought of her trunk, the false bottom, the linen folded over names written in her own careful hand—the men the rolls had lost, the dead who weren't dead, the deserters who had never deserted. She had spent a year believing she was alone in noticing what the army erased. She had trained herself not to hope otherwise, because hope was another luxury item, rarely in stock.

"Twelve," she said.

"Twelve."

She rose, crossed the ward, and checked the door flap, and the stove, and Corliss—who slept with the blessed completeness of the morphined—and the private in the next cot, breathing the shallow regular breath of the deeply exhausted. Then she came back and sat down, and lowered her voice to the register she used for telling men they were dying.

"In the morning," she said, "you will be well enough to sit up and not well enough to travel. That buys us a day before someone official notices you. You will spend it deciding how much to tell me, and I will spend it deciding how much to believe. Those are fair terms."

"That's not—"

"Those are the terms." She stood. "Your broth is by your hand. If you need to void yourself, the bucket is behind the screen, and you will use help whether you like it or not. Welcome to my dressing station, Mr. Vane. Try not to die in it. I'm over my quota for the month."

She was three steps away when he spoke again, so quietly she almost lost it under the wind.

"The boots," he said. "You're right. They're mine—they were always mine. But they walked away from the column eleven days ago, and everything that happened before that..." He stopped. Started again. "You asked what I am. I'm the man who wrote the lie down. All of it. Every word."

Mara stood in the dim ward with the wind worrying the canvas and the stove ticking, and felt the year's quiet work inside her shift and wake like something that had been sleeping in her chest since the first name she ever copied.

She did not turn around.

"Then you'd better start at the beginning," she said, "and you'd better not leave anything out. I'll know. I keep records."

Behind her, in the dark, Elias Vane laughed once—a short, broken sound, halfway to a sob—and began to talk.
