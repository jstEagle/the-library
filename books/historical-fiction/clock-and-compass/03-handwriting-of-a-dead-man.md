## 3. Handwriting of a Dead Man

The delousing was the worst hour of any day, and Mara had learned to make it useful.

It was simple arithmetic. A man's shirt came off, and everything he carried came out of it—tobacco, letters, a lucky coin, a photograph of a woman who was probably already someone else's wife—and for as long as the shirt boiled in the copper, his whole life lay folded on her table where she could read it. She knew which men hoarded bread. She knew which men wrote home on Sundays and which only when they were afraid. She knew Private Hollis kept his mother's letter against his chest until the paper wore soft as cloth, and she knew he never once mentioned the wound in his side to her, not in four pages of careful, cheerful lying.

You learn things delousing men that no officer learns inspecting them.

So when Elias Vane slept—which was often, and deeply, and with the suspicious completeness of a man who had not slept properly in weeks—Mara did what she always did. She took his satchel.

She told herself it was protocol. Every garment in the ward went into the copper; every seam got the nit-comb; every possession got aired by the stove because lice rode in paper and leather as happily as in wool. It was protocol. It was also true that she wanted to know him, and the wanting had been sitting in her chest for two days like a splinter too deep to draw.

He claimed to be a paymaster's clerk. He claimed frostbite, fever, misadventure on the road. But clerks did not have hands like rope, callused across the palm in the long diagonal that comes from hauling on a musket sling for months. Clerks did not sleep with their backs to walls and their boots on. And those boots—she had turned one over while he dozed, purely as a nurse assessing exposure—were army-issue, size large, soles worn through at the ball of the foot in the pattern of a man who had marched, not sat at a desk writing figures.

A clerk who marched like an infantryman and lied like a bad one. The combination interested her more than it should have.

* * *

The satchel was oilskin, good quality, army stores stamp still visible under a scab of mud. It was heavier than a clerk's papers had any right to be.

She worked methodically, the way she did everything. Comb first, then contents. She laid each item on the clean towel: a razor gone to rust at the hinge. A tin spoon, dented. A pencil stub worn down to a thumb's length—the tool of a man who wrote constantly and cheaply. A folded map of the river country, creased into squares small enough to hide in a fist, the ice-road marked in fresh graphite.

And beneath the map, wrapped in a second oilskin sleeve tied with cord, the letters.

There were eleven of them. She counted twice, because counting was how she kept herself honest, and because eleven was a number that snagged on something in her memory the way a burr snags on wool. Each envelope was sealed, addressed to different names—a sister in Ledbury, a former colonel retired to Shrewsbury, a Member of Parliament whose name she recognized from casualty dispatches, a newspaper office in London. Each was written in a hand she would have known anywhere in the world, because she had spent one terrible night in October copying it out word for word into a ledger hidden under linen.

Captain Aldous Reeve's hand was distinctive. Schoolroom-perfect uprights ruined by a fast leftward slant, as if the man wrote quickly against some private deadline. He'd dictated his own casualty report to her the night they brought him in, laughing brokenly at the absurdity of his own wounds, insisting on checking every name himself before he let her record it.

She had recorded his death three days later. Second Hand Company, killed in the action at the mill, entered in the official roll in the adjutant's copperplate and entered in her own ledger in her own cramped hand—*Reeve, Aldous, Capt., d. 14 Oct., abdominal wound, survived 3 days.* She had held his wrist when the pulse went. She had felt it stop. She did not make errors of that kind. Her ledger did not contain errors of that kind.

She opened the top letter with hands that were perfectly steady, which surprised her.

*Dear Mr. Cathcart,—You will forgive a dead man his presumption in writing to you...*

Dated the ninth of December.

Two months after she had closed his eyes.

* * *

She read four of them before she heard him move.

They were extraordinary documents. She understood within a page why they were sealed and why there were eleven and why the map showed the ice-road drawn in a hand that shook. They were testimony—dated, signed, witnessed, each one a full account of something that had happened at a place called Vosk Bridge, written in Reeve's fast leftward-slanting hand and countersigned at the foot by a name she did not know: *E. Vane, clerk.*

Elias Vane's name. On every letter. Countersigned, sworn, attested.

She sat very still on her stool with the letters fanned on the towel in front of her, and she listened to the ward breathe—forty men breathing, some whistling softly through torn lungs, one singing to himself in Welsh, low and endless—and she thought about what it meant that a dead man's handwriting could be this warm from his body's heat, folded against a living stranger's back.

When she looked up, Elias was awake. She didn't know for how long. He was watching her from his cot with an expression she had seen a hundred times on the faces of men caught doing something they believed was right: not guilt exactly. Bracing.

"That's army property," he said.

"So is the coat you're wearing, and I boiled that yesterday." She squared the letters, tapped them level against the towel. "Sit up slowly. You'll tear your stitches."

He sat up slowly, and tore nothing, and watched her the whole way.

"Paymaster's clerk," she said.

"Yes."

"With a paymaster's clerk's correspondence countersigned in his own hand." She held up the topmost letter so he could see the attestation line. "*E. Vane, clerk.* You witnessed whatever is in these. In writing. Which makes you either the most dangerous clerk in this army or its most expendable."

Something moved behind his eyes—she'd have called it respect if she'd had time to examine it. "Give them back," he said. "Please."

"In a moment." She set the letters down but kept her hand flat on them, feeling their edges, their weight. Eleven letters. Eleven. "I'm going to ask you a question, Mr. Vane, and I want you to understand that I ask it as the keeper of certain records, and that I will know if you lie, because I have spent two years learning the precise texture of lies told by men in uniforms, and yours are badly made."

"Ask, then."

"Captain Aldous Reeve." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for reporting deaths upward. "I nursed him. I recorded his death on the fourteenth of October. I held his arm when the pulse stopped—you understand what I'm telling you? This is not a rumor I half-remember. This is my hand on his skin." She lifted the letter. "This is dated December the ninth, in his writing, which I would know blindfolded by touch alone. So my question is not whether you are a clerk. My question is: whose death did I record, Mr. Vane?"

The ward noise went on around them—the Welshman singing, the orderly stacking firewood, the wind finding the same gap in the shutters every forty seconds—and between the two of them there was a silence with weight and temperature, cold from the floor up.

"You made an error," Elias said.

"I don't."

"Everyone errs."

"Not with death. Death is the one entry I don't get to approximate." She leaned forward. "Who did I hold?"

He looked at the letters. At her hand on them. At the door. She saw him run the arithmetic she'd already run—that shouting would bring the duty officer, that the duty officer would read the letters, that the letters meant a firing squad for their carrier and probably for anyone found in reach of them. She watched him decide, with visible effort, not to be a fool.

He decided instead to tell the truth, which was, she would think later, the braver stupidity.

"He isn't dead," Elias said quietly. "Reeve is alive."

* * *

He told it badly at first, in the clipped fragments of a man who had rehearsed a lie so long the truth came out in the same broken meter. She let him stumble. She had learned that you get more from a man's second telling than his first, so she waited, sorting his belongings back into the satchel with domestic calm while he found his way to it, and gradually the fragments became a story:

The winter march. Rations cut twice, then cut again—not by fortune but by order, requisitions diverted, stores sold, though Elias did not say yet sold to whom or by whom, and she noticed the omission and marked it. Men eating bark. Men eating their boot-leather boiled for soup. And at Vosk Bridge, twelve soldiers of Second Hand Company refusing to advance on empty stomachs while the officers' wagons stayed locked—twelve men laying down arms not in the face of the enemy but in the face of their own commissary, and Captain Reeve, senior officer present, declining to fire on his own men and formally protesting the order to do so.

"Formal protest," Mara repeated.

"Written protest. Three copies. I typed—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "I copied all three."

"And the army's answer?"

"The army's answer," Elias said, "was to record the entire affair as a deserter action, already dealt with. Twelve deserters, tried in the field, executed summarily. Clean lines. Tidy paperwork." His mouth twisted. "Very tidy. I know. I wrote it."

"You wrote it?"

"I entered the names. Twelve names, marked executed, dated, signed off. My hand, my ink, my blotting sand." He said it without flinching, and she gave him credit for that even as something in her went cold. "Except nobody executed anybody, because executing twelve men draws witnesses and questions and columns in newspapers, whereas deserters already shot can simply exist on paper and rot quietly in a file. Except—" he nodded at the letters "—they weren't shot. They're being held. Reeve escaped custody the night of the transfer and has been writing these ever since, one for every man who might help, hidden with the other eleven in a place I will not name even now, because the plan is that they die of legal process or they die of winter, and either way Colonel Hargreave's roll stays clean."

Mara sat with that.

Then she stood, walked the length of the ward to the shutter, checked the gap where the wind came through, checked the yard beyond it, and came back. Some habits were worth keeping.

"Hargreave," she said. "Colonel Hargreave. Tall man, grey, gloves indoors. He was here in November. He reviewed my ward personally."

"He reviews everything personally. It's how he keeps the version of events he prefers." Elias was watching her face. "Why? What did he do here?"

"He handed me a list." She kept her voice level. "Eleven names, marked deceased, and instructions that the effects of the deceased be burned without inventory, for reasons of hygiene. I burned the effects." She let that sit a moment. "I did not burn the names. They're in my ledger. Eleven names, and the notation that no bodies arrived at my station, and no graves were dug, and no burial details were drawn from the roster—which struck me at the time as an efficiency I had not previously encountered in this army, we being a institution that documents its dead the way other institutions document their money."

For the first time since he'd collapsed at her sentry line, Elias Vane laughed—a short, disbelieving breath of a laugh, gone almost before it arrived.

"You kept records."

"I keep records."

"Of the men he erased."

"Someone has to be the other copy," Mara said. "Paper burns. Memory is slower about it."

He looked at her then with an expression she couldn't name and didn't try to, and pushed his hand through his hair, and said, mostly to himself, "I've been carrying these letters across a frozen country for six weeks, checked at every sentry post, sleeping in ditches, and I walk into the one dressing station in the province where the nurse has been keeping a private count of the exact men I'm trying to save."

"The army teaches economy," she said. "Waste nothing. Not even truth."

But even as she said it, her mind was moving down a colder corridor. Because she had done the arithmetic already, in the first minutes, when the number eleven snagged like a burr. Eleven letters. Eleven men in her ledger. Twelve accused at Vosk Bridge.

"Your captain writes letters," she said slowly, "for eleven fellow prisoners. One each. To eleven doors that might open."

"Yes."

"Then where—" and she heard her own voice drop, and hated how much it cost her to ask, "where is the twelfth letter, Mr. Vane? Where is Captain Reeve's own?"

Elias went still. Not the stillness of a man withholding. The stillness of a man arriving, right now, at the edge of something he had refused to look at.

"There isn't one," he said.

"Every condemned man writes one letter. It's practically regulation."

"He said—he told me to take the eleven first. He said his could wait, that no one writes to a newspaper about himself, that—" Elias stopped. His hands had closed on the blanket. "He gave me the map. He gave me the route. He stood at the fence and shook my hand and told me the letters mattered more than his life, because the letters free the others and nothing frees him, and I took them and I went, and I never once—" His voice cracked down the middle. "I never asked the obvious question."

Mara looked down at the letters in their oilskin sleeve. Eleven names that might open eleven doors. And somewhere behind wire, in the cold, a twelfth man who had ordered no letter written for himself, because he had already done the arithmetic and found no door on the other end of it.

Her ledger had eleven names in it. It should have had twelve.

In two years of keeping the army's secrets against the army, she had committed exactly one omission, and it had a face, and a laugh, and handwriting she could identify blindfolded, and it was still alive.

"We leave tomorrow," Elias said hoarsely. "Before the ice breaks. There's a man—a correspondent—who can print these. Three checkpoints between here and Thorn Mill, and after the thaw, no road at all."

Mara folded the letters back into their sleeve with hands that were steady again, tied the cord, and understood that the quiet thing under her linen trunk had just woken up, learned her name, and started walking.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we'd better both finish our arithmetic."

Outside, the wind found the gap in the shutters, and somewhere down the river, under six weeks of snow, the ice groaned like a thing turning over in its sleep.
