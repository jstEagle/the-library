## 4. What the Colonel Struck Out

Elias Vane had been a clerk long enough to know that armies did not run on ammunition or flour. They ran on paper.

Every man in the column was a line in a ledger. Every biscuit, every cartridge, every grave was an entry somewhere, initialled twice and filed in a leather case that rode, at this moment, on the back of a mule three places behind Colonel Sir Marcus Hargreave's saddlehorse. Elias had copied those entries for eleven months. He knew the regiment better than the regiment knew itself — knew which sergeant watered the rum, which captain drew double forage for a horse he'd eaten in October, which privates could not read their own names on the muster roll and pressed their thumbs to the page where a signature belonged.

He had thought, in the way comfortable men think, that the paper merely recorded the army.

The winter march taught him otherwise. The paper *was* the army. What was written was true; what was unwritten had never happened; and a man struck from the roll did not die — he simply ceased, retroactively, to have been.

* * *

It began with the rations.

In November the commissary returns came down from division thinner than they should have been, and Elias, who reconciled the figures because reconciliation was his whole life, found the discrepancy in an afternoon. Twelve hundred men on strength, provisions for eight hundred. The difference existed on paper as a neat transfer — "to forward depots, per Colonel's order" — and the forward depots, when Elias checked, had received nothing at all.

He took it to Captain Aldous Reeve, because Reeve was the sort of officer who signed his own name properly instead of having a clerk do it, and because Reeve had once stood between a drunken paymaster and a sixteen-year-old trumpeter and said, in a voice like a closing door, *We do not do that here.*

Reeve read the return twice. He was a lean man going grey at the temples, with a habit of tapping the paper against his knee as though it might confess something.

"Twelve hundred men," he said quietly. "And food for eight."

"Yes, sir."

"And the depots show nothing." He tapped the page again. "Where does four hundred men's bread go, Vane?"

"I don't know, sir."

"You do know," Reeve said, not unkindly. "You simply know what you're permitted to say. There's a difference, and I'd thank you to keep hold of it."

Elias kept hold of it. It burned in his pocket all through December like a coal he couldn't put down.

The rations were cut again before Christmas. Then again. The men boiled hides. They stripped bark from trees along the column's route with a thoroughness that would have looked, from a distance, like madness — long pale scars on the winter landscape, marking exactly where the regiment had passed. They traded wedding rings for turnips. In January a grenadier named Cobb, six foot two and formerly the funniest man in the third company, sat down in the snow sixty miles short of the river and declined, with great politeness, to stand up again.

Elias entered Cobb in the casualty ledger. *Died of exhaustion.* He wrote it in his best hand, because even grief deserves good penmanship, and he hated himself a little for how steady the letters were.

* * *

Vosk Bridge happened on a Tuesday.

Later, when the army told the story, it would tell it wrong on purpose, and Elias would be the one holding the pen that made the lie legal. But on the Tuesday itself it was only this:

The regiment reached the bridge at dusk, half-starved, expecting the relieved rotation that had been promised in writing since October. There was no rotation. There were no rations waiting. There was a staff officer on the far bank with fresh orders: the bridge was needed for artillery, the infantry would bivouac in the open ground, and the promised stores — Elias saw the requisition himself, saw the signature — had been redirected.

Eight hundred men who had marched on hope for six weeks stood in a frozen field and watched the last of the hope get loaded onto wagons and driven away.

It would have ended there, in silence, the way such things usually ended. Men starve quietly. That is the miracle nobody ever writes ballads about — how much quiet a starving battalion can hold.

What broke the quiet was Private Daniel Foss.

Foss was a weaver's son from upcountry, twenty-three years old, illiterate, with a laugh that carried across a parade ground. When the wagon train pulled out he climbed onto the first wagon — not violently, Elias would swear to this later, not violently at all — and put his hands flat on the tarpaulin over the grain sacks and said, loudly, in the reasonable voice of a man asking for his wages: "This is ours. This was issued to us. Where is it going?"

A corporal told him to get down. He didn't get down. A sergeant told him to get down. He said, still reasonably, "Tell me where it's going and I'll get down."

And then — Elias saw it, from thirty yards, in failing light, and he would spend the rest of his life checking the memory like a suspicious figure in an account — a lieutenant drew a pistol and fired at the tarpaulin near Foss's hands, and the shot went wide, and the field, which had been quiet the way a full reservoir is quiet, broke.

They called it a mutiny afterward. Elias supposed that was even technically true. Three hundred men surged toward the wagons. Somebody — no one ever established who — threw a bottle that broke against an officer's horse. The officers formed a line. Reeve rode into the middle of it, alone, ahead of his own men, shouting orders that were, for once in his career, ignored, and then not ignored but obeyed by the wrong side, because half the angry men knew him and trusted him and stopped when he raised his hand, which meant the other half stopped too, which meant that Captain Aldous Reeve, by the simple fact of being respected by mutineers, became evidence.

It was over in eleven minutes. No one died. Elias checked. Eleven minutes, one broken bottle, one bruised sergeant, and three hundred men herded back into the freezing mud — and then the arrests began, and they took twelve names.

They took Foss first. Then eleven others: men who had shouted, men who had climbed, men who had simply been standing where the anger was thickest. And last, because he had stopped the mutiny and was therefore implicated in having had the power to stop it, they took Captain Reeve.

Elias watched them march the twelve into the stone barn by the bridge, and he felt something crack in his chest that no amount of careful bookkeeping would ever close.

* * *

Colonel Hargreave summoned him at midnight.

The colonel's quarters were warm. That was the first obscenity of the evening — the fire, the candle, the decanter, while three hundred men shivered two hundred yards away. Hargreave sat at his desk in shirtsleeves, grey-faced, precise, a man who believed with his whole soul that the world was an unruly document requiring a firm hand, and he pushed a single sheet of paper across the desk toward Elias.

"Copy it," he said. "Regimental format. Then enter it in the campaign journal under today's date."

Elias read it.

*Twelve men of the line having deserted their colours during the night of the 14th, and having been apprehended in the act by the provost, the sentence of death prescribed by the Articles was carried out at dawn. The example, it is hoped, will restore discipline to a regiment much tried by the rigours of the campaign.*

There was no dawn execution. There had been no sentence, no court, no articles read aloud. The twelve men were alive in the barn, and this paper said they were dead, and the paper was to become true by the simple mechanism of Elias Vane's handwriting.

"Sir," he said, and his voice came out in pieces, "the men are—"

"The men," Hargreave said, "are a scandal. A public flogging would bring reporters. A general court-martial would bring questions — questions about provisioning, questions I do not intend to answer, because the answers would damage the service more than twelve malefactors are worth." He poured himself wine with unhurried care. "Desertion, Vane, is understood. Everyone understands desertion. Desertion is regrettable, common, and closes the matter. Do you see?"

"No, sir."

Hargreave looked at him then, and Elias understood that the colonel had weighed him already, precisely, the way Elias weighed a column of figures — and found him a rounding error. Not a threat. An instrument.

"You are a clerk," Hargreave said. "You will copy the order, or you will be replaced by a clerk who will. And if you imagine refusing is the honourable course, consider: the man who copies it keeps his commission, his wages, and his usefulness to the twelve families who will want to know, someday, where their sons are buried. The man who refuses contributes nothing to anyone. Which of us, Vane, is serving the dead then?"

They weren't dead. That was the thing Elias's mind kept snagging on, the loose thread pulling the whole argument apart. *They aren't dead.* But he was twenty-six years old and cold and afraid, and the arithmetic Hargreave offered was seductive in the way all cowardly arithmetic is seductive: sign now, fix it later, survive tonight.

He copied it.

He remembered everything about the copying. The candle guttering. His hand so steady it seemed to belong to another man. The moment at the bottom where the colonel said, "*Sign* the fair copy, Mr. Vane — your signature attests the record," and Elias signed, and his signature looked exactly like his signature always looked, and that was the horror of it. Nothing changed. The same loop, the same slant. The lie fit his hand like a glove he'd been making all his life without knowing.

Then he entered it in the campaign journal, blotted it, and filed it, and walked out into the dark, and was sick in a snowdrift.

* * *

One man escaped.

Elias learned it two days later, and he learned it the way clerks learn things — sideways, through paperwork. A prisoner escort return came through showing twelve men taken, twelve confined, and a note appended in the adjutant's hand: *one (Capt., Retaken?) — report.* The barn had a broken rear window. The sentry had been changed at the wrong hour. And Captain Aldous Reeve, who had stopped a mutiny with his bare authority and been condemned for it, was gone into a countryside of snow and informers, carrying nothing but a greatcoat and whatever papers he'd had in his coat when they arrested him.

The hunt for Reeve was never announced. Instead Elias was given a different task, and this one he understood instantly, because it was the same task as before, refined.

"Update the rolls," the adjutant said. "All twelve. Struck off strength — deserted, 14th instant. Note the executions per the colonel's order of the 15th." A pause. "For the captain also. Consistency, Vane. If the record says twelve, the record says twelve."

So Elias Vane sat down and, in his beautiful, damning hand, killed Captain Aldous Reeve on paper. Entered the date. Entered the method. Watched the ink dry on a man walking alive somewhere through the snow, and understood, finally and completely, what he had learned to suspect on the winter march:

The paper did not record the army. The paper replaced it. Whatever Elias wrote was the truth, now, officially, forever — and the living man in the snow was the error.

He finished the entries. Then, alone in the tent after midnight, he did the one small thing he could not explain even to himself, the thing that would cost him everything: he made a second, private copy. Not of the false order. Of the true one. The names as he'd known them, in the order they'd been arrested, with Foss at the top and Reeve at the bottom. He wrote in the margin, very small: *Not dead. Misfiled.*

It wasn't courage. Courage would have been refusing at midnight in the warm room. This was just a clerk's instinct — the conviction that somewhere, someday, an audit comes due, and God help the man whose books don't balance.

* * *

Three weeks later, gaunt as famine itself, Reeve found him.

Not dramatically. Elias stepped out of the records tent at dusk and there was a scarecrow by the woodpile wearing a dead soldier's coat, and the scarecrow said, "Mr. Vane," in the voice of a closing door, and Elias's knees nearly went.

Reeve was thinner, frost-burned, hunted. He had been moving between farmsteads, and he had done the thing Elias could never have imagined doing: he had written. Letters — drafted in hiding, fair-copied in stolen hours, dated and signed in a hand the army had declared dead. Letters describing the rations fraud, naming figures Elias recognized because Elias had reconciled them himself. Letters describing Vosk Bridge honestly, including the part where Captain Reeve stopped it. Letters stating plainly that twelve men lived in a barn awaiting a murder dressed up as justice.

"I can't deliver these," Reeve said. "I'm a dead man, Vane. Dead men's letters are intercepted by definition — anyone hunting me watches the post. But a paymaster's clerk travels. Requisitions, escort returns, casualty confirmations." He held out the satchel. "Nobody watches a clerk."

"They'll watch me if they find these."

"They're watching everyone," Reeve said. "That's rather the point. The question is whether anyone carries the truth past them." He almost smiled; it was terrible on his frost-cracked face. "You signed my death warrant, Mr. Vane. I'm told that's the custom. Very well — now carry my appeal."

Elias took the satchel. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

He left the column that night with forged travel authority in his pocket — his own forgery this time, honest work for once — and a plan with one address at the end of it: Samuel Okafor, war correspondent, currently at Thorn Mill, beyond three checkpoints and a river that would soon swallow its own ice.

Eleven names in his head. One satchel on his shoulder. And somewhere behind him, in a stone barn by a bridge, twelve men who did not officially exist, counting down the days until the official record and the actual world collided — and knowing, the way Elias knew, that the paper intended to win.

He was three days into the frozen country when the fever took him, and six days when he collapsed at a dressing station's sentry line, refusing to let go of the satchel even as his legs refused him.

The last thing he registered before the dark came up to meet him was a nurse's voice, tired and exact, saying:

"He's not to be searched yet. Help me carry him in."

*Yet*, Elias thought, from underneath the snow. *She said yet.*

Then the ice of his own body closed over, and the satchel — and the eleven names, and the dead man's living hand — passed, for the moment, out of his keeping and into hers.
