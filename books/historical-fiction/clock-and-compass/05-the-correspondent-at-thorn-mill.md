## 5. The Correspondent at Thorn Mill

He was well enough to sit up by the third night, which meant he was well enough to be dangerous, and Mara had known it since the moment she'd pulled his boots off and found army issue stamped inside the tongues.

She came to him after the last lamp was trimmed, when the ward sank into that particular silence of men sleeping the sleep of the mutilated—heavy, animal, grateful. She set her candle on the crate beside his cot and did not sit down. Sitting down would mean staying. Staying would mean listening. And she had already decided, somewhere in the last three days of changing his dressings and counting his pulse, that whatever Elias Vane was about, she did not want to know it.

"You're leaving tomorrow," she said. "Your fever's broken. Sergeant Pryce can walk you to the road."

"I can't walk to the road." He said it without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "I have to walk sixty miles east, past three checkpoints, to a place called Thorn Mill. And I need you to come with me."

Mara picked up her candle.

"Don't," he said. Not urgently. Almost gently. "Don't do the thing where you become furniture. I've watched you do it for three days. You do it beautifully. Better than any clerk I ever knew, and I've known some very good ones. But I need two minutes of you not being furniture, and then you can go back to it and I'll never mention any of this again."

The candle guttered. Somewhere down the row a man groaned and subsided. Mara stood very still, and hated him a little for how precisely he had named her.

"Two minutes," she said.

* * *

He laid it out the way a paymaster's clerk would lay out accounts, because that was what he was, whatever else the calluses on his palms said—numbers first, then the arithmetic of risk, then the sum at the bottom that nobody wanted to read.

"Thorn Mill. It's a paper mill on the Dore, thirty miles past the second army boundary. There's a man there named Samuel Okafor. He's a correspondent—the foreign papers carry his dispatches under a pen name so the provosts can't hang him for them. He's been writing about this campaign for eight months. He writes about supply lines and frostbite casualties and the price of cavalry horses. He has never once written about what happens to the names on the rolls." Elias's voice stayed level, clerk-flat. "Because he doesn't have proof. Rumors travel faster than armies but they die on the road. Paper is different. Paper with signatures, paper with dates, paper in an official hand—that crosses borders. That gets read aloud in coffee houses in cities Hargreave has never seen and cannot court-martial."

"And you think my ledger—"

"I think your ledger is the only document in this entire theater that tells the truth continuously. Reeve's letters tell one story: twelve men, one bridge, one colonel's lie. Yours tells twenty. Forty. However many nights you've sat awake copying names out of fires." He paused. "How many?"

Mara said nothing. The number lived in her head the way a prayer lives in the head, unspoken because speaking it might break whatever compact kept it safe. One hundred and nine. She had counted again two nights ago, after copying Private Wendell's name by the light of a shuttered lantern while the officer who'd ordered his effects burned slept forty feet away.

"That many," Elias said, reading her face. "Good. Okafor will weep."

"Elias." She used his name deliberately, and saw him flinch slightly—he'd offered her *Vane* and she had refused it, and now she took the other thing too. "Do you understand what you're asking? It's sixty miles. Three checkpoints. And every checkpoint has a book of its own—a pass book, a casualty book, a deserter notice board with faces chalked on it. Mine may already be chalked."

"It isn't. I looked."

"When?"

"The second night. While you were sleeping. The notice board at the south sentry line has four faces on it and none of them are yours." He almost smiled. "You're very good at not existing, Miss Montrose. I mean it as the highest compliment."

"And when this is done?" She kept her voice below the snoring of the man in the next cot. "When Okafor prints his dispatch and London reads it over breakfast—what do you imagine happens to the nurse whose handwriting is all over the evidence? Do you think Hargreave's friends will want her alive to give interviews? Exposure means the gallows. For me it isn't a metaphor. It's a rope with my name on the ticket."

"Yes," Elias said.

That was all. No argument, no softening. *Yes.* And something in the flatness of it undid her more than persuasion could have, because it meant he had done the same arithmetic she had and written the same terrible number at the bottom, and he was asking her anyway.

* * *

She made him wait through the morning rounds. It was a cruelty and she knew it—making him lie there watching her pack wounds and change poultices and write the hourly report in her small invisible hand—but she needed the time, and she needed him to see what the ward was. What she was, when she was being herself instead of furniture.

Forty-one men. She knew all their names, though the roll knew only thirty-three; eight had arrived unconscious and been logged by regiment and number alone, and she had learned theirs from their own cracked lips in the night watches, and written them nowhere except behind her eyes until such time as they could be written properly. Private Anselm, both feet gone black to the ankle. Boy Corliss, seventeen, who cried for his mother in a voice that hadn't broken. The Polish trooper nobody could question, whom she'd named Janusz from the tag around his neck and who gripped her wrist whenever the fever climbed.

This was the whole of her empire. A dressing station, a linen trunk, a hundred and nine names hidden in a false bottom among the sheets. She had built it small on purpose. Small things don't get noticed. Small things survive winters.

At noon she sent the orderly away on a fabricated errand to the commissary and pulled the stool up to Elias's cot.

"Tell me about the checkpoints," she said. "All three. Don't spare me."

* * *

He told her, and she made him tell it twice, because the first telling had gaps where he was protecting her.

The first checkpoint sat at the Weir Bridge, a toll post manned by a sergeant and six men who searched every cart for liquor and deserters in that order. The second was the surgeon's table at Kesselhof—a medical cordon, actually, established after the typhus scare, where every traveler east of the river was examined by an army surgeon with authority to detain anyone showing marks of infection, and *marks* had a way of meaning anything a nervous surgeon wanted it to mean. The third was Fort Marrow, the provost's own gate, where the notices were chalked and the passes were checked against three separate ledgers and the commandant answered directly to Colonel Hargreave's desk.

"Three gates," Mara said. "And behind the third one, a man who knows a satchel is missing."

"Hargreave doesn't know yet what's in the satchel. If he did, we'd already be dead. What he knows is that a clerk who should be dead walked into your ward carrying something he wants back. That buys us days. Days, not weeks."

"And the ice."

Elias turned his head toward the narrow window, where the light came gray through frosted glass. "The Dore breaks in the first week of March most years. Some years late February. When it goes, the ford at Thorn Mill goes under and the mill road becomes the far side of a river nobody crosses until the water drops in April. Okafor stays at the mill through the thaw because it's the one place the provosts can't reach him without crossing open country in mud to their saddle girths." He looked back at her. "After the ice breaks, the letters stop being urgent and start being old news. Hargreave will have hanged his twelve witnesses by then and filed the paperwork, and Okafor will be printing history instead of stopping it."

"How long do we have?"

"Nineteen days. Perhaps twenty-two."

Mara sat with that. Nineteen days, three gates, sixty miles of frozen road, and at the end of it a stranger with a printing press who could turn her quiet hundred and nine names into a scandal that would echo for a decade—and turn her, in the same motion, into the woman the army would hunt for that decade.

There was a version of her that said no. She could see that woman clearly, the way she could see the ward in the dark: the woman who finished the winter, burned nothing, told nothing, buried her hundred and ninth name and her hundred and tenth in silence, and walked out in the spring with her discharge papers and her hands clean-ish and her ledger still under the linen, a secret she would keep until it curdled into shame or rotted into nothing. That woman survived. Mara had spent three years becoming her, and the becoming had cost enough that she was reluctant to spend it all in one gesture on a gaunt stranger and a dead captain's letters.

But.

But she had copied Private Wendell's name two nights ago while his effects went into the fire, and she had felt—not grief, grief was old work—she had felt the ledger getting heavier. Every name added weight. She had always assumed the weight was hers to carry indefinitely, like a mule assumes the panniers. It had never occurred to her until this gaunt man crawled through her sentry line that weight could also be *delivered*. That there might be somewhere to put it down.

"Okafor," she said. "What kind of man pays for a room at a paper mill and calls it journalism?"

"The best kind and the worst kind. He'll use you, Miss Montrose. He'll take your ledger and your testimony and he'll print every word, and he won't consult you about the timing or the framing, and if the story serves better with your name in it than out of it, your name goes in." Elias said it steadily, and she understood that this, too, was part of his honesty—he was not recruiting her, he was disclosing terms. "I'd rather you hate him going in than find out halfway."

"And you? What does Elias Vane get out of it besides a rope?"

For the first time since he'd arrived, he looked away from her. "A clerk who wrote one lie for a colonel gets to write one truth for himself. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances. But it's what I've got."

* * *

She went to the linen trunk at dusk, when the ward's stove needed feeding and the orderly was busy and nobody looks twice at a nurse hauling linens.

The false bottom was her own carpentry, crude but sound—a slat an inch above the true floor, lapped at the joints, weighted with folded sheets. She lifted it and there was the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth exactly as the letters in his satchel were wrapped, and she thought: we were always going to find each other, this clerk and I. People who wrap paper in oilcloth recognize their own kind across a winter.

She didn't take it out. She rested her hand on it through the cloth, feeling the corners, the slight swell of a season's worth of nights, and she asked herself the real question, the one underneath the checkpoints and the ice and the gallows.

Not *will I die for this.* She had watched enough men do arithmetic with their lives to know that fear wasn't the obstacle; fear was manageable, fear was just weather. The real question was smaller and worse:

*If I don't carry it, who will?*

And the answer came back with the certainty of a name on a casualty roll: nobody. Nobody else had the names. Nobody else had sat close enough to hear the omissions, night after night, month after month. The ledger existed because she existed, and if she chose invisibility now, the truth would stay folded under linens in a freezing hut until the hut burned or she died, and Private Wendell and his hundred and eight companions would remain what Hargreave's paperwork said they were: nobody, never enlisted, never dead, never here.

Mara Montrose had been invisible for three years by discipline and talent. She had told herself it was service. Tonight, holding the weight of it through a layer of oilcloth, she admitted the other half: it had also been safety, and safety was a bill that was coming due.

She rewrapped the ledger, closed the slat, and went back to the ward.

Elias was awake, propped on one elbow, watching the door. He didn't ask. He had the clerk's patience; he knew how to let an entry post itself.

"Conditions," Mara said, sitting down on the stool, and this time she sat all the way down, which meant she was staying. "First: my ledger travels with me or not at all. It doesn't leave my body. Not in your satchel, not in a cart, not in your clever pockets. Second: if we're taken, I'm a nurse treating a patient I found on the road, and you'll confirm it, and the satchel was stolen from you at the sentry line by persons unknown. Third—" she heard her own voice steady itself, and let it, "—when this is done, whatever happens, I write the ending myself. My record ends in my hand or it doesn't end at all."

Elias studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men acknowledge terms they intend to keep.

"There's a fourth condition," Mara said.

"Name it."

"We leave before dawn. And Sergeant Pryce knows nothing, the orderly knows nothing, and Boy Corliss—" her throat tightened unexpectedly, and she mastered it, "—Corliss gets his morphine dosed for six days before we go. He'll need it whether I'm here or not."

"He'll miss you."

"Everyone in this ward will miss me, and none of them will know it. That's what invisibility is." She stood, and took the candle up, and paused at the foot of his cot. "Sleep, Mr. Vane. Sixty miles starts in the dark."

But when she reached her own cot in the alcove behind the storeroom, she didn't sleep. She lay listening to the wind work the shutters and, beneath it, fainter, the sound she'd been refusing to hear all day: the river, two hundred yards off, muttering under its ice. Not cracking yet. Not breaking. Just talking to itself in the dark, counting down the nineteen days.

And at some hour past midnight, a different sound reached her—hooves on the north road, more than one horse, moving fast toward headquarters and slowing as they came. Dragoons riding at midnight carried orders.

Mara lay still in the dark and thought: *the hunter is already mounted.*

She got up and began packing the linens.
