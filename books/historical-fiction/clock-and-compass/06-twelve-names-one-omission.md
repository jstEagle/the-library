## 6. Twelve Names, One Omission

The dressing station after midnight was a different country. By day it belonged to the wounded—to the moaning and the sawing and the smell of carbolic fighting a losing war against gangrene—but at night it belonged to Mara. To the candle stub she wasn't issued. To the ledger she didn't officially possess.

She waited until Elias's breathing evened out in the corner cot they'd given him, until the sentry outside completed the far end of his circuit, and only then did she kneel at the linen trunk in the supply room, working her fingers through frozen canvas to the false bottom she'd built with a heated knife two winters ago.

The ledger came up wrapped in an old altar cloth she'd traded a sergeant three cups of gin for. She hadn't asked him where a fighting man got an altar cloth. Some questions you learned not to ask.

She carried it back to her corner of the ward, set the candle where her body would block its light from the doorway, and opened to the page she'd been avoiding since Elias said the name.

*Reeve, Aldous, Capt.*

Her own handwriting. Her own ink, mixed from powder because the army never issued enough. Dated the fourteenth of November.

*Reeve, Aldous, Capt. — died of wounds, femoral, post-op day two. Buried in the orchard east of the mill. effects to regimental store.*

She had written that. She had watched them sew him into his blanket—she'd done half the stitching herself, her fingers stiff with cold—and she had written him down the way she wrote all of them down: cleanly, without flourish, without comment. A name, a cause, a place. That was the whole liturgy of her ledger. The dead got their names kept when the rolls would not keep them. It wasn't much. It had seemed like everything once.

And now a living man wearing that dead man's handwriting sat twelve feet away from her, sleeping off a fever, and the page under her thumb had become either a mistake or a lie, and Mara Montrose could not bear for it to be either.

She counted.

It was not a thing she did deliberately. Counting was what her hands did when her mind refused to sit still—the way other women plaited and unplaited their hair. Eleven entries on the spread before her, eleven names taken from Elias's halting dictation two nights ago, cross-checked against the burial lists and the casualty returns she'd copied over the autumn:

*Brennan, T. Pvt.*
*Cole, Wm. Pvt.*
*Dunmore, A. Lc.-Cpl.*
*Ferris, J. Pvt.*
*Gow, H. Pvt.*
*Hollis, R. Pvt.*
*Ivans, P. Pvt.*
*Jarrow, S. Pvt.*
*Kessler, O. Pvt.*
*Lynd, F. Pvt.*
*Mabry, C. Sgt.*

Eleven. She read the names forward and backward. She checked the dates. She matched each against the official roll's terse verdicts—*deserted, 19 Dec.* *Deserted, 19 Dec.* *Executed, 20 Dec.*—the army's tidy fiction that eleven men had all deserted on the same December morning, in the same direction, in the same numbers, like birds deciding together to leave one tree.

Elias had said twelve. Twelve accused at Vosk Bridge. Twelve struck from the rolls in Colonel Hargreave's own hand.

She counted again, as if arithmetic were a witness that could be sworn twice.

Twelve minus Reeve made eleven. The eleven she had. So the record was complete—

Except it wasn't, and she knew it before she finished the thought, because completeness had never been the point. She had *Brennan* from the burial detail at Kolvic, a boy of maybe nineteen shot through the chest, whose name no officer had spoken aloud. She had *Mabry* because Sergeant Mabry's effects came through her station and a corporal told her, laughing, that the old man had deserted with a bad heart. She had every one of these eleven because someone had died near enough for Mara to catch the name as it fell.

She had none of them for Reeve.

What she had for Reeve was worse than nothing. She had a fabricated death—a death she had *witnessed*, or believed she had witnessed, or been shown by men whose business was making deaths look real—and the date, and the invented orchard, and the lie flowing out of her own pen onto her own paper. Somewhere in that week of November, Captain Aldous Reeve had walked into her dressing station as a corpse and walked out of her ledger as a fiction, and she had never once questioned it, because questioning was not what invisible people did.

Her omission. Hers.

Not the army's. Not Hargreave's. Not the clerk who'd stamped the return. *Hers.* She had built this book on a single rule—that every name struck from the official record would be entered in hers—and on the fourteenth of November she had broken the rule without knowing it, because she had recorded a death instead of doubting one.

The candle guttered. She trimmed the wick with her thumbnail and made herself look at the entry a moment longer.

Then she heard the cot creak.

"I can hear you writing," Elias said, from the dark. "You press too hard. You always press too hard."

"You're meant to be asleep."

"I've been asleep three days. I'm tired of it." He sat up slowly, the way he did everything now, as though his body were a debt being paid in installments. In the low light he looked less like a paymaster's clerk than he had the first night—which was correct, since he wasn't one—and more like what he actually was: a soldier gone soft in an office, then hardened again by whatever winter had done to him between Vosk Bridge and her sentry line. "Is that it? The book?"

"It's a linen inventory."

"Madam, I have seen your linen inventory. You recorded a man's name off a boot tag last Tuesday while pretending to count bandages."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she turned the ledger around and held it toward him, open to the page, and watched his face do the arithmetic hers had already done.

"Eleven," he said.

"There should be twelve."

"No." He leaned close, finger hovering above the column without touching it, a clerk's instinct not to smudge another man's ink even now. "There should be twelve *accused*. But Reeve escaped before—" He stopped. He looked up at her, and something in her expression must have stopped him too. "What?"

"Read my entry for Reeve."

He found it. She watched him read it once fast, then again slowly, the second read the way clerks read—checking the date against something internal, some other document in his memory. When he looked up his face had gone the color of the snow outside.

"The fourteenth of November," he said. "Died of wounds. Buried—"

"In an orchard east of a mill. I stitched his shroud myself. Or I stitched what I was told was him." She kept her voice level. Level was the discipline; level was the whole craft of her life. "They brought him through at dusk. Two stretcher-bearers I knew by sight. A surgeon's note. The wound was—" She stopped, and for the first time in years her voice found a crack and went through it. "The face was covered. We cover the dead. It is done out of respect, and it is done so that the exhausted stop looking at them, and it is done because nobody imagines the army has any reason to lie about which man is dead."

Elias said nothing for a long time. The stove ticked. Out along the perimeter, the sentry's boots crunched, paused, crunched on.

"They faked it," he said finally. "A staged death. That's why he could move afterward—no one hunts a buried man. God." He passed a hand across his eyes. "I processed the paperwork for his actual death sentence, madam. I sat at that desk and copied out the charges in fair hand, and all the while there was a version of him already dead on paper, traveling ahead of the truth like an outrider. No wonder Hargreave's books balance. He keeps two sets of everything."

"One of them is mine," Mara said, "and mine is wrong."

"It's not—"

"Eleven names, Mr. Vane. I have eleven names and one lie. Do you understand what that means?" She heard her own voice rise and forced it back down below the sleeping men's breathing. "For six years I have kept this book on one rule. Every name they strike, I enter. Not to change anything—I am not a fool, I know what one woman's ink does against a colonel's signature—but because somewhere there ought to be a true accounting. Somebody has to hold the number. And the first time the number matters—truly matters, when eleven men are waiting for the gallows on the strength of a fiction—my own page betrays them. I certified a dead man. I am part of his murder already. If they shoot Reeve at that bridge, I helped kill him in November."

"You didn't know—"

"Knowing is not the unit of guilt. Entry is." She closed the ledger. Her hand stayed flat upon the cover, as if it might otherwise fly open again and shame them both. "I wrote him into the ground, Mr. Vane. Now I will write him out of it."

* * *

He let the silence stand for a while. She respected that; it was more than most men managed. When he spoke again he had pulled his blanket around his shoulders and come to sit on the floor beside her trunk, so that they were both crouched in candle-shadow like conspirators, which was, she supposed, precisely what they were.

"You said before you wouldn't go," he said. "To Thorn Mill. Three checkpoints, the ice road, Hargreave's provosts on every road with our descriptions in their pockets. You said exposure means the gallows for you too, and you were right, and I told you I'd go alone. I'm telling you again."

"And I'm answering differently tonight. I'll go."

His head came up.

"On conditions," she said.

"Name them."

"The letters travel, yes. Okafor gets his dispatches, yes. But my ledger travels with them—not as evidence for a journalist to quote, but as a complete record. All twelve. Corrected." She unlocked her fingers, forced them still. "Which means before we reach the first checkpoint, you are going to tell me everything about Aldous Reeve that a ledger should contain. Full name as enlisted. Age. Where he was born, if you know it. The wound he truly carries or doesn't. The date the mutiny occurred, the exact wording of the charge, the exact wording of Hargreave's order, and the names of the officers who signed it. I will know how he was struck from the rolls and when, and I will strike the false entry myself, in my own hand, marked *corrected*, with the true account beside it."

"That's dangerous to write down."

"Everything worth writing is."

"It's more than dangerous. If we're taken—if that book is found on you—they won't even need the letters. A nurse carrying a list of the condemned men's names, with corrections to the regimental record? They'll hang you without trial and call it sanitation."

"Then I'll be hanged holding an accurate book," Mara said, "instead of an inaccurate one. I have thought about dying a great deal, Mr. Vane. It comes with the work. What I have never once been able to stomach is dying misinformed."

Something changed in his face then—not fear, not gratitude, something quieter and rarer. He looked at her the way starving men look at bread they'd stopped believing in.

"You want the truth even when it costs you," he said. "Even when it indicts you."

"Especially then. Truth that only flatters is just gossip with better manners."

He laughed—a short, cracked sound, quickly muffled, the laugh of a man who hadn't used the machinery in months. Then he grew serious again, and he reached into the breast of his shirt and brought out something she had not seen before: a small notebook, water-spotted, swollen with damp, tied shut with waxed thread.

"What's that?"

"My working book. Everything I couldn't put in official copy. Dates, times, weather, who signed what, what Hargreave ate the week his men starved—it's all in here. I've carried it since Vosk Bridge. I slept with it inside my coat through two searches." He set it on the floor between them like a man laying down a card he'd been hiding the whole game. "Between yours and mine, we nearly have the whole story. Your book holds the dead and the disappeared. Mine holds the orders that disappeared them."

"Then why did you wait three days to show me?"

"Because until tonight I thought you were keeping a diary." His mouth twisted. "Because I've spent half a year trusting no one, and trust, madam, turns out to be like rations—you start generous and learn to count. And because—" he hesitated, and she watched the cost of honesty move across his face like weather, "—because I forged part of Hargreave's order. The date of execution. There, it's said. If we're caught, my testimony damns me exactly as far as it damns him. I wanted you to agree to this journey believing I was clean."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Six years of triage had taught her to read faces faster than words, and what she read there was not a confession offered for absolution. It was a confession offered as currency—all he had—and presented anyway.

"Then we're both compromised," she said. "Good. I'd distrust a pure man on principle. Pure men always want someone else to do the dying." She untied the waxed thread on his notebook, opened her ledger to the Reeve page, and set the two side by side in the candlelight. "Now. From the top. Born name, enlisted name, every date you have. We don't sleep until the twelfth name is true."

* * *

They worked through the small hours, and it was the strangest surgery she had ever performed: not flesh but record, cutting away a lie and closing the wound with fact. Elias dictated; she wrote. His voice was flat and precise at first, a clerk's voice, but somewhere past midnight it began to break its banks—when he gave her Reeve's age (thirty-one), when he described the starvation rations at Vosk Bridge (four ounces of biscuit, and Hargreave's own table set with fresh meat the same evening), when he recited the eleven names in order and she entered them beside her originals and found that every one of his matched every one of hers, independently gathered, two witnesses to the same crime without ever having stood in the same room.

When they reached the false entry itself, she took a breath she felt all the way to her spine, drew a single line through *died of wounds, femoral* —through her own hand, her own certainty, her own November self—and wrote beneath it:

*Entry false. Subject alive at time of writing. See corrected account. — M.M.*

"Signed it," Elias observed quietly.

"I sign everything. It's the whole difference between a rumor and a record."

He looked at her then the way she suspected she'd been looked at perhaps twice in her life, and she understood that something had been built in this freezing room tonight, something neither of them had planned. Not romance—there was no warmth in the world left over for that, and no time. Something more practical and more permanent. Two people who had separately decided that the truth deserved a custodian, discovering they were no longer alone in the office.

"We leave tomorrow night," Elias said, folding the corrected page carefully. "Before dawn, on the ice-road side. Thorn Mill is four days' march if the weather holds and two checkpoints fewer if it doesn't."

"Then I have one task before we sleep."

"Another condition?"

"A letter." She pulled a sheet of paper toward her, the cheap grey army stock, and dipped her pen. "If we don't reach Okafor—if the book burns or the river takes us or Hargreave's dragoons find us in a ditch—someone must know what we attempted and why. I'm writing it tonight, sealed, and leaving it with Mrs. Hobb at the laundry, with instructions it goes to any chaplain not of our regiment if I don't reclaim it within ten days."

"You think of everything."

"I bury everything, Mr. Vane. Thinking is the least of it." She began to write—*To whomever finds this; the following is a true accounting…*—and had gotten three lines down when the door of the ward swung open without a knock.

Sergeant Brice stood in the frame, lamp raised, snow melting off his shoulders, and behind him the night wind came in like a debt collector.

"Nurse Montrose." His voice was careful, official, wrong. "Don't get up. Colonel Hargreave's compliments. He requests your presence at the staff house tomorrow—at your convenience, he says." A pause, thin as ice before it takes weight. "He's got the gout, he says. Wants a proper professional opinion."

He said *says* the way a man repeats a phrase he's been instructed to use exactly.

"He also asks"—Brice's lamp shifted, and its light crossed the corner cot, the rumpled blanket, the satchel-straps visible under Elias's coat where he had pretended to sleep through all of it—"whether your patient, the clerk, is fit to travel. Seems there's some question about a missing satchel, and the Colonel thinks your clerk might know where it went."

The door closed. The lamp-light went away across the snow. Mara sat with her pen still wet, three lines into a letter meant for a world after her capture, and across the ward Elias met her eyes in the dark.

Four days to Thorn Mill. One night to spare.

Hargreave had just spent theirs first.
