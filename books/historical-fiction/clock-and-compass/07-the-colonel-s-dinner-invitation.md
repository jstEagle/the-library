## 7. The Colonel's Dinner Invitation

The summons arrived at dusk, carried by a corporal with frost in his eyebrows and a paper he would not let Mara hold.

"Colonel Hargreave requests the nurse Montrose. Eight o'clock. His quarters." He looked her up and down the way men looked at baggage—assessing weight, not worth. "You'll come clean. Hands washed. He's particular."

Mara wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though they were already raw from scrubbing. "Requests," she said. "Not orders?"

The corporal's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "It's the same thing here, miss."

She watched him go, then stood in the doorway of the dressing station letting the cold find her face. Behind her, the ward breathed—forty men breathing in different rhythms, some of them the ragged shallow pull of men who would not see morning. Among them, under a blanket in the corner cot nearest the stove, Elias Vane slept with one arm curled around an empty space where a satchel was not. She had moved it hours ago, before the fever took him properly, wrapped it in oilcloth and lowered it into the linen trunk beneath the burial sheets, beneath the folded flags, beneath everything.

*He knows about the satchel.*

No. He couldn't. Nobody knew. She had told no one, not even—

But the corporal had said *requests*, and requests were what powerful men made when they wanted to watch your face while you answered.

"Tamsin," she said quietly, and the girl materialized from between two cots where she'd been pretending to sort bandages. Tamsin had appeared at the station three weeks ago with a limp and a talent for stealing that she wore as openly as other girls wore ribbons. "Watch him. If he wakes, he's to drink this—" she pressed a cup into the girl's hands, willow bark and laudanum, mostly laudanum, "—all of it, even if he argues. Especially if he argues."

"Where are you going?"

"To wash my hands for the colonel."

Tamsin's eyes went wide and knowing in a way that made Mara want to lie to her, which she wouldn't do, because lying to children was how adults taught them to expect lies. "Should I be frightened for you?"

"No," Mara said, and began unpinning her apron. "You should be frightened for him. Gout is very painful when nobody sympathizes."

That wasn't true, and they both knew it. But the girl grinned, and Mara put on her second-best collar, and went to treat a gouty colonel the way she treated any other wound: carefully, without flinching, and with her fingers counting everything they touched.

* * *

Hargreave's quarters occupied the front room of a farmhouse whose owners had been evacuated—or not evacuated; Mara had learned not to assume—two days after the regiment arrived. There was real glass in the windows, double-paned, and a fire that had been burning long enough to dry the air into a crackle. A table had been set for dinner. Two places. The plates were silver.

The colonel sat in a chair with his right foot propped on a stool, boot off, foot wrapped in linen gone faintly yellow at the toes. He was perhaps fifty, heavy through the shoulders in the way of a man once athletic, with the kind of face that had been handsome long enough to grow entitled to it. His eyes were pale gray and entirely awake.

"Nurse Montrose." He did not rise. Men like him never rose; rising was a concession. "Sit. Eat. You look like you've been eating hospital food, which is a form of punishment I didn't authorize."

"I ate at six, sir. Thank you." She sat anyway, because refusing the chair would have been a statement, and statements were currency she could not afford to spend. Her hands folded in her lap. Clean, still, empty.

"A man who eats at six." He poured wine into both glasses himself, laboring up out of the chair just enough to reach, making a small performance of the effort his foot cost him. "I like that. Discipline in small things predicts discipline in large ones. Don't you find?"

"I find that hungry people eat when there's food, sir."

He laughed—a real laugh, warm at the edges, and that frightened her more than coldness would have, because warmth meant he had decided to be charming rather than cruel, and charm was a tool with a blade in it somewhere.

"Honest, too." He pushed a glass toward her. She drank one measured sip and set it down. "Do you know why you're here, Nurse Montrose?"

"My foot, sir." She nodded at the stool. "May I?" At his gesture, she rose and knelt by the wrapped foot without touching it yet, studying it as she would study any wound. "How long has it been inflamed?"

"Three days. It came on after Vosk Bridge, if you must know. My surgeon says port wine. My surgeon is an idiot who drinks most of the port himself."

"Mm." She unwound the outer layer of linen, slow and professional, keeping her eyes on the task. "Your surgeon is correct about the port. Also about being an idiot, but that's a separate diagnosis. This is uric acid settling in the joint. It will flare whenever you're rich and well-fed, which I understand is a hardship you've borne bravely."

There was a silence with a knife in it. Then Hargreave laughed again, louder. "God. Where did they find you? You talk like a quartermaster's wife and work like a Carmelite."

"You requested me for my hands, sir. Not my conversation."

"On the contrary." All the warmth drained out of his voice so smoothly it might have been a trick of the firelight. "I requested you for your eyes. You see everything in that ward of yours. Every man who comes in, every man who goes out, every name on every list. I imagine you know my wounded better than their own mothers do."

She finished unwrapping the foot. The great toe was swollen tight and hot, the skin shining. "Their mothers generally know less," she agreed. "Mothers are rarely sent the casualty rolls."

"Ah." He shifted, wincing as the movement reached the joint. "You've seen the rolls."

"I copy them, sir. That's my employment. Names in, names out. I don't make them and I don't dispute them. Disputing them is above my pay and below my survival."

Another silence. Mara began wrapping the foot again, in fresh linen this time, with a compress of vinegar and sage she had brought in her pocket against exactly this eventuality. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and was grateful to them, the way one is grateful to a loyal dog during a house fire.

"The rolls have been giving me trouble," Hargreave said, watching her wrap. "Clerical trouble. Nothing dramatic. A name misplaced here, an entry amended there. Do you know what I mean by clerical trouble, nurse?"

"I believe I do, sir. I've treated it before. It presents as a colonel shouting at a lieutenant."

He snorted. "It does present that way." He reached for his wine and drank, and over the rim of the glass his pale eyes stayed on her hands, then traveled up to her face, unhurried, thorough. "Two nights ago a man collapsed at our southern sentry line. Frostbitten, half-dead, carrying a leather satchel. The sentries brought him to your ward. You treated him."

"Yes, sir."

"He gave his name as Vane. A paymaster's clerk, he says. Do you recall him?"

"I recall everyone, sir. It's the trade." She tied off the wrap and sat back on her heels. "Frostbite in both feet, hypothermia, a fever that broke this morning. He's weak but mending. Army boots, worn at the outer heel—he marches more than a clerk should. Calluses consistent with a musket. Either he was a soldier once or he's been impersonating one badly. In my experience, sir, most men in this army are doing one or the other."

Hargreave studied her for a long moment. "You inspected him."

"I inspect everyone. It's the trade," she repeated. "You asked me to see everything. I'm demonstrating."

"So you did. And the satchel?"

"What satchel?"

The question hung there. She kept her face arranged in its usual expression—the mild, tired blankness of a woman whose entire emotional range had been burned out of her by three winters of amputations—and she waited, hands loose in her lap, heart going hard and slow like a fist knocking on a door it doesn't want opened.

"The satchel he carried," Hargreave said. "Leather. Buckled. The sentries swear he had it when they found him. It was not among his effects when the effects were inventoried this afternoon."

"Was it inventoried by Sergeant Doyle? Because Doyle inventories pockets and calls it effects. Half the men in my ward arrive with things and leave without them, and the difference walks out in Doyle's knapsack. If the man Vane had valuables, ask Doyle. Ask whoever dragged him in last. Ask the man who died beside him in the third cot—dead men lose everything, sir, including the shirts off their backs, and I have buried more confiscated shirts than I care to count."

She heard herself and marveled a little: every word true, none of them useful, all of them aimed slightly past the target so that he would have to choose which deflection to chase. This was the whole art of invisibility—not hiding, but offering so much honest surface that nothing underneath seemed worth digging for.

Hargreave's expression did not change. That was the trouble with clever men; they didn't change, they only deepened.

"Doyle has been searched," he said mildly. "So has the ward. So, this evening, will be every trunk and box in this hospital, beginning with the linens."

The word *linens* landed in her chest like a swallowed stone. She felt it drop, cold, all the way down. But her face held, because her face had been drilled by worse than this—by surgeons asking her to hold dying men still, by officers reading casualty lists aloud while wives listened at tents.

"Good," she said. "There's a private of the 33rd who died owing me a pair of scissors. Perhaps you'll recover them."

He laughed, and this time the laugh had calculation in it, the sound of a man updating his ledger. "You're wasted here, Montrose. Truly. If circumstances were different I'd have you running my records department."

"How flattering, sir. Records departments have chairs."

"And longer life expectancy." He leaned back, flexing the wrapped foot experimentally, and winced. "That feels better. What did you use?"

"Sage and vinegar. And less port." She gathered her things. "I'll leave a preparation with instructions. Your surgeon can read them aloud to you nightly, like scripture."

"Nurse." Her hand was on the door. "One more matter of clerical trouble, since you have such a gift for it." His voice had gone pleasant again, terribly pleasant. "Captain Reeve's effects. You handled those, I understand. After the bridge."

The room got very quiet. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a horse stamped.

"I recorded his death, sir," Mara said, turning back. "October the fourteenth. Fever, per the regimental return. I entered him in the burial ledger myself." Every syllable of it true. She had dug the grave—small, because the coffin had been short, because the man inside it had been a boy of nineteen wearing Reeve's name on a tag swapped in a card game, and she had known that too, and written it nowhere.

"Did you," said Hargreave softly. "And you'd stand by that entry. Under oath, say. Before a board of inquiry."

"I stand by every entry I've ever made, Colonel. It's why my ledgers are trusted and my opinions are not."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—she thought he would push. She watched him decide. She watched vanity, gout, wine, and the sheer inconvenience of suspecting a woman who bandaged his foot all weigh themselves against whatever suspicion was actually coiling in him, and she watched suspicion lose by a nose.

"Go home to your ward, nurse," he said. "Give my compliments to the clerk Vane. Tell him the army takes care of its clerks."

She walked out through the cold with her breath standing in the air in front of her, and only when she was beyond the light of the farmhouse windows did she allow her hands to shake.

* * *

Elias was awake when she returned, propped on one elbow, gray-faced and sweating, listening while she told it all in a low continuous stream—the dinner, the wine, the questions, and then the sentence that mattered: *every trunk and box in this hospital, beginning with the linens.*

He tried to sit up. She pushed him flat with two fingers against his sternum, the same motion she used on men who tried to stand on newly stitched legs.

"They'll find it tonight," he rasped. "The trunk—"

"Will be empty within the hour. Tamsin's already moving it." The girl, in fact, was gone, having left with a bundle of "soiled dressings for burning" twenty minutes before Mara set out for the farmhouse. The oilskin was now inside a frozen manure heap behind the far stable, which no soldier in any army ever invented would search twice. "You've been asleep for a day, Vane. Things moved. That's what we do here."

He stared at her. "You sat at that man's table. You touched his foot. And he sat there telling you about a missing satchel, and you—you offered to help him find your scissors."

"Invisibility isn't cowardice. It's armor. It works until the day it doesn't, and tonight it worked." She sat down heavily on the edge of the next cot and, for just a moment, let her hands shake where he could see them. Only him. "He mentioned Reeve."

Elias went very still.

"He asked whether I'd stand by your captain's death entry under oath, before a board of inquiry. Nobody convenes a board of inquiry over a dead man unless somebody claims he isn't dead. Which means someone else knows. Or he suspects it and wants to know how far the knowledge has traveled." She looked at the fire, at the men sleeping in its glow, at the eleven—no, twelve—names living now in a manure heap behind a stable. "He also said to give you his compliments. That the army takes care of its clerks."

Elias made a sound that was half laugh and entirely fear. "That's a threat dressed in manners."

"That's Hargreave's whole wardrobe." She stood, straightened her apron, and became again the mild tireless woman whom no one would ever suspect of anything. "Sleep. Drink the cup. Tomorrow we begin planning in earnest, because as of eight o'clock tonight, Vane, the invisible woman has an expiration date."

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice groaned—a long cracking sigh, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

They had until it broke. Now the clock had a face, and the face was smiling at her across a dinner table, asking after his foot.
