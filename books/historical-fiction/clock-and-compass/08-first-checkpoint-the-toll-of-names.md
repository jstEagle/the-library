## 8. First Checkpoint: The Toll of Names

*Mara*

The dead rode better than the living. That was the first thing Mara learned about a burial detail: the corpses did not complain, did not shift on the cart, did not ask when the war would end. They lay in their row of six under sacking and frozen straw, and they kept their silence so completely that the living felt obliged to fill it.

"You're staring," Elias said, from the driver's bench where he sat in a dead corporal's greatcoat, two sizes too big, collar turned up against the wind.

"I'm checking the knots."

"You've checked them four times."

"And I'll check them a fifth." She climbed down from the cart before he could answer, boots crunching through the rime that had sealed the wheel ruts overnight. The road east of the dressing station ran straight as a bayonet toward the river, and three hundred yards ahead, where the road narrowed between a burned-out granary and a stand of black poplars, the first checkpoint waited. She could see the brazier's orange smear even in daylight, and the silhouette of the barrier arm, and—smaller, worse—the shape of a sergeant standing beside it with his hands clasped behind his back like a man waiting for church to let out.

They had rehearsed this. Papers first, names second, grief third if needed. Elias had the burial warrants, genuine ones—he had written enough of them in his clerk's life to know exactly how they should look, creased and blotted and slightly wine-stained—and Mara had her nurse's pass, signed by a surgeon whose handwriting no one could read, which was its own form of protection. Six bodies. A detail of two, which was thin but not unheard-of; the army was thin everywhere, stretched like tallow over too much country.

"Again," she said.

Elias sighed and recited: "We are conveying six deceased soldiers of the line to consecrated ground at Saint-Aubin chapel-yard, per warrant." He patted the satchel beneath his coat, where the papers rode flat against his chest. "Sergeant will want the count verified. He'll lift the sacking. He'll see six faces."

"He'll see six faces," Mara agreed. "And then what?"

"Then I offer him a drink from the corporal's flask, because no man refuses a dead man's flask without wondering about it for the rest of his life." Elias's mouth twitched. "And you say nothing unless he speaks to you first. You are a widow of the hospital, mourning, silent, invisible."

"I am invisible," Mara said. It was not quite a joke. Four years of dressing stations taught a woman how to make herself furniture—how to stand at the edge of an officer's vision, how to move without rustling, how to be looked at and not seen. She had spent those years turning invisibility into a trade. Now it was a weapon, and she was only beginning to learn its weight.

They walked the last stretch apart, Elias leading the horse at a funeral pace and Mara trailing a step behind with her head bowed, until the barrier came down across the road in front of them and the sergeant stepped into their path.

He was younger than his stripes suggested, broad through the shoulders, with the reddened, wind-scoured face of a man who had stood too many winters in one place. His eyes went to the cart first—it always did; everyone looked at the cart the way people look at a grave they haven't dug yet—and then to the papers Elias held out.

"Burial detail," Elias said. "Six for Saint-Aubin."

"Sergeant Bray," the man said, taking the warrant. He read it slowly, lips moving. Then he handed it back and walked the length of the cart, lifting each corner of sacking in turn, peering underneath with the detached professionalism of a man who had done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more.

Under the third corner, Mara watched his hand stop.

* * *

*Sergeant Bray had lifted a lot of sacking in his time. He'd been on checkpoint duty since September, and before that a baggage train, and before that a farm in Lincolnshire where the only thing he'd ever buried was sheep. But he knew faces now. He knew the grey of them, the slackness, the way frost made a kind of glass over the open eyes if you didn't get there fast enough.*

*This one under the third corner wasn't right. Not wrong, exactly. Just not right.*

"Corporal Whitlock," Elias said behind him. "Died of fever Tuesday last. The others are privates—Hollis, Danner, Pike, Merrick, and—" he paused, and Sergeant Bray heard the pause and understood it as grief rather than memory, which was the point of the pause, though Bray didn't know that yet, "—and young Fenn."

"Fenn," Bray repeated. Something cold walked up his spine. His grandmother, God rest her, had held that a man's name said over his body bound him to the next world, and a name unsaid left him wandering. She'd made the family speak the names of every dead animal on the farm, even the lambs. Even the crows. *Especially* the crows, she'd said, because nobody else would.

"That's the lot," Elias said.

Bray straightened up. The wind came off the river flat and hard, carrying ice-grit, and he found he wanted very much to be finished here and inside by the brazier, and also found that he couldn't be finished, not quite, not with that cold walking up his spine and his grandmother's voice in his ears saying *a name unsaid leaves him wandering.*

"You didn't say them proper," he heard himself say.

Elias blinked. "Sir?"

"The names." Bray gestured at the cart, embarrassed now, feeling the flush climb his neck. "You listed 'em off like items on a laundry docket. That's not how you say a man's name over his body. My gran—" He stopped. Sergeants did not explain their grandmothers to clerks. "It's bad luck. Saying them wrong. Worse luck not saying them at all."

Behind the clerk, the woman shifted. Small, grey-cloaked, eyes down. Mourning. Fine.

Bray waited.

* * *

*Mara*

She understood before Elias did—she saw it in the way Bray's hand kept returning to the sacking's edge, touching it like a charm, and in the way his eyes kept flicking to the bodies and away. Superstition, then. Fear of the unquiet dead. In the hospitals the orderlies had a hundred such protections: coins under tongues, boots pointed toward home, prayers muttered over amputated limbs so they wouldn't walk. Men who handled death daily built walls of ritual around themselves or broke.

Elias was reaching for the flask. Mara reached past him instead.

"Sergeant," she said, and her voice came out low and level, the voice she used over dying men. Bray turned to her with the relief of a man handed permission. "Would you help us? My colleague is a paymaster's man. He counts well but he doesn't... he never knew them."

Something eased in the sergeant's shoulders. "I'd stand witness," he said. "If it's wanted."

"It's wanted."

She took off her hood. It was a small thing, a calculated thing—let him see a woman's face, tired and ordinary, a face associated with bandages and broth and nothing dangerous—but Elias stiffened beside her and she ignored him. She moved to the head of the cart and laid her bare hand on the sacking over the first body's shoulder, the way she'd touched a hundred living shoulders to tell them the worst.

"Corporal James Whitlock," she said. "Of the Third Company. Fever. He spoke of his mother's garden the night before he went, and asked that his flask go to no one who wouldn't drink from it kindly."

Beside her, she felt Elias go very still.

She moved to the second. "Private Samuel Hollis. Fourth Company. Frostbite taken to fever. He gave his blanket away twice in the week before he died—to men still living. Remember that of him."

Third. The one Bray's hand had stopped on. "Private Ansel Danner. Second Company. Wounds of the leg. He was twenty-two years old and he wrote letters home for the men who couldn't write, and every letter he ever wrote told his family he was well."

She had never met any of these men. She had invented them out of the raw material of hundreds she *had* met—she had heard these fragments at a hundred bedsides, gathered them like coins, and now she spent them all at once, and the spending cost her something real, some part of her hoarded pity, and Bray's face was working and Elias had removed his hat and the wind was the only other sound.

"Pike," she said. "Merrick." And for Fenn, the youngest, she gave the longest story, because the young ones deserved the longest stories: a boy who had lied about his age in two counties, who had never once fired his musket at a man, who had died asking whether anyone would remember he'd been here at all.

"I'll remember," Sergeant Bray said, thickly. He said it like a vow.

Then Elias stepped forward, hat still off, and Mara saw what he was about to do half a heartbeat too late to stop it.

"There were twelve others," Elias said. "Not on this cart. Deserters, according to the rolls. Executed according to the rolls. But no one has said their names over their bodies—not properly, not once—and my grandmother—" he stumbled, improvised, "—my people hold that a name unsaid leaves a man wandering. So. Aldous Reeve. Thomas Crake. William Ferris. Joseph Mundy. Peter Lassiter. Henry Oakes. Robert Tam. James Whelding. George Ashby. Charles Dunmore. Francis Pell. And the twelfth—" his voice caught, and Mara realized with a jolt it was not entirely feigned, "—the twelfth I never learned."

Silence, except the wind.

Bray's face had gone strange. He was looking at the empty road behind the cart as if the twelve might be standing there. "Deserters," he said slowly. "Executed men don't wander, surely. They got what was coming."

"Do they?" Elias asked quietly. "Has anyone *said so* over them? Has anyone stood at a graveside and named them and closed the account?"

The sergeant's jaw worked. Mara watched superstition and discipline fight openly across his face, watched discipline lose. He took off his own cap. He mumbled something—not the twelve names, he hadn't the memory for them, but a general absolution, *all you poor souls adrift, be done and be home*—and crossed himself with a hand that shook slightly, and then put his cap back on and became a sergeant again, brusque and embarrassed.

"Road's clear," he said. "Saint-Aubin's six miles. Chapel yard's on the north side; can't miss it." He hesitated, then added, lower: "There's a second checkpoint at the toll bridge. Sergeant Odry has it. He's not..." He glanced at the cart. "He's not a believing man, if you take me. Count your names before you get there."

He raised the barrier. As the cart rolled through, Mara looked back once. Bray stood watching them go, and his lips were moving, and she would have bet her life—which was approximately what she was doing—that he was saying the names. All twelve. Over and over. Learning them.

* * *

*Elias*

*The twelfth I never learned.*

Elias drove the cart with his heart hammering and his face composed, and only when the checkpoint had shrunk to a smudge of brazier smoke behind them did he allow himself to look at Mara. She sat beside him on the bench, hood back up, hands folded, the picture of mourning—except her eyes, which were furious.

"You improvised," she said.

"You noticed."

"'My people.' You told a sergeant of the line that mutineers deserve the rites, and you attributed it to *your people.*"

"It worked."

"It worked," she agreed, "because he was a good man doing a bad job, which is the army entire. And because—" she stopped, and when she resumed, her voice was different, quieter, almost unwilling, "—because it was true. What you said. Nobody has closed their account. I have eleven names in my ledger and no graves and no words spoken over any of them, and I have been telling myself that recording them was enough. That ink was a rite." She looked at him. "It isn't, is it?"

"No," Elias said. "Ink is evidence. Words over bodies are mercy. Reeve's letters are justice. We're carrying all three, and we need all three, but only one of them will let those men rest regardless of what the court does."

Ahead, the road bent toward the river, and he could see the toll bridge now—or rather the smoke of it, hanging above the poplars. His hands were steady on the reins, which surprised him; inside, everything was the sound of ice shifting. Bray had let them through on grief and superstition and Mara's borrowed truths. Odry, the non-believer, would want papers, counts, signatures—and Elias had papers, but every document he carried was a forgery or a theft, and every mile brought them closer to a man whose job was arithmetic.

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and heavier: Bray would remember them. A clerk, a nurse, six bodies, twelve names. By nightfall the description might travel. By morning—

"Mara," he said. "When we cross the bridge. Whatever happens. Don't stop walking."

"Why?" she said. "What happens at the bridge?"

Elias watched the smoke thicken ahead and did not answer, because the honest answer was: *I don't know yet.*
