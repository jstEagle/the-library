## 9. The Ice Road

The river froze in layers, and you could hear them.

That was the first thing nobody told you about a frozen river—that it talked. Elias Vane had crossed it twice by day, in the safe gray light when the baggage trains moved and the sentries at the far bank were half asleep with cold, and even then the ice had murmured underfoot, small ticks and shivers like a man talking in his sleep. By night, with no wind and no wheels and a moon like a coin held behind cloth, every sound it made was enormous.

He stood at the reed bank at midnight and listened to it and thought: *I am going to die on top of my own lie, and the river will keep it.*

Behind him, Mara Montrose was cinching the last strap on the handcart they'd bought from a widow for more money than the widow had ever seen at once. The cart carried their disguise—two coffins, empty, lids loose, straw inside—and beneath the straw, wrapped in oilskin, the satchel. Reeve's letters. Her ledger. Twelve names that Colonel Hargreave had struck from the rolls of the living and the dead alike, filed somewhere no roll could hold them.

"Say it again," Mara said. She didn't look up from the strap. "The route."

"The ford at Sable Point is watched until the second bell. After that the picket pulls back to the fire line—they can't feel their fingers past midnight, so they stop pretending to guard anything. We cross there." He traced it in the air with his gloved hand. "Two miles of ice. Then the north bank, then the cart track to the chapel road. Third checkpoint isn't until Thorn Mill, but if we're seen on this bank before we cross, none of the rest matters."

"And if the ice fails?"

"Then we won't need papers where we're going."

She finished the strap, straightened, and looked at him the way she looked at a wound before she touched it. Assessing. Deciding whether to hurt him now or later.

"You've crossed at night before," she said.

"I've stood on this bank at night before." He pulled the cart's handle into his grip. The wood was wet and it burned cold through the wool. "Crossing's different."

*Everything is different,* he did not say. *I have been standing on banks my whole life, writing about crossings I never made.*

They went down onto the ice.

* * *

The first hundred yards were the worst, or he thought so until the next hundred proved worse still. Snow lay over the river in long pale drifts, and under the drifts the ice was any thickness God pleased. Elias walked ahead with an iron-tipped tent stake—their one concession to sense—probing each stride before he trusted it. Tap, tap, step. Tap, tap, step. Behind him the cart runners hissed, and Mara kept her hand flat against the coffin lid as though the dead inside were cargo she was steadying.

He had not told her yet. That was the arithmetic running under his feet alongside the fear of drowning. Two miles of river, and somewhere in those two miles he had to say the thing he had never said aloud, because she would learn it soon anyway—at the trial, if there was a trial; at the gallows, which was more likely—and better it came from his mouth than from Hargreave's paperwork.

He was the man who had written the order.

Not all of it. Not the decision. The decision had been Hargreave's, made in a warm tent at Vosk Bridge while eleven men sat chained in a wagon and a twelfth ran bleeding into the dark. But an order is not a decision. An order is ink. And someone had to sit at the folding desk with the frozen inkwell thawing against his knee and write out, in fair clerk's copperplate: *The twelve men condemned at Vosk Bridge, having deserted their colors in the face of the enemy, were executed pursuant to sentence of field court. Bodies buried without marker per regulation.* Dated three days before any of it happened. Signed with the flourish Elias had practiced a hundred times—Hargreave's signature, which was really just a confident-looking scar across the page, easy to learn, easier to fake once you'd copied it four thousand times as a paymaster's clerk.

Hargreave had dictated the substance. Elias had supplied the date. The date was the whole crime, because the date made it history before it was fact, and history cannot be argued with.

Tap, tap, step.

"Elias." Mara's voice, low, behind him. "Stop."

He stopped. The river ticked beneath them, patient.

Ahead, a dark seam crossed the snow—a pressure ridge where two plates of ice had met and shoved, building a wall of broken shards knee-high and glittering like glass teeth. Beyond it the moonlit stretch looked sound, but sound was a rumor on a river.

"We go over, not around," he said. "Around means the current channel. You can see it in daylight—a black vein through the white. At night you find it by falling through."

"Wonderful," Mara said, and put both hands on the ridge and climbed.

She was stronger than she looked, which he should have learned by now and kept forgetting. Six weeks of her at the dressing station, moving men twice her size off tables and onto pallets, holding amputated legs steady while surgeons sawed, and she still managed to seem like a woman who would blow away in a strong opinion. She hauled herself up the ridge, turned, and reached back for the cart rope, and together they dragged it up and over, the coffins grinding, one lid popping loose. Elias rammed it back down with the heel of his hand.

A piece of straw fell out onto the ice. He picked it up. There was nowhere to put it except his pocket, so he put it in his pocket, and hated himself for the smallness of the gesture.

"That's the third time you've done something like that," Mara said.

"What?"

"Apologized to a box." She took the rope from him. "You do it instead of saying what's in your mouth. I've watched you do it since the sentry line. You carry other people's weight beautifully. Your own you keep trying to hand to furniture."

The river cracked somewhere east of them, a long groan like a door opening in a house with no people in it. Both of them went still until it stopped.

"There's a reason I asked you to cross at night," Elias said. "It wasn't only the pickets."

"I know."

"It's—" His breath came out and hung between them, a small ghost dissolving. "There's something in the satchel that isn't Reeve's."

Mara didn't move. In the moonlight her face was all planes and shadow, a portrait someone had started and abandoned. "Show me."

* * *

He knelt on the ice to open the satchel, which felt like kneeling on the lid of his own grave, and dug under the oilskin to the false bottom he'd built with a razor and stolen glue in the paymaster's tent a lifetime ago. From it he drew a single sheet, folded twice, and did not hand it to her so much as hold it out at arm's length, the way you offer meat to a dog you aren't sure of.

She read it by moonlight, tilting it, and he watched her eyes travel the lines he knew by heart.

*...having deserted their colors in the face of the enemy, were executed pursuant to sentence of field court...*

"Bodies buried without marker," she read aloud, very quietly. "Per regulation." She lowered the page. "This is dated."

"Yes."

"The twelfth of December."

"Yes."

"The mutiny at Vosk Bridge was the fifteenth."

The river said nothing. For once it let the silence stand alone.

"I wrote it before it happened," Elias said. "Hargreave called me in on the twelfth. The men were already chained—they'd been chained two days, since the rations came up short again and Corporal Brann told the commissary officer what everyone already knew, that the colonel was selling grain out the back of the train while his own regiment boiled boot leather. Hargreave couldn't try them. A trial has witnesses. Witnesses have memories. So he decided there would be no trial, and he told me to prepare the record, and I—" His voice caught on the mechanical fact of it, the sheer clerical ease. "I asked him for the date, ma'am. That's all I did. I asked, *what date shall I enter for the execution,* and he looked at me for a long moment and then he smiled, and he said, *the twelfth, Vane. Put the twelfth.* And I understood that he wasn't asking me to record a lie. He was asking me to *author* one. Because if the record says they died on the twelfth, then whatever happens after is not murder. It's paperwork catching up."

Mara folded the sheet along its old creases. Her hands were perfectly steady, which frightened him more than trembling would have.

"You forged his signature."

"He dictated. I signed." He heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway, because halfway through the truth was worse than all of it. "I've signed his name perhaps two hundred times. Requisitions, returns, commendations. It's a clerk's ordinary work—you sign what you're given. Only this once what I was given was twelve lives, and I wrote it in my best hand. My *best* hand, Mara. Do you understand? I was proud of it. I remember being proud. The descender on the R, the way the ink feathered—I remember admiring it."

"Why?"

The question landed harder than an accusation would have. He opened his mouth and found nothing there but the true answer, which he had avoided for two months.

"Because I was cold and hungry too," he said. "Because Brann was right and I knew he was right, and I said nothing, week after week, and when the reckoning came I wanted—God forgive me—I wanted it *over*. Eleven strangers and a captain I'd met twice. I wanted my warm corner of the tent back. Hargreave saw that in me the way he sees everything in everyone. He didn't threaten me. He didn't have to. He just handed me the pen and let me be the coward I'd already chosen to be."

The ice boomed under them, a single deep note like a churchbell underwater, and neither of them flinched. Some part of Elias noted distantly that they had been standing on a frozen river for ten minutes and he had stopped being afraid of it. There were things worse than drowning. He was carrying most of them.

"So now you know," he said. "What you're hauling in a cart. It isn't only Reeve's letters proving Hargreave lied. It's the lie itself, in my handwriting. If Okafor publishes, the first question any court asks is *who wrote the order*, and the answer is standing in front of you wearing boots I stole off a dead sergeant." He made himself go on. "My testimony doesn't damn Hargreave without damning me. Forgery of a commanding officer's order, in wartime. There's one sentence for that, and it's measured in rope. You could take the letters and walk north alone and be cleaner by morning. I'd understand. I want you to know that I would understand it, so you don't have to pretend you considered it longer than you did."

He braced for her to weigh it. He watched her weigh it—he could see the scales working behind her eyes, the same cold ledger-keeping he'd admired in her from the first night, when he'd learned that the woman who patched soldiers' bodies by day spent her nights writing down the names the army unmade.

Mara tucked the forged order into her coat, against her chest, where the cold would have to negotiate with her body heat to reach it.

"No," she said.

"No, you won't leave?"

"No, I won't absolve you." Her voice was level and quiet and absolutely without mercy, and he loved it the way you love a surgeon's honesty. "Don't ask me to. You wrote a death warrant for men you'd never bothered to meet. There is nothing I can say tonight that makes that smaller, and if I tried, you'd use it. You'd fold it up small like that page and carry it in a false bottom and take it out when you needed to feel forgiven. I'm not going to feed you absolution, Elias. Absolution is free and it's worth what it costs."

Each word went in clean. He nodded, because there was nothing else his neck would do.

"But," she said.

She stepped closer, close enough that he could see frost forming in the wool at her collar, and held out her hand—not to shake, palm up, open.

"Partnership," she said. "You don't get clean hands. You get mine. They aren't clean either—I've got six years of looking the other way while surgeons marked men *dead of fever* who died of the surgeon's brandy, and I wrote the fever down because the alternative was the road. We are both forgers, Elias. You forged a colonel's name. I've spent six years forging the army's whole account of itself, one private at a time, in a book I hide under linens." Her fingers stayed open in the moonlight. "So here are the terms. You carry your guilt to Thorn Mill and you put it in front of Okafor with everything else. All of it. My name in your mouth too, if it comes to that—we hang together or we testify together. And when it's done, if it's done, you write the true order yourself. In your best hand. Twelve names, alive, restored to the rolls, and your own name signed underneath as the man who unmade the lie. That's the price. That's the only forgiveness on offer: the kind you build."

For a long moment Elias couldn't speak. The river groaned its long slow note beneath them, and the moon slid a finger's width behind cloud, and he understood that she had just done the cruelest kind thing anyone had ever done for him: refused to let him be forgiven cheaply, and refused to let him be alone expensively.

He took her hand. Her grip was a nurse's grip, made for holding things steady while they were cut.

"Together, then," he said. "Filthy hands."

"The filthiest." She released him and turned to the cart. "Now push, clerk. We've two miles of river and I'd rather drown mid-confession than mid-silence."

* * *

The pressure ridge nearly ended it. Going down the far side, the cart slewed, one runner skidding on black ice beneath the snow, and the whole weight of it swung toward Elias and carried him three sliding steps toward the black vein he'd warned her about. He felt the snow give. Felt the dark under the dark.

Mara's arm hooked his elbow and wrenched him back, and the two of them sat down hard against the coffin with the cart pinned between their boots, breathing like stolen horses.

"Noted," she said at last. "You almost drowned before confessing. That would have been like you."

"Efficient."

"Cowardly and efficient." But her shoulder was against his, warm through two coats, and she left it there for exactly five seconds, which he counted, and then she stood and said, "Up. The vein's thirty yards east. We angle west and keep probing."

They walked. Tap, tap, step. The moon came fully out and laid their shadows long across the white, two figures and a cart and two boxes, going north over the talking ice. Somewhere behind them, south, was the dressing station, the war, the tent where a colonel with gout and a clear conscience was sleeping on the proceeds of his regiment's hunger. Ahead was the north bank, the chapel road, and a chaplain Mara swore had once held a lantern for her while she sewed a boy's gut closed—*he owes me,* she'd said, in the tone of a woman keeping accounts.

Elias walked and probed and walked, and with every step the forged order rode against Mara's heart, and the strange peace of it settled over him like the snow: he was damned, and accompanied.

Halfway across, the river spoke louder than it had all night—a crack that ran from bank to bank under their feet, fast as a whip falling, and the whole white plain shivered.

Mara stopped. "Elias."

"I hear it."

"The ice," she said quietly, staring north to where the darkness of the far bank waited. "It's breaking early."

And far off, downstream, they both heard it now—deep, rhythmic, unmistakable. Not the ice talking to itself.

Hooves.
