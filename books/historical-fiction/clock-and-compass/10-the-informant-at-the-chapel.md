## 10. The Informant at the Chapel

The chapel appeared out of the dusk like something remembered rather than seen—a squat stone box with a leaning spire, half its windows boarded, the other half dark. Smoke rose thin and gray from a chimney that should have been cold in a parish this empty.

"That's smoke," Tamsin said. She'd spotted it before either of us. The girl had eyes like a hawk and the appetite of one.

"It's shelter," Elias said.

"It's smoke," Tamsin repeated, with the flat certainty of someone who had survived on her wits longer than either of us had survived on our principles.

I wanted to argue with her. I couldn't. Three days on the road since the ice crossing, sleeping in ditches and hay ricks, eating hardtack that tasted like the sacking it came in—and I knew exactly what a warm fire and a roof could do to a person's judgment, because I was feeling it do it to mine.

"I know this place," I said. "Or I know the man in it."

Elias turned. In the failing light his face was all hollows and shadow, the gauntness of the first day come back on him. "You know him?"

"Chaplain Whitcombe. He served at the field hospital at Berezina Crossing, two winters ago. When we ran out of bandages he tore up his own vestments for lint. When we ran out of brandy he gave the men communion wine and told them it was a sacrament either way." I could still see him—broad-shouldered, soft-voiced, sleeves rolled to the elbow, kneeling in the straw beside a dying sergeant and holding his hand while the man's own family was four hundred miles away. "He buried more of my wounded than the ground here could hold, and he said a word over every one of them. Even the deserters."

"Especially relevant, that," Elias murmured.

"He's a good man."

Tamsin snorted. "Good men are expensive."

"He owes me nothing," I said. "That's what makes it safe. A debt can be sold. Kindness given freely is the only currency nobody thinks to steal." Even as I said it I heard how it sounded—the kind of sentence a woman constructs because she has already decided and wants the deciding to look like reason.

Elias looked at me for a long moment. Under the grime his eyes were startlingly clear. "Mara. If you're wrong—"

"If I'm wrong, we'll know quickly." I shifted the satchel higher under my cloak, feeling the letters through the oilskin, that small hard heartbeat of paper against my ribs. "And if I'm right, we sleep warm for the first time in three nights, and we eat, and we're two days from Thorn Mill instead of four."

The wind came off the river plain and went through my coat like it wasn't there. That decided it, if anything did. Cold makes cowards of reasoning.

We walked toward the smoke.

* * *

Whitcombe opened the door before I knocked. He'd gone grayer at the temples and thinner through the middle, but the hands were the same—big, scarred, a mason's hands that had never quite stopped being a mason's hands even after he'd traded stone for scripture.

For a heartbeat he simply looked at me. Then: "Nurse Montrose."

"You remember."

"I remember everyone who worked harder than the surgeons." He stepped back to let us in, and warmth rolled out over us like water. "And I remember you especially. You held the line when Captain Doyle's men broke for the wagons. You stood in the road and shamed them back." He smiled, and there was real warmth in it, which made what came later worse, not better. "Who are your friends?"

"A clerk and a cousin," I said smoothly. "Traveling to rejoin their regiment's baggage train. We saw smoke."

Something crossed his face—quick, there and gone, like a bird's shadow on a wall. "Baggage train's that way." He nodded east. "This is the wrong road."

"The right road had soldiers on it," Tamsin said, with the flawless instinct of a liar who has never needed to be taught.

Whitcombe looked at her a long moment, then at me, then at the satchel's outline under my cloak, which I realized too late I had angled away from him as though it mattered. As though I could hide the shape of everything by turning my shoulder.

"Well," he said. "It's cold, and God sees the heart and not the paperwork. Come in. There's stew."

* * *

The chapel's interior had been converted into something between a rectory and a storehouse. Pews pushed against the walls, stacked grain sacks where the altar rail had been, a stove in the center radiating the heat we'd have committed crimes for an hour earlier. Whitcombe fed us barley stew with actual salt pork in it, and I watched Elias eat and understood for the first time how close to the edge of himself he'd been walking. Tamsin ate like a small animal hoarding against winter—one bite swallowed, one pocketed, eyes never leaving Whitcombe's face.

He told us about the parish. Empty, mostly—the village evacuated after the requisitions, the people scattered to towns that didn't want them. He stayed because the army kept a supply depot two miles north and paid him to bury what the depot produced. He said it without bitterness. He said most things without bitterness. That had been true at Berezina too.

"You've done well for yourself," I said, meaning the stove, the stores, the intact roof.

"God provides," he said, and then, quieter, with his eyes on the fire: "The army provides through God. It amounts to much the same thing, some days." He looked up. "You're a long way from any dressing station, Nurse Montrose. And clerks don't usually travel with nurses, and cousins of clerks don't usually flinch when a dragoon patrol is mentioned, which yours did, out on the step, before you thought to hide it."

The stew turned to lead in my stomach.

"I'm not asking," Whitcombe said gently. "I've learned not to ask. What a man doesn't know, he can't be made to say." He rose and put another log on the fire. "But I'll tell you what I do know, for free, because I owe you that much and more. There's a colonel—Hargreave—who has sent riders down every road within thirty miles these past four days. Offering promotion to any officer who assists, and coin to anyone else. Looking for a nurse and a clerk. The description—" he paused, and I felt the description settle over the three of us like snowfall, "—matches no one in this room, I'm sure."

Silence. The stove ticked. Tamsin's hand had found the bread knife on the table, casually, the way a child finds a toy.

"I'm sure," I said.

"Of course you are." Whitcombe sat back down. He rubbed those big scarred hands together, and I watched him do it, and I thought: *he is afraid*. Not of us. Of something larger, moving behind us, the way weather moves behind hills. "Sleep in the vestry. There's blankets. In the morning, take the river path south—it's slow but there's no checkpoint on it until Thorn Mill itself." He stood, blessed us absently, in the manner of a man who blesses things the way other men lock doors, and went up the narrow stair to his room.

We lay on the vestry floor in the dark, three bodies' width apart, listening to the wind work at the boarded windows.

"He recognized the satchel's shape," Elias said finally. "Or he guessed. Either way he knows."

"He gave us the river path. That's not the act of a man selling us."

"No," Elias said. "It's the act of a man deciding whether to sell us."

I didn't sleep. I lay listening—to the wind, to Tamsin's slow breathing, to the house settling above us—and somewhere past midnight I heard the sound I'd been waiting for without knowing it. Footsteps overhead. Soft. Deliberate. Going not to the door but to the small outbuilding behind the chapel where Whitcombe kept his horse.

Then hoofbeats, fading north. Toward the depot.

Toward the dragoons.

* * *

"Wake up. Now."

Tamsin came awake the way she always did—instantly, silently, hand already finding whatever was nearest that could cut. Elias took longer; exhaustion holds people. I shook him until his eyes focused.

"He's gone for the horse," I whispered. "North."

Elias sat up in the dark. I heard rather than saw him think. "How long do we have?"

"A rider to the depot, twenty minutes if he pushes. Dragoons back, forty minutes after that, maybe less if they were already mounted."

"So an hour. Perhaps."

"Less. They'll have standing orders. Hargreave doesn't send invitations twice."

We dressed in the dark, gathered what we carried—which was very little: the satchel, a little food Tamsin had salted away from her pockets, my ledger wrapped against my breastbone where it had lived so long I sometimes forgot it wasn't part of my body. At the vestry door I paused. On the table in the nave, where Whitcombe had left it, sat a stub of candle and a book of common prayer, laid open, deliberately, to a page I suspected he'd chosen.

I didn't read it. Reading it would have meant something, and I couldn't afford for it to mean anything.

"The crypt," Tamsin said.

Elias and I both turned.

"There's always a crypt," she said, with the weary patience of the young explaining the world to the old. "Old churches, they're all built on bones. And crypts have two doors, because priests used to fight about being buried closest to the altar and the diggers got tired of carrying them through the church. Look for the outside stair."

She was fourteen and she had learned this from living in baggage trains and graveyards, and I felt something twist in my chest that had nothing to do with fear.

We found it behind the vestry wall—an iron ring set in flagstones, a steep stair going down into cold black air, and at the bottom a low vaulted room lined with burial niches, some ancient, some recent, the newest holding the depot's output in coffins of unplaned pine. The far wall had collapsed at some point into the earth behind it, leaving a gap a child had widened, and Tamsin slipped through it before we'd finished arguing and called back, breathless, "It comes up in the yew yard. Behind the graves."

We could hear the dragoons before we saw them—six horses at least, coming up the road at a trot, the sound carrying in the frozen night like stones dropped in a well. Above us, through the crypt's low ceiling, the chapel door boomed open.

"Chaplain!" A voice like a whip. "Where are they?"

"Gone." Whitcombe's voice. Steady. I closed my eyes. "I gave them bread at dusk and they walked east, toward the baggage road, as I told you I would direct them if they came."

"The colonel said east would be too easy for them. Search the chapel. Search the grounds. Torches."

Light bloomed through the floorboards above us—or rather, through the gaps around them, laying thin gold lines across the coffins, across Elias's face, across the names I could just make out chiseled in the niches. *Here lies Private_. _ Here lies Private_ _. The chapel's whole history of the overlooked, filed in stone.

Boots overhead. Drawers pulled out, vestments thrown, the prayer book—I heard it fall—kicked across the floor. Then boots outside, circling, and torchlight pouring down through the collapsed section of the far wall, through the gap we hadn't used, throwing our shadows huge and wavering against the ceiling among the carved saints.

A soldier's face appeared at the gap. Young. Red-cheeked. Holding his torch high.

For one endless second he looked directly at me. At Elias crouched against the coffin wall. At Tamsin, flattened into the yew-shadow beyond.

I put a finger to my lips.

It was the maddest thing I have ever done, and I have done a number of mad things since. But I had spent years learning how invisible women become when men with power expect them to be invisible, and I gambled everything on the arithmetic: a nurse hiding in a crypt is a criminal; a nurse hiding in a crypt who commands you to silence might be anyone—might be sent by the colonel himself, might be someone worth reporting carefully rather than loudly.

His eyes narrowed. His mouth opened—

"Torch here!" someone shouted from the chapel steps. "Tracks in the yew yard!"

His face vanished. Boots thundered around the building. And we went through the gap and up through the frozen earth into the graveyard, among the yews, as six dragoons wheeled their horses back toward the church we'd just left.

Behind us, in the doorway of the chapel, Whitcombe stood in the torchlight with his prayer book hanging open in his hand. He wasn't looking at the dragoons. He was looking at the yew yard. At us, or at the place we'd been. And even at that distance, even in that light, I saw his expression, and I will carry it to my own grave alongside all the others I carry.

He wasn't sorry. He was counting.

Promotion, Hargreave had promised. Coin for the small, advancement for the ordained. And Whitcombe had led them east on paper and betrayed us in fact—had sent the dragoons circling to the yew yard with a shout about tracks, tracks we'd actually left, tracks that would have found us anyway—so that whichever way it fell, he'd kept a hand in each pot. If we escaped, he'd warned us with the river path and slowed the search. If we were taken, he'd summoned them.

Trust, I thought, hauling Tamsin over a wall while the horses wheeled, is now rationed like everything else. Small portions. Measured carefully. Never assumed.

We ran south along the river path, cold air knifing our lungs, the satchel banging against my hip, the chapel and its smoke and its mason's hands falling away behind us. When we finally stopped, a mile on, in the black throat of a culvert, Tamsin bent double, gasping, and Elias leaned against the stone with his eyes shut.

"He tried to have it both ways," Elias said at last. "That's the worst of it. An honest enemy I could hate."

"Don't hate him," I said. My voice surprised me—steady, when I felt anything but. "Pity him. He's what Hargreave is making of everyone. Men who want to be good and want to survive, in that order until the second week, and then the order reverses." I touched the ledger through my coat. "That's the whole war, right there. Not the fighting. The reversing."

Tamsin straightened, wiping her nose on her sleeve, and pointed south along the path. "There's a bridge ahead. Where the path meets the mail road. And if there's a bridge on a mail road—"

"—there's a checkpoint," Elias finished.

We looked at one another in the dark. One down, I thought. Two to go. And the next one would not be a superstitious sergeant or a frightened chaplain with divided loyalties.

The next one would be a surgeon, and surgeons know faces.
