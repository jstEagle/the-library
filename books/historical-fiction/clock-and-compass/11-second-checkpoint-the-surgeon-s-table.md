## 11. Second Checkpoint: The Surgeon's Table

The second checkpoint announced itself by smell before sight—charcoal and tar, the reek of a brazier burning wet wood—and then by the barricade itself: two wagons dragged nose-to-tail across the road, planking laid between them, lanterns hung on poles like ugly fruit. Beyond it the road ran white with old snow toward Thorn Mill, and beyond that, somewhere in the dark, the river waited with its groaning ice.

Mara counted soldiers. Four at the barrier. One sitting apart on an ammunition crate with a book of forms open on his knee, a stamp in one hand and a bottle of ink between his boots. And one more man standing back from the fire, apart from the sentries' easy slouch, wearing an apron under his greatcoat.

An apron. She knew that posture—the way a surgeon stands when he has been on his feet for thirty hours, weight shifted onto one hip, hands hanging loose because they have done everything they can do and the rest belongs to God or gangrene. She had stood that way herself for most of the winter.

"He's a doctor," she murmured to Elias, not moving her lips much. "Not an officer. A regimental or a contractor's man."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." Officers asked questions to catch you. Doctors asked questions because they wanted to go back to sleep. "Give me the papers. Keep Tamsin behind me and look like a clerk who is cold and resentful about it."

"That last part requires no acting," Elias said, but his hand shook slightly as he passed her the folded papers, and she saw he was doing sums in his eyes—hours since the chapel, miles since the ford, how fast could dragoons ride—and coming out short every time.

Tamsin stood pressed to the cart's side, wrapped in Mara's ruined cloak over Elias's spare coat, her chapped hands hidden in her sleeves. The girl had not spoken since the crypt. She did not speak now. She simply watched the checkpoint with the flat, total attention of someone who had survived the baggage train long enough to know that doors were where people died.

Mara took the reins herself.

* * *

The sentry flagged them down with a lantern and a boredom so profound it was almost a form of hospitality. "Papers."

"Burial detail returning to the mill depot," Mara said. "Two dead to hand over at the chapel ground, then reassignment." The cart behind them carried two actual shrouded bodies—they had buried three men at the treeline that morning, deserters from some broken battalion, and had taken two along as the most honest paperwork she'd ever forged. Dead men made excellent references.

The sentry waved them through to the table, which meant the surgeon.

Up close he was worse than he'd looked at a distance—young for the rank insignia, gray everywhere else, a man whose body had been conscripted by his profession. There was dried blood on the apron under his coat, brown-black, days old. His eyes went to the papers first, the way good ones always did, and then—Mara felt it happen, a small physical event, like a bone setting wrong—to her face.

He stopped reading.

"You," he said quietly.

The whole winter narrowed to a needle's point. Mara kept her face arranged into polite, tired blankness, the face she had worn past a thousand men who half-recognized her, because a nurse in uniform was a category, not a person, and categories passed checkpoints. But this man was not looking at the category. He was looking at her.

"Sister Montrose," he said. "Number Four Field Hospital. Bautzen road. You held the abdominal wounds section in October."

Every instinct she had said deny, deflect, dim. She opened her mouth to do all three.

Then she saw his hands.

They were shaking. Not with cold, though the cold was sufficient. With something else—a fine, continuous tremor, the tremor of a man who had been holding very still around a terrible knowledge for a long time. She had seen it before, on men who came off picket duty after a massacre, on orderlies after the amputation tents ran out of laudanum and the screaming did not.

She gambled.

"Yes," she said. "And you're the surgeon who sent me forty of your worst and came with them to show my head nurse how you'd packed the wounds, because you didn't trust anyone downstream to do it right. I remember your hands too, Doctor. You tied off a femoral on a boy of nineteen while the shells were coming over, and you apologized to him for the scar."

The silence stretched. One of the sentries laughed at something by the fire. The ink bottle between the clerk's boots caught the lantern light.

"I never apologized," the surgeon said.

"You did. He didn't hear it. He was insensible. But you said it, and I heard it, and I have thought of it since on days when I needed to remember there were still men in this army who saw patients as people." She set the papers on the table between them, deliberately, an offering rather than a plea. "Which is why I'm going to say something unwise, Doctor, and you're going to decide what kind of man you are in the next minute, and either way I'll understand."

His jaw tightened. "Say it."

"The twelve men Colonel Hargreave recorded as deserters executed at Vosk Bridge are alive. Eleven of them are in a stockade north of here awaiting summary execution without trial, and the twelfth is Captain Aldous Reeve, who is not dead, whatever the rolls say, and who wrote these." She touched the oilskin packet under her coat, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound you were checking for bleeding. "Hargreave sold the regiment's rations that winter. Starved them into mutiny. Then wrote the mutiny onto the men who complained about it. I have been keeping a ledger of every name this army has struck from its rolls for two years, and those names are going to a correspondent at Thorn Mill before the river ice breaks, and after that it will be out of both our hands and into print, and God help us all."

She heard herself finish and thought, distantly: well. Either the rope or the stamp, sister. No third door.

Elias had gone very still beside her. Tamsin's breath was a thin whistle in the cold. The surgeon read the papers again, slowly, not seeing them, and Mara watched him the way she watched a man on the table—not his face, his extremities, the truth-tellers.

His right hand had stopped shaking.

"Hargreave," the surgeon said. "Yes."

That was all. But the word *yes* came out of him like something lanced.

"I stood at Vosk Bridge," he said. "Afterward. He had me certify the deaths of men I had never treated, on a list I hadn't written, with causes of death I knew to be impossible—one of them died of 'wounds received in the action,' and there *was no action*, Sister. There was a bridge and a bread riot and then there was a list. I signed it." He looked at his own hands, the famous hands, the hands that had tied a femoral under fire. "I told myself the alternative was a firing squad and a replacement who would sign anything. I have told myself that for four months. It has gotten quieter each month, the telling. Last week I couldn't hear it at all."

"Doctor," Mara said gently, "the papers."

He looked down at them. Burial detail. Route pass. Reassignment chit. All of it forged in Elias's careful clerk's hand, good enough for sentries, never good enough for a man who spent his life reading documents for the lie inside them.

"They're false," he said. Not a question.

"They're necessary."

"And if I stamp them, I'm in it. Whatever happens at Thorn Mill, whatever Okafor prints—I'm in it."

"Yes," Mara said. She did not soften it. She had learned that from surgeons: they respected the prognosis delivered plainly.

The surgeon looked past her, at the cart, at the two shrouded shapes. Then at Tamsin, small and rigid under the wrong-sized coat. Then at Elias, who met his eyes and did not look away, though everything else about him was braced for the word *arrested*.

"My copy," the surgeon said abruptly. "Where's my copy? Corporal!" 

The clerk came over at a shuffle, stamp in hand, and the surgeon took the casualty roll from his forms case himself—the working copy, the one every checkpoint kept, the paper trail of the lie traveling the roads ahead of the truth. He set it on the table. He stamped the burial detail's route pass against it, mechanically, twice, the official rhythm of it almost comic in its ordinariness, and initialled the margin, and handed the papers back to Mara with steady hands.

Then he walked to the brazier.

"Doctor?" the clerk said. "That's the—"

"I know what it is, Corporal."

He fed the casualty roll into the fire page by page, not quickly, watching each one curl and blacken. Nobody moved to stop him. Sentries by a fire at midnight will forgive their officers almost any eccentricity that doesn't involve the words *advance on the enemy*, and besides, the thing burned like everything else that winter burned—unwillingly, sullenly, spitting sparks into the snow.

"There," the surgeon said, when the last corner went gray. "Now there is no record that you passed this point, and no record that anyone checked, and no record of the check of the record. If Hargreave comes asking, I was drunk, my corporal was drunker, and the wind took some paperwork. It happens constantly. It is practically regulation." He turned to Mara, and up close, in the lantern light, she saw how young he was under the gray. Twenty-eight, perhaps. Thirty. Old enough to have signed away eleven lives and young enough to want them back. "The stockade north of here. Do you know when?"

"Not precisely. Elias thinks the ice breaks within days, and Hargreave will want it done before the roads soften and the correspondents can travel."

"Then you have less time than your fear is telling you. Take the eastern track past the alder stand, not the main road—there's a culvert washed out and the dragoons won't post a picket at a crossing their horses refuse." He hesitated. His hands, steady now, rose halfway toward her and stopped, as if reaching for an instrument that wasn't on the tray. "Sister. The men I certified dead. If any of it matters—if any of it counts—"

"It counts," Mara said. "That's the entire point of the ledger. It counts. Every name, every signature, everyone who looked away and everyone—" she nodded at the cooling brazier— "who stops."

Something moved through his face that she would not have been able to name and did not try to; she had seen it on the other side of the table too many times, the moment when the fever breaks or turns, when no one yet knows which.

"Go," he said. "Corporal, log the detail through. Two dead, escort of two, onward to the chapel ground. Time of passing—" he glanced at the sky, theatrical, hopeless— "some time. I wasn't watching."

The clerk logged it. The sentries raised the plank. The wagon rolled through the gap in the barricades and onto the eastern road, and the checkpoint closed behind them like water closing over a stone.

* * *

They rode a mile before anyone spoke.

"He burned it," Elias said finally, to the road, to the dark, to nobody. "He just—he had it in his hands and he put it in the fire. In front of witnesses. He'll hang if this goes wrong. He knows that. He stamped us through knowing that."

"Yes," Mara said.

"Why?" The word cracked out of him with more force than he'd intended, and Tamsin stirred against the cart's rail. Elias lowered his voice. "We gave him nothing. No proof. A story and some forged papers and your word. Why would a man burn his own copy of the roll on the strength of that?"

Mara thought about it as the wheels complained over the frozen ruts, the alder stand rising black against the lesser black of the sky.

"Because he already knew," she said at last. "You don't shake for four months over paperwork you believe in. He signed a lie and he has been carrying it the way a wound carries its stitching—quietly, badly, waiting for someone to pull it out. We weren't the proof, Elias. We were the excuse. He needed someone to come through that checkpoint and make it possible to stop pretending, and we happened to be the ones." She paused. "It isn't so different from me, is it? Two years of copying names into a linen trunk. You can call it defiance if you like. Some nights it was just a way of not screaming."

Elias was silent for a long time. When he spoke again his voice had changed, gone softer and stranger, the voice of a man revising a document in his head and finding it better than the draft.

"I thought we were carrying the evidence," he said. "The letters, the ledger. Things. But it's not that, is it. It spreads. That surgeon tonight, the sergeant at the first checkpoint who let us through because we said the names aloud—it's not a package we're delivering, Mara. It's an infection."

"A remedy," Mara said. "Though I grant it works like an infection. Fever first."

Ahead, through the bare branches, she could see the eastern track bending north, and beyond it, faint in the starlight, the long pale seam of the river valley. Somewhere down there, behind them now but always ahead in every way that mattered, Samuel Okafor waited with his notebook and his press and his disbelief to be overcome. Somewhere closer, a stockade held eleven living men whose death certificates she could recite from memory. And somewhere behind them, on a road she could not see, Colonel Hargreave's dragoons were riding, because chaplains betray and colonels hunt and the ice, the ice, was already talking to itself in the dark, cracking its knuckles, counting the days.

Tamsin tugged her sleeve.

"Sister," the girl whispered. "The surgeon. When he burned the roll. He looked like you do. When you write in the ledger at night."

Mara looked at her. Fourteen years old, and seeing everything, as the overlooked always do.

"Yes," Mara said. "I expect he does."

She clicked the horse forward into the dark, toward the culvert and the alders and the third checkpoint she was trying very hard not to imagine—and failed, because at the third checkpoint there would be no tired surgeon, no superstitious sergeant, no ally waiting to be given permission.

At the third checkpoint, if the reports were true, there would be gallows.
