## 12. What Reeve Wrote

The chapel loft smelled of pigeon droppings and old incense, and the candle they'd stolen from the vestry burned with a guttering, tallow-smoke light that made the shadows lean in like eavesdroppers.

Mara sat on an upturned crate with her knees drawn to her chest, the oilskin packet open on her lap. Outside, the wind worried at the loose shingles, and somewhere below them a beam creaked as the building settled against the cold. They had chosen the loft because it could only be reached by a ladder through a trapdoor, and because Tamsin—posted below with strict instructions to whistle if anyone came—had declared herself delighted to play sentry for the first time in her thieving career.

"Read them," Elias said. He was pacing again, three steps one way, three steps back, in the narrow space between the rafters. He had been pacing since they climbed up here, since Mara had said the words *we should know what we're carrying before we die carrying it.* "I've read them so many times I've stopped seeing them. I need someone to hear them who wasn't there."

"You were there."

"That's why." He stopped pacing. His face in the candlelight was hollowed out, the frost-scars on his cheekbones standing pale. "Read them aloud, Mara. Make me listen to them properly this time."

She unfolded the first letter. The paper was soft from handling, creased into quarters and quarters again, and the handwriting slanted hard to the right, urgent, the ink blotched where a freezing hand had shaken. She had seen a thousand soldiers' letters. This was different. This was a man writing fast because he believed he might not finish.

"*To whoever finds this,*" she read. "*If you are reading it, then Vane has done what I asked, and God forgive him for what it will cost him.*"

Elias flinched. Mara kept reading.

"*You should know that we did not mutiny. I want that written plainly, because everything else depends on it. On the fourteenth of December, at Vosk Bridge, twelve men of B Company laid down their arms and refused to march. That is true. That is all that is true.*"

The candle flame bent in a draft. Mara turned the page.

"*We had eaten nothing but boiled hide and frozen turnip for eleven days. The men were chewing their bootlaces. Private Corrigan ate candle wax until his bowels failed. And on the twelfth of December I watched the commissary wagons pass us going the other way—full wagons, sealed wagons, escorted—and I asked where our rations had gone, because the requisitions said they had been issued to us. The quartermaster told me to mind my rank. So I minded my rank, and I wrote to Colonel Hargreave directly, as an officer is entitled to do, and asked him where the regiment's provisions were being taken.*"

Mara looked up. Elias had gone very still.

"He answered," Elias said quietly. "I wrote the answer. Hargreave dictated it. *Provisions are distributed according to operational necessity. Further correspondence on the matter will be treated as insubordination.*"

"Keep reading," he said, when she hesitated.

"*On the thirteenth of December,*" Mara read, "*a farmer came to our picket line selling bread—not army bread, just bread, dark rye, the kind that breaks your teeth—and Sergeant Doyle bought two loaves with his own money and shared them among the weakest men. A dragoon patrol took the bread from them and threw it in the snow. Stamped it into the mud. Doyle protested. He was flogged for theft of army provisions—the same provisions, mark you, that were never issued to us at all.*"

Her voice caught on the next part, though she made herself go steadily on.

"*That night the men asked me: Captain, if we starve here, is that soldiering? And I had no honest answer. On the morning of the fourteenth, when the order came to advance across the bridge under fire with empty stomachs and no cartridges resupplied, twelve men set down their muskets and would not pick them up. Not one of them ran. Not one of them raised a hand to an officer. They stood in the road and asked to be fed. That is the whole of the mutiny. Twelve starving men asking to be fed.*"

The wind rose outside, pressing at the shingles. Below them, faintly, Tamsin began humming—a child's tuneless thing, some half-remembered street song—to keep herself awake.

Mara unfolded the second letter. This one was shorter, and the handwriting had changed, grown smaller, more controlled, the writing of a man making himself careful.

"*Hargreave convened the court-martial himself within the hour. No advocate for the accused. No witnesses called. I spoke in their defense and he struck my words from the record—he had his clerk strike them out, actually, I watched the pen move—and sentenced all twelve to death by firing squad. Then, and this is the part I need you to understand, he did not record a mutiny at all. He recorded twelve desertions. Deserters shot attempting to flee. Do you see? A mutiny has causes. A mutiny gets investigated. Desertion is simply dealt with. He erased the question along with the men.*"

"I watched him do it," Elias said. His voice had gone flat and strange. "I typed the sentence myself. *Twelve private soldiers, having deserted their colours, were executed pursuant to field regulations.* I remember thinking the sentence was well-formed. Grammatically clean." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Grammatically clean. That's what I thought about while twelve men were being sentenced to die for asking for bread."

"There's more," Mara said, and unfolded the third letter.

This one was dated the day after Reeve's official death.

"*They have listed me among the dead. I find I am killed in action as of yesterday, which is convenient for everyone, since dead officers cannot testify. Hargreave visited me tonight in the guard tent—I am held with the men, awaiting execution with them, though he has not yet fixed the date—and he offered me a choice. He said: Aldous, you are a good officer and this is a bad winter. Sign a statement confirming the desertions, confirm that the men refused lawful orders out of cowardice, and you will be returned to duty. Refuse, and you will die with them, and no one will ever know there was anything to refuse about.*"

"*I told him I would rather be erased than useful to him. He took it calmly. That frightened me more than anger would have. He said, 'Erasure is a form of order too, Captain.' And then he thanked me for my service, sincerely, as if we had concluded a pleasant piece of regimental business.*"

Mara stopped. The candle had burned down an inch; wax pooled and hardened on the crate beside her knee.

"Elias," she said carefully. "Why does he want Reeve's signature? If he's willing to kill twelve men over a lie, why not simply kill a thirteenth?"

"Because a captain's confession closes the file." Elias had resumed pacing, faster now. "A deserter's execution needs no explanation. But a decorated officer confirming cowardice in his own company—that's corroboration. That's the difference between a lie that holds and a lie that needs holding up. Hargreave isn't afraid of the men. He's afraid of the *question*. As long as Reeve lives unsigned, there's a version of events walking around on two legs."

"And the rations?"

Elias stopped. In the candlelight his face did something complicated—shame and fury fighting for the same ground.

"The requisitions passed through my office," he said. "Every one of them. I processed the paperwork for those sealed wagons myself. I assumed—they were marked for forward depots, I assumed they were feeding the advanced companies, the ones further out, worse off than us. That's what the routing codes said." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "But I knew the routing codes were wrong. I knew it. Forward depot requisitions don't get escort details of eight dragoons. Rations don't travel under guard against the enemy—they travel under guard against *us*. I knew, and I filed the forms anyway, because filing forms is what I'm for, and because—" his voice cracked, steadied— "because it was easier to believe the lie was administrative than to believe it was hunger. You understand? It was easier to think of it as a misrouting than to think of Corrigan eating candle wax."

Mara said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be either absolution or condemnation, and he deserved neither from her, not yet.

He lowered his hand. His eyes were wet, and he let them be.

"I suspected," he said. "That's the word. Suspected. For weeks. And suspicion is such a comfortable sin, Mara—it lets you keep your hands clean while your eyes stay open. I suspected, and I typed, and I slept. And then the men I helped erase started dying, and I ran, and now I'm carrying the proof of my own crime toward a newspaper man and calling it courage." He shook his head slowly. "It's not courage. It's arithmetic. My guilt plus their deaths equals whatever this is. I don't get to call it virtue."

"No," Mara agreed. "You don't."

He looked up sharply.

"But hear me," she went on, and her voice came out low and even, the voice she used over men screaming on the table, the voice that worked when gentleness would not. "I have spent two years writing down names that officers struck out. Do you know what I learned? That the strike of a pen is not the end of a man. The man goes on existing somewhere—in memory, in consequence, in debt. Every name Hargreave erased is still owed to somebody. A mother. A sweetheart. God, if there's one. The debt doesn't vanish because the ledger says so." She stood, stiff-kneed, and held up the letters. "These pages are the debt coming due. And you—you're not the criminal here, Elias. You're the interest accruing. You're what the lie costs the liar. Hargreave sold the bread and shot the hungry and called it order, and every form you typed for him is a nail in *his* coffin now, not yours. Your testimony doesn't damn you. It damns *him*. You just have to live long enough to give it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll give it for you. I have a ledger full of names he never wanted remembered. One more voice won't frighten me."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The candle guttered, righted itself. Below, Tamsin's humming had stopped; she was listening, Mara realized, to every word, the way children listen—silently, completely, keeping what they hear.

Elias wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, like a man doing a chore.

"All right," he said. "All right. Read the last one."

The fourth letter was the shortest. The ink had smeared, as if water—or something else—had fallen on it, and the final lines were pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn through.

"*If these letters reach print, then the eleven may yet live, and I will count my erasure a fair price. Tell them Doyle's bread was shared fairly. Tell them Corrigan's laughter was the last thing heard in the guard tent each night, and that he died—if he dies—as a soldier, which is more than can be said for the man signing his death warrant. And tell whoever keeps the records of this army that the truth is heavier than any satchel, and travels slower, and always arrives.*"

Mara folded the letters along their worn creases and slid them back into the oilskin. Her hands were steady. Her heart was not.

"So," Elias said. "Now you know what we're carrying."

"Now I know what we're *owed*," she corrected. She looked at the candle, at its small stubborn flame, and thought of the river waiting somewhere ahead of them, groaning under its ice, counting down the days. "Hargreave thinks erasure is a form of order. Fine. Then we'll make the truth a form of disorder. We'll carry it through every checkpoint he puts in our way, and we'll put it in print, and when the ice breaks—" she met Elias's eyes— "it won't break over his secret. It'll break over him."

From below, soft as a mouse, Tamsin's voice floated up through the trapdoor:

"Begging your pardons—but there's lantern-light coming up the mill lane. Two lanterns. Moving slow."

Mara snuffed the candle between finger and thumb. In the sudden dark, Elias's hand found hers and gripped it once—hard, brief, a signature.

"They're stopping at the gate," Tamsin whispered.
