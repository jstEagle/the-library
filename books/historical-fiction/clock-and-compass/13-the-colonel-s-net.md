## 13. The Colonel's Net

Colonel Ambrose Hargreave had never in his life been late to anything, and he regarded the fact as the single greatest proof of his own character. Punctuality, he liked to say at mess, was the soul of discipline made visible. A man who could not govern his minutes could not govern a company, and a man who could not govern a company had no business governing twelve mutinous wretches awaiting a rope.

So when the report arrived that two lanterns had been seen moving up the mill lane at an hour when no honest lantern moved anywhere, and that by the time his dragoons reached Thorn Mill the lane held nothing but frost and rabbit tracks, it was not the failure itself that soured his supper. It was the lateness of the report. Six hours. Six hours between the sighting and his desk, like a wound left unbound until the blood had done its work.

He sat in the requisitioned farmhouse that served as divisional headquarters with his gouty foot propped on a stool, a map of the river country spread before him, and the letter from Captain Wray—his provost, his ferret, his increasingly expensive disappointment—lying where he had dropped it.

*A nurse, sir. And a man traveling as a paymaster's clerk. Seen together at Thorn Mill. Gone before we came.*

Gone before we came. Hargreave read the phrase four times, and each time it sounded less like an excuse and more like a prophecy.

He was not a stupid man. He wanted this recorded somewhere, because history would very likely decline to record it for him. Stupid men lost armies through blunder; he had lost nothing but a shipment of rations and the reputation of a dozen criminals, and he had lost those to weather and arithmetic, which were nobody's fault. No—the danger of stupid men was that they did not know what they were looking at. Hargreave knew exactly what he was looking at. He had known it the moment the clerk Vane vanished from the rolls office with three days' rations drawn against a name that no longer existed on any muster.

A dead man drawing rations. That was not desertion. Desertion was a soldier running from fear. This was a man running *toward* something, which was a different animal entirely, and required a different net.

He poured himself wine despite the hour and the surgeon's opinions, because his foot was a small red furnace and because he found that thinking went better with a glass. Order, he thought, sipping. Everything came back to order. These civilians, these nurses and chaplains and newspaper men with their ink-stained fingers—they believed order was a convenience, a garment you put on when convenient and shed when it chafed. They had never stood at the head of eight hundred starving men in January and understood that the only thing between that mass and dissolution into wolves was the fiction that the army knew what it was doing. The fiction *was* the army. Scratch the paint and you found panic underneath, and panic ate men faster than hunger ever had.

Twelve men. That was all it had taken, at Vosk Bridge. Twelve men who laid down their arms because their bellies had beaten their discipline, and Hargreave had understood at once—if he wrote down "mutiny," every staff officer in the chain of command would begin asking how a regiment came to be starved into mutiny, and the answers to *that* question led upward through the requisition ledgers to a door Hargreave very much wished to keep shut. So he had written instead: *twelve deserters, executed in the field.* A tidy sentence. A closed account. The alternative truth would have spread like spilled oil—first shame, then inquiry, then courts, then the slow public unmaking of a career built over thirty years, one honest brick at a time.

He had told himself, in the weeks since, that the lie was itself a kind of duty. That the twelve would have hanged regardless; that the manner of their dying mattered less than the survival of the machine that had condemned them; that a colonel who preserved his regiment's honor—even by forgery—served more men than a colonel who told a truth that broke it.

Most days he believed this. He had built a life out of believing such things before breakfast.

Tonight the belief sat poorly, and he examined why, because Hargreave distrusted unexamined feelings the way he distrusted cavalry. The reason was the nurse. Montrose, her file said. Mara Montrose. He remembered her now—remembered her from the dinner he had summoned her to, that gray little woman with her gray competent hands who had lanced his foot without once meeting his eye, who had answered four questions about her ward with five words total, and who had somehow, in the doing, made *him* feel like the object under examination. He had dismissed her with the peculiar irritation of a man who has been politely seen through.

And now her name sat in a reward notice on his desk, beside a price, like the name of a criminal.

He had drafted the notice himself that afternoon. He read it again, turning the page toward the candle.

*TWO PERSONS WANTED FOR THEFT OF MILITARY DOCUMENTS AND CONSPIRACY WITH DESERTERS.—MARA MONTROSE, NURSE, OF THE 3RD FIELD HOSPITAL, AGE THIRTY-ODD, PLAIN DRESS, GRAY CLOAK.—ELIAS VANE, LATE PAYMASTER'S CLERK, GAUNT, DARK, LAME IN THE LEFT FOOT OR FEIGNING SO.—FIFTY GUINEAS EACH, ALIVE, TO CAPT. WRAY, PROVOST MARSHAL, DIVISIONAL HQ.*

Fifty guineas each. He had doubled the customary sum without hesitation. You did not haggle with a fire.

Alive. He had written *alive* twice over, and struck it out once, and written it again. Partly this was procedure—a prisoner could testify; a corpse could only embarrass. But partly, he admitted, it was something less comfortable: he did not want the woman killed. Not out of mercy, precisely. Out of a need to *understand*. She had his satchel—or Vane did—and inside it letters in Reeve's hand, letters that should not exist, and she had looked at him across a dinner table with those flat gray eyes while the satchel was already, somewhere, moving. She had treated his gout with hands that were carrying the evidence of his ruin, and he had tipped her sixpence.

The vanity of it stung worse than the danger. He recognized that too, and set it aside, because a commander who acted from sting died stupidly.

* * *

Captain Wray arrived at midnight, stamped snow off his boots in the hall like an apology, and stood dripping before the map table with his hat in his hands. Wray was a good ferret—tenacious, unimaginative, honest in the narrow sense that he could be bought only by promotion rather than coin—and Hargreave valued him the way one values a dog that bites.

"They were at Thorn Mill," Wray said. "Chaplain swears he never saw them. I believe him about half."

"Which half?"

"The half where he saw them." Wray permitted himself the ghost of a smile. "I've posted men on the miller. If they come back for shelter, we'll have them. But they won't come back. They're making for the river road, sir, and after that—" he tapped the map, west of the frozen crossing, "—Thorn Mill is on the way to Okafor. The correspondent."

"I know whose mill it is," Hargreave said coldly, and did not add that he had learned it only yesterday, from a staff intelligence summary he had previously filed under *irrelevant civilians*. Okafor. Samuel Okafor. A name that traveled in dispatch bags and returned in newspapers, and newspapers, unlike bullets, could not be dodged.

That was the shape of it now, laid out on the map like a diagram of his own anatomy. Somewhere between here and the river were a nurse and a clerk carrying enough paper to hang him—paper in Reeve's hand, dated after Reeve's death, which meant Reeve lived, which meant the twelve executions were a documented fraud, which meant everything. Their destination was not a place. It was a printing press. And their clock was the weather.

"The ice," Hargreave said. "Your estimate."

"Breaks within the fortnight. The quartermaster says the thaw came early upstream. Once it goes, the ford road's gone and the ferry road runs through territory we don't hold. Whatever they're carrying either crosses before the break or doesn't cross at all."

"So we are racing a river."

"We are racing a river, sir. With respect, we're losing."

Hargreave studied him. "Why?"

Wray hesitated. Hargreave had learned to treasure the moments when Wray hesitated, because they always preceded something true. "Because there are three checkpoints between here and the crossing, and I've orders standing at all of them, and none of those orders say *shoot*. Because every man on those roads knows fifty guineas is a fortune and a nurse looks like a nurse, and yet they've passed at least one patrol already tonight, unseen. Because—" he stopped, rallied, "—because we are hunting two people across forty miles of winter with a description so thin it fits half the women in the province. Gray cloak. Plain face. Sir, I could stop every gray cloak from here to the coast and the war would go on without us."

"You propose to do nothing?"

"I propose to do less, better. Close the roads. All of them. Not patrols—*closures*. Barriers, papers checked against passes issued in the last week only, nothing older. Make every mile cost them a day. Let the river do the shooting."

It was, annoyingly, sound. Hargreave turned to the map and began working through it as he worked through everything—supply, sequence, time. Close the roads and the countryside notices his absence of traffic; close them slowly and the pair slips ahead of the net. Close them at once, all at dawn, and every checkpoint becomes a wall. Yes. And behind the walls, a slower certainty: the prisoners.

His eyes went, without his willing it, to the eastern edge of the map, where a penciled cross marked the ground the men called Gallows Field—not because it was one, yet, but because soldiers named things ahead of themselves, superstitiously, the way sailors whistle for wind. Twelve crosses. He had ordered the timber weeks ago. Twelve frames, cut and seasoned, waiting with the patience of all his works.

The execution was scheduled for the twentieth. A formal affair, after review, with the brigade drawn up to witness—order made visible, the whole point of the exercise. But the twentieth might be four days after the ice broke. Four days in which a nurse with a memory and a correspondent with a press could make the twelfth frame unnecessary and the other eleven a scandal in perpetuity.

"Wray," Hargreave said quietly, "if the execution were moved forward—to the day the ice breaks, or the day before—what would it take?"

Wray blinked. "The review board won't sit early. Regulations require—"

"Regulations require what I require. What would it *take*?"

"A written order. Over your signature. Advancing the sentence on grounds of"—Wray's mouth worked—"'operational necessity.' And the chaplain would have to be found, and the drummers, and the transport for the prisoners. Two days' work compressed into one."

"Then start the work tonight." Hargreave took a fresh sheet and uncapped his pen, and for a moment his hand hovered, steady as it had hovered over the first falsified roll months ago, steady as it had hovered over everything he had ever signed. He thought, absurdly, of punctuality. Of minutes governed. Here was a man who had never been late to anything, preparing to arrive at his own gallows-field ahead of schedule.

There was still a voice in him—the old voice, the thirty-years'-honest-bricks voice—that said: *stop. Confess. Take the court-martial like a soldier. Twelve men live, and you lose your rank, and you sleep.* He let the voice finish. He always let it finish. Then he noted, as he always noted, that the voice had no plan, only a preference, and that preference had fed no one, held no bridge, buried no dead in good order.

Order mattered more than truth. Truth was a luxury of peacetime, a toy of newspapermen; order was what stood between eight hundred men and the wolf. He had starved them—he could admit that in the privacy of his own skull, he had sold the rations and pocketed the difference and advanced his family and *someone* would have to answer for it eventually—but starvation had not broken them. His sentence upon the twelve had broken nothing either. Only truth could break an army, truth published, truth loose in the world like a spark in a powder store, and he would be damned before he handed the match to a gray-faced nurse and a thieving clerk.

He wrote: *In view of operational necessity and the imminent loss of the river passage, the sentences confirmed upon the twelve prisoners... shall be carried into effect on the day of the ice-break, or the day preceding, at the discretion of the undersigned.* Signature. Seal. The wax smelled like a chapel.

"Sir." Wray, reading over his shoulder, looked faintly ill. "Moving it up—there'll be questions. The review board—"

"There will be no questions if there is no one left to ask them, and no document to ask them about. Which is why the nurse and the clerk will be in custody before that order reaches the field." He looked up. "Double the pickets on the miller. Search the chapel—you were right to distrust our shepherd. And Wray—every gray cloak on every road. Shake the province. I want them found."

"And if they reach the crossing first? If the papers get across?"

Hargreave considered the map, the thin blue line of the river, the small penciled cross of Gallows Field, the vast blank white beyond it where presses waited.

"Then it stops mattering whether I was right," he said, "and starts mattering whether I was quick. Go."

Wray went. Hargreave stood alone with the candles and the map and his burning foot, and permitted himself, briefly, the admission he allowed no one else: that he was afraid. Not of the gallows—he had watched death too often to flinch at his own appointment with it. He was afraid of being *wrong*, publicly, irreversibly wrong, and having the wrongness printed and bound and placed on shelves for his grandchildren to shelve away in turn. Every concealment he had committed had been purchased with the same currency: the belief that a controlled lie served more men than a free truth. He had never once tested the belief against daylight. He was gambling now that he never would.

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, patient—came a sound he had been hearing for two nights in his dreams. The river ice, settling. Groaning like a ship. Like a great lock, turning.

He snuffed the candles and stood in the dark, listening to winter count down.

* * *

By morning the roads were closed. By noon the reward notices were nailed to church doors in eleven villages, fifty guineas staring out from under the banns of marriage. And by evening a courier rode east through the falling dark with Hargreave's sealed order in his saddlebag, galloping toward twelve frames of seasoned timber and the day the ice would break—

—which, at that hour, was one day sooner than anyone at headquarters knew. For the thaw had jumped the upper fords overnight, and the river, old and impatient, had begun keeping its own schedule.
