## 14. The Orphan of the baggage Train

The barn smelled of hay rot and horse, which was the best smell either of us had encountered in three days. We had come off the frozen ford at dusk with our clothes stiffening on our backs, and by unspoken agreement we had walked another mile in the dark before Elias found the gap in the boards wide enough for a man who no longer cared about splinters.

I got a small fire going in the corner where the draught couldn't find it—dry-ish planks from a broken crate, fed one at a time, the flame kept lower than my pride. Elias had the satchel open across his knees, checking the letters for damp, his lips moving as he counted them the way other men count rosary beads. Twelve letters. Eleven names. One captain writing to no one from inside a grave.

We thought we were alone. That was our first mistake of the day, and not our last.

"I can hear you counting," said a voice from the loft.

Elias's hand went to the satchel. Mine went to the fire, as if I could put it out before it betrayed us. The voice was young—a girl's voice, cracked at the edges the way voices crack when they've been shouted at and shouted over for years—and it came from directly above the satchel, which meant whoever owned it had been listening long enough to know what mattered.

"Come down," I said, "slowly."

Hay shifted. A pair of bare feet appeared through the trapdoor first, then a girl in an army greatcoat that had been cut down—or more likely hacked down—with a knife, sleeves rolled four times at each wrist. She was thin the way baggage-train children are thin, not starving but rationed, every calorie accounted for by someone else's arithmetic. Her hair was cropped close and unevenly, done by her own hand against lice. Her eyes were the problem. They were old eyes in a young face, and they moved straight past me to Elias and fixed on the satchel like a compass finding north.

"You're the ones," she said.

"We're nobody," Elias said.

"You're the nurse and the clerk." She dropped the last three feet instead of using the ladder, landing light as a cat that has learned falling is cheaper than climbing down. "Reward's fifty crowns apiece. A hundred for both, and the papers." She held up two fingers, then folded them down one at a time, savoring the arithmetic. "That's a horse. That's two horses. That's out."

* * *

Her name was Tamsin Reeve—which stopped Elias's heart visibly, though she waved this off before he could speak. "No relation. Half the camp claims relation to somebody dead. It's free." She was fourteen, or said she was; she had been with the baggage train since she was ten, orphaned at a place whose name she gave with the flatness of someone reciting a supply inventory. She mended, hauled, cooked, carried water, and knew everything.

"You can't know everything," Elias said.

"I know you crossed the ford at Miller's Bend because the main road's got a barricade since yesterday morning. I know the sergeant at the chapel took money from a dragoon officer with a red nose, because my friend Pie heard him bragging about it over cards. I know there's a runner who comes to Thorn Mill twice a month and carries a leather case chained to his wrist." She let that sit. "And I know the ice is going to break inside four days, because Old Marta in the sutlers' row reads the river like scripture and she hasn't been wrong yet."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, wind worried at a loose board.

"So," Tamsin said, sitting down on an upturned bucket as if she owned it, "here's how it is. You take me with you, or tomorrow morning everyone in the baggage train knows where you're sleeping."

"That's blackmail," Elias said.

"Yes," she said, with the patience of a schoolmistress and none of a schoolmistress's warmth. "It's also true. Both things can be true. Captain—" she caught herself, grinned without much humor, "—Clerk Vane."

* * *

I should have been afraid, and I was—but underneath the fear was something worse, which is to say recognition. I have spent six years among people the army counts the way a merchant counts nails: by weight, by number, by what they cost. The baggage train is where the army puts what it doesn't want to count at all. Children like Tamsin die of fever in February and are entered nowhere, buried under no stone, mourned by no roll call. I have sewn shrouds for children smaller than her. I have written *boy, age unknown, cause: ague* in margins that were never read, because the ledger under the linen trunk began, in its secret heart, with exactly such entries—names the army declined to keep, so I kept them instead.

She watched me watching her. Old eyes, reading me the way she'd read the river.

"You've killed children?" she asked. It wasn't an accusation. It was a professional inquiry.

"No."

"Then you can't hurt me worse than the army already has." She shrugged inside the vast coat. "So the blackmail's a bluff, mostly. Mostly. But you need me anyway, and we all know it, so shall we stop pretending anyone at this fire has clean hands?"

Elias looked at me. He had been looking at me more often lately—not asking permission, exactly, but asking whether the thing we were becoming together was still something he could recognize. I turned back to Tamsin.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Out." She said it immediately, no performance in it at all, which told me she had rehearsed it so many times it had worn smooth. "Not out of trouble. Out of *this*. The baggage train is a ditch that moves. I mend socks until I'm pretty enough to be worth something worse, and then I'm worse, and then I'm dead or a wife, and both of those are the same road. You're going somewhere that matters. I heard you talking about the mill, about the writer, about printing names." Her voice caught on the word *names*, just slightly, and smoothed itself over. "Names get printed. Names get remembered. Nobody remembers a girl who died mending socks in a ditch that moves."

"The somewhere that matters," Elias said quietly, "has gallows at the end of it. For us, likely. Possibly for you."

"Then I'll have died going somewhere," Tamsin said. "Better than arriving nowhere."

* * *

Here is the thing I did not say aloud, because saying it would have made it a weapon she could use or I could regret: I had already decided before she finished speaking. Perhaps before she came down from the loft. There is a moment when a child looks at you across a fire in a ruined barn and asks to be claimed, and every argument against claiming her is an argument made by a woman who wants to sleep at night, and I have never trusted my own desire to sleep at night. I buried a boy of eleven at Krasno, wrapped in a tent fly because there was no canvas left for shrouds. I buried twin sisters, fifteen and fifteen, within a week of each other, and their mother's regiment marched past the graves without breaking step. I am very good at burying children. I decided, somewhere in the last two years, that I would rather be good at keeping one alive.

But deciding and admitting are different ledgers, and I have learned to keep some accounts private even from myself.

"She eats half her rations and gives the rest away," I said to Elias, which was not agreement and was. "Did you notice? At the chapel she ate less than a grown soldier on short rations, and she's growing."

"You noticed her at the chapel," Elias said. "You noticed her before tonight."

I had. A girl in a cut-down coat, carrying water for men who never learned her name, watching everything with those old eyes. I had filed her away the way I file all the overlooked—the way I filed Private Wendell's name into my ledger while his officers ordered his effects burned—and I had told myself there was nothing I could do for her, because there never is, because there are hundreds of her, because if I start I will never stop.

Tamsin, meanwhile, was watching us deliberate over her head with an expression of magnificent contempt.

"For the record," she said, "I'd have blackmailed you anyway even if I liked you. Sentiment doesn't fill a belly."

"Noted," I said. "Rule one: you do what I say near soldiers. If I tell you to run, you run, and you don't look back, and you don't come find us. Rule two: if we're taken, you never saw us. You invent a story about searching for your brother's unit and you make yourself boring until they forget you. Rule three—" I reached out and turned her chin so those old eyes had to meet mine. "You are fourteen. Whatever happens, you act fourteen. Children survive wars by being children. Do you understand?"

Something flickered behind her face—the bluff cracking for half a second, the ditch that moves showing through—and then the armor came back down, and she nodded once, brisk as a quartermaster.

"And rule four," she said. "Nobody touches the satchel but him, and nobody touches whatever you carry under your shirt but you. I've worked that out too. Don't insult me by pretending."

* * *

We slept in watches. Tamsin took the second watch without being asked, sitting cross-legged by the embers with a mending needle in her hand—she could not be idle; I understood that completely—and I lay awake listening to the wind and thinking about the shape my life had taken in the last week.

A week ago I had been invisible. That had been the whole architecture of my survival: a woman who dressed wounds and changed dressings and was seen by officers as furniture, and who wrote names in the dark. Invisible people cannot be hanged. Invisible people have nothing to lose, which sounds like freedom and is actually its own kind of cell.

Now there was Elias, asleep with his arm across the satchel like a father over a cradle. Now there was a girl by the fire who had chosen us with a knife held backward and then, somewhere between rule one and rule four, had simply—chosen us. And the fear had changed key. Before, I feared for myself, and I found I could spend that coin freely; I had been doing it for years. Now I lay awake cataloguing ways a fourteen-year-old can die at a checkpoint, at a river, at the wrong end of a dragoon's temper, and I understood that Hargreave had done me an injury worse than the hunt itself. He had given me something to lose.

I hated him for it. I was almost grateful. Both things can be true. Tamsin taught me that.

* * *

We moved before dawn, and Tamsin earned her place before breakfast.

She took us off the forest track within an hour of setting out, holding up one bare-cropped head like a deerhound's. "Wagons," she said. "Two, maybe three. Heavy." And indeed, twenty minutes later, from the ridge above the track, we watched a supply column grind past below—six wagons, an escort, and at the tail a pair of dragoons stopping every farmhand on the road, turning over packs, studying papers by lantern light.

"How did you know?" Elias asked.

"Mud," she said, as if he were simple. "Frost last night, mud today. Wagons leave ribbons in the mud. You learn to read ground or the ground reads you." Then, without ceremony, she led us down a streambed for a mile, through a alder brake, along a hedgerow she seemed to know from a previous life, and delivered us to the far side of the checkpoint an hour before the column would have delivered us into it.

At noon we crouched in a drainage culvert while a patrol passed, and Tamsin shared out strips of dried fish she had produced from somewhere inside the greatcoat—her own hoard, I realized, her own emergency rations, saved against a hunger that had not yet arrived. She divided it three ways without being asked. Elias tried to refuse his share. She pushed it at him.

"You're no good to the truth if you faint," she said. "Eat."

He ate. I found I was smiling, and stopped, and then let myself smile anyway.

* * *

We made the edge of the Thorn Mill road by late afternoon, and there Tamsin stopped us, crouching in the shadow of a fallen wall.

"One more thing you need to know before you walk into whatever this is," she said. "The reward notice changed this morning. My friend Pie saw the new posting at the tollhouse. It's not fifty crowns anymore." She paused, and I watched her choose to give us the worst of it plainly, because that was her nature; she had decided we were hers, and hers got the truth. "It's two hundred, and it's not 'a nurse and a clerk' anymore. It says your names. Montrose. Vane." Her old eyes moved between us. "Somebody talked. Somebody who knew both names."

The wind came down the road from the direction of the mill, carrying the smell of thawing river.

Elias swore softly and reached for the satchel. I stood very still, doing sums I didn't like. The chapel chaplain knew Elias but not my surname. The surgeon at the second checkpoint knew my face and my name both—and had burned his copy of the roll, but burning paper is easier than burning knowledge. The net was drawing tighter, and now it had our names woven into the mesh, and beside me a fourteen-year-old girl was waiting to see whether I would send her away to save her.

Tamsin read the hesitation off me instantly, and lifted her chin with a defiance so familiar I might have invented it.

"Don't," she said.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Don't." She stepped closer. "I'm in it now. Two hundred crowns buys a lot of loyalty, and I've got none to sell, but the dragoons won't take my word for that. So we go together, or we hang separately, and I'd rather walk."

Behind her, downstream, the river made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—a deep, grinding groan, like a giant shifting in sleep, the sound of winter losing its grip. Old Marta, I thought, reads the river like scripture, and the scripture says four days.

It sounded like fewer.

"Stay close to me," I told Tamsin, and we turned toward the mill, and the light was going, and somewhere ahead Samuel Okafor was waiting for pages that might already be worthless, and somewhere behind Colonel Hargreave was no longer hunting a nurse and a clerk.

He was hunting Mara Montrose and Elias Vane, by name.
