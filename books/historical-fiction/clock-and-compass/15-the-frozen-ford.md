## 15. The Frozen Ford

The roadblock announced itself an hour before we saw it — a sound like a blacksmith's shop falling down a staircase, iron on iron, hooves and shouting carried thin and cold across the frozen fields.

I pulled Tamsin down behind a fallen drystone wall and put my hand flat on her back until she stopped squirming. Mara crouched beside me, breath steaming, eyes fixed on the road below where six dragoons were turning a farm cart sideways across the lane. They'd hung a lantern on the cart's rail even though it was barely midday. It was not a checkpoint. Checkpoints asked questions. This was a net, strung across the only road to Thorn Mill.

"They're not checking papers," Mara said quietly.

"No." I knew what papers they'd be checking. A description had probably gone out with the patrols by now: a clerk, thin as a fence rail; a nurse in a grey coat; and God help us, a girl of fourteen with a mouth like a bootjack, because Hargreave would have squeezed the innkeeper at the chapel crossroads and the innkeeper would have seen three travelers, not two. "We go around."

"Around where?" Tamsin hissed. "There's nothing around but river."

She was right, and that was the whole problem. The Vorsa ran along the eastern edge of the valley like a seam of grey glass, and every bridge for six miles stood inside Hargreave's reach now. Every ford worth the name was garrisoned or watched. Except one.

"There's a place," I said. "My father drove cattle over it, before the war took him. The Brantford sheep-wash. The water spreads shallow over a gravel bar for forty yards. In summer it comes to your knees."

"And in winter?" Mara asked.

"In winter it's a coin flip."

Tamsin looked at me the way soldiers look at a lieutenant who has just said *we'll be fine*. "You've done it? In winter?"

"Once. When I was fifteen. The ice held."

"You're a fountain of comfort, you are," she muttered, but she was already climbing the wall.

Mara lingered a half-second longer. She didn't say *is there another way*, which told me she'd already run the arithmetic and found no other answer. She just looked at me — that long, level look she had, the one that made you feel audited and absolved at the same time — and said, "The letters stay dry."

"The letters stay dry," I agreed.

I wish I could tell you I believed it. What I actually believed was that we had no better options, which is the belief most men march to their deaths on.

* * *

The wood above the river was birch and dead bracken, and we moved through it bent double like thieves, which we were, in the army's arithmetic. Below us the Vorsa ran flat and pale under a skin of ice that looked, from thirty feet up, solid enough to skate a regiment across. That was the lie of ice. It always looks strongest from a distance.

The sheep-wash was exactly as my father had left it twelve years ago: a sloping shelf of stone where the drovers used to crowd the flocks into the current, now furred with frost, the water beneath it dark and fast between two shelves of white. The gravel bar showed as a faint yellow-brown seam under the ice downstream, where the current slowed and spread. Forty yards of it. Then the far bank rose in a tangle of alder.

Mara made us stop and watch for ten full minutes. No smoke from the far side. No movement on the water. Somewhere upstream, muffled by distance, came the sound of the roadblock's hammering — men building their net while we slipped through a hole in the world.

"Tamsin stays here?" I suggested.

"I don't," said Tamsin.

"If the ice goes—"

"If the ice goes and you two are on the wrong side of it," she said, chin up, arms crossed, "then I'm alone in a war with a colonel hunting my family. Again." She let that sit. She'd learned that trick from Mara — dropping a thing heavy and walking away from it. "I'm coming."

Mara put her hand on the girl's shoulder. Not to argue. To steady. "Then you hold my hand and you do not let go of it, whatever happens. Whatever you see. Whatever the river does. Elias first, then you, then me. We test every step twice."

"And if it cracks?"

"We don't stop. Stopping is what kills you on ice. You'll want to freeze. Don't."

It occurred to me, watching her say it, that Mara Montrose had crossed more rivers than I had, in worse conditions, carrying men who couldn't walk. I had spent two days thinking of myself as the one leading this expedition, because I had the plan and the letters and the map in my head. But plans are paper. She was the one who knew how bodies survive.

I went first.

* * *

Ice speaks. Nobody tells you that unless you've been on it. It doesn't crack all at once like timber; it *talks* — a language of ticks and groans and sudden high whines, the sound of a thing under enormous slow stress deciding, inch by inch, how much more it will bear. My father called it the river clearing its throat. He also drowned, so perhaps take his folklore with salt.

I tested each step with the heel of my boot before committing my weight, and where the ice rang hollow I shifted toward the bank, following the seam of the gravel bar where the water ran shallower and froze thicker over the stones. Ten yards. Twenty. The cold came up through the soles like a living thing, patient, interested in me.

At the halfway mark I turned and waved them on.

Tamsin came out onto the ice with her hand in Mara's and her face set in that scowl she wore instead of fear, and for twenty yards it was going to work, I could see it working, and then the river cleared its throat.

Not near us. Upstream. A boom rolled down the valley like distant artillery, and I felt it through my boots before I heard it — a shudder in the whole white floor of the world. On the north-facing bend, half a mile off, a seam of open black water appeared as if drawn by a pen, and the ice between us and it began, very quietly, to sing.

"Don't stop," Mara said, to Tamsin, to herself, maybe to the river. "Don't stop."

They were fifteen yards behind me when the ice under Tamsin quit.

No warning. No dramatic splitting. One step she was on the world and the next step there was no world, just a hole with ragged white edges and a splash that sounded obscene in all that silence, and she was gone under so completely that for one heartbeat the hole was empty and I understood, in my marrow, that the river had simply taken her.

Then her hand came up and slapped the ice edge and slid off, and she came up gasping, and everything I have ever been able to do happened in the next ten seconds without any of it passing through thought.

I dropped flat. You distribute weight or you join her — some animal part of me knew that. I bellied across four yards of singing ice, and I heard Mara behind me doing the same, screaming something — Tamsin's name, or mine, or just sound. Tamsin got both hands on the edge and the edge broke off in her grip and she went down again and came up slower, and her scream when it came was small and shocked, a child's scream after all, fourteen years old stripped down to its last true number.

My arm went into the water to the shoulder. The cold hit my hand like a hammer — not pain first, just annihilation, the hand ceasing to be a hand — and I closed fingers on wool. Her collar. Her braid. Something. I hauled and the ice under my chest cracked a long silver line from elbow to elbow and I hauled anyway, because the line was already drawn whether I cracked it or not, and there is no version of me in any world that lets go of that collar.

Tamsin came out of the water the way a drowned thing comes out of a net — heavy, streaming, half-unconscious of how she got there. The ice held. I do not know why. I have stopped asking rivers why.

We crawled. All three of us, flat on our bellies like the animals we were, until the bank came up and the alder roots caught us and the ground was solid and I was shaking so hard I couldn't tell if it was terror or temperature. Probably both. Probably always both.

Tamsin was making a sound I hadn't heard from her before — not crying exactly, more the noise a kettle makes just before it goes quiet, air hitching through chattering teeth. Mara had her already, stripping the soaked coat off her, chafing her arms, and her own voice was running low and steady underneath it, a litany of nonsense comfort: *you're out, you're out, you're on the land, feel the land, that's the land.*

And then I saw the satchel, and stopped shaking for entirely different reasons.

* * *

I'd strapped it across my chest before we started, oilskin wrapped three times around the letters, buckled tight, the way I'd carried it since the chapel. Now the straps hung loose. Somewhere between the hole and the bank the wrapping had torn — the oilskin ripped along one fold, sodden, hanging open like a wound — and the top letter's corner showed through, and the corner was wet.

"Mara."

She looked. I watched her understand it in real time, the way her face did its accounting — not panic, never panic, just a rapid silent ledger of damage and options.

"How wet?" she said.

"Corner. Maybe more." My hands were useless, shaking too hard to work the buckles, and Tamsin's hands weren't any better, blue-white and jerking. So it was Mara who knelt in the frozen mud and unwrapped the satchel with those nurse's fingers, quick and gentle, peeling back the ruined oilskin page by page.

Reeve's letters came out one at a time. Seven of them. Six dry. The seventh — the earliest, the one where he named the officers who'd signed for the missing rations — had taken river water across its lower third. The ink had begun to bloom, brown ghosts spreading where he'd written *Hargreave received payment the fourth of December, witnessed by*—

"Witnessed by whom?" Tamsin croaked. Even half-dead, she wanted the story.

"Hush," said Mara, but not unkindly. She tilted the page against the light. "Some of it holds. The signature's gone soft. The rest—" She smoothed it on her knee with a tenderness I've seen her give to wounds. "Paper dries. If we get it dry tonight, flat, pressed, most of this survives."

"*If* we get anywhere with a fire," I said. "Which we can't. Smoke draws dragoons."

"Then we find somewhere with a chimney and no owner." She said it lightly, but her eyes went past me, upstream, to where the black seam of open water still gleamed half a mile off, and I followed her look and finished the thought for her, because it was the thought we'd been outrunning since dawn.

The ice was breaking. Not tomorrow, not in some abstract spring. Today, here, now, a mile at a time, the river was tearing up its own floor, and Thorn Mill lay beyond three checkpoints and the last one sat beside the gallows field, and Hargreave had moved his timetable once already. The river wasn't scenery anymore. It was a clock with a black hand, and we could hear it ticking.

Then Mara did the thing I will carry out of this war ahead of everything else I saw in it.

She stood up, shrugged off her cloak — the grey army-issue wool, patched at the elbows, the one garment she owned in the world — and laid it out flat on a stone. "Take your coat off too," she told Tamsin. "Both of you. Everything damp next to the letters has to go."

"That's your only—" I started.

"It's wool. Wool works wet, everyone knows that, I'll be fine." She was already folding the dry cloak's thickness around the letters, layering them the way she layered splints, corners tucked, nothing left to shift. "The oilskin failed. Wool won't. I've kept gunpowder dry in less."

I stood there in the freezing wind in my shirtsleeves, watching a woman give away her warmth to a satchel, and I understood something about her that I'd been circling for days. She had spent years writing other people's names down so the army couldn't pretend they'd never lived. Of course she'd wrap the names in her cloak before she'd wear it. To Mara Montrose, the record *was* the body. It was everyone we'd lost, wearing her coat.

"Tamsin gets the blanket roll," I said. "You share mine."

"Chivalrous."

"Practical. You're warmer than I am and I can't afford you sick." Which was true, and also the closest I could come, standing in the wind with river-water numbing my shoulder, to saying anything else.

She looked at me a moment longer than the sentence deserved. Then she tied the bundled letters against my chest herself, knotting the cloak's cords under my arms, her fingers brisk and precise and — I would swear to this — lingering one second past necessity. "There," she said. "Now you're carrying everyone. Try to float."

Tamsin, wrapped in the blanket roll with only her face showing, made a noise that might have been a laugh or the aftermath of nearly drowning. "You two are disgusting. There's a war on."

"There is," Mara agreed, shouldering her pack, and for the first time in days she smiled — small and fierce and aimed upstream, at the black water, at the whole thawing machinery of it. "And it's losing."

We climbed the far bank into the alders. Behind us the Vorsa boomed again, longer this time, and somewhere out on the white plain a slab the size of a barn door groaned, shifted, and settled, and the sound followed us into the trees like a warning we refused to read.

Two miles on, cold-soaked and cloaked in borrowed wool, we crested the ridge — and there, in the valley below, stood Thorn Mill's broken chimneys against the sky. And between us and them, drawn up across the mill road with their lanterns lit though the sun hadn't set, sat a line of dragoons. At their head, dismounted, reading a paper he held close to his face, stood a man with a colonel's bearing and a game leg.

Hargreave hadn't strung his net across the roads after all.

He'd guessed where we were going, and he was waiting at the end of it.
