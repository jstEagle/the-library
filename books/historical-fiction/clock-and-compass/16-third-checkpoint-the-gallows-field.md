## 16. Third Checkpoint: The Gallows Field

We smelled it before we saw it.

Tamsin caught it first—she had a scavenger's nose, honed on baggage trains—and she pulled up short on the frozen rut of the road and said, "That's pitch."

She was right. Beneath the woodsmoke and horse and the wet-wool smell of an army in winter, there was a dark sweetness riding the wind: pine pitch heating in a cauldron. I knew that smell from a hundred dressing stations. It was what they boiled tar in. It was what they used to seal coffins.

It was also, Tamsin told us later with the flat delivery of a child who had seen too much, what they poured down flagpoles when they wanted a hanging to burn bright enough to be seen at night.

We came over the last rise an hour before dusk, and the third checkpoint opened beneath us like something out of a bad dream you wake from grateful, then remember all day.

The road narrowed where it met the river valley, squeezed between a palisade of sharpened stakes and the frozen water. Across the ice, maybe half a mile downstream, the river bent around a bare hill, and on that hill stood twelve posts.

I counted them twice because the first time my eyes refused the number.

Twelve posts. Twelve ropes, each noose already tied, hung at the height of a man's throat, swinging slightly in the wind off the river so that from a distance they looked like a row of pendulums, keeping some terrible time. Scaffolding had been thrown up for spectators. Below it, a picket of dragoons stamped their feet against the cold, and beyond them, a field of trampled snow where—my nurse's eye did this work whether I wanted it or not—I could see the wheel ruts of a cart that had carried lumber, and the darker rectangle of a pit already dug for what the lumber would become.

"They moved them up," Elias said. His voice came out strange, thin. "Hargreave. He wasn't supposed to—" He stopped. He didn't need to finish. We both understood arithmetic now; we had lived by it for two hundred miles. The ice was due to break within days. Hargreave had moved the execution forward so that the river itself would close the road behind the deed, would drown the evidence and the witnesses in one clean act of nature he could blame on God.

"He's killing them before we can reach Okafor," I said.

"Then we don't have until tomorrow." Elias's jaw was set. "We have today. That checkpoint is the last wire between us and Thorn Mill, and after tonight the ford downstream goes soft and there is no crossing at all."

Tamsin looked from him to me, and for once she didn't have a quip. She just moved closer to my side, the way she'd done since the ford, as if proximity were warmth.

The checkpoint itself was a timber gate across the road, flanked by sandbags and a brazier, manned by eight soldiers under a corporal with a ledger of his own—a pass-book, a list of names permitted through. Behind it, drawn up on the near side of the gallows field with the easy menace of men who had been told to wait, sat a dozen dragoons.

And in front of the dragoons, being handed a cup of something hot by an orderly he didn't thank, stood Colonel Sir Reginald Hargreave.

I had never seen him. I knew that instantly, and I knew it mattered, because he didn't know me either—not yet. But I knew him the way you know a disease from its description. The gout-swollen foot propped on a muffled stool. The greatcoat cut too well for campaign wear. The face that was not cruel, exactly—that was the horror of it—a face of a man doing sums, and finding the sums satisfactory.

Elias saw him the same moment I did. I felt it go through him like cold water through cloth.

"That's him," he breathed. "That's the man whose order I forged."

"Then look at his boots," I said. "Not his face. Faces are remembered. Boots aren't." It was advice I gave myself as much as him. "Elias. If he stops us, he stops *us*. Whatever happens at this gate, the satchel cannot stop. Do you understand? The papers matter more than we do."

He looked at me then, and I watched him understand what I was really saying, which was: *one of us may need to be caught so the other can run.* He opened his mouth to argue.

"Don't," I said. "Save your breath for the walk."

* * *

We went through our story one more time in the shadow of the last stake before the gate, whispering, our breath mingling white.

Burial detail. Three of us, sent forward from the second dressing station to inter six dead of the camp fever at the riverside pit—because the ground upstream was iron and the graves couldn't be dug, and because an epidemic report had come down the line that morning requiring all fever dead to be buried below the bend, away from the water the regiment drew. Tamsin was our dead-carter's orphan assistant, which required no acting whatsoever. The papers were good ones. Elias had made them good; forgery, it turned out, was a skill that improved under moral pressure.

The weak point was me. I had no business being on a burial detail—I was a nurse, nurses didn't haul bodies—but the story held an answer: the dressing station was overrun with fever cases, the medical officer had ordered every pair of hands that could be spared to clear the dead out fast, and I had volunteered because someone had to sign the burial returns and the orderly couldn't write. It explained my presence. It explained my literacy. It even explained why a woman might be carrying documents.

What it could not explain, if Hargreave looked closely at my face, was everything else—the two hundred miles, the chapel crypt, the surgeon's stamp, the whole desperate machinery of us.

So we walked toward the gate the way you walk when you're tired and bored and slightly cold, which is to say we performed invisibility, and I have never worked harder at anything in my life.

The corporal at the book held out his hand for papers without looking up. Elias surrendered them. The wind came off the river and set the twelve nooses swinging behind him, and I fixed my eyes on the corporal's collar and thought about dressings. Clean linen. Boiled water. Small things. Countable things.

"Papers say six," the corporal said. "You've got—" he squinted past us at the handcart Tamsin had commandeered somewhere outside of God's jurisdiction, "—five shrouds."

"One died overnight," I said. "Return's amended." I produced the paper, kept my voice at the register I used for waking surgeons at three in the morning: apologetic, exhausted, beneath suspicion. "Sixth is wrapped in the tarpaulin. Fever dead. We're asked not to unwrap them again than needs must."

"Fever," the corporal repeated, and took a half-step back, and I saw the word do its work. Fear is the oldest passport in the world. "Where's this epidemic reported?"

"Second station, mostly. Some at the artillery depot." This was true, as it happened; I had heard it from a driver two days ago and folded it away like contraband, waiting for exactly this moment. "They're wanting it kept quiet. Morale."

"Everything's wanting to be kept quiet lately," the corporal muttered, and dipped his pen.

And that was when the orderly crossed the checkpoint ground and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "Corporal. The Colonel will see the party."

* * *

There is a discipline to terror. I learned it young, at my first field hospital, when a shell came through the ward wall and I discovered that my hands went on working regardless of what the rest of me was doing. Terror, properly managed, becomes fuel. Improperly managed, it becomes tremor. So while every animal part of me screamed to run, I curtsied to Colonel Sir Reginald Hargreave at precisely the depth I'd have curtsied to any colonel—respectful, incurious, spent—and I watched his eyes pass over my face the way a man reads a label on a bottle he has no intention of drinking.

"Burial detail," he said. Not a question.

"Yes, sir."

"Six dead of fever." He consulted a paper in his gloved hand—casual, unhurried, a man enjoying his own thoroughness. "Your station reported four yesterday."

"The count rose, sir."

"Counts do." He smiled faintly at his own wit, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was genuine. Here was a man entirely at his ease. He had moved his executions up a week and posted his net across every road, and he stood at the center of it drinking chocolate like a host at his own ball, and why not? From where he stood, the world was nearly tidy. Twelve names struck, twelve bodies soon to hang, one river about to wash the banks clean. "Names," he said, and held out his hand.

"Sir?"

"The names of your dead, nurse. For my book. We are"—the faint smile again—"very particular about names on this stretch of road."

Behind him, past his shoulder, I saw Elias go still in a way I recognized. Not fear. Calculation. He had shifted his grip on the satchel strap, and his eyes had gone, just for a heartbeat, to the far side of the checkpoint—to the wire fence that ran from the gate down to the ice, and to the figure leaning against the last post of it.

A runner. Our runner—a miller's boy from Thorn Mill, sixteen years old, waiting exactly where Okafor had promised a man would be waiting every evening until the ice broke, on the far side of the wire where civilians were allowed to sell hot cider to sentries. Forty yards from Elias. Twenty from me. One roll of wire fencing between the army's paperwork and the truth.

But first Hargreave wanted names.

And I realized, with a clarity that felt like standing in open door of a burning house, that this was the moment my invisibility got spent. Every coin gets spent eventually. I had paid in small denominations for two hundred miles—a curtsy here, a lowered gaze there, the whole currency of being a woman no one remembers. But Hargreave was asking me to recite names into his book, and somewhere in that recitation lay ruin, because I am a bad liar about exactly one thing in this world, and that thing is names.

"Nurse," he said again, patiently. "The names."

I curtsied. And then I did not give him names. I gave him an epidemic.

"Sir, the medical officer's report is enclosed with our passes," I said, and let my voice change—not louder, but slower, heavier, the voice I used when I needed a ward to listen. "Begging your pardon, but he charged me to tell any senior officer what we're seeing, because the depot hasn't grasped it. It isn't camp fever. The pattern's wrong. It starts in the bowels, sir, and it's in the water. We think the river above the bend." I gestured—upstream, toward the gallows field, toward the pit. "Which is why the dead are to go below it. And why I'd urge Your Honor not to draw cooking water here tonight, nor let the picket drink at the ice holes downstream of the burial ground. We lost two orderlies in four days who'd done nothing but handle the shrouds."

The wind carried my words across the checkpoint. I watched them land. Soldiers, whatever their rank, whatever their crimes, are all members of the same congregation, and disease is its devil. The corporal's pen stopped. The nearest sentry looked at the cup in his own hand and set it down on the sandbags with exaggerated care.

Hargreave frowned. Not at me—at the arithmetic. I could see him doing it, the sums he could never resist: a fever in the water supply, on the eve of an inspection he had not scheduled, with a regiment already restive over rations he had sold. A sickness loose among his men was a threat to his tidiness, and this man feared disorder the way other men fear God.

"The report," he said. "Give it to me."

"It's the sealed enclosure with the passes, sir. I only carried it." I fumbled at Elias's document case—slowly, clumsily, buying seconds the way a hemorrhaging woman buys minutes with pressure and prayer—and behind that fumbling motion of my hands, Elias began to move.

He walked to the wire. Not running—walking, with the satchel swinging loose at his side, calling to the cider boy in the cheerful bark of a soldier with money: "Here! Lad! Two cups, and be quick about it!" And when the boy came to the fence and Elias crouched, ostensibly for coins, the satchel passed through the gap under the lowest strand of wire in a single motion I nearly missed, smooth as a card trick, into the straw-lined pannier basket the boy carried. Then Elias straightened with two cups of cider and paid the boy and even drank—one swallow—and turned back toward me, empty-handed of everything except a wooden cup, and my heart made a sound I heard rather than felt.

Hargreave had the report open. His eyes were moving down it. My report. Which is to say, the report I had written myself at midnight two nights ago, by candle stub, forging a medical officer's hand with instructions from Elias on how a real one abbreviates, while Tamsin kept watch and my own pulse hammered so hard I'd had to flex my fingers between sentences.

It was a good forgery. It named a fictional major. It cited figures I had invented from real patterns. And it ended, at Elias's suggestion, with the sentence I was betting a colonel's caution on: *Recommend the matter be referred to the Director of Medical Services before any large assembly of troops in the valley, the miasma arising from disturbed ground being in the present state of the river a noted aggravation.*

Any large assembly of troops in the valley.

Hargreave read that line twice. I watched his jaw work. He was thinking of the morrow—of the spectacle he intended, of dignitaries and witnesses and a field full of assembled soldiers, of his perfect tidy demonstration spoiled by the one enemy he couldn't court-martial.

"Nurse," he said slowly. "This advises postponement."

"It advises nothing, sir. It recommends referral. I'm only the carrier." I curtsied again, deep and dumb and dutiful. "Though the surgeon did say, sir—if it were him—he'd want the ground tested before he'd stand a whole battalion on it."

Silence. The nooses ticked in the wind. Somewhere down the wire, the cider boy shouldered his basket, dropped a coin to a sentry as toll, and began to walk north along the ice road, unhurried, a boy going home in the dusk. Ten yards. Thirty. Fifty. Don't watch him. Watch the boots. Watch the boots, watch the boots—

"You have a steady manner about you," Hargreave said, and his eyes came back to my face, and stayed, and I felt the exact moment the label on the bottle interested him. "Have we met? You have the look of the hospitals."

"I've served where they've sent me, sir."

"Where was that?"

"Everywhere, sir. Wherever the lists ran long." It was true. Truth makes the best mortar for lies; I had learned that from watching Elias build our passes.

His eyes narrowed—not suspicion, not yet, but the sensation of a name almost remembered, which is more dangerous than either. And then Tamsin sneezed.

It was, I maintain to this day, genuine. The child was half-frozen and had been breathing river fog for a week. She sneezed enormously, twice, and wiped her nose on her sleeve, and Hargreave looked at her with the distaste of a fastidious man confronted with a grubby urchin, and whatever thread had been gathering between us snapped.

"Get your dead buried before full dark," he said shortly, turning away, waving the corporal. "And keep the shrouds off my road. Stamps, corporal. Move them through."

The corporal stamped. The gate swung. And we walked through the third checkpoint of our long war with five shrouds, one forged epidemic, and nothing left in the satchel but air.

* * *

We didn't speak until the palisade was behind us and the dusk had swallowed our outlines. Then Tamsin, walking between us with the handcart, said in a small fierce voice, "You lied beautifully. The water bit. I believed it and I *knew* you."

"I didn't lie." My voice came out strange. "Disturbed ground genuinely does worsen fevers. Everything I told him was true except that there was a fever." I heard myself and almost laughed, a cracked sound. "That's how he'll lose, do you see? He checks everything, he counts everything, and we beat him with truths arranged in the wrong order."

Elias said nothing. When I glanced at him, he was looking back over his shoulder at the gallows field, where lanterns were being lit along the scaffold line, one by one, twelve small flames in a rising wind.

"Tomorrow," he said quietly. "If the boy runs true tonight, Okafor has the letters by dawn and the dispatch goes out by noon. But the executions—" His fist tightened around the useless cider cup. "Mara. They hang at first light whether the dispatch reaches the coast or not. We bought the truth its life. We didn't buy those twelve theirs."

"No," I agreed. "Not with paper."

We crested the rise above the mill road, and below us Thorn Mill showed its one lit window, and behind us the nooses swung in the dark like slow clocks counting down the night, and I understood—with no dread at all, only a terrible calm—what remained to be spent.

Invisibility was gone. Names were next.

"Hargreave will want to know my name by morning," I said. "He half-has it already. So tomorrow, before he finds it, I'm going to stand in front of his gallows and give it to him—along with every name he ever struck."

Elias stopped walking. In the dark, his breath caught audibly.

"Mara—"

"He counts everything," I said. "Let him count this."

Below us, the mill window flickered as a lamp was carried to the door—and then, faint on the wind, came a sound that stopped all three of us where we stood: far off, downstream, a low groan and crack, like cannon fire under the earth.

The ice was beginning to break.
