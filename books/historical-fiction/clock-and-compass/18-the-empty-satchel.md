## 18. The Empty Satchel

They found the runner at dusk, two days after the ice broke, and Mara hated him on sight.

Not fairly—she knew that even as she felt it. He was a boy no older than Tamsin, powder-burned, one arm bound in a sling, and he had crossed forty miles of collapsing roads to keep a promise made to a dying stranger. But when he pulled the packet from inside his shirt and put it into her hands, warm from his body, and she saw the shape of it—the letters only, the oilskin gone dark with river water at one corner but the paper within dry—something in her chest closed like a fist.

"The satchel," she said. "There was a ledger. A leather ledger, this long." She held her hands apart. "Was there a ledger?"

The boy shook his head. "Only these, miss. The clerk—he made me take them before he went back over the ford. He said—" The boy hesitated, and she watched him decide whether to soften it. She was glad, in a distant way, when he didn't. "He said the rest of it was heavy. He said you'd understand."

She did understand. That was the worst of it. Elias Vane, who had spent a winter typing lies in a colonel's tent, had understood better than anyone alive what a thing weighed when it was true, and he had spent himself to carry the letters through, and her ledger—the eleven mutineers, the struck-out private whose effects she'd been ordered to burn, three years of names copied by candlelight while men snored around her—had gone under the ice with him, wrapped in an empty oilskin because the letters were already safe in her cloak.

He hadn't drowned for the record. He had drowned so she wouldn't have to.

She sat down on a crate in the baggage wagon where Okafor's people had sheltered them and held the packet without opening it. Tamsin sat beside her, hair still stiff with river salt, saying nothing, which was unlike her, and therefore everything.

* * *

Samuel Okafor was not what she had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected. He was younger than his dispatches, quick-moving, with ink permanently staining the side of his right hand, and when he read Reeve's letters by lamplight in the mill's back office he did not exclaim or swear. He simply read them twice, laid them in order by date, and began making notes.

"These will do," he said at last. "These, with your testimony, will hang him."

"They won't hang him," Mara said. "They'll hang twelve men first if we're slow."

"Eleven." He looked up. "Reeve makes eleven now. Your captain sent word ahead—a farmer's cart, a sick man smuggled past two pickets. He's in hiding south of here. The other ten are still under guard, execution set for the day the roads clear." He tapped the letters. "This is enough to stop that, if I can get a dispatch through to the press wire at Kalenberg before Hargreave can bury it. It is enough to destroy him."

"Then it's enough," Mara said. "Use it."

"It isn't whole." Okafor said it carefully, the way you tell a patient a limb must come off. "The letters prove Reeve lived after his recorded death. They prove protest at Vosk Bridge. They don't prove the roll was falsified—that the men were written down as executed deserters who never faced any court at all. That proof was yours. Every name you copied. The pattern of omissions, winter over winter." He paused. "I'm told you kept such a record for years."

"I kept it."

"Then you'll have to be the record."

She had known that too, since the ford, since Tamsin came up out of the water gasping and Elias went back across the groaning ice with a leather book against his chest. But knowing a thing and hearing it spoken aloud by a man with a printing press are different griefs, and she sat very still with her hands flat on the table and let the difference move through her like cold water moving through cloth.

Three years. Three hundred names, some nights. Boys from Lincolnshire and Lancashire, men who'd survived four campaigns and died of a bad wound badly tended, struck from the rolls so that a battalion could report itself fit and a colonel could report himself brilliant. She had never been able to save one of them. She had only been able to refuse to lose them twice—to refuse the second death, the death of the name. And now the river had done to her ledger what Hargreave had done to his casualties, and she was asked to believe the refusal still counted when there was nothing left to point to.

"Does it need me?" she heard herself say. "The truth. The letters are the truth. They'll stand without—"

"No," Okafor said. Not cruelly. "They won't. Letters can be called forgeries. A dead captain's hand can be disputed forever by men paid to dispute it. What cannot be disputed is a living witness who swears to it in front of the world and lets the world do its worst to her." He folded his hands. "I've published truths before, Mrs.—Montrose, is it? Truths that needed a witness and had none. They died in a fortnight. I will not let this one die in a fortnight. So yes. It needs you."

* * *

That night she dreamed of the river.

In the dream the ice was white and clean as fresh linen and stretched to the horizon, and beneath it, dim and far down, pages turned. Not drowning—turning, unhurried, as if some patient clerk below the world were finally reading the whole of it, every name in order, and she lay on the ice with her ear pressed to the surface trying to make out a single one, and could not, and woke with frost on the blanket and Tamsin's elbow in her ribs and dawn coming gray through the slats.

She dressed and went out into the mill yard, where the wheels stood locked in ice and the sky had the color of old pewter. Her hands were steady. They were always steady; it was the one thing the war had left her without asking payment. Steady hands, and nothing left in them.

Tamsin found her there. The girl had taken to appearing silently at Mara's elbow, as if proximity were a duty she had assigned herself, and Mara had stopped pretending she minded.

"You haven't cried," Tamsin said.

"No."

"He pulled me out. I keep thinking I should cry about that, and I can't either. It just—it happened. He just did it." Tamsin picked at the frozen knot of a rope on the fence rail. "Is that wrong?"

"It's shock," Mara said. "It keeps the account open a while longer. Cry when it closes."

"And you? When do you cry?"

Mara looked out at the fields, white to the treeline, and thought about the question honestly, because the girl had earned honesty. "When they're safe," she said. "The eleven. If they live, then I'll owe him tears, and I'll pay them. Not before. I can't afford them yet. Grief is expensive and I'm still working."

Tamsin considered this with the gravity of a girl who had buried her own family unrecorded and unwept. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and Mara understood she had just been told something back: *you count too, whether you've written yourself down or not.*

* * *

Okafor worked like a man outrunning weather, because he was. The ice on the river boomed at night now, great cracking reports like distant cannon, and each boom shortened everyone's clock. He wrote dispatches in triplicate, bribed a carter, argued with a half-pay lieutenant who had once served under Reeve and wept openly when shown the letters, and somewhere in those three days he found time to sit across from Mara with a blank page and say, "Now. From the beginning. Every name you remember."

"You want them all."

"I want the ones you can swear to. We'll start with the twelve."

And here was the thing she discovered, sitting in the lamplight with Okafor's pen scratching: she could not start with the twelve. She started with Private Daniel Hobbes of the 44th, whose effects Colonel's orders had commanded her to burn on a night so cold the ink had frozen in her well, and whose name she had copied instead onto a flyleaf by the light of a rush-candle, licking the graphite to make it mark. Then Hobbes led her backward, name by name, the way one stone in a wall holds the next. The order was wrong, the dates were approximations, and none of it mattered. The names were there. All of them, or nearly all. Three years of nights had built a shelf in her mind and loaded it, and the river had taken the book but not the library.

She recited until her voice went raw. Okafor filled six pages. When she reached the eleventh mutineer—Corporal Ashe Whitlow, who had once carried a wounded Elias Vane two miles on his back, though she hadn't known that then—she stopped.

"And the twelfth," Okafor said quietly.

"Captain Aldous Reeve." Her throat worked. "I never recorded him. He was already struck out when I began the ledger—or I believed so. He's my omission. My one gap." She heard how it sounded, how it had always sounded: the woman who wrote down everyone, failing the one man who mattered most. "Elias said—" She stopped.

"Say it anyway. It's the last thing anyone will ever get from him."

"Elias said Reeve laughed when they sentenced him. At the trial under the tree at Vosk Bridge. He said Reeve told Hargreave, 'You can starve us, sir, but you can't starve the arithmetic.' The ration figures. The letters prove the figures. So the twelfth name exists whether I wrote it or not." She met Okafor's eyes. "Write it down. Mark it as hers—mine. Let the gap be part of the record too. Let them see what she missed and why."

Okafor wrote it. Then he set down his pen and looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him say, in a voice stripped of correspondent's polish: "You know they'll call you a liar. Hargreave's people. Possibly a whore, possibly mad. Testimony costs, Mrs. Montrose. You've seen what it cost the last man who carried it."

"Yes," Mara said. "I carried him."

* * *

On the fourth morning a drab farm cart creaked into the mill yard and a tall man climbed down from it leaning on a stick, gaunt as a saint in a famine painting, and Mara knew him before he spoke, because she had imagined his voice from his handwriting for days.

"Mrs. Montrose." Captain Aldous Reeve's bow was ruined by the stick and the shaking of his hands, and he made it anyway, formally, as if manners were the last uniform he owned. "I'm told I owe you my life several times over. Once for the letters. Once for coming at all. And once for a ledger I'm told existed, containing my name."

"It didn't contain your name," Mara said. "That was my failure."

"Then we're matched," Reeve said, "because my existence failed your ledger. Between us, perhaps, the accounts balance." He smiled, and it was terrible and wonderful, a smile that had survived a sentence of death. "I've come to ask you something, and I'll ask it plainly because I haven't the strength for indirection. Okafor says you'll testify. That they'll try to destroy you for it. I watched twelve good men refuse to beg and be sentenced for it. I would rather you refused. Disappear. Tamsin goes with you. The letters alone may serve."

"The letters alone will die in a fortnight," Mara said. "Your correspondent told me so himself."

"Then let them die and you live."

"No," she said, and heard Elias in it, and let herself hear him. "I spent three years writing down men nobody would speak for. I'm not going to stop speaking for them because it's become inconvenient. Besides—" and here the steadiness of her hands finally broke, just slightly, just enough that she had to grip her stick of a spine straighter, "—someone has to finish the record. It's missing a twelfth entry. It's missing a clerk."

* * *

That evening Okafor sealed his dispatches, and the carter took them north through the booming dark toward the wire at Kalenberg, and there was nothing left to do but wait, which was the work Mara knew best. She sat in the lamplight with Tamsin asleep against her shoulder and did what she had done every night for three years: she took out a pencil stub and, on the back of Okafor's discarded draft sheets, began to write.

Not the ledger. She could not rebuild the ledger; the ledger was at the bottom of the river with a man who had chosen the truth over his own breath, and she was done pretending that grief could be filed and shelved. This was something else. A letter, unsent, unwritable, addressed to no address:

*Elias — You would laugh at this. You'd say a letter to a drowned man is just ink looking for a grave. But you taught me what paper is for, so you'll bear it.*

*The letters reached him. Okafor has them in order by date, and he read them twice, and he is not a man who reads anything twice unless it will burn. The eleven are not saved yet. But the machine is moving, and it is moving because of you, and I need you to know that I am not going to be quiet about whose fault that was.*

*You forged part of the order. You typed the lie and then you unmade it. I never gave you absolution and I don't give it to you now. I give you something better. I give you witnesses.*

*Hobbes. Whitlow. Kessler, Price, Anand, Doyle, Marchetti, the brothers Vosk themselves. Reeve. Eleven names I'll say aloud in front of whatever court they dare to convene, and a twelfth I'll write in myself, in my own hand, in the space I left open all along without knowing it was for you.*

*The ice is breaking. I can hear it from here. It sounds like artillery, and I find I am no longer afraid of artillery.*

She stopped. The lamp guttered. Downriver the ice gave another long report, then another, the river shouldering off its winter, and Mara Montrose sat in the sound of it with sleeping Tamsin warm against her side and the draft-sheets covered in her cramped, steady hand, and understood that she had answered the question she'd carried for three days without naming it.

Truth did not need its witness. Truth got along; truth leaked; truth floated up eventually like anything lighter than the water that drowned it. But justice—justice was vain and deaf and needed to be told things to its face, repeatedly, by someone with standing, and standing was bought exactly the way Elias had bought it, and the way she was about to.

Tamsin stirred. "Is it morning?"

"Not yet."

"Then why are you smiling?"

Because in the bottom of her satchel, wrapped in the cloak she'd given to the letters, Mara had found that morning a single loose page that had fallen from the ledger in the ford's chaos and ridden in her baggage unnoticed for four days—Daniel Hobbes, 44th, in her own hand, the first name she'd ever copied, the flyleaf leaf itself—and it proved nothing and saved no one and it was, God help her, the beginning of the record, recovered.

"Because I found a page," she said.

Outside, hooves on the frozen mud, many of them, coming fast toward the mill. Tamsin sat bolt upright. Mara blew out the lamp and slid the recovered page into her shirt, over her heart, where it could feel the argument it was going to start.

A fist hammered the door. "Open in the name of Colonel the Honourable Bertram Hargreave! A warrant for the arrest of the nurse, Mara Montrose!"

She put her hand on Tamsin's head, and waited for the door to break, and was not afraid.
