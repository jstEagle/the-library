## 20. Tamsin's Choice

They came for Tamsin on a Tuesday, when the mud at Thorn Mill had gone soft enough to swallow boots to the ankle and the war had retreated to something you mostly heard about secondhand.

She was mending a strap on the mill's water wheel housing—honest work, the kind the quartermaster's wife had found her after the trial began, the kind that kept her hands busy so her head didn't have to do anything but count stitches. A lieutenant and a sergeant-at-arms stood at the gate. The lieutenant had a new uniform and an old smile, the kind officers used on orphans and horses.

"Tamsin Corr?" he said. She'd given them the surname herself. It wasn't hers. She'd taken it off a dead woman in the baggage train two winters back because a girl with no name got assigned nowhere and fed nothing. The dead didn't mind sharing.

"That's me."

"Come inside out of the wet. There's tea." Tea. From an officer. Her belly did a slow, suspicious turn, because in her experience tea meant someone wanted something, and the more cups they poured you, the bigger the something was.

* * *

The room they took her to smelled of lamp oil and paper. A captain sat behind a table stacked with dispatch boxes, and he introduced himself as Captain Merrow, adjutant to the court-martial commission, which Tamsin understood to mean: the man whose job it was to make sure the court-martial went the way the army wanted it to go.

"You've been staying with Mrs. Quill at the mill," Merrow said. "Good woman. Steady. And before that, you were with the baggage train of the Fourth Column. Two years." He said it like he'd memorized it, and Tamsin thought: he has. He knows my teeth better than the army dentist does.

"I did all right," she said carefully.

"You did more than all right." He slid a paper across the table. She couldn't read fast, but she could read numbers, and the number on this paper was a shilling a day plus rations, plus a billet, plus the words *regimental apprentice, clerk-general* in a hand so fine it looked printed. "There's a place for girls who serve the regiment well. Not many places. But they exist, for girls with steady hands and quiet mouths."

Tamsin looked at the paper for a long time. A shilling a day. Rations. A bed with a roof that didn't leak. Two years she'd slept in wagons and haylofts and once, memorably, inside an overturned caisson with a gunnery sergeant snoring three feet from her ear. Two years she'd eaten what fell off the backs of better-fed people.

"What would I have to do for it?" she asked, because Captain Merrow seemed like a man who appreciated directness, and because she already knew the answer and wanted to hear how he dressed it up.

Merrow folded his hands. "Nurse Montrose has given testimony that is—" he paused, choosing the word with tongs— "*contested*. She claims to have memorized hundreds of names. Years of names. Names no one else wrote down. The commission finds this difficult to credit. A woman alone, keeping a secret ledger through a winter campaign? It strains belief."

"It's true," Tamsin said. "I saw the ledger."

"The commission understands you were traveling with Nurse Montrose for part of her journey." His voice stayed pleasant, warm as the tea going cold between them. "Traveling with her, and with the clerk Vane. What the commission would find helpful—what would settle the matter cleanly—is your own recollection. That the ledger you saw was small. Disorganized. Perhaps a few names, jotted idly. Perhaps not even a ledger at all. Girls misremember, under strain. No one would blame you."

"And if I remember it big?" Tamsin asked. "Full of names?"

The warmth went out of the room by degrees, like heat leaving a corpse. Merrow didn't raise his voice. Officers like him never had to.

"Then there is no apprenticeship," he said. "And there is also no billet, no rations, and no protection, because a girl of fourteen with no name of her own—I've read the muster rolls, Miss Corr; I know whose name you're wearing—has very few ways to remain fed through a winter except by the goodwill of people like me. People like the regiment." He pushed the tea toward her. "Drink that. Think it over. You have until tomorrow, nine o'clock, when the commission sits. Most girls in your position don't need until tomorrow."

Tamsin picked up the cup. She drank the tea slowly, all of it, because free tea was free tea and she hadn't survived two years of war by wasting anything. Then she set the cup down, thanked him for it, polite as church, and walked out into the mud.

* * *

Mrs. Quill asked no questions, which was one reason Tamsin liked her. But Mara was there that evening—she'd come out from the town where she was lodging while the tribunal sat, bringing bread and a jar of rendered fat for the mill's cracked hands—and Tamsin watched her across the kitchen and thought about what fourteen months with a person teaches you.

Mara Montrose moved like someone trying not to be noticed, even now, even when noticing her was the whole point of the season. Every newspaper in the country was printing her name. *The Nurse Who Kept the Ledger.* Men at the mill gate tipped their hats to her. Women pressed letters into her hands—letters from mothers asking, *did you write down my son, was my son one of yours?*—and Mara answered every one, in her flat careful hand, and never told anyone she did it.

But she still walked like a woman crossing a sentry line. Some habits were load-bearing.

"You've been quiet," Mara said, when Mrs. Quill stepped out to the well. "Quieter than usual, and you're usually quiet enough to worry a body."

"They offered me a shilling a day," Tamsin said.

Mara went still the way she went still when a wound started bleeding faster than it should—not panic, just everything narrowing down to the work. "For what?"

"For remembering it small." Tamsin turned the strap she was mending over in her lap. She'd taken it up without noticing, thumbs working the awl holes, and she made herself stop. Made herself sit with her hands open instead, the way Elias used to sit when he was telling the truth about something ugly. "A clerk apprenticeship. Rations. A dry bed. All I have to say tomorrow is that your ledger was a few scribbles. That girls misremember."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs the mill's other children—one of Mrs. Quill's grand-nieces and a boy evacuated from the coast—thumped around like furniture falling downstairs.

"And what did you tell them?" Mara asked quietly.

"I drank their tea," Tamsin said. "I haven't told them anything yet."

Something crossed Mara's face—not relief. Tamsin knew Mara well enough to know it wouldn't be relief. It was grief wearing relief's coat, because Mara had spent a lifetime learning that everyone could be bought, and it wounded her fresh each time she met someone who couldn't.

"Tamsin." Mara sat down across from her, knees nearly touching. "Listen to me. You take the apprenticeship."

Tamsin's stomach dropped. "What?"

"You heard me. Take it. You're fourteen years old, you've been hungry since before I met you, and you owe the army nothing—least of all the truth, which they've done nothing but spend and abuse since before either of us could walk. Take the shilling. Take the bed. I will testify alone and I will testify well and I will not have one more thing on my conscience that has a face."

"No," Tamsin said.

Mara blinked. In fourteen months Tamsin had seen her argue with surgeons, stare down a checkpoint sergeant, and lie to a colonel's face with her pulse steady as a clock—but Tamsin had never contradicted her outright, and it landed visibly, like a stone in still water.

"No," Tamsin said again, and found her voice didn't shake. "You want to know why I'm going to stand up tomorrow and say what I saw? It's not for Hargreave's twelve. They're saved already; your mouth did that. And it's not even for you." She swallowed. Everything she said next came out rough, like it was being dragged up from somewhere deep and stored-away. "Elias carried those letters four hundred miles on legs that were freezing off him, and at the end he carried them into a river rather than let the dragoons have them. He never once asked me to carry anything. He used to give me the first bite of his ration and pretend he wasn't hungry, like I didn't know, like I couldn't see his hands shaking. And the last night, at the chapel crypt, when we thought the dragoons were coming down, he said—if they get through, get the girl out, whatever it costs. Like I was the cargo. Like I was worth more than the letters."

Her eyes were stinging and she let them sting. She'd cried enough in the baggage train to learn to do it without stopping her mouth.

"He thought I was worth saving," she said. "Fine. So I'll be worth something. You taught me that nobody counts unless somebody writes them down. Well, I can write. I've been practicing my letters since Thorn Mill, ask Mrs. Quill. Tomorrow I'm going to stand in front of that commission and I'm going to say: I saw the ledger. This wide." She held her hands apart. "This full. Eleven mutineers' names and a hundred more besides, privates struck from the rolls for dying wrong, and I'll say the clerk Vane died getting it delivered, and I'll say it loud enough that the stenographer can't leave it out. And then they can keep their shilling, because I'll have spent the only coin I had, and I'd rather spend it than save it for people like Merrow."

The fire burned. Upstairs the children thumped and laughed. Mara reached across and took Tamsin's hands—her palms cracked from lye and cold, the nurse's hands that had stitched half a winter's worth of soldiers back together—and held them hard.

"You sound like him," Mara said finally, and her voice cracked too, just at the seam. "You sound exactly like him."

"That's the idea," Tamsin said. "I'm going to be him when I grow up. Except taller."

Mara laughed—the short, startled laugh Tamsin had coaxed out of her maybe five times total, ever—and for a moment they just sat there, two orphans of different sizes holding hands over a mending basket, which was, Tamsin reflected, the closest thing to family she had ever actually seen working.

* * *

The commission sat in the assize hall at nine, and it was packed. Word had gotten out; word always got out now; Okafor's dispatches had made sure of that. Farmers filled the back benches. Newspapermen crowded the aisle with pencils sharpened down to stubs. Colonel Hargreave sat at the defense table in his best uniform, grey at the temples, spine straight as a pikestaff, wearing the expression of a man being persecuted by ingratitude.

Tamsin waited through three hours of surveyors and quartermasters and a farrier who talked forever about horse shoes. Then Merrow caught her eye from the counsel bench, and gave her the small nod of a man purchasing goods he believed already paid for, and Tamsin felt her heart go once, hard, against her ribs like a fist knocking to be let out.

Then Mara was called, and Tamsin listened to her do the thing Tamsin had watched her do at checkpoints and dressing stations a dozen times: become perfectly calm, perfectly plain, perfectly unignorable. She recited names. Dozens of them. Private William Acker, struck for fever, recorded dead in action. Private James Hollis, struck for a leg lost at the ford. She recited them the way she'd recited them at the first checkpoint, the way Elias insisted on at the wire—names as incantation—and the hall went so quiet Tamsin could hear the stenographer's nib scratching.

Then Merrow rose, smooth as oiled leather, and said, "Madam President, the commission has heard remarkable claims of memory. But we have one further witness—a child, I must flag it, and one formerly attached to the accused's party—who may assist the commission in weighing these accounts."

He turned to Tamsin and smiled his purchased smile.

Tamsin walked to the stand on legs that felt borrowed. The oath book was cold under her palm. Somewhere behind the press benches, Mara sat absolutely still, and Tamsin understood suddenly that Mara was praying—not to God, particularly, but to her, to the girl, to the last unspent thing.

State your name, they said, and Tamsin almost smiled, because that was the easy trap, the first little test, and Merrow thought he had her.

"My name's Tamsin," she said. "Surname Corr. Which isn't mine—I took it off a dead woman in the baggage train because a girl with no name gets no rations. The army knows that. Captain Merrow knows that. He read my file." A rustle went through the hall. Merrow's face didn't move, but something behind it did. "I'm telling you so you understand I know exactly what things are worth. He offered me a shilling a day this morning to forget what I saw. I'm here to tell you for free."

The president leaned forward. "And what did you see, Miss Corr?"

Tamsin looked at Mara. Mara looked back, and shook her head once, tiny—a last offer, *you can still take the door*—and Tamsin squared her shoulders the way Elias used to when the ice groaned under him and he kept walking anyway.

"I saw the ledger," she said, clear, carrying, the voice of a girl who'd learned to shout down a baggage column. "It was this wide and this thick, wrapped in oilskin same as Captain Reeve's letters. I helped carry it. I saw eleven names of the men at Vosk Bridge, written before anyone knew they mattered. And I saw a man die at the third checkpoint making sure those pages got through, and his name was Elias Vane, and he was a clerk, and he chose the truth. So you can write that down. I know somebody in this room gets paid to."

Silence—then the hall broke like a dam, shouting and pencil-scratching and the president hammering for order, and through the middle of it all Tamsin watched Merrow lean back very slowly in his chair, watching her with the flat reassessing look of a man doing sums that have come out badly, and behind him, at the defense table, Colonel Aldous Hargreave's counsel bent and whispered something in the colonel's ear—

—and Hargreave, grey-faced, turned his head and looked directly at Mara Montrose, and then at the doors of the hall, where two military policemen had just appeared, and Tamsin felt the temperature of the whole room change.
