## 21. The Names Read Aloud

The thaw came to Thorn Mill before the truth did, though only by a week.

Mara stood at the window of Okafor's printing room and watched the river run brown and swollen where the ice had been. It was the same water, she told herself, that had taken the oilskin, the ledger, Elias. Water did not keep things. That was why she had trusted paper all those years—paper could be hidden, folded, smuggled in a linen trunk. But she had learned in the last month that water was not the only thing that failed to hold what it was given. Memory held too, if you fed it daily, if you recited it like a prayer until the names wore grooves into you.

Okafor had been printing for six days straight. She could hear the press below her, rhythmic as a heart, and the smell of ink had worked itself into her clothes and hair until she no longer noticed it. Each morning a boy ran the fresh sheets to the post rider, and each evening the rider returned with word: the dispatches were being read aloud in coffee houses, nailed to church doors, copied out by clerks who did not know their own copies would be copied again.

*The Vosk Bridge Twelve*, the headline read. *What the Army Struck Out.* Beneath it, in columns tight as stitches, Samuel Okafor had laid out everything: the sold rations, the forged order, Hargreave's signature on a lie. He had printed Mara's name. Not hers alone—Reeve's letters named him too—but when she first saw it there, *nurse M. Montrose, late of the Third Dressing Station*, she had felt the same vertigo as stepping off a kerb in darkness. For seven years her skill had been not being seen. Now her name sat in print in three cities, and there was no folding it away again.

She found she did not want to.

"You'll wear a groove in the floor," Okafor said from behind her. He was a broad man with ink-stained cuffs and a voice pitched for dictation even at rest. "Sit. Eat something. The mud will be hours yet."

"The mud is what I'm waiting for."

"I know." He set a bowl of barley soup beside her without ceremony. He had learned that about her already—she did not take well to being fussed over, but she never refused food placed within reach. A winter of feeding others first had carved its habits deep.

"Tell me again," he said, sitting across from her, "about the second checkpoint. The surgeon."

"You have it in my statement."

"I have it in your statement. I want it in your voice. There's a difference—the difference between a record and a story, and I'm learning the army only fears one of them."

Mara wrapped her hands around the bowl. The warmth was ordinary, and she had learned to notice ordinary things now, because the extraordinary ones still arrived without warning—an oilskin satchel glimpsed in a stranger's hands, a body of water—and took her breath like a blow.

"His name was Corvin," she said. "He'd treated my wounded at Kesselbach two years before. He looked at my papers, then at my face, and I watched him do the arithmetic. I told him about the twelve men. All twelve. And he stamped us through and burned his own casualty roll in front of his own sergeant." She paused. "He said—" She stopped. She had not told anyone this part.

"Say it anyway," Okafor said. "I'll decide whether it prints."

"He said: *I've been keeping a list of my own for years. I thought I was the only one mad enough.*"

Okafor wrote nothing down. He just looked at her for a long moment, and then he nodded once, the way men nod over a card game when they realize the hand they're holding is better than they knew.

"There are more of you," he said quietly.

"There always were. We just never signed each other's work."

Below them the press thundered on, page after page, eleven hundred copies by nightfall. Eleven hundred chances that somewhere, in some garrison or infirmary or quartermaster's office, another keeper of a quiet ledger would read the names and know they were not alone.

* * *

The orders came down on the fourth day of publication, carried by a captain who would not meet anyone's eyes: the court-martial findings suspended, the eleven surviving prisoners released into regimental custody pending review—which everyone understood meant *pending public opinion*, which had never before weighed anything in the army and now weighed everything.

They would march through Thorn Mill. Past the mill, past the press house, along the road that followed the river. Nobody said so officially, but everyone in the village knew, because Okafor had made sure of it the way he made sure of everything: he printed the route.

Mara stood in the road an hour before they came. The spring mud was spectacular—weeks of frost letting go all at once, the ground churning brown and sucking at boots and cart wheels alike. Tamsin stood beside her, having refused with great formality to be anywhere else, and had insisted on wearing her good shawl, which was to say her only shawl, washed and pressed for the occasion.

"You're gripping my hand too hard," Tamsin observed.

"I know."

"You're going to break my fingers."

"That's possible."

Tamsin did not pull away. Fourteen years old and wise enough not to name grief directly; she simply stood closer, so that the grip became something else, a thing shared rather than suffered.

The crowd along the road was small—a village does not turn out in the hundreds—but dense: millworkers, a few soldiers' wives, Okafor's boys with ink on their fingers, the innkeeper who had begun refusing to serve Hargreave's dragoons the moment the first dispatch hit his tables. They stood in the mud and waited, and the waiting had a texture to it, a hush that was not empty but full, like the silence before a surgeon makes the first cut.

Then the column came around the bend of the road.

Eleven men. That was all. No escort of honor—they had been marched out under guard as prisoners and turned loose under guard as something else, and the guards hung back now, uncertain of their own role, weapons slung. The eleven walked in loose formation, and they were thin, thin the way men are thin after a winter of punishment rations and cells, but their backs were straight, and someone among them had gotten hold of a drum, and the drum beat slow time, not a march but a procession, the kind of beat you use for carrying something heavy and precious.

Mara counted them without meaning to. It was the habit of a lifetime: count the wounded, count the dead, count what remains.

Eleven.

One short. One name struck from every roll, drowned in every river, missing from every muster since Vosk Bridge. Her chest did the thing it had been doing for weeks—closing around a shape that was not there.

She had rewritten the list from memory. It had taken her four nights at Okafor's table, writing until her hand cramped, reconstructing every entry from seven years of nightly copying: the men struck from the rolls as deserters, the men written off as dead who had died of wounds the army would not spend money treating, and finally the twelve of Vosk Bridge, eleven living and one whose fate she had never recorded, whose absence from her own pages had started all of this. Okafor had printed it as an appendix. *A Roll of Those Erased, Restored by One Who Kept Count.* Eleven hundred copies, and here in her apron pocket she carried a single sheet of it, folded twice.

She had told herself she brought it for the men. So they could see their own names come back.

She knew, standing in the mud, that she also brought it because Elias was not in the crowd, and the list was the closest thing to him she could carry.

The column drew level with her.

The eleven men were looking straight ahead, discipline of long habit, but the crowd was calling names now—wives shouting, mothers, a woman with a baby held up like an offering—and one by one the discipline broke. A man broke ranks to catch a woman who had stopped pretending to stand. Another took a child on his shoulders. The formation dissolved into embraces, and the mud took them all, and nobody minded.

Except one.

Captain Aldous Reeve did not go to anyone. He walked forward alone, out of the dissolved column, a gaunt man with a beard gone grey at thirty and a limp he would keep for life, and he crossed the churned road toward where Mara stood, and the crowd quieted around him without being asked to.

He stopped an arm's length away. Up close she could see what the winter had written on him: the hollowed cheeks, the frost-split lips healed badly, the eyes of a man who had spent months waiting to die and then not died.

"You're Montrose," he said. "The nurse."

"I am."

"Elias Vane spoke of you." His voice caught on the name, steadied. "He said there was a woman who kept a book the army didn't know it was missing."

"He exaggerated."

"He said," Reeve went on, as if she had not spoken, "that if any man could carry those letters to the end of the earth, it was the woman who'd already been carrying everyone else for seven years." Reeve looked down at his own hands, at the mud on them, and when he looked up his eyes were wet and furious and alive. "I owe him a debt I can't repay. I owe you one I might."

Mara reached into her apron pocket.

Her fingers shook—not from cold; the morning was almost warm—and unfolded the single printed sheet, and held it out.

"Your name is on it," she said. "It was the only one I never managed to record. Everything else, seven years—I had them all. You were my one omission." Her voice did what voices do; she let it. "So I wrote you back in from memory. And I put Elias at the top of a new page, because he chose the truth, and I intend to keep choosing it, and somebody has to keep the count."

Reeve took the sheet the way men take sacraments.

He read it. He read it slowly, moving his lips over the names of his own men, and Mara watched him arrive at each one the way a surgeon arrives at a wound he knows he can close. When he reached his own name, Aldous Reeve, captain, restored, his jaw worked and nothing came out for a while.

Then he did something no one expected, least of all him.

He saluted her.

A full salute, correct to the regulation, a captain's salute rendered to a nurse standing in the mud of a mill road, and behind him, one by one, ten ragged men who had been sentenced to die for asking to be fed turned and saw what their captain was doing, and ten ragged hands rose in ten ragged salutes, and the crowd made a sound Mara had never heard from a crowd before—not cheering, something lower and larger, a sound like a river deciding its course.

"Hold onto that page," Reeve said, lowering his arm. "There will be others who need to see it. There are always others."

"I know," Mara said. "That's rather the problem. And rather the point."

Reeve almost smiled. Then his face went serious, and he leaned close, and what he said next was quiet enough that only she and Tamsin heard it.

"There's something else you should know before the trial resumes. Something I couldn't put in the letters—it wasn't safe to write. Hargreave wasn't working alone. The ration contracts trace higher than a colonel's pay grade, and there are men in the capital who will want you discredited before you testify. Okafor has enemies now too. Watch your post. Watch your witnesses." He glanced at Tamsin. "Watch each other."

He straightened, touched his brow once more, and limped back to his men.

The column re-formed, or something like it, and moved off down the river road toward whatever the world would make of them, and the drum faded, and the crowd began to disperse into the ordinary business of a spring afternoon, and Mara stood in the mud holding a single sheet of paper with both hands.

Tamsin slipped her own hand back into Mara's free one.

"You're doing the face," Tamsin said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting something. Your lips move."

Mara realized she had been doing it—mouthing names, the whole list, beginning to end, an old habit newly load-bearing. She stopped, and then, deliberately, did not stop.

"Someone has to keep the count," she said.

"You could teach me," Tamsin said. "To keep a ledger, I mean. A proper one. In case there's ever another you, or another me, and only one of us."

Mara looked down at her—the sharp chin, the washed-and-pressed shawl, the eyes that had watched baggage trains swallow children whole and decided to become unswallowable—and felt the future shift its weight, the way ice shifts before it decides.

"Yes," she said. "First lesson tonight."

But even as she said it, she was looking at the road where Reeve had vanished, hearing again his low warning—*higher than a colonel's pay grade*—and thinking of the trial resuming in nine days, of summons she carried in the same pocket as the list, of a courtroom in the capital where men with clean hands and expensive coats were right now deciding how much a nurse's memory was worth and how cheaply it could be buried.

Okafor appeared at her shoulder, notebook already open.

"Mara," he said. "We have a problem. A letter came on the post rider an hour ago. It's marked with the seal of the Judge Advocate's office—and it isn't addressed to me."

He handed it to her. Her name was written across the front in a hand she recognized, because she had seen it ordering her to burn a dead private's effects, and seen it again on a dinner invitation, and seen it once through glass while dragoons searched a chapel above her head.

Colonel Hargreave, writing from his cell, had requested a visitor before his defense called its first witness.

And he had asked for her.
