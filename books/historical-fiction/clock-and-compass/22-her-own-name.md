## 22. Her Own Name

The stationer's shop in Thorn Mill smelled of glue and lamp oil and the particular dust of paper that has waited a long time for someone to need it. Mara stood at the counter with her discharge pay folded in her palm—nine weeks' wages, less the deductions they had invented to keep it small—and looked at the ledgers on the shelf behind the old man's head.

There were three. One was fat and expensive, bound in calf leather with a marbled endpaper, the kind a merchant would use to track shipments of wool. One was thin and cheap, its cover already curling. And one sat between them: plain black cloth over board, sewn signatures, ruled lines fine enough to hold a name without crowding it.

"That one," she said.

The stationer brought it down and set it on the counter between them like something being weighed. "For accounts?"

"For names."

He didn't ask what kind of names. He was an old man in a town that had watched an army pass through it twice, once going toward the war and once coming back from it, and he had learned which questions were worth the asking. He wrapped the ledger in brown paper anyway, out of habit, and Mara unwrapped it before she was even out the door, because she had spent a winter learning that things hidden are things that can be lost, and she was done hiding.

Tamsin was waiting on the step, sitting with her knees drawn up, wearing the coat Mara had bought her from the rag stall two streets back. It was too big. Tamsin had rolled the sleeves twice and declared it fashionable, and Mara had not argued, because there were battles left in the world but that was not one of them.

"Well?" Tamsin said. "Is it right?"

Mara turned the ledger over in her hands. The cloth was smooth and cold. "It's right."

"Then write in it." Tamsin stood up, restless the way she always was when a thing mattered, as if stillness might jinx it. "You've been walking around all morning like a woman going to a funeral. It's a book, Mara. Books are for the living."

* * *

They took a room above the Wheelwright's Arms because it was the cheapest and because the window faced east, and Mara had discovered since the thaw that she could no longer sleep in a room that didn't get the morning. She told herself this was practicality. She knew it was because she had spent too many nights in rooms where the light never came, writing by stub of candle, and some part of her had made a vow she hadn't spoken aloud.

She set the ledger on the table. She sharpened the pencil with a knife, turning it slowly, letting the shavings fall, and Tamsin watched her from the bed with the patience of someone who had learned that Mara Montrose did things in order or not at all.

"You'll want the date," Tamsin said.

"I have the date."

"And the place."

"I have the place."

"And you'll spell his name right. Elias Vane. E-L-I—"

"Tamsin."

"I'm only saying. You'd never forgive yourself."

Mara almost smiled. It came partway and stopped, the way her smiles had done for weeks now, arriving and then remembering themselves. She opened the ledger to the first page. The lines ran clean and white across it, waiting, and she understood suddenly why men feared blank pages—not because of what they might write, but because of what the page demanded they admit.

That it was real. That it had happened. That someone had to be the one who said so.

She wrote the date. She wrote the place. Then, in the careful hand she had used through two winters of dressing stations and burial details and nights by candlelight while officers slept two tents away:

*Elias Vane. Clerk. Who chose the truth.*

She stopped there, pencil hovering, because the line wanted more and she did not know how much more a ledger could hold. In the old ledger—the one at the bottom of the river now, gone with him—she had written only names, ranks, dates of death. Facts stripped bare, because facts were what survived scrutiny. But this was a new book, and no officer would ever strike anything from it, and she found that her hand wanted to say what the old rules had forbidden.

*Bore the letters past three checkpoints,* she wrote. *Forged the order that saved eleven lives and lost his own. Went under at the third crossing carrying nothing but an empty oilskin, so that I would not carry it either.*

She read it back. It was not a ledger entry. It was closer to an epitaph, and she thought perhaps that was allowed. Perhaps a woman who had recorded only deaths for two years had earned the right to record one life properly.

"Read it to me," Tamsin said quietly.

Mara read it aloud. Her voice held. She noted this the way she noted a pulse—steady, sixty to the minute, no cause for alarm.

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then: "He'd have hated it. All that fuss."

"He'd have pretended to hate it."

"He'd have made a joke about spelling."

"He spelled worse than any clerk I ever met," Mara said. "I saw his requisitions. He wrote *flour* with an extra *o* half the time."

"He said it was the cold."

"It was not the cold."

And then, absurdly, horribly, wonderfully, they were both laughing—laughing in the rented room above the Wheelwright's Arms with the evening coming down and the river running free somewhere below the town, ice long broken, water moving the way water is supposed to move. Mara laughed until her ribs hurt and then laughed a little more because the hurt felt earned, and when it passed she wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and picked up the pencil again.

Because there was one entry left.

* * *

She had thought about this moment every day since the trial. Through the weeks of Okafor's dispatches spreading outward from Thorn Mill like frost across a window. Through the court-martial, where she had stood in a room full of gold braid and recited names until a colonel's face went the color of tallow—Hargreave, stripped of rank, his voice cracking on the word *order* as though order were a prayer that had stopped working. Through the morning the sentence was read out and Tamsin had gripped her hand so hard the bones ached, and neither of them had let go until it was done.

Through all of it, she had known this entry was coming. She had avoided thinking about why it frightened her more than the checkpoints had.

She wrote:

*Mara Montrose. Nurse.*

Four words. She stared at them. They looked small on the page, smaller than Elias's entry, smaller than any of the hundreds of names she had copied by candlelight while the wind tried the tent poles. Those men had been struck from the rolls by men with power, and her whole work had been to put them back—to insist, line by line, that a person erased is not a person unmade.

But no one had struck her name from anything. That was the thing she could not make Tamsin understand, the thing she had circled for weeks without landing. No one had erased Mara Montrose. She had done it herself, carefully, professionally, for years. She had made herself the instrument and not the record, the hand and not the page, because invisible women survive wars that visible ones do not. She had watched it work. She had watched it keep her alive through Hargreave's dinner table and the chapel crypt and the gallows field, where her invisibility had finally been spent like the last coin in a purse.

And now the war was over, the trial was done, the eleven men were alive and Reeve had saluted her in the spring mud, and the invisibility remained, out of habit, like a scar that serves no purpose but stays.

Below her own name she added the date. Then, after a pause long enough that Tamsin shifted on the bed:

*Present. Accounted for.*

"There," Tamsin said, when Mara showed her. "Was that so hard?"

"Yes."

"I know." Tamsin took the ledger gently, turned it in her hands, looking at the two entries as though checking sums. "It's a good book. Plain. Honest." She handed it back. "Where does it go?"

"With me," Mara said. "Everywhere. Like the other one."

"The other one drowned."

"No," Mara said. "The other one finished. This one starts."

* * *

They left Thorn Mill on a Tuesday, on foot, because the coach cost more than Mara was willing to pay and because Tamsin claimed—loudly, repeatedly, to anyone within earshot—that her legs were the finest legs in the county and deserved the exercise. They walked south along the river road, where the willows were breaking into leaf and the mud pulled at their boots with the cheerful malice of spring.

The hospital was two days' walk. A civilian institution, run by a Mrs. Quennell, a widow who had lost a son at Vosk Bridge and who had written to Mara after the dispatches appeared—a letter Mara kept folded inside the new ledger, next to the page with Elias's name. *I have six beds and no nurse,* the letter said. *I am told you count people. Come and count mine.*

*I am told you count people.* Of everything printed about her that spring—all of Okafor's fine language about courage and conscience—that was the sentence Mara kept returning to. Not *heroine*. Not *witness*. Someone had understood that the whole of it, the ledger and the river and the courtroom, had come down to counting. To refusing, with a pencil, the arithmetic of powerful men.

Halfway through the first day they passed a column of soldiers marching north, replacements for a garrison being rebuilt, young faces under old caps. Tamsin watched them go with an expression Mara couldn't read, and Mara remembered the regimental apprenticeship the army had offered the girl—quietly, through a sergeant with kind eyes and orders he clearly disliked. Silence in exchange for a trade, a uniform, a future.

Tamsin had stood in the middle of the courtroom instead and corroborated every word, fourteen years old and steady as a surgeon's hand, and Mara had understood in that moment that whatever family was—if it was anything at all—it was this: people who choose each other when choosing costs.

"What are you staring at?" Tamsin said now.

"Nothing. Walking."

"You're staring at me like I'm a ledger entry."

"I stare at everyone like that. It's my profession."

They walked on. Toward evening they crested a low hill and saw the town where they would spend the night, smoke rising straight in the still air, a church spire, fields going green. Tamsin sat down abruptly on a milestone and began retying a bootlace she did not need to retie, and Mara recognized the maneuver because she had invented it—stopping when you cannot bear to arrive, delaying the moment a hope becomes a thing that can be refused.

"She'll take us," Tamsin said, to the bootlace. "Won't she? Both of us. She said you, but she didn't say me. Fourteen-year-olds aren't nurses. I'd be scrubbing floors."

"Probably."

"Then why are we—"

"Because Mrs. Quennell lost a son," Mara said, "and she counts people too. And because you can read and write and add figures faster than I can, and because someone in that hospital is going to need to learn what you learned this winter, and it won't come from me. It comes from being fourteen and deciding who you are while everyone tells you that you're nobody." She paused. "Also, I asked her for two beds. She agreed to two beds."

Tamsin looked up. For a moment her face was entirely unguarded, the way it had been the first day on the baggage road, before she'd learned to armor herself in cheek—and then the armor came back up, but slower than before, and Mara suspected that someday soon it might not come up at all.

"You asked before you told me."

"I'm telling you now."

"That's cheating."

"That's planning," Mara said. "It's also my profession."

* * *

That night, in a room above another inn, in another town on the way to another life, Mara opened the ledger one last time before sleeping. Not to write. Just to look. Two entries on the first page, the ink dry, the lines holding.

Somewhere south of here, eleven men were planting a spring crop on land the army had granted them in settlement, and Captain Aldous Reeve—who signed his letters with a loop on the *R* that she could have drawn from memory—was among them, alive against all documentation. Somewhere a colonel sat in a cell considering what order was worth when it was built on lies. Somewhere Samuel Okafor was arguing with a printer about whether the full list of names should run on the front page or the second, and winning.

And somewhere beneath a river that no longer froze, a satchel's empty oilskin lay in the silt, and a man named Elias Vane rested with it, counted at last, accounted for.

Mara closed the book. She set it on the table beside the letter from Mrs. Quennell, and she blew out the candle, and in the dark she heard Tamsin breathing in the next bed, slow and even, a child's rhythm becoming a woman's.

Two days to the hospital. Six beds. A lifetime of names, most of them not yet known to her, waiting in wards and lanes and towns she had never seen, all of them wanting the same simple thing she had wanted all along—for someone to pick up a pencil and refuse to look away.

She slept. In the morning, they walked.
