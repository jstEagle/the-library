## 2. A Bundle Against the Door

Dawn came to the harbor the way it always did in late autumn — not arriving so much as leaking, a gray seepage through the fog that made the masts across the street look like the ribs of drowned ships. Theo Nystrom had been awake all night, and the hour meant nothing to her anymore except as a change in the quality of light against the workshop's back wall, where the plaster held the shadow of the press like a stain that could not be washed out.

She had been sorting type since midnight. It was work she did with her hands and none of her mind, the way her father had taught her: *let the fingers know their letters, and the head can go wherever it must to survive.* The California job case lay open before her, its compartments a small city of lead, and she moved down its streets with a composing stick in her left hand, returning each escaped letter to its district. A nine. Two e's gone soft at the shoulders from too many years of pressing against paper. An ampersand worn nearly faceless, more ghost than glyph.

Outside, the first cart of the morning rattled over the cobbles on Krämerstrasse, and the driver coughed the wet, deep cough of a man who stood on open water for a living. Then silence again. Then gulls. Then the groan of the customs house shutters going up, which was the true bell of the city's morning, more reliable than any church.

Theo reached for the last stray sort at the edge of the case and heard the sound.

It was small. So small that in another season she would have folded it into the general noise of the harbor and thought no more of it — a settling of wood, a rat behind the flour sacks, wind finding the gap under the door. But this sound had breath in it. Breath with a shape. She sat very still, the way she had taught herself to sit when gendarmes walked the street outside, and listened.

There it was again. A thin, cracked mewing from the direction of the shop's front step, where the door opened onto Krämerstrasse and the world.

Her body understood before her mind did. Her body had been trained for five years in the grammar of danger, and it read this sound correctly at once: something alive, something abandoned, something that would draw every eye on the street if it continued. She set down the composing stick. She crossed the workshop floor past the press — old Johannes, her father called it, older than both of them together — and through the drying room where sheets of government proclamations hung like laundry, proclamations she had printed and felt nothing about, because feeling nothing was the trade within the trade.

The bolt was cold enough to bite. She drew it slowly, an inch, then all the way, and eased the door open onto the fog.

* * *

The bundle lay against the doorframe as if placed there to be posted, like a parcel or a summons. Sailcloth, good sailcloth, the kind of weave that costs money and lasts twenty years at sea, folded with the neat corners of a hand that knew how to make things secure. It was maybe two feet long. One end of it moved.

For a long moment Theo did not touch it. She stood in the doorway with the fog folding around her shoulders and looked up and down Krämerstrasse — left, toward the corner where the lamplighter was making his slow rounds pinching wicks; right, toward the harbor master's office, dark still, its windows reflecting nothing. The street was empty. The street was never empty. Someone had chosen this hour, this fog, this exact seam between the night watch going off duty and the day trade waking, and they had chosen it well.

Whoever they were.

She crouched. The stone of the step was damp and it soaked through her skirt at the knee and she did not notice. The sailcloth smelled of tar and salt and, beneath that, of milk gone sour. She worked one fold loose with two fingers, careful, the way she handled a forme of set type, as if everything in the world now was something that could be spilled and lost forever.

A face looked up at her.

It was red and furious and no bigger than her palm spread flat, screwed tight around a wailing that had worn itself down to hiccups. Dark hair, a quantity of it, plastered to a skull still soft at the crown. Small fists like knots in thread. It was perhaps three weeks old. Theo knew this without knowing how she knew; the city teaches you things sideways, through neighbors' gossip overheard across courtyards, through the rhythm of births and christenings and deaths that moved along Krämerstrasse like weather fronts.

Tucked into the fold of the cloth at the child's throat was a piece of paper, folded twice.

She took it. Not the child — not yet, not yet — but the paper, because the paper was the thing she understood, the paper was her country and her language, and some part of her mind was already reading before the rest of her had consented to be involved.

Three words, in ink slightly feathered by the fog-damp:

*Forget the face.*

* * *

She did not forget the face. That was the first treachery, and it happened instantly, before she could stop it: the face imprinted itself on her the way ink takes to paper, complete and permanent and beyond recalling. Later she would understand that this was what the note feared, that faces are the one text no censor can pull from circulation — but in that moment, crouched on her own doorstep with the fog thickening, all she felt was the vertigo of having been made an accomplice to something she could not name.

Because she knew the hand.

Not recognized — knowing would come slower, arriving in her stomach before her eyes confirmed it — but she knew it the way a musician knows a voice heard through a wall. The *f* was wrong. Everyone's *f* is wrong in some particular way, and Aldis Vemmen's *f* leaned backward, apologetic, the crossbar dropped low as though the letter flinched as it was written. She had seen that *f* hundreds of times. She had set that *f* into lines of type herself, back when Vemmen brought copy to her father's shop in the years before his own press, back when he was just another hungry printer with bad handwriting and dangerous friends. She had even cut a replacement *f* for him once, from his specimen sheet, laughing about how a man could write a whole underground newspaper and not hold a pen properly.

That had been four years ago. Fourteen months ago, Aldis Vemmen had been arrested with the plates of the pamphlets still warm in his cellar. Nine months ago he had been hanged in the courtyard of the citadel on charges of sedition, and the newspapers — the ones her father's shop was sometimes hired to print, the ones with columns she set and never read past the first line — had called it justice with the smooth efficiency of type that has been used many times before.

And now his handwriting was folded against a baby's throat on her doorstep, instructing her to forget.

Theo looked at the child. The child looked back, or tried to, its eyes still swimming somewhere short of focus, its fury subsiding into a kind of exhausted attention. The fog pressed close. Somewhere out on the water a ship's bell rang twice, muffled, like a voice through a wall.

"No," she said aloud, and was startled by the sound of her own voice, rough from disuse at this hour, rough from years of saying little. "No, you cannot do this to me."

The baby hiccupped.

* * *

She should carry it inside. Every second it lay on the step was a second exposed to the first early walker, the fishwife coming up from the harbor with her baskets, the gendarmerie patrol that passed Krämerstrasse at irregular hours precisely because irregularity kept everyone honest. And yet she stood there a moment longer, frozen not by indecision but by arithmetic. Because taking the child inside was not an act; it was a sentence. It was a change of state from which there was no return, and every survival instinct her father had spent five years installing in her lit up at once like the lamps of a city under threat, screaming the same word: *not yours, not yours, nothing here is yours to keep, you are the invisible daughter of an invisible man and your continued existence depends on owning nothing that can be taken.*

But the sailcloth was already in her hands. She had lifted the bundle before she had finished deciding not to, the weight of it astonishing — seven pounds, eight, no more, and yet heavier than the iron chase of the press, heavier than anything in the shop. The child's warmth came through the canvas like a coal through sacking.

Inside, she bolted the door and stood in the dimness of the front room, among the sample books and the framed specimens and the smell of oil and lead that was, to her, the smell of safety itself, and understood that the smell had just become a lie.

The baby had stopped crying. It slept, suddenly and completely, the way the very young and the very old sleep, without negotiation, its face slackening into something ancient and absurd. Theo laid the bundle on the counter beside the ledger — on the counter, next to the book that recorded every legal transaction of Nystrom & Son, *Son*, the fiction that had kept her fed and hidden since she was fourteen — and she went to fetch the note from where she'd dropped it on the step.

She held it to the window. The fog gave back a gray, sourceless light, enough.

*Forget the face.* Nothing else. No name, no instruction beyond those three words, no plea. Vemmen had been a man who wrote thousands of words a week, who believed — she had heard him say it once, in this very room, standing exactly where the counter met the floor — that ink was the only honest army. And his last message to whoever found this child was three words long. Which meant he had believed that three words were sufficient. Which meant he had known exactly who would find it, or had gambled everything on the hope, and had trusted to the character of a printer to do the rest.

He had addressed it, in other words, not to her. To the shop. To the craft. To whatever remained in the world of people who set type and asked no questions.

Theo sat down slowly on the stool by the counter, and the silence she had mastered for five years — the strategic, load-bearing silence, the silence that kept customers comfortable and gendarmes bored and her father able to sleep at night — settled over her like the sailcloth itself, and for the first time in her life it did not feel like protection.

It felt like complicity. It felt like the moment a man watching a hanging realizes his hands are not clean merely because they held no rope. She had known about Vemmen's pamphlets. Everyone in the trade had known; the pamphlets were discussed in whispers in every ink-smelling back room in the city, their arguments quoted half-accusingly, half-longingly. She had said nothing, done nothing, thought carefully designed no-thoughts, and had survived, and had called the surviving a virtue because her father called it a virtue and because the alternative was unthinkably expensive.

And now the cost of that virtue lay hiccupping softly on her counter, wrapped in sailcloth, wearing the handwriting of a hanged man.

* * *

Upstairs, the floorboards announced her father's waking — the specific sequence of creaks she could have identified blindfolded, boot, boot, the pause at the washstand, the third stair's complaint. Marek Nystrom rose before the light as he had risen for forty years, and in ten minutes he would come down the inside stairs to the workshop, and she had perhaps that long to decide what story she was living in.

She looked from the sleeping child to the note and back. The sensible thing — the survivable thing, the thing her father would say with his jaw set and his eyes anywhere but on hers — was to take it to the church orphanage before it woke fully, leave it on *their* step, let the Sisters write the next line of this text. The sensible thing was to scrub the incident from the morning the way one washed ink from skin, thoroughly, before anyone saw.

The baby opened its eyes.

Not properly — newborns do not see properly — but it turned its face toward her, toward the warmth and movement of her, and made a sound that was not a cry but a question. Its eyes were dark. Vemmen's eyes had been dark. She had never noticed the color of a man's eyes before in her life, and she noticed them now, remembered them now, across fourteen months and a courtyard and a rope.

*Forget the face.*

"I can't," she whispered to the child, to the dead man, to the fog still pressed against the windows like a listener. "Do you understand? I am not anyone. There is no one here to remember you."

The baby considered this, and yawned, and gripped her finger with a strength that astonished her — such a small engine, such ferocious grip, as if the whole will of the child had retreated into one hand and closed around her like a fist around a rope.

Above her, the third stair complained.

Theo stood, and the decision she had not made had somehow already been made; the bundle was in her arms, the note was crushed in her pocket, and her heart was going like the treadle of the press. She heard the inside door open above and her father's tread begin its descent, and she arranged her face into the blank competence she wore like an apron, and she understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than the note did, that in one minute she was going to have to lie to the only person in the world who could read her —

— and that the handwriting in her pocket was going to make that impossible to explain, because Marek Nystrom had known Aldis Vemmen for thirty years, and had drunk with him, and had mourned him in his wordless way, and had never once, not ever, told his daughter what the two of them had really been to each other.

The door at the top of the stairs opened. Her father's footsteps stopped.

From below, in the gray light of the workshop, Theo watched his shadow fall down the stairwell ahead of him, long and thin as a column of set type — and heard him say, quietly, before he had even reached the bottom, in a voice she had never heard him use:

"So it came after all."
