## 3. What Father Cut Away

The note stayed in my apron pocket all through the morning, and I felt it the way one feels a splinter driven deep under a nail — small, invisible to anyone else, and impossible to stop touching with the mind.

Father rose at six as he always did, even now, even with the city holding its breath around us like a man who has heard his name called from across a street and is not certain he wants to turn. He lit the stove, set the kettle on, and began wiping down the composing stone with a rag gone gray from years of the same motion. He did not ask about the bundle I had carried up from the step at dawn. He had heard me come in. He heard everything in this shop; that was the trouble and the architecture of our whole lives together. The floorboards spoke to him, the hinges, the settling of the lead in the type cases. A man who cuts letters for a living learns to read every silence for its shape.

I fed the baby first. That was the thing I could not explain even to myself — how it had stopped being *the baby* somewhere between the second spoon of milk-thinned gruel and the third, and become *her*, with her furious little fists and her eyes the exact gray-brown of wet river stones, the same eyes that had looked out of Aldis Vemmen's face when he stood at our counter three winters ago and argued companionably about kerning while snow came off his shoulders. She ate with the desperate solemnity of the very young, gripping my thumb as if I were rope thrown into water, and I stood by the cold window of the back room and let myself be gripped.

Then I went downstairs to confront my father, and found that the word *confront* dissolved the moment I entered the room, as all my sharp words dissolved in this shop, worn smooth before they ever reached my tongue.

"Someone left a child on our step," I said. Not: I found an infant wrapped in sailcloth. Not: the note says forget the face. I gave him the plainest version and watched him not stop wiping the stone.

"I know," he said.

"You knew."

"I heard you carry her up." He wrung the rag over the basin. Water ran out of it the color of thin ink, which is to say the color of everything in this shop. "And I heard you sit with her until your legs went numb. You've been sitting still since dawn, Theo. You only do that over other people's grief or your own."

There it was again — that terrible attentiveness of his, that way of loving me through observation alone. Five years of it. I had been raised inside it the way type is raised inside a case: each letter in its compartment, sorted, silent, waiting to be arranged into whatever sentence someone else required.

"The note," I said, and took it out, and put it on the stone between us, "is Vemmen's hand."

My father looked at the paper for a long moment. He did not touch it. Outside, a dray rattled past on the cobbles, and somewhere near the fish market a woman was arguing about herring in the flat, carrying voice of someone who has decided the world owes her an apology. Ordinary sounds. The city pretending to be itself.

"I know that too," he said quietly.

* * *

We did not speak of it immediately. That was our way. We banked the conversation like a fire overnight, laid the coals, covered it, and walked around it while it glowed. He made tea — real tea, hoarded, which told me he had been expecting some morning like this one — and we drank it standing up, because Marek Nystrom considered sitting during difficult speech to be a kind of surrender.

"You'll take nothing to the authorities," he said at last. It was not quite a question and not quite a command. It was the middle thing, the thing our family spoke fluently: a statement shaped so the other person could break themselves against it if they chose.

"She's evidence," I said. "You understand that. Whoever left her — whoever wrote that note — she is the last living proof connected to Vemmen, and the men who hanged him would very much like that proof to stop existing. If we keep her, we are keeping a live coal in a paper house."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*" I heard my own voice crack upward, unpracticed, the voice of the girl I had been permitted to stop being. "That's all? Yes?"

"Yes," he said, and set his cup down with the exaggerated care of an angry man, though I had never once in my life seen my father angry in the loud way. His anger was a press. It came down slowly and it left an impression. "The authorities, Theo, are Captain Reval's gendarmes, who shot four strikers on the quay last autumn and called it crowd management. They are a harbor master requisitioning ships for men who will not tell him why. They are a tribunal that tried Aldis Vemmen behind a closed door and hanged him behind another one. You want to walk a child into that building and say *here, this is the granddaughter of a traitor, please treat her according to regulation.*" He picked the cup back up. "They would not even make her disappear quickly. They would make her disappear usefully. There is a difference, and it favors them."

I turned away from him because my face was doing something I did not want him to read. He read everything; it was his gift and my cage.

"You should have woken me," I said to the window. "Last spring. When they arrested him. When they searched the shops. You should have told me what he was to us."

"He was a customer."

"He was a *friend*. He sat where I'm standing. He taught me the three-em space trick for justifying verse, Father, I learned it from *him*, you were in the back room sulking over an invoice—" And then I stopped, because saying his name aloud had opened something, and what came through it was not argument but memory, arriving whole and unbearable: Aldis Vemmen laughing, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to the wrist the way printers are inked, saying *Theo, girl, never trust a font whose italic won't look you in the eye.*

I had been Theo to exactly two people in this world. One of them was dead. The other was watching me now with an expression I had no practice in decoding, because I had spent five years learning to decode everyone except him.

"Sit down," my father said.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down. Please."

I sat. He remained standing, and I understood that this cost him something, that he had rehearsed whatever came next and rehearsed it standing, like a man giving testimony.

* * *

"There are things I cut away," he said, "when your mother died. You know this. You have known it since the night I cut your hair, though neither of us has ever said it in words, because words are your mother's country and I only ever visited." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the stone, an unconscious printer's gesture, testing a surface for flaws. "I cut them away because I thought weight was the danger. I thought: if the cart is lighter, the road will be kinder. It was wrong thinking. But I was a man with one child left and I chose the error I could live inside."

"Father—"

"Let me finish. I have been finishing things badly all my life and I would like to finish one thing correctly." He drew a breath. "Aldis Vemmen was more than a customer. Before your mother married me, before any of it, he and I worked the same press in Riga, under old Kessler, and we cut type together, and there was a period of about four years when I could not have told you which ideas in my head were mine and which were his. Then your mother happened, and the war happened, and other things happened, and we ended up in the same city again by accident, the way rivers end up in the same sea without ever having agreed to it."

The stove ticked. Upstairs, faintly, the baby made a sound — not crying, that particular snuffling negotiation infants conduct with sleep — and both of us tilted our heads toward it at the identical angle, like compasses finding north, and pretended we hadn't.

"When they started hanging printers last year," he went on, "Vemmen came here. Not to print — he knew better than to bring that work to my door, and God forgive me, I would have refused him. He came to hide something. He wouldn't tell me what. He carried it down to the back room himself, at night, and locked the door with his own key, and gave me the key, and said—" my father closed his eyes, retrieving the words with the care of a man lifting letters from a case, each one exactly where he remembered putting it — "he said: *Marek. If they come for me, don't give them anything, and don't go looking until you're ready to be the kind of man who found it.*"

"And you never looked."

"And I never looked." He opened his eyes. "Are you going to ask me why?"

"No," I said, and meant it, and hated that I meant it. "Because the answer is the whole story of this family. Because looking is dangerous and knowing is dangerous and you taught me that before I could write my own name. Which, incidentally—" my voice went low and I could not stop it, the fracture widening whether I willed it or not — "you also chose. The name. Theo. Shortened, blunted, a boy's name with the edges filed off, so that no clerk would ever look twice at the daughter of Marek Nystrom. You didn't protect me, Father. You edited me. And I have spent five years being a proofread copy of a person, and I did not complain, because complaining is loud and we are quiet people, quiet people survive, that is the whole catechism — but there is a child asleep above my head right now whose parents trusted their silence to someone, and I need to know, I need to actually know, whether you are telling me to keep her because it is right, or because it is quiet."

He flinched. My father, who had watched gendarmes run their thumbs along his type cases and never blinked, flinched like a struck man, and for one long moment I thought I had finally broken something in him that five years of widowhood had not.

Then he straightened, and did the last thing I expected. He laughed — softly, terribly, a laugh with no floor under it.

"You sound like your mother," he said. "She asked me the same question on our wedding day. Whether I did things because they were right or because they were safe. I never answered her either." He crossed to the back-room door and stood before it, and I saw then that he had already taken the key from its hiding place, had probably taken it last night, had probably slept with it in his fist like a boy with a coin. "I can't tell you which it is. What I can tell you is this: Aldis Vemmen hid something in that room eight days before they took him. He hid it with a printer's thoroughness, wrapped against damp, and he chose this shop out of every shop in a city full of presses. He chose it because he believed that underneath all my careful cutting-away there was still the man from Kessler's workshop. I have spent a year hoping he was wrong. This morning I find I hope he was right instead."

He unlocked the door.

* * *

The back room smelled of cold paper and stopped time. Nothing in it had moved since spring: the spare rollers in their cradles, the stacks of blank covers, my father's old apron hanging on its nail with the ghost of his shoulders pressed into the leather. Dust lay on everything in an even gray tide, and there, in the tide, a single line of footprints leading to the far shelving, preserved like fossils — Vemmen's boots, from the night he came with his hidden burden.

My father lifted down three flat stones from the bottom shelf — ballast, apparently — and behind them was a cavity in the wall I had never known existed in a building I had lived in for nineteen years, and that fact alone seemed to summarize my entire childhood.

Inside the cavity: a package wrapped in oilcloth and waxed thread, and beside it, a wooden box the length of my forearm.

He handed me the box first. It was heavier than it looked, dense, and when I slid the lid back my breath went out of me in a rush, because inside, nested in wool, lay steel punches — a complete set, cut by hand, each letter a mirror-image jewel — and I knew them before I touched them, the way you know a song from its first bar. Every apprentice in this part of the coast had heard of the typeface that appeared in the suppressed constitution drafts of '05 and was then hunted out of existence, every fonder melted, every specimen burned. We had been told it was destroyed. Here it was, alive, in my hands, the actual cutting edges from which every letter had been struck — the ancestors of the type, older than type, permanent where metal cast from them was born to be melted down.

"These aren't Vemmen's," I whispered.

"No," said my father. "They're mine. I cut them. Thirty-one years ago, when I still believed letters could outrun governments." He looked at his own hands, the knuckles gone broad and scarred with age, and I understood that I was holding the work those hands had done when they were young and unbent. "Vemmen kept them for me. All these years. I couldn't bear to have them in the house after your mother — I sent them to him the week you were born, and I told myself it was for safety, and maybe some of it was." His voice dropped. "The rest of what he hid — the oilcloth — I don't know. I never looked. But he left it with my punches, and he left it with a message for whoever opened this wall, and I think, Theo, I think we are past the point where not-looking is a virtue."

I set the punches down with the reverence one shows a reliquary, and untied the waxed thread.

The oilcloth fell open on paper. Dozens of sheets, closely set in a fine hand — not printed, handwritten, corrected, annotated in two inks — and beneath them a folded cover sheet bearing a title in Vemmen's upright, uncompromising script:

*Concerning the Orders of 12 October: Names, Ranks, and Testimony of Survivors.*

Below the title, smaller, almost apologetic, the sort of line a man adds at midnight:

*A forme is already set. It wants only hands.*

We stood there, my father and I, in the stopped-time smell of cold paper, and I felt the inheritance settle onto me — not the comfortable inheritance of a trade, of clean hands and a quiet shop, but the other kind, the kind that comes with weight and a date attached. Somewhere across the city a harbor bell rang the half hour, and above our heads a child woke and began, tentatively, to cry.

My father looked at me across the open oilcloth.

"The armistice delegations arrived yesterday," he said quietly. "Which means the soldiers will start confiscating presses within the fortnight. Whatever we decide—" he nodded at the testimony, at the wall, at the child upstairs — "we decide soon."

He held out the key to the back room, handle first, and waited for my hand.
