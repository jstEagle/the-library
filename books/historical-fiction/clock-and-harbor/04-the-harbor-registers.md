## 4. The Harbor Registers

The harbor master's office smelled of tar and cold coffee, and Theo stood in it for eleven minutes before anyone acknowledged her existence.

She had counted. Counting was a habit the shop had given her — counting ems and ens, counting the turns of the press screw, counting the seconds between the sound of boots on cobbles and the moment they passed the door. She counted now because counting kept the fear in a small room at the back of her skull where she could lock it when her hands needed to be steady.

The office occupied the ground floor of the customs house, a long room with windows fogged by the breath of too many men waiting in too little warmth. Ship models hung from the rafters on strings, dust-furred, their tiny rigging gone gray. Behind a waist-high partition sat six clerks behind six desks, and above them, mounted on the wall like a saint's icon in some austere northern faith, the great register board: the harbor's whole living inventory chalked in columns that were updated each morning by a clerk with a long pole and a damp cloth.

Three days ago, when she had last allowed herself to think about the harbor — which was to say, when she had permitted herself one deliberate minute of thinking about anything beyond the bundle sleeping in the back room, beyond milk measured out in thimbles, beyond Vemmen's handwriting burning a hole in her memory — the requisition column had held two entries.

She counted seven now.

*Seven ships taken by the Provisional Authority for transport of military materiel,* the chalk said, in a clerk's hand so tidy it looked apologetic. *Sailings suspended until further notice.*

Theo turned her face toward the fogged window and let the city see only a young man in a patched coat, bored, waiting on business.

The pretense was simple and therefore safe. The *Aldruna*, a timber carrier whose owner bought his bills of lading from them, wanted a new stamp cut — her father had sent her with the old one wrapped in oilcloth as proof, and with an estimate written in Marek's careful commercial hand. A real order, a real errand, nothing in it worth a second glance. She had rehearsed the young man's slouch on the walk down. Shoulders forward, chin tucked, hands in pockets like a boy who resented being sent on errands. Boys who resented errands were invisible. Boys who stood too straight made clerks look twice.

"Stamp work," said the clerk at last, a woman of about fifty with spectacles mended at the hinge with wire. She was the only woman in the room and the only one working while two of the men traded cigarettes and a third slept upright with his hat over his eyes. "You'll want the third desk. Henriksson."

"The queue—"

"There is no queue." The wire-mended spectacles assessed her over their rims. "There is only Henriksson, and Henriksson is slow. Give me the piece and I'll tell you what he'd tell you."

Theo slid the oilcloth packet under the partition. The clerk unwrapped it with the flat competence of someone who had handled ten thousand objects belonging to other people, held the old stamp up to the light, grunted.

"Copper's cracked. You'll recut it?"

"We can cast or engrave. Engraving holds longer. My father recommends—"

"I don't care what your father recommends." But there was no venom in it; the words were worn smooth, ritual. "Eight days. Maybe twelve if they close the bridges again." She wrote a ticket, tore it, pushed it across. Then her eyes came up, and stayed, and something moved behind the wire spectacles that was not about stamps at all. "You're Nystrom's. The print shop. Off Rope Lane."

"Yes."

"You did the harbor manifests. Before."

*Before.* The word had a cliff edge in it. Two winters ago, yes — Marek had printed the cargo registers for three shipping houses until the contracts went, without explanation, to a printer in the capital who charged triple. They had never learned why. Theo felt the old question rise and pressed it down. "We still do such work," she said carefully. "If there's volume."

"There won't be volume." The clerk began rewrapping the stamp, unhurried, her back half-turned to the room, her voice pitched exactly beneath what her dozing colleagues could catch. "Not after this week. Not after any week, maybe, for a while." She tied the string with a hard little knot. "They're taking everything that floats, boy. Everything with a hull and a hold. If I were a shop that knew how to make papers look official—" Her eyes flicked up once, sharp as a type knife. "—I'd think very carefully about which side of that trade I wanted to be found on."

"I don't know what you mean, madam."

"No," the clerk agreed. "Nobody ever does. That's why this city still stands." She pushed the packet back under the partition and raised her voice to its ordinary flatness. "Ticket forty-one hundred. Ask for Henriksson. He'll lose it, so keep your own copy."

* * *

Outside, the fog had lifted just enough to show the harbor, and Theo made herself look at it properly, the way her father had taught her to look at a forme of locked type: not reading it, yet. Reading it would come later, in safety. First, the looking.

The inner basin held perhaps thirty vessels at moorings. Of those, seven flew the naval pennant above whatever colors they'd been born with — the requisitioned ones, chalking made flesh. Coal bunkers being filled at the coaling wharf. A troop of soldiers on the quay doing nothing in the particular way soldiers did nothing before they were ordered to do something, which was the most dangerous kind of idleness in any port city Theo had ever heard described. Cranes stood still. Stevedores stood in knots, not working, not leaving, smoking and watching the soldiers watch them.

And at the foot of the customs stairs, where a man could stand and see every gangway and both gates of the dock yard without moving his feet, a gendarme officer was talking to no one.

He wore civilian clothes badly, the way a man wears them when he has been told to wear them and resents the instruction. His coat was good wool but he stood like it was a uniform; his hat was soft-brimmed but he touched its brim at intervals as though confirming it was still a peaked cap. He held a notebook. He did not appear to be writing in it. Every few minutes his gaze traveled the same arc — coal wharf, gate east, gate west, cranes, the knots of stevedores — and returned to rest, always, on the customs house itself. On the office door Theo had just come out of.

She did not change her pace. Changing pace was the first mistake of the frightened; her father had taught her that too, in his oblique way, the winter the strikers' bodies came home. *Walk like your errand is duller than you are.* She crossed the open ground in front of the customs house at the speed of a boy late for nothing, and as she passed within ten paces of him, she gave him the brief, incurious glance one stranger gives another, and saw him give her the same, and understood that his glance weighed more than hers and discarded less.

Then she was past, and the arc of his attention released her, and she let herself breathe again only when she reached the fish market and its blessed crowd and stink.

*He is counting printers,* she thought, among the barrels, among the gulls screaming over herring guts. *Or he is counting everyone, and printers will come later, in alphabetical order of suspicion.* Either way: someone was watching the docks with a notebook and patience, and patience was the quality Theo feared most in men, because she had spent five years cultivating it herself and knew precisely what it was worth.

* * *

She should have gone home then. The errand was done, the estimate delivered, the ticket in her pocket. Home was the whole of her duty: the shop, the type, the small warm weight that woke every three hours and demanded milk Theo rationed out of a supply that shrank like a coastline.

But the harbor master's office kept its registers out in the open, on the wall, and Theo had seen, in her eleven minutes of being ignored, that the older volumes — the closed years, the bound years — lay on a shelf by the stove where anyone could ask to consult them. Anyone with a legitimate reason.

Anyone printing a bill of lading for the *Aldruna* had a legitimate reason to verify vessel names against registry. It was even true; Marek's estimate included a clause about verifying the registered name, because Marek's estimates contained clauses about everything.

So she went back in. The wire-spectacled clerk watched her return with an expression that suggested she had expected nothing else.

"The *Aldruna*," Theo said. "Registered name verification. For the die."

"Shelf by the stove. 918 through last year. Don't take them off the shelf and don't drip on them."

The registers were ledgers the size of tombstones, canvas-bound, spines labeled in fading ink. Theo pulled the current year's volume and the year previous and set them on the stand, and began, ostensibly, at the index under *A*.

*Aldruna.* Built Riga yards, 1897. Timber carrier. Owner: Halversen. Gross tonnage, dimensions, home port — all of it dull as sand, all of it faithfully copied into her memorandum book to make the consultation honest.

But the register was open near the middle sections too, because the clerk used weighted snakes to hold pages, and the page under the left-hand snake was *V*.

*Vemmen.* Not the printer — a different Vemmen, a fisherman dead nine years, his registry struck through in red. But the name on the page, in the ledger ink, in the government's own hand, hit her like cold water. She stood very still and let the page teach her what it actually held: not names alone but movements. Departure dates. Cargo declarations. And, in the margin column that clerks used for irregularities, a scatter of initials and codes she did not recognize — until she recognized the pattern of them, three entries in a row, clustered around dates she remembered because everyone in the city remembered those dates: the week of the strike, the week the pier police fired, the week the first pamphlets appeared with Vemmen's typeset signatures in the corners like confessions.

Ships leaving loaded and returning light. Ships logged as carrying machinery and coaling in four hours. Marginal codes repeating beside the same three vessel names, over and over, through autumn and into winter.

Arms, she thought. Or men. Or money moving in the dark while the city argued loudly about bread. Someone had been using the harbor, and someone in this office had been recording it in code rather than reporting it — which meant either complicity or cowardice or a private archive, kept against a day when the day itself might arrive.

The armistice delegations had been talking for six weeks in a villa outside the capital, and the newspapers printed hopeful paragraphs every third morning, and the price of milk climbed with every paragraph. Everyone spoke of the talks as weather — coming, coming, surely coming. Nobody spoke of what the terms would do to the men who had fired on the pier, or to the men who had ordered it, or to the witnesses.

But the harbor had already voted. Seven ships requisitioned. Sailings suspended. You did not suspend your own merchant fleet if you believed the war was ending and the borders opening. You suspended it if you believed the opposite — that whatever document emerged from the villa would arrive too late, or arrive shaped like a trap, and that the weeks between here and there were weeks in which certain things needed to leave the country and certain other things needed to be prevented from leaving, and certain people needed to be standing where they could be found.

The clock was running down. Not toward peace. Toward a door closing.

Theo copied three vessel names into her memorandum book beneath the *Aldruna*'s particulars, in her smallest hand, disguised amid shipping figures. Then she closed the register, thanked the clerk, who said nothing and looked at her one second too long, and walked out into the gray morning with her heart going like a treadle.

* * *

She took the long way home, along the harbor wall, because the long way let her pass the coaling wharf, and she wanted one more look at the arc-man.

He had moved. He stood now at the wharf's end speaking with the naval petty officer overseeing the bunker loading, and though the wind carried their voices away, it carried their posture clearly enough: the officer deferential, the civilian in bad plainclothes not deferential at all. A man giving instructions to the navy, in borrowed clothes, at a requisitioned wharf, while the armistice talks stalled — that was not surveillance. Surveillance waited. That was preparation.

And when he turned away from the petty officer, he did not resume his arc of watching. He walked — unhurried, notebook still in hand — directly toward the western gate of the dockyard, the gate that opened onto Rope Lane. Onto the street where, between a sailmaker and a shuttered tavern, a brass plaque read *Nystrom & Son, Printers*.

Theo stopped at a chestnut seller's brazier and bought a bag of chestnuts she did not want, because stopping to buy was ordinary, and counted his progress in the polished surface of the copper urn. Gate. Street. Gone — swallowed by the lane's elbow, in the direction of the shop, where her father was even now at the composing stone, and where, behind the locked back room door, in the deepest silence she had ever engineered, an infant was sleeping in a sailcloth bundle that smelled faintly, forever, of another man's press.

Her father could perform deference. Her father could bore any inspector into goodwill; she had seen him do it with tax assessors and fire wardens. But her father did not know about the note in her apron pocket, did not know that the handwriting belonged to a hanged man, did not know that the child upstairs was a loose thread in somebody's weaving — and a man walking toward the shop with a notebook and a navy officer's obedience in his pocket was not collecting almanac orders.

Theo paid for the chestnuts, dropped the memorandum book deep into her coat, and ran.

She ran the way she had never run in five careful years — openly, badly, a girl's run, not caring who saw the shape of it, because the arithmetic had changed. There had been a time when invisibility was the whole of survival, when every silence was a wall laid true. But walls kept things out only while nothing inside was worth seizing, and something inside was worth seizing now: small, breathing, hungry, hers in every way that mattered and none of the ways that counted on paper.

The city's clock was running down. She had seen it in chalk and marginal codes and a borrowed-coat man's stride. Whatever she was going to do — about the child, about Vemmen's hidden hand somewhere in the shop, about the testimony she could feel gathering in the dark like water behind a dam — she was going to have to do it quickly, and quickly was a language she had never been permitted to speak.

Rope Lane opened ahead of her, narrow and dripping. Halfway down it, outside the shop's painted window, a figure in a soft-brimmed hat stood reading the brass plaque, notebook closed, patient as lead.

Theo slowed to a walk. Put her shoulders forward. Tucked her chin. Became, in eight strides, a dull boy on a dull errand, carrying chestnuts, while everything in her screamed.

The man turned at her footsteps. Smiled pleasantly. And stepped aside to let her pass her own front door — watching her all the way in.
