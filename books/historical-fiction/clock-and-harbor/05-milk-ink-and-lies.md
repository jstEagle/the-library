## 5. Milk, Ink, and Lies

The lie arrived before Theo had finished inventing it, the way type sometimes seemed to set itself in her dreams — a full line of justification appearing in her sleep, letters she had never consciously chosen. She woke on the third morning of the child's presence with the story already composed, whole and smooth as a planed board: *the widow Vinter, cousin to my father's first wife, from Liepāja; her husband drowned in the herring season two winters past; she has taken fever herself and been carried off, and the child has come to us because there is no one else.*

It was a good lie. It had the texture of truth — grief in it, geography in it, a woman dead of nothing scandalous in a year when everyone was dying of something. Theo rehearsed it while she pumped the treadle of the grinding stone, rehearsed it while she sorted sorts, the *e*'s clicking into their brass house like small obedient animals. By the time Frau Kessler from the bakery stopped her on the step with the particular tilt of the head that meant *I have heard a child crying at night through your wall*, Theo could deliver it without her hands shaking.

"A cousin's little girl," she said. "The mother died of lungfever in February. Marek Nystrom does not abandon blood, whatever else may be said of him."

Frau Kessler's eyes did the arithmetic Theo had watched every neighbor's eyes do since the first night — *a baby, an unmarried print shop's daughter, no wedding ring, no mourning band* — and then, finding no sum she liked, closed themselves into the flat kindness that was cheaper than questions.

"Bring her by the bakery," Frau Kessler said. "When she's fussy. The oven warmth settles them."

Theo thanked her and went inside and was sick into the sink with the violence of relief, and then washed her face and heated milk she could not afford.

* * *

Because that was the arithmetic that mattered now. Not Frau Kessler's sum but this one: the city's shelves were emptying like basins tipped over a rail. The requisitions had begun in earnest since the harbor registers — grain boats commandeered, cattle driven through the square at dawn under escort, the creamery on Salt Street issuing milk by card and half-pint measures to mothers who could produce papers proving they were mothers. Theo stood three mornings in the queue outside the creamery holding the child and a ration card that belonged to a woman who had never existed, and each morning the line moved slower and the clerk behind the grille looked more like a man counting down to something.

"Name?" he said the second morning, not looking up.

"Vinter. Widow Vinter." The lie came out level. Five years of being someone she wasn't had built the muscle for it.

"Card."

She slid it under the brass grille. He turned it over, held it against the light as if watermarks confessed, stamped it, pushed back a half-pint tin of thin blue-white milk with a skin already forming on it.

"That's for two days," he said. "Rations are cut again Friday. There's a notice going up." He looked past her to the next woman in line, and Theo understood she had been dismissed not rudely but completely, the way the clerk dismissed everyone now, hoarding his attention like the milk itself.

Half a pint, two days. A growing infant wanted four times that. Theo walked home doing sums she couldn't make come right, the child riding against her chest in the sailcloth sling, warm as a loaf, breathing that quick shallow bird-breath that Theo had learned in five days of broken sleep to listen for the way sailors listen for wind change.

At home she stretched the milk with boiled water and a spoon of sugar, and hated herself a little for how grateful the child was, how she sucked the thinned milk down without complaint, as if she'd already learned that in this world you took what was offered and didn't ask what had been removed.

"You'll be angrier about it later," Theo told her, wiping the girl's chin with a rag gone soft from washing. "That's a promise. I'll teach you myself."

* * *

The days found a shape, and the shape was a kind of trap laid over hours instead of floors.

Morning: the queue, the lie, the walk home past the harbor where the cranes stood idle in increasing numbers and men loitered along the quay with the specific stillness of those paid to watch rather than work. Theo catalogued them without turning her head — the two in peacoats by the fish sheds who never bought fish, the officer who came each morning to the customs house and stayed an hour too long. She had spent five years learning which customers to serve with lowered eyes and which silences kept people whole; now the same skill ran outward, mapping the city, marking safe streets and unsafe hours.

Midday: the shop. Marek worked the front room on legitimate orders — funeral broadsheets, mostly, that spring; the city consumed death notices the way it once had consumed almanacs — while Theo kept the child in the back among the paper stock, where the press's morning noise would cover any cry. She printed invoices with one arm and rocked the cradle-frame with one foot. She learned to do everything one-armed. She learned to feed the child with her left hand and set type with her right, and if the lines came out crooked she corrected them, because crooked lines got noticed, and being noticed was the thing the whole architecture of her life had been raised to prevent.

Evening: the lying-in. She told herself she would put the child down and sleep, and instead she lay in the dark watching the small chest rise and fall, counting breaths the way her father counted em quads, and feeling something open in her chest that had no name she dared give it. Attachment, she supposed, if she were being clinical. Trapdoor, if she were being honest. Every hour the child lived in her keeping was another hour in which the silence Theo had mastered — the silence that had kept her safe, kept her invisible, kept her her father's son — became not a shield but a complicity. Because she knew whose child this was. She knew the hand that had written *forget the face*, the steep ascenders, the ink pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, the hand of Aldis Vemmen who had stood on the gallows scaffold last April and said nothing that would implicate anyone else, and the city had called his silence dignity when really it had been the same skill Theo had, weaponized at last: knowing which silences keep people alive, and paying for them with everything.

She was keeping his daughter alive on watered milk and lies, and telling herself it was enough, and knowing it was not.

* * *

On the sixth day Lotta came to the shop.

Not the paperhanger Lotta — Theo hadn't earned that Lotta yet — but word traveled between the two, the way everything traveled in the port quarter except officially. This was Lotta the fishwife's sister, who sold butter off the books from a basket, and who stopped at the print shop door because she had heard there was a baby.

"Heard there was a motherless child here," Lotta corrected herself, setting the basket on the counter with the confidence of a woman who had decided years ago which rooms she belonged in. She was broad, weathered, maybe forty-five, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that measured everything twice. "Kessler says you've taken in your cousin's girl. Sad business. Liepāja, is it?"

"Two winters drowned," Theo said, delivering the dead husband like correct change.

"Mm." Lotta looked at the child, and Theo felt the look go through the sling, through the shawl, through the lie, all the way to the small face beneath — the dark hair, the particular set of the brow — and hold there a moment longer than courtesy required. Then Lotta reached into the basket and brought out a crock of butter and, beneath it, wrapped in newspaper, a stoppered jug. "Goat's milk. My sister keeps goats up the dune road. Richer than what the creamery sells, and no card needed, and no name asked."

"I can't pay for—"

"You can pay Thursday." Lotta named a price that was robbery and knew it, and Theo paid it in advance because the alternative arithmetic — the creamery's cut rations, the thinning milk, the child's hungry night-crying — had only one solution and both of them knew it. Black markets were made of exactly this: a need on one side of a counter, a price on the other, and a mutual agreement that the space between contained nothing worth examining.

Lotta paused at the door, basket on her hip.

"The mother," she said, conversational, to the street rather than to Theo. "The Liepāja cousin. What was her Christian name?"

The question hung there. Theo felt the whole structure of the lie stand up and present itself for inspection — the drowned husband, the lungfever, the two winters of invented grief — and understood in one cold flash that Lotta had not come for the goat's milk money. Lotta had come to see whether the story would bear weight.

"Ilse," Theo said, because her mother's name was Ilse and some part of her refused, even in fiction, to spend a stranger's name on the lie.

"Ilse," Lotta repeated, filing it wherever such women filed things. "Well. God rest her. And mind yourself, Nystrom's girl. Milk's not the only thing getting scarce and watched." And she was gone down the street, unhurried, leaving Theo standing in the doorway with the jug warm against her ribs and the certain knowledge that she had just been weighed, and measured, and not yet priced.

* * *

That night the child would not settle.

She had taken the goat's milk well — too well, perhaps, gulping it with a greediness that suggested she had known all week what she was being denied — and then spent the evening arching and crying in a register that slid under the doors and along the party walls of the whole terrace. Theo walked her. Marek, unprecedentedly, took a turn walking her, holding the small body against his ink-stained chest with a stiffness that was almost tenderness, or almost fear; with Marek the two had always shared a border. By midnight the crying had thinned to whimpers and the whimpers to a hot, unhappy sleep, and Theo sat in the chair by the stove and touched the child's cheek and found it flushed.

Fever, then. Or colic. Or the beginning of something worse. Theo had no vocabulary for infants' illnesses beyond what the neighborhood women dropped in queues like coins — *teething, gripes, the third-night fever* — and every term terrified her equally. The child slept hot and restless against her shoulder, and Theo sat in the dark of the workshop smelling of ink and sour milk and fear, and did the sums again.

If the child worsened, she would need a doctor, and doctors reported. If the child worsened, she would need a midwife, and midwives were licensed now, registered with the city since the unrest, their ledgers subject to inspection — births recorded, names taken, the state's long fingers reaching even into the business of who came into the world and how. To take this child across the city to a registered healer was to write her existence into a ledger that Reval's people could read.

To keep her home and pray was to gamble the only innocent life in the whole affair on Theo's ability to distinguish colic from the killing fevers.

The child stirred and whimpered, and Theo's whole chest contracted around the sound like a fist closing, and she understood — not as thought but as fact, the way she understood the weight of a well-cut letter — that the arithmetic had changed. Five years she had spent making herself unnoticeable, spending her own invisibility like currency, hoarding silence. Now every calculation bent around a small hot sleeping weight that was not hers, that she would burn the shop down for, that she had known for six days and would die for without negotiation. The lie of invisibility had been armor when it protected only her. Now it was a wall she had to build high enough and fast enough to shelter someone who could not even hold up her own head.

Marek appeared in the doorway with two cups of chicory, said nothing, set one within her reach, and withdrew. It was the closest he had come to acknowledgment in six days, and she drank the chicory burning hot and was grateful, and resented being grateful, and watched the stove door's orange seam of light move across the sleeping face.

* * *

Thursday she paid Lotta, and Friday the notice went up at the creamery, and Saturday the queue contained a gendarme.

He stood at the end of the arcade with his collar up against the drizzle, doing nothing visible, which was the most visible thing a man could do. Theo collected her half-pint with the widow Vinter's card and her widow Vinter's voice, and walked home the long way, past the shuttered union hall with its new noticeboard of proclamations, past the corner where the pamphlet-sellers used to gather before the arrests, and she felt the city tightening around her week by week like a screw in a press — the armistice talks stalled somewhere in a distant room, the soldiers massing, the requisitions eating the harbor's ships one hull at a time, and everywhere the watchers thickening like sediment in old ink.

The child rode against her chest, cooler today, the fever broken or never real. Theo bent her head over the small warmth as she walked, shielding her from the drizzle, and spoke into the wool of the sling where no one could hear.

"Ilse Vinter," she murmured. "That's your grandmother's name wearing a costume. We'll get you your own when it's safe. I'm told names can be crimes here, so we'll choose yours carefully, and we'll make it one nobody can hang a man for."

The child slept. Somewhere behind them a boot scuffed, and Theo did not turn, did not quicken her step, did nothing that a guilty woman would do. She walked home through the gray morning at the pace of the innocent, and let herself in, and bolted the door, and only then allowed her hands to shake.

Marek was at the composing stone, and he looked up, and he did not ask where she had been, and that was the trouble. For six days he had circled the child the way a man circles a fire — warming himself on her existence, refusing to touch the question of her future. And this morning there was something new in his face, something gathered and grim, and when he spoke it was not about milk or rations or the gendarme in the arcade.

"There's a room in the back of the shop," Marek said quietly, "that I locked the night Vemmen was arrested, and haven't opened since. It's time you saw what he left in it."
