## 6. Vemmen's Last Composition

The key had been hanging on the same nail for five years, and in all that time I had never once let my fingers close around it.

It hung in the shadowed corner of the composing room, behind the rack of brass rules, a small iron thing gone brown with age and the salt air that crept up from the harbor into everything we owned. Father called it the spare key to the cellar, though we had never needed a spare for anything in our lives. A lie so old it had worn smooth, like a stone in a riverbed — you stopped seeing it because it had always been there.

But now there was a child asleep in the drawer of my old type cabinet, breathing with the wet, determined rhythm of the very young, and there was a note in Aldis Vemmen's hand folded into my apron pocket, and the word *forget* sat in me like a splinter too deep to draw out with tweezers.

*Forget the face.*

I had spent five years learning to forget faces. My own first among them. And here was a dead man asking me to do it one more time, as if forgetting were a kindness, as if it were not the very thing that had hanged him.

*They will ask what you know. You know nothing.*

Father's voice from three nights ago, when I had laid the sailcloth bundle between us on the workbench and watched his face go through winter. He had refused the authorities, refused the midwife, refused even to say Vemmen's name aloud — but he had said one thing more, in the smallest voice I had ever heard him use, the voice he kept for truths too heavy to carry at full volume:

*He hid something here. Before they took him.*

I turned the key over in my palm. It was warm from the stove's reach, or perhaps from mine.

* * *

The back room had no name in our household. We called it *the back room*, the way sailors call the sea *the water* — a name so plain it functions as a warning. It sat behind the composing room, past the drying racks, its door painted the same gray as the wall so that strangers' eyes slid off it without catching. As children — as the son my father pretended he had — I had been told once, flatly, never to enter it. I had obeyed for five years the way one obeys the shape of a scar: not out of fear exactly, but out of respect for whatever wound lay beneath.

Now I stood before the door with the lamp held high and the key in my fist, and I made myself notice everything, because noticing is what printers do instead of praying.

The hinges did not scream. Someone had oiled them recently — within the year, I judged, by the clean brightness of the metal where the pin emerged. Father had been in here. Father had been keeping something alive in here the way one keeps a fire banked under ash.

The room breathed out at me: cold paper, tallow, dust, and beneath it all the faint mineral sweetness of lead type — a smell I have loved longer than I have loved any person, and which that night smelled like a grave opened onto spring.

Shelves along the left wall, mostly empty. A paper press, small, its screw wrapped in cloth against rust. And against the far wall, under a sheet of sacking that had been tucked with care rather than thrown —

A forme.

Not loose type. Not galleys waiting to be distributed. A full forme, locked in its chase, wedges tightened, furniture set, ready for the press. Someone had composed this and then — stopped. Locked it away whole, like a letter sealed and never sent.

I set the lamp on the floor and knelt, and pulled the sacking away, and the dust rose up golden in the lamplight like something exhaling after years of holding its breath.

* * *

You must understand what it means, what it means to a compositor, to read someone else's setting. Every hand has its habits. The way spaces are justified, the width of the leads, whether the compositor breaks lines at sense or at measure. Reading another printer's forme is like hearing a voice across a crowded market — you know them before you know what they are saying.

I knew the hand before I read a word. The tight, economical spacing. The em-quads used sparingly, almost stingily, as if paper itself were a moral question. The long s cut slightly narrow in the fount — I recognized those letters the way you recognize a neighbor's cough through a wall.

Aldis Vemmen had set this himself. Had stood where I was kneeling, in this same cold room, and locked these letters into their frame knowing he might never pull a single proof.

I began to read.

*To whom it may concern, being all persons of conscience in this city and beyond it—*

My lips moved. I am not ashamed of that. Some texts demand to be spoken; silence would have been a second murder.

*We, the undersigned compositors and pressmen of this city, do testify to what we saw and what we were ordered to print and what we refused...*

The lamplight flickered in the draft from under the door. Somewhere above me, through the floorboards, the baby stirred and settled. I read on.

It was the strike of last autumn, the one the newspapers had covered in two columns of official language — *disturbances*, *regrettable excesses*, *order restored*. I had set some of that language myself. I remembered the particular shame of it, the way the words had felt wrong in my fingers even as I locked them into place, like handling coins I knew to be counterfeit.

Vemmen's forme told what the official columns had buried. The order had come down from the garrison command — and here were names, set in capital letters with the deliberate spacing of a man who wanted each one legible to a half-literate reader, wanted them hammered into the eye like nails:

*Colonel Brandt, commanding. Captain Reval, provost authority. Major Ehlvest, who signed the order for the harbor square.*

Three names. Three men whose signatures I had seen in reverse, mirrored in lead, when the government printing orders came through shops like ours for the commemorative broadsides — *the city thanks its protectors.* We had printed praise for the men who gave the order. Father had taken the job without comment, because a shop takes jobs or it dies, and I had set the letters, and neither of us had said one word about it, and that wordlessness had been the truest sentence of my life until this one.

*On the morning of the seventeenth, the strikers in the harbor square numbered fewer than four hundred, of whom perhaps sixty were boys under fifteen. They carried no arms beyond staves. Captain Reval ordered the square cleared by any means. When the soldiers hesitated — and they did hesitate, I saw them hesitate, I stood at the window of the chandlery and saw a sergeant lower his rifle twice — Reval gave the order again, and then the firing began, and it did not cease while any remained standing.*

*I, Aldis Vemmen, printer, was arrested eleven days later for pamphlets I did write and do not repent. But understand: the pamphlets were the lesser crime in their eyes. The greater crime was that I kept the correspondence. I keep it still, and it will be found by hands worthier than mine.*

There it was. The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts when a ship takes the swell, and I put my palm flat against the cold stone to steady myself.

The correspondence. Somewhere in this room — or hidden elsewhere, in some other place a dead man had trusted — lay the papers themselves. The orders. The signatures. The ink that matched the blood.

And below the testimony, in smaller type, the part that made my breath go shallow and strange:

*Let it be recorded that I die a printer and nothing else. Let the child be told, when she is old enough, that her father's trade was truth, and that truth is a type that cannot be distributed — only cast anew in every generation, by whoever has the courage to melt down their fear.*

*Her* father. *She.*

I looked up from the forme toward the door, toward the dim passage, toward the drawer where a baby slept who had Vemmen's jaw — I saw it now, I could not stop seeing it, that stubborn square set of the chin already forming in milk-soft flesh.

He had known. Sitting in his cell last spring, writing his note in that careful hand, he had known exactly whose doorstep to choose. Not because we were friends — I barely remembered his face from the guild meetings, a big quiet man who smelled of pipe smoke and stood with his weight on both feet like an anvil. He had chosen us because we were silencers. Because Marek Nystrom's shop was the quietest place in a loud city, and the safest hiding place in the world is inside a person who has practiced not existing.

He had handed me his daughter the way a dying man hands off a flame cupped in bare palms.

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there. Long enough for the lamp to burn low and the oil to gutter. Long enough for the cold of the stone floor to climb through my skirts into my knees and make a home there.

I read the forme twice more. Then a third time, aloud, in a whisper, because I found I needed to hear the names in my own voice — needed to prove to myself that saying them did not summon anything, that the walls did not fall, that the world's terrible machinery did not grind to a halt merely because a woman in a locked room spoke three names into the dark.

*Brandt. Reval. Ehlvest.*

Nothing happened. That was the horror of it and the hope of it, both at once. Nothing happened, which meant it was not yet too late — and nothing would ever happen unless someone made it happen.

I thought of the harbor registers, the ships being chalked off the lists one by one, requisitioned. I thought of the gendarmes walking the quay in twos now, their greatcoats buttoned to the throat against a cold that hadn't fully arrived yet. I thought of the armistice talks everyone spoke of in the lowered voice reserved for weather and funerals.

And I thought: *paper is still paper. Ink is still ink. The press downstairs is still the press.*

Justice existed in lead. It was sitting right in front of me, locked in its steel frame, patient as only metal can be patient. It had waited a year for hands brave enough. It did not need me to be brave. It only needed me to be a compositor — and that, God forgive me, that I was, better than any apprentice in this city, better than anyone knew, because no one had ever been allowed to know.

My hands were shaking. I noticed this with a kind of distant professional interest, the way you'd note a loose quoine in a locked forme. Shaking hands can still set type. Shaking hands can still pull a lever. There is work the body remembers how to do even when the mind has gone white as unprinted stock.

I heard footsteps overhead. Father, moving through the kitchen, pausing — I knew the exact pause, the weight of him stopping near the head of the back stairs, listening. Listening for me. Listening for the key.

For five years I had answered that listening with silence. Silence was the inheritance: *which customers to serve, which questions never to answer, which doors stay shut.* He had built me out of silences the way a typefounder builds a letter out of a matrix — struck me, molded me, trimmed away everything that stuck out.

But a matrix exists to be filled. And something was pouring into me now, hot and bright and dangerous as molten lead, and I understood suddenly that I had been a mold all along, waiting, and that my father had known it too — why else keep Vemmen's forme alive in the dark like a heart kept beating?

I rose. My knees cracked. In the drawer upstairs, the baby slept on, dreaming whatever infants dream — milk, warmth, the drum of a heartbeat, the face she was supposed to forget.

I would not help her forget it. That was the decision, made there on my knees in the dust and the lamplight, before I had planned a single page or counted a single sheet. Whatever it cost. Whatever burned.

I took the lamp and left the forme locked — it was safer locked, safer in its steel, the way truth is safer in type than in a loose tongue. I closed the door. The hinges made no sound; Vemmen's oil, Vemmen's foresight, still serving.

Then I climbed the stairs, and I did not hang the key back on its nail.

I put it in my pocket, next to the note, next to the folded sailcloth corner I had cut away and kept without admitting why. Two small weights against my hip as I walked through the dark shop, past the press that had been my whole horizon for five years, past the cases of type standing in their ranks like soldiers who had not yet received orders.

They had orders now.

At the top of the back stairs, my father stood in the doorway of the kitchen, a candle in his hand, his face half light and half dark, watching me come up out of the ground floor like something surfacing from deep water. His eyes went to my pocket. To the shape of the key under the fabric.

"Theo," he said. Just my name — the false one, the safe one, the son he'd invented to save me.

"Yes," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "We need to talk about what he left."

Behind him, in the kitchen, the candle flame leaned as a draft came down from the harbor, carrying the smell of coal smoke and cold water and the enormous, indifferent turning of the city's night.
