## 7. The Inspector Calls

He came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to me then and everything to me now. Tuesdays were ledger days; my father sat in the back with his spectacles low on his nose, ruling columns in pale gray ink, and I worked the front of the shop sorting the week's orders — funeral cards for the Halvorsen family, three hundred handbills for a chandler's auction, a run of customs forms whose official watermark we were not quite licensed to reproduce but no one had ever thought to ask about.

The bell above the door gave its small brass cough, and Captain Reval stepped through it as if he had been invited.

I knew him before he spoke, not by face but by the way the room changed. Men who carry authority wear it differently. Some wear it like a coat they can take off at night, and those men you can manage — offer them a chair, a drink, a compliment, and the coat comes off and underneath is only a tired body wanting to go home. But Reval wore authority the way a scar wears skin. It was not on him. It was him. The gendarme at his shoulder stayed by the door, hat under his arm, young enough that his uniform still smelled of the tailor's shop. Reval's uniform smelled of nothing at all, which frightened me more than any smell could have.

"Master Nystrom." His voice was pleasant. That was the first wrong thing. Gendarmes who come to inspect are supposed to be bored or bullying, and pleasantness from a gendarmerie captain is a hook dressed as bait. "I hope this is not an inconvenience."

My father emerged from the back wiping his hands on his apron, and I watched him become someone else in the space of four steps. It was like watching a man put on a glove finger by finger. His shoulders rounded into apology, his mouth arranged itself around humility, and when he bowed over his own handshake it was the bow of a tradesman to a superior, practiced and bloodless.

"No inconvenience, Captain. An honor. Will you take tea? My daughter will make it."

*Daughter.* The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well, and I heard Reval hear it — heard the small pause while he reconciled it against whatever list he carried in his head, the shop registered to Marek Nystrom, *and son*. I kept my eyes down and my face smooth and understood that I had just been weighed.

"Tea would be welcome," Reval said, and did not look at me again, which was its own kind of message. You do not study prey before the snare is set.

While I boiled water in the back, I listened. That is the trade I never chose but learned anyway: listening through walls, through doors, through the particular quality of silence that means someone is being careful. My father's voice ran along in its merchant register — yes, Captain, the press is a Heidelberg, twenty years old, yes, she still holds a good impression, no, business is slow, everything is slow, the war, the times, God keep us all. And Reval's voice came back in pieces, each one placed like a typestick into a composing stick: *routine inspection... registration of equipment... new regulations since the strikes...*

The kettle shrieked and I shut it off with my thumb on the lever, hard, so it wouldn't cry twice.

When I carried the tray out, Reval was standing at the press.

He stood the way my father stood at it, which unsettled me in a way I could not immediately name. Most men look at a printing press and see iron — a big dumb animal of wheel and screw and platen, good for making posters and price lists and nothing that matters. But there are men who look at a press and see a mouth. Reval walked his fingers along the frame the way a man walks them along a banister in the dark, and he tipped his head to read the upside-down type locked in the chase — the customs forms, harmless, watermarked, dull — and something in his face moved. Not a smile. The place where a smile would be, warming itself in anticipation of arriving.

"Beautiful machine," he said. "They don't appreciate what these can do. Or what they *have* done."

"The customs house has been a loyal customer," my father said smoothly.

"I'm sure." Reval took the tea. He held it without drinking. Steam rose past his chin and he watched it as if reading something in it. "You knew Aldis Vemmen."

It was not a question, and my father did not answer it as one. "Everyone in the trade knew Vemmen," he said. "A fine compositor. Better than he needed to be, for the work he took."

"And yet he took that work anyway." Reval turned the cup slowly in his ringed fingers. "Curious, isn't it, Master Nystrom. A man of his gifts, wasting himself on pamphlets. Sedition is a poor trade. Short career." He smiled then, finally, and I wished with my whole body that he hadn't. It was a courteous smile, a patient smile, the smile of a man who has all the time in the world because the world is doing his paperwork for him. "Still. Some men can't help composing. It's in the hands. You'd know that better than anyone — I've seen your cutting bench. Those punches are museum work."

"They're tools," my father said. "Nothing more."

"Everything is more than it is," said Reval, pleasantly, and drank his tea at last.

I poured. I did not spill. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger of small survivals: the pot shook inside me like a struck bell, and my hand came out level, and the tea went into the cup in a thin unbroken thread, and Reval never looked up to see whether the daughter trembled. Perhaps he already knew. Perhaps knowing was enough for him that morning.

* * *

He stayed forty minutes. I counted them against the case clock in the corner, whose pendulum I have trusted longer than I have trusted any living person. Forty minutes, and he spent thirty-five of them near the press or the type cabinets, opening drawers I had organized myself, running his thumb across the reglets, lifting the lid of the stone where yesterday's forme still lay locked. He asked questions that sounded like courtesy and were inventory. How many cases. What faces. Whether we cast our own type or bought it in. Whether we had ever done work for other shops when their presses were overloaded.

"All the time," my father said. "A print shop survives winter on other people's overflow."

"And in spring?" said Reval. "What does a shop take in spring, when the overflow dries up?"

"Whatever pays," said my father.

"Mm," said Reval, in the tone of a man filing something. "Yes. Whatever pays."

The young gendarme by the door wrote nothing the entire time. He was there to be seen, not to record; the record was being kept somewhere behind Reval's pleasant face, in that interior office where men like him keep their real ledgers. Twice Reval paused at the back wall — our back wall, the one with the locked door to the storeroom, the ordinary locked door behind which an ordinary dead stock of paper and pasteboard waited, and behind *that*, though Reval could not know it, could not possibly know it, the forme set in Vemmen's own hand lay wrapped in sacking under a false floor my father built the week after the hanging.

Twice Reval paused at that wall, and twice my father crossed the room ahead of him, talking — the roof leaks above that corner, Captain, forgive the mess, the damp ruins the stock — steering him away with words the way a man steers cattle with a stick, gently, patiently, and Reval let himself be steered, smiling, letting my father believe in the success of the maneuver, and I understood that he was allowing it. A cat does not fail to catch the mouse in the first pass. The cat is learning the mouse.

At the door, he stopped.

"You've been in this shop a long time," he said to me directly, for the first time since the tea. Up close his eyes were the color of dishwater and just as candid. "Your father tells me you do the composition yourself. Unusual, for a girl."

"My father says a spare pair of trained hands is never wasted."

"A sensible man." He put on his hat, and the brim shadowed his face so that only the smile remained visible. "If anything ever comes through here that shouldn't — papers, parcels, things left behind by careless men — you'll send for me. I'm easy to find. I make a point of being easy to find."

"We are printers, Captain," my father said behind him. "We print what is brought to us and forget it by supper. It's how we stay healthy."

"Of course you do," said Reval warmly. "So does everyone."

The bell coughed behind him. The young gendarme's boots rang on the cobbles, and Reval's made no sound at all, or none I could hear over my own pulse, and then the street had them both and the shop was ours again and the clock was ticking and the tea sat cold in two cups, one untouched entirely, which told me something too: a man who drinks nothing in your house trusts nothing in your house, and wants you to know it.

* * *

My father stood at the window a long time, watching the street in the slanted way he watches things, not looking at what he looks at. When Reval's carriage — he had a carriage; inspectors ride, apparently, even three streets — rolled out of sight, he let the curtain fall and stood with his hand still on the cloth as if the fabric might tell him something his eyes had missed.

"He'll come back," I said.

"Yes."

"Not for the regulations."

"No." My father turned. In the gray light his face looked older than it had at breakfast, and I realized with a small cold shock that I had never truly seen him afraid before. Worried, yes — worried over money, over my mother, over me, over the slow strangulation of the trade — but fear was a different country and I was watching him arrive there now, passportless. "He didn't come for the press. He came to smell the shop. To learn its shape, so that when he's ready, nothing surprises him."

"He asked about Vemmen."

"He asked about everything. That's worse." My father took off his spectacles and pressed the bridge of his nose. "You know what I noticed, Theo? The girl notices what the inspector notices, that's her training, that's her inheritance, God help her." He said it bitterly, as if handing me something heavy. "He never once mentioned the pamphlets themselves. Never quoted them, never sneered at them. A man hunting sedition hates sedition. This one didn't hate anything. He was—" my father searched for the word, turning it over like a piece of type he couldn't identify by touch, "—he was *collecting*. He wanted to know who printed for whom. Who owes us, whom we owe. He wasn't looking at the crime. He was mapping the trade. Every press in this city is a node now, and every printer a name on a list, and lists, Theo, are the true currency of the new times. Not lead. Lists."

I thought of the forme under the false floor. Names, in reverse, punched into lead — the officers who ordered the executions at the shipyard gates, Vemmen among the condemned, every letter of the testimony standing in relief like a wound in mirror image. A list of another kind. One that could be multiplied by ink and pressure into ten copies or a thousand, and carried anywhere, and read aloud years from now in rooms where Reval's name meant nothing anymore.

Two lists in one city. Two futures competing for the same paper.

"What did he want with the case files?" I asked. "He mentioned them—"

"He did." My father's mouth thinned. "Asked if I'd seen the evidence inventory. Asked whether confiscated materials passed through private presses for copying. Official copies, he said. For the tribunal record." He laughed once, without any humor in it at all, a sound like a typecase dropped edge-on. "There is no tribunal record, Theo. There never was going to be. Vemmen swung in nine days. Nine days — no printer's trial takes nine days unless the verdict was written before the arrest. So why does a captain need copies of evidence that convicted a hanged man?"

I felt it then, the thing I have never managed to unfeel since: the true shape of Captain Reval, assembled out of forty minutes of teacups and drawer-lids and pleasant questions. Duty inspects and departs. Hunger stays. Hunger walks its fingers along the press frame. Hunger asks what pays in spring. He had not come to find Vemmen's hidden materials because he feared them.

He had come because he wanted them. Because somewhere in the wreckage of the suppressed constitution drafts and the burned pamphlet runs and the nine-day trial, there was a gap — a page missing, a name unrecorded, something that could embarrass men above him or enrich him or arm him — and he intended to find it before anyone else did, and he had begun his search in the shop of the only master cutter in the city who had been friendly enough with Aldis Vemmen to drink with him, argue typeface with him, and never once ask what he printed.

"He's not cleaning up after the execution," I said slowly. "He's finishing it. Whatever Vemmen hid, Reval needs it gone — or needs it his."

My father looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something, watch the decision move across his face like weather. Then he walked to the back wall, took the ring of keys from his belt, and stood before the locked door without unlocking it.

"Then we have less time than I hoped," he said. "And there's more behind this door than paper."

The key was already in the lock when the bell above the shop door coughed again — and we both turned, cold to the marrow, toward the front of the empty shop.
