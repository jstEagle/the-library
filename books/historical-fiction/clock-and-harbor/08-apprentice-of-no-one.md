## 8. Apprentice of No One

The scissors were the first thing Marek reached for, before the lamp, before the water, before he could lose his nerve in the dark of the kitchen.

They were his own shears — the heavy ones he used for trimming paper edges, cold in his palm the way iron is always cold, as though it had been waiting somewhere else and only agreed to be here. He set them on the table between the bread knife and the oil lamp and stood looking at them for a long moment, as if they might reconsider what he intended.

Behind him, in the room that had been his wife's and was now no one's, his child slept.

Five years old and already she slept the way her mother had — face turned to the wall, one hand curled under her cheek as if holding something small and secret there. He had watched her sleep a hundred nights since the burial and could not stop watching, because every gesture was a resurrection he had not asked for and could not put down. The tilt of the head. The way she said *why* with the second syllable rising, an upward inflection like a gull lifting off water. Her mother's mouth. Her mother's hands, already quick, already reaching for anything with edges.

And the hair. God help him, the hair — black where his was river-mud brown, thick where his thinned, the kind of hair that announced itself across a street, through a window, past a soldier's lowered gaze. Hair like her mother's, and everyone in this city knew Ilse Nystrom's hair, knew her voice at the harbor gates, knew her name on the petition lists that had gotten eleven strikers shot and their organizers hunted house to house.

Marek picked up the scissors again. Put them down. Picked them up.

Outside, the frost had come early, and the windowpanes wore it like ground glass. Somewhere beyond the rooftops a patrol passed, boots out of rhythm — two men who did not care about their step, which meant they felt safe, which meant they felt unwatched, which meant they were more dangerous than any who marched well. Marek had learned to read the city by its sounds the way sailors read weather. It was one of the few lessons his father had given him that proved useful: *a pressman hears everything, boy, because he must be heard by nothing.*

He went into the bedroom and lifted the sleeping child, and she stirred against his shoulder and murmured a name — not his, not her own, but a syllable or two of her mother's name, dissolved in sleep — and his chest closed around the sound like a fist around a coal.

*Forgive me,* he thought, though he did not say it, would never say it, because saying things aloud made them real and real things could be used against you. *Forgive me for what I am about to take from you.*

He set her on the kitchen chair, wrapped in her blanket, and she woke slowly, blinking at the lamplight, at him, at the scissors in his hand.

"Papa?"

"Hold still."

* * *

She trusted him. That was the unbearable part, remembered five years later across the smoke and ink of everything that followed: she simply trusted him. She sat still as a saint while he wet her hair with water warmed on the stove, and when the first cut came — a long lock coming away whole, heavy as rope in his fist — she only laughed, a child's laugh, delighted by the strangeness of it.

"It tickles!"

"Hold still," he said again, because his voice would betray him otherwise. There was a stone in his throat the size of her whole name.

He cut badly on purpose in some places and carefully everywhere, working around the small skull with the concentration he usually gave to kerning, to the millimeter spaces between letters where meaning lived or died. Outside the trade no one understood that — that type setting was mostly silence, mostly subtraction, deciding how much air a word needed to breathe. He was doing the same now. Subtracting. Making space around her so that she might survive.

Ilse had worn her hair braided and pinned up, then loose in the house, black falling past her shoulders, and when she argued — and she argued the way rivers run, constantly and without apology — she pushed it back from her face with both hands, and that gesture had been quoted back at her in the gendarme reports afterward, *the woman gestured defiantly*, as if hair could be testimony. They had photographed her at the gate of the prison yard. Marek had seen the photograph once, held briefly by a sympathetic clerk who owed him for wedding invitations, and in it Ilse's hair had come loose from its pins and blown across her face, and even in that grainy silver ghost of her she looked like she was about to push it back and say something that would get her killed.

It had already gotten her killed. That was the arithmetic he could not stop performing. Not the petition alone — hundreds signed, thousands — but the petitions in her hand, the pamphlets in her satchel, printed on his press in hours he had pretended not to know about, and the name *Nystrom* spoken twice in the trial transcript he had paid another clerk to let him copy. *The woman Nystrom.* As if marriage had been the crime. As if he had been complicit by grammar alone.

He had not been complicit. That was the wound inside the wound. He had told her, the last argument, standing in this very kitchen: *Not under my roof, Ilse. Not with the child here. Take your politics to the union hall, print your papers where they don't sleep.* And she had looked at him with such tired love, such bottomless disappointment, and said, *Marek, there is no place they don't sleep. That's rather the point.*

Eleven days later the shots at the shipyard. Fourteen days after that, the knock at the door.

So: the scissors.

"I want long hair," the child said, with her mother's rising *why* folded into it. "Like Mama."

The lock came away in his hand. He laid it on the table, and it lay there gleaming like something drowned.

"Long hair gets caught in machines," he said. "You'll work in the shop. Long hair near a press is a dead girl's hair." True, and true, and not the truth. He was already learning the craft of it — the double speech, the sentence with two floors. He had watched Ilse do it for years without understanding, and grief was teaching him faster than love ever had.

"When do I start? In the shop?"

"Soon."

"I want to set letters. I want to make words."

"You will." He turned her head gently with two fingers at her jaw. Snip. Snip. The sound was very loud in the frosted quiet. "But first you learn to be quiet in a shop. A shop is a place where people keep secrets for other people. Confessions. Love letters. Warrants." He hadn't meant to say warrants. The word escaped him like steam from a valve. "You keep what you hear behind your teeth, and your teeth stay healthy all your life."

"Why?"

"Because teeth are the only part of us that can't lie and can't forgive."

She considered this with a gravity that undid him. Five years old and she weighed everything he said as if it might be scripture. He wanted to tell her it was a joke. He could not remember how jokes were built anymore; the mechanism was rusted shut.

* * *

The letters took longer than the haircut, and he hated himself more thoroughly for each minute of it.

They kept them in a cedar box on the wardrobe's top shelf — he had never once opened it while she lived, because a man does not read his wife's mail like a thief, and now he read them like a thief, by lamplight, fast, looking for names. Looking for his own name. Looking for the names of anyone still breathing whose existence in that cedar box could become a noose.

There were forty-one letters. He counted them the way he counted type when sorting a case, lips moving, and the counting was a kind of prayer, the only liturgy left to him. Some were from her sister in the south, harmless, domestic, full of recipes and complaints about a brother-in-law's drinking. Those he set aside to keep — a mercy he granted himself so that the burning would not be total, so that somewhere in the ash of this night one living thread would remain. But most were from the movement. From organizers whose names appeared in court transcripts. From women in the co-op. From a correspondent who signed only with a drawn bird and wrote in a slanted, urgent hand about printing, always printing: *paper secured, ink secured, the shop on Kessel Street has a press that works nights.* One letter mentioned Vemmen — Aldis Vemmen, the young compositor with the beautiful italic hand — mentioned him in passing, warmly, *he sets a page like a man composing music.*

Marek sat with that letter a long time. The lamp guttered. Frost climbed the window in feathered continents.

Then he fed the stove, and when the fire had built itself up honest and hot, he began feeding the letters to it one at a time, and the child — he had forgotten, God help him, he had entirely forgotten — the child stood in the doorway in her nightdress with her new short hair sticking up unevenly at the crown where his hands had shaken, and watched.

"What are you doing, Papa?"

He should have sent her back to bed. He should have lied. Instead he heard himself answer with the truth shaped into something she could carry: "Cleaning. Sometimes a house needs cleaning in ways brooms don't reach."

"Whose letters?"

"Your mother's."

Silence. The fire ate a line of handwriting he had loved once, a joke about his own stubbornness, ink going orange then black then nothing. When he finally turned, she was not crying. She was watching the stove with her mother's eyes, and he understood in one cold drop that she would remember this night for the rest of her life, that he was building her memory right now the way he built pages, line by deliberate line, and there was no proofing this, no pulling a clean impression, no second pull.

"Come here," he said.

She came. He knelt on the cold flags so that they were level, and he gathered her in, and she smelled of soap and sleep and woodsmoke beginning, and he held her hard enough that she wriggled, and he did not loosen his grip until he had mastered his own face, because a child reads faces before letters and he could not have her read this one.

"Mama is dead," she said. Statement, not question. Children of five are often done with grief sooner than their parents, having smaller vessels for it.

"Yes."

"Are you burning her so the soldiers can't have her?"

The accuracy of it stopped his heart. He pulled back to look at her and found her looking back, steady, waiting, and there was nothing of the child in the patience of that look — or rather there was everything of the child, because children see plainly what adults spend their lives learning to obscure.

"Yes," he said. "That is exactly what I am doing."

"Will it work?"

"No," he said, honestly, because she deserved one honesty tonight and it was the only one he had left. "But we will pretend it does. Pretending is also work. You'll learn that too."

* * *

He began her apprenticeship the next morning, and it was the cruelest kindness he ever performed, and he performed it every morning for years.

Not the trade first — never the trade first. First the invisibility. He walked her to the shop through the fish market with his hand on her shoulder, steering, and taught her the walk: *eyes down but not bowed, a bowed head asks questions; move like you're expected somewhere dull.* He renamed her in his own mouth, practicing on the walk, feeling each version break against his teeth until he settled on the one least false: his son. Theo. A name with no history, a name like a plain envelope. He told the neighbors his sister's girl had gone south to an aunt and a boy had been sent to learn the trade, and the neighbors — this was the mercy buried in the city's cruelty — the neighbors chose to believe him, because believing cost nothing and disbelieving invited questions no one wanted aimed their way. Frau Osten brought bread for the boy and looked at the boy's fine wrists and said nothing, and her saying nothing was the loudest kindness Marek ever received.

In the shop he taught her the way his father had taught him, which was to say without tenderness, with correction, with the assumption that competence was the only inheritance worth the carrying. She learned the California job case in nine days. She learned to distribute type back into its compartments by touch in the dark, which he had never managed, her fingers reading the little brass walls like a blind woman's. She learned which customers paid cash and which paid in favors and which were gendarme informants — old Pell at the chandlery, who always ordered invoices in duplicate and always let his eyes travel over whatever lay on the stone. She learned to serve Pell quickly and blandly, giving him nothing to read.

By the time she was eight she could cut a matrix better than any apprentice in the harbor quarter, and word of it traveled, and travelers to the shop would ask to see the boy who cut steel like butter, and Marek would watch his son stand in the lamplight, hands ink-black, face carefully empty, being praised for a skill that could feed her for life and kill her tomorrow if anyone ever looked too closely at the fine bones under the boy's clothes — and he saw her learn, year over year, to hold still under the praise the way she'd held still under the scissors. Competence without a name. Excellence addressed to no one.

He told himself it was survival. Most nights he believed it.

Some nights — the bad ones, when the press stood silent and the stove ticked and somewhere across the city a woman's voice was raised in an argument he couldn't hear and couldn't stop — some nights he understood what he had actually done. He had not saved his daughter. He had erased her, patiently, professionally, the way one erases a line of set type by distributing it back into the case, letter by letter, until the sentence that was a person becomes only raw material, sortable, reusable, anonymous. He had made her safe by making her no one. And the worst of it, the thing he carried like slag in his blood, was that she had become *good* at being no one. Good at the silences. Good at the empty face. He watched her master disappearance with the same frightening speed she mastered everything, and each mastery was a small funeral, and he attended them all, and said nothing, because saying things aloud makes them real.

* * *

On the fifth anniversary of the haircut — she was ten, quick and silent and already taller than her mother had been — he came down to the shop before dawn and found her already there, asleep with her cheek on the imposing stone, one hand resting open beside a forme she had locked up perfectly, a broadside for the ropewalk, twelve lines, no errors. He stood in the doorway a long time, watching her breathe.

Then he crossed to the cabinet at the back, the deep one with the false bottom he had never explained, and put his hand flat against its lid, and stood there, feeling through the wood the weight of what waited inside — steel punches, older than both of them, cut for words the government had drowned in the harbor along with a constitution.

Not yet, he told it. Not yet. She isn't ready. I'm not ready.

Behind him, unheard, his daughter slept on, her fingers curled loosely around a piece of type she had been holding when she fell asleep — and if he had turned, if he had looked closely, he would have seen which letter it was, and it would have kept him awake for the rest of his life.

The bell above the shop door rang. Morning's first customer stamped frost from his boots, and Marek turned away from the cabinet, and put on his face, and went to see what the world wanted printed.
