## 9. Paper Is Rationed Too

The spoon had been her mother's.

That was the thing Theo kept not thinking about as she wrapped it in a square of linen and tucked it into the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, where it lay like a small cold bone. It had been her mother's before it was anyone's inheritance — part of a wedding set that had come apart piece by piece across the years of Theo's childhood, forks sold in lean winters, knives traded in worse ones, until only two spoons remained in the drawer beneath the shop's stairs, nested together like sleeping birds. One Marek had used for his salt. The other had sat untouched through five years of ration books and queue lines and soup made from soup bones already boiled twice, because neither of them would say aloud whose mouth it was meant for.

Now she carried it toward the fish market with her collar up and her breath steaming, and she told herself it was only metal. Only weight. Only the past converted to currency, which was, after all, what currency was supposed to be good at.

The city did not make lying to yourself easy. Every wall she passed had been papered and re-papered with decrees pasted over decrees, so that the notices about flour rations were half-covered by notices about requisitioned shipping, which were themselves bleeding at the edges under newer bills announcing curfew hours no one obeyed except the gendarmes enforcing them. The paste pots still came out every morning; the city's one reliable industry now seemed to be the manufacture of announcements about what could no longer be had. Paper, too, was on the lists. Paper was rationed like bread, like coal, like hope — official stock reserved for official lies, sold by weight to registered printers who signed registers, and every signature a name delivered into someone else's ledger.

Marek's register was clean. That cleanliness had cost them years of careful smallness: almanacs, invoices, funeral cards, labels for pickle jars. Nothing worth remembering. Nothing worth investigating.

But Vemmen's forme lay hidden behind the false wall in the back room, three hundred names' worth of testimony set in lead, and lead without paper was only a grave with better diction.

So: the spoon. So: Lotta.

* * *

Lotta worked out of a paperhanger's shop on Sill Street, or rather the shop existed and Lotta was understood to be its proprietor's widow, though everyone on the street knew the actual proprietor had gone to sea eleven years ago and Lotta had simply continued as if he were a rumor she declined to confirm. She hung wall coverings for the respectable houses on the hill. She also, in the manner of water finding its level, knew where every unregistered ream in the port city slept at night.

Theo had met her twice before, both times for the shop's ordinary needs — a batch of endpapers during the winter shortage, a favor of grey board for a coffin maker's order. Both times Lotta had looked at Theo with pale, unhurried eyes and asked after nothing but measurements.

Sill Street smelled of herring and creosote, and beneath both, faintly, of old glue — Lotta's trade baked into the very bricks. The shopfront window had been painted over in whitewash except for one hand-span circle scraped clear, through which a brass bell's shadow was visible, and beside the door a strip of wallpaper sample hung like a flag: roses, faded to the color of weak tea.

Theo pulled the bell rope. Somewhere inside, a chime answered, muffled, like a sound heard underwater.

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, then the chain slid free without a word, because Lotta had seen her through the circle first — she always saw everyone through the circle first — and whatever calculation she performed behind those eyes had evidently concluded in Theo's favor.

"You're thinner," Lotta said, closing the door and shooting the bolt. "Sit."

The shop's interior was papered floor to ceiling in mismatched samples, so that standing in the room felt like standing inside a dozen strangers' parlors at once — damask here, woodgrain there, a border of hunting scenes running along the ceiling cornice above stripes meant for a nursery. Rolls stood in barrels like cordwood. And everywhere, stacked in ways that looked accidental but were not, paper: wrapping paper, blotting paper, newsprint, ledger stock, the plain sturdy rag-content sheets that took ink like a confession takes silence.

Lotta herself was small and square and moved like a woman who had spent decades carrying heavy rolls up narrow stairs — economically, with her whole body. Her hands were enormous for her frame, scarred at the knuckles with paste scars and blade nicks. She poured chicory coffee from a pot that lived permanently on the stove, in two cups that did not match, and pushed the less chipped one at Theo.

"I need stock paper," Theo said. "Printing stock. Good rag content, medium weight. A quantity."

"How large a quantity?"

Theo had done the arithmetic a hundred times in the dark. Three hundred formes, if each forme held the testimony whole — no. Vemmen's composition ran to perhaps forty printed pages when imposed properly. Two thousand sheets to be safe against spoilage, misfeeds, the press's appetite for ruining exactly the sheet you couldn't spare.

"Two thousand sheets," she said. "Maybe twenty-five hundred."

Lotta drank her coffee. Outside, a gendarme's boots rang past on the cobbles, unhurried, the sound of a man with nowhere urgent to be because everything urgent was already his. Neither of them moved until the sound faded.

"Registered stock I can't give you," Lotta said at last. "Registered stock has a paper trail wider than the harbor road. What I have is salvage. Ship's manifests seized off a condemned vessel. Overruns from a wallpaper house that went bankrupt — the backing stock, unprinted, decent enough. Some archive paper out of a solicitor's office that burned." She set down her cup. "Unnumbered. Unregistered. Unremembered. It never existed, so you can't be caught with it."

"And the price?"

"That," said Lotta, "is an interesting question."

* * *

Negotiation with Lotta was not negotiation as Theo understood it. There was no haggling, no theater of walking to the door. Lotta named what she wanted by looking at Theo for a long moment and saying, "You brought something to trade. I can see the shape of it in your coat. Show me."

The linen square was warm from her body now, which somehow made it worse. Theo unwrapped it on the sample-littered table, and there lay her mother's spoon among the dead strangers' wallpapers, catching what little light the whitewashed window allowed, throwing a coin of brightness onto a hunting scene where a painted dog chased a painted fox forever.

Lotta picked it up. Turned it over. Held it to the light and read the hallmarks the way she probably read manifest stamps — fluently, without sentiment.

"Silver," she said. "Old. Good weight." Then, because she remembered everything even while asking nothing: "Family?"

Theo's throat closed. She managed, "My mother's."

Something crossed Lotta's face — not pity; Theo would have refused pity; something more like recognition, the look of a woman seeing a familiar species of grief arrive at her counter in a new wrapper. She set the spoon down gently, gentler than the transaction required.

"It's not enough," she said. "Not alone. Not for twenty-five hundred sheets, not this month, when every fool with a press is hoarding against the armistice and the price has tripled since the frost. It buys you five hundred sheets. Maybe six."

"Then I'll come back when I have more to trade."

"With what? What does a type cutter's son have left to sell?" Lotta's voice wasn't cruel. It was precise, and precision can cut deeper than cruelty. "I've watched you walk here, girl. You wore your good coat, and your boots have been resoled twice with different leathers, and you didn't take a tram though the fare is nothing now the soldiers ride free. You've already sold everything easy. So either you go home with five hundred sheets and print a fifth of whatever it is you're printing—"

Theo must have flinched, because Lotta stopped. Looked at her. And then did the thing that Theo would turn over in her memory for weeks afterward: she reached across the table and laid one huge scarred hand flat over the spoon, covering it, as if hiding it again.

"—or," Lotta continued, quieter, "you tell me what kind of work this is, without telling me, if you understand me, and I extend credit against a future I may never collect on. Because I hang paper on walls, and walls hear things. And lately the walls have been hearing about a printer who was hanged in the spring for words instead of drowned quietly like everyone else, and they've been hearing about men going through his affairs like pigs after truffles, and they've been hearing that Captain Reval has taken a personal interest. Walls hear a great deal about Captain Reval."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — the widow who was or wasn't a widow, or a lodger, or nobody.

"I understand you," Theo said.

"Good." Lotta removed her hand from the spoon but did not push it back across the table. It stayed where it was, midway between them, belonging to neither. "Here is my understanding, then. You are buying paper that doesn't exist, to print something that officially never happened, about men whose names will get us both hanged. I don't want to know if that's true. But if it is true, then it isn't commerce anymore, and I don't price commerce the same way I price—" she searched for the word, found it, chose it deliberately, "—debt. Debts to the dead. Do you follow?"

"I think so."

"Then: the spoon buys six hundred sheets. The rest I'll advance against delivery. Not money — I don't want your money, money leaves trails like a bleeding man leaves footprints. Delivery means this: when it's printed, some pages come through me. My people move things. Things move better than people, these days; nobody searches a crate of wallpaper." She leaned back. "That's my price. Not just silver. A share of the risk, which is also a share of the reason. At my age you stop wanting profit and start wanting to have been somebody."

Theo looked at her mother's spoon on the table between them, gleaming against a foxhunt frozen mid-gallop, and understood that she was watching it become the last thing it would ever be. In Lotta's till it would be weighed, broken from its history, melted perhaps into some anonymous ingot. Its curve that had fit a living mouth, the monogram worn soft on the handle — all of that would end in a crucible, and the metal would go on without the memory, which was either a tragedy or a mercy depending on how you believed matter thought.

Her mother had died nameless in every way that mattered. Now her silver would die useful. Of the two fates, Theo could not decide which Marek would call kinder.

"Done," she said.

* * *

They packed the first six hundred sheets into two oilcloth bundles, and Lotta's lodger — a silent boy with a wallhanger's shoulders who might have been her son, or nephew, or no relation at all — carried them out the back and around through the fish gutters to the corner Theo indicated, well away from Sill Street, well away from any route that connected the shop on the harbor to the shop on the hill. Lotta's methods were old and smooth with use.

At the last moment, at the door, Lotta caught Theo's sleeve.

"The child," she said.

Theo's blood went to ice water. "I don't—"

"Don't waste us both." Lotta's pale eyes were unreadable as unmarked paper. "I saw you cross the market square Tuesday. You carry laundry differently when there's nothing in it. And you bought milk off the Andersen woman twice in one week, and the Andersen woman sells to maybe four households, and I am the Andersen woman's paperhanger, and paperhangers see everything, because everybody's walls belong to somebody who talks." She paused. "The fever's been in the north quarter. Babies go fast when it goes through them. Keep it away from other children, keep it warm, and if it sickens anyway, go to Mother Ilse on Rope Lane and tell her I sent you. She asks nothing. It's a profession, asking nothing. We're a guild, of a kind."

Then she shut the door, and the bolt shot home, and Theo stood in the gutter-smelling alley with six hundred blank sheets in her arms and the sensation — vertiginous, almost funny — of having been comprehended completely by a stranger and permitted to continue existing anyway.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, because the long way had fewer eyes and because she needed the wind. The water was the color of gunmetal, working itself into small furious peaks under a sky the same shade, so that sea and air met somewhere without a seam and the ships rode in the ambiguity. Two of the berths that should have held fishing boats held gray transports now, requisitioned, their decks crawling with figures loading crates. The armistice talk was in its second stalled week. Everyone in the city could feel it the way animals feel weather: the pressure dropping, the whole port holding its breath before the front came through.

Six hundred sheets. Against her ribs, where the spoon's weight had been, there was now only absence — a cold spot shaped like nothing, like a name.

She thought about Marek at breakfast, salting his bread from her mother's remaining spoon, not meeting her eyes. She hadn't told him what she meant to trade. She hadn't told him she was going out at all; she'd left while he sorted the morning's jobbing work, and by the time she returned the lie of errands would be old enough to pass unexamined. This was new between them, this small secession. Five years of practicing silences together, and now she was keeping one from him, and the strange thing was how little it differed from keeping one with him. Same skill. Different allegiance.

Or the same allegiance, grown up. She didn't know yet. She suspected she wouldn't know until the last sheet was printed and the question resolved itself into whatever shape it had been forming all along.

At the harbor's elbow she stopped, out of habit, at the fishmongers' row where the day's unsold lot was dumped cheap at dusk. Herring today, silver-brilliant, piled like coins — like spoons, like anything small and shining that the sea gives up and the city devours. She bought two, wrapped in newsprint that she smoothed out later at the shop's counter and read twice: more requisitions, the curfew moved earlier, and buried at the bottom in eight-point type, a single paragraph noting that the amnesty under discussion in the armistice terms would apply to "persons bearing arms," with no language at all about persons bearing presses.

Peace was coming. She could read it coming, in the shrinking of the notices, in the gendarmes' unhurried boots, in the transports loading for departure. Peace was coming like a tide comes in — and tides do not care what they float away.

In the back room, behind the false wall, Vemmen's testimony waited in its forme, patient as lead. On the table beside the press, six hundred sheets waited, blank as snowfall. Between the waiting paper and the waiting type lay only the press, the treadle, the night hours, and her own two hands, which had learned from her father how to cut letters into steel and had never once, in five years, been allowed to sign anything.

She squared the first bundle against her hip and carried it to the stone that held the composing stick, and as she worked, fitting salvage sheets to the tympan, testing margins, she found she was whispering — not words, only counting, the rhythm printers breathe by — and that the rhythm had steadied into something close to prayer when the shop bell above the street door rang, once, sharply, in the particular double-tug Marek used when he wanted to warn her before he was even through the door.

He came in fast, white around the mouth, and put his hand flat on the counter the way Lotta had put hers on the spoon.

"Gendarmes," he said. "Two streets over. Going door to door. They're asking about paper."
