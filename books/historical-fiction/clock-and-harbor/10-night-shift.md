## 10. Night Shift

The clock in the harbor tower struck twelve, and Theo counted the strokes the way she counted everything now — against the time it would take for someone to hear.

She had waited three nights past the moment she'd promised herself she would begin. Three nights of standing before the locked back room with the key cold in her fist, of listening to the city settle into its winter quiet, of telling herself that tomorrow the wind would be right, or the patrols would be elsewhere, or her nerve would return from wherever it had gone to hide. The forme of type sat on the stone shelf where Vemmen had left it a year ago, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, waiting with the patience of lead. Lead was patient. Lead could wait out empires. It was only flesh that ran out of nights.

Tonight there was no wind to speak of, which meant sound would carry — down the cobbled lane, across the frozen canal, into the barracks where gendarmes drank coffee and complained about their boots. Tonight was as bad a night as any. She had run out of good ones.

Theo lit no lamp. She knew the shop the way her father knew the grain of boxwood: by touch, by memory, by the small geography of obstacles. The press stood in its corner like a sleeping animal, the iron frame blacker than the dark around it, and she laid her palm flat against the platen as if greeting something that might startle. Beneath her hand the metal held the day's warmth still, faintly, the way stones hold summer.

"Forgive me," she whispered, though she wasn't sure whether she spoke to the press, to Vemmen's ghost, or to the child asleep upstairs in the drawer lined with sailcloth.

She unlocked the back room and carried the forme out in both arms, cradled against her chest like a second infant. The type clicked softly as she walked, a sound like teeth. In the lamplight she allowed herself one match's worth of flame — cupped, shielded, dying fast — to check the lockup. Quoins tight. Furniture true. Vemmen had been a compositor of the old school; his forme would have survived a fall down the stairs. She thought of him setting these letters at night too, some night a year ago, working with the same terror in his wrists, knowing what each word would cost him. He had set them anyway. That was the whole of it. He had known the price printed plainly on the first proof sheet of his life, and he had set them anyway.

She inked the rollers. The smell rose up around her — linseed and lampblack, the smell of her entire childhood, the smell she had once believed was the smell of safety itself.

Then she put her foot to the treadle, gripped the lever, and pulled.

The press spoke.

It was not loud, not truly — a wooden handpress makes a sound like a great door closing, a rhythmic groan-and-clap, muffled by walls and snow. But in the silence of three o'clock in a city under martial law, it sounded to Theo like a confession shouted from the roof. Each pull of the lever sent her heart into her throat. Between pulls she stopped entirely, breath held, head cocked toward the window, listening for boots on the lane, for the scrape of a lantern shutter, for anything. The frost ticked in the window glass. A dog barked twice near the fish market and went silent. Nothing else. Nothing yet.

The first sheet came off the tympan crooked, half-blinded with ink. She pulled it free anyway and held it up, and even in the dark she could feel the truth of it under her fingers — the crisp bite of the letters, the names marching down the page in columns like soldiers who had finally been given leave to speak. *Captain Reval.* She read the name with her fingertips before her eyes could find it, the way you touch a burn to confirm it is real.

*Colonel Brandt. Major Ilves. Captain Reval.* The men who had signed the order. The men whose signatures Vemmen had copied from documents smuggled out of the gendarmerie at the cost of — how many lives? She would probably never know. The paper didn't say. Paper never said the half of it.

She corrected the frisket, re-inked, pulled again. The second sheet came clean. The third. By the tenth her arms had found the rhythm and her mind had wandered ahead of her hands, which was dangerous, because her mind kept going to Joss, two days in a cell somewhere, and to the rumor moving through the quarter like damp through plaster: *they say he named a printer.*

*Let them come,* she thought, pulling the lever. *Let them—*

No. Not that. She was not brave enough for that thought and she refused to pretend otherwise. What she thought instead, what she made herself think with every stroke of the treadle, was smaller and harder: *one more sheet. One more after that.* Courage, she was learning, was not a thing you had. It was a thing you did in units small enough to survive.

* * *

Somewhere past the hundredth impression, the floorboard at the top of the stairs sighed.

Theo froze with the lever half-drawn, the platen open, the fresh sheet clinging wet to the type. Her father stood on the third step, in his shirt, barefoot on the cold boards, his gray hair unbound and wild around his face. In the thin light of the banked stove he looked less like a man than like something carved — all planes and hollows, the face she had studied her whole life trying to read and never quite deciphered.

Neither of them moved. The press ticked as the iron cooled. Somewhere upstairs the baby slept on, mercifully, dreamlessly.

"You'll ruin your friction," Marek said at last. His voice was rough with sleep and something else. "The tympan. If you don't pack it evenly."

"I packed it evenly," Theo said.

A silence. Then he came down the stairs without another word, crossed the room, and stood over the press the way a priest stands over an altar he has not approached in years. He looked at the forme — at the testimony, at the names — for a long time. Long enough that Theo felt the silence begin to curdle into the old silence, the silence of five years, the silence they had built together brick by brick until neither of them remembered the shape of the door.

Then Marek rolled up his sleeves.

He took the brayer from her hands, charged it with ink in four strokes — the way he had taught her, the way his own father had taught him, the motion so old it lived in the bone — and said, "You're pulling too hard on the return. Let the counterweight do it. You fight the machine and the machine wins."

Theo stared at him.

"It's not an apology," he said, not looking at her. "It's a correction. Take the lever. I'll ink."

And that was how they began.

* * *

They worked through the coldest hours, and neither of them named what was happening between them, because naming it would have broken it, the way light breaks the dark-adapted eye.

But Theo understood it anyway, understood it in her body before her mind caught up. For five years she and her father had worked this shop side by side and never together — he at the composing stone, she at the finishing bench; he serving the safe customers, she serving none; two people sharing a trade the way two prisoners share a wall. He had taught her everything except why. He had cut away her name, her hair, her mother, her future, and called it love, and she had swallowed it because the alternative was starving, and because — this was the part she admitted only now, in the dark, with ink on her knuckles — because some part of her had agreed that invisibility was the price of staying alive, and had hated him for being right.

Now here they were, breaking every rule of their shared survival at three in the morning, and he had come downstairs barefoot to do it beside her.

He did not say *I was wrong.* He did not say *I was afraid for you,* though she could see the fear living in him still, in the way his eyes went to the window at every distant sound, in the way his hands paused over the ink slab when a sledge runner scraped past on the canal road. He did not say any of it. Instead he showed her a trick for keeping the frisket cords from chafing, one she'd never seen, one he must have learned from his father in Riga forty years ago, and the showing of it was the sentence, and her learning of it was the answer.

"Vemmen set this himself," Marek said once, during a pause while she changed sheets. It was not a question.

"He hid it in the back room. Behind the paper stock."

"I wondered." Marek smoothed a sheet onto the tympan, and his voice went somewhere far away. "I wondered, for a year, what he'd hidden. I told myself I didn't want to know. Knowing things is what got him hanged." He drew the bar; the platen closed; the sheet took its impression. "Knowing things is what will get us hanged."

"Then why are you here?"

Marek was quiet for the length of three pulls. When he answered, he answered the press rather than her, his eyes on the rising and falling iron.

"Because your mother set type too. Before you were born. Did you know that?" Theo shook her head, and he nodded slowly, as if confirming something to himself. "She had a fine hand. Finer than mine. She used to say that ink was the only place a poor man's word weighed the same as a rich man's." Another pull. Another sheet. "I burned her letters to keep you alive. I told myself that was the same as loving you." His jaw worked. "It isn't. It was only half of it. This—" he touched the wet page, the names, gently, the way you touch a wound you've decided to stop pretending isn't there, "—this is the other half. I owed you the other half. I'm late paying."

Theo pulled the lever. The platen fell and rose. She found that her eyes were burning and blamed the ink, the way he had always blamed the ink, and understood suddenly that he had never once been fooled by her either.

They did not speak again for an hour. They didn't need to. The press filled the room with its slow heartbeat, and the two of them moved around it like a single body remembering itself — she at the lever and the tympan, he at the ink slab and the drying lines strung along the ceiling beams, sheets hung like strange laundry, like flags of a country that did not yet exist. Two hundred copies. Two hundred and fifty. The pile grew on the bench beneath a covering of clean newsprint, and above it the baby slept, and outside the frost deepened, and the harbor clock struck four, and five, and the sky beyond the frosted glass stayed black as fresh ink.

Once, near the end, Marek said, "When this is done — the last sheet — we burn the forme. All of it. The type goes back in the case mixed with the rest, indistinguishable. No one can prove what was printed here."

"Yes," Theo said. And then, because the night had made her honest: "Papa. If Reval comes before it's done—"

"He won't get the forme." Marek's voice was very level. "And he won't get you. Those are the only two orders I have left in me, and I intend to keep both."

It was the closest thing to a vow she had ever heard him make. She bent over the tympan and laid the next sheet true.

* * *

Near dawn, with perhaps thirty sheets remaining, the baby woke.

The cry came thin through the floorboards, and Theo's whole body turned toward it like a compass needle. But before she could move, Marek said, "Go. I'll hold the pace. Ink stays warm better than temper does." And there was — she heard it clearly, unmistakably, for the first time in five years — humor in his voice, dry and worn but real, the ghost of the man her mother must have married.

Upstairs, the child lay red-faced and furious in her sailcloth nest, and Theo lifted her, and the fury dissolved instantly into that astonishing, unreasonable trust infants extend to whoever comes. Theo carried her down against her shoulder, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, and the baby watched the press with enormous grave eyes as the platen rose and fell, rose and fell, mesmerized by the rhythm, by the smell of ink, by the lamplit dance of her grandfather's arms.

"She likes the noise," Theo murmured.

"Her father liked the noise too," Marek said. "All printers' children do. It's the first music we hear." He glanced at the child, then at Theo, and something passed across his face — grief and tenderness tangled so tightly they could not be separated, the expression he had worn the day he cut her hair. "We should give her a name. Even a false one. Children shouldn't be *the child.*"

"Not tonight," Theo said softly. "Tonight she's just ours."

Marek looked at her — really looked, the way he had not since before the haircut, since before the burning of the letters — and nodded once, and pulled the bar.

The last sheets came off the press as the windows went from black to charcoal to the bruised gray of a Baltic winter dawn. Theo hung the final impressions on the lines, counted the stack twice — two hundred and eighty-one perfect copies, plus eleven flawed ones she would burn — and stood looking at them, her arms aching, her back a knot of fire, ink to her elbows, the child heavy and warm against her chest.

It was done. The testimony existed now in a way that could not be undone. Somewhere in the world, in ledgers and manifests and mail sacks, two hundred and eighty-one small paper bombs waited to go off.

Marek began breaking down the forme, distributing the type back into the cases with quick, sure fingers, erasing the evidence letter by letter. Theo watched him work and felt the strangest sensation — that she was watching her father for the first time. Not the man who had raised her as a son, not the craftsman, not the frightened keeper of silences. Something older and simpler: a printer. Just that. A man whose whole life had been an argument conducted in lead, and who had, at the end, chosen which side of the argument to stand on.

"Papa," she said.

He looked up, a *g* in his tweezers.

"The midwife asked me yesterday — she asked whether the child would be christened, and under what name." Theo's heart was going hard, though her voice stayed steady. "I told her I'd decide soon. And I realized I couldn't answer for her until I—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a name I haven't said aloud in five years. Mine. And I think—"

The bell above the shop door rang.

Not loudly. A small, cheap, cheerful sound, the sound of customers, the sound of ordinary mornings — and it went through the room like a rifle shot. Marek's hand closed around the tweezers. The baby startled and began to cry. Through the frosted glass of the front window, distorted into pale ghosts by the old panes, Theo could see shapes: two of them, three, standing on the step, and one raising a gloved fist to knock again.

Through the door, muffled but perfectly clear, came a voice she knew — smooth, unhurried, almost friendly, the voice of a man who enjoyed arriving early.

"Master Nystrom. Open, please. Captain Reval. We've come about the almanac."
