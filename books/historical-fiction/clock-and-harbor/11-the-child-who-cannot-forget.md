## 11. The Child Who Cannot Forget

The inspection lasted eleven minutes. Theo counted them by the clock above the type racks, the way she had learned to count everything in this shop — in beats of metal, in intervals between sounds that could mean safety or ruin.

Captain Reval did not ransack anything. That was the worst of it. A man who ransacks tells you what he fears; a man who strolls tells you what he owns. He walked the length of the workshop with his gloved hands clasped behind him, tilting his head at the cases like a buyer at an estate sale, pausing once to run a fingertip along the bed of the press and study the ink that came away — still faintly red-tinged, though Theo had scrubbed it twice since the night shift. Marek stood beside him performing the oldest play in the shop's repertoire: the tired craftsman, half-deaf from decades of iron, answering questions a beat too slowly, apologizing for dust, offering coffee he knew would be refused.

"The almanac," Reval said, and smiled at the specimen Marek produced, a thin thing printed three years ago for a government office that no longer existed under the same name. "Good work. Clear hands. We may have need of clear hands."

"Trade's slow," Marek said. "Paper's short."

"Paper is always short for someone." Reval let the sentence sit in the room like damp. Then his eyes moved — not to the press again but to the back wall, to the locked door of the rear room, and rested there perhaps two seconds longer than curiosity required. "And the child?"

Theo's blood went to ice water. She had draped a blanket over the crate-cradle behind the composing stone when the bell rang, but the crying had already happened; the crying had been heard through the door before it was even opened.

"My late cousin's daughter," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and bored, a voice belonging to a woman with no secrets worth the trouble. "Widow. No money. She works days at the cannery."

"Cousin," Reval repeated pleasantly, as if tasting the word. "Family is a comfort, these days. So few people have any left."

He tipped his cap. The bell rang its small cheap cheerful sound behind him, and they watched through the frosted glass until the pale ghosts dissolved into the ordinary gray of the street.

Marek did not speak for a long time. When he did, it was only to say: "He'll come back." And then, quieter, in the voice he used for truths too heavy to lift at full volume: "He looked at that door the way a man looks at a strongbox he intends to buy the key to."

* * *

The fever came four nights later.

It began as restlessness — the baby fretting at the breast of the bottle, turning her face away from milk Theo could ill afford to waste — and Theo told herself it was the weather, the damp rolling off the harbor, the teeth. All winter she had catalogued the ordinary miseries of infancy and matched each one to an ordinary cause, because ordinary causes were survivable. But by midnight the small body in her arms was hot as a stone left too near the stove, and by two o'clock the baby was making a sound Theo had never heard from her, a thin cracked mewing that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than the chest, and her eyes when they opened were glassy and wrong, looking at things that were not in the room.

Theo stood in the middle of the dark workshop holding the child and understood, with the cold clarity of the fully awake, that she was going to lose her.

Not to gendarmes. Not to Reval and his pleasant appetite. To a fever, an ordinary fever, the kind that emptied cradles in every quarter of the city without anyone being hanged for it. There was a poetry to that which made her want to break something.

There was no doctor. A doctor wrote things down. A doctor asked names, registered births, kept ledgers — and a ledger entry saying *infant, female, approximately five months, presented with fever* would sit in some office drawer waiting for exactly the man who had stood in their shop counting seconds with his eyes. There was no doctor.

But there was Mother Selma.

Every port quarter had one — a woman past the age of caring what she was called, who delivered children into the world for eggs and firewood and gratitude, who set bones and knew herbs and asked nothing on paper. Theo had never met her. She knew of her the way everyone on the harbor streets knew of her: the crooked house on Rope Lane with the blue door, the woman who had been present at more first breaths than the parish priest at last rites. She also knew — the way you know things without knowing how — that Mother Selma had attended Aldis Vemmen's wife, all those years ago, when the household on Tanners' Row had still been whole.

Which meant Mother Selma might know faces.

Theo dressed in the dark. She wrapped the baby in sailcloth — the same cloth, she realized with a strange lurch, that Vemmen had chosen, as if it were the only material in the city fit for carrying precious cargo — and pinned it tight, and strapped the bundle against her chest beneath her coat, where the heat of the small burning body pressed against her own like a second heart working double shifts. Marek was asleep, or pretending to be; she left without waking him. There are conversations a person cannot have twice in one week, and *we must trust someone* was one of them.

The streets at three in the morning belonged to cats and patrols. Theo moved the way she had taught herself to move over five years of invisibility — not hiding, never hiding, because hiding drew the eye, but simply being unremarkable: a woman in a shawl moving fast through fog, head down, someone's tired servant on someone's errand. Fog came off the water in slow white tongues and ate the lamplight at twenty paces. Somewhere across the basin a ship's bell struck, muffled to almost nothing. The baby burned against her ribs and made her small cracked sound at each jolt of the cobbles, and Theo found herself talking to her — low, tuneless, wordless murmuring, the sound she had promised herself she would never make in front of witnesses, because mothers make it and she was not supposed to be a mother, she was supposed to be nobody's anything.

Rope Lane smelled of tar and old hemp and, underneath, the ghost of the tanneries that had been pushed out years ago. The blue door was exactly where rumor said. Theo knocked softly, then less softly.

The door opened on a chain, and one eye appeared in the gap — brown, ancient, unimpressed.

"Fever," Theo said. "A baby. Please."

The eye considered her for a long moment. Considered, perhaps, the hour, the fog, the risk, the thousand reasons old women in occupied quarters had learned to keep their doors chained. Then the chain scraped, and the door opened wide enough for a body.

"Bring her to the warm end," said Mother Selma. "And you — stop praying out loud. It makes the child nervous."

* * *

The warm end of the house was a kitchen with a stove that had seen forty winters and a table scrubbed so often its grain had gone soft as cloth. Herbs hung from every beam in dusty constellations — Theo recognized none of them, but the smell was immediate and physical, camphor and something bitter and something green, the smell of a hundred years of other people's emergencies.

Mother Selma unwrapped the sailcloth with hands that were spotted and swollen at the knuckles and moved, nonetheless, with terrifying economy. She did not ask the baby's name. She did not ask Theo's name. She laid her palm flat on the small forehead, then against her own cheek — an old calibration, flesh to flesh — and clucked once, a sound containing an entire diagnosis.

"How old?"

"Five months. Perhaps a little more."

"Whose?" the old woman asked, but it was not really a question; it was a door held slightly ajar, to see what Theo would do with it.

"A dead cousin's," Theo said.

"Mm," said Mother Selma, in the tone of a woman filing a statement in a drawer she would keep anyway.

She worked. Willow bark steeped in a chipped blue pot. Cool cloths wrung from water with vinegar in it, laid along the small limbs, changed and changed again. She talked the whole time, not to Theo, exactly, and not quite to herself — a running low commentary on fevers she had known, on the Kolb girl who'd burned for three days and grown up to marry a ship's chandler, on the importance of not wrapping a hot child in wool because *everyone wants to wrap them in wool, and wool is for after.* Theo sat on the stool she was pointed to and watched the baby's face and thought about nothing else in the world.

Somewhere past the worst hour, near dawn, the fever broke. It went the way it had come — not dramatically, but by degrees, the glassy look clearing from the baby's eyes until they focused, at last, on Theo's face, and the small mouth bent itself around something that was unmistakably, impossibly, a smile.

Theo did not cry. She had trained herself out of it long ago. But her hands shook as she took the child back, and Mother Selma watched the shaking with the expression of a woman reading a page she'd read many times.

"She'll want feeding," the old woman said. "Real milk, not whatever chalk they're selling at the ration shops. Frau Imhof on Cooper Street has a goat and a soft heart; tell her I sent you and don't tell her anything else."

"I can't pay you," Theo said. "Not yet. Not properly. But I—"

"You'll pay me by keeping her alive," Mother Selma said, cutting the air with a spotted hand. "That's the whole fee. Now hold still."

She had turned to gather the cloths, and then stopped. Stopped entirely, the way a clock stops — and Theo saw the change come over her face, saw the old woman turn back and look at the baby properly, not as a fever but as a face.

The silence stretched. The stove ticked. Outside, the fog was beginning to thin toward the color of dishwater, and somewhere a first cart rattled over the cobbles, and Theo sat absolutely motionless with her heart slamming against the small hot weight on her chest, because she had seen recognition before — she had spent five years learning to see it in customers' eyes when they looked at her jawline and almost remembered where — and she knew every stage of it. The pause. The frown. The reaching-back.

Mother Selma reached back.

"Vemmen," she said quietly. Not a question either. This one was a key turning.

Theo's mouth opened and produced, by pure reflex, the lie: "I don't—"

"Don't," the old woman said, without heat. "I've caught more babies in this quarter than the priest has baptized. I caught that man's daughter in a house on Tanners' Row with a blizzard coming off the water, and I held her before her father ever did, and there is a shape to a face, girl, that runs in blood like a vein. Around the eyes. Here." She touched her own cheekbone, lightly. "You've covered the hair. You've covered everything coverable. But I've known that face since before it had teeth, and now she has hers coming in, and so help me, you will not stand in my kitchen and insult us both."

The room tilted. Theo gripped the edge of the table. Every scenario she had rehearsed — the patrol, the inspection, the knock at the shuttered window — and here at the end of it all was a barefoot old woman with herb dust on her apron, holding the truth up to the stove light like a coin to be tested.

"They hanged him," Theo said. Her voice came out low and rough. "In April. For pamphlets."

"For telling the truth on paper," Mother Selma said. "Yes. I know what they hang people for in this city. My husband was shot on the quay in '05 for refusing to load guns onto a ship full of men who hadn't done anything." She took the pot off the stove with a rag, poured the willow bark tea through muslin into a cup, and set it aside with the unhurried competence of a woman whose hands would not be hurried by history. "So. What will you do now? Run to Reval and confess you've been harboring evidence?"

"No."

"Then we understand each other." She brought the cup over and put it in Theo's hands, folding the girl's fingers around the warmth. "Here is what you seem not to know, because you're young and you've been living among men. Men think silence is theirs. They think it's a discipline they invented — soldiers standing at attention, printers keeping their presses shut, all that proud grim quiet." Her eyes were very steady. "Women have been keeping silence since before your press was smelted, child. We keep it in childbirth and in cellars and in kitchens exactly like this one. We keep the silences that keep other people alive. I have kept so many I could paper a house with them." She looked down at the baby, who had fallen into an exhausted milk-drunk sleep against Theo's collarbone, one fist curled open. "So no, I will not say what I've seen. Not to Reval, not to the priest, not to God if He asks impolitely."

"Why?" Theo whispered. It came out before she could stop it — the question she was never supposed to ask anyone, the question that broke every rule of survival she had built her life on. *Why would you risk it?*

Mother Selma smiled. It transformed her whole ruined face into something suddenly, startlingly young.

"Because somebody once kept mine," she said. "And because—" she nodded at the sleeping child, at the small face slack and peaceful and, yes, even to Theo's frightened eye now, unmistakably shaped — "because the dead leave us their debts, girl, and their faces, and we pay what we can. Go home. Keep her out of drafts. Come back in three days and tell me she's fat."

* * *

Theo walked back through the thinning fog with the baby breathing slow and even against her chest, and the whole city looked different to her, the way a room looks different when a lamp is lit in one corner.

For five years she had believed her silence was a wall she alone maintained — brick by brick, lie by careful lie, a fortress with a population of one. She had believed that invisibility was a solitary art, that every person who kept their head down kept it down alone. But the lane she walked through now was full of people who had said nothing: Frau Imhof with her goat and her soft heart, who would hand over milk without asking; Lotta with her ledgers of favors; dockwives who saw crates move at night and reported only weather; an old midwife on Rope Lane who had just weighed a child's life against her own safety and paid out the latter without blinking.

*A whole invisible country,* Theo thought. *We've been living in it all along. We just never wave to each other.*

She let herself in the shop's back way, past the cold press, past the composing stone where the day's legitimate orders waited in their trays like patient animals. Marek was awake — of course he was awake — standing at the window with his arms folded, and the look he gave her as she came through the door held a whole argument in it: fear, relief, accusation, and beneath all of it, so faint she almost missed it, something that might have been awe.

"She's well?" he asked.

"The fever broke." Theo unwrapped the sleeping child and laid her in the crate-cradle, tucked the blanket to her chin. Then she straightened, and heard herself say the words she had sworn on Rope Lane she wouldn't say, because some doors, once opened, refuse to close again:

"Marek. She knows whose child this is. And it changes nothing — she keeps it. There are others like her. Others who keep quiet not out of fear but out of—" she searched for the word and found it, unexpectedly, in her father's trade — "out of *composition*. Set deliberately. Held in place." She looked at him across the dim workshop, at the man who had taught her that silence was armor, and felt the old armor loosening at the straps. "We're not as alone as you taught me."

Marek was quiet for a long moment. Then he crossed to the back wall, put his hand flat against the locked door of the rear room — the door Reval's eyes had rested on, two seconds too long — and stood there as if listening to something on the other side.

"There's something you should see," he said. "Before he comes back for it."
