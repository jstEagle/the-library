## 12. Reval's Offer

He came on a Thursday, when the light through the shop's front windows went the color of weak tea and the whole street smelled of coal smoke and the herring carts making their last rounds before the frost took even the gulls' appetite away.

Theo saw him first from the composing stone, a shape in the glass that did not belong — a man standing too straight for a customer, hat held at an angle that spoke of barracks rather than bureaus. Her hands stopped over the case without her willing them to. Her body had learned faster than her mind which silences mattered; it had learned this one first.

"Father," she said, very quietly. "Gendarme."

Marek Nystrom did not look up from the galley he was locking. His hands continued their work — quoins tightened, furniture set, the forme made fast as a coffin lid — and only when it was secure did he lift his head and study the window with the mild, nearsighted expression he wore like an apron.

"The almanac man," he said. "From the ministry. I told you he'd come."

She had no memory of being told. But that was her father's gift: he arranged the past so smoothly that doubt slid off it. She nodded, and turned back to her type, and positioned herself where she could see the door without appearing to watch it — the oldest skill she had, older than cutting, older than reading.

The bell above the door gave its small, cheap cry, and Captain Reval stepped into the shop as if stepping into a church he had paid to build.

* * *

She had seen him once before, at a distance, the week after Vemmen hanged — a gray figure on the steps of the courthouse, gloved hands folded, watching the crowd disperse the way a fisherman watches water he knows is empty but checks anyway. Up close he was smaller than fear had painted him. A compact man, neat, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten: pale brows, a jaw of ordinary resolution, eyes the gray of old newsprint. He wore his uniform well but not lovingly, the way a craftsman wears good tools he has outgrown.

"Mister Nystrom." He pronounced the name carefully, as though setting it in type himself, checking it against some inner galley. "Captain Reval. Ministry of Information. You'll forgive the hour."

"The hour is fine for work," Marek said. He came around the counter wiping his hands on his apron, and Theo marveled, even as dread closed its cold hand around her throat, at how completely he became a shopkeeper — shoulders stooped just slightly, gaze deferential and slightly dull, the posture of a man whose ambitions ended at the edge of his own doorway. "You're welcome in Nystrom & Son."

*Son.* The word landed on her like a dropped chisel. Five years she had answered to it, worn it the way the baby downstairs wore its swaddling — not by choice, but because someone had decided what she was before she could speak. She had never heard her father say it to an officer of the state before, though. Never heard him spend it like coin.

"The almanac," Reval said. "For the new year. The government requires twelve thousand copies — tables of tides, feast days, grain prices, the usual devotionals of ordinary life." He smiled, and the smile fit his face poorly, like a forme locked too tight. "Your shop came recommended. Honest work, they said. Discreet."

"We print what is brought to us," Marek said. "And bring nothing else out."

"Exactly the reputation I hoped you'd have." Reval walked. That was the thing about hunters, Theo thought, watching him move between the cases — they liked to walk the ground first. His gloved fingers trailed along the frame of the press without touching the iron itself, hovering, a blessing withheld. He read the specimen sheets pinned above the stone. He glanced, briefly, professionally, at the ceiling beams, at the floorboards, at the closed door of the back room.

Theo kept her eyes on her type. She was sorting fourteen-point bodoni and her hands were steady, and she knew they were steady because they had to be, the way a drowning woman's legs are steady. Each letter clicked into the composing stick. *R. E. V. A. L.* If she had wanted she could have set his name right there, in lead, and it would have been more honest than anything happening in the room.

"My superiors," Reval said, still walking, "are compiling an inventory. After the unpleasantness of last spring — the pamphlets, the strikes, the regrettable executions—" he said the word *executions* the way other men said *prices*, a fact among facts— "there is a desire for tidiness. Records consolidated. Materials accounted for." He stopped at the specimen wall and tapped one sheet with two fingers. "You knew Aldis Vemmen, I believe."

The shop went very quiet. Quiet the way the harbor goes before ice.

"We bought type from him," Marek said. "Years ago. Before his... politics."

"Everyone bought type from him. He was the best cutter in three ports." Reval turned from the wall. For the first time he looked directly past Marek, at Theo, and his gaze moved over her the way an assessor's moves over livestock — noting age, condition, sex beneath the binding of the shirt, filing all of it somewhere behind the gray eyes. She looked back with the flat, empty competence of a boy who cared about letters and nothing else, and after a moment he dismissed her, and something inside her unclenched half a degree. "His estate, such as it was, came to the crown. But estates are rarely complete. Men like Vemmen leave things lying about. Matrices. Punches. Manuscripts. Sentiments." He let the last word sit. "The ministry is offering — consideration — to establishments that assist in recovering such materials. Consideration in the form of contracts. Protection. Continuity."

*Continuity.* Theo understood the word precisely. It meant: your fire stays unlit, your son stays unshaved and unexamined, your door stays yours.

Her father was silent long enough that she risked a glance at him.

* * *

What she saw frightened her more than Reval had.

Her father's face had not changed — same stoop, same dull shopkeeper patience — but underneath it something was moving, and she could see the movement the way you see a current under ice, a traveling shadow that deforms the surface without breaking it. He was thinking. Not the slow, hoarded thinking she knew, the careful arithmetic of a man who had spent five years rationing his own existence. This was faster. Newer. He was improvising, and she had never once in her life seen her father improvise.

"Materials," Marek said slowly, rubbing his chin as though the question were merely tedious. "Vemmen cut matrices for half the shops in this city. If the crown wants them inventoried, they'll be digging through every cellar from here to the border. Half of them will be fakes within a month — men selling the ministry lead and calling it sedition." He shook his head with a small tradesman's bitterness. "I bought forty pounds of type from him in the year of the dock strike. Am I to surrender my stock because the man who cast it turned traitor? Then take my press too, Captain, and my name with it, because none of it is innocent by your arithmetic."

It was a good answer. It was better than a good answer — it was a *performance*, and Theo stood at her case feeling the floor tilt gently under her, because her father had never performed in his life. He had endured. He had calculated. He had taught her which questions to swallow whole. But he had never lied — not fluently, not with rhythm, not with the small human irritability that makes a lie breathe. And now here it was, coming off him smooth as ink off a roller, and the worst of it — the part that made her stomach turn over slow and cold — was that he was *good* at it. Where had he learned this? In what five silent years had he practiced this voice, against what interrogation she had never witnessed?

Reval studied him. The gray eyes did not narrow; they simply went still, the stillness of a man comparing two documents under lamplight.

"You misunderstand me, Mister Nystrom. There's no accusation in it." He smiled again, that ill-fitting smile. "But consider. Twelve thousand almanacs is a contract that would carry a shop like this through two bad winters. The ministry prefers to reward cooperation. It prefers — how shall I put it — to know its printers." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "And to be remembered by them fondly."

"And if there's nothing to give?"

"Then there is nothing to give." Reval drew off one glove, finger by finger, and looked at his bare hand as though checking it for ink. "Of course, searches do happen. Random ones. Wartime makes men thorough. And a search is such an imprecise instrument, isn't it? Things get damaged. Presses especially. Iron cracks when it's moved carelessly." He drew the glove back on. "A shame it would be, if a shop with a government contract suffered a careless search."

The threat was so beautifully wrapped that Theo almost admired it. Almost. What she actually felt was the vertigo of standing on a high place at night — no rail, no bottom visible, only the knowledge of the fall.

"I'll speak plainly, since you've been plain with me," Marek said. And now his voice changed again — dropped lower, slower, and Theo realized with a fresh lurch that this too was technique, that her father had gears in him she'd never suspected, that the man who raised her on silence had a whole theater of voices stored behind it. "I had a brother. Hanged after the naval mutiny, for a leaflet he may or may not have printed. So understand me, Captain: whatever I feel about printers who die for their politics, I keep it in my chest, where it does no one harm. I have a son. I have a trade. I want the almanac contract and nothing else — not favors, not protection, not the honor of informing on dead men's ghosts. Give me the work and leave me my silence. It's all I've ever asked of this country."

Silence filled the shop like floodwater.

Then Reval laughed — softly, without warmth, a laugh that was really a receipt. "An honest man," he said. "They told me so, and here you are proving it expensively." He produced a card from his breast pocket and set it on the counter, squaring its edges with one finger until it lay perfectly parallel to the counter's grain. "The offer stands through the end of the month. Almanacs, or inventory. Cooperation is remembered, Mister Nystrom. So is reluctance." His eyes crossed the room once more — the press, the cases, the closed back-room door, Theo's face — taking his final measurements. "Keep your boy out of trouble. These are poor years for young men with opinions."

The bell cried at his exit. His footsteps receded down the street, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world and everyone else's clock in his pocket.

* * *

Neither of them moved for a full minute. The coal stove ticked. Downstairs, faintly, the baby made a small waking sound and settled again, and Theo's heart turned over toward her like a compass finding north.

"He knows," she said at last. Her voice came out lower than she intended, sanded down by fear. "About the back room. He looked at that door twice."

"He looks at every door. It's how they're trained." Marek picked up the card from the counter, read it as if checking a bill, and held it over the stove grate. It caught, curled, and was ash between one breath and the next. "End of the month," he said quietly, watching it burn. "That's not courtesy. That's arithmetic. He's telling us how long we have."

"Father." She came out from behind the case. Her legs felt strange, weightless, as if the vertigo of watching him lie had loosened something structural in her. "You lied to him."

"I've lied to worse men for less reason."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You haven't lied. Not ever. You taught me the opposite — you said a lie is a debt that collects with interest, you said we keep safe by keeping *empty*—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and pressed on. "Who taught you to do that? To sound annoyed? To mention a brother you never told me existed?"

For a moment — only a moment — the shopkeeper fell away, and beneath him she glimpsed something older and rawer: her father at her mother's graveside, perhaps, or her father the night he burned her mother's letters, some original grief that all his masks were cut from. Then the mask resettled, gentler now, tired.

"You learn things," he said, "when you decide something matters more than hiding."

The words hung in the ink-smelling air between them, and Theo felt their meaning arrive slowly, the way a printed page darkens as the ink dries. Something mattered more than hiding. The forme in the back room. The testimony sleeping in lead. The child asleep below their feet with a dead man's jaw and a living man's name waiting for her.

"End of the month," she repeated. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone still at last — not the forced stillness of survival but something else, something with direction in it. "How many sheets is that, Father? At night, at full treadle, without rest?"

Marek looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle some account of his own.

"At night," he said, "with two of us — enough."

Outside, the tea-colored light failed altogether, and down at the harbor the lamps came on one by one, and somewhere among the requisitioned ships a bell rang the change of watch, counting down hours neither of them could see but both of them now heard.
