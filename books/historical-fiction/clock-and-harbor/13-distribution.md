## 13. Distribution

Lotta came on a Tuesday, in the hour when the harbor fog thinned enough to show the masts but not the faces behind them. She did not knock the way customers knocked — three brisk raps and a step back to be seen — but pressed her palm flat against the door and waited, the way family knocked, or the dead. Theo opened it with the bar half-lifted, ready to close it again.

"You've been printing at night," Lotta said. Not a question. She slipped inside smelling of tar, wet wool, and the anise drops she sucked constantly, a habit she believed disguised the smell of smuggled tobacco. "The whole street hears your press after midnight. They think you're mad, working for orders that don't exist."

"Let them think it."

"Madness is good cover." Lotta set her market basket on the composing stone without being asked, as if the shop were hers, as if she had always belonged behind this counter and everyone else had merely been keeping her place. She was sixty or thereabouts, broad through the shoulders from thirty years of hauling paper bales up gangplanks, and her eyes were the pale gray of old newsprint, taking everything in and committing none of it aloud. "Show me what you're asking men to carry."

Theo hesitated. The bundle of printed sheets sat under the floorboard behind the proofing press, wrapped in oilcloth, and every instinct she had learned at her father's elbow said: *nothing leaves this shop that doesn't have to.* But the sheets had been multiplying for two weeks now — Vemmen's forme was set and locked, and Marek had taught her the lock-up so well that she could produce four hundred impressions a night before her arms gave out — and four hundred pages in a cellar were not testimony. They were kindling. Evidence waiting for its fire.

She lifted the board. She put the packet in Lotta's hands.

Lotta read the way dockmen read manifests: fast, lips barely moving, missing nothing. Her thumb stopped at the third page, where Vemmen's type named Captain Reval by rank and regiment, and stayed there a long moment. Then she folded the sheet along its crease with a bookbinder's precision and looked up.

"Three ships leave this port for Copenhagen before the borders shut. The *Selma*, the *Andrea*, and a coal barge called the *Petra* that no one inspects because no one wants to inspect coal." She tapped the folded page against her palm. "Manifests go out with each of them. Bills of lading, crew lists, letters of carriage. A page slipped between the manifest and the duplicate — stamped, sealed, official-looking — travels farther than any passenger. Inspectors read stamps. They don't read content."

"And the dockworkers?"

"The ones who hate what happened last spring? More than you'd think. Fewer than you need." Lotta's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I can give you three names. Joss Eklund works the *Selma*'s berth — big man, quiet, lost a brother on the pier the day they fired on the strikers. He'll do it for grief. Greta Sand does cargo tally; she'll do it for coin, which is more reliable than grief, though less warm. And there's a boy, Anselm, sixteen, who runs messages and would carry the plague itself across a border if someone told him it would make the world fairer." She paused. "He worries me most. Fairness makes young men loud."

Theo heard her father's tread overhead — he had taken to pacing at night now, the boards speaking his restlessness down through the ceiling — and lowered her voice further.

"What happens to them if they're caught?"

Lotta drew the anise drop from her cheek, considered it, and put it back. "What happened to Vemmen," she said. "That's what you're really asking me, isn't it. Whether the thing you're making is worth becoming a rope." She stood, retied her basket, and settled her shawl. "I don't answer questions like that. I told you. I carry, I remember, I don't judge. Bring twelve copies sealed in brown paper to the *Selma* berth Thursday, dawn tide. Address them to a shipping house in Copenhagen — I'll write you the name. Make them look like trade correspondence." At the door she stopped, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The girl should stay indoors Thursday. Fog or no fog."

Then she was gone, and the door closed on the smell of tar, and Theo stood in the gray half-light of the shop holding the knowledge that her silence had just grown hands and legs and a route to the sea.

* * *

Joss Eklund had carried things his whole life, and he had developed, over twenty-two years and eleven of them on the docks, a philosophy of weight.

Everything weighed. That was the first truth of the piers. Crates weighed, barrels weighed, iron plate weighed, sacks of grain weighed more than anyone expected because grain breathed and shifted and settled into its own gravity overnight. Men who had never worked cargo thought weight was fixed. Dockworkers knew better. Weight was behavior. It moved. It argued. It decided, at the worst moment, to become something else entirely — a swinging arc, a crushing settle, a run of loose coal down an inclined plank toward a man's ankles.

So when the woman at the print shop — thin, ink-stained, eyes like someone perpetually listening to a room two walls away — handed him a brown-paper packet weighing perhaps four hundred grams and said, *Copenhagen shipping house, it goes in the mail bag, not the cargo hold,* Joss weighed it the way he weighed everything.

Four hundred grams. Lighter than a hatch cover by roughly the weight of a cathedral. Heavier than anything he'd ever carried.

"It's paper," the woman said. Her name was Nystrom; Lotta had given him that much and no more. Up close she was younger than her voice suggested, and there was ink worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that it looked like shadow, like something grown there rather than spilled. "Trade correspondence. It needs nothing from you but to be forgotten the moment it leaves your hand."

"Forgetting is my best trade," Joss said, and it was nearly true.

His brother Matz had been forgotten by everyone else already. Shot on the north pier the fourth of May, in front of the fish market, in front of forty witnesses who all afterward remembered looking away. The gendarmes' report used the phrase *disorderly assembly dispersed with appropriate firmness*, and Joss had read it eleven times in the newspaper office window, standing in the street, letting the words arrange themselves into the shape of his brother's body on the wet cobbles. *Appropriate firmness.* He had gone home that night and sat in the dark until morning, and somewhere in that darkness a decision had formed that he had never spoken aloud, the way a splinter works itself so deep you stop feeling it as foreign and begin to feel it as part of your own hand.

Now the splinter had a name and an address and a weight of four hundred grams.

Thursday, dawn tide, the fog lay on the harbor like a held breath. Joss came down the ramp with the morning's manifest folder under his arm — bills of lading, crew list, letters of carriage for the *Selma*'s timber and pit-props — and the brown packet rode between the duplicate manifest and the master copy like a leaf pressed in a book. He had done exactly what the woman asked. He had made himself forget it. He thought about the weather instead, about the grain shipment due Friday, about whether his boots needed re-nailing before winter. He shook the second mate's hand. He watched the customs clerk, a myopic man named Petersen who took bribes in coffee and never counted past the third crate, stamp the folder without opening it.

The stamp came down green and square. *CLEARED.*

Joss felt the weight leave him the way a load leaves your shoulder when a crate settles onto the deck — sudden lightness that is almost vertigo, almost grief. He walked back up the ramp into the fog and let himself think of Matz, briefly, deliberately, the way you visit a grave: here, brother. Something is moving now. Something with your name in the margins of it, even if no one ever prints the margins.

Behind him the *Selma*'s mooring lines went slack, one by one, and her hull slid out into the white water toward Denmark and the world beyond it.

* * *

It worked twice more.

Greta Sand took pages aboard the *Andrea* tucked into a coil of tarred rope the customs men wouldn't touch with their gloves. The boy Anselm ran a sealed envelope to the *Petra*'s mate inside his coat, walking so fast and so proudly that Theo watched from a doorway and thought, with a lurch: *he looks like a boy delivering a love letter. He has made it a romance. God keep him.*

Twelve copies went out the first week. Twenty the second. Lotta brought word — never written, always murmured in passing, in the fish market, at the chandler's, once in the churchyard where they stood a decorous moment beside Lotta's long-dead husband's stone — that a Copenhagen shipping clerk had found the packet and passed it upward, and that upward, in this case, meant journalists, means committees of lawyers in neutral capitals, means *it is no longer only yours.*

"They're calling it the Harbor Testimony already," Lotta said, sucking her anise drop to a sharp point. "In three cities that don't exist yet on any map you'd recognize. Word travels faster than ships, girl. It always has. We just gave it a stamp."

Theo went home through the fog with her heart beating strangely, and could not tell whether the feeling was triumph or terror, and understood after a block or two that it was both, braided tight as hawser, indistinguishable at any distance. The network was real. She had touched it and it had borne weight. And a network, unlike a secret, had blood in it — blood that could be let.

She said as much to her father that night, standing at the case while he cleaned the press rollers, and Marek was silent a long time, working the leather with lampblack, his hands black to the wrist like a man who had reached into the earth.

"When I was young," he said finally, "in the years of the first suppression, we distributed too. My master's shop. Twelve of us. I was the youngest." The roller turned, turned again. "Within a year, four were arrested. Two were released. One fled to Sweden and lived to be an old man whose grandchildren never knew why he flinched at knocking." The roller stopped. "And one was my master. They took him out the back of his own shop and he did not come back, and I stood in the crowd at the trial and listened to the sentence, and I learned that day what a distribution is."

"What is it?" Theo asked quietly.

"A promise," her father said, "that other people will suffer for your conviction. On a schedule you don't control." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more black than cloth. "I'm telling you this so you know I understand what you've started. Not so you'll stop. I know better than to say that now." And he went back to the rollers, and the shop smelled of lampblack and fear and something that might, in a kinder light, have been pride.

* * *

Joss took the third run himself because Anselm's mother had come to the pier in tears and Greta's nerve had visibly thinned, and because — he admitted it to no one, least of all himself — the carrying had begun to feel like visits. Each packet that left his hands was another flower laid down. Four hundred grams of remembering.

The *Petra* was delayed by a coal strike upstream, so the third run went with the coastal freighter *Maren*, a smaller berth, a different customs desk. Different desks meant different men, and the man at the *Maren*'s desk was not Petersen.

He was new — Joss noted that first, the way he noted everything: new uniform, new ledger, the particular eagerness of a man proving something to somebody. And he was counting. Not crates. Signatures. His finger traveled down the crew list, cross-referencing, and then he pulled the manifest folder toward him and began, for the first time in Joss's living memory of that desk, to actually open it.

The fog had come in thick that morning. Thick enough that the ramp was slick, the lamps halos, the whole harbor reduced to sounds — hawsers groaning, gulls arguing over something invisible, coal shifting in a barge's belly. Joss stood six feet from the desk with a coil of rope over his shoulder and calculated, with the cold speed of a man who has spent his life beneath swinging loads, what he could reach in the time it took a man to unfold four sheets of paper.

Too far. Always too far. That was the second truth of weight: you never got there in time.

The clerk's finger stopped on the brown packet. He looked up — not at Joss, at the room, checking who was watching — and that look was the whole world's warning compressed into a second: *this man has found something and is deciding what it makes him.*

Joss did not run. Running was confession, and besides, his legs had gone strange. He stood with the rope on his shoulder and watched the clerk slide the packet into his drawer, close the drawer, and smile at the next man in line as if nothing in the world had changed — and everything in the world had changed, and Joss Eklund stood in the harbor fog holding four hundred grams of nothing at all, and understood that the splinter in his hand was about to be dug out in a room with no windows.

They let him finish his shift. That was the cruelty of it, or the method — he couldn't tell, hauling timber all morning with the drawer, the packet, the smile replaying behind his eyes. They let him clock off at noon. They let him walk fifty meters up Strandgade, past the net-mender's, past the wall where Matz's name had been chalked last spring and scrubbed off since, leaving only a faint ghost of letters that Joss alone could still read.

Then the door of the net shed opened, and two men in civilian coats stepped out, and one of them said his name — his full name, *Eklund, Joss Matzen* — using his father's name, his brother's name, as though they had a right to it, and Joss set down his rope coiled neatly, neatly, because a dockworker leaves nothing loose where it can fall on someone, and went with them into the dark of the shed.

* * *

By evening the harbor had the story, the way the harbor always had the story, passed hand to hand in fragments too broken to repeat and too clear to doubt. *Eklund. Taken. The net shed. Gendarmes' boat came at dusk, no lights.*

Theo heard it from Lotta, who appeared at the shop door after closing with no basket, no anise drops, her face gray as her eyes.

"They have him," she said. "And they have the packet. And by morning they'll know it came from the *Selma* berth, and the day after they'll know who tallies cargo, and the day after that—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "Girl. The question isn't whether they'll come asking. It's whether Eklund can hold his tongue, and how long, and what a man's tongue is worth under their hands." She gripped the doorframe, and for the first time since Theo had known her, Lotta looked old — old and tired and furious at her own arithmetic. "You have sheets still in that cellar. Sheets not yet shipped. Every hour you keep printing, that man's silence buys you more of them. Think about what that costs. Then decide what kind of printer you are."

She was gone before Theo could answer, and there was no answer anyway, and upstairs the baby woke and began to cry, and Theo stood in the dark shop between the sleeping press and the crying child with the whole weight of the harbor settling onto her shoulders — and above her, the floorboards creaked as her father rose from his bed, and his footsteps crossed to the head of the stairs, and stopped there, waiting, as if he already knew that everything had just changed.
