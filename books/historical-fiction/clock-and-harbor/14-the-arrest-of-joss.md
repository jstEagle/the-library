## 14. The Arrest of Joss

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and Theo knew before she opened the door that the world had tilted.

Not a knock, exactly — a scraping, a dragging, the sound of a body that had forgotten how to stand upright. She had been at the press since two, pulling sheets of the testimony in the dark with the shutters latched and wool blankets nailed over the window frames, and she had learned to read every sound the street made. The milk cart at five. The gendarme's boots at seven, passing without stopping, his rhythm so familiar she could have cut his stride into type. But this — this wet, labored scraping against her threshold — belonged to no schedule.

She opened the door a hand's width, the bolt still thrown, and saw Joss folded against the doorframe like a sack of wet rope.

His face was wrong. That was the first thing she understood, before the blood, before the torn knuckles, before the way his left eye had swollen to a slit weeping something clear and pink. His face was wrong the way a page is wrong when the forme has been disturbed — everything still there, still legible, but shifted a hair's width out of true, so that the whole reads as a stranger's sentence.

"Let me in." His voice came out crushed, gravel over gravel. "Please. Before the fog lifts."

Theo looked past him at the street. Fog still held the lane in a grey fist; the lamp at the corner burned inside it like a coin at the bottom of a well. Nothing moved. She threw the bolt.

Getting him inside was like hauling a drowned mast. He was heavier than he looked, or perhaps fear had weight, and he sagged against her shoulder, and she smelled on him the harbor, coal dust, and beneath it all the iron-and-vinegar stink of a beating. She got him to the back room, onto the old cot where her father slept in the afternoons, and lit no lamp — she knew the room by touch, the way she knew the type cases, and she worked by feel: water, cloth, the bottle of spirit her father kept for cleaning rollers that Joss pressed to his split lip without being told.

"Who?" she asked.

"Does it matter?" He tried to laugh and it became a cough that bent him double. "Reval's men. Three of them. They were waiting at the coal yard." He looked up at her with the one eye that still opened, and in it she saw something that frightened her more than the blood: not fear. Shame. "They didn't follow me here. I made sure. I walked the long way, doubled back through the fish market, waded in the shallows by the breakwater to kill the scent dogs might—" He stopped. Started again. "They let me go, Theo. You understand? A man doesn't walk out of a coal yard with three gendarmes unless they want him to walk somewhere."

The spirit bottle trembled in her hand.

"They wanted you to lead them to us."

"I lost them." He said it too quickly, too cleanly, a line rehearsed on the walk through the fog. "I lost them, I'm certain of it, I—"

"Joss." She knelt so their faces were level. His breath was sour and fast. "Look at me. Did you lose them?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them the house settled, a beam ticking as the night's cold released its grip, and Theo thought: even the wood is counting the seconds.

"I don't know," he said finally, and it was the first true thing, and it was worse than any lie. "I don't know."

* * *

Her father came down at six, as he always did, and stopped on the bottom stair with his hand still on the rail, taking in the scene the way he took in a misprinted sheet — one long look, cataloguing every fault. The dockworker on his cot. The blood on the towel. His daughter kneeling in her ink-stained apron, the press behind her still warm, the smell of fresh ink in a room where no legitimate work happened after midnight.

Marek Nystrom did not ask what happened. He had not asked a question in five years that he did not already know the answer to.

"They took the others," Joss said from the cot. "The two who ran the manifests for the eastern piers. Anders and the boy — Petter. They came for them at dawn yesterday. I heard it from the night watchman at the coal gate, and then I was stupid enough to go back for the last bundle."

"You went back." Marek's voice was flat as a setting rule.

"For the pages." Joss's good eye flared. "Two hundred sheets. Two hundred names that don't exist anywhere else in this world except in that forme downstairs. You don't leave that in a coal yard, old man. You don't leave the dead waiting to be found."

"No," Marek said. "You burn them."

The word dropped into the room like lead into a quoin. Theo felt it in her chest.

"You burn them," her father said again, softer now, and he was not looking at Joss at all. He was looking at the floor, at the boards above the cellar where the punches slept in their oilcloth, where the forbidden typeface of a suppressed constitution waited in the dark like a seed waiting out a winter. "You burn them, and you scatter the type, and you take the child and you go north on the next coal barge, and you live. That is the whole of the arithmetic. Everything else is vanity."

"It's not vanity." Theo heard her own voice and was surprised by its steadiness. "It's testimony."

"It's lead." He turned to her then, and in the grey light his face looked carved, the deep lines around his mouth like cuts from a graver. "Theo. Listen to me as your father and not as your— as my apprentice. Listen. A man was taken yesterday. Two more this morning, if what he says is true. By tonight Reval will have three men in a room with lamps and pincers, and three men are two too many to keep a silence. When the thread is pulled, it doesn't stop at the dockworkers. It comes up the lane. It comes to this door. And when it comes, there will be a child in this house who cannot run."

"The armistice—"

"The armistice is a rumor." He said it with terrible gentleness, the gentleness he used on customers who could not pay. "Rumors do not stop pincers. I have watched this city for sixty years, daughter. I have set type for every government it has ever had, and I will tell you what I know: regimes do not fall on schedule. They fall late. They fall after the people who could have testified are already in the ground. Peace is a door that closes, not opens. I told you this."

"You told me the work was suicide from the first night," Theo said. "And then you came down and worked the treadle beside me until dawn."

He said nothing. The silence between them had been, for five years, a wall; in the last weeks it had become a bridge, and now she felt it swaying under both their weights.

Joss stirred on the cot, trying to rise. "I can be gone by full light. I'll go east, take the blame with me if they take me again, tell them it was my network, my—"

"Lie down," Theo said. "You'll tear the stitches of your face open." She turned back to her father. "How many sheets are left?"

"Two hundred and forty," he said, before he could stop himself, and she saw him hear his own answer, saw the number hang in the air between them like a struck bell. He knew the count. Of course he knew the count. He had been feeding the treadle beside her every night; he had been counting every sheet as it came off the press, wet and ghostly in the lamplight, each one a small resurrection of the dead.

"Two hundred and forty," Theo repeated. "At three hundred an hour with the blankets up and the treadle worked soft. One hour. Perhaps two if a roller needs re-inking." She held his eyes. "One hour, Father, and then we burn the forme and scatter the type and go north, exactly as you say. But we go with the work finished. Not with it dying in the cellar two hours short."

"Two hours is a lifetime. Two hours is the width of a noose."

"Two hours is the difference between Vemmen being a criminal and Vemmen being a witness." Her voice cracked on the dead printer's name, and she let it. "They hanged him in the spring and called it justice. The pamphlets they killed him for — I set some of them myself, you remember, before we knew, before anyone knew — those pamphlets said *there were orders*. They said *we have seen the orders*. But they never printed the names. The names were still in his head when they hanged him, and Reval and the others thought the names went into the ground with him." She knelt and pulled the folded testimony sheet from her apron pocket, opened it, laid it on the cot between them like a card turned face up. The type marched down the page in Vemmen's last composition, the forme he had set and hidden and died rather than betray. Names. Ranks. Dates. Orders signed. *Reval* at the head of them like a capital letter beginning a sentence no one had been permitted to finish.

"Two hours," Theo said, "and the names are out of the ground. Out of this city. On ships to ports where no one can unpublish them. That isn't vanity, Father. That is the only immortality our trade has ever offered anyone, and you taught it to me yourself. Every letter you ever cut — you cut it so that a word could outlive the mouth that spoke it. Well." She looked down at the page, at the names. "Here are words that have to outlive the men who want them dead."

Marek Nystrom stood for a long time without moving. The fog outside the shuttered window was turning from grey to the color of unprinted paper. On the cot, Joss watched them with his one eye, and Theo understood that he was watching his own fate being weighed in the scales, and that he was too proud to beg and too honest to pretend he wasn't afraid.

"You'll work the treadle soft," her father said at last. "No more than two hundred an hour. If a roller squeals, you stop it. If a gendarme's boot passes the door, you stop it. And when the last sheet is pulled—" his voice did something she had never heard it do, something old and tired and terribly like her mother's voice in the one memory Theo had kept of it, "—when the last sheet is pulled, we burn the forme, we pack the punches, we take the child, and we go. Not after. Not one sheet more. Swear it."

"I swear it."

"Then wake the baby quietly," he said, turning toward the stair, "and heat the glue pot. If we are going to be fools, we will be fools with a clean press."

* * *

They worked through the last of the fog and into the raw gold of morning, the blankets nailed over the windows turning daylight to dusk, the press speaking its soft iron language — the treadle's sigh, the platen's kiss, the whisper of paper taking ink. Theo fed the sheets. Marek worked the treadle, his shoulders rolling with the rhythm she had known since childhood, the rhythm that had been the sound of safety all her life and was now the sound of danger, and she found she could not separate the two any longer, that perhaps they had never been separable, that everything safe she had ever loved had carried danger folded inside it like a sheet carries ink.

The baby slept in the drawer of the type cabinet, lined with felt, rising and falling with her breath. Between sheets, Theo touched her small warm foot through the blanket, a ritual now, a countersign: *still here, still here, still here.*

At nine sheets from the end, the boots came.

Not one pair — many. Not passing — stopping. Theo's hands went still over the feed board. Through the nailed blankets, muffled to a heartbeat, she heard them in the street: the jingle of the gendarmerie's harness, the low orders, and then — worst, most intimate — a voice she knew, smooth as a freshly cast slug, giving instructions too quietly to be parsed.

Reval was in the lane.

Marek did not stop the press. He looked at her across the machine, his hands never breaking rhythm, and shook his head once: *work*. She fed the next sheet. The platen came down. The platen rose. Ten sheets. Eight. The boots moved along the lane, and stopped — she could feel it through the floor, the way she could feel the press through her bones — stopped directly outside, where the light showed under the door, where the shadows of feet would fall if the fog had not burned away and the sun stood high and merciless.

Seven sheets. Six.

A fist struck the door. Not a knock. A blow, iron-shod, that made the type cases ring in their frames and the baby stir and whimper in her drawer.

Theo's heart went white. Marek's treadle did not falter.

"Open in the name of the Provisional Authority." Reval's voice, through three inches of oak, courteous as an invoice. "Master Nystrom. We know you're awake."

Five sheets. Four.

Marek released the treadle. He crossed the floor without a sound, took Theo's shoulders, and put his mouth against her ear.

"The back stair," he breathed. "The cellar. Take her, take the pages, take the punches. I'll hold the door."

"Father—"

"I held it once before," he said, and she did not know what he meant, and she knew exactly what he meant, and there was no time. "Go. *Go.*"

Three sheets. Two.

She lifted the baby from the drawer, wrapped her against her chest with shaking hands, swept the finished stack into the oilcloth with the punches, and stood one last instant in the room where she had spent her whole life being no one — the smell of ink and glue and her father's tobacco, the type cases with their cold constellations of letters, the press with its last two sheets waiting in the feed like words left unsaid.

The fist struck the door again, and this time the wood complained.

Marek turned to face it, straightening his apron, an old craftsman preparing to receive a customer, and Theo went down the back stair into the dark with her daughter against her heart and the truth against her ribs, and behind her, above her, she heard the bolt draw back, and her father's voice, level and courteous as a setting rule:

"Captain Reval. What a surprise. Come in — mind the ink."
