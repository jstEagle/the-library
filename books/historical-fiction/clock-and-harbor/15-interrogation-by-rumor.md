## 15. Interrogation by Rumor

The rumor arrived before the fear did, and it arrived the way weather arrives in a harbor city: first as a change in the light, then as a pressure in the ears, then all at once.

Theo heard it on the third morning after Reval's visit, standing at the counter with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her hands black to the second knuckle. Herr Brandt, who had bought his funeral notices from Nystrom & Son for eleven years and had never once looked her father in the eye while doing it, came in for his standing order of invoice paper. He did not come to the counter. He stood just inside the door with his hat in his hands, turning it, turning it, and when Theo asked what he needed he said, "Nothing. Nothing, forgive me," and left without buying anything at all, and through the window she watched him cross the street and pause at the corner and look back at the shop the way a man looks at a house he believes will not be there next spring.

By noon, Frau Ilsen had cancelled the programs for her daughter's christening — postponed, she said, until calmer days, though there had been no calm days in this city for two years and everyone knew it. By evening the tobacconist on the lane had stopped nodding to Marek across the street. And Lotta came after dark with a half-ream of stock wrapped in brown paper and a face like a closed ledger, and stood in the doorway breathing the ink-smell and said, quietly, so that only Theo heard:

"They're saying the dockworker named a printer."

"Joss would never—"

"Joss can't say anything." Lotta set the paper down on the counter as though it were something breakable, which it was, though not in the way of glass. "That's what I'm telling you. They're saying it *for* him. Reval's men are in every tavern on the waterfront saying a printer gave names, saying it loud enough that whoever that printer is will hear it and run. Running men are easy men, Theo. A man who runs leaves a door open behind him."

"He isn't running," Marek said from the shadows by the press, where he had been oiling the same screw for an hour. "And neither are we."

Lotta looked at him a long moment. Then she looked at Theo, and then, briefly, at the basket behind the type cabinet where the baby slept with one fist curled against her cheek, and something moved behind Lotta's eyes that was gone before it could be named. "Paper's paid for," she said. "Don't waste it."

The door closed behind her. The shop settled around them like a ship taking water slowly.

* * *

After that, the staring began.

It was a small thing at first, the way rot is a small thing at first. The baker's boy lingered too long outside the window. The woman who kept the lodgings across the lane developed a habit of sweeping her step at whatever hour Theo passed, her eyes following like the eyes of portraits that have learned to move. Old men who had played dominoes in the tavern beside the shop for thirty years now played their dominoes two streets over, and the tavern beside the shop grew quiet in a way that was louder than noise.

They know, Theo thought, and then corrected herself: no. They know nothing. They suspect everything, which is worse, because suspicion needs no evidence and cannot be disproved, and Reval knows this better than any man in the city. He has not spread a lie. He has spread a *possibility*, and possibility is the cheapest poison in the world; one drop flavors the whole well.

She caught herself counting the windows opposite. She caught herself listening for boots with a rhythm too regular for civilians. She caught herself, at odd moments, holding her breath — and each time, the baby would squirm against her, or the forme would shift under her fingers, or her father would strike the quoins another quarter-turn, and she would breathe again, because the work did not permit a woman to stop breathing. The work required breath. The treadle required legs, and the legs required a body fed and slept and functioning, and so she functioned, the way a press functions, on repetition and iron.

Two hours of sleep a night. That was the ration now, self-imposed, non-negotiable. She slept from four to six, in the narrow bed in the back room with the baby strapped against her chest because the nights had turned cold and the coal was counted, and even asleep some part of her stayed upright in her like a watchman who never comes off his wall. She dreamed in galley proofs. She woke with her fingers moving, setting type against the mattress, composing sentences no one would ever read.

Marek did not tell her to slow down. That was the strangest thing, the thing she turned over during the long hours at the stone: the old man who had taught her that survival meant small runs and quiet customers and never, ever letting your politics be legible in your work — that man now stood beside her at the case at midnight and handed her sorts before she reached for them, reading her composition hand the way he read scripture, and said only, once, in the voice of a craftsman correcting an apprentice:

"Your spacing is drifting. You're tired."

"I'm fine."

"You're tired," he said again, not unkindly, "and tired hands set loose pages, and loose pages shake apart in the press and cost us the sheet and the time. Sleep your two hours. The type will wait."

"The city won't."

He had no answer for that. No one in the city had an answer for that anymore; the armistice delegates were still meeting in their borrowed villa by the sea, still issuing communiqués that said *progress* the way a doctor says *comfortable*, and every day the shelves in the market were emptier and the uniforms in the streets were more numerous and everyone went about their business wearing the particular smile of people who have decided not to look directly at the sky.

* * *

She worked with the baby against her back.

It had been Lotta's suggestion, delivered flatly along with a length of sailcloth: "Your mother carried you the same way, if you'd believe it, through the '09 strikes. Women have always worked with what they love tied where they can feel it breathing." So Theo sewed the cloth into a harness with her own hands at three in the morning, testing each seam twice, because a seam that failed at the wrong moment would fail at the only moment that mattered, and after that the child rode high between her shoulder blades, above the ink smell, above the smell of lamp oil and cold iron, close enough that Theo could feel each breath as a small warm pulse against her spine.

The child had learned to sleep through the press. That was the miracle no one remarked upon: that an infant could slumber through the clatter of iron on iron, the groan of the tympan, the rhythmic thud of platen falling on paper — but she did, rocked by it perhaps, the way sailors' children sleep through storms because the storm is the sound of home. Sometimes, deep in a run, Theo would forget she was there, and then the small weight would shift, and Theo would feel it travel down through the harness through her back through her ribs into her heart, this anchor, this cargo, this reason, and she would pull the next sheet and lay it in the delivery board with a care that bordered on liturgy.

Because that was what it had become. She would not call it prayer — she had been raised in a household where prayer was a thing done quickly and quietly and mostly for appearances — but she could not find another word for what happened at the press after midnight. The setting of the testimony had the structure of devotion: the preparation, the washing of hands, the hours of exact and repetitive labor performed in near silence, the offering up of the finished page into darkness. *In the spring of the year, by order of Captain Reval, commanding the third district, eleven men were taken from the yard of the works and shot against the east wall.* Each line she composed was a resurrection. Each sheet she pulled was a soul given back its name. Vemmen had set these letters knowing what they would cost him, and now Theo drew them out of the stick and locked them into the chase and felt their weight — real weight, leaden weight, the weight of the only kind of justice that had ever survived a bad government, which is the kind that gets printed somewhere they cannot reach.

*Reval,* she thought, inking the roller. *Your name is in lead now. Your name is going to outlive your uniform.*

On the fifth night, the press threw a roller bearing. It seized with a shriek that seemed certain to wake the lane, and Theo froze at the treadle with her heart hammering and the baby stirring against her back, and Marek was already there with the lamp and the wrench, already stripping the assembly with hands that shook only slightly, and they fixed it in forty minutes of held breath and then stood looking at each other across the open machine like two swimmers treading water.

"It held," he said finally. "Forty years and it holds. Machines are honest, Theo. It's only people who—" He stopped. He wiped his hands on a rag very thoroughly. He did not finish the sentence, and she understood that the sentence ended with *betray*, and that he was thinking of Joss in a cell somewhere, or of himself five years ago with a scissors and a bundle of burning letters, or possibly of Reval sitting in their parlor drinking their tea and memorizing their shelves.

* * *

The neighbors' eyes changed the way she worked, though she fought against letting them.

She began taking the baby out through the cellar hatch at dawn for her airing, down the alley that smelled of cabbage-water and cat, instead of walking her openly in front of the shop. She let her hair stay pinned under her father's old cap when she fetched milk, though the widow's story had held for months and no one had questioned it. She found herself rehearsing answers to questions no one asked — *the widow? oh, gone to her sister's, south, weeks ago* — polishing lies the way she polished brass, and hating the smoothness of them, and making them smoother anyway.

But at night, at the press, she refused all caution that cost speed. This was the arithmetic she lived by now, chalked on the inside of her skull: nine hundred sheets ordered. Six hundred and twelve pulled. At two hundred a night, with cleaning and repairs and the bearing that would surely fail again, seven more nights. Seven nights of treadle-work, of ink ground to the right stiffness, of dampening paper in the dark like a woman raising something from the dead. Seven nights, and every one of them borrowed, and the interest on the loan collected in stares and whispers and the memory of Joss's laugh on the dock.

Sleep two hours. Print until the lamp burned low. Feed the child. Set type. Sleep.

Her body began to feel like a poorly locked forme — everything held under tension, everything straining at the quoins. Her eyes burned. Ink worked itself into the cracks of her knuckles and stayed there, so that her hands looked older than her face, and once, catching her reflection in the black glass of the window at four in the morning, she did not recognize herself, and did not mind. Recognition, she was learning, was a currency, and she was spending none of it. Let the street see a tired printer's son. Let Reval hear rumors of ghosts. The woman at this press did not exist, and the woman who did not exist was going to carry the truth out of this city in a coal sack if she had to.

* * *

On the eighth night, with six hundred and eighty sheets stacked and interleaved in the false bottom of the paper cabinet, Marek came up from the cellar where he had been checking the escape route — the hatch, the drain-grate, the gap between the cooper's fence and the tannery wall — and he was not alone in his expression, though he was alone in the room, and what his expression carried made Theo stop mid-motion with the ink knife in her hand.

"What."

"Nothing happened," he said carefully. "I want you to hear that first. Nothing has happened."

"Marek. What."

He sat down across from her at the work table, heavily, the way old men sit when they have been standing in cold places. "The cooper's boy came by the side door. There's talk in the taverns again — new talk. The delegates have agreed on most of the terms. Could be signed within days, they're saying. Within a week at the outside."

For one absurd moment she felt joy rise in her like water finding a level — *the war ends, the killing stops, the city breathes* — and then she saw his face, and the joy turned over and showed its underside.

"And?"

"And amnesty," he said, and his voice was very quiet, very even, the craftsman's voice, the one he used for prices and funerals. "Broad amnesty. For everyone. Except." He laid his folded hands on the table. "Except crimes of the press. Their words. Anyone whose offense was printed matter is excluded — the delegates agreed to that too, weeks ago apparently, quietly. It seems both sides found it convenient to agree that the men who told the truth should not be forgiven for it."

The lamp hissed. Above her back, where the harness usually sat, Theo felt the ghost-weight of her daughter, the phantom pulse against her spine, and beneath her ribs the other weight, the six hundred and eighty sheets, the testimony that was suddenly not just dangerous but expiring — because borders would close when the treaty signed, or harden at least, and neutral ports would stop taking unmanifested cargo, and Lotta's dockworkers would have nowhere to carry it, and the truth would sit in a false-bottomed cabinet in a city that had just legalized forgetting everything.

"How many days," Theo said.

"A few. Perhaps less than the sheets need."

"Then we print faster."

"Theo—"

"We print *faster*." She heard her own voice crack against the rafters and did not lower it. "They've built the amnesty so that it convicts him twice — killed him once and calls him criminal forever. Vemmen set those words knowing exactly this would happen, do you understand? He knew the peace would be built on their graves and he set the type anyway and hid it in our walls and put his daughter on our step. We are not finishing a job, Father. We are racing the door closing."

Marek looked at her for a long moment across the lamplight and the ink-smell and the sleeping city, and then he rose, and took off his apron, and hung it on its peg, and said, in the tone of a man beginning a night shift he intends to finish:

"Then we'll need more paper."

And it was then, as he reached for his coat, that they both heard it — faint, distant, unmistakable in the dead-still hour before dawn — the sound of gendarme whistles, not one but several, sounding from the direction of the harbor.
