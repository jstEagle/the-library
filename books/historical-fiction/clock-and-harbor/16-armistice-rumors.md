## 16. Armistice Rumors

The news came the way bad news always came to the shop: folded inside an ordinary order.

A clerk from the shipping office brought a job ticket on Tuesday morning — five hundred manifests, ordinary stock, blue ink for the borders — and beneath his signature, scrawled in pencil as if it were nothing, as if it were gossip about weather: *Terms agreed in principle. Signing expected within the week.*

Theo read it twice while the clerk examined the specimen wall of typefaces with the idle curiosity of a man who had never considered that letters were made by hands. She kept her face arranged into the mild blankness of a tradeswoman hearing about weather. Inside her chest, something turned over slowly, like a body in sleep.

Peace, then. Peace within the week.

She thanked him. Quoted him a price. Promised delivery by Friday. And when the bell above the door had clattered shut behind him she stood very still among the cases, listening to the city beyond the glass — the trams running again on the Rue de Fer, someone laughing outside the tobacco shop, a church bell that had been silent through the worst months now swinging through its whole arc — and understood that all of it, every ordinary sound, was the sound of a door beginning to close.

* * *

Her father heard it before she could tell him. Marek Nystrom had a sense for news the way sailors had a sense for weather; he came up from the basement that evening rubbing ink from under his nails and said, without preamble, "They'll sign before Sunday."

"You knew."

"I guessed. The garrison stopped drilling. That's how it was last time — they go quiet before they change their shape." He hung his apron on its nail. "Quiet men are dangerous men. Quiet cities too."

"Then amnesty—"

"Will not cover us." He said it flatly, the way he said *the press needs a new frisket* or *we're out of gall-nuts*. He had already done the arithmetic. He had perhaps been doing it for days. "Vemmen is dead. Dead men are easy to leave dead. But anyone who prints what he wrote — that's not rebellion, Theo, that's not pardoned with the rest. Rebellion ends when the war does. A printed page doesn't end. It keeps accusing, year after year, long after everyone's signed everything. They can't forgive what won't stop."

He said it looking at the wall, at the specimen cards, at anything but her. And Theo felt the strange vertigo of hearing her own fear spoken aloud in her father's voice, laid out in his careful craftsman's logic, and finding it worse than when it had lived only in her.

"So we run," she said.

"So we finish." He turned then, and his eyes in the lamplight were the color of old lead. "There are eleven sheets left in the run. Eleven. We did not come this far to stop at eleven."

* * *

The rumor rippled outward through the week like dye dropped in water.

Wednesday: the baker spoke of it openly, flour to his elbows, saying peace prices would come down by spring and wasn't it wonderful, wasn't it finally over. Thursday: two of their regular customers failed to appear — the chandler who ordered invoice heads each quarter, the widow Pern whose memorial cards Marek printed at cost — and those who did come looked at the shop's dark windows a half-second longer than courtesy required. Reval's seed had taken root. *Joss named a printer.* The words moved through the quarter the way damp moved through plaster, invisible and everywhere.

Friday morning Lotta came herself, which she almost never did, appearing in the doorway wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, a paper-wrapped bundle of sugar — real sugar, white, a small fortune — held out like a passport.

"They're closing the harbor," she said, once the door was bolted behind her. "Requisitions frozen. No clearances issued after tomorrow night except naval. My cousin at the registry says the word is *quarantine of paper* — their phrase, not mine." Her eyes went to the back room door, then returned to Theo's face, and Theo understood that Lotta had long ago assembled most of the truth and had chosen, as a kindness and a discipline, never to say it. "Whatever you're not shipping, ship now. After the signing there will be no neutral bottoms loading here for weeks, maybe months. And after months—" She shrugged, and the shrug said everything the silence didn't: after months there would be raids, tribunals, a new government tidying its inherited crimes into unmarked drawers.

"The baby," Theo began, and stopped. She had meant to ask about passage. What came out instead surprised them both. "Lotta. When the borders close — do they close for people, or only for cargo?"

Lotta looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a tram bell rang, bright and absurd, the sound of a city pretending to be healed.

"For people first," she said. "Cargo second. Lists first, questions later." She set the sugar on the counter and pushed it across. "But fog doesn't read lists."

Then she was gone, coat and bundle and all, leaving the smell of coal smoke and the sentence hanging in the air like smoke of another kind.

* * *

That night Theo fed the child by lamplight and tried to think like a typesetter, because it was the only way she knew how to think clearly: one character at a time, each in its proper bay.

The terms would be signed — call it Sunday, call it Wednesday; the pencil note had said *within the week* and clerks did not lie upward, only downward. Upon signing, amnesty. Broad, celebrated, photographed — amnesty for strikers returning to mills, for deserters drifting home, for councillors and colonels alike, everyone washed in the same forgiving water. Except. Except *crimes against the state's printers' ordinances*. Except the special category being drafted even now, she would have bet the shop on it, some clerk in some ministry drawing a neat circle around Vemmen's ghost so the men who hanged him could stand safely outside it. Peace was coming, and peace was going to need scapegoats the way a press needed packing paper, and there was only one category of criminal small enough to sacrifice and useful enough to matter.

Printers' crimes. She turned the phrase over. It had a maker's mark on it somewhere; she was sure of it. Somewhere a man with good handwriting and clean cuffs had coined it, and she thought — with a coldness that startled her — that she might one day like to set his name in twelve-point and hang it where the whole harbor could read it.

The child slept against her shoulder, breathing in the quick shallow rhythm of small animals. Fever gone these six days, thank God and the midwife both. Aldis Vemmen's chin, unmistakably, in the line of the jaw. Forget the face, the note had said. As if faces could be forgotten. As if she hadn't spent five years practicing forgetting her own and failing at it every single day.

Below, the press waited. Eleven sheets' worth of forme locked in the chase, the testimony sleeping in lead. And across the border — any border, all borders — a closing door, and on the other side of it, if she could get the pages through: neutral ports, foreign presses, newspapers that loved a scandal about a falling government, lawyers who loved documents, history, which loved almost nothing but got written anyway.

Before, the deadline had been Reval. Now the deadline was diplomacy. It was almost funny. She had spent weeks printing against the gendarmerie, and now she must print against the peace itself.

* * *

Saturday she went down to the harbor because she could not bear not to.

It was a gray windless morning, the masts motionless against a sky the color of dishwater, and the waterfront wore that strained festive look of a port about to be strangled. Men clustered at the registry steps reading a posted notice. Two lighters lay loaded and idle, their crews smoking on the hatches, going nowhere. A naval launch patrolled the fairway at slow speed, its gun crew lounging but its ensign newly brightened, and Theo counted — without meaning to, the habit of a girl raised on watching — four gendarmes along the quay where normally there'd be one drunk and asleep.

At the customs shed, a clerk was chalkboarding the clearance list, and the list was short. Shorter than short. A collier bound for Copenhagen, held pending inspection. A timber ship, held. A Swedish coaster — *held*.

Only at the far end of the quay, half-hidden behind the coal stacks, did she see movement that mattered: Lotta's cousin's barge, black-hulled, taking on coal by the shovelful, its papers presumably as dull as mud. Fog didn't read lists. Neither did coal dust, which got into everything, onto everything, made everything the same anonymous black.

She was turning away when she saw the man in the civilian coat.

He stood near the registry steps, hatless despite the wind, hands clasped behind him, watching the crowd watch the notice — and even at forty paces she knew the set of those shoulders, the unhurried way he let others make room for him. Captain Reval did not walk through crowds; crowds adjusted. He was smiling slightly, she could see, though distance smoothed away whatever the smile was aimed at. Then he turned his head, following the line of the quay, past the shuttered chandlery, past the customs shed, and his gaze crossed the place where Theo stood as easily as a finger crosses a line of type, pausing on nothing, marking everything.

She walked. Did not hurry. Bought herring she did not want from a stall because a woman alone buys things, women alone with bundles are invisible, and she had built her life on that arithmetic. But her heart was setting lines of type at speed, and every character said the same thing: *he knows the harbor matters. He knows what leaves a country convicts what stayed in it.*

* * *

Marek took the news of the coaster and the patrol boat standing in the fairway more quietly than he took anything else that week. He listened, asked two questions — the barge's draft, the tide times — and then did something unexpected. He climbed to the loft and came down with the family silver, such as it was: three spoons and his mother's little salt dish, and he wrapped them in newspaper as calmly as if wrapping tools for repair.

"Lotta takes payment in metal," he said. "Paper money will be wallpaper by spring."

"We gave her the spoon already."

"Then she takes the rest as credit. She'll understand the language." He tied the string with a workman's knot and set the bundle by the door, and only then did Theo see how thin her father had gotten, how the bones of his hands stood out when he handled even this light thing. Five years of erasure, and now weeks of fear, and still he stood straight at his bench each morning and set his apron on its nail each night as if the world were a workshop that would go on needing him.

"Papa." She used the old word deliberately, the one permitted since childhood, before *son*, before everything. "If the signing comes before the last sheets are through—"

"Then we will have lost a race we should never have been allowed to enter." He sat down heavily on the composing stone's edge, and for a moment the craftsman fell away and she saw the man underneath: tired, widowed twice over — once by the grave, once by his own scissors — holding a future he had never been trained to cut. "Your mother used to say the powerful count on people getting tired. That exhaustion is their best policeman. Cheaper than gendarmes. Never strikes, never mutinies." His mouth bent, not quite a smile. "I am very tired, Theo. I have been tired for twenty years. But I find I am not tired enough."

* * *

They worked that night as they had not worked since the beginning — not furtively but furiously, the treadle keeping time like a heart pushed past caution. The press noise seemed smaller now, or they had grown larger around it. Marek fed sheets; she pulled the lever; between pulls they talked in the shorthand of shops, *tighten the ribs, there's ghosting on the left margin,* and beneath the shorthand the other thing ran silent as a watermark: *eleven became nine, nine became seven.*

Somewhere past two in the morning the church bells began.

Not the hour — no hour had that many voices. All the bells, every tower in the old town, breaking out together into the cold air, ragged at first, then finding one another, and beneath them a rising human sound from the direction of the great square: cheering, horns, a rifle shot that might have been celebration or might not, singing — the old song, the banned one, sung badly and joyfully by hundreds of ruined throats.

Marek stopped the press. Ink on their fingers, proof-dust in their hair, they stood in the doorway and listened to a city celebrate its own forgiveness.

"They've signed early," Theo said. The words tasted strange, metallic, like biting foil.

"Or agreed to agree." But his voice had gone flat, and she knew he believed it, and so did she. Somewhere across the rooftops men in good coats were signing their names — men whose names she could recite, having set them in testimony type — and the bells were washing the streets clean of memory, and the circle around *printers' crimes* was drying even now in some ministry ledger, neat as a rule drawn under a sum.

Peace. She had imagined wanting it — had assumed, distantly, that she would want it the way everyone wanted it, the way the drowning want shore. Instead she stood in her doorway listening to the bells toll the closing of doors, and felt the cruelest clarity of her life settle over her like the harbor fog coming up the street: that peace was arriving not as an opening but as a lid. That the world was about to be forgiven, all but the ones who remembered why it needed forgiving.

The child woke upstairs at the noise and began to cry, thin and furious, a small voice refusing the celebration on principle. Theo almost laughed.

"Six sheets," she said.

Marek looked at the press, then at the sky going gray at its eastern seam, then at her. "Six sheets," he agreed. "And then the fog."

He turned back to the treadle. But at the door of the workroom he paused, and without turning around said, in a voice she had never heard him use, quiet and urgent and young:

"And Theo — if it comes to running, you take her and the punches and you don't wait for me. Promise me the way I taught you to promise: once, plainly, meaning it."

Above them the bells went on ringing, and downstairs the baby cried, and out on the black water the fog was already gathering, patient as paper, waiting to be read.
