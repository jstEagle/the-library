## 17. The Locked Room Opens

Marek Nystrom had kept the key on a cord around his neck for five years, and for five years it had pressed a small cold rectangle into his sternum each night when he lay down. He had grown so used to its weight that he sometimes forgot it was there, the way a man forgets a scar, or a debt, or the name of someone he has failed. Tonight he did not forget. Tonight he stood in the doorway of his bedroom with the cord in his fist and listened to his daughter sing to Vemmen's child in the room below, a low, tuneless humming that was not a song at all but a sound she made when she was frightened and did not want the baby to know it.

He had told himself for years that the locked room was Vemmen's secret. That was a lie, and he had known it was a lie from the first night, and he had kept it anyway, because lies kept in the dark keep their shape. He was sixty-one years old. His hands had cut more letters than he could count, letters that had gone into hymnals and customs manifests and almanacs of tides, and he had told himself that a man who made letters could not be blamed for what was spelled with them. That lie had kept him alive through the strikes, through the hangings, through the year they took the Lindeqvist brothers from the shop two doors down and returned them in boxes. He had survived by being a man who made letters and never used them.

And now the gendarmes were two streets away in their imaginations and one signature away in their reality, and his daughter was printing sedition at his treadle every night with a baby strapped to her back, and the lie had finally outlived its usefulness.

He went down the stairs the way he always did, one hand on the rail, and stood in the doorway of the workroom watching Theo rock the child. She had cut her hair short again that week, the way she did when it grew long enough to be noticed, and in the lamplight her jaw looked like his jaw, her hands like his hands. He had spent five years teaching the city not to see her. He had never once managed to stop seeing her himself.

"Theo."

She looked up. She had her mother's way of going still when she was wary — not freezing but *settling*, like water finding its level. "The forme's cleaned. I greased the screw."

"I know. I checked." He turned the cord over in his fingers. The key clicked against the button of his waistcoat. "There's something you should see. In the back room."

She did not ask what. He watched her decide not to ask, watched her hand the baby to the crook of her own arm and rise, and he understood that she had known about the locked room her whole life the way children know about the silences of their parents — not its contents, but its existence, its weight, the way the house leaned around it.

* * *

The back room had been locked since before she could remember. Marek unlocked it now, and the key turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of tallow and cold iron and, beneath it, faintly, of the linseed oil they used to preserve steel.

He lit the lamp on the shelf just inside. Theo came in behind him and stopped.

The room was small, no wider than a coffin stood on end, and it was not empty. It had never been empty. There was a workbench along one wall, and on the workbench, laid out with the care of a surgeon's instruments, were steel punches — dozens of them, scores of them, each one a rectangle of hardened steel no longer than a thumb, each one bearing at its tip a letter cut in mirror, each one polished to a dark gleam by years of handling. Beside them lay the gravers, the files, the smoke proofs on thin paper, the little lead casts called strikes that a cutter pulls to test each letter's fit.

Theo crossed the room as if the floor might be rigged. She set the lamp down and did not touch anything at first. She only looked, and her looking had the quality he knew from her work at the case — the total absorption, the world narrowing to the thing before her.

"These aren't mine," Marek said. "You know whose they are."

"Aldis Vemmen's." Her voice was flat with concentration. Then, quieter: "These are constitution letters."

He had not heard anyone say it aloud in eleven years. *The constitution letters.* The typeface cut in secret in the winter of the first suppression, when the drafters of the free charter had needed a face that existed in no government registry, that could not be matched to any licensed foundry, that would let a printing of the constitution be set and struck and scattered in a single night and leave the censors nothing to trace. Vemmen had come to him — Marek — not to print it, but to hide it, because Vemmen had understood what Marek had spent a lifetime teaching himself: that the safest place for a dangerous thing is inside a quiet man.

"He came to me in the February of the suppression," Marek said. His own voice sounded strange to him, rusty, like a press long idle. "He brought them in a fish crate. Two hundred and six punches. Every letter, every figure, every ligature. He'd cut them over four winters, working nights, and he said — " Marek stopped. His throat closed on the words and he had to work them loose like a knot. "He said he'd cut them for a country that didn't exist yet, and that someone had to keep them alive until it did."

Theo had picked up one of the punches now. She held it the way she held everything — competently, without ceremony — but he saw her thumb find the bevel of the letter, the counter, the way the serif flared, and he saw her understand, in her hands before her mind, that this was work of a different order than anything either of them had ever done. This was not a letter for a hymnal. This was a letter that had been *outlawed*.

"Why you?" she asked. "Why not the Lindeqvists, or the—"

"Because I was nobody." He said it without bitterness, or trying to. "Because I printed tide tables. Because no gendarme who ever walked through that shop saw anything but a tired man and his son and a press that smelled of boredom. Vemmen was loud, Theo. He was brave, and loud, and brave loud men die first. He knew it. He chose it. But he wanted the letters to outlive the choice." He looked at his own hands, at the ink in the creases that would never come out. "So he gave them to a coward and asked him to keep them safe."

"You weren't a coward."

"I was exactly a coward." He said it steadily, because it was the one thing he had never said to anyone, and saying it felt like setting a line of type — each word placed, justified, locked. "I was a coward for five years with these in the next room. I was a coward the night they hanged him and I stood in the square with my cap in my hands and my mouth shut. I was a coward every time you asked me about the locked room and I told you it was nothing. I kept his letters alive and I let him die alone, and I let them hang him for pamphlets that were *lesser* than what he'd hidden in my house, because I would not set them. That is what your father is, Theo. Now you know. Now you have it with the rest of your inheritance."

The baby stirred against her shoulder. Theo did not look at him. She was turning the punch in the lamplight, and when she spoke her voice was very quiet.

"You cut your own letters. Your whole life. You cut the Nystrom text face, the one the harbor office uses. You're as good as he was."

"I'm better." It was not vanity. It was arithmetic. "Vemmen cut fast and true. I cut slow and truer. That's why he chose me — not because I was safe, but because I was *precise*. He said the letters had to survive a man who would never be brave enough to use them, so they had to be perfect enough to outlast cowardice." He laughed, a short, terrible sound. "He said that to my face. In this room. And I shook his hand."

Theo set the punch down among its brothers, gently, the way one lays a sleeping child in a cradle. Then she turned and looked at him, and Marek saw in her face — for the first time in five years, fully, without the disguise, without the boy's haircut and the lowered eyes and the silence — his daughter. His daughter looking at her father.

"The forme downstairs," she said. "The testimony. It's set in your text face."

"Yes."

"Set it again." Her eyes went to the workbench, to the two hundred and six small dark gleams. "Set it in his. Set the whole testimony in the constitution letters. Let them hang him for the pamphlets and let the pamphlets be nothing. Let the men in the testimony read their own names in a face that was cut for a free country." Her voice did not rise. It *sharpened*, the way a graver sharpens, taking away everything that is not the edge. "Let them see it and know it was never destroyed. Eleven years, and it was never destroyed."

Marek stood in the little room that smelled of tallow and iron and his own long silence, and he felt the key on its cord against his chest, and he understood that he was holding the last lie of his life and that it had just been taken from him — not roughly, not with accusation, but the way she took everything, competently, without ceremony.

"There's something else," he said.

* * *

He knelt at the workbench because his knees were old, and lifted the false bottom of the drawer Vemmen had built into it, and took out the oilcloth bundle, and unwrapped it on the wood with the care of a man handling a relic, which is what it was.

Inside lay the smoke proofs. The original ones. Vemmen's own hand — the first pulls of every letter, struck from the punches the day each was finished, dated in Vemmen's small slanted script. And beneath them, folded into eighths, gone soft as cloth at the creases, a single printed sheet.

Theo took it and opened it, and read, and Marek watched her read the first page of the constitution that had never been ratified — the preamble, set in the forbidden face, struck in a single night eleven years ago, one of perhaps two hundred copies, all but this one hunted and burned. The ink had faded to the brown of old blood. The paper was rag paper, good paper, paper meant to last.

"He printed one extra," Marek said. "For the man keeping the punches. He said every letter needs a body somewhere, or it's only a ghost." He swallowed. "I never told you. I never told anyone. I kept it the way I keep everything, and keeping is not the same as—"

"I know what keeping is." Theo folded the sheet along its soft old creases. "You taught me."

Outside, the harbor clock began to strike the hour — nine strokes, muffled by fog, the sound the city made when it was counting down to something. Marek thought of the armistice delegates in their warm rooms, of the amnesty lists that would not carry the word *printer*, of Captain Reval's soft voice asking after the materials of the deceased, and he felt the fear he had lived inside for five years shift its weight, the way a ship shifts when the ballast is moved — not lighter, only differently balanced, and for the first time pointed somewhere instead of merely holding.

"The punches go with us," Theo said. It was not a question. She was already wrapping them, letter by letter, into the oilcloth, her hands moving with the economy he had taught her, each punch nested against its fellows so the steel edges would not touch and mar. "All of them. Whatever happens."

"All of them," he agreed.

"And the smoke proofs. And the sheet."

"Yes."

She worked in silence, and he worked beside her, handing her punches in the old alphabet order — A to her, B, C — the way they had sorted type ten thousand evenings, and the silence between them was not the old silence, the silence of things unsaid. It was the other kind. The kind a press makes between pulls: the brief held breath before the platen comes down and the impression is made.

When the last punch was wrapped and the bundle tied with cord, Theo stood holding it against her chest, and the baby slept against her other side, and the lamp threw their two shadows up the wall until they seemed one figure.

"Papa," she said. She had not called him that in five years. She had called him Father, or nothing, or the wordless sounds of the shop. "Why tonight? Why now?"

Marek looked at the locked room, at the empty drawer with its false bottom, at the lamp burning down.

"Because Reval asked me for the materials of the deceased," he said, "and I lied to his face, and it was the first lie I ever told that I wasn't ashamed of. And I understood, standing there, that I had been keeping these letters safe from the world for eleven years, and that the world had gotten no safer, and that it never would, and that safe was never what Vemmen asked me for." He took up the lamp. "He asked me to keep them *alive*. Alive means used. Come downstairs, Theo. We have a forme to reset."

They went out together and he locked the door behind them out of habit, and then, standing in the dark of the passage with the key in his hand, he unlocked it again and left it standing open.

* * *

Below, the workroom waited with its smell of ink and its single lamp and the press standing in the corner like a patient animal. Theo set the oilcloth bundle on the composing stone and unwrapped it there, in the working light, among the working tools, and began to lay the punches out in order — A, B, C, the whole forbidden alphabet gleaming in the lamplight like a fleet at anchor — while Marek banked the fire and brought the matrix box and the casting metal, because a punch is only a beginning: letters must be struck into copper, matrices cast, type founded, and the night was long and the armistice was close and the borders were closing like a hand.

He was melting the first ladle of metal when Theo spoke, without looking up from the stone.

"The testimony," she said. "In his face. And under it — the imprint."

Marek waited.

"Set it as: *Printed by Nystrom and Vemmen.*" She looked at him then, across the glowing metal and the forbidden letters, and her face was calm and absolutely certain. "Both names. His and ours. Let them come for all of us."

Marek held the ladle steady over the matrix box, and his hand did not shake, and he thought: *this is what I made instead of a son. I made this.*

"Both names," he said, and poured.
