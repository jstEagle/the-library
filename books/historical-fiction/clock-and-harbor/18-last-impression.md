## 18. Last Impression

The last sheets were still wet when the church bells began.

Not the hour — the bells had gone strange weeks ago, rung at odd intervals by men who wanted the city to feel time slipping. Theo lifted the sheet from the tympan with two fingers, held it to the candle flame's light without touching it to the light, and read the colophon one more time. *Printed by Nystrom and Vemmen.* The two names sat side by side in the forbidden face, the letters her father had cut from Vemmen's punches, and they looked, in the wet ink, like two people standing shoulder to shoulder before a firing squad.

Four hundred and six sheets. She counted them again, because counting was a thing hands could do when the heart had no further instructions. Four hundred and six, stacked in quires of twenty, wrapped in brown paper, wrapped again in oilcloth. One copy of everything — she kept one of each page loose, unbound, tucked into the false back of her father's type cabinet, because if the rest burned she wanted the truth to have a body somewhere, even a paper one.

Outside, the sky over the harbor was going from black to the color of dirty pewter. Dawn came late now, or early, depending on how you counted; the city had never agreed on it, and lately neither had the seasons.

Marek stood at the window with the shutter opened one finger's width. He had not slept. Neither of them had, but he wore it differently — she went thin and quick with exhaustion, a wire drawn taut, while he seemed only to grow heavier, denser, as if the night were being pressed into him like metal into a matrix.

"Two streets," he said.

She heard nothing. Then she did: boots. Not marching — gendarmes marched when they wanted to be heard, and these were past caring who heard them, which was worse. A casual rhythm, unhurried, the walk of men doing their rounds, and that meant the round included this street. It meant Reval had not needed to hurry.

"How many streets is two?" she asked, and hated the childishness of it, and did not take it back.

"Fifteen minutes if they're lazy. Five if someone pointed."

Theo began moving before he finished. There is a kind of work that is faster than thought, and she had been raised inside it: hands knowing where the quoins sat, where the wrench lived, how the forme came free of the chase with two turns and a lift. She had burned type before — every printer had, bad casts, spoiled settings — but this was different. This forme was four months of Vemmen's last composition, testimony gathered in a condemned man's cell or smuggled out by hands she would never learn, naming nine officers and the orders they gave and the dates the strikers died in the shipyard gate, and it had taken her eleven nights to print it, and now she was going to feed it to the stove.

"Theo." Marek's voice, low.

"I know."

"It has to be the firepot. Not the stove — the stove leaves letters. Lead melts but it doesn't lie."

She knew. He was telling her anyway because that was what he did now, what he had learned too late to do when she was young: he said things aloud so that she would know he knew them too, so that the burden would be witnessed rather than merely carried. She took up the iron poker and worked the coals in the little firepot they used for soldering until they glowed white at their centers, and then she lifted the forme — heavy, oh, heavier than any child, a full forme of long primer in a wooden chase — and set it face-down into the coals.

The smell was immediate and terrible. Ink burning off hot lead, resin and smoke, the smell of every lie ever told neatly in reverse. The type began to give up its shape slowly, letter by letter, the softening metal slumping into itself like snow sliding off a roof. She watched the R go first. Then the E. She made herself watch all of it, because there was an arithmetic here she refused to cheat: the words existed in four hundred and seven places now, and this one place she could surrender, and watching it die was part of the price.

*Printed by Nystrom and Vemmen.* That line she had added herself, in the new-old face, the forbidden constitution cuts. That line would remain in four hundred and six copies scattered across the holds of ships bound for ports where names were only names. But here, in this room, the sentence was ending.

Her father put his hand on her shoulder. Just rested it there. Neither of them spoke, and it was the right silence, the good silence, the kind she had spent five years learning to tell apart from all the others.

* * *

They worked after that with the economy of people who have rehearsed disaster so many times it has become almost domestic.

Marek took the melted slug of metal — a shapeless gray tongue now, nothing legible, nothing provable — and buried it in the ash bin under a week of ordinary sweepings. He damped the stove ashes so no spark would show. He swept the stone where ink had spilled and rubbed the stains down with pumice dust until the floor told no stories.

Theo packed.

The punches came out of the locked room for the last time. Her father had shown them to her three nights ago, laid out on velvet gone stiff with age: the complete series, every character of a face that had never legally existed, cut for the suppressed constitution drafts and hidden twice over — once by Vemmen, once by Marek, once by the wall itself. She wrapped them now in flannel, in oilcloth, in sailcloth left over from the bundle that had come against the door nearly a season ago, and she packed them into a small cedar box that had once held her mother's gloves. It seemed right. It seemed like something the woman would have understood, though Theo had no way of knowing and no time to wonder.

The baby slept in the cradle Marek had built from packing crates, breathing in the shallow, absolute way of infants, one fist curled against her cheek. Theo dressed her without waking her — this was a skill now, one of dozens she had acquired without ever intending a life that required them — swaddled her tight, fed her a few spoonfuls of the last of the milk thickened with bread, and strapped her against her chest with the wide canvas sling. The child's head lolled into the hollow of Theo's collarbone, and a fist found the edge of Theo's shirt and gripped.

Above the workbench hung the type cabinet, and in the cabinet's false back lay the single loose copy of every page. Theo took those out too. Twenty-two pages. She folded them into a packet the size of a prayer book and sewed it into the lining of her coat with waxed thread, working by touch mostly, the needle finding its holes, and she thought, absurdly, of her mother, whom she did not remember, who had perhaps also sewn things in secret, letters or hemlines or hopes. Some inheritances skip a generation and land anyway.

"What about the press?" she asked.

Marek looked at the old Albion the way a man looks at his own reflection in dark water. "It stays."

"They'll destroy it."

"Yes."

"And you?"

He crossed the room and took her chin in his ink-stained hand, tilted her face up the way he had when she was six and learning to hold a brayer, correcting her grip, and he studied her as if committing her to memory for some future setting.

"I stay until you're around the corner," he said. "Then I follow, slowly, by the long way, like a man going to buy tobacco."

"That's not what Lotta arranged."

"Lotta arranged a boat. Boats don't care who walks to them." He let go of her chin. "If they find three of us, they take three of us. If they find one old printer sitting among clean ashes, they take one old printer's story, and my story is boring, Theo. I've spent thirty years making it boring. Let me spend it now."

She wanted to argue. She catalogued the arguments quickly — she was good at arguing with him lately, they had five years of unsaid things finally saying themselves — and she found them all true and all useless, because underneath every version was the same fact: the baby, the pages, the punches, the boat. Heavier than argument. Heavier than the forme had been.

"If they take you—" she started.

"Then I'll have printed the most important thing I ever set," he said, "and I'll have a daughter who can cut a letter truer than any apprentice in this city, whatever name she goes by." He picked up the cedar box and put it into her arms beside the sleeping child. "Go be seen."

* * *

The fog was coming off the harbor in slow rollers, swallowing the masts first, then the warehouses, then the lamplight, and Theo moved through it along the wall with the child warm against her and the box under her arm and her father's footsteps fading behind her — unhurried, deliberate, a man buying tobacco.

Two streets away, the boots had stopped. She heard a door kicked, somewhere, and shouting that the fog softened into something almost gentle, and she kept walking, because the work was finished, and finished work asks only one thing of the living: carry it.

At the corner by the chandlery she stopped, once, and looked back. The shop's windows were dark and blank, the shutters closed, the whole building sitting quietly among its neighbors like every other building, keeping its silences, and inside it her father was standing very straight in the middle of the empty room, she knew it without seeing, straight the way he stood at the stone when the metal poured true.

She turned the corner.

Behind her, faint through the fog, came the sound of the gendarmes' boots turning onto their street — and, beneath it, already beginning, thin and orange against the harbor mist, a glow that was not dawn.
