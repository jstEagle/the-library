## 19. Fire

The gendarmes came at the hour when the harbor lamps still burned but had stopped meaning anything—when the fog had thickened past the point of lamplight and become merely a smear of amber in a grey world. Theo saw them from the harbor wall, where Lotta had told her to stand and not move, and she counted them the way her father had taught her to count type: quickly, by weight, without naming each one.

Fourteen men. Two carrying the long iron bar they used on stubborn doors.

She could not see the shop from here. That was the cruelty of the position Lotta had chosen for her—the coal barge lay at the foot of the wall, low in the black water, its hold open like a mouth waiting to be fed—but the shop itself was hidden behind the customs house, so that Theo was asked to watch the destruction of everything she loved by proxy: by smoke, by sound, by the faces of the men who went to do it and did not come back quickly enough.

The baby was wrapped against her chest under her coat, warm as a second heartbeat, asleep or pretending to be. Marek's punches rode against her hip in the oilcloth packet, hard little edges pressing into her body with every breath, a bruise she had chosen. The testimony—one copy of everything, four hundred sheets pulled over nine nights, ink still sharp enough to stain a careless thumb—lay in the barge's hold between layers of coal dust, disguised as ballast.

And Marek was walking toward fourteen gendarmes alone.

* * *

He had made it simple, in the way he made everything simple once he had decided it, which was the only way he had ever said love aloud. *They will look for a man leaving a burning print shop*, he had said last night, his hands flat on the forme of type they were about to destroy, not looking at her because he never looked at her when he said things that mattered. *A woman with a child slips through fog like water through a net. A man my age has no other use but to be seen.*

She had argued. She wanted, even now, standing on the cold stone with the fog beading in her eyelashes, to believe she had argued well. But he had only done what he always did when she was right and it changed nothing: he had begun cleaning the press.

*You will keep the punches*, he had said. *Not because I give them to you. Because you already took them. I watched you do it Tuesday and said nothing.* And then, quieter, in the voice he used for the names of the dead: *Your mother would have liked you very much, I think. I am sorry I made it necessary for you to wonder.*

Now she stood on the wall and watched him walk into the fog toward his own arrest, and she understood that he had been saying goodbye for five years—that every lesson in silence had also been a farewell, rehearsed nightly, so that this morning he could perform it without trembling. He had cut away everything flammable about himself long ago. There was very little left to burn.

* * *

She heard the door go before she saw anything. Even at this distance, across the drowned lanes of the old town, the iron bar against the shop's oak frame made a sound like a rib cracking—a sound she felt in her own back, in the place where she carried the child. Then voices, mangled by fog into something without words, and the ordinary sounds of a search conducted by men who expected to find something: drawers run out and upended, the wooden clatter of a type case dumped to the floor, lead letters scattering like dropped coins.

Theo counted her breathing. Four in, four out. The rhythm of the treadle. She had learned years ago that terror and printing used the same muscles if you disciplined them properly.

The baby stirred. Without thinking, without taking her eyes off the grey wall of fog where the shop must be, she began to hum—the tuneless thing she made up at night to lull him, half a lullaby, half the cadence of the press itself, *ka-chunk, ka-hiss*, the sound he had slept inside since before he could focus his eyes. He settled. Her milk let down, absurdly, painfully, at the wrong hour. The body kept its own accounts no matter what the mind was doing.

In the forme she had burned at dawn there had been Vemmen's name set in eight-point roman, and beneath it the names of five officers, and beneath those, in the small careful italics her father had cut for emphasis forty years ago and no one else in the city possessed: the dates. Eleven dates. Eleven men from the shipyard who had walked to the courtyard wall in pairs and come back as none.

She had stood over the kitchen stove breaking that forme apart, letter by letter, distribution being the printer's final mercy to his work—you unmake the words so they cannot testify against you—and she had wept over each *d*, each *a*, each common little *e*, until Marek put his hand over hers and finished the job, because grief slows the hands and the gendarmes did not slow theirs.

The lead had gone back into the melting pot. The words existed now only in four hundred sheets of paper and in her memory, and memory, her father had taught her, was the one forme no raid could distribute.

* * *

She saw the first smoke at a quarter past the hour—not rising yet, just lying along the lane like something spilled, a grey-brown thread unspooling above the roofline of the customs house. Then a window bloomed orange somewhere behind it, briefly, like a coal struck in a dark room, and the thread of smoke thickened and began to rise.

Her knees nearly went. She locked them. Lotta's instruction had been absolute: *stand, don't run, don't pray out loud, don't give the fog any reason to notice you*. The coal barge would leave when the fire drew the watch and the patrols to the north lanes, and not before, and if Theo was not on the gangplank when the skipper cast off, Lotta would not wait, because waiting was how smugglers died and Lotta had survived thirty years by loving no one enough to die for them.

But it was too early. The fire had started too early. Which meant—

Which meant Marek had already given them the excuse. She worked it through with the cold part of her mind, the sorting-type part, and the answer assembled itself line by line the way a page assembled in the chase, upside down and backward until you knew how to read it.

They had found nothing. Ash where the forme should have been—the hearth still warm, the melted lead cooling in its ingot molds, an empty cradle frame stripped of linens and rocking gently when one of them brushed it, the press clean, the cases lawful, the almanac order sitting openly on the bench as if awaiting delivery. Nothing seditious. Nothing to charge except disappointment. And Reval, denied his evidence, denied even the theater of a seizure, would take the only property left to punish: the building itself, and whatever man remained inside it.

A lamp had fallen. Or been fallen. It did not matter which, and she understood with a clarity that felt like the first pull of a fresh sheet—crisp, terrible, true—that it had been meant not to matter. Marek had left the shop ready to burn. Had perhaps left it *arranged* to burn, oil where oil belonged to be spilled, so that the choice Reval believed he was making had been written for him days in advance, set in lead, proofed, and locked in the chase. Her father's last composition: a scene in which the gendarmerie destroyed their own credibility, and a printer was nowhere to be found.

Some prices are paid, she thought, watching the smoke darken and stand up straight against the pale sky, so that others don't have to be.

* * *

The gendarmes emerged from the fog in twos and threes, falling back from the lane the way water falls back from a rising tide, and among the last pair she saw him—Reval, unmistakable even at three hundred paces, even rendered in fog, because he walked the way he had walked through her father's shop two weeks ago: slowly, hands clasped behind him, head tilted as though reading a room. He stopped at the edge of the lane. He considered the smoke.

Then he turned his head, and though it was impossible—it was fog and distance and lamplight, it was nothing—Theo felt his gaze travel the harbor wall, the mooring posts, the black water, and pause on the exact stretch of stone where she stood.

She did not move. Four in, four out. The baby's breath against her sternum, small and even. Somewhere below her, in the hold of a coal barge, four hundred sheets of testimony waited with her name nowhere on them but Vemmen's handwriting everywhere, and Reval's at the top of the first page, in the place where an author signs.

Reval raised one hand. A gesture she could not parse at this distance—an order, a wave, a salute—and the gendarmes around him moved with new purpose, and a moment later the smoke coming from the lane doubled, tripled, went from grey to brown to a black that meant the fire had found the paper store, the stock room, the chest of rags soaked in turpentine, twenty years of her family's work becoming light and heat for no one's benefit.

He had ordered it anyway. She had known he would, and still some part of her—the daughter, the apprentice, the girl who had slept in the loft above the press through fever nights listening to her father work late because the sound of the treadle was the sound of being safe—some part of her had hoped the law, even this law, kept some small covenant with meaning. You burn what incriminates. Not what doesn't.

But punishment had never been about evidence. Punishment was a language, and Reval was fluent, and the sentence he was pronouncing on the empty shop said clearly to anyone in the old quarter who could read it: *this is what a house costs when it holds nothing we can prove*. Every neighbor peering from a shuttered window would translate it correctly. Fear needed no paper, no press, no distribution network. Fear printed itself.

Let them read it, then, she thought, and was startled by the ferocity of her own thought. Let them read it and know what it cost. Let the smoke carry the testimony the fire couldn't find.

* * *

She saw Marek then, or believed she did—a figure between two gendarmes at the far end of the lane, hatless, unhurried, held not roughly but in the firm ceremonial grip of men who had been given a prop for their play. He was not struggling. Of course he was not struggling. At the mouth of the lane he stopped, turned his head, and looked back once—at the fire, she was almost sure, only at the fire, checking his composition, confirming the burn—and then the fog folded over the three of them and they were gone.

Theo's hand found the oilcloth packet at her hip and pressed down on the hard small shapes within, punch after punch after punch, her father's mother-work, the alphabet of the country that had never been allowed to exist. *You will keep the punches. Not because I give them to you. Because you already took them.*

"I have them," she said aloud, and her breath made a ghost of it, and the ghost went up into the fog with the smoke.

* * *

The fire bells began at half past the hour, distant and wrong-footed, ringing in districts that would arrive too late because the requisitions had taken their horses and the war had taken their men. The watch would come. They would stand in the lane with their useless leathers and their hollow cisterns and they would save the customs house and lose the print shop, and everyone would agree afterward that nothing could be done, which was true, and that nothing could have been done earlier, which was the lie.

Below her, on the barge, a lantern swung twice—Lotta's signal, once for wait, twice for come now—and Theo took one last inventory of the stone under her feet, the harbor wall her grandfather might have helped lay, the water that had carried every ship her father's labels had ever sailed on, the whole small geography of a life she was leaving in flames behind the customs house.

She should feel more destroyed than she did. That was the strangest thing. Grief was there—grief was enormous, a hold full of wet coal, ballast she would carry for the rest of her life—but threaded through it ran something harder and cleaner, and she recognized it because she had felt it only at the press: the certainty of a completed impression. The sheet pulled. The image transferred, whole, irreversible, whether or not anyone was ready to read it.

Marek had paid so that she would not have to. Joss had paid, wherever he was, in a cell with his wrists ruined and his mouth—she prayed—still shut. Vemmen had paid on the gallows with his face turned toward the crowd so that someone would remember it. The ledger of this revolution was written in exactly one currency, and she had finally learned to read the columns, and she was not too proud to admit what it taught: that survival and justice were not opposites, had never been opposites, were the same word spoken in two different languages, and both of them were hers now to carry.

She descended the iron ladder rung by rung, the baby's weight shifting against her chest, the fog closing warm and salt above her like a hand. At the bottom, Lotta stood on the gangplank with the lantern, saying nothing, offering her arm, and Theo took it because her legs had discovered what her mind had refused all morning: that she was made of exhausted human material and nothing more.

"Look at me and not the fire," Lotta said quietly. "The fire wants witnesses. We are not its witnesses anymore."

The hold swallowed them—coal smell, tarpaulin, the dark that smelled of her father's workshop and nothing else, thank God, thank the ash and the empty cradle and the melted lead, nothing of them was in here to burn—and the skipper cast off without a word, and the wall of the harbor slid away into the fog.

Behind them, above the roofs, the glow of the shop rose and spread, staining the fog the color of the ink she would never mix again in that room, and Theo sat with her back against the coal and the baby against her heart and the punches against her hip and the testimony under her hands, and she watched the light through a gap in the tarpaulin until the barge reached the channel and the current took them, and the fog closed over the fire like a page turning.

Ahead, out on the black water, the sea waited with the second note sewn into the sailcloth bundle—she had never dared read it whole, and her fingers found its fold through the fabric as if it were another kind of punch, another inheritance pressed into lead.

When the shore lights were gone, she would open it.
