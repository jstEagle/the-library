## 20. Fog on the Water

The fog came off the sea an hour before dawn, and Theo learned later that Lotta had been praying for it since Tuesday.

She stood at the harbor wall with the child strapped against her chest and the satchel of punches digging into her hip, and she could not see the water at all. She could hear it — that slow slap of hulls against pilings, the groan of rope — and she could smell it: coal smoke and tar and herring brine, all of it thickened by the fog into something she could almost chew. Behind her, three streets back and rising, the sky had begun to turn the color of a forge.

That was her father's doing. That was the shop burning, or beginning to.

*Don't look*, she told herself. *You already know what it looks like.*

She looked anyway. Everyone on the quay did; there was no pretending otherwise. The glow sat above the rooftops like a second sunrise arriving in the wrong direction, orange bleeding up through the fog's grey belly until the whole southern sky seemed bruised with light. Somewhere in that light was the press Marek had bought before she was born. The floorboards she'd slept under as a girl during the fever winter. The type cases she knew by touch better than by sight, every compartment a letter, every letter a small weight she had carried in her hands for twenty years.

Gone, or going. And Marek inside it, or near it, standing in the street in his good coat with his hands folded, performing delay the way he had once performed deference — slowly, politely, asking gendarmes to mind where they stepped, insisting on inventories, on signatures, on procedure. A man who had spent five years teaching his daughter to be invisible had made himself the most visible thing in the district, because visibility was the only gift he had left to give her.

"Stop counting," Lotta said beside her. "I can hear you counting."

"I'm not—"

"You're counting everything you lost. I can hear it. It sounds like arithmetic." Lotta pulled her cap lower over her eyes and took Theo's elbow with a grip like a cleat. "Walk. Don't run. Running people look like thieves, and thieves get stopped, and stopped people get asked questions, and tonight I don't answer questions for anyone but myself."

They moved along the quay through a crowd that had gathered the way crowds do at firelight, faces tilted upward, mouths open, everyone warm with someone else's disaster. Nobody looked at two women walking briskly with a bundle. In a city eating itself, a woman carrying a baby was the least interesting thing on the street, and Theo clung to that anonymity like a rail in high wind.

The child stirred against her chest and made a small sound — not a cry, not yet, a sound like a question. Theo pressed her lips to the sailcloth wrapping, tasting salt and ash.

"Not now," she whispered. "Hush now. Everything is exactly as terrible as it seems, so there is nothing to announce."

* * *

Lotta's barge lay third from the coal ramp, low in the water, blacker than the night around it. Her brother — Theo had not known there was a brother until this moment, which was Lotta's way; the woman was a ledger with legs, and she never showed a page early — stood at the gangplank holding a lantern shuttered down to a coin of light.

"Cargo?" he asked.

"Wet coal and two passengers who were never here," Lotta said. "Same as always."

He swung the lantern once toward Theo, toward the bundle at her chest, and something passed across his face — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition of cost. He'd seen what happened to couriers this month. Everyone who worked the docks had seen, or heard, or knew a man who knew. Joss's name had gone around the waterfront the way names do when they're attached to silence: spoken once, then everywhere, then nowhere.

Theo had not let herself think of Joss in six hours. She thought of him now, briefly, deliberately, the way she pressed a proof to check the ink — one clean impression, then cover the plate again. His laugh, too loud for whisper-work. The way he'd tucked pages into manifests like a man dealing cards he knew were marked. The last time she'd seen him he'd waved from the end of the pier without turning around, cocky as a boy, and she had watched him go and felt — she searched for the word and found only the absence of one.

His fate remained a closed case in her chest. No verdict. Just the docket, open forever.

"Down," Lotta said, and pushed her gently toward the hold ladder.

The hold smelled of coal dust and old bilgewater, and it was dark the way a grave is dark, except for the seams of fog-light leaking through the hatch above. There was a nest of sacking behind the coal bunkers that someone had prepared — blankets, a crate turned on its side to make a windbreak, a clay jug of water. Someone had thought about a baby. Lotta had thought about a baby, weeks ago probably, while making lists in her head and telling no one, and Theo felt her throat close on a gratitude too large to speak.

"Lotta."

"Don't." Lotta crouched at the edge of the lamplight, her face all angles and shadow. "Don't thank me yet. We're not clear of the breakwater. The patrol boat at the mouth has been boarding everything heavier than a rowboat since the armistice was signed — checking papers, checking cargo, checking men's hands for ink." She said that last flatly, but her eyes stayed on Theo a beat too long. "If we're boarded, you're my cousin's widow from Kalmund, come to work the herring season. You've got a paper in the sacking that says so. You don't talk unless they ask. When they ask, you bore them."

"And if they look in the satchel?"

Lotta was quiet a moment. Below them, in the hull, the water spoke in its patient voice.

"Then you tell them it's type for a printer in Halsing," she said. "Which is true, as far as it goes. The punches are just metal, girl. Metal doesn't testify. Paper testifies, and the paper isn't on this boat."

It wasn't. It was in four places by now — sewn into Lotta's coat lining, rolled inside a shipmate's sea boots, folded into a bible belonging to a widow Theo had never met, and — the largest share — already out beyond the breakwater Wednesday last, hidden in a barrel of pickled sprats bound for a neutral port, if the gods of freight were kind. Reval's name headed every page. Reval's signature, transcribed in Vemmen's careful hand from documents Theo had found and photographed into memory and reset letter by letter in the small hours while her city slept and her father pretended not to hear the treadle. Reval first, then the colonel, then the captain whose name still tasted like iron filings, then the orders, the dates, the numbers of men shot in the shipyard yard with their hands tied to crane hooks.

Escape without victory, she thought. That's what this is. We are leaving, and leaving is not winning.

But the testimony was loose in the world now, like ink dropped in water, and ink does not come back.

"Sleep if you can," Lotta said, rising. "The fog will hold till mid-morning. After that we're just a boat."

She climbed the ladder and the hatch slid shut and Theo was alone with the child and the dark and the distant orange glow filtering even here, faintly, through everything.

* * *

The baby would not sleep.

Perhaps it was the smoke smell, which had followed them onto the water and clung to their clothes and hair. Perhaps children taste grief in milk, the way sailors taste weather in their teeth. Theo unwrapped the sailcloth in the dimness and held her up — her, always her, though Theo had never once said her name aloud, because a name spoken is a name known — and studied her face by the seam-light. Furious blue eyes. A fist escaping the blanket with the determination of a compositor reaching for a sort.

"You are," Theo told her softly, "the least forgettable creature ever abandoned on a doorstep. He told me to forget your face. Imagine. As if faces were the things I'm good at forgetting."

Vemmen's face was in there, though. She saw it more each week — the strong brow, the stubborn set of the chin, the particular way the left eye narrowed when the child was deciding whether to cry or to conquer. A hanged man's face, rebuilt in miniature, sleeping in a coal hold while the city that killed him glowed on the horizon like a bad conscience.

She thought of Aldis Vemmen setting that note in his own hand — pen, not press, which had struck her even then; a printer choosing the unrepeatable instrument, the one no forme could duplicate. *Forget the face.* Meaning: don't look for me. Don't look for her mother. Don't let her be found by looking. And beneath the instruction, which she had obeyed for months, the plea from the second note she still hadn't finished reading, folded and refolded in her breast pocket like a splinter working toward the heart. *Raise her where names are not crimes.*

Tonight names were crimes again, and worse than crimes — they were sentences. Marek Nystrom, printer, would be in a ledger by noon. Joss was in one already, or in the ground, or in neither, which was worst of all. And somewhere north of the burning streets, Captain Reval was reading the ashes of a print shop with those patient grey eyes, and finding — she prayed — nothing but ash. She had burned the forme herself. She had raked the coals through the melted leads with her own hands while gendarmes were still two streets away, and the letters of the men's guilt had run together into a single silver puddle, nameless, unreadable, innocent as ore.

One copy of everything, kept. That had been the rule. The rest given to the fog.

The fog. She listened to it working outside the hull — the muffled bells, the foghorn at the mole coughing its long low syllable every ninety seconds, boats calling to boats with voices disguised as ghosts. The whole harbor speaking in code. She had spent her life learning which silences keep people safe, and here was the sea practicing the same craft, wrapping every danger and every mercy in the same grey cloth.

Marek should have seen this, she thought. Marek loved fog. He used to say a compositor should study it — *all those letters floating, Theo, none of them in order, and yet the meaning gets through somehow.* She caught the memory before it could become a wound, held it instead like a punch held in a smith's tongs — hot, precise, formative — and set it down carefully in the place where she was keeping her father: not buried, not yet mourned, just composed, awaiting its proper page.

The baby yawned, enormous, and finally slept with her fist still free, as if keeping hold of something even in sleep.

* * *

They were boarded at the breakwater.

Theo heard it before she understood it — the barge losing headway, the wash of another hull alongside, boot-heels on the deck above, Lotta's voice pitched into a register Theo had never heard from her: bored, aggrieved, faintly insolent, the voice of a woman who had been boarded forty times and expected to be boarded forty more. Papers rustled. A man's voice, young, uncertain, reading aloud badly. Lantern light swam across the hatch seams, throwing moving bars across the coal dust like type being sorted by an unseen hand.

Theo pressed herself and the child into the space behind the bunkers, wrapped the blanket around them both, and became furniture. It was the oldest skill she had. Five years of being Theo-who-was-Theo, the son nobody questioned, the apprentice nobody remembered, and it came back to her now with a horrible smoothness — the slackening of the face, the flattening of the eyes, the body learning to occupy exactly as much world as a sack of grain occupies. She had hated that skill. She had survived by it. Tonight she used it to keep her breathing silent while a gendarme's boots crossed the deck six feet above her daughter's head.

The hatch opened. Fog and lantern light poured in together.

Two silhouettes. One young, one older, and the older one carried himself wrong — not like a boarder, like an inspector. He came down two rungs, lifted his light, and swept it across the hold: coal, sacking, the crate, the jug, the woman sitting among the sacks with a sleeping infant and the blank, patient stare of the very tired.

"Herring season?" the older one asked.

"Cousin's widow," Lotta said from above, descending half a rung so her voice arrived with her body. "From Kalmund. You'll find her paper boring, officer. Everything about her is boring. That's why I took her on."

The light held on Theo. She let her eyes focus slightly past the man, the way livestock focus, and the baby chose that moment — mercifully, absurdly — to snuffle and root toward her without waking. Whatever the officer saw, it wasn't sedition. It was a woman up all night with a child on a coal barge, and some part of him visibly lost interest in real time.

"Papers."

Theo produced the paper Lotta had promised. He read it slowly, mouthing the words. Kalmund meant nothing to him; herring meant less. Above them Lotta sighed theatrically and began, loudly, to describe the price of coal.

"Fine," the officer said, and handed the paper back, and paused — one heartbeat, two — with his light angled down at the satchel between Theo's feet. Heavy, square, smelling faintly of machine oil. "Cargo manifest says type castings?"

"For a printer in Halsing," Theo said. Her voice came out level, and she marveled at it from a great distance. "Lead and brass. Heavy to carry, worthless to steal."

Something crossed his face — the word *printer*, this month, landing like a match near straw — and Theo watched him decide. Not decide she was innocent; decide she was not worth the paperwork. He straightened, the light withdrew, the hatch thumped shut, and the boots crossed the deck and crossed the gap between hulls and faded into the fog, and only then did Theo discover she had been holding the baby hard enough to wake a stone.

She did not shake. She noted, distantly, that she probably ought to shake, and filed the trembling away for later, for some future decade with time allotted to it. Above, the anchor chain began to run. The barge leaned, gathered, leaned again, and the foghorn answered somewhere off the starboard bow, and the breakwater slid past them unseen, felt only as a change in the water's temper — harbor chop giving way to the long deep swell of open sea.

They were through. They were out.

* * *

Toward morning Theo climbed the ladder and stood at the rail.

The fog was thinning from below, burning off in rags, and behind them — far behind, reduced by distance to something almost gentle — the glow of the fire still stained the underside of the clouds. The shop. What was left of it. Her father standing before it in his good coat, if he still stood, if the delaying had delayed long enough, and she could not know and would not know for months perhaps, and she understood that this not-knowing was the shape her life would take now: everything she loved reduced to a glow on the horizon and a rumor on the water.

Joss, unknown. Marek, unknown. Reval, alive, promoted, certain to remain so until a barrel of pickled sprats reached a neutral shore and a foreign typesetter read the first page and went very still.

Grief came aboard quietly, the way ballast comes aboard — loaded low, below the waterline, invisible and essential, the thing that would keep her upright in the swells to come. She let it settle. She did not try to throw it overboard. A boat rides truer weighted, and she had cargo now: a child, a set of punches, a name in her pocket that was not yet hers to say.

The baby woke at dawn in the clean cold air and regarded the sea with ancient disapproval. Theo held her up to see it — the grey water, the grey sky, the gulls wheeling out of the dissolving fog like thoughts escaping.

"That way," Theo told her, pointing south, where the fog was opening in long bright seams. "Wherever that is. We'll find out together."

And at the bottom of the fog, in the pocket of her coat where it had ridden all night against her heart, the second note waited — Vemmen's hand, Vemmen's plea, the words she had never dared read to the end — and as the sun broke through and laid a road of gold across the water ahead of them, Theo drew it out at last and unfolded it, fully, for the first time.
