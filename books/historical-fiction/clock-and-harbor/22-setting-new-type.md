## 22. Setting New Type

The free port smelled of tar and oranges, and Theo had not yet learned to trust either.

It was the smell that undid her, in those first weeks — not the salt, which she knew from home, but the sweetness rotting gently under it, crates of fruit going soft on the quay because this city had too much and did not guard what it had. She had stood at the harbor wall with the baby screaming against her chest and wept, quietly and without quite understanding it, over an orange. Abundance was a language she had never been taught. She was learning it now, one season at a time.

The print shop was a narrow room above a chandler's store, its windows fogged with the breath of the sea and its floor uneven enough that the press rocked when she worked fast, which she still did; two hours of sleep a night for a year had carved the habit into her body like a letter into lead. She could not slow down. Perhaps in time. But the press — a battered hand-press bought from a defunct shipping gazette, its iron pitted, its platen slightly askew — bore her weight each morning the way her father's had borne hers, and when she leaned into the pull of the lever she felt the whole architecture of the past swing through her arms: the forme of testimony set by a dead man's hands, the sheets pulled by candlelight while gendarmes walked two streets away, Marek's silence at the treadle, which had been his only way of saying *I am with you* and which she had understood only when there were no more chances to say anything at all.

She kept his photograph — no. There was no photograph. She kept his loupe instead, hanging on a nail above the type cases, and sometimes she caught herself looking through it at nothing, at the grain of the workbench, magnified and strange, as if he might be hiding somewhere inside the smallness of things.

The baby — Aldina, she had named her, for the man whose handwriting she would never forget, though the midwife said the child should have her own name someday and Theo had promised she could take another when she was old enough to choose — the baby was walking now. Walking was the wrong word. Aldina traveled between the type cases like weather, sudden and total, dragging a wooden composing stick taller than her arm, babbling to herself in a language of her own invention that had, Theo noticed with a laugh she couldn't suppress, an unusual number of hard consonants. The click of sorts against the stick. The girl already loved the sound of letters being handled, even before she knew they meant anything. Some inheritances came through the blood regardless of what names were written in what registers.

"Careful of the case," Theo said, for perhaps the hundredth time that morning, not looking up. "The nine-point em quads are not for eating."

Aldina said something emphatic and unrepeatable and continued her survey of the shop's geography.

Outside, the gulls argued over fish heads, and the bells of three different churches rang the hour within a minute of one another, none agreeing — the free port could not even agree on what time it was, and gloried in the disagreement. Theo had come to love that too. In her city, every clock had answered to one tower, and everyone had known exactly when the executions began.

* * *

The testimony had been published in March, six months after the barge, four months after Theo had found a printer in this city who spoke a dozen languages and owed favors to men who owed favors to other men, and had handed him, across a table in a café smelling of burnt coffee and revolution, the copy she had carried out of the fire sewn into the lining of her coat.

He read the first page standing up. He read the second page sitting down. When he finished, he looked at her over the top of the paper for a long moment and asked, "Vemmen is dead?"

"Hanged last spring. Before any of this was printed once."

"And you?"

Theo had thought about the question for longer than anyone watching would have guessed necessary. "Nystrom," she said finally. "Theo Nystrom." And then, because the word had been waiting five years behind her teeth and would not wait anymore: "My name is Ulyana. Put both if you like. Put all of them. I'm done choosing which silences keep people safe."

The pamphlet went out under two names: *Aldis Vemmen* and *Ulyana Theo Nystrom*, compositor. It named eleven officers. It named Captain Reval third from the top, because she had counted the sheets and weighed the testimony and decided, in the end, that accuracy was its own kind of justice and she would not soften even the order of a list. It described the executions at the shipyard gates in the words of witnesses Vemmen had gathered and Theo had never met and never would. It ended with a sentence Vemmen had set in type and never printed, and which Theo had found in the locked back room beside the punches she now owned: *Let it be read where names are not crimes.*

Twelve hundred copies. They crossed borders in coal barges and flour sacks and the false bottoms of suitcases belonging to people who asked nothing, and they spread the way water finds the low places, and Theo received news of them the way one receives news of birds — fragmentary, unverifiable, and somehow sufficient. A student group in one capital. A reading in another, broken up, reassembled, printed again by strangers. Someone translated it into a language she could not read and sent her a copy out of sheer exuberance, and she sat on the shop floor with Aldina asleep on her knee and sounded out the shapes of the foreign letters and wept again — she cried so easily now, the year had cracked something open in her that refused to reseal — because the testimony had escaped her hands entirely. It was loose in the world. It belonged to everyone now, which is the only safety ink ever gets.

Marek was not in it. She had written him into the account twice, revised him out both times. He had stayed behind at the treadle; he had bought her the hours she needed with his own; and some debts, she decided, were paid in silence, in the currency both of them understood best.

* * *

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, in the middle of a job she could not abandon.

She was setting a wedding announcement for the chandler's daughter — real work, paid work, the kind that required no courage whatsoever — and she heard the postman's knock and called down to sign for it herself, and Aldina immediately abandoned her post by the ink stone to conduct a full military inspection of the envelope, gripping Theo's trouser leg with one fist as though the letter might otherwise escape.

"Stand down," Theo told her. "It's paper. Paper is on our side."

But her hands had gone cold anyway, the way they still went cold at certain envelopes, certain knocks, certain hours, and she slit it open with the tip of a bodkin standing at the window so the light fell full across the script.

The hand was unfamiliar until it wasn't. Then she saw it whole: the careful downward pressure, the letters formed by someone accustomed to writing quickly at night, in bad light, between crises — the midwife's hand, the woman named Sigrid who had cooled a fevered infant with wet cloths and recognized a dead man's jawline in a living child's face and said *nothing*, and had pressed bread and milk into Theo's hands at the door and told her which streets the patrols avoided.

*Dear daughter of the print shop,* the letter began, which was how strangers who knew everything addressed her now, and Theo felt the familiar tightening in her chest — grief and pride braided together so tightly she could no longer tell which was which.

The letter said that the city had changed. That the amnesty had come in the end, wider than promised, or narrower than feared depending on how you counted, and that the new authorities — such as they were, such as anything was — had begun pulling the old officers out of their houses one by one, not by decree first but by demand, crowds at the doors, papers waved, the pamphlet itself held up like a warrant. That Reval had tried to leave on a requisitioned ship in April and been taken off it at the gangway by men who had read his name third from the top. That he sat now in the same wing of the same citadel where he had once signed interrogation orders, awaiting a trial that would be imperfect and slow and might, despite everything, be honest.

That Joss had been released in February. Alive. Missing two fingers on the left hand and none of his opinions, working the docks again, asking after the printer's daughter whenever ships came in from the free port.

Theo put the letter down on the composing stone, next to the wedding announcement with its cheerful swashes half-set, and stood very still.

Reval, arrested. Not by her. Not by the testimony alone — Sigrid was clear that it had taken crowds, and committees, and the general collapse of everything the old officers had stood on. Justice had not arrived the way she had once imagined it, clean and singular, a sheet pulled perfect on the first impression. It had arrived the way most things arrived: late, smeared at the edges, carried by many hands.

And yet her hands had been among them. She had set the type that the crowd had held up at the gangway like a mirror. Whatever else the year had taken — the shop, the press with its rocking platen, her father, the city where every clock answered to one tower — it could not take that back. It was set. It was published. It was in the world.

Aldina, finding her mother motionless, resolved the silence in the manner of children everywhere, by seizing the hem of the letter and attempting to eat it.

"No," Theo said, laughing now, catching the small wrist. "No, little one. This one we keep."

* * *

She wrote back that evening, after Aldina slept in the crate lined with sailcloth — the original sailcloth, softened now by a year of washing, too worn for anything but a bed, and Theo could not bring herself to cut it up and could not have explained why to anyone who asked.

*Dear Sigrid,* she wrote. *Tell Joss the fingers were not wasted. Tell the city that from here the fires looked smaller than they felt. Tell whoever stands at Reval's trial that there is a child alive in a free port who will one day be able to read the transcript, and that I intend to see she learns.*

She hesitated over the closing. Then she wrote:

*I go by Ulyana here. My shop prints under that name. If you ever get out — and you should, you of all people, who kept the right silence at the right door — there is a press upstairs over a chandler's store that smells of tar and oranges, and there is always work for hands that know what letters weigh.*

*Yours,*
*Ulyana Theo Nystrom, compositor*

* * *

In the morning she opened the shop early, as the light came gray-gold through the fogged windows, and began setting the day's job: a broadside for the dockworkers' association, announcing a meeting, ordinary and legal and unafraid, the kind of printing that in her old city would have been worth a cell and in this one was worth only its fee. She worked the case with the ease of twenty years compressed into six, her fingers finding the letters upside down and backward, the world reversed and made readable only by patience.

Aldina supervised from the floor, composing stick in hand, striking the boards at random and demanding acknowledgment. Outside, the three churches disagreed about the hour. The fog burned off the harbor slowly, ship by ship, and somewhere among those masts was a coal barge, and somewhere on that barge perhaps a man with nine fingers who asked after her, and somewhere across the water a city rebuilding itself around the absence of the men she had named.

Theo pulled the lever. The platen came down. The impression was clean.

"Marek," she said quietly, to the press, to the room, to the nail where the loupe hung — using his name the way one touches a scar, to check that it has healed into something you can carry. "Watch this."

And she began, letter by letter, to set the day.
