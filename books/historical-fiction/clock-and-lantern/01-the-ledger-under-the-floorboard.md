## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The cold at Marrow Creek came up through the floorboards before it came through the walls, as though the earth itself had grown tired of holding warmth and had begun, in its slow mineral way, to give the war back everything it had taken. Wendell Eriksen felt it first thing each morning, kneeling on the ward's pine planks to bathe a fever or bind a wound, and thought—not for the first time—that the hospital had been built with its bones nearest the ground, and that everything above them was only pretense. The pretense was necessary. Pretense always was, at Marrow Creek.

By daylight the ward was a country of groaning timber and canvas cots, forty-two beds in a long room that had once been a granary, its rafters still dusted with the ghost of old chaff. The stoves worked badly. The windows fogged and wept. And the men lay in their rows like letters in an unfinished sentence, waiting for someone to come along who could read what they meant.

Wendell read them. That was the work, though no one had ever written it down in any manual of nursing: you learned to read men the way clerks read ledgers. This one would live if the leg came off. That one was already leaving, whatever his pulse insisted. The boy in bed nineteen—Halloway, seventeen, lying about his age with his whole trembling body—was going to survive his wounds and die of something else entirely, some smallness of spirit that the army would never have a column for. Wendell saw it all, and said little, and did the work.

It was after dark that the other work began.

* * *

Night at Marrow Creek belonged to two kinds of people: the dying and the watchful, and often these were the same. The lamps were rationed like everything else—one wick per ward, trimmed low—and beneath that stingy circle of light Wendell moved from cot to cot with water and laudanum and the particular silence the job required. But there was a third occupation, unlisted, unwitnessed, that began when the last of the sleeping draughts had done its slow arithmetic and the ward settled into its chorus of breath.

Wendell knelt beside the third bed from the stove, where a loose board had been pried up and set back so many times that its nails no longer held anything but habit. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp that rose from the earth, lay a ledger.

Not the hospital ledger. That one lived in the adjutant's tent, a fat official volume ruled in columns, into which names went in and, sometimes, quietly failed to come out again. No—this was a private book, a school exercise book bought in another life from a stationer who could not have imagined its use, its pages filling now in a small, careful, upright hand: *Private Record of Men Deceased Whose Deaths Are Not As Recorded.*

Wendell lifted it out by lamplight when the ward slept, and added to it, and read it over, and put it away again. It had begun as grief-work. It had become something else, something with edges.

The first name in it was Private Elias Mott, Company F, recorded 14 March 1862 as *deserted while under escort*, though Wendell had watched him die of a belly wound on the transport road and had helped bury him under a cairn of river stones because the burial detail had no shovels. The second name was Corporal Denby, entered as *suicide*, which was the army's word for a man who died of a self-inflicted wound nobody could explain and nobody was permitted to investigate. The third was simply a name and a company and the notation *struck from rolls; cause unknown*—and it was the unknown ones, Wendell had learned, that multiplied fastest. A man could be erased for desertion, for cowardice, for debt, for knowing something. Paper was cheaper than truth and lighter to carry.

There were sixty-one names now. Wendell knew all sixty-one the way a priest knows penitents: Private Anselmo Reyes, fever, listed as deserted; Sergeant Croft, shot by his own lieutenant in a dispute over rations and logged as killed in action at a place he had never been; the three men of the pontoon company whose entire detachment had been struck off after Harrow Ford, as though the disaster had been so embarrassing even its dead had to be disowned.

Wendell dipped the pen, blotted it, and wrote tonight's entry in the low light while a man four beds away dreamed aloud of a farmhouse and someone named Lottie.

*Private Tobias Finch, Co. D, admitted 9 Dec. with gunshot wound, left thigh. Died this date, approx. 3 in the morning. Wound was healing well. Death entered in ward book as "fever." No fever was present. Surgeon's assistant signed the entry without examination.*

Wendell read it back, and the reading back was the ritual, and the ritual was how the dread was kept at bay—naming things made them fixed, fixed things could be witnessed, witnessed things could not wholly vanish. So Wendell believed. So Wendell had believed for a year.

Tonight, the belief sat less easily. Tonight, the words looked different under the lamp: not a record but an accusation. Not a kindness but a weapon left lying where anyone might pick it up.

Wendell put the book away, pressed the board down until the nails complained softly into their holes, and stood in the dark listening to the ward breathe.

* * *

The trouble arrived on a Tuesday, in the person of Colonel Merrick Shawe, who came to Marrow Creek in a hired carriage rather than a military one, which was the first odd thing.

Wendell noticed such things. A year of watching men die of small oversights had sharpened the noticing into something like a trade. Shawe was not the hospital's colonel—the surgeon commanded here, in the loose way surgeons command anything—and he had no obvious business at a rear echelon of sixteen beds' worth of convalescents and a graveyard already thickening under the snow. He wore his coat well, though. He had the kind of face that had been handsome and then decided usefulness mattered more, and he came through the ward doors stamping the frost from his boots and asking, in a voice pitched for pleasantness, about the state of the roads.

"Bad, Colonel," said Brann, the other nurse on night duty, without looking up from her bandage-rolling. Brann was broad-shouldered and plain-spoken and had been at Marrow Creek eight months longer than Wendell, and she gave officers exactly as much of herself as regulation demanded and not a grain more.

"And the supply situation?" Shawe asked. "Rations, dressings, laudanum. I hear there have been... irregularities."

"There's never enough," Brann said. "That's regular enough."

Shawe smiled at that as though it had wit in it, and walked the ward. He stopped at beds. He asked the wounded men gentle questions—where were you hit, who brought you in, do you remember the names of the men beside you when you fell—and Wendell, working at the far end, watched him work and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that none of the questions were about the men at all.

He was asking after paper. Who kept the records. Who wrote them. Whether the ward book was ever copied. Whether the copies went to division or stayed here. Whether the nurses could write.

"Do you write, Nurse—" he glanced at the brassard, "—Eriksen?"

"Some," Wendell said. "Supply forms. Ward entries when the clerk is drunk, which is often."

"Your hand is good." He had picked up a supply requisition from the desk near the door—a routine form in Wendell's careful upright script—and was looking at it the way a man looks at a horse he is thinking of buying. "Very regular. Legible. The kind of hand a man can trust."

"The kind of hand a clerk can read, sir. Nothing more."

"No," Shawe said, setting the form down precisely where it had lain, aligning its corner with the desk's edge. "Nothing more." He smiled again. "I'm conducting an audit, you understand. Records of the late actions. There are discrepancies. Names that appear in casualty lists but not in burial returns, and vice versa. Sloppy staff work, most likely. War makes clerks of us all, and clerks make errors." His eyes moved along the row of cots with what might have been tenderness in a different man. "Errors accumulate. One wants to find them early. Before they become... something else."

"What else can an error become, sir?" Wendell asked.

"Almost anything," said Shawe. "A scandal. A question in the papers. A career with a stain on it." He turned from the window. "You see much, in a place like this. Death comes through your hands. You'd be surprised how much paperwork death generates—or how much paperwork it ought to generate, and doesn't. If you should notice discrepancies, Nurse Eriksen—an entry that doesn't sit right, a name that appears twice or not at all—you'll bring them to me. Directly to me. Not to the surgeon, not to the adjutant. To me." From his breast pocket he drew a card and laid it on the desk. There was no address on it. Only a name and a regimental crest. "I reward thoroughness. And I remember discretion."

"I'll keep it in mind, sir."

"Do more than keep it in mind." For just a moment the pleasantness thinned, and Wendell saw through it to something older and colder, a face like a locked door in winter. "There was a captain. Vey. Aldous Vey. His file has gaps in it. Gaps attract me the way a wound attracts flies—I can't help it, it's my nature. You've heard the name?"

"No, sir."

"He was at Harrow Ford. The files say he disgraced himself there. The files also say he's dead, and dead men generate very little paperwork indeed—which is why I find the gaps so interesting." Shawe put his gloves back on, finger by finger. "If his name turns up anywhere—in conversation, in correspondence, scratched on a wall—it would be worth something to the right people. And costly to the wrong ones."

He left the way he'd come, stamping the frost from his boots, and the ward doors swung twice behind him before they settled.

* * *

That night Wendell did not go to the floorboard immediately. Wendell sat instead on the upturned crate by the stove, in the last warmth of the coals, and looked at the ward full of breathing men, and listened to the wind work at the eaves, and thought about the shape of the danger that had walked through the door wearing a colonel's coat and a pleasant voice.

An audit. There was no audit. Audits were announced in orders and conducted by clerks with trunks of forms, not by a field officer traveling alone in a hired carriage asking after handwriting. What had come to Marrow Creek was a search, and searches have objects, and the object had a name, and the name was gaps.

Sixty-one names. Sixty-one small discrepancies, gathered lovingly, nightly, in a hand a man could trust, buried under a board three beds from the stove. Shawe did not know the ledger existed. But he suspected that somewhere in the world, paper was accumulating against men like him, and he was moving from lamp to lamp lifting each one in turn, and today he had lifted Marrow Creek's.

And Vey. *Dead men generate very little paperwork.* Wendell turned the sentence over and found the hook in it: why does a colonel audit a dead man? You do not chase a deserter's ghost across a winter of record offices unless the ghost knows something, or signed something, or failed to die convincingly enough.

The coals ticked and settled. In bed eleven, a man moaned and was still. Wendell rose, crossed the ward, checked his pulse out of pure habit, found it steady, and stood a moment longer than the task required, looking down the long dim room at the ranks of the wounded and the sleeping and the almost-gone.

They trusted the records. That was the terrible thing, the thing Wendell carried alone: every one of these men believed that what happened to him would be written down truly somewhere, that the army kept faith in ink if in nothing else. And Wendell knew better than anyone alive how freely the ink lied.

So the ledger was dangerous now—that was the truth of the evening, arriving cold and complete. Not because it was wrong. Because it was right. Because somewhere above this ward, above this creek, above the whole grinding front, sat men whose careers rested on the proposition that certain soldiers had never existed, and Wendell had spent a year writing those soldiers back into being, one line at a time, and hiding the evidence beneath a loose board like a sin.

Wait for the search to pass. Bury it deeper. Burn it, whispered the prudent voice, the voice of survival, the voice that had gotten Wendell through two years of wards and amputation buckets. Burn it and be only a nurse again, safe inside the pretense.

But sixty-one names. Elias Mott under his cairn of river stones. Sergeant Croft logged dead at a battle he never saw. The pontoon men struck off as though drowning in their own disaster. They had no one else. That was the whole of it, and Wendell knew it the way the dying know things, plainly and without appeal: there was no other record. There was only this one, and the hands that kept it.

Wendell knelt at the third bed from the stove. The board came up quietly, as it always did, its nails surrendering without protest. The oilcloth was cold. Inside, the pages waited, patient as the dead themselves.

By lamplight, Wendell turned past the last entry and opened a fresh page, and did not write a new name. Instead, in the same careful upright hand that Colonel Shawe had praised as trustworthy, Wendell wrote at the top of the page a single line:

*If anything happens to me, look under the floorboard.*

Then, below it, smaller: *Begin with Harrow Ford.*

The pen hovered. Somewhere out in the dark, miles down the frozen river road, a lantern was moving between the trees—Wendell could see its faint swing through the frosted pane, too steady and too deliberate for a patrol, a light carried by someone walking toward the hospital with purpose.

The knock, when it came an hour later, was soft. Three taps at the ward's side door, in the deep cold, from a gaunt stranger half-frozen to his bones who called himself Tomas Rill and asked, in a ruined voice, only for treatment.

But that was later. First Wendell blotted the page, folded the ledger closed, and paused—pen in hand, lamplight low, the wind rising—to listen to the distant, deliberate footsteps of whoever was coming through the snow toward Marrow Creek, and felt the quiet dread settle into place like a nail driven home:

The ledger had grown dangerous. And now, apparently, it was about to grow visitors.
