## 2. A Stranger at the Lantern

The cold came down off the ridgeline in the last hour before dawn, the way it always did at Marrow Creek, sliding through the tent seams like something with intent. Wendell Eriksen felt it first at the wrists, where the cuffs of his coat had worn thin, and then in the fingers that no amount of work could keep warm anymore—fingers that had learned to move by habit rather than feeling, lifting a bowl, wringing a cloth, turning a man's face gently toward the light to see whether the light was still worth giving him.

There were eleven men in the surgical tent that night, and Wendell knew each of them by wound before he knew them by name. That was the way of it now. Names came later, if they came at all, murmured through cracked lips or scrawled on a tag by some corporal in a hurry to be elsewhere. The wounds arrived first and spoke most honestly: this one the thigh, this one the belly, this one the shoulder taken apart so thoroughly that Wendell privately doubted the arm would ever again agree to be an arm.

He moved down the row with his lantern held low, because low light kept men sleeping and sleeping kept men from feeling, and because after a year of this he had come to distrust anything done brightly. The lantern's tin shade threw a soft yellow oval ahead of him, and inside that oval the war was briefly manageable—one chest rising, one bandage needing changing, one boy of perhaps nineteen whose fever had broken sometime in the evening and who would, against all odds and all precedent, live.

"Water," the boy said, or tried to. Wendell gave it to him, tilting the cup with a thumb over the rim so the flow came slow.

Outside, the wind worried the guy-lines of the tents and set the whole hospital breathing—a long, uneasy inhale-exhale, as though the camp itself were a patient too far gone to save but refusing, out of stubbornness, to die.

Wendell had been at Marrow Creek for eight months. Long enough to know which floorboards were loose, which orderlies stole laudanum, which surgeons drank, and which colonel's paperwork never quite matched the number of men who went into an engagement versus the number who came out. Long enough to have developed the private, careful habit that occupied the small hours of his nights—the habit that lived beneath the third floorboard of the supply tent, wrapped in oilcloth, written in a hand so small and regular that it might have been mistaken for a stock inventory if anyone had ever thought to look.

No one looked. That was the point of it.

He finished his rounds near four o'clock and stood a moment in the tent's doorway, letting the cold scour his face. The sky had cleared. Stars over a winter field, hard and indifferent, and beyond the picket lines the frozen creek running its shallow silver course between banks the color of old bone. Somewhere out there, the war ground on without any particular enthusiasm, two armies starving each other across a border nobody could draw twice the same way.

Wendell pulled his collar up and went back inside. There was still charcoal to grind, still water to haul, still the ledger of record to bring up to date before the surgeons woke and began their cheerful arithmetic of the day.

* * *

The stranger came with the morning, half-frozen and carried between two of the supply drivers who'd found him folded into the lee of a stone wall two miles up the river road, as though he had lain down there intending to rest for only a moment and had been surprised by night.

"Found this one on the north track," said one of the drivers, a red-faced man named Ost who talked the way other men breathed. "Didn't have a horse, didn't have a pack, didn't have a blessed thing but them rags and a cough you could hear across a field. Thought he was dead till he bit me."

"I didn't bite you," the stranger said. His voice was roughened, quiet, and entirely unapologetic.

They put him on the cot at the end of the row, the one reserved for men nobody expected to argue about. Wendell got the blanket off him in layers—first the ragged greatcoat, grey once, then a civilian jacket beneath it that fit him badly in a particular way, too broad through the shoulders, as though it had been bought for a smaller idea of a man—and understood immediately why the drivers had taken him for dead. He was gaunt the way winter makes things gaunt, all planes and hollows, with a beard grown wild and frost still clinging to it, and hands that were the worst of it: hands split at the knuckles, blue at the tips, the nails broken and black.

But under the ruin of him there was architecture. Wendell saw it when they lifted him—the way the man's body arranged itself even in extremis, spine straightening against the cot, chin level, eyes doing what eyes do when they are accustomed to assessing a room and its occupants in a single pass. It lasted three seconds. Then the stranger let himself sag, deliberately, into the shape of a man who has been beaten by the road, and Wendell would have missed it entirely if he hadn't already seen the first part.

Men who had spent years being carried didn't carry themselves like that.

"Fever?" Wendell asked, laying two fingers at the stranger's wrist. The pulse was fast but strong, and the skin, once the chill was worked out of it, burned dry. Yes—fever, and the cough Ost had mentioned, a wet sound deep in the chest that meant the lungs had been negotiating with pneumonia for days.

"No fever," the stranger said.

"There is. You'll have opinions about it anyway, I expect." Wendell worked as he talked—boots off, feet inspected for frostbite (the left foot was bad, not lost), shirt opened, ribs pressed one by one while the man held himself rigid and gave nothing away except a fractional tightening around the eyes when Wendell reached the fourth rib on the right. Old injury. Weeks old, healing crooked.

"That's healed wrong," Wendell said.

"It's healed."

"Held together by spite." Wendell pulled the blanket up. "What's your name?"

A pause. Short enough to seem natural; just long enough not to be.

"Tomas Rill."

"And where does Tomas Rill come from, walking the north track in January with no coat worth the name?"

"Upstream," Rill said, and coughed then, really coughed, bending double, and Wendell stopped asking because the answer to the next question was going to be a lie anyway and there was no sense making a sick man spend the strength to tell it.

He dosed him with what the stores allowed—bark tea, a mustard plaster for the chest, hot stones wrapped in flannel at the feet—and moved on down the row. Behind him he heard the stranger settle into silence, and thought little more about it. Marrow Creek received strangers the way the creek itself received snow: constantly, indifferently, most of them melting away before anyone thought to mark where they'd been.

* * *

The fever kept Rill in the hospital three days, and in those three days Wendell noticed several things, each of them small, each of them filed away in the growing drawer of observations he kept behind his forehead.

First: Rill ate like a man who had learned to eat slowly on purpose, dividing what was given him into equal portions, finishing everything, leaving nothing, and never once asking for more no matter how thin the portion was. Soldiers begged. Starving civilians begged harder. Men who had commanded a mess table did neither.

Second: Rill's eyes followed the officers. Not the nurses, not the orderlies, not the pretty young volunteer who read aloud in the evenings—but every time Colonel Marsh or one of the staff captains crossed the tent, Rill's attention shifted with the smooth economy of a gun turret, cataloguing insignia, noting who saluted whom, measuring the space between ranks. He thought he did it invisibly. He did not do it invisibly.

Third: on the second day, when the fever broke and Wendell changed his plaster, Rill said, quietly, "You count well."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The supplies. Yesterday. You counted the lint dressings by feel, without looking at the tally sheet. Two hundred and forty, you said, and the quartermaster's boy wrote it down without checking." His voice was still gravelled from the coughing. "Most people can't hold numbers like that without paper."

"Eight months in a place where everything runs short teaches a person to count," Wendell said, and turned the conversation to the state of his lungs.

It was nothing. It should have been nothing. But that night, sitting in the supply tent with the lantern trimmed low and the real ledger open on his knees—his ledger, the secret one, the one with its columns of names and dates and the cold little annotations in his own precise hand—Wendell found his pen slowing.

*Private Wm. Adlard, Co. B, 3rd Rifles,* the entry read. *Reported deserter 14 Oct. Treated by me for gunshot wound, right forearm, 11 Oct. Too weak to walk ten miles. Died of wound fever 13 Oct. No deserter walks ten miles on a leg I dressed.*

Beneath it, others. So many others. *Struck from rolls.* *Recorded as suicide, no note, no witness.* *Died at Harrow Ford per regimental return; survived Harrow Ford per my own two hands.*

Harrow Ford. Even the name of the place had a weight now, a year after the disaster, when an entire column had been fed into a killing ground and destroyed, and the army had needed someone to blame, and had chosen a dead man because dead men made convenient ones. Captain Aldous Vey, the returns said. Disobeyed orders. Lost the ford. Killed in the rout—or vanished into it, depending on which rumor you preferred—and posthumously stripped and condemned in absentia by men who had never once risked the trouble of producing his body.

Wendell wrote the names because the alternative was letting the army write its own version, and he had decided, somewhere in the second month at Marrow Creek, in the smell of carbolic and the sound of men calling for mothers who were forty years away, that somebody had to keep the true count. He had not thought of it as dangerous. Dangerous implied that what he was writing might matter to anyone.

He blew out the lantern and sat in the dark and listened to the wind, and thought about a starving man who counted dressings by ear and watched captains the way farmers watch weather.

* * *

On the fourth day Rill was well enough to be discharged, and discharged he was, with a bundle of bread and a warning about the cold. But he did not leave. Wendell saw him at dusk standing by the woodpile as though considering whether to steal an armful, and the next morning he was still there, and by noon he had talked himself into the position of general handyman—an arrangement nobody had approved and nobody objected to, because Marrow Creek was perpetually short of hands and long of things needing carrying.

Wendell watched him work. That was the honest word for it, though he told himself otherwise at first. Rill hauled water and split kindling and turned the muck barrow with the grim competence of a man who had done worse labor under worse conditions, and he did it all with his shoulders drawn in and his face arranged into dullness, the disguise of an ordinary drifter, and it fooled everyone.

It nearly fooled Wendell. Nearly.

The mistake—if it was a mistake—happened on the sixth day, in the supply tent, of all places. The quartermaster sergeant had gone off to settle a dispute over oats, and Rill had come in with an armload of firewood, and the requisition forms lay out on the trestle where Wendell had been working through the morning's issue sheets. A gust through the tent flap lifted the top pages and scattered two of them across the floor, and Rill, obligingly, stooped to gather them.

Wendell saw it happen. Saw the man's eyes drop to the nearest page—innocently enough, at first, the way anyone glances at paper—and then stop. Saw the stillness come over him, total and instantaneous, the stillness of a man reading not words but handwriting.

Because Wendell's hand was distinctive. He had known that for years. Small, upright, exact; the numerals formed in a peculiar closed style he'd taught himself as a clerk's apprentice, the letters never varying, never slanting with fatigue or hurry. A hand like print. A hand you would remember, if you had ever seen it before somewhere that mattered.

Rill squared the pages. Laid them back on the trestle in a neat stack, edges aligned, which no casual gatherer of dropped papers ever bothers to do. Then he straightened, met Wendell's eyes for exactly one heartbeat, and looked away, and said, in his roughened voice, "Cold enough to crack iron out there," and carried his firewood out of the tent.

That was all. Nothing was said. Nothing was done.

But Wendell stood among the crates and the lint boxes and the smell of hemp and lamp oil, and felt the ground of his ordinary days shift subtly beneath him, the way ice shifts underfoot before it gives. Because he knew—he knew the way you know a thing in the body before the mind consents to it—that the stack of pages Rill had aligned so neatly had included the morning's issue sheet, signed in Wendell's unmistakable hand, and that somewhere in this gaunt stranger's past he had seen that hand before. Seen it often enough to know it on sight. Seen it in circumstances a hospital handyman had no business having witnessed.

And if the hand was familiar, then so was whatever else had been written in it.

Wendell went about the rest of the day with his face composed into its usual professional mildness and his mind running like water under ice. At evening rounds he checked Rill's chest—the pneumonia was retreating, sulking, but not beaten—and pronounced him fit for travel within the week. Rill thanked him politely. Their fingers did not touch. Neither of them looked at the other longer than decency required.

Then, near midnight, when the camp slept and the wind had dropped to a mutter, Wendell pried up the third floorboard of the supply tent and took the oilcloth packet out, and unwrapped it, and sat looking at his own careful columns by lantern light.

So many names. So many erased ones.

He heard the crunch of frost outside the tent wall—light, deliberate, stopping. Someone standing in the dark, three feet from where he sat with the evidence of a year's quiet treason open in his lap. The footsteps waited. Wendell did not blow out the lantern and did not cover the ledger and did not move at all, and after a long minute the footsteps crunched away toward the woodpile, unhurried, almost courteous, as if to say: *I know. And when I'm ready, we will speak of it.*

Wendell sat until the candle guttered, listening to the creek talking in its sleep under the ice, and understood that the stranger who called himself Tomas Rill had not come to Marrow Creek by accident at all.

He had come for the man who kept the count.
