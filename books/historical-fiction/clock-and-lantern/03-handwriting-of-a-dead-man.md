## 3. Handwriting of a Dead Man

The stranger stayed three days.

Wendell Eriksen noticed him the way one notices a splinter working toward the bone—not at first, and then constantly. Tomas Rill, as he called himself, had come in off the river road with a fever that was either genuine or a very good imitation of one, and he occupied the cot nearest the stove with the patience of a man who had nowhere else to be and no intention of leaving until whatever he was waiting for arrived. He did not complain. He did not ask questions. He ate what was given him, slept when the lamps were doused, and watched—always, when Wendell turned unexpectedly, those grey eyes were open, tracking, cataloguing, like a clerk taking inventory of a warehouse he intended to rob.

It was the hands that troubled Wendell most. A deserter's hands went soft or scarred; these were neither. They were officer's hands, kept clean at the nails out of long habit, and they moved with a certain economy—the way Captain Vey's hands had once moved, two winters ago, signing requisitions at Marrow Creek before Harrow Ford took him. But that thought was absurd, and Wendell put it away each time it surfaced, the way one puts away a letter that cannot be answered.

On the third evening the hospital emptied itself into its nightly hush. The worst of the wounded had been dosed down to sleep; Brann had gone to her bunk after a shift that had run four hours over; the orderlies were smoking in the latrine shed where the wind couldn't find them. Wendell sat at the supply table with the ledger of stores open under the lantern, entering the week's expenditure in a hand kept deliberately small and even—small enough to fit more on a page, even enough to be read by anyone who might someday need to read it.

"You count everything," Rill said from the shadows near the stove.

Wendell did not look up. "A nurse who doesn't count loses patients to theft and rot. I've seen both."

"Twice-ruled columns. Marginal notations in a cipher of your own devising—I've watched you check them against nothing but memory." The voice came closer, unhurried. "You keep two books, Nurse Eriksen. One for the army. One for the dead."

The pen stopped. Ink gathered at the nib in a dark bead that trembled with Wendell's pulse.

"I don't know what you mean," Wendell said, which was true in the sense that all denials are true: there were words, and they had been said.

"The man on page eleven. Corporal Hesk. Listed as a deserter on the fourth of October, though I stood beside his body for an hour before they buried him in the wrong uniform. And the boy on nineteen—Private Ollas, marked *suicide*, though the wound was in his back and the powder burn was a foot away." Rill stepped into the lantern light, and up close the fever-look fell away from him entirely, replaced by something harder and more deliberate. "I've been reading your face for three days while you thought you were reading my chart. You write their names down because someone above you is striking them out. And you're frightened of exactly the right person."

Wendell set the pen down with great care, as if it might go off. "Who are you?"

"You know who I am." A pause, and then, softer: "Or you knew. Before they killed me."

* * *

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, wind worried the canvas of the ward tents, a long low moan like a large animal in pain. Wendell found that the room had gone slightly distant, the way it did during amputations—a trick the mind performed to let the hands keep working.

"That's not possible," Wendell said.

"No," Rill agreed. "But here we are."

He reached into the ragged coat—slowly, telegraphing the motion so there could be no mistake—and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with surgical thread. He laid it on the table between them but did not untie it.

"Captain Aldous Vey," he said. "Eleventh Border Rifles. Reported dead at Harrow Ford, the seventeenth of September, last year. Killed, according to the official record, in the collapse of the east ford line—a collapse the same record attributes to my failure to hold it. There is a stone in the officers' plot at Corvenne with my name on it. My mother visits it." His voice did not waver, but something behind it did, briefly, like ice shifting on a river. "She brings flowers to a grave that holds a lieutenant named Duvall who died of typhus. They needed a body for the ceremony. I understand they chose me because I was already dead in every way that mattered administratively."

Wendell stared at him. The face was thinner than the face in memory, the jaw shadowed by a year of hard living, the hair gone grey at the temples in a way that thirty years should not have accomplished—but beneath the ruin of it, yes. Yes. The set of the eyes. The way the left eyebrow sat a fraction higher than the right, giving the permanent impression of a question half asked. Wendell had stood close enough to this man, once, to smell the ink on his fingers.

"You've been alive," Wendell said, and heard how foolish it sounded, "for a year."

"A year and two months." He began, at last, to work the knot on the oilcloth. "Fourteen days of it drowning in the marsh below the ford. Three months crawling north through country where my face was worth five hundred crowns. The rest learning how to be nobody—which, I'll tell you, is a discipline far harder than soldiering. Nobody has no papers. Nobody has no history. Nobody can be shot by either army and buried without a form." The knot gave. He unwrapped the cloth with the tenderness men usually reserve for infants or relics.

Inside lay a letter.

Wendell knew it at once—not the letter itself, but its kind. Army paper, the pre-printed field-report stock with the border seal in faded violet at the head. The creases had gone soft and grey from folding and refolding. It had been carried a long way against a body, and the body had been living hard.

"This is—" Wendell began.

"Read it."

Wendell unfolded it under the lantern. The handwriting struck first, before any sense of the words: a strong forward-slanting script, the capitals tall and narrow, the descenders slashed rather than curved. Wendell had seen that hand on a hundred requisitions, on the duty rosters, once on a condolence note written to a sergeant's widow in a beautiful, terrible effort at comfort. It was a hand one remembered.

*By order of this command,* the letter read, *the position at Harrow Ford east was held beyond all prudent limit upon the direct instruction of Capt. A. Vey, who refused repeated counsel to withdraw and thereby sacrificed the Eleventh Rifles to no military purpose. Capt. Vey being deceased, this finding is entered posthumously.*

Below it, a signature. Major T. Quist, for the commanding general.

"There were witnesses," Rill said quietly. "Eleven men who survived the ford and heard the actual order given. Do you know how many are still alive? Two. One is a drunk in Halver's Bend drinking himself to death on money someone pays him to stay drunk. The other—" he tapped the letter, "—signed this. Quist didn't write it. His hand is different; I served under him for six years, I'd know his script anywhere. But his name is on it, and his name is the thread. Pull it and the whole garment comes apart."

"Whose hand wrote it, then?"

Rill looked at him for a long moment. "That," he said, "is the question that has kept me breathing for fourteen months. Because whoever wrote it had access to the general's own report stock, the general's own seal conventions, and the authority to have it entered into the record without challenge. That is a very short list of men, Nurse Eriksen. And at the top of it sits a man whose name you already suspect, or you would not flinch every time Colonel Merrick's adjutant comes through asking about paperwork."

The name rose in Wendell's throat and was swallowed back down. Saying it aloud in this room felt like opening a vein. *Corvane.* Everyone at Marrow Creek knew General Corvane's name the way farmers know the name of the weather that ruined their harvest—said rarely, and never above a murmur, and never with the door open.

"Why me?" Wendell asked instead. "Why bring this to a nurse?"

"Because of the ledger."

"I told you—"

"You didn't have to tell me. I've seen you write in it." Rill leaned forward, and the lantern threw his shadow huge across the tent wall, a giant hunched over the small bright world of the table. "Every man the record calls a deserter, you've checked. Every suicide, you've questioned the burial detail. You have a book, hidden somewhere in this hospital, containing the true circumstances of deaths the army has rewritten. I know because I spent three nights watching you through the supply-tent seam, and because no one checks a dead man's story unless he believes the dead were lied about." He paused. "I also know because Corporal Hesk was my sergeant's brother, and when I saw his name in your marginal cipher—yes, I can read some of it; I taught codes for the Eleventh before the war made codes fashionable—I understood that God, or luck, or whatever governs this filthy border had finally put one honest record within reach of one buried truth."

Wendell's hands had gone cold. Not fear, exactly—or not only fear. It was the cold that comes when a thing long suspected steps out of the dark and shows its face, and one discovers the face is worse than the suspicion and also, somehow, a relief.

"If what you're saying is true," Wendell said slowly, "then the ledger isn't just a memorial. It's evidence."

"It's the only evidence left that wasn't written by the men who committed the crime." Rill spread his hands flat on the table, and Wendell saw that they were trembling now, finely, the tremor of a man who has held himself steady too long. "Harrow Ford wasn't lost. Harrow Ford was sold. The east ford was stripped of its reserve forty minutes before the assault came—forty minutes, on written order, from a level of command that had no business knowing our dispositions at all. When the line broke, the order to stand was countermanded by no one, because the man who should have countermanded it had already signed my death warrant in advance. They needed a dead captain more than they needed a live one. A dead captain can't testify. A dead captain can be blamed." His voice dropped. "Two hundred and six men died holding a position that had been sold out from under them. I have spent fourteen months learning their names. You have spent a year writing down the names of other men erased the same way. Don't you see? We've been keeping the same book from opposite ends."

Outside, the wind gusted hard against the tent, and the lantern flame bent and recovered. In the ward behind them, a wounded man murmured in his sleep—a fragment of a prayer, or a woman's name, or a place none of them would see again.

Wendell looked down at the letter. At that unmistakable hand, writing words its owner had never written. At Major Quist's signature, a thread waiting to be pulled.

"They'll hang you," Wendell said. "Not for the corruption—for the desertion. A dead man who turns up alive after a year is a deserter before he's a witness. The tribunal won't care what he witnessed. The law will want its pound of flesh for the uniform he abandoned, and Corvane will make sure the law gets it loudly, publicly, before anyone reads a word of testimony."

"Yes," Rill said simply. "I've had a year to consider the arithmetic. I may hang. But two hundred and six men are lying under stones that say they died for their captain's cowardice, and their mothers visit those stones, and I—" the tremor in his hands stilled, forced still, "—I signed the muster roll that put every one of them at the ford. Their names are mine to carry whether I'm alive or dead. If the price of setting those stones right is my neck, then the army and I have finally agreed on a fair exchange."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Wendell became aware of the ledger of stores lying open, of the hidden book beneath the floorboard three feet to the left, of a year of small careful entries suddenly transformed from mourning into ammunition.

"You understand what you're asking," Wendell said. "If I help you, I stop being a nurse who keeps private notes and become a conspirator. Everything I have here—the cover, the name, the work—it all burns. There's no halfway. Men like Corvane don't leave halfway people alive."

"I know."

"And you trust me. A stranger. On three days' acquaintance and a fever."

"I trust the ledger," Rill said. "The ledger says everything that needs saying about the hand that keeps it." He almost smiled; it was the ghost of a smile, worn thin by fourteen months. "Besides, I've been dead a year, Nurse. Dead men are permitted a few reckless investments."

Wendell looked at the letter a moment longer. Then, moving with the deliberation of a surgeon beginning an incision, Wendell crossed the supply room, knelt, and worked loose the third floorboard from the wall—the one beneath the shelf of lint bandages, the one that had opened perhaps forty times in a year and never once for another living soul.

The book came up wrapped in its own oilcloth. Wendell set it on the table beside the letter, side by side: the false record and the true one, the signature and the names.

"Then we do this properly," Wendell said, and heard in the steadiness of the voice someone new being born inside the old skin. "Not a confession, not a pamphlet—we build a case. Your letter gives us the lie. Quist's signature gives us the thread. My ledger gives us the pattern: every erased man, every rewritten death, dated and cross-referenced. If the same hand rewrote Harrow Ford that's been rewriting deaths ever since, the ledger will show it. Patterns convict, Captain. Corpses don't, but patterns do."

Rill—Vey—was staring at the ledger as a man stares at water in a desert. "How many?" he asked hoarsely.

"Forty-one names," Wendell said. "So far."

Vey closed his eyes. When he opened them again, the fever-patient was gone entirely, and something older and harder looked out—an officer reporting for duty after a year among the dead.

"Then we have work," he said, "and not much time. Because a man like Corvane already knows his ghost is walking. Sooner or later, he'll send someone to bury it properly."

As if in answer, far down the river road, a horse's hooves rang on frozen ground—too fast for a courier carrying routine dispatches, and coming toward Marrow Creek through the dark.
