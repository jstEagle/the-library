## 4. Names That Cost Money

The hoofbeats passed. Vey heard them slow at the hospital gate, heard the muffled exchange of voices—Wendell's low and unhurried, a stranger's clipped and official—and then the sound receded east along the river road, swallowed by the wind coming off the water. A dispatch rider. Nothing more. But his heart went on hammering long after the silence returned, and he understood that this was what the rest of his life would be: every hoofbeat an accusation, every lantern a tribunal.

He sat on the edge of the cot in the little storeroom Wendell had given him, and he made himself breathe the way he had once taught subalterns to breathe before an assault—slow, counted, mechanical—while the nurse across from him held the ledger in both hands as though it were something that might go off.

"Corvane," Wendell said. "You said that name before you slept last night. And again just now."

"I said it because it's the whole of it." Vey flexed his fingers. The cold had settled into the old break in his left hand, the one set badly by a country surgeon who had charged him two silver pieces and done the work of a butcher. "Everything else—the rout, the blame, my death—all of it is Corvane. If you want to understand why your book of names is worth hanging for, you have to understand what Harrow Ford actually was. Not a battle. A transaction."

Wendell did not move. That was the thing about this nurse, Vey thought—not courage exactly, or not only courage, but a stillness. Men in hospitals learned stillness the way soldiers learned to dig. It was the stillness of someone who had watched enough end suddenly to know the value of waiting one more moment before acting.

"Tell me," Wendell said.

* * *

"It begins with the river." Vey rose, stiff-legged, and went to the narrow window where the frost had grown feathers on the glass. Through the gap at the sill he could see the black ribbon of the Marrow, ice-edged, running south toward the ford that had killed his reputation if not his body. "You know the geography better than I do now—you've patched the wounds it makes. The Marrow valley is the throat between the two provinces. Whoever holds the fords holds supply. Both armies learned that in the first winter of the war, and both armies spent the second winter bleeding to prove it.

"By October of last year, we held three crossings. Harrow Ford was the middle one, and the only one with a road heavy enough for guns. The General Staff knew it. Corvane knew it better than anyone, because Corvane *was* the General Staff, in practice—Third Army, its logistics, its deployments, its paper. Every order that moved a man or a mule east of the capital passed over his desk."

"And he sold it," Wendell said. Not a question.

"He sold it the way he does everything—through intermediaries, through bills of lading, through men whose names I didn't know until after I was supposed to be dead." Vey turned from the window. "I was staff-adjutant at Harrow Ford. Captain, Third Army. My job was the unglamorous arithmetic of war: rations, ammunition returns, rotation schedules, the movement orders for four hundred infantry and two light batteries. I signed things all day. I was good at it, and being good at it meant I noticed when the numbers stopped making sense.

"The first anomaly was ammunition. Our returns showed expenditure we hadn't fired. Cases drawn from the central magazine at Drenmark and marked expended at positions that had seen no action. I queried it—politely, in writing, the way a careful man does. The reply came back countersigned by Corvane's own office: *accounting error, corrected, no further correspondence.* Then the anomalies multiplied. Grain drawn and never delivered. Remounts purchased at triple rate from a horse dealer in the capital whose name I later found on a shipping manifest bound for the enemy coast. And always, underneath everything, the same pattern: materiel leaving our lines on paper and vanishing off paper entirely."

"Vanishing how?"

"That took me six weeks to see, and when I saw it I was sick in a ditch like a green recruit." Vey's voice had gone flat, which was the only way he could carry it. "It vanished under the names of dead men. Every army keeps its rolls, and every roll carries ghosts—men killed whose paperwork lags, deserters never caught, dead whose deaths were never confirmed. Corvane's clerks harvested those ghosts. A consignment of army grain becomes, on the books, provisions issued to Company D—which lost eleven men at the chapel action and whose rolls were a shambles. Who audits grain eaten by the dead? No one. The dead don't file complaints. The dead don't ask where their boots went."

The lamp guttered. Wendell trimmed the wick without looking away from him.

"So it was theft."

"It began as theft. Grain, remounts, greatcoats, gunpowder—a private commerce dressed in army grey, with Corvane taking his share at the top and letting his contractors take theirs beneath. If it had stayed theft, Harrow Ford might never have happened. But in September, one of the shipping houses got careless. A manifest was seized—not by us, by the enemy's customs men on the coast road—and it found its way back across the lines as an act of spite, and it landed, God help me, on my desk, because manifests were mine." Vey closed his eyes briefly. "On that manifest were quantities of artillery ammunition. Army calibre, army markings, drawn against Third Army requisitions over eighteen months. Enough shells to keep their batteries speaking for a season. Sold to the very army we were fighting, through three intermediaries, paid for in letters of credit out of a bank in the capital.

"And I did the thing that ruined everything. I copied it. I wrote a report. I addressed it, following protocol, to the corps commander, with a duplicate to the Inspectorate—because I was a careful man, and careful men follow protocol, and protocol ran directly through Aldous Vey's desk into Aldous Vey's hands and nowhere else. I handed Corvane's evidence to Corvane's own channels and waited, righteously, for justice."

"And instead?"

"Instead there was a reassignment. Instead there were new movement orders, in a hand I half-recognized, thinning the Harrow Ford garrison and rotating the batteries to 'reserve positions' forty miles from any crossing. Instead there was a week of strange quiet on the enemy side of the river—the quiet of men who know where the door will be left open. And then, on the night of the ninth of November, they came across the ford in force, at the exact hour the garrison was thinnest, through the exact sector whose pickets had been redrawn by the newest orders, and Harrow Ford ceased to exist."

Vey's hand had closed around the windowsill hard enough that the frost cracked under his fingers. He made himself let go.

"Two hundred and six men died holding a crossing that had been sold out from under them. I don't say that number loosely. I counted afterward. Counting was what I was *for*."

* * *

The storeroom smelled of carbolic and wet wool. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed the deep, tearing cough that meant the winter would keep him or kill him, and neither of them looked toward it; they had both grown deaf to the ordinary sounds of dying.

"Afterward," Vey said, "came the second crime, which was larger and cleaner than the first. A rout that size demands an explanation, and explanations demand a culprit, and Corvane understood before anyone else did that the culprit was already prepared. There I was—staff officer, author of a formal report accusing unnamed persons of treasonous traffic in munitions, survivor of a disaster caused by precisely such traffic. Guilty of nothing but bad timing and honesty. Do you see? He didn't have to invent me. I had invented myself. All he had to do was turn the report around, make my accusations look like a guilty man's cover, add the forged order—"

"The letter," Wendell said quietly. "You said you'd brought proof."

From inside his shirt Vey drew the oilskin packet he had carried for fourteen months through swamps and hedges and the houses of strangers who asked no questions, and he laid it on the cot between them, and unwrapped it with hands that were not quite steady.

Three documents. The first was the captured manifest, brittle now, foxed with damp, its columns of figures marching in neat clerk's script. The second was his own report, the original, the copy that should have gone to the Inspectorate and instead had gone into hiding with him—dated, sealed, witnessed by his own clerk, a boy named Pellar whom he prayed nightly had reached the coast.

The third was the order.

"This is the thing that killed me," Vey said. "The order withdrawing the reserve companies from the west bank six hours before the assault. The order that made the rout inevitable. Officially, I wrote it. Officially, I signed it, panicked when the attack came, and then drowned in the Marrow trying to run—a coward who sabotaged his own post and died in the water. They recovered a body downstream in December. Wrong build, wrong teeth. It didn't matter. A captain's coat fits any corpse if the need is great enough. They buried him under my name with honours, read the burial service over me, struck me from the rolls, and published the finding: negligence, bordering on treachery, of Captain A. Vey, deceased. Case closed. Corvane got his scapegoat, his stolen army got its quiet grave, and the manifest, the report, the whole chain of theft—buried under two hundred and six dead men who couldn't testify."

Wendell leaned forward and held the forged order close to the lamp. For a long moment there was no sound but the flame.

"You said your own hand," Wendell said at last. "It's good. It's very good. The *A* has your slope, the crossbar of the *t*—"

"A forger worked from my requisitions. Two years of my requisitions—they had drawers of my handwriting at Third Army HQ, everything I ever signed. He got the hand. What he couldn't get was the form. Look at the heading. Regimental orders are numbered by series and quarter. This number belongs to a series that was retired in August—six weeks before the date on the face of it. I know because I *filed the retirement notice myself.* It's in the archive at Drenmark, probably, gathering mould, one sheet of paper that proves the order is impossible. One sheet." Vey laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "One sheet of filing, against a general with the army, the government, and my own corpse on his side."

"Then why hasn't anyone—" Wendell stopped.

"Because everyone who could corroborate it is dead, scattered, or bought. My brigade commander at the ford—dead in the assault. The clerk who handled the series retirement—transferred to a garrison in the far north the same month. Major Quist, the staff major who actually drafted and signed the withdrawal order on Corvane's instruction—I've spent fourteen months looking for him and he does not want to be found, which tells you he knows exactly what he signed. And the manifest and the report?" Vey touched the oilskin almost tenderly. "These are copies of copies. In any honest court they are paper. Paper can be called forgery. Paper has no face, no voice, no regiment. Paper alone hangs no generals."

* * *

Then Wendell did something Vey had not expected. The nurse set down the forged order, picked up the ledger instead, opened it to the first page, and turned it toward the light.

"Then tell me about the dead," Wendell said. "Not the battle. The names. Because I've been writing them down since spring, and I think you'd better look at what I've written."

Vey bent over the page.

It was a roll call of the erased. Not casualties—the hospital saw plenty of those, honestly recorded—but the other kind. Men reported deserters whose desertions made no sense. Suicides with no witnesses. Names struck from company rolls mid-winter with no discharge papers, no transfer, no next of kin notified. Wendell had recorded, beside each name, the date, the unit, the last known station, and—in a column that must have cost some effort and more discretion—whatever fragments of circumstance could be gathered from orderlies, pay clerks, letters home.

"Private Hask, Company D, Fourth Foot," Wendell read aloud. "Struck from rolls in March. Reported deserter. His mother writes the hospital every month; she says he sent money home until February and then nothing, and no one will tell her anything." A page turned. "Sergeant Olem, commissary, Drenmark magazine. Suicide, per report, in April. Two orderlies told me separately he was cheerful the day before and owed no one money." Another page. "Corporal Vash and Private Ellery, transport pool, listed as drowned in the Marrow on the same night, different fords, twelve miles apart."

Vey had stopped breathing.

He knew these names. Not all of them—he could not know all of them—but Hask, yes, Hask had been Company D, the shattered company whose rolls had fed the ghost-ledger; Olem had been a commissary sergeant at the Drenmark magazine where the phantom ammunition was drawn; the drowned men of the transport pool had been boatmen on the resupply route the year the grain moved by water. These were not random erasures scattered across the war's ordinary waste. They were a pattern, and the pattern had a shape he recognized because he had spent fourteen months tracing its negative image in the dark.

"They're witnesses," he said. His voice sounded distant to himself, as though someone else were reading the ledger over his shoulder. "Don't you see what you've done? Every man in this book is connected to the machinery. The ones who drew the goods. The ones who carried them. The ones who asked questions at the magazine, at the pay table, at the shipping offices—and were removed. Deserted. Drowned. Committed suicide." He looked up, and found his own fury reflected and doubled in the nurse's face. "Corvane's ghost-entries needed ghosts, but the system also needed *silence*, and silence was manufactured the same way—under dead men's names. You've been keeping a record of the murders that kept the theft invisible, and you didn't know it."

"I knew something was wrong," Wendell said. "That's why the book exists. I thought—I don't know what I thought. That someday someone would ask. That a list might matter."

"It matters." Vey straightened, and felt something ignite in his chest that was not quite hope and far more dangerous. "Listen to me. The manifest proves goods moved. The forged order proves the frame. But the manifest can be dismissed and the order can be disputed—unless they're anchored to a chain of real men, real dates, real stations, each one corroborating the next. Which is what you have built, name by name, night by night, in a book small enough to burn in a stove." He put his hand flat on the ledger's cover, feeling the cheap boards, the worn cloth of the spine. Fourteen months he had searched for a single document that would hold; this nurse had assembled a map without knowing there was treasure buried under it. "Quist, if he lives, can sign one page. Miss—there was a clerk, there must be clerks—whoever keeps the rolls at Third Army can confirm another. But the spine of it, the chronology, the pattern no clever advocate can explain away as coincidence—that is here. In your handwriting."

"Which makes me what?" Wendell asked. There was no fear in the question yet, Vey noted—only the stillness again, the professional calm of a man triaging a wound before deciding whether the limb can be saved. "An accessory? A target?"

"Both, if we're clumsy. A witness, if we're not." Vey wrapped the documents and slid them back inside his shirt, against his heart, where they had lived so long they had taken on the rhythm of it. "There is a mechanism for this. Civilian tribunals sit in the capital on corruption matters—military courts would bury us in an afternoon, but the civilian bench has jurisdiction over army supply contracts, and there are men on it Corvane does not own, because owning civilians costs more than owning soldiers. Evidence laid before that bench, properly witnessed—"

"Properly witnessed," Wendell repeated. "By a dead captain and a field nurse."

"By a living captain," Vey said, "and a nurse whose name appears nowhere in Corvane's files, because until tonight you were nobody—and I am beginning to understand that nobody, right now, may be the most powerful rank in this army."

Outside, the wind had swung north and was pushing snow against the shutters in long dry gusts. Down the ward the coughing man had gone quiet—one way or another. Vey stood at the window and looked out at the dark road where the hoofbeats had come and gone, and thought: he knows his ghost is walking. He does not yet know the ghost has friends.

"We'll need time," Wendell said behind him. "Days. To copy the ledger safely. To find out where and when the tribunal sits."

"Then we'll take the days," Vey said, "before someone comes to take them from us."

Far off, at the edge of hearing, the river ice groaned—a sound like a door opening somewhere under the world—and neither of them spoke again for a while, because both of them were counting, silently, the days they might have left.
