## 5. Eleven Days to the Tribunal

The letter came back on the fourth morning after Vey revealed himself, folded inside a bale of surgical lint that no one had ordered.

Wendell found it because Wendell found everything—that was the talent no one praised and everyone relied upon. A miscounted crate, a fever climbing by half a degree, a name missing from a roll. The lint bale sat at the bottom of the supply wagon with the freight stamp of a mercantile house in the capital that Marrow Creek had never dealt with, and when Wendell slit the twine and worked a finger into the loose weave, paper met fingertip like a pulse.

Wendell did not open it there. Wendell carried it into the tent with the worst of the wounded—the men too far gone to notice anything but their own breathing—and read it standing between two cots, where anyone watching would see only a nurse reading an invoice.

*The civilian commission sits on the twenty-sixth,* wrote Miss Ansel, her hand small and upright as fence posts. *It will hear matters touching the public purse and the conduct of officers, but hear me: it will not hear rumor, and it will not hear grief. It hears documents. Corroborating documents, sworn copies, supply manifests against order papers. Bring me paper or bring me nothing. Eleven days from the date of this letter. The river road has three gates between you and me. I cannot tell you which are honest. I can tell you that none of them are mine.*

There was no signature. Only a small inked circle, pressed hard enough to dent the page, like a coin laid on a dead man's eye.

Eleven days. The letter was dated three days prior.

Wendell folded it along its creases, tucked it into the bodice where the ledger's key already lived against the sternum, and went back to counting lint.

* * *

Vey heard the number and went very still, the way men do who have learned that stillness is the only posture that cannot be reported.

"Eight days," he said.

"Nine, if we ride the morning ferry and nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing will go right." He was sitting on the upturned crate that served him as chair, table, and altar, the dead man's letter spread before him again—the one in his own hand, the one that had hanged his reputation a year before his body could be hanged for anything else. He had read it perhaps forty times since Harrow Ford, Wendell guessed. He read it now the way a surgeon reads over old mistakes: not for information but for the shape of the wound.

"Tell me about the gates," Wendell said.

Vey's eyes came up. There was something almost grateful in them—not for the question itself, but for being asked as though he were still an officer, still a man whose knowledge had weight.

"Toll Bridge first. Sixty miles downriver. It's a revenue post—customs dressed up as cavalry. They'll search a cart for untaxed brandy before they'd search it for treason. Young lieutenants mostly, boys earning their commissions by being suspicious of flour sacks."

"And the second?"

"Fenlow." He said the word the way one says the name of a sickness. "Quarantine camp. Fever cases coming down from the northern lines get held there, and everything that passes gets a surgeon's inspection and a clerk's inspection and God's inspection if the chaplain's sober. Papers must match faces, faces must match papers. It is," he added, with a thin humorlessness, "the sort of place where a dead man is poorly advised to travel."

"And the third?"

"The Capital Gate itself. That's not a checkpoint, that's a wall with manners. The commandant there answers to the Ministry, and the Ministry answers—" he stopped, and his jaw worked, "—to whoever pays. To Corvane, in all but signature."

Wendell took the stub of pencil from behind an ear and pulled the flattened edge of a ration crate toward the lantern. On the cardboard, in the same careful hand that filled the ledger each night, Wendell drew the river: a long uncoiling line, Marrow Creek a small cross-hatch at its head, the capital a square at its foot. Then three marks, spaced like stitches.

"Sixty miles to Toll Bridge. Another ninety to Fenlow. Another hundred to the gate." Wendell did the arithmetic aloud, because arithmetic could be trusted when people could not. "With a loaded cart and the roads what they are—twelve days if we're lucky. Eight if we're reckless. We have neither the time nor the choice between them."

"We need horses and papers."

"We need to look so dull that no one wants to see our papers at all." Wendell tapped the cardboard river. "A nurse escorting a wounded cousin to convalesce with family in the capital. It's done constantly. Half the carts on that road are carrying some sister's brother home in pieces."

"And your cousin will be?"

"Aldous Vey is dead," Wendell said quietly. "Your cousin is Tomas Rill, shot through the shoulder at the second fight for Kessel Ridge, slow to mend, inclined to silence from the morphine. If anyone asks why a nurse travels so far with one patient, it's because the family pays, and the family pays because the family is fond, and there has never been a soldier yet who questioned a reason involving money and sentiment together."

Vey looked at the drawn river for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, stripped of the officer's cadence, closer to the voice of the gaunt man who had come limping through the snow.

"You understand what you're carrying," he said. "Not the ledger. I mean the risk. Ansel says documents, and documents are safe—paper doesn't hang. But you're not paper, Wendell. You're a person with a face and a name and a bed at Marrow Creek that anyone can count. Corvane buried Harrow Ford under dead men's names. If he learns what we carry, he will not hesitate to add yours."

"He would have to find it first." Wendell said it more steadily than it was felt. "And he would have to know what a nurse's handwriting is worth."

* * *

That night the hospital slept badly, as it always did when convoys came through—one hundred men breathing in uneven chorus, the stove ticking as it cooled, wind worrying the guy-ropes like a large impatient animal. Wendell lay awake beneath the thin blanket and let the fear arrive, because Wendell had learned at Marrow Creek that fear was like pain: refusing it only made it louder.

Fear of the road. Fear of the gates. But underneath, colder than either, a fear with a specific shape: the memory of Colonel Harse's visit two weeks past, his soft questions about paperwork, his fingers drumming once on the supply desk before he left. *You keep very thorough records, Sister Eriksen.* Thorough. The word had been crawling under Wendell's skin ever since, and tonight it finally showed its teeth.

If Harse had been asking for himself, fine. But Harse answered to divisional command, and divisional command answered to Corvane, and somewhere up that chain of obedience a lamp might already be lit over a map, a finger resting on Marrow Creek.

And yet—

Wendell got up, moved by the old habit that no danger had ever broken, and knelt on the cold boards behind the supply cabinet. The floorboard came up silent; Wendell had oiled its edges weeks ago. The ledger lay where it always lay, wrapped in oilcloth, and Wendell did not take it out, only rested two fingers on the wrapping, feeling through the cloth the faint ridges of stitched signatures.

Forty-one names. Forty-one men erased—deserters who had never deserted, suicides no one had examined, dead men struck from rolls so that their rations, their pay, their ammunition allotments could walk off invisibly into someone's ledgers elsewhere. Wendell had started it as mourning. Somewhere along the way it had become testimony, and tonight, kneeling in the dark with nine days left, Wendell understood for the first time that it had also become a weapon—which meant it had become a target.

Ansel wanted corroborating documents. Wendell had none, only names. But names written beside dates, beside units, beside supply figures remembered and copied—laid against order papers and manifests in the archives of the capital, they would make a pattern no tribunal could call grief. Pattern was evidence. Wendell had been building evidence for two years without knowing it, the way water builds a channel.

Wendell replaced the board, lay down again, and found the fear had changed its texture. It was still there—it would be there all the way to the capital—but it had been overtaken, the way a column of retreating soldiers is overtaken by a column marching. Purpose had the road now. Fear rode in the baggage.

Sleep came eventually, and with it a dream of the river, wide and gray, and three gates standing in it like needles waiting to be threaded.

* * *

In the morning they planned properly, at the far end of the laundry tent where steam hid faces and the wash-women's singing covered voices—a trick as old as armies.

Vey brought the route; Wendell brought the logistics, and it became clear within the hour that this was how the two of them would work: Vey thinking in terrain and timing, Wendell thinking in supplies and appearances, and somewhere in the middle the plan assembling itself out of both.

"Clothes first," Wendell said. "I'll requisition a civilian cart—there's a farmer outside camp who owes me for pulling his boy through typhoid, and he asks no questions. Grey horse, older mare, nothing worth stealing. We leave as travelers, not fugitives. Fugitives get remembered."

"Papers?"

"Forge them slowly. Forged papers fail because forgers rush—I've copied enough transfer orders to know the format blind, and you know what genuine ones smell like." Wendell glanced up. "Can you write with that shoulder?"

"I can write with my teeth if the occasion requires."

"Then you'll sign as Rill's brother-in-law, witness to his discharge travel permit. Practice the signature until your hand forgets it's lying."

Vey practiced. Wendell watched him: the ex-captain bent over the laundry table, ink-stained, tongue caught between his teeth, transforming himself stroke by stroke into a civilian nobody—an erasure performed voluntarily, in reverse, as if identity were merely another uniform one could be issued or stripped of. There was something terrible in watching it, and something steadying.

They argued only once, over the second gate.

"We bypass Fenlow," Vey said, tapping the cardboard map hard enough to crease it. "There's a ford six miles upstream—"

"—that floods with any thaw, that you haven't seen in a year, and that every smuggler in three counties uses, meaning every inspector watches it. No. We go through Fenlow, through the front entrance, with perfect papers and a perfect story, because the most dangerous thing on that road is being interesting." Wendell held his gaze. "You were an officer. You know how inspections work. They find what they're looking for. So we give them something small to find—two bottles of untaxed gin under the seat, surrendered with theatrical embarrassment—and while the lieutenant is enjoying his own cleverness, he isn't looking at us."

Vey stared, and then, slowly, the ghost of a smile. "You've thought about this before."

"I've imagined it. Every night since your letter arrived, I've imagined it. Imagination is free. Everything else on this road will cost us."

They settled the order of march: leave Marrow Creek in three days' time, before the ledger's nightly habit could draw any last scrutiny; overnight at the burned mill; Toll Bridge by the fourth day. Nine days gave them margin enough for one delay and no more. Any further trouble and the tribunal would sit without them, and the forty-one names would go on being nobody, forever.

When the steam thinned and the wash-women's songs wound down, they parted—Vey to his crate and his forged signatures, Wendell to the wards. At the tent flap Vey paused.

"Wendell." He used the name alone now; the honorific had fallen away sometime in the night, unremarked. "Why? You owe these names nothing. You could burn the ledger tomorrow and sleep soundly."

Wendell considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty.

"Because someone wrote them down wrong on purpose," Wendell said. "And I write things down right on purpose. It's the same work, in the end. One of us has to be accurate."

Vey looked at Wendell for a moment longer than comfort allowed, then nodded once—captain to subordinate, or witness to witness—and was gone into the white morning.

Three days, then. Three days of bandages and broth, of smiling at Brann across the supply table while hiding arithmetic behind the eyes, of walking past the loose floorboard each evening with a heart that knocked like a fist on a door. Nine days after that, a room in the capital, a clerk with a coin-shaped ink mark, and the whole rotten architecture of Harrow Ford laid out on a table for strangers to measure.

Wendell finished the ward round at dusk and stood a moment in the tent opening, watching snow come down on the hospital rows, soft and indifferent, covering everything equally—the honest graves and the dishonest ones.

Behind, in the shadow of the supply wagon, someone stood watching the snow too. Small, solid, unmistakable even at that distance and that angle.

Brann.

She didn't move and didn't speak, and neither did Wendell. She only looked—looked at Wendell, then deliberately, slowly, at the supply cabinet against the wall, the one that stood in front of the loose floorboard—and then turned away into the falling white.
