## 6. What the Nurse Carries

The copying began at midnight, when the lamps in the ward were turned down to their lowest blue flame and the only sounds were the wet rasp of lungs and the occasional groan that no one woke for, because waking meant feeling.

Wendell Eriksen sat at the little table in the supply alcove with a candle guttering inside a tin lantern, and opened the ledger for what might be the last time.

It was a poor thing to look at, honestly. A quarter-bound account book bought off a quartermaster who had asked no questions because Wendell had paid in salt pork. The spine was cracked, the corners soft with damp, and the pages inside were filled edge to edge with a hand so small and even it looked less like writing than like stitching—row upon row of names, dates, regiments, and the manner of each man's erasure. *Private Halloway, 3rd Rifles, listed as deserted at Kessel Ford; body buried by this hand, feet shot through.* *Surgeon's mate Odum, recorded dead of fever; hanged at the provost's post behind the commissary, order unsigned.* Page after page. Forty-one names now. Forty-one men who had died twice—once in the flesh and once on paper, the second death administered by clerks with better handwriting than conscience.

Vey slept in the far corner of the alcove, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of carbolic, still wearing the borrowed face of Tomas Rill, civilian, teamster, wounded cousin. He slept badly. Even asleep his jaw stayed set, as if he were arguing with someone even in dreams—perhaps with the army, perhaps with himself. Wendell had learned in three nights to read the stages of his waking: the frozen instant where he did not know where he was, the flicker toward the knife under the blanket, and then the remembering, and the shame of the flinch, which he covered every time by asking about the weather.

Tonight Wendell did not wake him. This work was easier alone.

The cipher had taken two evenings to build, and it was built out of the only code Wendell had ever trusted: medicine. Each name became a course of treatment. The initial letters mapped to doses; the regiments to tinctures; the dates to days of administration. To anyone rifling through the copied pages, they would find only a nurse's meticulous case notes—a record of fevers treated, wounds dressed, laudanum measured out in scruples. It was the kind of document an army would confiscate and never read twice, because it said nothing except that somewhere in this war a nurse had been diligent. The diligence itself was the message, if you held the key. And the key lived nowhere but in Wendell's head, which could be searched but not read, and could be killed but not compelled—at least, Wendell hoped so. One never truly knew until the moment arrived, and Wendell prayed daily never to arrive at it.

The pen scratched. Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and moved through the hospital tents like a slow animal, snapping canvas, rattling the lantern hook. Somewhere beyond Marrow Creek an artillery exchange grumbled on the far side of the hills, distant enough to be almost domestic, like thunder over a neighbor's farm.

*Corporal Ellet, 11th Foot,* Wendell wrote in cipher, *listed as suicide. Witness: this hand. He was alive when the retreat was called and dead within the hour, and the man who ordered him forward is the same man who signed the supply requisitions dated Harrow Ford.*

That last thread had come from Vey, spoken low across this same table two nights ago, in a voice scraped down to wire. Corvane. General Edran Corvane, whose name appeared in the war gazettes beside words like *steadfast* and *sacrifice*, who had held the line at Harrow Ford—held it, the gazettes said, at terrible cost, abandoned by subordinates, betrayed by the incompetence of Captain Aldous Vey, who had failed to hold the ford and had thereafter perished, or fled, or both, depending on which report one read. Wendell had known none of this when recording the names. That was the horror of it, understood properly: Wendell had been keeping a map for a year without knowing there was treasure anywhere on it. Every erased soldier, every convenient fever, every unsigned order—each had seemed like a private grief, a small local sin. Now they resolved into something else entirely. They were the wake behind a single ship, and the ship had a name.

The candle spat. Wendell trimmed the wick and kept writing.

It was near three in the morning when the floorboard outside the alcove creaked.

Wendell's hand stopped mid-word. There was no reason for anyone to be in the east corridor at three in the morning except death, and death made its own rounds without needing the floor to announce it. Wendell waited, breath shallow, listening past the wind.

Another creak. Closer. Then a voice, pitched low and roughened by sleep:

"Whoever's burning midnight oil back there had better be boiling water for my feet, because I've been coughing since dusk and I intend to complain about it at length."

Brann.

Wendell closed the ledger with a sound no louder than a page turning and slid it beneath the folded blanket on the table just as Nurse Brann filled the doorway of the alcove, holding her own lamp up against the gloom. She was fifty if she was a day, broad as a doorframe, with grey hair pinned in a knot that had lost its war with the wind and forearms that could turn a struggling man by herself. She had been at Marrow Creek eight months longer than Wendell. She had taught Wendell how to pack a chest wound with one hand while holding a lantern in the other, and she had never once asked why Wendell sat up late with paperwork.

She asked now, without asking. Her eyes went to the table, to the blanket, to the ink-stained fingers, and then settled on Wendell's face with the patient weight of a woman who had spent decades watching liars in ward beds.

"Couldn't sleep," Wendell said.

"No," Brann agreed. "Nor could I. Nor him." She tipped her chin toward Vey's blanketed shape. "Your cousin snores like a dying ox, by the way. I heard him through two tents of canvas."

"He's been ill."

"He's been *military*." Brann set her lamp down on the table with a click that sounded horribly final. She lowered herself onto the stool opposite, creaking, and folded her arms. "I changed his dressing Tuesday. Did you know that? You were down at the creek washing bandages, and your cousin was sleeping, and his bandage wanted changing, so I changed it. And I'll tell you what I saw, since we're being honest insomniacs together. I saw a healed wound dressed to look raw. I saw calluses on a teamster's hands that come from sword grips and reins both. And I saw a scar—" she tapped her own collarbone, "—right here, that I last saw on an officer at Kessel Ford, before the army decided he was dead."

The blue flame in the ward lamp hissed. Wendell discovered that the heart can stop and keep working anyway; it simply goes very quiet, like a house where everyone is pretending not to be home.

"You're mistaken," Wendell said.

"Likely," said Brann comfortably. "I'm often mistaken. I was mistaken last month too, when you asked me—ever so casually, over the linens—whether requisition forms from the Fourth Division passed through the capital or through Corvane's field office. Nobody asks a question like that out of idle nursing curiosity, Eriksen. I've been in this war long enough to know what questions cost. My brother asked one, once." Her voice didn't change, but something behind it did, some deep timber shifting like a hull taking water. "He was a farrier, Third Cavalry. The roll says he deserted at Ash Mill. I got a letter saying he died of fever. Both cannot be true, and I have spent four years knowing neither was."

The wind shoved at the tent wall. In the ward, a man called out faintly for his mother, and subsided.

"I'm not going to report you," Brann said. "I want that understood plainly, because I can see you're doing arithmetic behind your eyes and getting a bad sum. Whatever you're carrying in that book—and don't insult me, I watched you hide it under a blanket like a child hiding sweets—I am not the danger to it. I've buried too many men whose only crime was being inconvenient. If you're keeping a list of them, then God keep your pen sharp." She stood, took up her lamp, and paused at the alcove's threshold. "But I'll say this, and then I'll go boil water for my feet as advertised. Whatever you're doing, do it faster. Sergeant Hollis was asking Matron yesterday why your cousin's transfer papers hadn't come through from division. Hollis doesn't ask questions for his health. Hollis asks them the way a dog sniffs a hedgerow—for whatever's hiding inside."

Then she was gone, footsteps receding down the corridor, unhurried as ever, leaving Wendell alone with a stopped heart restarting and the cipher pages and the sleeping ghost of Captain Aldous Vey.

* * *

Morning came up grey and hard-frosted, and with it came the day's arithmetic of the living.

Wendell worked the ward the way every other morning began: changing dressings, spooning broth, holding the basin for the boy from the 9th whose fever broke in the night, sitting with old Corporal Meech whose fever would not break and who gripped Wendell's wrist with terrible strength and asked, as he asked every morning, whether the mail had come. It had not come for Meech in five weeks. It would never come again, though Wendell lied to him with a smile that had long ago stopped costing anything and started costing everything instead.

That was the thing about Marrow Creek that no one warned you about, Wendell thought, wringing out a cloth in water gone pink. Not the blood. Blood was easy; blood was honest. It was the accumulating weight of all the small mercies told to dying men, the way they stacked up inside a person like sandbags, until you were fortified and entombed in the same structure.

And now Wendell was about to add the largest lie yet: walking out of this place. Walking away from the wards and the creek and the roster of faces, away from the identity that had held together like stitched skin for three years—Nurse Eriksen, quiet, reliable, writes a fine small hand, keeps to herself. The plan required a departure that raised no flags and left no trail, and so the departure had to be woven out of true things. There was a convalescent convoy scheduled south in two days. There was a real letter—obtained honestly, which felt almost scandalous—from a hospital at Wethering Down requesting experienced hands. Wendell would request the posting openly, pack openly, and vanish between here and the river road, replaced on the convoy manifest by a clerk's error that Miss Ansel's contact had already promised to arrange.

On paper, Nurse Eriksen would arrive at Wethering Down a week late and a week silent, and then simply fade into the great anonymous churn of the war. In fact, Nurse Eriksen would be riding a grain cart up the river road beside a dead man, carrying a book full of names sewn into a false bottom under the oat sacks.

Wendell stood at the ward's open flap and looked down the long rows of beds, and understood, with the strange clarity that comes at the end of things, that this was a goodbye being said early.

Three years. The stove that smoked unless you coaxed it. Brann's terrible coffee, boiled to the color of regret. The particular way the light came off Marrow Creek at evening, copper on the shallows, so beautiful it felt like an accusation. The names—not the forty-one in the ledger, but the ones that lived in the body rather than the book: the boy from the 9th, who would live. Corporal Meech, who would not. The girl in the laundry tent who laughed at everything. All of it had made the person who could sit up at midnight building a cipher, and to cut loose from it now felt less like escape than like amputation—the removal of a healthy limb because the body it belonged to had to become someone else.

"Talking to the frost again?" Brann said, materializing at the flap with two tin mugs. She handed one over. "Drink that. It's terrible."

It was. Wendell drank it.

"I leave Thursday," Wendell said. "Convoy south. Posting at Wethering Down."

Brann looked out at the frost-hard mud, at the tethered mules steaming in the cold, at the creek beyond with its copper promise of evening. "Thursday," she repeated. "Fast, then." She drank. "You'll want to burn what can't travel. Ash tells nothing, but paper travels forever."

"You said you weren't the danger to us."

"I'm not." Brann turned, and her face in the flat morning light was older than her years, and tired in the bones. "But I'll tell you what I am, Eriksen, since you're determined to ride off into the fog playing at mystery. I'm a woman who has spent four years reading other people's lies for a living, and I know what a half-burned secret looks like, and I know this camp is starting to sniff around your cousin. So either you take me into your confidence, or you'd best pray your confidence travels faster than Sergeant Hollis's suspicions." She shrugged, vast and unbothered. "Or option three. Option three is you tell me nothing, and I mind my own business, which I am famously excellent at, and you go get yourself hanged for whatever's in that book without ever learning that I'd have gone with you for the asking."

She drained her mug, crushed it flat one-handed the way she crushed everything, and stumped off toward the laundry line.

Wendell stood holding the terrible coffee going cold, and felt the ground shift in a way that had nothing to do with frost heave.

* * *

That night, the copying finished.

The last entry went down at half past one: *Private Ellet*, completed, the cipher closing around the name like a fist. Wendell read the whole thing through twice, checking doses against tinctures, dates against days, until the hidden text lay clean and entire beneath its innocent surface. Then the original ledger came out from under the floorboard where it had lived for a year, and Wendell wrapped it in oilcloth, and wrapped that in a surgeon's rubber sheet, and considered the problem of where a book could hide that no search would think to open.

The answer had been waiting in the ward all along. Wendell fetched the spare mattress ticking from stores—the ones stuffed with chaff, which lumpy comfort had doomed to the storeroom—and unpicked a hand-span of seam. The ledger slid in among the chaff like a fish returning to weed. Wendell restitched the seam with sacking twine, using the clumsy stitch of an inexperienced hand, and set the ticking at the bottom of the luggage pile where luggage piles belong. Anyone searching would find the cipher first, because Wendell would make certain it was findable—a decoy of diligence, pages of treatment notes that read as nothing and were therefore worse than nothing, a locked box with no lock.

Vey watched from his corner, sleepless tonight, his gaunt face half-lit by the lantern.

"You should sleep," Wendell said.

"I've done enough sleeping in ditches to last a life." He rose, joints cracking, and came to stand over the packed bags. His eyes lingered on the mattress ticking. "You understand what happens if they catch us with that."

"Yes."

"Not just hanging. They'll erase us too. That's how Corvane works—you've seen his signature all through your book without knowing it. We won't be executed, we'll be *amended*. Deserters. Spies. A nurse of no fixed address found traveling under false papers." He smiled without any humor in it. "You'll make forty-two, in somebody else's ledger. If anybody's left to keep one."

Wendell tied off the last knot on the luggage and looked at him—that ruined officer's posture under the teamster's coat, that dead man's name worn like a borrowed coat himself—and heard the answer come out steady, which surprised them both.

"Somebody will," Wendell said. "I've seen to it."

Vey studied the small neat hands, the ink stains, the face of the person who had spent a year recording the dead so they couldn't be disappeared twice. Something moved behind his eyes that might have been awe, or grief, or the beginning of trust.

"Hollis," Wendell added quietly, "will come to check the transfer papers Wednesday night. Matron confirmed it today. Which means Wednesday night is our last night, and Thursday morning we need to already be gone."

Outside, boots crunched frost on the path—not Hollis yet, only a sentry making rounds—but the sound drew both their heads up like deer.

Two days, then. Two days of wards and broth and lying smiles, of being Nurse Eriksen for the last time, wearing the name like a costume soon to be folded away. Two days of watching the road north and wondering whether the sniffing at the hedgerow had stopped.

And on the second evening, as Wendell was banking the ward stove for the night, Brann appeared in the doorway carrying a packed carpetbag, her face set in the expression she wore for arterial bleeding and other emergencies.

"Change of plan," she said. "Hollis isn't waiting for Wednesday."
