## 7. The River Road

The cart smelled of turnips.

Aldous Vey had commanded two hundred men at Harrow Ford. He had dined with colonels, argued logistics with quartermasters who feared his signature, and once—on a night he still did not entirely regret—told a brigadier general to his face that the man's plan would drown a company in the ford shallows, which it duly did. He had not traveled anywhere in three years without a horse beneath him, an escort behind him, and the weight of rank parting the road ahead.

Now he lay on a bed of burlap sacks in the back of a farmer's borrowed cart, wrapped in a gray blanket that smelled of lanolin and someone else's sweat, with a turnip pressing against his hip and a bandage around his forearm he did not need and was forbidden to remove.

"You're slouching," Wendell Eriksen said from the driver's bench, without turning around. "Cousins convalescing don't sit like officers."

"I am lying down."

"You are lying down like an officer. There's a difference. Shoulders back is what I mean—no. Shoulders *down*. You're a man shot through the arm at Kessel's Mill who's too proud to say it hurts. Slouch into it."

Vey rearranged himself with what he felt was theatrical misery, and Wendell clicked his tongue at the mule, and they went on along the river road with the frost steaming off the fields and the water showing silver between the bare trees.

It was intolerable. That was the truth of it, the plain truth he chewed on mile after mile while the cart jolted over frozen ruts: it was intolerable to be cargo. To have his fate in another man's hands—not even another officer, but a nurse, a hospital steward, a quiet man with ink-stained fingers who had looked at Vey's letter in the dead man's handwriting and said only, *Then we'd best go before the thaw ruins the road.*

Vey had spent the first day of travel trying to be useful. He had suggested routes—he knew the river country from staff maps, could recite the fords and their seasonal depths—and Wendell had listened politely, thanked him, and then taken none of his advice, because the maps were a year out of date and the bridge at Kessel's Mill had burned in October and the ford at Harrow Ford itself, irony upon irony, was now garrisoned.

He had tried to help with the mule. The mule had bitten him through the blanket, and Wendell had said, "She bites everyone she doesn't trust," and Vey had spent an unreasonable portion of the afternoon wondering whether the remark was about the mule at all.

He had tried, finally, to plan. They sat in the lee of a collapsed barn the first night, sharing hard bread and colder bacon, and Vey laid out the situation as he saw it: eleven days, three checkpoints, the tribunal convening in the capital, Corvane's reach extending along the river like frost along a windowpane. He spoke for perhaps ten minutes, organizing the problem the way he had been trained to organize problems, and when he finished, Wendell fed a stick into the small fire and said:

"What will you eat tomorrow?"

Vey stared at him. "What?"

"Tomorrow. Breakfast, dinner. What will you eat?"

"I—" Vey looked at their provisions, which consisted of a sack of bread, a heel of cheese, the bacon, and a jug of watered cider. "The same as today. Rationed."

"And the day after?"

"The same. Until we resupply at—" He stopped. He did not know the towns. He did not know where a man might buy eggs without papers, or which farmhouses sold to travelers and which reported them, or how much a lie about a wounded cousin cost in small coin versus large. He had planned campaigns and could not plan a breakfast.

"Rye loaf goes stale fast in this cold, so we'll eat that first," Wendell said. "Cheese keeps. There's a woman at Halfway Mill who sells to me twice a year when I run hospital errands; she thinks my name is Anders and that I collect for a parish. We'll buy from her tomorrow noon, and you'll keep your mouth shut because your accent is wrong for a miller's nephew." He handed Vey the bread. "You command the war, Captain. I'll command the road."

*Captain.* It was the first time since Marrow Creek that anyone had used the word aloud, and it went through Vey strangely—not as comfort but as a kind of debt. The nurse gave it to him freely, casually, the way one hands over a tool, and expected nothing back except silence about eggs.

They slept in shifts. When it was Wendell's turn to sleep, Vey watched him do it—a trick learned in the field, watching subordinates rest to know what they were made of—and noted that the man went down all at once, like a lamp blown out, and woke exactly when he meant to, before dawn, without being called. Officers Vey had served with could not manage that.

* * *

On the second day the road bent away from the high bluffs and dropped toward the water, and the world changed character. Here the war was older than at Marrow Creek; it had passed through, eaten, and moved on, leaving behind its leavings. A burned gristmill stood in the river shallows like a rotted tooth. Fence rails had gone for firewood along a mile of verge. And there were people on the road—more than Vey expected, more than he liked. A family walking east with everything they owned in a handcart, the children silent in the way children are silent when silence has been ordered. Two soldiers of no visible regiment, moving with the loose-hipped walk of men between armies. An old man fishing through a hole chopped in the ice, who looked up at their cart with eyes that catalogued and priced everything in it.

"They see everything and tell whoever pays," Vey murmured.

"They mostly want to be left alone," Wendell said. "Same as us. Look at your lap."

Vey looked down. His right hand had drawn itself into a fist on his knee without his permission—the old habit, the commander's fist—and he made himself open it, finger by finger. It felt like unlearning his own skeleton.

At midday they stopped at Halfway Mill, and Vay kept his mouth shut and his shoulders slouched and played, badly at first and then adequately, the role of Cousin Tomas: a man hurt at Kessel's Mill, invalided home, sore and stupid with pain powders. Wendell performed the whole thing without appearing to perform anything. He haggled over flour with the widow at the mill in a voice Vey had never heard at the hospital—a lower register, a touch of the river dialect in it—and bought eggs and salt pork and, with a straight face, a bottle of laudanum that Vey suspected would never touch either of their lips but might, at some checkpoint, prove worth more than gold.

"How long have you been doing this?" Vey asked when they were under way again. "Being other people."

Wendell was quiet so long that Vey thought he would not answer. Then: "You learn it in a hospital. Every man who comes in tells you a story, and half the stories are lies, and you learn to tell which half because your treatment depends on it. Fever or shame. Gangrene or desertion. After a while—" He shrugged, a small movement the cart made large. "After a while you notice you've got stories of your own that fit different pockets."

"And which pocket does the ledger come out of?"

That earned him a look from the bench—a long, measuring look, the first time Wendell had turned fully around since morning. The mule took advantage of it to stop and consider a hedge.

"The ledger isn't a story," Wendell said at last. "That's the whole point of it. Everything else gets rewritten. Names get struck, orders get lost, dead men get blamed. The ledger is just—" he searched, flicking the reins, "—it's just what happened, written small, in a hand nobody important can read." He clucked the mule forward. "Sleep, Captain. You'll want your face dull by the bridge."

But Vey did not sleep. He lay watching the sky darken over the western hills and thinking about the ledger, which he had seen only briefly, by lantern light in the hospital's cellar, a page at a time: columns of names, dates, units, causes of death as officially recorded beside causes as witnessed. *Private H. Loomis, 4th Rifles — died of wounds, Harrow Ford,* and beside it, in smaller script, *shot crossing the ford against orders, body recovered downstream, boots missing.* Page after page after page. He had known intellectually what Corvane had done—had lived inside the knowledge for a year—but seeing it in Wendell's careful clerk's hand had been different. The general had written his crime in orders and omissions, in the negative space of official paper. Wendell had written it in names. And somewhere in those names, Vey was certain, lay the corroborating thread: the supply requisitions, the ammunition returns, the men moved and provisioned in ways that only made sense if someone had sold the position's dispositions and needed the paperwork drowned.

Corvane had buried his crime under dead men. Wendell had spent a year digging each one up by hand, gently, and laying them out in rows.

*I couldn't have done this,* Vey thought, and the thought was not humble exactly—it was appalled. Give him a company and an objective and he knew what he was worth. But this slow, anonymous, clerical courage, years of it, in a cellar, by lamplight, with discovery meaning a quiet grave in the potter's field—he did not believe he possessed it. He had deserted rather than sign his name under Corvane's order, and desertion had cost him everything in one terrible night. This man had stayed and paid by the inch.

* * *

The third day brought the cold down like a lid. The river began to carry ice at the edges, plates of it grinding softly against the banks, and the road ran close along the water, hemmed between the current and a rail cut, until late in the gray afternoon when the cut opened out and Wendell reined in beneath a stand of leafless oaks and said, quietly, "There."

Vey sat up despite himself, despite the slouching, despite the blanket, and looked.

The Toll Bridge crossed the river in three spans, stone-pierred, its timber deck worn pale in two wheel-ruts a century deep. On the near bank stood the checkpoint: a plank barrier across the road, a cook-fire smoking, two tents pitched in the lee of the tollhouse, and sandbags stacked in a low parapet facing the road as though an army might charge a toll bridge, which no army in history had done. A flag hung above it all, limp and wet. Beyond, on the far bank, the river road climbed toward the capital country, and Vey felt the distance to the tribunal compress suddenly into those three stone piers and whatever men stood on them.

Even at this distance he could read the post the way he had once read positions. Not regulars—the drill was sloppy, men wandering between fire and sentry-post without purpose. Militia, or detached line troops rotting on a backwater assignment, which was worse, because bored soldiers invented work, and inventing work at a checkpoint meant searching carts. He counted six, possibly seven. One wore sergeant's stripes and stood apart, arms folded, watching the road with a patience that suggested he was the only one there who had ever been shot at.

"Wagon search every cart, or only suspicious ones?" Vey asked.

"Every cart going north. They're watching for arms running upriver, so they say." Wendell's voice was flat, professional, doing arithmetic. "Papers first, then the bed of the cart, then—if the sergeant's thorough—the person."

"Then we don't cross tonight."

"We don't cross tonight." Wendell backed the cart off the road among the oaks, unhitched nothing, left everything ready to move. "There's a lane back a half-mile, a woodcutter's track. We'll go cold, no fire, and watch the bridge through the morning. Cross at midday when the guards are fed and sleepy and the light's flat."

Vey lay back against the turnips and studied the distant barrier through the trees until the dusk ate it. Somewhere beyond that bridge was a tribunal, eleven days minus three, and a general whose agents had already found him once. Somewhere behind them was Marrow Creek, and Brann the sharp-eyed nurse, and a cellar with a hollow floorboard. And here was this: a cold oak stand, a blanket, half a rye loaf, and a nurse who rationed fear like cheese.

"You're angry," Wendell said. It was nearly dark now; Vey could barely see the bench, only the ember-draw of the man's pipe, which he permitted himself one bowl of and no more.

"I'm not angry."

"You've been angry for three days. Since the mule."

Vey almost laughed, and the laugh turned into something else on the way out, something quieter. "I am not accustomed," he said, carefully, "to being carried."

Silence from the bench. The river ground its ice. Then Wendell said, "At Marrow Creek we had a boy last summer, seventeen, took shell fragment through the thigh. Artery clipped. There was one thing to do and one man able to do it, and I held him down while the surgeon worked, and the boy screamed for his mother and looked at me the whole time like I was betraying him. Because from where he lay, that's exactly what it looked like." The pipe glowed, dimmed. "Being carried feels like that. It isn't. It's just the other end of the same work."

Vey found he had no answer prepared. In twenty years of soldiering he could not recall a single superior or subordinate ever saying anything to him that landed quite so far past his defenses, and he suspected the nurse had not aimed at his defenses at all—had simply spoken a true thing, the way he wrote true things in a ledger, without regard for who it undressed.

"Midday tomorrow," Vey said finally. "Flat light."

"Midday tomorrow." A pause. "And Tomas—when the lieutenant asks about your arm, let it hang. Don't favor it like an officer favors a wound. Let it be ugly and boring."

"Tomas," Vey repeated, tasting the false name. "Yes."

They settled into the cold. Vey slept in fragments, waking to the sound of the river and once, in the smallest hours, to the murmur of Wendell's voice on the bench—awake, watchful, keeping the night for both of them without being asked.

Dawn came gray and windless. Through the oaks they watched the checkpoint wake: fire rebuilt, kettles, a relief sentry yawning through his rounds. And then, while the frost still lay hard on the wheel ruts, a rider came down the far bank at a trot, crossed unchallenged, spoke briefly with the sergeant, and rode on south—toward Marrow Creek, Vey thought, or toward Fenlow, or wherever Corvane's reach kept fingers.

Wendell watched until the rider was out of sight. Then he shook the reins and said, in the river-country voice he saved for strangers:

"Well, cousin. Let's go spend your reputation."

The mule put her head down, and the cart rolled out of the oaks onto the open road, and ahead of them the plank barrier lifted for a grain wagon and fell again, waiting.
