## 8. First Checkpoint: The Toll Bridge

The river appeared first as a sound—a low, constant muttering beneath the wind, like a crowd that never dispersed—and then as a long gray seam in the white landscape, ice-fringed at the edges, moving with a patience that made the winter feel older than it was. The road bent to follow it. Somewhere ahead, where the water narrowed between two shoulders of rock, stood the Toll Bridge, and beyond the bridge, the first of the three checkpoints standing between them and the capital.

Wendell had spent the last mile composing faces.

It was a habit from the hospital, though he had never thought of it that way until now. Before delivering bad news to a wife or a mother, he would decide in advance what his face would do—how much sorrow to show, how much steadiness, how much of himself to keep behind his eyes. He had done it hundreds of times without naming it. Now, driving the cart along the frozen ruts with Vey slumped against the grain sacks behind him, bandaged and gray-faced and playing his part of wounded cousin with a commitment that was only partly performance—the fever from his untreated shoulder had returned two nights ago—Wendell found himself arranging his features for a different kind of grief. Not the grief of a family. The grief of a lie about to be tested.

*You are taking your cousin Tomas Rill to Saint Verene's in the capital,* he told himself, reciting the story like a prayer. *His people have money there. The army hospitals are full. Your own hospital released you because there is nothing more they can do.* Every piece of it was built on truth, which was the only way Wendell knew to build a lie: load it with enough real things and it would carry weight. The transfer papers were forged, but the signature they imitated was real; the surgeon's note was invented, but the wound it described was genuine enough that any man who looked under the bandage would believe it instantly. That was the terrible economy of deception. It fed on whatever honesty you could spare.

"Stop the cart."

Vey's voice, low and roughened by fever, came from behind the sacks. "There's smoke. Chimney smoke—see it? That's the guardhouse. We're close enough now that I ought to say it while I can still think straight." A pause, and then, quieter: "If it goes wrong, drive at the lieutenant, not past him. A frightened man steps into the road when you swerve. If you go around him, he'll remember he had a choice."

Wendell did not answer at once. He was watching the bridge resolve out of the morning haze: a heavy timber structure on stone piers, snow crusted along its rails, and beside it, exactly where the road pinched down to a single lane, a barrier of crossed poles and a small cluster of blue-gray coats. Two soldiers. A third figure standing apart from them, wearing an officer's greatcoat too large for him, its collar turned up against the cold.

An officer. Not what Wendell had hoped for. Sergeants could be bored into carelessness; lieutenants were still trying to prove something.

He reined in the horse ten paces from the barrier and waited, hands visible on the reins, the way one approaches any authority in wartime. The nearer soldier came forward, a private with a red, chapped face and a carbine held with the anxious grip of someone who had never pointed it at anything that mattered. The officer followed more slowly.

"State your business." The officer's voice cracked on the second word—not with fear, exactly, but with the rawness of a voice being used harder than it was built for. He could not have been twenty. His cap sat slightly askew, and beneath it his face was pale in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. Wendell recognized that pallor. He had seen it on new nurses their first week in the ward, before the dead became furniture. This boy had been posted to a river crossing in the middle of a grinding war, far from anything glorious, and he was afraid all the time, and ashamed of being afraid, and would be grateful for any excuse not to have to act.

"Wendell Eriksen," Wendell said, and passed down the folded papers before being asked. "Nurse, contracted to the Third Field Hospital at Marrow Creek until last week. I'm taking my cousin to Saint Verene's in the capital. The army surgeons have done what they can for him." He let a beat pass. "What they could do wasn't much."

The word *cousin* sat strangely in his mouth. He had no cousins living that he knew of; his family had thinned out early and gone west. But the lie needed kinship, blood, obligation—the kinds of ties that made a man drive four days through winter rather than leave someone behind. So Tomas Rill, born Aldous Vey, was his mother's sister's son now, and Wendell found that saying it made it somehow more true than it had been on paper.

The lieutenant took the papers. His gloves were civilian ones, wool, with a hole beginning at the left thumb, and Wendell noted this the way he noted everything now—the way he had learned to note things since a stranger arrived at his lantern carrying a dead man's handwriting. Details were currency. You collected them even when you didn't know yet what they'd buy.

While the boy read—or pretended to read; Wendell watched his eyes and judged that he was reading every line twice without comprehending any of it—Wendell spoke again, filling the silence the way one fills a pause in triage, keeping the patient's mind occupied so panic couldn't take root.

"He took a musket ball in the shoulder at—" he hesitated, chose the safest vagueness, "—back in the autumn. It's festered twice. The bone's involved. They want him somewhere with a proper theater and a surgeon who's worked on joints." He glanced back at Vey with an expression he did not have to manufacture: genuine concern, worn thin by days of it. Vey lay against the sacks as instructed, coat open just enough to show the stained bandage wrapped over his shoulder and down across his chest, his face slicked with the honest sweat of fever. He had insisted on skipping the morning's dose of willow bark tea so that his shivering would be real. *A lie with enough true things in it,* Wendell thought again, and felt the old guilt stir in him like sediment in disturbed water.

The lieutenant looked up from the papers. "These transfer orders—they're signed by Colonel Marsh."

"Colonel Marsh signs everything that leaves Marrow Creek," Wendell said mildly. "I've seen him sign requisitions for lamp oil without reading them. I don't imagine these gave him any more trouble."

It was a gamble. Humor from a civilian to an officer could offend—but the right kind of humor, the weary kind, the kind shared by two people who had both dealt with the same bureaucracy from opposite ends, might make them colleagues. The boy's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. Something loosened anyway.

"I'm supposed to search all conveyances," the lieutenant said. "Orders. There's contraband moving on the river road—deserters, forged passes, that sort of thing." He said *that sort of thing* as if it were a disease he hoped not to catch. "It'll only take a few minutes."

"Of course," Wendell said. "Search whatever you like. But I'd be obliged if you let me get him out of the cold after. He shouldn't be sitting in this wind, and neither should your men." He looked past the lieutenant at the two privates, both young, both stamped with the same misery. One of them was holding his left arm stiffly against his body, and had been since they drew near.

That arm. Wendell had seen it from fifty yards and filed it away, and now he took it out and looked at it properly. The private kept his hand tucked near his hip, fingers curled wrong, and there was a dark stain seeping through a hasty wrapping at the wrist, old at the edges but not dry at the center. A bayonet accident, most likely, or a careless knife while working some camp chore. Three days old, maybe four. And going bad. Wendell could smell it from here—not a smell, precisely, but a certainty, the same certainty that let him walk down a ward of moaning men and stop at the one silent cot.

"You," Wendell said to the private, gently. "Let me see that hand."

The private looked at the lieutenant. The lieutenant looked at Wendell. "His hand?"

"It's poisoned," Wendell said. "Or very nearly. You can argue with me, and in four days he'll be arguing from a surgical table while someone takes the hand off at the wrist. Or you can unwrap it now and I'll tell you what to do about it. I've dressed perhaps six hundred wounds like it. It's the one subject on which I claim expertise."

For a moment nobody moved. Then the lieutenant nodded, jerkily, and the private came forward and held out his arm like a schoolboy presenting a ruined copybook.

Wendell climbed down from the cart and unwound the filthy wrapping with the unhurried economy of long practice. The wound beneath was ugly—a deep puncture through the heel of the palm, edges swollen and shiny, a thread of red inflammation already climbing the forearm toward the elbow. Lymphangitis. He had seen it kill men who laughed about it on Monday. He cleaned it with the spirits from his kit, packed it with the iodoform he had hoarded against just such occasions—he had told himself the hoarding was for the wounded he'd meet on the road, which had been true, in the way that a loaded gun is true regardless of what it is later aimed at—and bound it in clean linen while the private watched with the dazed gratitude of a man who has been ignored by everyone with power for months.

"You'll redo this dressing tonight and again tomorrow," Wendell told him. "Keep the arm raised. Higher than your heart—that means up, not just not-down. And if the red streak gets past your elbow, you find a surgeon immediately. Not your corporal. A surgeon." He dug in his kit and produced a paper packet. "This is iodoform. Show whoever dresses it next."

"Yes, sir—I mean—" the private floundered. "Thank you, sir. Nurse."

The lieutenant had stopped pretending to read the transfer papers. He was watching Wendell work with an expression that had come loose from its military setting entirely, and for a moment he looked simply like a boy watching someone competent, which in the winter of 1863 was among the rarest sights on the river road.

"The cart," the lieutenant said at last, and seemed embarrassed by his own persistence. "I still have to look in the cart. I'm sorry. Truly. But if I let one thing pass—"

"You'd have to let everything pass," Wendell finished for him. "I understand. Look wherever you need to. Mind the grain sacks; they're mostly chaff and disappointment."

Two of the privates went over the wagon thoroughly and awkwardly, lifting the sacks, prodding the straw bedding, peering into the false bottom of the medicine chest that contained nothing but actual medicine, because Wendell had decided weeks ago that the safest hiding place was no hiding place at all. The ledger rode where no search would find it: sewn into the lining of Wendell's own coat, page against his ribs, close enough that he could feel its weight shift when he breathed. He had copied the whole of it into cipher in three nights of work by guttering candle, and then, on Miss Ansel's advice, had written a second copy in plain hand with half the names altered and Corvane's name nowhere in it—a decoy designed to be found, designed to satisfy, designed to make whoever seized it feel clever. The original slept against his heart like a second conscience.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and after a while the lieutenant grew tired of supervising a search that insulted no one, and came back to the front of the cart.

"The cousin," he said. "He doesn't talk much."

"He talks," Wendell said. "Just not to the army anymore. He had a bad autumn." Which was true in ways the lieutenant could not begin to count.

Vey lifted his head a fraction and produced a mumbled, fever-slurred greeting, and the lieutenant studied him with a frown that lasted two seconds too long, and Wendell felt the whole fragile structure of the morning tilt on those two seconds—

—and then the wounded private sneezed, spectacularly, into his freshly bandaged hand, and swore, and the lieutenant turned to scold him, and the moment broke and slid past like ice shifting on the current.

"Right." The lieutenant handed back the papers. His relief was obvious and slightly shameful to him; Wendell watched him try to restore dignity by making his voice gruff. "Passage granted. Keep to the capital road. There's another post at Fenlow—quarantine camp, so have your papers ready, they're stricter than we are." A hesitation. Then, quietly, almost inaudibly, as if it cost him something: "The dressing. Tell me again about the red streak?"

Wendell told him again. He explained the limits of infection's speed, the signs that meant hurry, the signs that meant pray. He wrote the instructions on the back of a page from his notebook—on the back, carefully, never on a page that had writing on its face—and tore it free and put it in the lieutenant's glove. The lieutenant thanked him. Neither of them smiled. There are transactions between strangers that are too honest for smiling.

The poles were lifted. The horse leaned into the harness. The cart rolled onto the Toll Bridge, and the timbers boomed hollowly beneath the wheels, and below them the river ran dark and swift between shelves of ice, indifferent as arithmetic.

Only when the bridge was behind them and the road had bent out of sight of the guardhouse did Vey speak, without opening his eyes.

"You did that well."

"Did what?" Wendell asked, though he knew.

"All of it. The papers, the cousin business, the—" Vey gestured vaguely with his good hand, a gesture that encompassed the bandaged wrist, the boy officer, the entire gray morning. "The hand. You saw it before we ever reached them. You dressed it before he asked. You bought us passage with a wound that wasn't ours to treat."

"I bought passage with kindness," Wendell said. "There's no other coin accepted."

"No," Vey said softly. "You bought passage with kindness. Do you understand yet what that makes you, out here? At Marrow Creek you were a nurse. Out here, on this road, kindness is a tool you keep in the kit next to the iodoform, and you know exactly which wound to use it on, and you chose the one that would open the gate." He turned his head then, and his eyes—fever-bright, terribly clear—found Wendell's. "I'm not condemning it. God knows I'm grateful. I'm telling you I watched a man decide to be generous *strategically*, and I want you to know that I saw it, because I don't think anyone else would have."

Wendell drove in silence for a long while. The road ran north along the river, and the afternoon came on thin and colorless, and the guilt Vey had named sat in the cart with them like a third passenger, patient, uncomplaining, going wherever they went. Wendell had treated that private's hand with genuine care—every word of instruction had been true, every fold of the bandage done as well as he knew how. And he had done it, all of it, deliberately, as a transaction, having calculated before he ever dismounted from the cart that a poisoned hand was worth more than forged papers, worth more than a colonel's signature, worth perhaps a hundred miles of road. Care had become a weapon in his hands, and the worst of it, the part that would keep him awake tonight in whatever barn they found, was that the weapon had felt so natural. He had not learned a new skill. He had discovered what the old one was always for.

Ahead, the road forked where a burned farmhouse marked the miles, and the right-hand branch dipped toward a river town whose lights, by nightfall, they would reach. Quist was there. Major Emmerich Quist, the man whose signature actually sat at the bottom of the Harrow Ford order—the man whose handwriting, unlike Vey's, belonged to a living officer whom no one had bothered to declare dead.

"Tomas," Wendell said, using the false name aloud so it would sit easier in his mouth, "tomorrow we find the man who signed the order."

Vey closed his eyes again. When he answered, his voice had gone somewhere Wendell hadn't heard before—not fear, exactly, but the particular dread of a man about to meet the person who wrote his obituary.

"Then pray he's still drinking," Vey said, "and not yet dead. Because Corvane has had a year to tidy up, and I am the only loose thread he's left dangling—and tomorrow we go looking for another."
