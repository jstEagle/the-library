## 10. Brann on the Road

The packet boat's whistle faded upriver, and with it went whatever hope Wendell had carried out of Tanner's Lane. Drenlow was gone—taken, or fled, or floating somewhere beneath the brown water of the river—and the bloodied collar button sat in Wendell's pocket like a small cold coin paying for a debt no one had agreed to.

They did not speak of it. They crossed the ford at Millrace two miles below the town, Vey wading with his trousers rolled and his teeth locked against the cold, Wendell carrying both their bundles above the waterline, and by the time the sun found the road on the far bank the river had closed over their tracks as if it had been paid to forget them.

Vey walked badly that morning. The fever from his half-healed shoulder had come back in the night, and though he would not say so, Wendell could read it in the way he favored his left side, in the three extra rests he called strategic pauses. He was a man who had commanded three hundred soldiers at Harrow Ford, and now he could not command his own body to keep pace with a nurse's stride, and every rest stop cost him something harder than breath.

"We'll find a barn," Wendell said, at the third pause.

"We'll find the tribunal is what we'll find." Vey's voice was rough. "Nine days."

"Dead men don't testify. Rest."

Something flickered across Vey's face—the old command instinct rising to contradict her, then folding under. He sat down on a milestone with the gracelessness of a sack of meal, and Wendell almost pitied him, and then remembered Quist's collar button, and stopped.

* * *

They took supper at dusk in the lee of a fallen drystone wall, hard bread and a rind of cheese Wendell had traded for at a farmstead where the woman asked no questions because her own son was somewhere at the front and she could not bear to hear any answer about anyone. Wendell was watching the road behind them when she saw it.

A figure, far off, walking the way soldiers walk when they don't want to be seen walking—using the hedgerow, keeping to the shadowed side, stopping whenever they stopped. It had been there, Wendell realized, since the ford. Since Millrace.

She did not reach for the knife in her bundle. She put her hand instead over Vey's wrist and pressed once, a signal they had arranged without ever discussing it, and Vey went very still, and then very slowly began gathering his bread crumbs as if nothing in the world were wrong.

"Behind us," Wendell said, barely moving her lips. "Hedgerow. Half a mile, maybe less."

"Alone?"

"One. Small. Light step."

Vey exhaled through his nose. "Corvane doesn't send one. Corvane sends twelve and a wagon for the bodies."

"Then who?"

"Whoever it is," Vey said quietly, "has been following us since before Quist vanished. Which means they either want to help us or want to watch us fail. And I've known very few people in this army who enjoy watching."

The light was failing. Wendell calculated distances the way she calculated doses—by feel, by habit, with a margin left over for error. The next farmstead was dark. The wood beyond it began a quarter mile on and ran down to the river, thick with ash and alder, full of places a single person could vanish into or be vanished from.

"We don't run," she said. "Running tells them everything."

"No," Vey agreed. "We walk. Slowly. Like two cousins going nowhere important."

"And when they close?"

Vey's hand found a stone the size of a fist and weighed it, and Wendell saw the officer surface in him for a moment like a pike under still water. "Then we learn who they are," he said. "One way or another."

* * *

The figure did not close at the wood. She—it was a woman; Wendell could see it now in the last grey light, the set of the shoulders beneath a trooper's greatcoat cut for a bigger frame—did something worse. She stepped out onto the open road a hundred yards back and simply stood there, letting them see her, letting them choose.

Wendell turned and waited. Beside her, Vey shifted his weight off his bad leg and arranged his face into the blankness of a wounded civilian, which was nearly true.

The woman came on at a soldier's pace, not hurrying, boots ringing on the frozen mud. She was perhaps thirty, wind-burned, with dark hair chopped short in a way that had more to do with lice than fashion, and she wore the greatcoat of a hospital matron over an ordinary dress, which meant she had stolen one garment or been issued the wrong one, and either story interested Wendell very much.

Ten yards away she stopped. Her eyes went first to Vey—and Wendell watched something move through them, recognition and disbelief fighting for the same space—and then to Wendell, and there they stayed, and hardened into something Wendell knew.

Anger. But anger with grief underneath it, the way a river has stones underneath it.

"Nurse Eriksen," the woman said.

Not a question. Wendell felt the ground tilt under the words. "You have the advantage of me."

"I should think I do." The woman's jaw worked. "Six weeks you spent in the Marrow Creek ward. Six weeks you changed my dressings and counted my pulse and never once looked at me the way you look at everyone else—like you're writing my name down somewhere."

The voice landed before the face did. And then it landed: Ward Four, the fever cases, the young matron who had sat up nights with the dying sergeant in the corner cot and never once wept where anyone could see.

"Brann," Wendell said. "Nurse Brann."

"Matron Brann, until four days ago." She said it flatly, but the flatness cost her; Wendell saw the tremor come and be mastered. "Until I asked the record office why Private Hollis Brann was marked *deserted at Harrow Ford* when I held his last letter in my hand, posted three days before the battle, saying he was going into the line. Deserters don't write home from the firing step, Nurse Eriksen. So I asked. And asking got me dismissed within the hour, and followed within the day, and followed—" her eyes cut to Vey, and her voice dropped as if the word itself might be overheard— "followed all the way to you."

The road was silent. Somewhere off toward the river a dog barked twice and gave up.

"You followed me," Wendell said carefully, "because I asked questions too."

"I followed you because you asked *his* questions." Brann's hand went into her coat and came out—not with a weapon but with a folded newspaper, a provincial sheet three weeks old, and she opened it to a paragraph circled in pencil so hard the nib had torn the page. Wendell knew the paragraph. She had read it six times herself. The official account of Harrow Ford, the rout blamed on Captain Aldous Vey's unauthorized retreat, the names of the fallen appended in small type like an apology no one had bothered to make sincere.

"I read that name," Brann said, "and I thought: here is a dead man being made to carry it. And dead men are very useful, because they can't argue." She folded the paper along the torn crease, precisely, like someone folding a shroud. "And then a week later you're in the supply tent at Millrace filling out a requisition form, and you write the word *Corvane*, and your pen stops. Just stops. Mid-word. Like the ink itself was afraid. I've thought about that stopped pen every day since."

"You were at Millrace?" Vey said. It was the first thing he'd offered, and his voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of a man walking out onto ice.

"I was transferred there after Marrow Creek. Before they dismissed me." Brann looked at him fully then, and Wendell watched her do the arithmetic that Wendell had done in the lantern light weeks ago: the officer's posture under the rags, the hands, the way he scanned the horizon without appearing to. "You're him," she said. Not a question either. "You're Vey. You're supposed to be in the ground at Harrow Ford."

"A great many people are supposed to be in the ground at Harrow Ford," Vey said. "I'm told it's crowded."

Brann did not smile. She came two steps closer, and her voice cracked at last, the flatness giving way to the thing beneath it. "My brother is in that crowd. Or he isn't in it, which is worse—he's in some unmarked trench, or he walked away shell-shocked and was shot for it, or he's alive and someone erased him for reasons that have nothing to do with anything he did. I have written eleven letters to the record office. Eleven. Every one comes back stamped *no further information available.*" Her fists tightened on the folded paper. "So don't talk to me about crowds, Captain. Tell me why my brother's name is in the same paragraph as yours, and tell me why a nurse I trusted is traveling under forged papers toward the capital with a dead man, and then I'll decide whether I've been chasing a conspiracy or a miracle."

* * *

They gave it to her there on the road, because there was no other place and no more time. Wendell talked and Vey corrected, and between them the shape of it came out: Harrow Ford, the positions sold, the order signed by Quist in Corvane's name and buried under the rout, the ledger of erased men kept in cipher by a nurse with a good memory and a bad temper about lies.

At the ledger, Brann stopped walking entirely.

"Erased men," she repeated. "How many?"

"Two hundred and six," Wendell said. "As of the last entry."

"Names? Actual names?"

"Rank, name, regiment, date, and how the roll explains them away. Desertion, mostly. Some suicides. A few marked *struck, clerical error.*"

Brann stood very still in the darkening road, and Wendell saw her lips move once—*Hollis*—and saw her close her eyes and open them again, and understood that this woman had already searched the list in her mind, already begged silently to find the name there and dreaded finding it, both at once, the way families do.

"Is he in it?" Brann asked finally. Her voice was stripped down to boards and nails. "Private H. Brann, Fourth Voltigeurs. Is he one of your names?"

Wendell did not answer at once, because the answer mattered more than comfort, and lying to this woman would undo everything the truth might build. "There is a Hollis Brann," she said gently. "Fourth Voltigeurs, marked deserted the second day after the battle. But Matron Brann—I copied those entries from the rolls at Marrow Creek, and the clerk who kept them was sloppy. Half the dates are wrong. Some of the names are wrong. It may be your brother. It may be a man who shared his name. I won't tell you which, because I don't know."

Brann nodded, once, twice, absorbing it the way soldiers absorb wounds—compartmentalizing, stanching, deferring the bleeding to a quieter hour. "Then I'll come with you," she said, "and we'll find out together. At the tribunal. In front of everyone."

"It's not a pilgrimage," Vey said. "It's nine days through three checkpoints with Corvane's people already on the road. Quist was taken off a packet boat four hours after we left him. If they take you—"

"They've already taken everything from me, Captain." Brann said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "My commission. My post. My brother, one way or another. The only thing they haven't taken is my say in the matter, and I mean to spend it." She settled the stolen greatcoat on her shoulders and looked down the dark road toward the capital she could not yet see. "Besides. You need a third. A nurse and her wounded cousin travel better with a maiden aunt. Nobody searches maiden aunts."

For the first time in days, Wendell laughed—a short, surprised sound, startled out of her like a bird from cover—and beside her she felt Vey's shoulders drop a fraction of an inch, tension he probably didn't know he was carrying.

* * *

They walked until full dark and made camp in the alder wood, no fire, sharing Brann's oilcloth against the frost. Wendell lay awake longer than the others, listening to Brann's breathing—even, disciplined, the breathing of someone who had slept in hospitals through bombardment—and thinking about the ledger.

She had thought of it, these weeks, as hers. Her private accounting, her small rebellion of ink, her burden. Two hundred and six names in cipher under a floorboard, each one a theft she had witnessed and recorded alone. But Brann had read a newspaper paragraph and found her brother in it. Brann had been dismissed for asking. Brann had walked a hundred miles of winter road on nothing but a stopped pen.

The names had families. Of course they did. Every erased man had a Brann somewhere—a sister writing eleven letters, a mother setting a place at table for a boy the army called a coward. The ledger had never been Wendell's. It had only been in Wendell's keeping, the way a lantern is in a keeper's hand while all around it people stumble in the same dark, looking for the same light.

Tomorrow, or the day after, they would reach Fenlow and the quarantine camp, and the second checkpoint, and whatever waited there. But tonight there were three of them instead of two, and the cold seemed fractionally less absolute.

She was nearly asleep when Brann spoke from the darkness, very quietly, as if to the fire they weren't allowed.

"Eriksen. Your ledger. The Hollis Brann entry—when we get where we're going, I want to see the original. Not the copy. The original. In your hand."

"You'll see it," Wendell murmured. "Every page."

A pause. Then, softer, almost lost under the wind in the alders:

"Good. Because if some clerk in the capital says *no further information available* one more time, I swear to God I will burn that building to its foundations, and I'd rather have your book to hold instead."

Wendell slept. And some hours later, deep in the black before dawn, Brann's hand on her shoulder brought her up out of sleep with a lantern shuttered to a slit shining in her eyes and Brann's voice low and urgent in her ear:

"Wake up. Wake up both of you—there's cavalry on the river road, coming from Fenlow. A lot of it. And they're stopping every traveler, and asking for a woman nurse traveling with a box."
