## 12. What Corvane Buys

The messenger came at dusk, as messengers do—when the light fails and men are tired enough to want any ending, even a bought one.

Vey saw him first from the doorway of the ferryman's shed where they had taken a room for the night. The river ran wide and gray below them, carrying the last barges down toward the capital, and the road above it stood empty except for one rider coming up through the alders at an easy canter. No urgency in him. That was the first thing Vey noted, the old officer's habit asserting itself before he could stop it: read the horse, read the seat, read the man. A soldier rides hard when he carries orders. This one rode like a gentleman paying calls.

He was better dressed than any courier had a right to be on this road—good boots, a gray coat cut close in the military fashion but without insignia, gloves that had never held a rein for longer than an afternoon's pleasure. He dismounted at the fence, tied off with two neat turns, and looked at the shed with the mild appraisal of a man checking his watch against a train he already knew would come.

"He's back," Vey said.

Wendell looked up from the basin where she was washing bandages—or rather, he corrected himself, from the basin where Eriksen was washing bandages; he was still learning which name to carry in his mouth, and it mattered here more than anywhere. She followed his gaze to the road and her hands went still in the water.

"That's not the same man."

"No," Vey said. "That's worse. The first one was hired eyes. This one is staff."

Brann, who had been mending a harness strap by the stove, laid it down without being told to. Three days out of Fenlow and the troopers' sweep behind them, three days of ditches and hedgerows and one long freezing night in a drainage culvert while a patrol passed close enough that they could hear the troopers complaining about their boots—and still none of them spoke above a murmur unless the river covered it. They had learned each other's silences. Brann moved now to the window without being asked, and Wendell set the bandage knife down within reach of her hand with a small deliberate motion, and Vey understood, with something that was not quite comfort and not quite pain, that these two people were prepared to fight and die over a dead man's name.

"Through the front," he said quietly. "Let him think we're simple. Let him think we're flattered."

* * *

The man knocked at the door like a guest. Two raps, gloved, patient. When Wendell opened it he swept off his hat and inclined his head with such practiced courtesy that Vey, standing in the shadow behind her, felt the years fall away—he knew that manner. It was the manner of the adjutants and aides around Corvane's table, the men who poured the general's wine and carried the general's paper and did the general's arithmetic in other people's blood.

"Good evening," the man said. "I'm looking for a Mr. Rill. Traveling with his cousin, a nurse." His eyes went past Wendell's shoulder and found Vey without hurry, without surprise. "I believe I've found him."

"You've found a sick man and his family," Wendell said. Her voice was flat, weary, exactly right—the voice of a woman who has been on the road too long and wants only a bed. "We've no money to spare and nothing worth your trouble."

"Money is precisely why I've come." The messenger smiled. It was a good smile, kept in good condition like his boots. "Might I come in? What I have to say shouldn't be said on a doorstep. And it concerns your cousin very particularly."

Wendell didn't move. It was Vey who spoke past her, low and rough, using Rill's country burr: "Let him in, cousin. Men like this don't go away because you keep the door shut. They just stand there getting cold, and then you're rude as well as poor."

The messenger laughed at that, genuinely pleased, and stepped inside.

He took off his gloves finger by finger, warming his hands at their stove as if he owned a share in it, and accepted the tea Brann poured him with a nod of thanks. He sat where he was invited and crossed one well-booted ankle over his knee. Everything about him was designed to say: *I am comfortable. You should consider becoming comfortable too.*

"I'll be quick, since I can see you're all tired of strangers," he said. "I represent a person whose name I won't insult you by speaking aloud. That person has asked me to convey certain terms to Mr. Rill. Or Captain Vey." He let the name sit in the room like smoke. "Whichever he prefers to be called tonight."

Nobody answered him. The stove ticked. Outside, the river muttered over its stones.

"The terms are these," the messenger went on, unhurried. "A full pardon. Not a quiet one—a public one, printed in the army register, restoring rank and reputation, signed by the proper authorities. An amnesty for everything since Harrow Ford, including the matter of the desertion, which would be struck from the record entirely, as though it had never been entered." He sipped his tea. "In addition: a commission. Major, with seniority backdated to the Ford, which matters for pension and precedence. A posting somewhere pleasant—Corvane's influence runs far enough that the posting could be named. Somewhere with a garrison, a decent house, a climate that agrees with a man who has spent a year sleeping rough."

He paused, and the pause was masterly, and Vey hated how well he remembered these pauses, how he had once heard them used on frightened subalterns and stubborn quartermasters and once, memorably, on a whole room of officers deciding what to do about a village that had stopped feeding the army.

"And in exchange?" Vey asked. His own voice surprised him—steady, uninflected, the captain's voice.

"In exchange, Mr. Rill goes to the posting. Alone." The messenger set down his cup. "And a certain ledger, and whatever copies of it may exist, and whatever papers have been gathered in support of it, go into the river. And a nurse of no regiment returns to her hospital and says nothing further, and is never troubled again. Everyone lives. Everyone is restored. Everyone's name comes back to them." He smiled again, warmer now, the salesman's warmth. "You'd be surprised, Captain, how many men have been offered less and taken it faster."

* * *

Later—years later, perhaps, if he lived to have later—Vey would try to remember the exact shape of the temptation, and find that it had none he could describe. It was not a moment of weakness. It was worse. It was reasonable.

He sat there with the tea cooling in his hands and let himself do the arithmetic, because he was an honest man even in treachery, and honest men count honestly. Eleven days until the tribunal. Three checkpoints between here and the capital, one of them already passed by luck and one sergeant's conscience, and Corvane evidently knowing every mile of their route. Brann had been taken once already and had escaped through a latrine trench; her luck would not hold twice. Wendell had no legal existence that Corvane's lawyers could not dissolve with a stroke. And Vey himself—if they reached the tribunal steps at all, they would reach them with soldiers waiting, because the law wanted him for desertion, and the law's memory for a hanging offense was longer than its appetite for justice.

Against that: a pardon. Printed. Public. His name given back to him in ink that everyone would have to read. Rank restored, seniority backdated—backdated to Harrow Ford, of all things, so that the disaster itself would become the foundation stone of his career, the rout that killed four hundred men promoted retroactively into his qualification for major. He would live in a garrison town with a decent house. He would grow old under his own name.

And all it cost was the ledger. Four hundred names in Wendell's careful hand, the ones the rolls had swallowed—marked deserted, marked suicide, marked nothing at all, simply gone, erased so that supply figures would balance and a general's reports would shine. All it cost was walking away from the only map of the crime that still existed, and letting the river take it, the same river that had taken the men themselves.

He looked at Wendell. She was watching him—not anxiously, he realized. She was watching him the way she watched a wound she had decided not to touch yet. Letting it show him what it was.

He looked at Brann. Her jaw was set, but her eyes weren't hard; they were grieving, already, and he understood that she had done this arithmetic before he had, days ago, maybe at the second checkpoint or in the culvert, and had arrived at her answer alone and in silence, and was now sitting in that silence waiting for him to catch up.

And he thought of Quist. Poor drunk ruined Quist, who had agreed to testify and lasted one night before vanishing into the dark and leaving a bloodied collar button on the floorboards. Quist had presumably been made an offer too. Perhaps a smaller one. Perhaps just a threat. It hadn't mattered which. The machinery worked at every price point.

"There's a question I'd like to ask," Vey said.

"By all means."

"The men at Harrow Ford." He kept his voice level with an effort that cost him more than the effort of staying silent ever had. "The four hundred. In your master's terms—in the terms you've been authorized to offer—what were they?"

The messenger considered him with real attention for the first time, and Vey saw that beneath the polish the man was not stupid, and understood, in some recess of himself, that the stupidity was all on the surface, deliberately maintained, the way a duelist maintains a lazy guard.

"They were casualties," the messenger said gently. "Of a regrettable action. Which is why the pardon is so generous, Captain. Grief makes men unreasonable. Reasonable men are owed consideration." He leaned forward slightly. "You were there. You know how it happened. You know that no order of yours could have saved them, and no testimony will bring them back. The tribunal can hang General Corvane, if it comes to that—it won't, but suppose it does. Will the dead rise? Will the Ford give them back? You'll have traded your life, and the lady's, and Mrs.—the other lady's—for a gesture. Whereas what's on the table tonight is a life. Yours. Whole. Under your own name, which I understand—" and here the man's voice softened with what might have been genuine feeling, which was somehow the cruelest cut of all— "you have missed."

The stove ticked. The river ran. And Aldous Vey, officially dead for a year, sat in a ferryman's shed with a cup of cold tea in his hands and discovered the exact weight and shape of the thing being sold to him, and found that it was himself.

That was the temptation. Not the pardon, not the commission, not the house in the garrison town. Those were the wrapping. The gift inside was simpler and more terrible: permission. Permission to stop. Permission to be a living man again instead of a name in a nurse's ledger. Permission to let the dead stay dead and the erased stay erased, and to walk away whole, with everyone's blessing, everyone reasonable agreeing that everyone reasonable had done everything anyone reasonably could.

Every exhausted bone in his body wanted it. He had slept in ditches. He had eaten from pig troughs. He had watched his own obituary read aloud in a tavern by a corporal who called him a coward and spat, and had kept his hood up and his peace. A year of being nobody. And here, at last, was somebody offering to make him somebody again, and all it required was that four hundred actual nobodies stay nobodies forever.

"No," Vey said.

The messenger sighed, softly, like a doctor hearing a symptom he had expected.

"Captain—"

"No." He set the cup down and stood, and his knees ached, and his voice came out lower and steadier than he had any right to expect. "Tell your master I received him courteously and refuse him completely. Tell him the ledger isn't mine to sell—it belongs to the names in it, and they've already paid the price in full, and I'll not cheat them of the only payment left. Tell him—" and here Vey found, rising in him, not fury but something colder and older, the thing that had made men follow him at the Ford right up until the order that killed them, the thing Corvane had never possessed and therefore feared in others— "tell him I died at Harrow Ford. He wrote it himself; he should stand by his work. And the man who walked out of that river has no rank to purchase, no name to restore, and no fear of him left to spend."

The messenger rose also, unhurried, and put his gloves back on, finger by finger.

"You understand," he said, not unkindly, "what happens now. The pardon expires at dawn. After that you're simply a deserter, and deserters are hanged wherever they're caught, and the catching has already been arranged. There will be no tribunal scene, Captain. No dramatic reading of names. You will not reach the steps. My master has decided how this ends, and he has decided it ends quietly, in a ditch, with a report of a fugitive shot resisting arrest. The law is only useful to men while it's cheaper than the alternative."

"Then he's finally told the truth," Vey said. "Even if only to you."

The messenger paused, hat in hand, and studied Vey with something that in a better man might have been respect.

"He said you'd say something like that," he admitted. "He said to tell you: *sentiment is expensive, and someone always pays the bill.*"

"He's right," Vey said. "It's the four hundred who paid his bills for two years. It ends."

At the door the messenger stopped, and turned half back, and for a moment the polish slipped and Vey saw the functionary beneath, tired, a man doing a job in the dark on a cold road, and that glimpse frightened Vey more than anything else the man had said—because it meant Corvane's machine now sent ordinary men, and ordinary men follow whoever pays, and there were so many more of them than there were of the four hundred.

"For what it's worth," the messenger said, "he hoped you'd take it. He doesn't enjoy this part."

"Which part?"

"The part," the messenger said, "where the offer changes."

He put on his hat, went out into the dark, and untied his horse without hurry, and the last Vey saw of him was his silhouette at the fence line, pausing to look back at the shed once—not at Vey, he realized afterward. At the cart. At the coffin they believed was still riding with them.

Then the hoofbeats faded toward the capital, and the river ran on, and the night closed over the road like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

They sat a long while without lighting more than one candle.

"He'll send men tonight," Brann said at last. "Not tomorrow. Tonight. The offer was the courtesy; the courtesy is finished."

"Yes," Vey said. He found he was calm—dangerously calm, the calm of the Ford, the calm that came when the situation passed beyond anxiety into arithmetic. "We move within the hour. Not along the river—up, through the hills, the drover's track. It costs us half a day but he'll have watchers on the water road."

"He knows about the coffin," Wendell said quietly.

"He suspects. He doesn't know." Vey looked at her. "There's a difference, and eleven days from now it may be the only difference left. If he knew, he wouldn't have sent a man to bargain. He'd have sent men to burn this shed with us in it and sift the ashes after."

Wendell nodded slowly, and he watched her arrive at the conclusion he had already reached, and hated that she had to reach it at all: the coffin could not ride with them anymore. Too visible, too known, too much the thing a watcher looks for. From here the pages would travel sewn flat and silent, distributed, hidden in the lining of ordinary luggage and ordinary coats, and the coffin itself would continue to the capital empty—decoy and lure both, drawing eyes that belonged on them.

"He'll stop offering now," Brann said. "What comes next isn't terms."

"No," Vey agreed. "Next comes the ditch and the report of a fugitive shot resisting arrest. He told me so, more or less. He's stopped pretending the law is his instrument. Now it's only his costume." He picked up the candle and held it, and the small flame threw his shadow huge across the boards of the ferryman's shed, a dead captain grown suddenly taller than the room. "So we go to the capital and take the costume off him in front of witnesses."

Wendell was already packing, quick and silent, her hands sure in the candlelight. But at the door she paused, and looked back at him, and asked the only question that mattered.

"The offer," she said. "Was it ever truly going to be honored? The pardon, the commission—all of it?"

Vey thought of Quist's collar button. He thought of four hundred men ordered into a ford that their own general had already sold.

"It was honored every day for two years," he said. "For Corvane. By everyone else he's ever bought." He snuffed the candle. "Saddle up. We've ten days left, and he knows every road but one."
