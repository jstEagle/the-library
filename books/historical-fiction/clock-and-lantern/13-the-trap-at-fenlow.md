## 13. The Trap at Fenlow

The mistake announced itself as a smell before it announced itself as a sound: woodsmoke where there should have been none, coming from the direction of the quartermaster's tent at an hour when no honest fire was lit. Wendell Eriksen lifted his head from the thin pillow in the nurses' lean-to and listened to the winter dark of Fenlow quarantine camp, and understood, with the cold certainty of a surgeon feeling a wound turn septic beneath a clean dressing, that something had begun.

They had stayed one night too long. That was the arithmetic of it, simple and unforgivable. Vey's face had been known by one old sergeant among hundreds of strangers, and mercy—Wendell had believed this since Marrow Creek, had built a whole fragile theology upon it—mercy was supposed to be the one thing the war could not requisition. But mercy left fingerprints. The sergeant had saluted with tears standing in his eyes, and somewhere behind him a corporal had seen the salute, or heard the name spoken low, or simply noticed that a gaunt man in a nurse's cart had passed through the inspection line without being searched, and had done what soldiers in Corvane's army did with such knowledge: he had written it down and sent it up the chain, where it traveled faster than any tribunal summons ever could.

Now the camp was waking to torchlight.

Wendell was dressed and moving before the first shouts came, boots half-laced, the cipher copy of the ledger already bound against his ribs beneath the nurse's coat where it lived night and day, warm from his body, stiff with sewn oilcloth. Brann was awake too—he could see her silhouette through the canvas partition, already pulling her coat on, her movements quick and unpanicked, a woman who had been woken by soldiers before and had learned the grammar of it.

"Cart," she said, not a question but an assignment.

"The cart." His voice sounded far away to him, level and thin. "Vey?"

"Gone to water the mule ten minutes ago." Brann's jaw tightened. She did not say *if they've taken him*. Neither of them would say it. Some sentences were load-bearing walls; speak them and the whole house came down.

The torches were moving along the palisade now in a double line, and beneath the crackle of pitch Wendell could hear the sound he had learned to dread at every checkpoint and had never once stopped dreading: the particular cadence of orders given for a sweep. Not a search—a sweep. A search hoped to find; a sweep intended to take everything, sort it later, apologize to no one. Boots in the mud in the rhythm of men spread wide and driving forward, driving whatever they found ahead of them like beaters driving game.

He thought, absurdly, of the wounded. Old habits. There were forty men in the fever tents behind him, men with typhus and trench-rot and the gray cough of the camps, and none of them would be spared a moment's terror by whatever was about to happen, because terror was free and the army had never once paid for anything it could take. Then he put the thought down the way he set down an instrument after use, and made himself think only of the three things that mattered: the ledger, Vey, and Brann. In that order, though he hated himself a little for the order.

* * *

They met Vey at the mule line, all three converging in the blue-black gap between the fodder shed and the fence, and for a moment nobody spoke. Up close, by the growing orange wash of the torches, Vey looked older than he had on the river road. The desertion had hollowed him over the year; the last two days had filled the hollows with something harder.

"They're not asking for papers," Vey said quietly. "Look at the perimeter. They've sealed it first. That's not a document check, that's a net."

"Then we cut the net before it closes." Wendell heard his own voice do the thing it did in triage—go flat, go efficient, go somewhere the fear could be stored instead of felt. "Brann, the cart's under tarp by the farrier's. The decoy satchel is in the false bottom, the one with the cipher pages I salted with errors. Take it toward the latrine trenches. If they stop you, let them find it."

Brann went very still. In the weeks on the road she had become the third leg of a stool; you forgot a stool had legs until one shifted. "And you'll have the real ones."

"In the coffin." He said it fast, because saying it slowly would let him hear how mad it was. The coffin had come into the camp two days earlier with a dead drover, one of the camp's own laborers taken by the fever, and it sat in the mortuary tent awaiting the supply wagon that ran corpses to the capital twice a week—the army's grimmest regular service, its schedule posted and punctual as a train. Wendell had helped prepare the body himself. He knew the coffin's lining the way he knew his own coat pockets.

Vey caught it immediately, of course. He had been an officer; he thought in contingencies. "You want to ride with it."

"I want the pages to ride with it. Whether I do is another matter." Torches were spilling into the main avenue now, and with them voices—harsh, official, ordering the fever tents sealed and the occupants counted. Counted. Names read against lists. Every name in this camp was a door the troopers could open, and one of those doors had *Vey* written on it in an old sergeant's guilty hand.

"There's no way out of Fenlow except the gate," Vey said, "and the gate has a garrison, and the garrison has orders, and I am the reason for those orders. If I run alone, they follow me and never look at either of you. It's the oldest trade in war, Wendell, and it's mine to make."

"No," Wendell said. The word came out with more force than he'd sent with it, and he felt Brann glance at him through the dark. "No trades. We didn't carry this thing eleven days down a frozen river road so you could spend yourself buying us an hour. You are going to sit in front of that tribunal and say the words only your mouth can say. Everything else is paper. Paper can be seized. You can't be replaced."

For a heartbeat Vey looked at him the way men look at a map when the terrain has changed overnight—as if recalculating everything. Then the torchlight reached them fully, and there was no more time for looking at anyone.

* * *

The sweep hit the nurses' quarter first, which told Wendell the report had been specific. They did not roust the fever tents, did not turn over the kitchens; they came straight for the lean-to where a nurse named Eriksen slept, and they came with a lieutenant who held a lantern high enough to light the whole interior and a list held low enough that no one else could read it.

"By order of the Provost Marshal's office," the lieutenant said, in the voice of a man reciting something he'd been given an hour ago, "this camp is closed. All persons will assemble in the yard. Possessions will be laid out for inspection."

Wendell stood in the doorway with his sleeves rolled, having arranged in the ninety seconds available the only costume he had: the busy professional, mid-duty, offended by interruption. "I have four men in the isolation tent with fever, Lieutenant. Two of them won't survive being moved to a yard in this cold. If you want their deaths on your report, move them. Otherwise my possessions are where possessions always are, and your men are welcome to trip over them."

It nearly worked. It worked for perhaps forty seconds—the lieutenant's eyes flicking past him to the cots, the basins, the ordinary debris of nursing—and then a sergeant behind him said, "Sir, there's a cart at the farrier's, tarp over it," and the lantern swung, and the whole careful architecture of the moment collapsed like a table with one leg kicked away.

They took the yard by squads. Wendell stood in the freezing open with thirty other civilians—drovers, farriers, laundresses, the camp's small economy of the war's margins—while troopers went through their belongings with the thoroughness of men paid by the find. The coffins in the mortuary tent they passed entirely, twice, and both times Wendell watched them pass and kept his breathing even. A coffin was the one thing in a quarantine camp that asked nothing and offered nothing; death was the war's most trusted courier, and no one searched its bags.

Then a trooper in the farrier's yard gave a shout of triumph, and the false-bottom satchel came up out of the cart tarp into the lantern light, and Wendell's stomach dropped through the frozen ground—not because they had found the decoy, but because Brann was standing three feet from the finding of it.

She had done exactly as he'd asked. She had walked the satchel toward the latrines and allowed herself to be caught near it, so that the seizure would attach to her and not to the cart, not to the story of a nurse and her wounded cousin. And now she stood with her hands visible and her chin up while the lieutenant knelt over the satchel and drew out page after page of close-written cipher, and Wendell watched comprehension move across the officer's face like weather.

"Who wrote these?" the lieutenant demanded.

"A dead woman taught me," Brann said. "Does it matter?"

"It matters to a firing squad." The lieutenant stood, pages clutched against his chest the way a man carries fire. "Take her. Take the cart. Search the coffin tents—" and here Wendell's heart performed a maneuver he would remember for years, a single hard contraction and then a terrible calm— "*after* the muster. Nobody leaves the gate until every soul in this camp is accounted for."

They marched Brann across the yard past him. She did not look at him—of course she didn't; she was too good for that—but as she passed, in the half-second when their shoulders might have brushed in a crowd, she said, very softly, in the accentless tone of a woman complaining about the cold: "Coffin rides at dawn. Wagon driver owes everyone money."

Then she was gone between the torches, and the cost of the whole enterprise stood up inside Wendell's chest and made itself known. This was the price. He had known prices were coming; he had carried the ledger for two years knowing that names cost money and money bought soldiers, but knowing a price and paying one were different countries, and he had just crossed the border into the second one, leaving Brann behind in the first, in custody, because of his arithmetic.

* * *

They assembled the camp in the yard and counted it, and the counting took an hour, and during that hour Aldous Vey stood four places to Wendell's left with a blanket around his shoulders and a cousin's dull stoicism on his face, and Wendell did not once look at him directly. This was the hardest discipline of the night. Every instinct screamed to check him, to confirm him, to stand between him and the list; instead Wendell stared at the mud and rehearsed the cousin's history they had built on the river road—how Vey had taken shrapnel at Kessel's Mill in the hip and thigh, how he was slow on cold mornings, how he'd been discharged to family care and was being moved south where it was warmer. A lie needs rehearsal the way a wound needs dressing. He dressed it over and over.

A captain came through the assembled ranks of civilians with the lieutenant's captured pages in a leather wallet and a photograph in his glove. Wendell saw the photograph for half a second when the captain opened the wallet to compare faces down the line: a younger Vey in full uniform, pre-Harrow Ford, mustached and confident, the face of a man who had not yet been killed on paper. The captain matched it against farmer, against drover, against laundress, and arrived at Vey, and looked, and paused.

"Cold tonight," Vey said, before the silence could acquire meaning. He said it hoarsely, in the flattened regional drawl they had practiced, and he coughed after it, a deep wet congested cough he had learned from the fever tents, and he shifted his weight off his right hip with the authentic clumsiness of a genuinely injured man. "Beg pardon, sir. Chest's been bad since Kessel's Mill."

The captain looked at him for five seconds. Wendell counted them. In those five seconds hung the entire enterprise—the ledger, the testimony, the eleven days, the fall of a general—and they were suspended on nothing but a cough and a limp and the fact that the photograph showed a healthy man and the man in front of the captain was convincingly ruined.

"Move along," the captain said finally, and moved on down the line, and Wendell learned in that moment that the body could age five years in five seconds and survive it.

But the captain did not leave the yard. He stood by the fire pit with the wallet under his arm and gave new orders, and Wendell, listening with the peripheral attention of a man who had spent years pretending not to hear officers, heard the shape of the trap assembling itself around them: the gate sealed until dawn; the coffin wagon searched before departure, thoroughly, lining and all; the prisoner—the woman—held for escort to the provost marshal at Harrow town; and a rider already gone ahead to the capital gate with a description of *a nurse traveling with a box*.

That last phrase. *A nurse with a box.* Someone above the captain's pay grade had distilled them into six words, and six words would travel faster than any cart.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it the wagon, and with it the last hour in which anything could still be arranged. The camp's dead drover lay in his coffin under a canvas shelter, and Wendell, whose professional services no sweep had thought to cancel—dead men still needed washing, wrapping, nailing; the war's paperwork demanded it even when the war's soldiers were searching for spies—worked over the body with the wagon driver standing by, an old man with a rheumy eye and the profound indifference of someone who had hauled soldiers and corpses in alternating shipments for three years and found little difference between them.

"You'll want to nail it proper," the driver said. "Roads to the capital are rough."

"I'll nail it proper." Wendell worked the bottom board loose with a chisel edge and a patience he did not feel. Inside the coffin's shallow space, between the straw bed and the plank, he drew out the oilcloth packet that had ridden there since the previous midnight, sewn into the lining seam with waxed thread from the medical stores—sixty-one pages of cipher, sixty-one erased names rendered in a code of his own devising, the true copy, the real ledger, the thing for which a woman now sat in a guard tent and a general's riders combed the countryside. He weighed it in his hands. Two years of nights at Marrow Creek. Every entry made by candle stub with his ear tuned for boots.

Then he turned the coffin's lining back, split the seam further with a needle, slid the packet home into the channel between lining and wood where no casual search would reach and even a thorough one would need time and knives, and sewed it shut with stitches so fine that grief itself could hardly have found the seam. His hands shook only once, when he thought of Brann's face passing him in the torchlight, saying *coffin rides at dawn* like a woman giving him directions to a church social.

Outside the mortuary tent, the camp seethed. Troopers were pulling the nurses' lean-to apart board by board. The lieutenant stood over a burning heap in the yard—the contents of the false-bottom satchel, or rather the decoy pages Wendell had salted among the real-looking ones, now feeding a bonfire with a soldier's officious efficiency, because papers seized in a sweep were copied and burned and the copies sent onward, and the copies of a cipher no one could yet read would take days to matter. Let them burn. Let them send their unreadable copies galloping to the capital. Every hour their cryptographers bent over Wendell's deliberate errors was an hour the true pages gained.

He nailed the coffin shut with measured blows. Each one rang in the frosty air like a bell with the clapper half-muffled, and each one said what he could not: *forgive me, forgive me, forgive me*—to Brann in her guard tent, to Vey waiting beyond the wire with a mule and a plan, to the dead drover sharing his last journey with sixty-one stolen names, to the self he had been at Marrow Creek, the quiet man with the floorboard and the candle stub, for whom the worst thing in the world had been an officer's odd questions about paperwork.

The driver climbed onto the box. The gate detail waved the wagon toward the search point, where a sergeant with a crowbar was waiting with instructions to open everything, and beyond the sergeant the road ran north through the frost fog toward the capital, toward the tribunal, toward eleven days that had somehow become nine.

Wendell walked beside the wagon wheel with his medical bag on his shoulder and the driver's papers in his hand, and he did not look back at Fenlow, and he did not look forward past the crowbar. He looked only at the next hundred yards, which contained a search, a risk, and—if the seam held and the luck held and Brann's sacrifice held—one coffin's passage through the wall of a general's making.

The sergeant raised the crowbar over the lid Wendell had nailed shut an hour earlier, and grinned at him with bad teeth, and said:

"Orders are everything gets opened, friend. Even the quiet passengers."
