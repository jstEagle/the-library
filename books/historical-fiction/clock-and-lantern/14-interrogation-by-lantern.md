## 14. Interrogation by Lantern

They took her spectacles first.

Brann had worn the same wire-rimmed things for eleven years, since her brother Tam had won them off a card sharp in a river town whose name she'd forgotten, and losing them now was like losing a small window onto the world. The trooper who lifted them from her face did it with an apology in his shoulders but not his mouth—a boy of perhaps nineteen with chapped hands and frostbitten ears, folding the spectacles into his breast pocket as though they were contraband of war.

"For safekeeping," he said.

"Everything is," Brann said. "That's what this army keeps telling me."

He didn't laugh. He pushed her forward between two mounted men toward the long canvas tent that had been raised at the edge of the swept camp at Fenlow, its lanterns already lit against the early dark, its flaps tied shut except at the front. Behind her, where the quarantine camp had been—where Wendell and Vey had been standing only minutes before—there was shouting, the clatter of bayonets fixed to rifles, someone's voice cracking on a word she couldn't hear over the wind coming down off the water. The wind smelled of latrines and river ice and burnt coffee. It would be the last ordinary smell she would know for some time.

Inside the tent it was warm, which was worse. Warmth meant they intended to keep her.

* * *

The man across the table was not a monster, and that was what made him frightening.

He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the neat, unremarkable face of a man who had spent thirty years writing other people's orders down. His coat was quartermaster's wool, unadorned. On the table before him lay a single lantern with a green shade, a ledger of his own—heavier than hers, bound in black oilcloth—and a tin cup of coffee he never drank. When the guards brought her in and set her on a stool, he waited until they had gone all the way outside before he spoke, and when he spoke it was quietly, almost kindly, the way a man talks to a horse he does not want spooked.

"Sister Miriam Brann," he said, reading from a paper. "Of the Marrow Creek field hospital. Attached there how long?"

"Two years," Brann said. It was even true.

"You were found traveling with a wounded man and another woman. Papers say cousins."

"Papers are expensive things to argue with."

"They are." He turned the paper around so she could see it—the forged transfer Wendell had written, the careful hand that had fooled one lieutenant at a toll bridge and would fool no one tonight. "This one fooled Lieutenant Ashe. I've read his report. He believed you. That says something about your friend's skill, or about his youth." He set it aside. "Which brings us to the man."

"What man?" Brann said. "You took him from us an hour ago. You tell me."

"The wounded cousin." The gray man folded his hands. "Sister Brann, I am going to tell you something that most interrogators would not tell you, because I find it saves time. I do not care about you. I care very little about the woman. I care about the man you were traveling with, and I care about a certain book of names that came out of Marrow Creek, and if you give me those two things cleanly, you will sleep tonight in a heated tent with your spectacles back, and tomorrow you'll be released at the crossroads with a pass signed by me. I have signed eleven such passes in my career. No one has ever regretted taking one."

"That's a remarkable record," Brann said.

"It is," he agreed, without irony. "Because no one has ever been foolish enough to refuse."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside a horse complained in its traces, and the wind carried the sound of men still searching, canvas flapping, boots in frozen mud. Brann thought of Wendell's face in the last moment before the troopers came—Wendell, who never looked afraid, going suddenly and completely still, like a hare that has heard the dog before the dog knows it. She thought of the way Wendell's hand had moved, so small a motion she'd nearly missed it: a touch to the left breast of the coat, where nothing lived anymore except the shape of pages. Whatever happened tonight, that hand had told her everything she needed. *It's not on me. It's somewhere they haven't looked.*

"I don't know anything about a book," Brann said.

"No?"

"I'm a nurse. I know dressings, dosages, and which officers lie about their pain. Beyond that my education is limited."

The gray man nodded slowly, as though she had said something worth noting, and opened his black ledger, and began, in a manner of speaking, to work.

* * *

He worked for three hours.

Not with fists—that was the thing she would remember afterward, in the nights when she could not sleep and could not explain why, since no blow had ever landed. He worked with patience, with the clock, with the particular cruelty of a man who understands that endurance is a muscle and every muscle fails. He asked the same questions in different orders. He asked about Marrow Creek's supply ledgers and then about its mortality rolls and then about its supply ledgers again, and each time he phrased it slightly differently so that answering honestly once did not arm her to answer honestly twice. He let silences grow until they had weight and texture, until the silence itself seemed to press on her chest like a hand.

He knew things. That was the part that frightened her most. He knew there had been a colonel's visit to Marrow Creek in the winter. He knew Wendell kept a private habit of copying forms—"your handwriting appears on documents you had no duty to write, Sister; you copy things, don't you, it's a compulsion of yours"—and he knew, though he said it lightly, as if it were gossip, that the second woman, the quiet one with the soldier's posture, was not anybody's cousin.

"We believe he is a deserter from Harrow Ford," the gray man said, somewhere past midnight, watching her over the green-shaded lantern. "We believe he has been alive this whole year while his name sits on a memorial stone. Do you understand what that makes him, Sister? Not a criminal. A wound in the army's side. An embarrassment with legs. There are men above me who want him very badly, and men above them who want him more, and none of them, I promise you, care whether you rot in a stockade for obstructing them."

"And what do you want?" Brann asked. Her throat was dry. They had given her no water in three hours, and she understood that too—it was arithmetic, all of it, the body treated as a lock to be picked.

"I want to go home to my wife in the spring," the gray man said simply. "So I want this finished quickly. Tell me about the book."

"There is no book."

"There is a book." For the first time something moved behind his face—not anger; fatigue. "There is always a book. Men who keep accounts cannot help themselves. I keep accounts. You keep accounts—I've seen the forms. The question is only whether yours ends up in mine, or whether we spend another four hours discovering together that you can't tell me what I already know."

Brann sat with her hands in her lap. Under the table, where the lantern light didn't reach, her thumbnail worked at a splinter in the stool's edge, worrying it loose grain by grain, because her hands needed a task or they would shake and he would see it. She thought about Tam. She thought about the letter that had come, or rather the letter that had not come, in the autumn of '61, and the officer at the depot who had told her, with genuine sympathy, that Private Tamasin Brann was listed as *absent without leave*, and how the word *absent* had done its work on her family like acid—how her mother had died the next winter with the shame of it still in her mouth, unable to believe her own daughter a coward, unable to prove otherwise, because the roll said *absent* and the roll was the law.

She had started asking questions after that. Quiet ones. That was how she had come to notice, months later at Marrow Creek, the way Wendell Eriksen's eyes moved down casualty lists like a man counting money that had been stolen from him.

"My brother is in somebody's ledger," Brann said at last.

The gray man looked up.

"Tamasin Brann, Fourth Rifles," she said. "Reported absent without leave, October of '61. Except I've talked to a man who saw him fall at Cane Run, and I've talked to a woman who buried him, and neither of them exists in any record your army will show me. So when you ask me about books of names, Captain—Captain, is it? You're wearing no insignia, which means either you're a spy or you're important enough not to need one—you should understand that I've been giving you exactly what you gave my brother. Nothing."

The silence that followed was long. The lantern hissed. And then the gray man closed his ledger, laced his fingers together, and did the last cruel thing of the night, the one she could not have prepared for because it was not a cruelty aimed at her at all.

"You misunderstand your situation," he said. "You think this ends in a courtroom. You think your captain friend will stand a trial, that there will be a tribunal, that names will be read and blame assigned and justice—somehow—done. Sister Brann." He leaned forward into the light, and up close she could see he hadn't slept in days himself, that his kindness and his cruelty came from the same exhausted place. "There will be no trial. General Corvane cannot afford one; too many witnesses, too much paper, too many chances. As of this morning there is a standing order regarding the deserter formerly known as Aldous Vey: he is to be shot on identification. Summary execution under field authority. No tribunal. No record beyond a line in a report that will say he resisted arrest." He paused. "Your testimony cannot save a man who will never be tried. Consider what your silence buys you, then."

The world went very still, the way it does in the moment after a shell lands nearby and before the ringing starts.

*Shot on identification.* Not hanged, not tried, not even the pretense of law—she had braced herself all night against the machinery of courts and tribunals, and now the machinery wasn't even bothering to turn. They would put a bullet in Vey behind some guardhouse wall and write *resisted* in a book, and that line would sit in a file forever, official and clean, and no ledger she helped keep would ever reach far enough down to correct it. She thought of Vey's face in the firelight on the river road, the night he'd finally told her about Harrow Ford—not the strategy, just the men, the names he still carried—and she understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that the law she had appealed to all her life, the law that had written *absent* beside her brother's name, was not merely failing here. It was being used as the weapon. Justice lived somewhere else entirely—in a nurse's cipher, in a sergeant's hesitation at a checkpoint, in whatever stubborn private accounting Wendell carried against all sense—and the law was the thing hunting it.

She had been silent out of loyalty. Now she was silent out of contempt, and contempt holds longer.

"I have nothing to tell you," Brann said.

The gray man held her eyes for a moment. Then he sighed, and stood, and buttoned his coat with the weary competence of a man closing a shop.

"Hold her until morning," he said to the guard at the flap. "If she changes her mind, I'm in the command tent." At the entrance he stopped, half-turned, and added—not unkindly—"Your brother, Sister. I'll look into the name. If there's a record to be corrected, I'll correct it. That offer stands regardless."

"That's the trouble with men like you," Brann said. "You think a corrected record is the same thing as the truth."

He went out. The flap dropped. The lantern stayed.

* * *

They gave her water after that, and a blanket, and left her on the stool inside the tent with a single bored sentry—a different boy, younger, yawning, sitting on a crate by the flap with his rifle across his knees. Outside, the camp settled by degrees. Wagons rolled out toward the capital road around two o'clock, carrying, she guessed, the seized papers and the prisoners' effects north for inspection. Boots passed and repassed. Somewhere a man sang three lines of a song about a girl in Lowell and forgot the rest.

Brann counted.

It was a nurse's habit—counting pulse, counting breaths, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder—and she turned it now on the camp as if the whole of Fenlow were a patient in crisis. The sentry changed at intervals she judged to be two hours; she heard the relief come at what she took to be near four. The new sentry was colder and less attentive; she could hear him stamping, then walking, then stopping to shelter from the wind behind the tent's north corner, where the flap wouldn't show him. The latrine trench, she knew from her own body's needs and the camp's geography, lay off that same corner—twenty paces, maybe twenty-five, dug deep for a camp meant to hold hundreds, screened by brush hurdles, and drained, because the ground sloped toward the river, by a runoff channel at its lower end where the earth had been cut away.

Twenty paces of open ground. One sentry with his collar up. And below the trench's back wall, if the drainage was built the way drainage was always built in these hastily raised camps, a culvert gap where the runoff passed under the hurdle line—a gap a thin woman might squeeze through if she did not think too carefully about what else passed that way.

Her hands were steady now. That surprised her. Fear had burned down through loyalty, through grief, through contempt, and what was left underneath was something older and simpler, the thing that had carried her through fever wards and amputation lines: *work to be done, therefore do it.* She would not buy Vey's life with words in a tent, because the tent was Corvane's country and words died at its border. But there was a road north, and a coffin—or something—somewhere ahead of her, and a woman named Wendell Eriksen who was at this moment, wherever she was, doing the bravest and most invisible work in the world, and none of it would matter if Brann sat on this stool until morning waiting for a corrected record.

She stood. The sentry didn't hear it; the wind covered her.

At the back of the tent the canvas met the ground in a wet seam. She found the place where a peg had pulled loose in the day's commotion, worked the flap of it up with fingers gone numb, and slid out into a dark that felt, after hours of lantern, like being born. The cold hit her lungs. Twenty paces. She went low and slow along the tent line, past the picketed horses—who knew her smell, nurses' smells, laudanum and carbolic, and stood calm—and reached the hurdle screen with her heart beating so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

The trench was exactly as bad as she had imagined. Worse. The cold filth took her to the waist; the smell would live in her hair for weeks. At the far end, where the drainage cut ran beneath the hurdles, there was the gap—a hand's width of cleared earth under the lowest board, packed hard by water, opening onto the black slope of the riverbank. She went through it the way she had seen soldiers go under wire: flat, exhaling, thinking of nothing but the next handhold. A board tore her sleeve and the skin beneath. Her remaining boot—she had lost one somewhere in the tent, she realized absurdly, and would not miss it until later—found purchase in frozen mud, and then she was through, and sliding, and rolling down the bank into the whispering reeds with the camp's lantern-glow a smear of gold above her like a sky she had escaped.

She lay in the reeds and breathed. Above, faintly, she heard the sentry stamp twice and cough, unaware, and the sound of it was so mundane she nearly laughed, and the laugh turned into something else in her throat and stayed there.

North, then. Along the water, away from the road, toward the capital, toward whatever remained of the plan. Eleven days had become ten. Vey was in their hands and condemned without trial; the ledger's fate she didn't know; Wendell was somewhere in the dark ahead of her or behind her, alone.

Brann got to her feet in the freezing reeds, one-booted, stinking, spectacle-less, squinting at a world gone soft at every edge—and began, with a nurse's unhurried stride, to walk.

Behind her, upstream, a bugle sounded the hour, and then, a moment later, a second call she knew from a hundred hospitals and hated with her whole heart: the one that turned a camp out of its blankets to search for what was missing.
