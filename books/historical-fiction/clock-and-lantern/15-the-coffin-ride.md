## 15. The Coffin Ride

The dead man's name was Private Eliah Voorman, and he was twenty-three years old, and he had died of fever in the Fenlow quarantine camp four days before Aldous Vey climbed into his coffin.

Vey knew these things because Wendell had read them from the burial detail's manifest, copied out in that careful ledger hand while Brann stood watch at the tent flap and the camp still smoldered with the panic of the sweep. *Voorman, E., 23, Co. B, 4th Rifles. Fever. Effects: one knife, one photograph (unclaimed), no letters.* The army had already written him down. The army was finished with him. All that remained was to ship him home—or what passed for home, a farm village upriver whose name Vey did not know—and so the coffin would travel under flag and seal, untouched by any sergeant's greed or any checkpoint officer's suspicion. No one searched the dead. The war had taught even the corrupt that much reverence, or something shaped enough like reverence to serve.

"He is the same size as you," Wendell said. It was not an apology. Wendell did not apologize; Vey had learned that in eleven days of hard travel. It was only an accounting, delivered in the flat nurse's voice that could state the worst fact in the world as though reading a temperature.

"I noticed," Vey said.

He had noticed at Harrow Ford too, in the aftermath, walking the line of the dead because someone had to and because no one else would look at their faces. He had been a captain then, and captains were supposed to be made of harder stuff than looking, but Vey had always suspected he was made of exactly the wrong stuff—too much looking, not enough forgetting. That suspicion had been confirmed by a posthumous letter in his own hand, lifted from his effects and doctored into a confession, and by a year of being dead.

Now he lay down in a dead man's box to prove he wasn't.

* * *

They worked at midnight, in the wagon shed behind the undertaker's lean-to, and the undertaker—a rheumatic old civilian named Pell who hated the army with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of a man who had buried too many boys on government credit—turned his back and let them.

The coffin was pine, cheap, the kind the quartermaster issued by the dozen. Wendell had already done the work of it: the lining unpicked along one side, a pocket of sacking sewn back in place with stitches fine as hair, wide enough for a ledger wrapped in oilcloth. Vey watched those hands work—the same hands that had stitched soldiers' flesh for two years of war—and thought, not for the first time, that the ledger had found the right keeper. A book of erased names hidden in the lining prepared for a man the army had tried to erase. There was a symmetry there that would have pleased the chaplains, if chaplains had ever been honest.

"Once we're on the road you don't come out," Wendell said. "Not at the checkpoints. Not if they open the lid. Not for anything."

"And if I need to breathe?"

"You'll breathe through the gap at the seam. Half an inch. It's enough. I checked."

Of course Wendell had checked. Vey believed Wendell had measured everything on this journey twice—the distances, the guard rotations, the hours between reliefs, the exact depth of a man's need for air. It was Brann who laughed, a short ugly laugh with no humor in it, because Brann had spent three days in a holding tent and had come out of it thinner and harder and unwilling to pretend otherwise.

"They hanged my brother without a box," she said. "He got a rope off a wagon tail and a ditch." She looked at Vey lying there among the shavings. "You get pine and a cortege and a nurse driving. Don't waste it."

"I won't," Vey said, and meant it more thoroughly than he had ever meant a field order.

Wendell laid the ledger in first, then the oilcloth, then sewed the final inches closed with fingers that did not tremble, and Vey understood that he was watching his whole case—every erased man, every forged signature, every barrel of sold gunpowder and every position abandoned on paper before it was abandoned in fact—being buttoned into a shroud beside him. If the coffin reached the tribunal, the truth arrived. If it didn't, the truth would be buried with him in a pauper's field, and General Corvane would go on being promoted for the men he had spent.

"Captain." Wendell's face appeared above him, lantern-shadowed. "One thing. When they carry you—if they set you down hard, if there's jostling—you'll want to cry out. Every part of you will want to. You won't."

"No," Vey agreed. "I won't."

It was, he reflected as Wendell drove the last nails home—for authenticity, Pell said; coffins that traveled had better be nailed—it was the strangest order he had ever accepted without argument. He had commanded three hundred men at Harrow Ford. He had sent forty-one of them up a ridge that his own army had already sold. He could lie still in a box.

The lid came down. The dark was total. Somewhere beyond it, muffled as prayer, Wendell said: "Eliah Voorman, Company B, Fourth Rifles. You carry a passenger tonight. Be gentle with him."

Then the first nail went in, and Vey stopped being anyone at all.

* * *

He had believed himself prepared for the dark. He had spent a year in the dark, in barns and root cellars and the flooded cellar of a burned church, learning to be a dead man moving carefully through a world built for the living. But the year's darkness had always had edges—a seam of lantern light, a draft, the sound of weather. This dark was upholstered. It pressed against his open eyes like a hand. It smelled of pine shavings and sawdust and, beneath that, faintly, of the fever that had killed Eliah Voorman, though Vey knew rationally that fever had no smell that survived four days and a bed of fresh-cut wood. Knowledge did not help. The dark did not care what he knew.

He catalogued what he could feel, the way he had once catalogued artillery positions. His body: prone, arms at his sides, knees straight—a soldier at attention, or a corpse at rest, the two postures identical from inside. Above his chest, a half-inch of air moving through the seam, cold and thin. Against his left hip, the weight of the oilcloth bundle, which meant the ledger rode where his hand would fall if he were the sort of corpse who reached for things. Around all of it, the pine—not a wall, he told himself, not a grave, a vehicle, merely a vehicle—

The wagon lurched. The coffin slid an inch on the boards beneath it and his shoulder struck the side, and the sound of his own skull knocking pine filled the entire universe.

*Don't,* he told himself, in the voice he had used on green lieutenants. *You will not cry out.*

They came for the coffin at dawn. He heard it through the wood: voices, the scrape of many boots, Wendell's voice pitched low and professional, reciting the manifest. *Private Eliah Voorman, fever, escorting to family plot upriver, per burial regulations, section nine.* Section nine. Vey almost smiled in his box. Wendell had memorized the burial regulations. Somewhere in the capital a tribunal waited, and here was a nurse who had learned the army's rulebook the way other people learned psalms.

Then other hands took hold of him—six of them, at least, at head and foot—and the coffin rose, swung, settled onto a bier or a gun-carriage, and Vey felt the moment of transfer the way a man feels a river current take his boat from the shore. He was no longer carried by friends. He was cargo now, in the custody of strangers, and every stranger was a risk wearing a uniform.

The cortege moved out.

* * *

He heard the road through pine, the way a blind man hears weather. Wheels—one wagon ahead, the bier-wheels beneath him, another wagon behind. Horses, the creak of harness, the slow walk that decency demanded of funerals and that also, conveniently, suited a route planned around checkpoints. And voices, when the wind allowed: Brann riding flank, her voice roughened since Fenlow but steady; Wendell up front, answering the courtesies of passing officers with a nurse's tired gravity; and once, terribly close, a sergeant saying, "Who's in the box?" and Wendell saying, "Fever case, going home to Marlow Bend, his mother paid for transport," and the sergeant saying nothing, because mothers who paid were the one authority the army still feared.

For hours Vey lay in the dark and listened to the war pass by him at a funeral's pace.

He heard troops marching the other way—hundreds of boots, a rhythm he knew in his spine, and a band playing something jaunty that the wind shredded and reassembled. He heard a train whistling at some junction, hauling guns north. He heard, near midday, the ordinary commerce of a river town waking: carts, dogs, a woman singing snatches of a hymn, children running alongside the cortege until a mother called them back. Life, everywhere, indifferent to the box. He had commanded men through worse than this. He reminded himself of it. It helped less than it should have.

Because the truth—the truth he could not say aloud even to himself in daylight, and so said now, in the dark, where no one could hear him think it—was that this was familiar. He had been riding in a coffin for a year.

Officially dead since Harrow Ford. Blamed for Harrow Ford. His name spoken only as a lesson: *the captain who broke, the captain who fled, the captain whose letter confessed it all.* They had used his handwriting against him the way Corvane used everything—efficiently, without waste. And so Aldous Vey, Captain, 4th Rifles, had ceased to exist on paper, and a man who does not exist on paper cannot surrender, cannot defend himself, cannot stand trial, cannot even be hanged properly. He could only hide. He had hidden so long that hiding had begun to feel less like a condition and more like a substance, like the wood around him now—as if he had been interred alive a year ago and was only now making his way, box by box, toward the ground that had already been assigned him.

And here was the thought that came to him in the third hour, uninvited, in the voice of the dead:

*You are one of ours now.*

He had walked the line at Harrow Ford. He knew their names—God, he knew their names, forty-one of them, and Wendell's ledger held more, dozens more, hundreds, the men Corvane had spent and struck from the rolls to keep his ledgers clean. Deserters, the rolls said. Suicides. Unaccounted. Men who had died holding a ridge their general had already sold, and then died a second time in the paperwork, erased by clerks with clean fingernails. Vey had spent a year among the living wearing the face of a dead man. Now he lay among the dead in the body of one, and the coffin was not a disguise at all. It was a promotion. Eliah Voorman had been erased; Vey was merely being erased more slowly.

*I am sorry,* he told the boy whose box he occupied. *I will give it back. I will see your mother gets you whole, or as whole as anything comes back.*

The boy did not answer. The wheels turned. Somewhere ahead, Vey knew, the capital sat on its hill with its tribunal and its law, and somewhere behind, General Corvane sat in a warm room deciding how many men it would cost to stop a funeral.

* * *

In the late afternoon the cortege halted, and Vey heard the thing he had been dreading since the lid came down: a barrier being dragged across a road. Voices in formation. The particular cadence of a checkpoint—the challenge, the response, the bored cruelty of men with power over paper.

Toll Bridge had been a frightened lieutenant. Fenlow had been an old sergeant choosing memory over orders. This one he could not predict, and unpredictability, in a coffin, was death.

Boots approached the bier. A hand struck the coffin lid—twice, casually, the way a man knocks on a door he has no intention of waiting at.

"Open it."

"Regulations forbid—" Wendell began.

"*Open it,* or I open it for you and bill the escort for the nails."

A pause. Vey felt the whole cortege hold its breath—Brann's horse shifting somewhere to the left, Wendell's silence arranging itself, he knew, into whatever this required. Then Wendell said, mildly, the way one remarks on weather:

"Sergeant, do you know what section nine provides for escorts of the dead?"

"I don't care what—"

"It provides that any man disturbing a sealed military coffin forfeits his conduct record to the escorting officer and answers to the burial board at the capital. It was written after Kessel's Field, when quartermasters were caught selling bodies to medical colleges." A beat, precisely measured. "But of course you may open it. Only understand that you'll be doing it in front of the escort, the drivers, and that corporal there who I'm sure has no ambitions past his current rank—and I'll be obliged to write all three of our names in my report. I write very small. Reports of mine tend to be read."

Silence. Vey counted his own heartbeats in the dark, five, six, seven.

"Nurse," the sergeant said at last, and the word had gone sour, "your papers are in order."

"So are yours, Sergeant. May God keep them that way."

The barrier lifted. The wheels rolled on. Vey lay in his pine dark and felt something he had not felt since Harrow Ford: not safety—he did not believe in safety anymore—but the fierce, astonished gratitude of a man who has been saved by someone else's courage and knows he can never repay it in the same coin. Wendell had not drawn a weapon. Wendell had never needed one. Wendell carried a book and a rulebook and a face that would not flinch, and against such armament, Vey thought, Corvane's whole machine might yet break itself.

That night—they had halted at a ferry crossing, and Wendell raised the lid just wide enough, by lantern-light shielded under a blanket, to pass water to his lips through a bent spoon—Wendell looked down at him a long moment before speaking.

"You were talking earlier. Under the bridge. I heard it through the boards."

Vey's throat closed. Then, because lying to this person had become impossible somewhere on the river road: "I was apologizing to Voorman."

Something moved in Wendell's eyes—not pity, which Vey could have borne, but recognition. "Good," Wendell said. "Keep doing that. A man who talks to the dead keeps his own name sharp." And the lid came down again, gently this time, like a blanket drawn up.

* * *

Dawn of the second day brought the smell of the city before any sound of it—coal smoke and tanneries and the river's industrial stink, seeping through the half-inch seam like a summons. Voices multiplied around the cortege. Carts crowded them. Somewhere a bell tolled the hour, and Vey, counting, understood that the tribunal would convene in two days, that the coffin must reach the archives or the courthouse by nightfall, that the plan's last mile lay through streets where Corvane's men could be anyone and everyone.

Then the wheels slowed, and he heard a new voice, official and unhurried, saying words that fell through the pine like cold water:

"—gate's closed to private convoy. Commandant's orders. Nothing comes through today carrying freight, and nothing at all comes through carrying *boxes.*"

The cortege stopped.

Above him, faintly, Vey heard Wendell draw one slow breath—the breath before triage, the breath before a wound was opened—and he lay very still in a dead man's box, holding a stolen coffin and a stolen life and a book of stolen names, and listened to the last wall of the law begin, quietly, to argue with itself.
