## 16. Third Checkpoint: The Capital Gate

The capital announced itself first by smell—the river's rot giving way to coal smoke and horse dung and the sour breath of ten thousand lives pressed behind walls—and then by the wall itself, which rose out of the morning haze like the hull of a beached ship. Grey stone, black with old rain, crowned by sentries who looked down the funeral road at the procession Wendell had joined two days before and would now have to leave.

The cortege moved slowly, as corteges must. Six wagons of the dead, draped in grey blankets because the army had run short of flags, drawn by mules that seemed to understand the dignity of the work better than their driver did. Muffled wheels. No music; bands cost money and the dead of Marrow Creek had died on the wrong side of the ledger for anyone to spend it on them. Behind the wagons walked a handful of mourners in civilian coats, and among them, walking with a coffin-maker's assistant's slouch beside the third wagon, a gaunt man in a borrowed suit whose hands wanted very badly to hold a sword or a pen and had been given neither.

Wendell did not look at him. They had practiced not looking at each other until it had become its own language.

*He is lying in a coffin,* Wendell reminded himself, and then corrected: *No. The pages lie in the coffin. He walks beside it.* The distinction mattered. It was the only thing keeping Wendell's thoughts from sliding into some colder place. For eleven days the ledger had been a thing carried; now it was a thing hidden inside a thing hidden inside a procession of the war's own erasures, riding toward the tribunal through the last gate Corvane controlled, and every yard of road felt like walking on river ice.

The gate itself was a tunnel through the wall—forty feet of stone throat, iron gates at either end, lanterns burning against the gloom though it was barely noon. And in the tunnel, stopping the cortege's lead wagon with an upraised hand, stood a commandant.

Wendell knew the type at forty yards: Major Halvard Renne, if the boards were honest, a thick man gone soft at the edges, wearing his authority the way some men wear a coat too heavy for the season—because he'd been told to and hadn't thought about it since. Two squads of troopers flanked him. Not the bored garrison soldiers of Toll Bridge, not the shivering boys of the quarantine camp. These were Corvane's men; you could tell by the way they didn't fidget, by the way they watched the crowd instead of the wagons, by the clean newness of their carbines.

"A box," Brann had said, when she'd heard the rumor from a coffin-maker's apprentice in the last town—a rumor passed hand to hand like contraband: *the gate has orders. A nurse with a box. Detain them.* "They know," she'd said, and her jaw had been set the way it set when she spoke of her brother. "Not your face. Not Vey's face. Just the shape of what you're carrying."

So they had reshaped themselves. Wendell no longer wore the nurse's grey; a mourning dress, borrowed, plain to the point of severity, with a veil folded back from the face because full veils drew attention rather than deflecting it. The transfer papers named no regiment. Miss Ansel's documents—God help them, Miss Ansel's documents, and may the thought of her waiting in the capital mean nothing worse than eagerness—sat in an oilskin wallet against Wendell's ribs, over the heart, where fear could feel them with every beat.

The cortege halted. The commandant walked the line of wagons with a list in one hand and a pencil stub in the other, ticking names of the dead off against names on paper, and Wendell understood with a small cold shock that he was counting. Counting bodies against manifests. Somewhere behind those grey stones, someone had done arithmetic on the dead of Marrow Creek and found it wanting.

Then the commandant reached the third wagon, and stopped, and looked up—not at the wagon but along the line of mourners, reading faces the way Wendell read ledgers, hunting one entry in particular.

"Which of you is the nurse?" His voice carried in the stone tunnel without effort; men built like him learned early that volume was cheaper than thought.

Silence. The mourners looked at their boots, at the sky, at each other—an honest confusion, rehearsed into honesty over two days of coaching. Wendell let three heartbeats pass, then stepped forward, just far enough to be distinct from the group, veil folded, hands folded, grief arranged in the face like furniture in a house for sale.

"I am attending these remains to the Hall of Records," Wendell said. Calm. Even. The voice of a person to whom nothing interesting had ever happened.

"Name."

"Widow Sarel." The false name came out smooth as water. It had been chosen weeks ago, common enough to be everywhere and nowhere, belonging to no parish anyone would trouble to search. "My husband's company is in the third wagon. I was permitted to accompany—" a small gesture that took in the whole sad parade— "this."

The commandant consulted his list anyway, running a blunt finger down it, and Wendell watched him do it and understood that the name wasn't on any list, that the list held perhaps four entries and all of them descriptions: *nurse, cart, papers, box.* The finger stopped. He looked up.

"You're carrying a box."

"The army carries boxes, Major. I carry my husband." Let them open the coffin, then, and find a man of the wrong height, breathing, alive, and pages sewn into the lining—no. No, they could not open it, and everyone standing in that tunnel knew why they could not, and the knowledge hung there between them like the smoke from the lanterns.

"The box comes down," the commandant said. "All cargo enters by the inspection yard. You'll wait."

A murmur moved through the mourners. One of the older women began, softly, to weep—the genuine article, Wendell suspected; grief needs so little provocation—but the commandant's men were already moving, two of them reaching for the tailboard of the third wagon where the coffin-maker's assistant stood with white knuckles and a face that had gone the color of tallow.

And there it was, then. The moment eleven days of river road had been walking toward. If they lifted that lid in an inspection yard, with time and privacy and good light, they would find Captain Aldous Vey's testimony sewn into the sacking, and within the hour it would be ash in a brazier, and within the week General Corvane would be promoted, and every name in Wendell's ledger would stay erased until the last person who remembered them died. All of it resting now on whether a soft major in a stone tunnel chose to obey a piece of paper he'd been handed in private, or a different piece of paper Wendell was about to put into his hands in public.

Wendell stepped forward again.

"Major Renne." The name landed precisely; Wendell watched it land, saw the flicker of surprise—he was nobody, how did a widow know his name?—and pressed the advantage before he could assemble a question around it. "Before your men touch that coffin, you will want to see this."

The oilskin wallet came out from under the ribs. Fingers steady. Heart hammering so hard it seemed impossible the major couldn't hear it. Inside: three documents, and Wendell laid them out on the tailboard of the wagon one at a time, unhurried, as though handling them were routine—as though widows produced legal instruments at funerals every day of the week.

The first was a certified copy of the tribunal's writ of summons, stamped in the violet ink of the civilian judiciary, bearing the seal of the capital circuit. Genuine. Ansel had procured it honestly, or as honestly as a turned woman procures anything, and it stated—in language old enough to predate the war—that persons and evidence summoned before the tribunal were privileged from seizure by military authority *at any gate, post, or checkpoint of the armed forces*, under penalty of contempt, which was a civil offense, which meant it was tried by civilians, which meant no soldier could shield behind his uniform or his general.

The second was a schedule of witnesses and exhibits, listing—entry nine—*remains and accompanying documentation, Marrow Creek casualties, material to counts one through six.*

The third was nothing at all. A receipt for the coffin, properly witnessed, and beneath it a single line in Miss Ansel's small clerk's hand: *The statute binds whoever reads it. Make him read it in front of witnesses.*

"These remains are entered as tribunal evidence," Wendell said, while Renne read—or made a show of reading; his lips moved. "Under the Witness Privilege Act of 1841, section four, seizure of summoned evidence by military personnel is contempt of a civilian court. Not a matter for your general, Major. A matter for a judge. Who will ask you, specifically, by name, why you touched it."

"I have orders," Renne said. The pencil stub turned once in his fingers.

"Of course you do." Wendell kept the voice level, almost gentle—the triage voice, the voice used with men who were bleeding out and needed to believe someone was in charge. "Orders are easy. They're written for you. Contempt proceedings aren't. They'll want to know whether you were shown the writ"—a fingertip rested on the violet stamp— "and the law says that once you've seen it, following other instructions becomes your decision. Yours. Not the general's. He'll be in uniform when they come asking. So will your men. Only you will have read this."

In the tunnel, twenty soldiers and a dozen mourners and a coffin-maker and a man in a borrowed suit were all very carefully not moving. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere beyond the inner gate, the city went on with its morning—cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, a church bell striking the half hour with the fat indifference of institutions that survive wars.

Renne looked at the writ. Then at the third wagon. Then—this was the moment Wendell would remember afterward, in the years when remembering was bearable—at his own men, at their clean carbines and their stillness, and something moved behind his face that was not cowardice and not courage but the ordinary arithmetic of a man discovering which master holds the longer whip. Corvane could break him quietly. A civilian court would break him loudly, in a room with newspapers.

"Sergeant," he said at last, and his voice had lost a layer of its certainty. "The cortege passes. Cargo unexamined—it's covered by—" he gestured at the tailboard, at the papers, at the whole suffocating apparatus of law he'd just discovered applied to him— "witness privilege. Log it. Time, my name, the writ number. Full notation."

"Yes sir." The sergeant wrote it out in a hand like fence posts, and Wendell watched the pen move and thought: *there. That's the weapon. Not the carbine, not the bluff alone. A statute and a stamp and a man made to write his own exposure in a logbook.* The law, used as a lever, by a nurse of no regiment, against the entire machinery of a general's will. Barely. By inches. With the wood creaking.

"Go on, then," Renne said heavily. "Move along. And Widow Sarel—" his eyes came up, and there was something in them Wendell hadn't expected, something tired and old and almost apologetic, "—whatever's in that box. See it gets where it's going. My brother's at Harrow Ford. Or he isn't. Nobody's ever told me straight."

Wendell met his eyes and gave him the only honest thing available: a single nod, grave as a signature.

The cortege groaned forward. The iron gates swung—first the outer, then the inner, each dragged open by soldiers who did not meet anyone's gaze—and the funeral rolled through the stone throat of the wall, out of the army's country and into the city's, past the customs sheds and the coal yards, into streets that narrowed and multiplied like the branching of a river's delta.

For two hundred yards nobody spoke. Then the coffin-maker's assistant exhaled the breath he'd been holding for a quarter hour, and beside the third wagon, low enough that only Wendell heard it, the borrowed suit said:

"He saluted you, in his way. The major. Did you see it?"

"He signed a logbook."

"That's what a salute is, when the times are bad enough." Vey's voice was rough from disuse and disguise, and underneath the dryness there was something else, something almost unsteady. "Eleven days I've watched you carry us. Through three gates and a coffin. I commanded two hundred men at Harrow Ford and I never once—" He stopped. Started again. "It's close now. I can feel it. The way you feel weather in an old wound."

Ahead, above the rooftops, the dome of the Hall of Justice caught the pale sun—a bruised silver gleam through the coal smoke, exactly where Miss Ansel's directions promised. The tribunal convened there in eight days. Eight days to hide a coffin, find a witness, and pray that whatever waited at the top of those steps was still the ally they'd been promised.

The cortege turned onto the Processional Way, and Wendell saw, half a street distant, at the foot of the great stairs, a figure in a clerk's grey coat, standing very still among the traffic, watching the funeral come.

Miss Ansel. Waiting. Smiling.

And behind her, in the shadow of the colonnade, four men in civilian coats who stood the way soldiers stand.
