## 17. Miss Ansel's Betrayal

The capital smelled of coal smoke and river rot, and Wendell had not stopped smelling it since the gate.

They had come through the third checkpoint on the strength of a statute and a bluff, and for six hours afterward Wendell had allowed himself something dangerous: the belief that they had won. The city's cobbled streets rang under the cart wheels like a bell that would not stop tolling, and Vey sat up straighter with every block, as though the stones themselves were carrying him toward vindication. Brann rode behind them in the cart bed among the funeral flowers they'd bought to make the coffin plausible, her hands folded over her knees, watching the rooftops slide past with the flat attention of a woman who had learned never to trust an open street.

The tribunal convened at the Hall of Assizes on Corvane Square—of course it was Corvane Square; the general's name was carved into the lintel above the doors, and Wendell wondered how many of the erased men in the ledger had walked beneath those letters without ever reading them.

They left the cortege two streets away. The coffin went into the custody of the hall's under-marshal, who signed a receipt without looking at it, and the real pages stayed sewn where they had ridden all along, against the lining, above the dead man who had lent them his box. Wendell carried only the cipher copy now—the decoy of the decoy, the one they'd rebuilt from memory at a ferry inn—and the original ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath the nurse's coat, flat against the ribs, where it rose and fell with every breath like a second heart.

"You're certain about the summons," Vey said for the fourth time. He stood very straight in his borrowed civilian coat, and even gaunt and gray he could not quite shed the officer out of his spine. "Ansel confirmed the hour herself."

"She confirmed it twice." Wendell checked the street. Morning crowds, market carts, a pair of provosts loitering by a pie stand who were probably just cold. "She wrote the citation numbers. She told us which clerk to ask for. If she wanted us taken, she's had eleven days of chances before this one."

"And yet." Vey looked up at the Hall of Assizes, at its soot-darkened stone and its tall windows glowing amber with morning light, and something moved across his face that Wendell had learned to read as fear wearing the mask of calculation. "I have been dead once already, Eriksen. I find I dislike the sensation. I should prefer not to repeat it on these steps."

"Then don't," said Brann.

They climbed.

* * *

The steps were granite, worn hollow in the middle by a century of boots, and Miss Ansel was waiting at the top.

Wendell saw her before she saw them—or believed she hadn't seen them—and felt the strange lift of relief that comes from seeing a familiar face in an unfamiliar place. She stood near the great doors in a good gray coat, her dark hair pinned with the same plain combs she'd worn in the records office at Marrow Creek, a leather folio held against her chest the way a schoolgirl holds a primer. She looked small. She looked tired. She looked exactly like the woman who had spent three nights copying supply manifests by lamplight because she believed the names mattered.

"Miss Ansel," Wendell called, and heard their own voice come out warmer than intended.

She turned.

And Wendell saw it—the whole of it—in the half-second before anything else happened. Not guilt. Not shame. A kind of terrible steadiness, the look of a person standing on ground they have already decided to die on or live on, whichever is asked. Her eyes went past Wendell to Vey, and lingered there, and did not soften.

"Captain Vey," she said. "You're punctual. He said you would be."

The words arrived wrong. Wendell heard them arrive wrong—the way a surgeon hears a heartbeat arrive wrong, one beat before understanding why. *He said.* Not the tribunal. Not the presiding magistrate. *He.*

"Miss Ansel," Wendell said again, slower now, and their hand found the ledger through the coat without deciding to. "Who is *he*?"

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. The doors behind her opened—not swung wide, but eased, the way doors open when the men holding them are trying not to make noise—and four soldiers came out onto the steps in undress uniforms with sidearms, moving with the unhurried economy of men who had done this before and been paid well for it. Behind them, filling the doorway like weather, stood a captain Wendell had never seen but recognized instantly from the cut of his coat and the boredom in his face: Corvane's people wore a particular kind of cleanliness, an armor of pressed wool and indifference, and this man wore it like a dress uniform.

"There it is," the captain said pleasantly. "All three of you. General Corvane sends his compliments and his regrets that the tribunal has been—rescheduled."

"The tribunal convenes today," Vey said. His voice was level. His hand was not; it had gone white around the strap of his bag. "I have the summons. Signed by the clerk of assizes."

"You have a summons written by this woman," the captain said, nodding at Ansel without heat, without contempt, which was somehow worse than either. "On paper supplied by this woman, citing statutes selected by this woman, delivered through channels this woman controls. Captain, you have been holding a lantern made of paper and calling it the sun." He smiled thinly. "It was a good trap. I'll grant it that. The statute invocation at the gate—that was elegant. We nearly lost you at the wall. But the summons itself? That was ours from the beginning."

Wendell's mind was moving fast and finding nothing to hold. Eleven days. Eleven days of planning built on a confirmation, on citation numbers, on a sympathetic clerk who had wept once, quietly, over a list of names. *She turned months ago,* some cold part of Wendell thought, doing arithmetic with horror instead of numbers. *Months. Before Harrow Ford was even mentioned. Before any of it.*

"Why," Wendell said. It came out cracked. They cleared their throat. "Miss Ansel. You copied those manifests. You read the names. You knew what he did to them."

"I know what he did to my brother."

The words dropped between them like a stone into a well, and Wendell waited for the splash and heard none.

"Private Daniel Ansel, Fourth Rifles," she said, and her voice did not shake, which took a strength Wendell would spend years failing to understand. "Struck from the rolls in 'sixty-one. Reported drowned in a supply accident. There is no supply accident. There is a grave outside Harrow Ford with forty-one men in it, and my brother is one of them, and I spent eight months trying to reach a tribunal before I understood that there is no road to justice in this country that does not run through General Corvane's front hall." She lifted her chin. "So yes. I was turned. Eight months ago. And I have spent those eight months learning his machinery from the inside, waiting for one piece of evidence strong enough to trade."

"Trade for what?" Vey asked quietly.

"For the exhumation order." For the first time her composure flickered—just at the corner of her mouth, just for a breath. "Corvane believes your ledger is the only surviving map of Harrow Ford. He wants it destroyed and everyone who touched it hanged or vanished. In exchange for delivering both, I have his signature on an order to open the graves. Forty-one names. My brother gets his name back off your page and onto a military record, and no one can strike it again, because it will be carved in the one ink Corvane cannot erase—a court order bearing his own seal."

"That's not how it works," Wendell said. "Men like him don't honor trades. You're not buying a grave marker. You're selling us."

"I'm buying the only thing that was ever purchasable," she said. "I'm sorry. I truly am. But you were always going to be currency. From the night you wrote to me, you were currency. The only question was whose."

The soldiers had come down three steps while she spoke. Wendell barely registered them; the world had narrowed to the woman's face and the weight against their ribs rising and falling, rising and falling.

"Miss Ansel," Wendell said, and heard their own voice go quiet and strange, the voice they used for men dying in the last minutes. "There were sixty-three names in the ledger when I wrote to you. Sixty-three families who think their sons deserted them. You've read every one. You can recite them—I watched you do it, that night, with your eyes shut." A step closer; a soldier's hand twitched toward a holster and the bored captain waved him still, curious. "Is your brother's name worth more than the other sixty-two?"

For one moment—one long, suspended moment in which the coal-smoke wind moved the hem of her gray coat and somewhere below in the square a fishmonger cried his prices to a crowd that did not care—Miss Ansel's eyes filled.

"Yes," she whispered. "God forgive me. Yes."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Wendell would spend the rest of their life replaying it in fragments, because that is what the mind does with catastrophe—it keeps the pieces and loses the shape.

Vey moved first. Later, Wendell would understand why: a soldier's instinct, a year of hiding, the body deciding before the mind that capture meant a rope and that the rope was here, now, four steps away. He swung his bag at the nearest soldier and shouted something—Wendell never caught the words—and the steps erupted. The captain barked an order. Two soldiers closed on Vey; he fought like a man with nothing left to lose, which is another way of saying he fought badly, wildly, and was brought down against the granite with his cheek split open on the third blow, his arms wrenched back, his voice still going, still naming names, shouting *Harrow Ford* and *Quist* and *the order was signed* until a gag ended it.

And Wendell stood frozen with sixty-three names against their chest and did nothing.

That was the fragment that would return most often, in the years after, at 3 a.m., in whatever town they'd landed in: not the violence, but the stillness. The nurse's paralysis. The body trained to run toward blood standing perfectly still while blood ran ten feet away.

Brann was not paralyzed.

Later—much later—Wendell would learn that she had seen the soldiers before any of them, had counted them coming down the steps, had measured the distance from the top landing to the square and the crowd in it, and had made her decision in the time it took Wendell to fail to decide anything at all. She came off the landing like a landslide, shoulder-first into the two soldiers holding Vey's left arm, and the knot of bodies broke apart and reformed, and in the breaking—

"In the square!" she screamed at Wendell, and her eyes found theirs across the chaos, fierce and furious and absolutely clear. "Run, you stubborn fool! *Run!*"

Wendell ran.

It was not a decision. It was obedience—the oldest kind, the kind drilled into nurses by surgeons screaming over amputation tables: *when I say move, you move, and you don't look back, because looking back kills the patient.* Wendell took the steps in leaps, took the corner of the square at a stumble, heard behind them the crash of the fight collapsing into capture, heard Brann's voice rise once more—not a scream now but something worse, a laugh, wild and defiant, the laugh of a woman giving her captors nothing—and then the crowd swallowed everything, market noise and cart wheels and the ordinary morning roar of a city that did not know what had just been surrendered on the steps of its Hall of Assizes.

Wendell walked. That was the hardest discipline of all—to walk, when every nerve screamed to run, because running drew the eye and walking was invisible, and invisibility was the only armor they had ever owned. Past the fishmonger. Past a church with its doors open on candlelight. Past a window displaying mourning clothes in black rows, like a joke told by a god with a cruel streak. Three streets. Four. The ledger against their ribs, rising and falling, rising and falling.

At the fifth street, in the shadow of a tannery, Wendell finally stopped and put a hand against the wall and was sick into the gutter.

When it was over, they wiped their mouth with the back of their wrist and stood shaking in the tannery stink and took inventory, because that was what remained, inventory: Vey, taken. Brann, taken. The summons, worthless. The plan, never theirs—never theirs from the first line of the first letter, a trap dressed in a tired woman's handwriting, baited with a brother's stolen name. Everything gone except the thing that had never had a plan attached to it at all: sixty-three names sewn into a coffin lining two streets from the men who wanted them burned, and one nurse of no regiment, no papers, no ally, and no idea what came next.

Except that wasn't true. There was one more thing.

Miss Ansel had said it herself, standing on those steps with tears in her eyes and treachery in her mouth: *an exhumation order.* Corvane's signature. Corvane's seal. Which meant Corvane believed the graves mattered. Which meant Corvane, for all his power, for all his bought clerks and trapped tribunals and clean-coated captains, was afraid of the same thing Wendell was carrying against their ribs.

He was afraid of dirt. Of what forty-one bodies would say when the ground gave them up.

Wendell straightened. Somewhere across the city, in a cell, Vey was alive—they needed him alive, needed him for the show trial, the hanging, the erasure; dead men made poor examples. And if he was alive, there was a hearing, and if there was a hearing, there was procedure, and procedure was a door, and doors had been Wendell's weapon since the capital gate.

The Hall of Assizes had scheduled a tribunal for this morning. Tribunals, once summoned on the public record, could not simply vanish—not entirely, not cleanly, not without paperwork, and paperwork left traces, and traces could be followed by someone who had spent two years reading other people's erased names and learning what the army wrote down when it thought no one was looking.

Wendell turned south, toward the records quarter, and began to walk.

Behind them, in a cell, a captain bled. Above them, on the steps of the Hall of Assizes, a woman wept over a signature. Ahead of them lay the archives, the loophole, and the last eleven days' only remaining truth:

The plan had never been theirs. So they would need a new one—and this time, there was no one left to betray it but themselves.
