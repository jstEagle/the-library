## 22. A New Ledger

The prison at Coldharbour had been a granary once, before the war taught the capital to store men instead of grain. Wendell saw it from across the frozen canal, its windows bricked to slits, and thought of all the mornings spent in tents where the wounded lay so close together that breathing was a communal act. This place held its men apart. One to a cell. Silence as architecture.

The visitation pass had cost three letters and eleven days of waiting—eleven days during which the tribunal's verdict traveled the city like weather, discussed in bakeries and barbershops by people who had never heard of Harrow Ford until a quiet voice began reading names into a hushed room. Corvane was to be court-martialed in the spring. The word *disgraced* had already attached itself to him like a burr; it would ride him to his grave whether or not the sentence did.

Wendell carried the ledger under one arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the snow, and felt it the way one feels a limb.

The guard at the gate read the pass twice, looked at Wendell's plain gray coat and unremarkable face, and evidently decided there was nothing worth remembering. That had become Wendell's condition in the world, deliberately cultivated: a person of no regiment, no fixed address, no name that could be spoken back with certainty. In the archives, hiding while the tribunal deliberated, Wendell had learned that anonymity was not emptiness. It was a room with the door locked from inside.

"Ten minutes," the guard said. "He's due for the yard."

Vey stood when the cell door opened, and Wendell barely kept from flinching. A year of hiding and a month of chains had thinned him to geometry—the officer's posture remained, but now it seemed less like bearing than like scaffolding holding up something that had been emptied. His hair had gone gray at the temples since Harrow Ford. He was thirty-four years old.

"Nurse Eriksen," he said, then caught himself, and almost smiled. "No. Forgive me. I don't suppose that's your name anymore."

"It's someone's name," Wendell said. "It can stay in here with you."

They sat on the cell's single bench, knees nearly touching. Through the slit window, snow came down over the canal with the patience of something that intended to go on forever.

"I watched the sentencing from the dock," Vey said. "I kept looking for you in the gallery. Brann told me afterward what you'd done—walking in with no name they could hang papers on. Ansel's own statute, turned around on her." He shook his head slowly. "I have commanded men under fire. I have never seen courage shaped quite like that. It was so quiet. Nobody even stood up."

"Somebody should have stood up a year ago at Harrow Ford," Wendell said. "Nobody did. I've stopped expecting trumpets."

Vey laughed—a short, rusty sound, as if the machinery of it needed oil. Then his eyes went to the oilcloth bundle, and the laughter went out of him.

"You brought it."

"I brought it."

Wendell unwrapped the ledger and set it on Vey's knees. Up close, in the gray light, the book showed its history: the water stain from Marrow Creek, the burn mark along one corner from the decoy fire at Fenlow, the spine resewn with surgical thread because that was what had been at hand. Names filled page after page in a hand that had grown steadier and smaller over two years, as if the writer were trying to fit more world onto less paper.

"I want you to read it before your year begins," Wendell said. "Not the tribunal copy. Not the cipher. This one. These were written at night, an hour at a time, next to men who were dying. The tribunal heard the names read out in forty minutes. It took me two years to earn them. You should have them at the proper speed."

Vey's hands rested on the cover without opening it, the way one rests a hand on the flank of a horse that startles easily.

"The names of my company are in here," he said quietly. "Sergeant Olem. The Pell brothers. That boy—Corliss—who used to whistle while he cleaned his rifle, and everyone pretended to hate it." He looked up. "You wrote them down when the army wrote them out. Do you understand what you did? Corvane erased them to make a story. You put them back to make a true one. There were only ever two people who thought those men mattered enough to fight for: me, and a stranger with a notebook."

"Three," Wendell said. "Brann's brother is in here too. Page sixty-one. She found him the way I found all of them—one line in a supply record where a man should have been."

Vey opened the book at last, carefully, at the middle. He read. The snow ticked against the window slit. Somewhere below, a cell door closed with a sound like a full stop.

"There's something else," Wendell said. "When your year is served, this comes back to me—or to whoever is keeping the new ledger by then. But while you're in here, it should live with you. Every name in it belongs to a man who followed you or fought beside you or died of the same lie. You're the only prisoner in this building serving a sentence connected to every other sentence in that book."

"I don't deserve to be its keeper."

"No," Wendell agreed. "But neither does anyone else, and you'll take care of it. That's more than most can say."

Vey held the ledger against his chest for a moment—an unconscious gesture, the way a man holds anything he has decided to defend—and then laid it open on his knees again.

"They're offering me a commission when I come out," he said. "Some board of inquiry wants the story told properly. Medals for the dead, a plaque at Harrow Ford, my rank restored with back pay." His mouth twisted. "A year ago I would have crawled through broken glass for my rank back. Now I think about standing in a dress uniform in front of the same army that shot my name off its rolls, and I feel nothing but cold."

"What will you do?"

"Tell the truth plainly, once, and then get out of the uniform forever. There's a farm in the east my mother's family lost thirty years ago. Perhaps farms can be re-lost and re-found like everything else." He glanced sideways. "And you? The tribunal wanted your name for the record. They never got it, did they?"

"Ansel's loophole cut both ways. A witness of no regiment testifies under protection; the protection extends to identity. It's in the statutes—she checked, believe me. By the time anyone thought to close the door, I had walked through it."

"So there's no Wendell Eriksen anywhere in the records."

"There's a Wendell Eriksen who died of fever at Marrow Creek," Wendell said. "Struck from the rolls. It seemed fitting—he was erased, so now he can't be found either. We're a pair, he and I. He keeps the old life. I keep the book."

Vey studied him for a long moment. Outside, the snow thickened, erasing the far bank of the canal.

"Where will you go?" he asked.

"Somewhere the war hasn't finished learning about. Somewhere with a river, because rivers bring strangers, and strangers bring stories that need writing down." Wendell rose, and Vey rose with him, and for a moment the two of them stood in the narrow cell like men in a doorway between lives.

"Will I see you again?" Vey asked. "In a year, when—"

"You'll see the ledger again," Wendell said. "That's the honest answer. Whether it comes in my hand or someone else's, I can't promise. A person of no name can't make appointments." A smile, small but real. "But I'll know how you are. Brann writes to me through her aunt in Millbrook, and her aunt hears everything. If your farm grows wheat, I'll hear about the wheat."

The guard knocked—not urgently, but with the institutional insistence of all doors everywhere—and Wendell stepped back into the corridor. At the threshold, Wendell turned. Vey stood with the ledger open on the bench behind him, already reading, his lips moving slightly around the name of some man dead two winters, giving it back its sound.

* * *

Brann was waiting outside the prison walls, stamping her feet on the frozen towpath, and she fell into step without greeting, which between them had become a form of tenderness. She wore civilian wool now, badly, like a costume she hadn't rehearsed in. Her brother's name was inked on a locket she wore under her collar; Wendell knew because she had shown it once, in the coffin cart, at the worst hour of the night, and neither of them had spoken for a long time afterward.

"Well?" she said.

"He's thinner. He's angrier in a useful way. He cried a little, but he'd deny it."

"He would."

"At the end he was reading. Page ninety. Your brother's page."

Brann walked several paces in silence. The canal lay gray and locked beside them; a barge sat frozen mid-river, abandoned, its cargo of timber furred with ice.

"My aunt says there's a town called Verlaan, four days south," she said at last, in the tone of someone continuing a conversation begun weeks ago. "Two ferries, one church, a doctor who drinks. No garrison larger than six men. She says the mill owner's wife lost a son at Kessel Field and has never been told the truth of it, if there is a truth."

"There's always a truth," Wendell said. "Sometimes it's only that nobody bothered to write it down."

"Then you'll want paper." Brann reached into her coat and produced a parcel, wrapped in brown paper and string, and thrust it over with the fierce awkwardness of a woman who had learned that gifts were dangerous and gave them anyway. Inside was a notebook—new, blank, its pages crisp and smelling of sizing and glue—and beneath it, folded, a small tin lantern with a shuttered door, the kind bargemen used, that threw light in one direction only.

"Brann—"

"Don't make a speech. I couldn't bear a speech." She looked out at the frozen river. "My brother existed for exactly one line in somebody's records, and the line was wrong. You fixed him. Whatever town you sit in next, there will be others. There are always others. So you'll need light and you'll need pages, and I had the money, and now it's done."

Wendell held the notebook a moment without opening it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"The first name will probably be the mill owner's son," Wendell said. "Kessel Field. I'll write to tell you what I find."

"Don't write to me. Write to my aunt. I'm going west—there's a hospital train needs nurses and I've decided the war doesn't get to keep me from the work just because the work broke my heart." She stuck out her hand, then ruined the formality by gripping Wendell's forearm like a soldier, like a sister. "You were the best nurse I ever served beside. Whoever you are next—you'd better be just as good."

Then she turned and walked away along the towpath, straight-backed, into the falling snow, and did not look back, and Wendell loved her for it, because looking back was a luxury for people with addresses.

* * *

Four days south, the river appeared between hills the color of old pewter, and Verlaan appeared with it—a scatter of red roofs around a mill, two ferry landings facing each other across the water like arguments, smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys. The coach let Wendell off at the top of the hill road with one carpet bag, one tin lantern, and one blank book, and asked no questions, because coaches in small towns never do.

The boarding house keeper looked Wendell over and asked for a name for her register.

Wendell considered the question honestly. The old name belonged to a dead nurse at Marrow Creek. Vey's name belonged to a farm in the east. Brann's name belonged to a hospital train. The tribunal's record belonged to a witness of no regiment.

"Sandoval," Wendell said, the first surname that surfaced, plucked from a gravestone glimpsed somewhere on the long road south—I. Sandoval, beloved father, the year before the war. It would do. Names were tools; this one was clean and unused.

"And your trade, Mr.—"

"Nurse," Wendell said. "And a bit of a writer. Nothing anyone's heard of."

There was a room at the back with a window over the river, and by evening the ferries had run their last crossing and the mill had gone quiet and the doctor's lamp burned late in the house by the bridge, as drinking lamps do. Wendell set the tin lantern on the table, turned its shutter so the light fell in one narrow beam across the blank page, and opened the notebook to the first leaf.

The pen hovered.

Outside, the river ran dark under ice at the edges, carrying whatever it carried toward towns downstream. Somewhere north, a captain read a ledger by slit-window light and gave dead men back their voices. Somewhere west, a nurse in a uniform again bandaged strangers whose names the army had already gotten wrong.

Wendell dipped the pen, and wrote, in a careful hand, steady and small:

*Kessel Field — Private Daniel Averill, son of Margit Averill of Verlaan, mill-owner's wife. Reported: killed in action. Records at the provincial depot suggest otherwise.*

The pen paused. Downstairs, someone was asking the landlady about the new lodger—a stranger's voice, unhurried, too smooth for a river town—and the landlady's answers came muffled through the floorboards. Wendell listened without moving, then quietly closed the lantern's shutter to a thread of light, and waited, pen lifted, listening to footsteps on the stairs.
