## 1. The Driver's Hands

The motorcar woke before the house did.

Astrid Delacroix had learned its moods the way other women learned the tempers of husbands — by listening in the dark, by touch, by the small betrayals of metal and oil. This morning it wanted attention. She could hear it in the tick of the cooling engine, a sound like a clock deciding whether to keep honest time. So she rose at half past five, dressed in the grey wool that hid the grease, and went down through the sleeping house with her boots in her hand, because Miss Halloway said the stairs complained and Astrid had made a private religion of not being heard.

Outside, London was still more suggestion than fact. Fog lay along Russell Square like something poured, thick enough to hold the lamplight upright in yellow columns, and the plane trees dripped with a patience that seemed older than the city. The pavement was cold through the soles of her boots even here, under the portico where the fog reached less boldly. Somewhere a milk cart; somewhere a horse coughing. The city clearing its throat for another day of history.

She unlocked the garage and let the smell of it fold around her — petrol, leather, cold iron, the ghost of last week's rain caught in the canvas hood. Her hands knew every inch of the Darracq without looking. Bonnet catch, crank, the quarter-turn that always fought her and always lost. The engine caught on the second pull and settled into its idle with something that felt, if one were foolish enough to anthropomorphise machinery, like approval.

"Good morning to you too," she said, because there was no one else to say it to.

This was the hour she owned. Not wages, not kindness, not the thin slice of evening after the supper things were done — this. Dawn belonged to the driver the way midnight belonged to burglars and poets, and Astrid took her property greedily. She fetched the chamois from its tin, breathed on the headlamp brass, and began to polish in slow circles while the sky over the rooftops went from slate to dirty pearl.

Her hands were good hands. Not pretty ones — the knuckles had been split by crank handles and cold water so often they'd developed a topography all their own — but competent. In the orphanage at Rothwell they had called her quiet hands, though never kindly. Quiet hands stole nothing and fixed everything and were therefore suspect on principle. Sister Edith used to hold them up by the wrists, palms out, as if reading a fortune in the calluses. *These hands will get you work,* she'd said once, almost grudgingly. *Work is not the same as love, girl. Don't confuse them.*

Astrid was twenty-six years old and still working out whether the woman had been cruel or simply honest. With nuns the two wore the same habit.

She worked the chamois down the mudguard, and the brass came up gold under the gaslight, and she allowed herself — briefly, the way one allows oneself a sweet stolen from one's own kitchen — the pleasure of a thing done well and seen by no one.

* * *

The letters came at twenty to seven, as letters did, in the hands of a postman whose boots had memorised the three steps to the door. Astrid intercepted him on the step, as she did every morning, with a nod and the word "early today," which was neither complaint nor gratitude but simply true, and he touched his cap and went, and she carried the envelope stack inside to the hall table.

Five pieces. She counted them first — counting was instinct, the way blinking was — then turned each over and read the backs.

One from the Women's Social and Political Union, stamped with their violet ink, addressed in a clerk's hurried hand. One from a solicitor's office in Lincoln's Inn, heavy paper, personal weight. One with a French stamp, soft-cornered, smelling faintly of lavender even through the envelope — that one would be for Odette, and Odette would go pale and carry it unopened to her room, and later would be found sewing twice as fast. One from a boarding house in Southampton with handwriting Astrid didn't know. And one postcard, cheap stock, bearing only a printed image of Brighton Pier and, on the reverse, a single line: *They are making lists.*

No signature. No address beyond this one.

Astrid stood in the hall with the postcard in her hand and the fog pressing at the fanlight above the door, and she read the line four times. Then she set it beneath the others, squared the stack against the table's edge until all the corners agreed, and carried the tea tray up two flights instead, because her hands needed occupation and her mind needed a place to put itself.

Miss Halloway took her breakfast upstairs these days — an affectation, since there was nothing wrong with her legs — and received letters there like a queen holding audience with paper. Astrid knocked, was admitted, poured, and retreated exactly as far as the window seat, which was as far as she ever retreated and no farther.

Miriam Halloway read her correspondence the way she did everything: swiftly, with total commitment, and without any visible doubt. She was fifty-three, straight-backed as a lamppost, with silver coming into her dark hair at the temples like frost claiming high ground. She read the WSPU letter twice, smiled at something in it, and set it face-down with the finality of a chess move.

"Lincoln's Inn next," Astrid said. "Then the French one for Odette, the boarding house, and a postcard."

"You've catalogued my morning already."

"The post comes through my hands, miss. It seems rude not to know what I'm carrying."

"It seems rude," Miriam said, not looking up, "or it seems useful?"

Astrid kept her face the way she kept the motorcar: polished, running, revealing nothing underneath. "Both, presumably."

That earned her a look — the long, level look that Miriam Halloway aimed at people the way a marksman aims at targets, taking measurements. Astrid met it. She had been meeting it for two years now and had learned that the trick was not to blink early. Blinking early told people you had something behind your eyes worth protecting.

"Hmm," said Miriam, which was how she filed things she intended to think about later, and returned to the solicitor's letter.

*They are making lists.* The postcard sat in the stack by the toast rack like a stone in a bread loaf. Astrid poured her own tea from the second pot — drivers drank tea standing, in kitchens, from saucers; Astrid drank hers from the good china on the window seat, a small insubordination she permitted herself daily — and watched the fog burn off the square below. A man was walking a terrier. A sweeper worked the gutters with a broom longer than himself. Ordinary morning, ordinary city, the great machine of London grinding through its gears.

History happened elsewhere. That was Astrid's creed, arrived at young and defended ever since, the way a person defends a belief that has kept her alive precisely because it demanded nothing. History happened in capitals she'd never seen, to families with names in Debrett's, to kings and strikers and polar explorers. It happened in newspapers, which were printed far away and sold by boys who shouted the headlines so you could pass by without buying. It happened to other people. Her own life, by careful arrangement, happened to no one at all — it merely continued, quietly, in a grey wool dress, with clean brass and full ledgers and letters read before anyone else could read them.

Because that was the other thing about information. Sister Edith had run the orphanage on it the way other houses ran on coal. Who had eaten, who had erred, who was to be sent to the laundry at fourteen and the world at sixteen — all of it lived in the ledger on the matron's desk, and the children who understood this survived, and the children who didn't learned late. Astrid had learned at seven, standing in the corridor outside the office, watching through the door glass as her own name was written and ruled and annotated. Names in that book were not descriptions. They were fates. She had spent eleven years learning everything the ledger knew before the ledger knew it — which girls were quarantined, which were favoured, which adoptions were real — and when her own page finally came due, when the matron's pen had come for her at sixteen with a scullery position in Leeds, Astrid had already known for three weeks. Knowledge hadn't changed her fate. But it had meant she walked into it upright, with her collar buttoned, choosing her own stride. That was all information ever gave you. The choice of posture.

She had been reading other people's mail ever since. Not stealing it — she returned everything, unopened-seeming, corners squared — just knowing. Knowing was free. Knowing was the only inheritance she'd ever received, so she collected it assiduously, the way poorer orphans collected buttons.

"You're miles away," Miriam said, without looking up from the solicitor's letter.

"Counting the mileage, miss. Forty today, if the deputation goes ahead. Sixty if it rains."

"It'll go ahead." Miriam folded the letter, tapped it once against the table — her gavel gesture, the one that ended meetings and began campaigns — and looked directly at Astrid for the second time that morning. "There's a census on Sunday. You knew that."

"Everyone knows, miss. They're very keen on counting us."

"They're keen on counting everyone." Something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "There will be forms. The forms ask who sleeps in a house. Not who lives in one — sleeps. The Government has opinions on the difference." She said it lightly, but her thumb pressed the folded letter flat, harder than folding required. "If anyone should ask you about this household, you'll tell them what's true: that you're the driver, that you lodge here at the mistress's convenience, and that the rest is none of your business."

"And is it true, miss? That the rest is none of my business?"

Miriam smiled then, one corner only, and Astrid was reminded — uncomfortably, the way one is reminded of a smell from childhood — that she was not the only person in this house who collected information. "It's true," Miriam said, "that you drive extremely well and ask extremely few questions, and I have often wondered which of those qualities I value more." She gathered her gloves. "The car at nine. And Astrid—"

"Miss?"

"The postcard. The one with the pier on it." Miriam stood, settling her jacket with two precise tugs, and for a moment she looked tired in a way she would deny under oath. "You may leave it on the hall table. Or you may bring it to me privately, whichever your conscience suggests." At the door she paused. "I'm aware my conscience and yours don't consult each other often. But they do seem to arrive at similar conclusions."

She went downstairs to be formidable at someone else, and Astrid sat alone with the cooling teapot and the fog dissolving off the square, and did not move for a full minute.

So. Miss Halloway knew. Had perhaps always known — two years of letters passing through her driver's hands, two years of corners squared and envelopes steamed and resealable wax, and the whole time those level eyes had been taking measurements. Astrid examined the feeling this produced and found it wasn't fear, exactly. It was closer to what she imagined a burglar felt discovering the house had known about him all along and left the window open anyway.

Which meant the open window was either an invitation or a trap, and Miriam Halloway did not, in Astrid's experience, set traps. She set tests. And she never announced which.

Astrid washed the china herself, dried it, set it back in its place, and went down to the hall. The postcard lay where she'd left it. *They are making lists.* She picked it up, held it a moment, and slipped it into her apron pocket instead of the stack — not hiding it, she told herself, merely filing it. Information was her inheritance; she was simply managing the estate.

In the pocket, her fingers found the postcard's cheap edge again and again through the morning, the way a tongue finds a cracked tooth. By eight she had polished the brass a second time it did not need. By nine, when Miriam descended with her hat pinned at the exact angle of defiance and announced the deputation route, Astrid had decided nothing at all and memorised everything: the postmark smudged but likely Brighton, the card stock sold in thousands, the single line written in a woman's hand, hurried, leftward-leaning, and — this was the detail her mind kept returning to, turning it over like a coin suspected of being foreign — written by someone who had pressed hard. Whoever she was, she had wanted the words to survive rough handling.

The engine was warm, the fog burned off, the day assembled itself around them like a stage set. Astrid took the wheel with her quiet hands and pulled out into the traffic of Russell Square, into the great grinding ordinary morning, and told herself — as the motorcar merged among carts and hansom cabs and omnibuses, as London swallowed them and bore them toward whatever the day intended — that history was happening elsewhere. In newspapers. To other people.

Behind them, on the hall table of Number 14, the day's letters sat in their squared stack, one short, and the front door stood open just long enough for the postman's second round, when a man in a bowler hat who was not a postman paused at the railings, consulted a small notebook, and wrote something down.
