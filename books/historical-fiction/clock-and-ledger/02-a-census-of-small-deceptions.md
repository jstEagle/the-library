## 2. A Census of Small Deceptions

Census night came in on a cold spring wind, the kind that rattled the loose pane in the scullery and carried the smell of the river up through the streets like a rumour. Astrid Delacroix felt it before she saw it — a change in the house's breathing, the way the boards under the hall runner seemed to hold their creaks, as though even the timbers understood that tonight the nation would count its heads and every head had better be where it could be accounted for.

She had known the date for weeks. Everyone had. The papers had been full of it for a month — THE CENSUS OF 1911, the suffrage journals running columns about how women, being uncounted as voters, ought at least to be counted as persons, and the more excitable pamphlets urging households here and there to spoil their schedules in protest. Miriam Halloway had considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, pacing the length of the morning room with her hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing an unfavourable field.

"We will fill it in honestly," she had decided at last. "Honestly enough. My name, my profession, my politics stated plainly. It is the *extra* names we needn't be honest about."

And so the household had spent the better part of a week becoming smaller than it was.

Odette Marchand, seamstress, late of Paris by way of a dressmaker's shop in Kensington and a husband whose fists had made the decision for her, was to be recorded not as a boarder but as a visiting cousin from Boulogne, staying only the one night, caught by the census as travellers are caught in nets. Tomas Halloway — cousin to Miriam by some distant and conveniently untraceable branch — was already a cousin, which was the beauty of him; his difficulty lay elsewhere, in questions no census taker would think to ask about regimental numbers and the dates of departures. And Wren.

Wren was the problem. Wren was always the problem, in the way that children who belong to no one are always the problem of whoever has been foolish enough to feed them.

Astrid stood at the kitchen window at half past five in the evening, watching the lamplighter make his slow way down Delancey Street, and turned the child's situation over in her mind the way she turned a starting crank, looking for the catch that would make the engine turn over. Wren had appeared on the step in February with chalk dust on her fingers and nothing else to her name — no name, indeed, that anyone could get out of her, for the girl did not speak. Not wouldn't; Astrid had begun to suspect couldn't, or at any rate hadn't in so long that the muscle of it had gone slack as an unused limb. She had appeared, she had eaten, she had stayed. Nine months old the household was not, but it had developed the instincts of a foundling: it kept what washed up on its doorstep.

"You'll be Miriam's niece," Astrid said now, without turning around. She could feel Wren behind her, seated on the low stool by the range where she always sat, close to the warmth, her small hands folded. "Nieces don't require explanations. Nieces are simply there, like furniture that talks."

The child did not answer, of course. But when Astrid glanced back there was a look in those dark eyes — wary, appraising, entirely too old for the face they occupied — that suggested furniture had heard the joke and filed it away.

* * *

Mr. Pell arrived at seven o'clock precisely, and Astrid disliked him within four seconds.

It was not his appearance, which was unremarkable in the way of little grey men everywhere — a thin coat brushed too often, a bowler hat worn shiny at the crown, a face like a receipt waiting to be filled in. It was not even the leather satchel he carried, though she noted it, noted the way it hung heavy against his hip, noted the brass clasp polished bright with handling. It was that he arrived precisely on time. Men who were merely doing a job arrived whenever the job let them. Men who were *looking* for something arrived on time.

"Miss—" He consulted a card. "Delacroix. The schedule is for Mrs. Halloway's household?"

"Mrs. Halloway is engaged this evening. I act for her." Which was true, and had the additional virtue of being true. "You may come into the parlour."

The parlour had been prepared the way a stage is prepared, and Astrid took a moment's professional satisfaction in the set dressing. Odette's sewing things had been moved upstairs hours ago; the third chair at the supper table had vanished as if it had never existed; the smell of a double supper — for the household had eaten twice tonight, once early and once ostentatiously, so that any caller might catch them at a meal of perfectly ordinary size — still hung pleasantly about the rooms. On the mantel, positioned where a casual eye would find it, stood a photograph of Miriam's brother's family, all four of them including the two nieces who lived, in fact, in Manchester, and one of whom was, tonight, fictionalized in the person of a mute child eating bread and dripping in the kitchen.

Pell sat where he was put, opened his satchel, and produced the schedule with the reverence of a priest producing scripture. A pen. An inkwell of his own, because he trusted no one else's ink. Astrid watched his hands — small, neat, deliberate — and thought, quite suddenly and quite uselessly, of the ledger in the orphanage office at Harmondsworth, the great black book in which her own name had been entered at six years old in someone else's careless hand, *Astrid Delacroix, parentage unknown*, and later crossed through with a single stroke when she was deemed old enough to be somebody else's problem. Ledgers, she had learned early, were never neutral. Somewhere behind every column of figures stood a person deciding what you were worth writing down.

"Now then." Pell smoothed the schedule flat on his knee. "Head one. Name and surname of each person abiding herein on this night."

"Miriam Halloway. Astrid Delacroix." She gave her own name second, deliberately, folding herself into the structure of the house as though she had always been part of its architecture. "Halloway cousins, two. Odette Marchand, visiting from Boulogne — she'll be gone by the week's end, so you may note her as a visitor. And Miriam's young niece, stopping with her aunt while her parents are abroad."

Something happened to Pell's face then. Nothing dramatic — a stillness, rather, the stillness of a man setting down a load carefully before examining it.

"Abroad," he repeated.

"In India," said Astrid, with the mild boredom of a woman discussing weather in a country she'd never visited. "Army family. One keeps moving."

"Mm." His pen moved. She watched the letters form — watched, with the particular skill of a woman who had read upside-down correspondence for three years, the word *India* appear and then, beside it, in the margin, something short and private. A tick. A dash. She could not tell which, and the not-telling sat down inside her chest and stayed.

He worked through the columns. Ages — she gave ages, smooth as poured cream, each one rehearsed that afternoon with the household over the washing-up. Occupations — *seamstress* for Odette, because a lie works best wearing a true coat; *of independent means* for Tomas, delivered while she looked steadily at Pell's left ear. Relation to head of household — niece, niece, cousin, visitor. The pen scratched. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them a floorboard shifted under a foot that was trying very hard not to exist, and Astrid raised her voice slightly, continuing a sentence about the household's excellent health, until the board went quiet.

At last Pell set down the pen and looked at her directly for the first time since entering the room. His eyes were pale, watercolour eyes, the sort that seemed weak until you noticed they missed nothing.

"A tidy household," he said.

"We do our best."

"Mrs. Halloway. That's the suffragist. The one who speaks at the halls." He said it without inflection, the way a man reads an item off a list. "I've seen the name in the papers."

"I believe she has been in the papers, yes."

"And the young lady cousin — Marchand. French, you said."

"Boulogne," Astrid agreed. "Though her English is very good. Better than her stories about why, if you take my meaning, but one doesn't pry into a guest's history." She smiled, letting the smile do a small amount of work: *we are both professionals, you and I; we know people have histories.*

Pell did not return the smile. He gathered his papers with small precise taps, squaring the edges, and then — pausing with the schedule half-slid into its folder — asked the question that was one too many.

"The little girl," he said. "Your mistress's niece. What is her name? Her Christian name. For the schedule — the enumerator requires it."

The fire ticked. Upstairs, silence pressed itself into the floorboards.

And here was the crack in the evening, hairline-thin, running through the plaster: Astrid realized she did not know. In eight weeks of feeding her, bathing her, mending her one good coat and lying awake listening to her breathe through the night terrors, Astrid had never once needed to give the child a name, because the child had none, and the household, in its gentle conspiracy, had simply never said one aloud where the walls might hear. They had called her Wren — but Wren was a thing they called her, soft and private, a name like a held breath, never entered anywhere, never written down.

"Wren," Astrid said. Her voice did not hesitate; she had trained it too well for that. "Her name is Wren. Her father was a great one for birds."

Pell wrote it. And as his pen moved, Astrid watched him add, after the name, a small mark in the margin — the same mark, or nearly, that he had made beside *India*. Two marks now. A pattern forming, like the first drops before rain finds your shoulder.

"That's an unusual Christian name," Pell observed mildly, capping his ink.

"It's a family name," said Astrid. "On the mother's side."

"Of course." He rose, buttoned his coat, settled the satchel against his hip with that heavy, familiar weight. At the parlour door he paused, and turned back with the air of a man remembering a courtesy. "You'll understand, miss, that the enumerators check these schedules against one another. Names come up again, you see. Names that shouldn't be where they are. It's remarkable, the little arithmetic errors a household can make. A person counted twice. A person counted who was never born at all." He put on his bowler hat. "Good evening to you."

The door closed. Astrid stood in the quiet parlour with the fire dying and the marginal marks burning in her memory like brands, and told herself she was being fanciful. Little grey men wrote little grey notes; the machinery of the state ground on indifferently; history, as she had always known, happened to other people, in other houses, on streets she would never drive down.

Then she went out to the pavement to fetch in the milk, as she did every night, and looked down.

There, in the gutter by the area steps, drawn in chalk that the evening damp had not yet reached: a small figure of a man. A tall stick of a man with a hat, and beneath the hat-brim two dots for eyes, and beside the figure, drawn with the grave precision children give to important things, a little black book.

Wren had seen him arrive. Wren had been watching from the window the whole time.

Astrid crouched in the cold, the milk bottle forgotten in her hand, and looked at the chalk man looking back at her with his dot eyes, and understood — slowly, the way cold comes through boot soles — that the first crack had already opened. Not in the house. In the world's attention. Someone had noticed them, had written something in a margin, and the household of borrowed names had passed, in the space of one evening, from invisible to observed.

Behind her, the front door creaked. She turned. Wren stood in the light of the hall, one hand still dusty with chalk, looking at Astrid with those too-old eyes as if to say: *I saw him. Did you?*

"Yes," Astrid said quietly, to the child, to the street, to the closing dark. "I did."

She brought the milk in, bolted the door, and stood a long moment with her back against it, listening to the house breathe around its secrets — and thinking, for the first time in three years of reading other people's letters, about the letter that might someday be written *about* them.
