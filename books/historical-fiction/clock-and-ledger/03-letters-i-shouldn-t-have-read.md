## 3. Letters I Shouldn't Have Read

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing at the time, though I would come to think of Tuesdays differently for the rest of my life.

It came with the four o'clock post, wedged between a circular from the Coal and Light Company and a thick envelope addressed to Miriam in a hand I did not recognize — small, cramped, pressed hard enough into the paper that I could feel the ghost of each letter under my thumb before I ever broke the seal. The others went into the wicker basket on the hall table, where they belonged. This one stayed in my hand a moment longer than it should have.

I want to be honest here, since honesty comes so rarely and costs so much: I read every letter that passes through this house.

I do not steal them. That is the distinction I have built my conscience upon, the little brick wall between myself and the woman I might otherwise be. The letters come through my hands because I am the one who answers the door, who signs for the registered post, who carries the basket up to Miriam's study at half past five when the light has gone gold behind the plane trees on Russell Square. And in the carrying — in those forty steps up the stair, in the pause I take on the landing where the wallpaper roses have faded to the color of old bruises — I read.

I tell myself it is diligence. A driver must know where her mistress's affairs stand; a household like ours runs on knowing which questions might come and from whom. I tell myself it is habit, and habit it certainly is, though the root of the habit goes down into soil I do not like to dig in.

* * *

St. Agatha's Home for Orphaned and Destitute Girls kept a ledger. It was green, bound in cloth gone bald at the corners, and it lived on Miss Winterbottom's desk like a third person in the room. Every child in that place had a line in it. Name. Date of intake. Provenance, if known. Disposition.

I learned to read upside down before I learned to read right way round, crouched by the cold radiator in the office while Miss Winterbottom took her sherry in the next room, tracing the entries from across the desk with my eyes. There is a particular literacy that belongs to children who have nothing — we learn to extract information from posture, from the length of a pause, from which coat gets mended and which is let out at the seams. The ledger was only the written form of everything else.

My line read: *Delacroix, Astrid. b. 1893. Father unknown. Mother deceased, St. Pancras workhouse infirmary.*

And then, in November of 1901, when I was eight and had done nothing but exist in the wrong place at the wrong hour: *Transferred.* My name struck through with a single ruler-straight line, black ink over the faded brown, so that for a moment — just a moment, until the matron corrected herself and moved me to a new page under a new heading — I ceased to be a person and became an error, corrected.

I have never forgotten what ink looks like when it decides you are finished. Most people go their whole lives without seeing it. I was eight years old and I understood, with the terrible completeness that only children understand things, that a name on a page is not a description of a person. It is a handle. Whoever holds the book can lift you by it, carry you, put you down somewhere else, or cross you out entirely.

So yes. I read the letters. Not because I am curious about other people's lives, but because I have known since I was eight years old what happens to people who do not know what is written about them until it is too late.

* * *

On that Tuesday, the letter with the cramped hand sat in my apron pocket through supper, through the washing-up, through Odette humming over her mending by the kitchen fire while Wren stacked chalk sticks on the flagstones in order of size — white, white, blue, white — and Tomas cleaned his boots for the third time, which is what he does instead of pacing.

At half past ten the house went quiet in its particular way: Odette's candle up the stairs, Tomas's door clicking shut, Wren's small weight settling into the box bed off the linen room that she still would not sleep in unless Astrid — unless I — had checked under it first. I checked. There was nothing under it. There is never anything under it. She watches me check anyway, and I watch her watch me, and this is our liturgy.

Then I sat down on the second stair from the bottom, where the gaslight from the hall does not quite reach, and I opened the letter.

*Dear Madam Halloway,*

*I write as a friend, though we have not met, and I must ask you to forgive the anonymity which prudence requires. There is a man. You will know him soon if you do not already — Vane, of the Public Control Department, a tall creature with a soft voice and a ledger he touches more often than he reads. He keeps names. Yours is among them. I have seen the page.*

*It is not only your speeches they mind, though they mind those. It is the house. He asks after the house — who sleeps there, how many at table, whether the census taken in April answered truly. Pell the enumerator has been back twice to the district office with amendments. A man who amends a census once is a clerk. A man who amends it twice is a hunter.*

*I will say no more by post. Burn this.*

*Yours, etc.*

There was no signature. Of course there was no signature. But the paper was good stock, and the postmark was E.C., and the hand was a woman's — I knew it the way I know weather, from the loop of the g and the way the crossings of the t's slanted back as if the writer flinched even while writing.

I read it three times. On the third reading my own hands were shaking, which offended me. I have carried this body through ice, through engine trouble on the Great North Road at midnight, through the whole apparatus of keeping four irregular lives running on one regular income, and my hands do not shake.

But this was different. This was not weather. This was the ledger.

*A man who amends a census twice is a hunter.*

I thought of Mr. Pell — oh, I could see him still, that evening in April when the whole country sat down to be counted, the little man with his satchel and his official pencil and his questions that slid sideways like feet on wet pavement. How many persons slept in the house last night? Seven, I told him, and gave him seven names, seven true-enough names, and watched him write them down while something behind his eyes ticked and sorted like a cash register. He had looked at Odette's workbasket. He had looked at Tomas's boots, military boots, whatever Tomas believed about them. He had looked longest of all at Wren, who looked back with that flat dark patience she has, and then Pell wrote something in the margin of his schedule — a small mark, a tick or a dash, I could not see which — and thanked us, and touched his hat, and was gone.

I had thought, these six months, that we had passed through. Papers filed, night survived, life resumed. That is the arrogance of the unexamined life: you believe the absence of consequences is the same thing as safety.

Now some anonymous woman with good stationery was telling me the clock had never stopped. It had simply been ticking somewhere I couldn't hear.

* * *

I took the letter toward the kitchen range.

This is the sensible part of the story, and I want to give it its due. The instruction was clear — *burn this* — and I am, whatever else, obedient to sense. The coals were banked but alive; one breath of the poker and the red showed through like an eye opening. I held the letter by its corner. The paper was heavy enough to catch slowly, the way good paper does, curling first at the edges like a leaf in autumn.

I nearly did it. Let that stand in the record. There is a version of Astrid Delacroix, perhaps the wiser version, who fed that page to the fire at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday night and went to bed and slept, and never knew how close history had come to her address until it knocked.

But I stood there with the fire breathing at my knuckles and all I could think was: *a name crossed out cannot be uncrossed.*

If I burned it, the words would survive nowhere but in my memory, and my memory — I know this about myself — is a loyal servant with a talent for improving stories. If it came to it, if Vane stood in the hall with his ledger open and asked me what exactly the warning had said, I would need the thing itself, the actual strokes of the actual pen, because a lie can be caught but a document defends itself. Evidence is the poor man's witness. I had learned that too, at St. Agatha's, the year Miss Winterbottom's ledger contradicted three separate claims about a missing shilling and saved me from a caning, because ink does not get nervous and ink does not forget.

Besides — and here is the part I like least — I wanted to keep it. I wanted the letter the way a soldier wants the map. Because folded inside that warning, between the lines of another person's fear, was information, and information is the only inheritance I have ever been left.

I pulled it back from the heat. The top corner had begun to brown, a scorch the shape of a comma, as if the fire had paused mid-sentence. I pressed the paper flat against my stomach until it cooled.

* * *

Where does one hide a thing in a house that may be searched?

Not the flour bin — everyone remembers the flour bin from novels. Not under a floorboard; the boards in that house announced themselves like stage doors. Not sewn into a hem, because Odette sewed the hems in that house, and Odette missed nothing, and I was not ready — God knows I was not ready — for Odette to know what I knew.

In the end I thought of the motorcar.

People hide papers in houses because houses are where papers belong, and so houses are where people look. Nobody looks in a motorcar. And the Darracq had a place — every old car has places — a gap behind the kick panel where the wiring ran, warm and dry and dark, accessible only if you lay half into the footwell with your cheek against the leather and knew precisely where to reach. I had found it the first winter, chasing a rattle. I wrapped the letter in oilcloth from the tool roll, worked it flat into the gap, and sat back on the gravel drive with the smell of engine grease on my fingers and my heart going like a low idle.

Above me the house was dark except for Miriam's study window, where the lamp still burned. Her shadow crossed the light once, unhurried, a woman at her desk in the last hour of her ownership of her own life, though neither of us knew it yet.

I thought: *she would want to know.*

I thought, immediately after: *and what would she do with knowing? She would burn nothing and change nothing and stand in the front hall when Vane came, and say something magnificent, and be taken for it — and the house would empty within the month.*

Miriam Halloway is the bravest person I have ever known, and bravery, I have noticed, is frequently the luxury of people who have someone else holding the practical end of their courage. Someone has to drive the car away from the prison gates. Someone has to count the coal. Someone has to know, ahead of time, exactly how many mouths will need feeding when the world starts taking names.

That someone, apparently, was me. It had been me for years, in the small ways — the letters, the ledgers in my head, the seven names rehearsed against questions. Tonight it had simply become official.

I looked up at her window again and felt something I could not immediately name, and then could, and wished I couldn't: it was the feeling of being eight years old in an office, watching a ruled line descend toward my own name. Except this time the name under the pen was hers. And this time — this was the strange, new, frightening part — I found I cared what the ink did. Not because of what losing her would cost the household. Because of what losing her would cost me.

History, I had always maintained, is a thing that happens to other people. It happens in Westminster and in Manchester factory yards and in the Balkans, wherever they were being Balkan that season, and it is carried to my awareness in newspapers which I also, incidentally, read before Miriam does.

But history, it seemed, had learned to write letters. History had a cramped female hand and an E.C. postmark and instructions to burn. History had a name on a list, and the list had a keeper, and the keeper had not yet come to the door — but a man who amends a census twice is a hunter, and hunters know the way to any address they please.

* * *

I did not sleep well. At some grey hour I heard Wren crying out in the linen room — the night terrors, wordless as always — and I went and sat by her until she subsided, her small hot hand fastened around two of my fingers, her breathing ragged and then slow. In the dark she looked at me with those eyes that seem to hold accounts of everything, and I whispered the usual nonsense — *nothing under the bed, nothing at the window, nothing but night* — and I wondered, for the first time, whether it was already a lie.

Dawn came up thin and silver. I was in the drive before six, polishing the Darracq's lamps with the letter's oilcloth square burning a small hole of awareness in the pocket of my mind, going over the seven names like a rosary.

And then the post came early, and the boy whistled, and there at the hall table, among the ordinary envelopes, lay a buff-coloured one with an official crest and a printed corner that read:

**Public Control Department — Enquiries Division.**

Addressed, in a hand that was not cramped at all but broad, patient, unhurried, to Mrs. Miriam Halloway.

Beneath it, in smaller type, the words that stopped my blood: *Re: Enumeration Irregularities, April Census.*
