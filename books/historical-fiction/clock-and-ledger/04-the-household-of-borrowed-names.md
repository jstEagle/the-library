## 4. The Household of Borrowed Names

Miriam Halloway had learned long ago that a house was not made of bricks. Bricks were merely what houses wore when they wished to be mistaken for something honest. A house was made of names — the ones spoken on the doorstep, the ones whispered up the stairwell, the ones never said at all, kept warm in the chest like stones carried from a fire.

Hers had four names now, if you counted her own, and she did, though some mornings she woke uncertain which of them she was meant to be. To the rate-collector she was Mrs. Halloway of number eleven, widow, woman of independent means and inconvenient opinions. To the women who came through her kitchen on Thursday evenings she was Madam, or Miss Miriam, or simply *her* — the one who signed the bail papers and knew which magistrate took bribes and which merely took offence. To Odette she was, tentatively, almost; to Tomas she was the last argument against despair; to Wren she was nothing so grand as a guardian, only the tall shape at the end of the table who always left the crusts.

And to Astrid Delacroix — ah. To Astrid she was an employer, a cause, a puzzle, and perhaps, if the girl ever allowed the thought past her guard, something dangerously near a home.

Miriam stood at her bedroom window in the grey beginning of the day and watched the motorcar below, its bonnet already open, Astrid bent over the engine with her sleeves pinned back and her fair hair escaping its net in pale wisps. The girl polished that car as other people prayed: thoroughly, ritually, with her whole attention given over to a machine that could not disappoint her. There was comfort in watching it. There was also, Miriam admitted, a kind of heartbreak, though she would have bitten through her own tongue before saying so aloud.

She had been observing Astrid Delacroix for two years now, and she flattered herself that she observed well. She had seen how the girl's eyes moved across an envelope before handing it over — not greedily, not carelessly, but with the quick, hungry fluency of someone who read the way other people breathed. She had noticed how Astrid could recite, unprompted, which correspondence awaited a reply, which letters had come from solicitors and which from sisters, which bore French stamps and which the smudged postmarks of northern mill towns where the movement's money mostly lived. Once, early on, Miriam had tested her deliberately: a letter left face-up on the hall table, containing nothing but a recipe and a fictitious sum of five pounds. Astrid had mentioned the recipe that evening. She had not mentioned the five pounds, because there had been no five pounds, but she had not known there was none until she'd read it.

That had been nearly eighteen months ago. Miriam had said nothing then, and she said nothing now. It was not forgiveness she was withholding — there was nothing to forgive, or rather, the thing to forgive was so entangled with the thing to admire that pulling one thread would unravel both. She let Astrid read. She watched what the girl did with what she read. And so far, in two years of stolen glances and memorised postmarks, Astrid Delacroix had betrayed every secret precisely nowhere — except into the careful vault of her own silence.

Which was, Miriam thought, drawing on her gloves by the cold window, either the most loyal thing she had ever witnessed or the most dangerous. With this girl it was impossible to say where one ended and the other began.

* * *

Downstairs, the house was performing its morning transformation.

This was Miriam's favourite hour and the one that cost her the most, this daily conjuring in which a household of irregular persons became, for the benefit of the street, a respectable establishment of exactly the persons listed on the rent book. It happened in stages, like a theatre struck and reset between acts. Odette went down first, always, because Odette's story was the simplest to maintain: a seamstress employed by the day, arrived at half-past seven through the area door as any needlewoman might, her trunk having entered the house weeks ago under cover of darkness and now masquerading in the attic as a collection of hatboxes. Then Tomas, whose performance required more discipline — he must be Cousin Edwin today, Edwin of the weak chest and the private income, Edwin who took his walking tour of the garden at precisely the hours when the neighbours' curtains were least likely to twitch. Edwin did not exist. Edwin had never existed. But Edwin walked the garden twice a day with his hands clasped behind him like a man with nothing on his conscience, and Miriam sometimes thought the fiction deserved better than it got.

Wren required no performance at all, which was the difficulty. Children were the one irregularity no alias could smooth. A child appearing at a breakfast table asked questions of every visitor faster than any inspector could: whose is she, where does she sleep, why does she not speak. So Wren lived upstairs during daylight hours, in the room Miriam had fitted out with a slate, a box of chalks, and a window facing the plane tree, and came down to meals only when the house was sealed — doors locked, blinds drawn, the kitchen converted by lamplight into the warm country it truly was.

Second suppers, Astrid called them, and Miriam had laughed the first time — a startled, undignified sound she hadn't produced in years — because the girl was right. The household ate twice each evening: once at six, respectably, the family at table, Edwin presiding over the cold mutton; and once at nine, truly, when the lamps came low and Odette sang Provençal songs under her breath and Wren arranged her chalks in order of colour and Tomas allowed himself, for one hour, to stop being Edwin and remember that his name was Tomas and that he had once had a brother.

It was a fragile architecture. Miriam knew this better than anyone, having designed it. Every refuge she had ever built was a thing of matchsticks and held breath. She had been building them since she was nineteen and had sheltered her first runaway — a governess, that time, pregnant by her employer's son, hidden for five months in Miriam's rented rooms in Bloomsbury while Miriam learned to forge references and intercept post and lie to landladies with a serenity that astonished even herself. That was thirty years gone. Since then there had been others, dozens, a quiet succession of the hunted passing through her doors: women fleeing husbands, men fleeing armies, girls fleeing the workhouse, one memorable bishop's curate fleeing his own convictions. The house in Ladbroke Grove was only the largest and longest-lived of her constructions, and like all her houses it ran on a single fuel, which was the conviction that a person's life ought to belong to the person living it.

She descended the stairs now with her hand on the banister, counting, as she always counted. One — Odette in the kitchen, the smell of coffee and scorched toast. Two — Astrid in the hall, sorting the morning post into the wooden rack with those quick hands. Three — the sound of Edwin's measured footsteps in the garden, gravel crunching at the pace of a man with no cares. Four — silence from above, which meant Wren still slept, and Wren sleeping was worth more than most of the peace treaties Miriam had spent her life negotiating.

"Post," Astrid said, without looking up. "Two circulars, the *Votes for Women*, a bill from the coal merchant — I've put it on your desk — and this." She held out a thick envelope, cream-coloured, addressed in a hand Miriam did not immediately recognise.

Miriam took it. Their fingers did not quite touch. Astrid's gaze rested on the envelope a half-second longer than courtesy required — reading, of course, the way she always read: the postmark, the direction of the slant, the weight of the thing. Miriam watched her do it and felt again that peculiar doubled sensation, being the watcher and the watched at once.

"Anything of interest?" Miriam asked mildly.

Astrid's eyes came up, steady and blue-grey and utterly unrevealing. "The seal's unbroken, ma'am."

"I didn't ask whether you'd opened it. I asked whether it interested you."

A pause, brief as a caught breath. Something moved behind the girl's composure — surprise, perhaps, or the beginning of a calculation about what was safe to admit. "The handwriting's legal," she said at last. "Solicitor's clerk, by the formation. They charge by the letter, that class of penmanship." And then, because she was Astrid, and because evasion sat on her like an ill-fitting coat: "It's heavier than a bill. Paper like that costs more than most people earn in a week."

"Thank you," said Miriam, and went into the morning room, and broke the seal, and read, and felt the floor of her carefully built world tilt one degree beneath her feet.

* * *

She did not burn the letter. She had burned letters before — there was a particular skill to it, choosing the moment when the coals were right, feeding the paper in a corner at a time so no legible fragment survived — but this one she folded along its original creases and placed inside the false bottom of her writing desk, beside the list of names she kept of everyone who had ever passed through these doors. Some documents were more dangerous kept than destroyed, because keeping them meant she believed she would need them again.

The letter said, in the circumspect language of solicitors obliged to be discreet about their own discretion, that certain enquiries had been made. That a government office had retained counsel. That the matter concerned the census and its schedules and the penalties attaching to false statements therein, and that a name resembling hers had appeared in correspondence not intended for her eyes. It said, in its final line, *I would advise you, madam, to put your affairs in order*, and Miriam sat looking at that sentence for a long while while the morning light crawled across the carpet.

Put her affairs in order. Her affairs. Odette upstairs pretending to hem napkins. Edwin pacing the garden with his borrowed calm. Wren asleep under the plane-tree window with a chalk stub curled in her fist. Astrid in the hall, memorising the mail.

Her affairs were people. There was no ledger in which they could be ordered.

She rose and went to the window and saw Astrid crossing to the motorcar, and it occurred to her — not for the first time, but with new weight — that everything she had built was now standing in a driveway wearing driving gauntlets and trying not to look frightened.

* * *

They drove that afternoon to Hampstead, ostensibly to deliver a petition to a sympathetic alderman, actually because Miriam wanted to talk where the walls of the house could not listen. Even her own rooms had begun to feel porous. Pell's visit three nights ago still sat in her mind like a stone in a shoe: the little man with his brass-clipped forms and his questions dressed as arithmetic. *Names of all persons sleeping here on the night of April the second.* He had written something down after Astrid's answers — Miriam had seen the pencil move from the corner of her eye, had heard the small industrious scratch of it — and whatever he had written had not yet stopped scratching.

"You handled him beautifully," Miriam said, as the plane trees of Finchley Road slid past.

"He handled himself," Astrid replied, eyes on the road. Her hands on the wheel were sure and separate from her voice, the way a pianist's hands are separate from her breathing. "Men like that don't need help finding things. They just need you to stand still while they look."

"And did we stand still?"

"We stood," said Astrid, "exactly where you told me to stand."

There it was again, Miriam thought. The loyalty offered like a fact rather than a feeling — measurable, verifiable, and therefore, to a woman of Miriam's experience, infinitely more precious than any declaration. Astrid Delacroix did not say *I would die for you*. She said *I stood where you told me to stand*, and expected nothing in return, and this reticence told Miriam more than any speech could have about the orphanage the girl had come from, the institution that had taught her that affection was a currency one earned through usefulness and could lose through error.

"Pell will report to someone," Miriam said. "Census men always do, when the numbers don't please them. And the someone will likely be a man named Vane, and Vane keeps lists."

"I know."

Miriam turned her head. "You know."

"The shipping clerk at Royal Albert Docks talks when he's bored," Astrid said, still watching the road. "He mentioned an inspector making enquiries about emigrant berths. Flagging departures. He thought it was about anarchists." A beat. "It isn't, is it."

For a moment Miriam could not speak. Not from fear — fear had become, over the decades, a familiar lodger she no longer troubled to entertain — but from the strange vertigo of seeing her own deductions arrive secondhand, out of the mouth of the person she had been most careful never to confide in. Astrid had known. Astrid had pieced it together from a bored clerk's gossip and said nothing, told no one, carried it alone the way she carried everything: silently, efficiently, in that vault of hers.

"How long have you known?" Miriam asked quietly.

"Since Tuesday."

Three days. Three days of polishing the motorcar and sorting the post and standing exactly where she was told to stand, with that knowledge folded away inside her like a letter she would not open.

"Astrid." Miriam waited until the girl glanced sideways, just briefly, before returning her eyes to the road. "Why do you read my letters?"

The question landed in the car like a dropped glass. Astrid's knuckles whitened on the wheel; the engine sang; a dray horse clopped past going the other way, and the driver touched his cap to the lady in the fine motorcar without knowing he had just driven through the middle of a siege.

"Because information is the only thing nobody can take off you once you've got it," Astrid said finally, to the windscreen rather than to Miriam. "In the home they locked everything. The linen cupboard, the store room, the ledger with our names in it. Everything except what you could overhear. So I learned to overhear. I learned to read upside down and through envelopes and between lines." Her jaw tightened. "I'm not sorry. I should be, and I'm not."

"I know you're not," said Miriam. "That's rather the point of you."

And because she was who she was — a woman who had spent thirty years believing that trust was not a gift one bestowed but a wager one laid down and let ride — she reached into her reticule and drew out the cream envelope, empty now, its letter resting in the false bottom of her desk three miles behind them.

"This came this morning. It says my name has appeared on an inspector's list. It advises me to put my affairs in order." She held it out, flat on her open palm, the way one offers proof to a jury. "You've already read it, of course."

Astrid's glance flicked down and back up. "No, ma'am. This one I haven't."

Miriam looked at her, and looked, and believed her — believed her precisely because Astrid did not protest too much, did not dress the denial in wounded virtue, simply stated it as fact and returned her eyes to the road. The girl was telling her the truth. Which meant that somewhere in the last three days, without instruction or invitation, Astrid Delacroix had drawn a line — had decided that some things in this house were hers to carry and some were Miriam's, and had chosen, unasked, to bear the heavier share.

"Well," Miriam said, folding the envelope closed. "Then you'll want to read it now. Before we're home. There's a bench by the pond in the park ahead, and I find conspiracies go better with water."

Astrid looked at her then — really looked, the guard dropping for one unguarded instant, and Miriam saw underneath it the thing the girl had been hiding all along, which was not guilt but hope, hope so old and starved it had learned to pass for competence.

"Yes, ma'am," said Astrid, and turned the motorcar toward the water.

Behind them, in the garden of number eleven, a man called Edwin paused in his circuit of the roses and looked up at a sky going the colour of pewter, and did not know — none of them knew, not yet — that by Friday there would be a rally at Caxton Hall, and that history, which had so far been content to circle the house like a cat around a birdcage, was preparing to put its paw through the bars.
