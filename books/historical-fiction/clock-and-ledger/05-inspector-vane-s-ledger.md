## 5. Inspector Vane's Ledger

The ledger weighed four pounds, eleven ounces. Edmund Vane knew this because he had weighed it, once, on the brass scales in his office at Scotland Yard, in the small grey hour before dawn when the building belonged to him alone and a man could admit to himself what he was carrying. Four pounds, eleven ounces of calfskin and India paper and the names of two hundred and six persons whose lives did not fit the forms the Crown had provided for them. He had carried it every working day for nine years. His shoulder had begun to list to the left, like a ship taking on water it refused to name.

He arrived at No. 14 Cranbourne Row at half past ten in the morning, when the fog had thinned to a yellow gauze through which the house showed itself reluctantly — three storeys of sooty brick, white sills scrubbed by some diligent hand, geraniums dying honourably in a window box against the season. A motorcar stood at the kerb, polished to an obscenity of brightness, its lamps catching the light like the eyes of something awake.

Vane stood before it a moment and let his gloved palm rest on the bonnet. Still warm. The engine ticked as it cooled, a sound like a clock deciding whether to keep honest time. He found, as he often did, that machines told him more about their owners than faces did. This one was cared for with a devotion that bordered on the devotional. Somebody in that house believed in polish. Believed that if every surface gleamed enough, no dirt of any kind could touch them.

He almost pitied them.

Mr. Pell waited at the gate, notebook clutched to his chest like a hymnal, breath steaming. He was a narrow man, Pell, made entirely of angles and diligence, the sort of clerk who counted other people's spoons. Vane did not dislike him — dislike required warmth of some kind — but he noted how Pell's eyes skittered away from the upstairs windows, and filed the skittering where he filed everything.

"The household," Pell said, consulting his notes though he plainly knew them by heart, "consists of the widow Halloway, aged forty-six, occupation given as 'writer.' A chauffeuse — a female driver, sir, Delacroix by name, foreign-sounding. And then—" his voice dropped, pleased and frightened at once, "—the irregulars."

"Say them again."

"A seamstress registered nowhere, Odette Fournier or Fourniér or some such, appeared on the census night without papers. A male lodger answering to Tomas, who claims kinship to the widow but whom the widow's late husband's family — I wrote to them, sir, I wrote to Shropshire — has never heard of. And a child." Pell hesitated over this last, and Vane watched his pen-hand tremble faintly. "A girl of perhaps seven. No name on the schedule. The widow wrote 'Wren' in the space for surname and drew a line through the space for Christian name. As if the bird were sufficient."

"As if," Vane said, "the child were weather."

"Yes, sir. That is — yes."

They went up the path together. The door opened before Pell's knuckles reached it, which told Vane the household had been watching from an upper window, counting their spoons in their turn. The woman who admitted them was tall, dark-haired, dressed in the plain good wool of a person who had decided mourning was a colour rather than an event. Her handshake was brief and level. She looked at Vane the way people look at weather they have decided not to be inconvenienced by.

"Inspector Vane," he said. "Home Office inquiry. This is Mr. Pell, census enforcement. We shan't take above an hour."

"You'll take precisely as long as you take," Miriam Halloway said, "and you'll take it in the morning room, where there's a fire. The parlour chimney draws badly and I won't have you breathing soot on my conscience as well as everything else."

He liked her immediately, which was inconvenient, and he allowed himself exactly four seconds of liking before he folded it away with the rest of his private inventory.

* * *

The morning room was warm and smelled of coffee, burnt toast, and sewing-machine oil — an odd trinity, and Vane catalogued it. A mending basket sat open by the settee. On the table lay a newspaper folded to the letters page, where someone had underlined a paragraph about the Cat and Mouse Act with a pencil stroke so furious it had torn the newsprint. Everything in the room was evidence, and none of it was admissible, and all of it was alive.

Miss Delacroix was already there.

She stood at the sideboard pouring coffee, and she did not ask whether he took sugar; she simply set a cup before him, black, and a small jug beside it, unasked. He marked that. A servant's instinct, or a spy's. With women of her apparent class — and her accent, when she finally spoke, would be neither cockney nor Mayfair, but something scrubbed and careful, an accent built rather than born — one never knew which instinct paid better.

Pell arranged himself on the edge of a chair with his notebook open. Vane remained standing. It was not theatre, whatever Miss Delacroix might think; his knees had been unreliable since Kandahar and standing kept the ledger's weight distributed. He opened it across his palms.

"Mrs. Halloway. On the night of Sunday the second of April, a schedule was delivered to this address and completed, per statute, by the head of household. That schedule records four persons resident: yourself, your driver, your cook-maid, and a child called Wren. Is that correct?"

"It is what I wrote."

"It is not," Vane said gently, "what is true."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard complained beneath a foot that was trying very hard not to exist. Vane heard it; he saw Miss Delacroix hear it too; he saw her decide not to look at the ceiling, and her not-looking was more eloquent than any glance.

"Madam," he continued, turning a page, "I hold here Mr. Pell's supplementary notes. They record a seamstress, resident since at least February, who gave no surname to the census taker and whose work — I am told by the draper on the corner, a Mr. Hobbes, who talks more than he stitches — bears a label reading simply *O*. They record a young man seen carrying coal at six in the morning who wears his hat indoors and does not appear on any schedule. And they record a child for whom no birth certificate exists in any parish registry between Whitechapel and Ealing. I have looked. I am thorough. It is my single virtue and it has ruined every dinner party I have ever attended."

Miriam Halloway did not smile. But something moved at the corner of her mouth, a muscle remembering the shape.

"What is it you imagine I've done, Inspector? Fed people? In my own house?"

"I imagine," Vane said, "that you have done what many well-meaning persons do, which is to mistake your drawing room for the world. The world, madam, has forms. A girl sleeping in your attic without papers is, to the world, a discrepancy. A man of military age who is not in France and not on a register is a discrepancy. A child who cannot be produced for inspection—" and here his voice did not change at all, which was the cruelty of it, "—is a discrepancy of a kind the law takes seriously indeed."

"And what does the law do with discrepancies?" It was Miss Delacroix who spoke, setting down the coffee pot with a click of china on silver. "Weigh them? File them?"

Vane turned to look at her properly for the first time.

She was younger than he had expected — five-and-twenty perhaps — with hands that told a plainer story than her face did. Knuckles nicked and oil-stained despite their washing. A burn scar at the base of one thumb, old, from an engine manifold. Eyes the flat grey-green of the Thames in January, and behind them, moving constantly, a reckoning. He recognised the reckoning. He wore the same one behind his own eyes every day: *how much do you know, how fast can I count it, what will it cost*.

"Everything," he said, "is weighed eventually, Miss Delacroix. That is what ledgers are for. Yours—" he tapped Pell's notebook, then his own, "—and everyone's."

"My employer pays me to drive," she said. "I drive. If you want a confession, Inspector, I once exceeded eleven miles an hour on the Euston Road, and I'd do it again."

Pell's pen scratched, delighted. Vane let the silence sit until even Pell stopped writing.

"Here is what will happen," Vane said at last, quietly, folding the ledger closed upon his thumb. "Nothing, today. Today I make inquiries. I verify references. I return to my office and I write down what I have seen, which includes a very clean motorcar, a torn newspaper, and a floorboard on the landing that needs a carpenter far more than this house needs secrets. Within the fortnight I shall call again. If by then every soul under this roof can be matched to a page in somebody's book, I shall close my ledger and trouble you no further. If not—" he inclined his head to Miriam Halloway, courteous as an undertaker, "—the discrepancies become charges, and charges become courts, and courts are weather no umbrella has ever beaten."

"You've done this before," Miriam said. It was not a question.

"Nine years, madam."

"And does it ever weigh on you? All those pages?"

Vane considered the question honestly, because she had asked it honestly, and because liars deserve the courtesy of true answers occasionally.

"The ledger weighs four pounds, eleven ounces," he said. "I know because I weighed it. Everything else—" he adjusted the strap on his shoulder, the small habitual motion, "—weighs nothing at all. Good morning."

* * *

In the hall, waiting while Pell fussed with his galoshes, Vane saw the photograph.

It was pinned inside his own coat — had been pinned there for six years, so long the pin had rusted a small orange halo into the cardboard — and it was habit, not accident, that made him check it sat secure, the way another man might pat a watch pocket. But his hand moved too slowly, or her eyes were too quick, and when he glanced up Miss Delacroix was looking directly at the small rectangle of card where it showed between his lapels: a girl of eight in a sailor collar, gap-toothed, squinting into sun that had gone out of the world years ago.

He did not cover it. Covering it would be an admission, and admissions were things he collected, never spent.

"She was my daughter," he said, because silence invited questions and questions invited lies, and he preferred to disarm both. "Edith. She would be sixteen now."

Miss Delacroix said nothing. Her face did the thing that faces did — the flicker of sympathy, quickly banked — and then did the thing that faces rarely did: it went past sympathy into study, as though she were reading the photograph the way she evidently read everything that crossed her path, thoroughly and without permission.

"She liked chalk," Vane heard himself say, and was astonished at the words even as they left him. "Drew stars on the pavement outside our house. Hundreds of them. The whole street, some nights. The neighbours complained and I paid the lamplighter to leave them alone." He straightened his collar, and the photograph vanished beneath the wool as though it had never surfaced. "You will find, Miss Delacroix, that I am not in the business of harming children. Only of ensuring that every child in England belongs somewhere a book can find it. There is a difference, whatever your mistress tells you over the supper you are all pretending not to share."

"Stars don't hurt anyone," Astrid Delacroix said.

"No," Vane agreed. "They don't. It's the wishing on them that does the damage."

Pell had his galoshes on. The door opened onto fog and the smell of coal-smoke and the river far off. Vane stepped out, and paused on the top step, and looked back only once — at the hall with its mended umbrella stand, its polished rods, its air of a place where coats were hung for people whose names appeared on no hooks anywhere.

Behind him, upstairs, the floorboard creaked again. Small feet. Quick ones.

He wrote the sound down, mentally, in the margin of the day.

At the gate, Pell cleared his throat. "The seamstress, sir. And the deserter. Shall I apply for warrants?"

Vane looked back at the house. In an upstairs window, half-hidden by a curtain, a small pale face watched them go — and below it, at the drawing-room glass, the driver stood with her arms crossed, grey eyes fixed on him with a ferocity she thought she was hiding and was not.

Two watchers, he thought. One window. A household of held breath.

"Not yet," he said. "Watch the shipping offices first. Irregular families always, always run for the sea."

* * *

That evening, in his rooms in Westminster, Vane unlocked the drawer where the photograph lived at night and set it beside the lamp. Edith grinned up at him out of 1899, gap-toothed and sunlit, forever eight.

He opened the ledger to the page for Cranbourne Row and dipped his pen.

*Halloway, M. — writer. Delacroix, A. — driver. Observant. Reads what is put before her; likely reads what is not.* He paused, pen hovering, listening to the rain begin against the pane. Then, beneath the entries, in his small upright hand, he added the line that would sit there for the rest of the affair, the line he did not yet understand he had already written the ending to:

*There is a child in that house who draws.*

He blotted the page, closed the book — four pounds, eleven ounces — and sat a long while in the lamplight with his daughter smiling at him, until the rain had washed the last of the daylight from the window and the gaslight guttered low, and somewhere across the dark city a ship's bell rang out the tide.
