## 6. Wren Speaks in Chalk

The child was awake before the house again.

Astrid knew it not by any sound — Wren made none, that was her way — but by the quality of the silence on the landing, a held-breath quiet that meant small feet were already padding down the stairs, past the third step that groaned, avoiding it with a dancer's precision, out through the kitchen where the bolt had been left loose for exactly this purpose. Miriam Halloway ran her household like a country run by a benevolent conspirator: every rule had an exception carved into it like a private door in a castle wall, and the loose bolt was Wren's door.

Astrid came down after her, coat over her nightdress, breath fogging in the grey April morning. The motorcar would need cranking soon, and the windscreen wiped of dew, and she wanted ten minutes of pavement before London filled up with itself — before costermongers and clerks and the great grinding machine of the day drowned out whatever the early hour still had to say.

Wren was at the front step, crouched over a length of chalk stubbed down to the size of a thumb. She had found it somewhere; no one knew where. Things simply appeared in Wren's pockets — marbles, buttons, a brass key to nothing, chalk — as though the streets of Pimlico paid her tribute.

"Good morning, sparrow," Astrid said softly.

Wren did not look up. She never looked up when spoken to, not at first. It was as if words reached her a moment late, arriving by some slower post than they used for everyone else. But her hand kept moving, quick and certain, white against the blackened stone of the step.

Astrid crouched beside her and watched.

The child drew stars. She always drew stars first — five-pointed, slightly lopsided, arranged in patterns that only made sense if you tilted your head, which Astrid had learned to do. Tonight's pattern — this morning's — was a scatter of them across the lower step, dense at one corner, thinning toward the other, like a sky seen through a torn curtain.

Then Wren drew something else.

She drew a rectangle. Tall. A door, or near enough. Beside it, a second shape: a stick figure, long-coated, with a hat brim drawn as a single hard line across the head. The figure stood apart from the door. Not knocking. Not walking away. Just standing — and beneath its feet, Wren pressed the chalk so hard the stub snapped, and she bore down twice more, three short strokes, like roots. Like a man planted.

Astrid felt the cold move from the air into her chest.

"Wren." She kept her voice level, the way she'd learned to keep her voice level in the orphanage dining hall when the matron read the ledger aloud and you waited, hands folded, to hear whether your name came with bread attached or consequences. "Who is that?"

The child pointed. Not at the drawing. Past it. Across the street, to the corner where Hanover Row bent south and the plane trees began — the corner with the lamp standard and the low wall where delivery boys sat to eat their lunches.

The street was empty. The lamp still burned yellow-grey in the pre-dawn murk. Nothing stood at the corner but shadows.

But the wall, Astrid saw, was scuffed at its top edge, pale marks in the soot, the size of a coat sleeve. Someone had leaned there. Recently. And the gravel beneath the lamp standard was disturbed in a fan-shape, the print of heels pushed off from, the stance of a man who had stood a long time facing the house, then gone when the light changed or when he chose.

Wren tugged Astrid's sleeve. She picked up the broken half of chalk, spat on her finger, wetted the stub, and drew — with the deliberation of a scribe who has all the time in the world — two more figures inside the rectangle of the door. One tall. One small. Then she drew a line between the man at the corner and the door: a dotted line, careful dots, the kind children use for paths in treasure maps.

The path led from him to them.

"He's watching the house," Astrid said. Not a question. Her mouth had gone dry.

Wren looked at her then — directly, fully, with those enormous grey eyes that seemed to hold everything she never said, banked behind them like coal behind a grate. And she nodded once, slow, solemn as a magistrate.

* *

Breakfast was porridge and arguments, as usual. Odette had claimed the end of the table and was defending a new client — a West End dressmaker with more orders than conscience — while Tomas stirred his tea endlessly without drinking it, his eyes doing their daily inventory of the window, the door, the sound of boots on pavement outside. Miriam presided from the head like a general at ease, pretending to read the *Times*, which was folded to a page she had clearly already memorized.

And Wren sat beside Astrid, eating her porridge in small precise bites, and drew on the tabletop with a wet fingertip: water only, invisible ink, a rectangle and a line and a man with a hat.

Astrid watched the finger trace the shapes and understood, with a sensation like a floorboard giving way, that the child had been trying to tell them for days. The chalk on the paving stones she'd taken for play. The stars she'd taken for a child's decoration. Last Tuesday, the drawing on the cellar door that Odette had scrubbed off with a sigh about dirty habits — Astrid had glanced at it only in passing, a smear of white shapes, and thought nothing. A child, chalk, an idle hour.

But Wren did not have idle hours. Wren had watch-fires. Astrid had begun to suspect the child slept with one eye open the way soldiers and cats do, and now she suspected why. Somewhere in whatever country Wren had crossed before washing up on Miriam Halloway's doorstep — silent, unclaimed, with a name pinned to her coat like a luggage label and no history attached — someone had been taught, or had taught herself, that being watched comes before being taken. That the man at the corner is the knock at the door three nights later. That you warn the people you belong to in the only language that can't be overheard.

"The pavement," Astrid said quietly, when the others had gone to their arguments. "Your drawings. You're not making pictures, are you, sparrow. You're writing."

Wren's spoon paused midair. She set it down with exaggerated care, the way she did everything, as though the world might startle if handled roughly.

"You see more than any of us," Astrid went on, barely above the hiss of the kettle. "You see Tomas flinch when the postman whistles. You see Odette count the stairs twice. You saw the man on the corner before any of us did, and you've been telling us, and we've been too clever and too grown-up to listen."

Wren picked up the salt cellar and moved it to the middle of the table. Then she took Astrid's teacup, with permission asked in a look, and placed it near the window's edge. Then she looked at Astrid with terrible patience.

The salt was the man. The cup was the house.

"Yes," Astrid said. "I understand. He comes here."

Wren moved the salt closer. Then closer again. Then she stopped it, precisely, at the rim of the safe distance, and withdrew her hand, and folded both hands in her lap, and looked at Astrid as if to say: *now you know what I know. What will you do?*

It was, Astrid thought, rather a lot to put on a woman at seven in the morning.

* *

She should have told Miriam immediately. She told the pavement instead, pacing it, because there was a problem and the problem was this: what could they do? Report a man loitering on a corner to the police? Invite Inspector Vane's constables to the very doorstep they were trying to keep clean? The household's whole architecture was built of things that could not survive daylight — Odette's wages undeclared, Tomas's name absent from the census roll, Wren herself a child with papers for no one and a claim on nobody. Call the police, and you rang the bell of the wolf's own house.

No. This went to Miriam, yes — but carefully, and without the word *police*, and perhaps without the word *watching* either. Perhaps framed as... what? Astrid turned the problem over as she cranked the motorcar, the handle kicking back against her palm, the engine catching on the third try with its usual asthmatic roar. Miriam trusted her judgment. Miriam also, Astrid was increasingly certain, knew about the letters. There was something in the way Miriam handed her the post each morning — a half-beat of delay, eyes flicking up, a small test conducted daily and passed daily — that said *I know you read them, girl, and I let you*. Which meant Astrid's information had a value here beyond errand-running. She was becoming, without anyone naming it, the household's second brain, its rear guard, its keeper of small intelligence.

She did not know how she felt about that. Frightened and proud, mostly, in roughly equal measure — the same mixture she remembered from the orphanage the year she worked out the kitchen rotation and started trading shifts, holding the whole fragile economy of eight hungry girls in her head. Power, even the small kind, is lonely. Nobody thanks the lookout until the fire.

She drove Miriam to a committee meeting in Kensington, and while Miriam inside argued the eternal argument about whether to march or petition, Astrid walked back along the row and stopped at the corner where the man had stood.

Nothing. Morning deliveries, a milk cart, a woman shaking a rug from an upstairs window. Ordinary London going about its ordinary business, and Astrid hated it a little, its smugness, its insistence that nothing remarkable could be happening on a street where she loved people.

But at the base of the lamp standard, half-scuffed, she found what the heel-prints promised: a boot mark, pressed deep where someone had stood for a long time in soft ground beside the gravel. A large boot. Well-shod. And on the low wall, where the scuff-marks showed, a faint smear of chalk — white on stone — where someone had brushed against the very spot where Wren's warning-drawing had faced.

He had seen the drawings. He had stood where she drew him standing, close enough to touch his own portrait, and then he had gone — unhurried, she'd have bet — because a man who watches likes to be watched back. It tells him the message is received.

Astrid stood a long time on that corner, letting the passers-by flow around her, and made herself think it through to the bottom. Pell the census man had written something down; she'd seen his pen hesitate and then move. Vane had come asking questions with his ledger and his careful, wounded voice. And now a watcher on the corner, and a mute child who read the street the way Astrid read letters — because reading the currents had once been the difference between eaten and not eaten.

History, Astrid had told herself all her life, happened elsewhere. To ministers and martyrs, to women thrown under horses' hooves and men shot at dawn in other countries' fields. But history had developed a habit lately of standing on corners opposite her house, wearing a good coat and a patient expression, taking notes.

* *

That evening she washed Wren's hair in the kitchen, in the big tin bath before the range, because it was Astrid's turn and because — though she'd have denied it under oath — she had begun arranging her turns to make sure they came.

Wren sat in the water like a small serious queen, enduring the indignity of soap with dignity. Astrid worked the lather through the dark hair, gently, feeling the small skull beneath her hands, the astonishing fragility and stubbornness of it.

"I used to hide in the laundry," Astrid said, conversationally, the way one talks to children without expecting answers. "At the home. There was a cupboard behind the mangles where the steam pipes ran, and it was warm, and nobody ever looked there because nobody ever imagined a girl would choose a hot dark cupboard over the dormitory. I'd sit there and listen to the whole house through the wall. Who was crying. Who was lying. Who was planning to run." She poured water over the child's hair, shielding her forehead with her hand. "I thought listening was my secret talent. Turns out you've got it worse. Or better. You don't even need the words, do you? You just *see*."

Wren reached up, dripping, and touched Astrid's wrist. Just that. Two small fingers, soap-slicked, resting where the pulse was.

Astrid went very still.

It was the most the child had ever given anyone — more than the chalk, more than the salt-cellar pantomime. An address. A marking, the way she marked her stars: *this one is mine; I am keeping this one.* Astrid had spent twenty-six years learning not to be kept by anyone, and here was a seven-year-old undoing the whole education with two wet fingertips.

"Well," Astrid managed, her voice gone rough as unswept gravel. "We'll manage, sparrow. Whatever's coming up the street, we'll manage it. I'm rather good at managing."

Wren considered her with grave eyes, then reached for the towel, and — this was the part Astrid would carry for the rest of her life — dried her own hands first so that she could take Astrid's face in them, briefly, solemnly, the way you seal a compact.

* *

Later, past midnight, Astrid went down to check the bolt and found the front step freshly redrawn.

The man with the hat was there again — but changed. Wren had added details in the day's intervening hours, the way a witness remembers more the second time. The coat now had buttons. The hat had a crease in its crown. And at the figure's breast, small and unmistakable, a rectangle within the coat-line: a notebook. A ledger.

Beside it, in Wren's smallest, most careful hand-strokes, a new symbol: the door of the house, and above it, instead of stars, a single letter-shaped mark — not any letter Wren could have known how to write, but an approximation, laboriously copied from somewhere. Astrid knelt on the cold stone and sounded it out, and her stomach dropped into the cellar.

It was a *V*. Rough-hewn, doubled-struck, but a *V*.

Vane. The child had heard the name — of course she had; silence is not deafness, and names travel through houses like damp — and she had copied it from somewhere. From the census paper, perhaps, or the card Vane had left on the hall table. She had seen the shape, kept the shape, and now she had set it on the pavement like a plague-cross on a door.

*Astrid,* the step said, in the language of a child who spoke nothing. *It isn't a stranger anymore. It's him.*

Astrid rose, knees cracking, and looked up and down the empty midnight street. The lamp at the corner burned steady over bare gravel. No boots, no coat, no creased hat. Only the sense — new, cold, permanent as frost — that the house had acquired a shadow with a name and a notebook, and that a seven-year-old had understood it before them all.

She fetched a cloth and warm water, and hesitated, and did not wash the drawing away. Let him see it, if he came again. Let him know he was known.

Then she went inside, bolted the loose bolt properly for the first time since Wren had come to them, and stood a long while in the dark hall, listening — the way she'd listened through the laundry wall, years ago and another life — to the house breathing around its secrets.

In the morning she would have to tell Miriam that the inspector was not merely reading files anymore. He was standing at corners. He was learning their address the way a hunter learns a waterhole.

And somehow, before he drank, she would have to move the whole herd.
