## 7. Rally at Caxton Hall

The Daimler smelled of beeswax and petrol, and Astrid loved it the way other women loved perfume — as a signature, a claim on the world. She had polished the bonnet until it held the morning sky in miniature, clouds sliding across green paint like ships across a sea she had never sailed. Her hands knew every bolt of the machine. They did not know what to do with tenderness, so they worked instead.

"Half past two," Miriam said from the back seat, though Astrid had told her the time not four minutes earlier. "And take Bishopsgate, not the Strand. I won't arrive at a rally looking as though I've been dragged through a crowd."

"You'll be arriving at a rally to be dragged through a crowd," Astrid said, easing the car out of the mews. "The distinction may not survive the afternoon."

Miriam laughed — that low, unbothered laugh she used like a weapon against dread — and unfolded her newspaper. Astrid watched her in the mirror: grey silk, violet scarf knotted precisely, gloves buttoned. A woman dressed for tea who intended to break something open.

Bishopsgate was slow with carts and costers, and Astrid took the delay gladly. It gave her time to think about the letter hidden inside the lining of her coat, sewn there in the small hours with needle and thread and a fear she could not name. *Your name has been noted.* Noted by whom? Kept where? She had spent three days turning the sentence over like a coin worn smooth, unable to read its face.

She was still holding the wheel too tightly when the hall came into view, banners hanging from the upper windows like washing — *Votes for Women* in white on purple, snapping in the wind off the river. The pavement already crowded. Women in sashes, men with placards, newsboys shouting a headline about something happening in Portugal or Persia — history, always, somewhere else.

Astrid drew the car up at the kerb and got out to hand Miriam down, and that was when she saw Odette already on the pavement, helping Wren chalk stars along the gutter's edge.

"You brought the child?" Astrid kept her voice low.

"She wouldn't be left." Odette's accent curled softly around the English words; her hands, quick as ever, smoothed Wren's collar. "Mrs. Halloway said the fresh air would do her good. And I—" Odette glanced toward the hall, at the banners, at the noise of women's voices braided together, and something moved through her face like weather. "I wanted to see it. Once. To stand in a crowd where I am only a woman, and not a—a situation."

Astrid understood. She understood better than she wanted to. Every one of them under Miriam's roof was a *situation*, an irregularity, a name written carefully in someone else's ledger. Out here, among four hundred women, they might pass for ordinary.

"Stay near the car," Astrid said. It was what she always said. It was what she said to Tomas, to Odette, to anyone she could not bear to lose track of. *Stay near the car*, as if the motorcar were an ark and she its pilot, as if proximity to her hands were safety itself.

Wren looked up at her then, chalk dust on her fingers, and pointed one small finger past Astrid's shoulder — at nothing Astrid could see. Then she went back to her stars.

* * *

Caxton Hall was hot as a hothouse and twice as humid, packed with bodies and hats and the smell of lavender over sweat. Astrid found a place near the side door, where she could keep one eye on the platform and one on the street through the wired glass. Old habits. In the orphanage she had always taken the seat nearest the exit; the matron had called it sullenness. It had been arithmetic. Doors were variables. Distance from them was survival.

Miriam spoke fourth. Astrid had heard her rehearse in the drawing-room, pacing between the good chairs, but rehearsal was nothing to this: Miriam's voice rising into that register that seemed to come not from her throat but from some shared place beneath the floorboards, so that when she said *We are not asking to be given what is ours; we are remembering how to take it*, four hundred women made a sound Astrid had never heard before — not a cheer, not a cry, but a kind of collective inhalation, as if the room itself had drawn breath to become one body.

Astrid felt it move through her chest and was alarmed by it. This was the trouble with crowds: they made you believe you had a pulse in common with strangers.

She looked for Odette and found her halfway down the hall, standing on the balls of her feet to see over the hats, her face lit like a girl's at a pantomime. Wren was not with her. Astrid's stomach performed its small cold trick — the one it had learned in orphanage corridors — before she spotted the child near the doors, crouched against the wall with chalk from heaven-knew-where, drawing stars on the flagstones where no one would notice them until they scuffed away. Marking a path home, perhaps. Or marking that she had been here, existed, counted — the thing the census had refused to do for any of them.

Outside, a whistle blew.

It went through the hall like a splinter through skin. The speaker stumbled. Somewhere a woman said, *They've come for the platform,* and the word *platform* travelled outward in whispers while the sound of boots multiplied on the pavement — not the scattered boots of constables on beats but rank upon rank, the deliberate percussion of men sent rather than merely sent-for.

"They're clearing the street," someone said. "There's no warrant. There's no order—"

There was an order. There is always an order; Astrid would learn that later, would read it in Vane's tidy hand if she ever got near his ledger. But in the moment all she knew was that the side door burst inward and the hall became water finding a breach — women surging toward the exits not in panic exactly, but in that terrible confusion of people who have been assured the law is on their side and are meeting the law's fists instead.

Astrid did not think. Thinking was for afterwards. Her body, which had survived six institutions by reading rooms and staying small, did the opposite of everything it had been trained for: it moved *into* the crush, elbows out, toward the doors, toward the street, toward the sound of Odette's voice — she heard it now, high and frightened, cut off mid-word.

The pavement was chaos under a hard blue sky. Constables had formed a line along the kerb and were pushing it forward, and women were being folded into that line and passed down it hand over hand like parcels — seized by arm, by collar, by hair. A banner lay trampled in the gutter, its letters printing themselves in mud. Somewhere glass broke. A woman was bleeding from the mouth and laughing, which frightened Astrid more than any scream.

And there — pinned against the iron railing of the railings opposite, a constable's fist closed in the back of her dress so the seam tore and her shoulder wrenched wrong — Odette. Her hat gone, her hair down, crying out words in French that needed no translation.

Every instinct Astrid owned said: *stay small, stay clear, be a driver, be furniture, be no one.* Those instincts had kept her alive since she was six years old. She felt them rise in her like a tide, familiar and cold.

She walked into the tide and through it.

Later she could not describe the interval between deciding and arriving. She remembered the railing's cold under her palms, remembered the constable turning — heavy, young, more startled than brutal — remembered saying, in a voice that came from the same place Miriam's speech had come from, some shared cellar beneath the city: *"Let go of her."*

"This is police business, miss."

"She's a seamstress. Look at her hands." And she took hold of Odette's wrist — the left one, the one nearest — and pulled, and the constable, whether from surprise or shame or simple physics, loosened his grip for half a second, and half a second was enough. Odette came free like a cork from a bottle, staggering into Astrid, and Astrid turned them both toward the motorcar with her arm around the other woman's waist and her eyes fixed on the bonnet, the radiator cap, the one fixed point in the spinning street.

Behind them the constable was shouting. Of course he was. He had lost his parcel and parcels did not simply escape; someone must answer for it.

"Name!" he called after them, breathless. "Your name, miss! I'll have your name!"

Astrid put Odette in the car. Wren was already scrambling in — when had she come? she had come the way children come, silently, drawn by some instinct of her own — and Astrid got behind the wheel with her heart slamming like a loose bonnet in wind.

"Miss! The driver! Your name for the report!"

Her mouth opened. Twenty years of training said: give a false one. Give the first false name in your pocket; you carry them the way other women carry pins. *Say nothing. Be furniture.*

"Astrid Delacroix," she heard herself say, clearly, over the noise of the crowd and the breaking glass and the whistles. "Spelled as it sounds."

Then she started the engine and drove, hands steady on the wheel because her hands had always known how to work even when the rest of her shook, and in the mirror the hall shrank behind her with its torn banners and its blue line and its four hundred women, some running, some bleeding, some already in the black marias, and Astrid Delacroix understood — with the strange calm of a woman watching her own house burn — that she had just written her name into the world.

Not in an orphanage ledger, where names were crossed out when the fees stopped coming. Not in the margin of someone else's letter. In a policeman's notebook, in front of witnesses, attached to an act.

History, which had always happened elsewhere — to queens, to martyrs, to women in photographs — had happened here, on a Tuesday, to her, and she had happened back.

* * *

They regrouped at the house in the long dusk, curtains drawn, the gas lit low. Miriam arrived at eight, bruised at the cheekbone, immaculate otherwise, and surveyed her household with the expression of a general counting her regiment.

"Tomas?"

"Out. He'll have seen the crowds; he'll keep to the back streets." Astrid poured tea nobody wanted, because pouring things was what her hands did when they could not fight. "Odette's shoulder is wrenched but nothing's broken. Wren—"

Wren was asleep on the parlour sofa, chalk still in her fist, and on the hearthstone, where she had been drawing before sleep took her, there were stars. Dozens of them. And among the stars, unmistakable, drawn with the grave precision children reserve for important documents: a row of small figures holding hands, and one figure apart, taller, wearing what might have been a helmet, and beside that figure, in careful chalk, a circle with lines radiating from it — a whistle, or a sun, or an eye.

Miriam studied the drawing for a long moment.

"She sees everything," Miriam said quietly. "That child. We should remember that she does."

"There's something else." Astrid set down the teapot. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "My name was taken. At the rally. A constable has it in his book, with my description and the registration number of this motorcar, which is rather worse."

Silence. The clock ticked. Odette, bandaged at the shoulder, met Astrid's eyes across the room and did not look away.

"Then we must assume," Miriam said at last, folding her gloved hands, "that by tomorrow morning, someone will have connected that name to this address." She paused, and something flickered behind her composure — not fear; calculation. "And we must assume it will be the same *someone* whose name appeared in my letter."

The letter Astrid had hidden. The letter Astrid had sewn into her own coat lining, where it lay now against her ribs like a second heart, beating guilt with every step. She opened her mouth to confess it — *I read it, I hid it, I have known for days* — and at that moment there came a knock at the front door. Three measured raps, unhurried, official.

Everyone in the room went still. Even Wren woke without stirring, her eyes opening flat and watchful in the firelight.

Astrid moved to the window, eased back the curtain a finger's width, and looked down through the gap in the railings at the man standing beneath the gas lamp on the step. He was tall, grey-suited, patient as paperwork. He carried a leather folio under one arm, and he stood the way men stand when they are certain the door will open, because it is their profession to make doors open.

He looked up — directly up, as if he knew exactly where she stood — and Astrid stepped back from the glass with her heart going like a piston.

"That's him," she whispered. "That's the inspector."
