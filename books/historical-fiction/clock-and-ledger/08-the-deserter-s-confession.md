## 8. The Deserter's Confession

The cellar of Number Fourteen Bellwether Lane smelled of coal dust and apples — the apples because Odette had claimed one corner for the baskets she was forever brining down from the market man's surplus, the coal because that was what cellars were for, and Tomas Novak had learned in two months of hiding that a man could measure his freedom by what he was permitted to smell.

He was mending a chair leg when he heard her come down the steps. Astrid Delacroix moved like the motorcars she drove: precise, quiet, with a low mechanical hum of purpose underneath. He knew her footsteps now the way he had once known the footsteps of his sergeant — by what they demanded of him. The sergeant's boots said *stand up straight*. Astrid's heels on the cellar stairs said *I have counted you, and you are all still here.*

"You missed supper," she said.

"I ate."

"Odette left you a second supper. That's not the same as eating it." She came to stand at the foot of the stairs, holding a covered plate and a candle stub, and the light moved across the low ceiling like something alive. "Miriam's rule. Everyone eats twice at this table if they need to. You're insulting her hospitality by taking only the first."

He set down the chair and the glue pot and wiped his hands on his trousers, which were beyond insulting, which had gone past ruin into a kind of landscape. "She knows I eat down here?"

"She knows everyone eats twice if they need to," Astrid repeated, and gave him the plate. Cold mutton, bread, a spoonful of pickle gone soft. It was the finest meal he could remember, which was not true either — he had eaten magnificently once, in a mess tent outside Pretoria, the night before his brother died — but hunger rearranges memory, and two months in a coal cellar will make a beggar's philosopher of any man.

She did not leave. She sat down on an upturned crate beside the apple baskets, folded her hands, and looked at him with those grey eyes that saw everything and confessed nothing. He had watched her read the post every morning when she thought no one observed her — standing in the hall with the letters fanned in her long fingers like a card player deciding which hand to lose. He had never told her he'd seen. Some debts you let lie.

"You've been three days avoiding me," she said.

"Three days repairing furniture."

"Tomas." She pronounced it the way Miriam did, with the emphasis where his mother had put it. *TOH-mash.* "You flinch whenever the knocker goes. Wren has noticed. And Wren notices everything, so when she notices something extra, it means it's very large."

At the child's name something turned over in his chest. Wren, who drew stars on the pavement with her chalk and would not put a sound past her own teeth, had taken to leaving a piece of chalk on the cellar step for him. As if she understood that a man who could not go out might still want to draw his way out. He had used it to mark tallies on the wall behind the coal. Fifty-three days. He had stopped after fifty-three because the number had begun to feel like a sentence being served rather than a wait being endured.

"The census man," Tomas said. "Pell. He wrote something down about me. I saw him do it. I was at the pantry window and I saw his pencil stop."

"Astrid handled Pell."

"Astrid handled Pell," he agreed. "And Pell wrote anyway. Men like Pell always write anyway. I know the breed." He broke the bread and did not eat it. "I should tell you what I am running from. You've earned the truth, and besides—" he almost smiled— "you'll read it in some letter eventually. Better from my mouth."

Her expression did not change, which told him everything about whether she denied the habit. But she said, quietly, "Only if you wish to."

"I wish to." And found, to his astonishment, that he did — that the words had been sitting in him like shrapnel since the day he'd come limping to Miriam Halloway's door with a false name stitched into his collar, and that saying them might be a kind of surgery.

* * *

"My name isn't Tomas Finch," he began. "It's Tomáš Novák. My people are from a village outside Pilsen, in Bohemia, though you'd never know it from my accent — my father was a foreman in an English-owned ironworks and swore we'd be Englishmen before God noticed otherwise. I enlisted in 'ninety-nine because my brother Josef did, and where Josef went, I went. That was the whole of my philosophy for twenty-two years." He turned the plate slowly on his knee, watching the pickle juice creep toward the rim. "Josef was the older by four minutes. Twins. Our mother called us her bookends. He was the loud one, the laughing one, the one who could get a pub singing in two languages. I was the one who stood behind him and held his coat.

"We served through the whole of the South African business. Came home. Took work on the railways. And then last spring the regiment recalled us — some colonial trouble, always some colonial trouble — and we went, because that's what men like us do. We go."

Astrid had gone very still. In the candlelight her face was half gold and half shadow, and the shadow half seemed to be listening harder.

"There was a village," Tomas said. "I won't give you its name. Not because I'm protecting anyone — there's no one left in it worth protecting — but because I refuse to carry the name around in my mouth along with everything else. There was an incident. A farmer, a dispute over cattle or water or some such nothing, and an officer decided the village required a lesson. And the lesson required an example, and the example required a firing squad, and the squad required twelve men, and I was one of the twelve."

The cellar was silent except for the settling of coal somewhere in the dark behind him, the small sighs a house makes when it thinks no one is attending.

"They put six rifles with ball and six with blank," he went on. "That's how it's done — so each man can go to his grave telling himself his was the empty chamber. A kindness, they call it. To whom, I'd like to know. To the man tied to the post?" His hands had begun to shake, and he gripped the plate to still them, and the plate rattled anyway. "I knew which rifle was mine. Don't ask me how. A man knows. The weight of it, the balance, God knows — but I knew, the way you know your own toothache. And I looked down the barrel at that farmer — a thin old fellow with white hair, standing against the post with more dignity than any officer I ever saluted — and I *did not fire.*

"I lowered my rifle. In front of the squad, in front of the captain, in front of God and everybody. Just — lowered it. Like laying down a tool at the end of a day's work. And then Josef—" His voice cracked clean through, like ice underfoot. "Josef stepped into the gap. Raised his own rifle and fired where mine should have spoken, so that the count came out right, so that no one would have to do it twice, so that *I* wouldn't hang for refusing. Twelve shots for eleven men who fired. He took my sin onto his rifle and made it arithmetic."

Tomas set the plate on the floor, carefully, because his hands could no longer be trusted with crockery.

"He was shot four weeks later. Not by the enemy. By our own side, at another village, for another example — or perhaps for me, perhaps someone remembered, perhaps the captain kept a little list the way your Mr. Pell keeps his little lists. They said it was random. Nothing is random. And when they sent me to collect his effects, the sergeant handed me the envelope and said, 'Hard lines, Finch,' using the name Josef had given at the last muster to cover my absence — because my brother, even dead, was still covering me — and I took the envelope and I walked out of the camp and I did not stop walking until London."

He looked up. Astrid had not moved, but her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them, and he was grateful to her for that — grateful she let him see the cost of hearing, instead of performing composure for his comfort. People had been performing composure for him since he arrived. It exhausted everyone involved.

"So when your census man asks who I am," Tomas finished, "I am nobody. I am a dead man's coat. And men who wear dead men's coats get written down in ledgers, Miss Delacroix, and men in ledgers get found. So what I have been avoiding telling you these three days, while I repaired chairs I have no right to sit on, is this question, and here it is at last: can this house hold me much longer? Because I can hear the knocker the way a dog hears thunder that hasn't come yet. And I need to know if I should start walking again — for all your sakes — or if I may be allowed to stay."

* * *

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. She rose from the crate and walked the length of the cellar and back, past the apple baskets, past the chair he'd mended, her heels silent now on the earthen floor as though even her shoes understood the gravity of the ground.

"You refused to shoot a farmer," she said at last, "and your brother died making your refusal legal."

"That's the shape of it, yes."

"And you think that makes you a burden."

He started to answer and found he had no answer. She crouched down in front of him, so that their eyes were level, and the candle threw both their shadows huge against the coal-blackened wall, two giants conferring.

"I grew up in an institution," Astrid said. "St. Ambrose's, out past Hackney. Do you know what an orphanage ledger looks like? Two columns. Names and dispositions. Every child a line. When you turn fourteen they cross you out — a single stroke through your name, done with a ruler, very tidy — and you walk out the gate with your bundle and the world receives you exactly as warmly as you'd expect." Her voice was flat and even, a road driven many times. "I have spent my life believing history happened to other people. Kings, wars, movements, causes — all of it elsewhere. My job was to drive the motorcar and mind my hands and keep my name out of every column in every ledger in this city. And then I came to this house, and this house is full of crossed-out names. Odette, running from a husband with fists. Wren, whose line in somebody's book just says *found*. Miriam, who will certainly be written down and crossed out herself before this year is done. And you."

"Tomas—"

"Let me finish, because I'm going to say something I've never said aloud, and then I'm going to say something foolish, and I'd rather get them both out in one breath." She drew a breath and did take them both in one breath, nearly: "You are not a burden, you are my family, and yes — I promise this house can hold you. For as long as it takes. However long that is."

The word *family* landed in him like a bell struck in an empty church. He had not been anyone's family since April. He had been a deserter, an alias, a pair of hands good with glue pots. He reached out and took her hand — a liberty, he knew, but she let him, her fingers cold and steady in his ruined ones.

"You don't know that you can keep that promise," Tomas said gently. "You're promising other people's survival, and that's a heavy purse to carry."

"I know exactly what I'm carrying," said Astrid Delacroix, and for a moment he saw the weight of it settle visibly on her shoulders, the way a yoke settles on an ox that has decided, finally, to pull. "I've been carrying it since the night Mr. Pell's pencil stopped moving."

* * *

Later — much later, after she had gone up and taken the candle with her, leaving him in the dark with the cold mutton he finally ate, all of it, every scrap, because Miriam's hospitality deserved better than his grief — Tomas sat against the wall behind the coal and found the child's chalk where he had left it.

He did not add a tally. Instead, by touch alone, in the blackness, he drew a crude shape he had not drawn since Bohemia: a little house with a slanted roof, the kind children draw, the kind his mother had drawn in the margins of his school copybooks to show him where home was. Underneath it he drew a second, smaller figure standing beside a third. Then, because the chalk was in his hand and the dark was honest, a fourth figure off to the side, arms raised — waving, or reaching, or perhaps simply glad.

Josef, he thought. Even here. Especially here.

Above him the house creaked through its nightly litany — pipes contracting, floorboards confessing, Odette's sewing machine falling silent at last, Wren's small feet padding somewhere overhead, and beneath it all Astrid's even breathing, audible somehow through two floors as though the whole house breathed when she did. A house full of irregular persons, the inspector would say. A house full of crossed-out lines.

But ledgers could be lied to. He had learned that much from watching her forge Miriam's signature on the laundry receipts. Ledgers could be forged, evaded, burned — and somewhere in that thought was a door, and somewhere beyond the door a ship, and somewhere beyond the ship a country with no captain keeping lists of Bohemian railwaymen.

He slept, eventually, with chalk dust on his fingers and the drawn house above his head, and dreamed — for the first time in months — of morning.

He did not know, could not know, that two streets away a shipping clerk was already being asked questions about berths to Canada, and that the name doing the asking would soon be written down in a ledger of its own.
