## 9. Passage and Price

The shipping office on Leadenhall Street smelled of ink and cold tea, of ledgers kept in rooms where the windows had not been opened since Queen Victoria died. Astrid climbed the stairs with her hands folded like a woman come about an inheritance, though the only inheritance she carried was other people's lives, bundled and tied, weighing less than a hatbox and more than she could say.

She had dressed for it. That was the first lesson of the orphanage: dress as though you belong, because belonging is mostly costume. Her grey wool coat was Miriam's cast-off but cut well, her gloves uncracked at the knuckles, her hat pinned at the severe angle of a woman with business to conduct. She had practised her voice on the drive across the city — not the flattened vowels of the orphanage schoolroom, not yet the polish that would invite suspicion, but something between, something respectable and forgettable. *A widow's sister. Arranging passage for family. Nothing remarkable.*

The clerk behind the counter had a moustache like a scrubbing brush and the eyes of a man who counted everything twice. A brass plaque on the wall read CANADIAN PACIFIC OCEAN SERVICES — ROYAL MAIL STEAMSHIPS, beneath it a lithograph of a vessel so white and serene it looked painted by someone who had never seen open water. Third-class passengers stood on the deck of the picture like grains of pepper scattered by a careless hand.

"Madam?"

"I'm enquiring about berths to Canada," Astrid said. "Halifax crossing. For three persons."

"Three." He said it as though tasting it. "When?"

"Soonest possible. Within the fortnight, if there is anything."

He pulled a ledger toward himself — another ledger; the world seemed made of nothing else — and ran a finger down columns ruled in red. "There's the *Grampian*, sailing Tuesday next from Liverpool. Then the *Royal George* the Friday after. Berths available in third class, four to a compartment. Steerage is full until month's end."

"Third class will do admirably. What is the fare?"

He quoted a figure, and Astrid kept her face still while arithmetic happened behind it, fast and merciless. She had done sums like this all her life — in the orphanage ledger room, counting linen that never added up, counting mouths against bowls. The fare for one person was nearly two months of what the household lived on when things were flush. Three fares, plus railway tickets to Liverpool, plus food for the crossing, plus whatever was left over to begin a life with at the far end —

"And is that inclusive?" she asked, because her voice must keep working while her mind did the dying.

"Inclusive of meals, madam, which in third class means there are meals." He permitted himself the smallest satisfaction at his own wit. "Bedding can be hired separately. Inspection fees at Halifax. And of course the head tax does not apply to your party, if they're British subjects."

"They are," Astrid said, and heard how smoothly the lie came, and hated it a little less than usual, because some lies were load-bearing walls. "British-born, all three."

She counted the money out in her head again, differently this time, the way you re-count a wound. There was the housekeeping tin under the kitchen floorboard — eleven pounds and change. There was the sale of the motorcar, which could not happen because the motorcar was how they would get to Liverpool without being seen at a railway station, and besides, it was registered to Miriam, and selling it would be a signature, and signatures were how people were found. There was her own wage, saved against sense in a post-office account since 1908 — six pounds, fourteen shillings, the whole quiet hoard of a woman who had believed for years that she needed no one and therefore need save only for herself.

Six pounds, fourteen shillings. She thought of Odette's hands, quick as birds, that could turn a bolt of cloth into three dresses and had been doing so for strangers' wives at half-rate to add coins to the tin. She thought of Wren's shoes, which were too small and which Astrid had been meaning to replace for a month and had not, because the cobbler asked questions and children's feet grow in patterns only inspectors find interesting.

"How much to secure the berths now," she said, "and pay the balance later?"

"A deposit of ten percent per berth holds them until the manifest seals." The clerk turned the ledger around and pushed it toward her with a stub of pencil. "Names here, madam. Full names, ages, occupations. The manifest goes to the Dominion immigration officer before you do. Your people will be known in Halifax before their ship leaves sight of land."

Astrid looked at the blank lines. Three ruled rectangles, waiting. Names were such small things to write and such enormous things to promise. She thought of the household's borrowed names, the careful architecture of them — Odette Marchand, who was really Odette Brissac with papers that told a story; Tomas Halloway, whose claim to cousinhood was a fiction Miriam had built with a straight face and a wedding photograph produced at strategic moments; Wren, who was nobody on paper at all, which was both her peril and her shelter.

If she wrote false names here, they went into an official book. Official books crossed oceans faster than ships. Official books sat in offices in Liverpool and Halifax and London, waiting to be compared against other official books by men whose profession was comparison.

"The names," the clerk prompted, pencil hovering.

"May I have the form to complete at home?" Astrid said. "I'd rather present it properly than scrawl it at your counter. My sister's particulars are with her solicitor."

It was a good lie. It even had furniture in it — solicitors, sisters, propriety. The clerk nodded, mollified, and slid a cream-coloured form across the counter with the deposit amount written in the corner: one pound, nineteen shillings per berth, balance due before sailing.

"You'll want to act quickly," he said, stamping something. "There's been a deal of interest in the Canadian crossings this spring. And—" he paused, and the pause had texture to it, the particular hesitancy of a man deciding whether he is being helpful or indiscreet— "a word of advice, madam, since you seem a reasonable woman. Have your particulars exactly right. Exactly right. We've had instructions lately about documentation. Names checked against— well." He straightened his moustache. "Against lists."

"Certainly," Astrid said. "Whose instructions? If I may ask. So that my solicitor might address any concern properly."

"Oh, nothing you'd know, no cause for worry in a regular case." He was already retreating into the tone clerks use to un-say things. "A gentleman from Scotland Yard called last week. Inspector, no less. Making enquiries on behalf of— some department or other. Asked us to flag certain departures. Persons of interest, that sort of thing. Very courteous fellow. Grey suit. Carried a notebook everywhere, wouldn't let it out of his hand." The clerk laughed a small laugh. "As if we're running smugglers across the Atlantic rather than families to a new life in the Dominions."

The room did not change. That was the terrible thing. The lithograph ship stayed white and serene; the clock above the filing cabinets continued its dry ticking; somewhere a door closed and a typewriter began. Only Astrid changed, standing very still in the middle of the ordinary room, holding the cream form in her gloved hand, understanding that the noose she had felt tightening these past weeks had just clicked another notch, and that the man pulling it carried a ledger everywhere and would not let it out of his hand.

"Inspector," she repeated, mild as milk. "How thorough. Did he say what manner of persons he was interested in?"

"Deserters, I shouldn't wonder. There's a general circular out. And—" the clerk dropped his voice as though the file cabinets had ears— "between ourselves, he asked odd questions. Not 'is anyone travelling' but 'who is travelling.' Wanted to know if we'd sold berths recently to households with— irregular compositions. His phrase." He shrugged apologetically. "I told him we sell tickets, madam, we don't take censuses. Though I suppose that's his trade, isn't it." He chuckled at his own joke and found no one laughing with him.

"You've been most helpful," Astrid said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it, later, remembering. "I'll return with the completed form."

"Do. And the deposit secures the berths through—" he consulted the ledger— "the sealing of the manifest, Tuesday next, six o'clock in the morning. After that, no name comes off and no name goes on. It's law, madam, not policy. The ship sails when the paper says it sails."

*The ship sails when the paper says it sails.* She carried the sentence out with her into the street like a stone in her pocket, and it weighed what it weighed.

* * *

Outside, London was doing its loud indifferent morning: carts and omnibuses, a barrel-organ somewhere off Cornhill, newsboys shouting about Parliament and a mine disaster in Wales, history happening everywhere at once to everyone except, apparently, her. Astrid walked past the motorcar twice before she remembered to walk toward it. Her hands wanted the wheel. Her hands always wanted the wheel — the one place in the world where competence was simple and the road answered steering the way roads should.

She did not drive home. She drove instead to the Embankment and parked in the shadow of Waterloo Bridge, engine off, hands still on the wheel, and made herself think.

Ten percent per berth. Three berths. One pound nineteen each — five pounds seventeen shillings to hold the passage, and the balance — she did the sum again and the sum did not improve with repetition — the balance came to something over thirty pounds by sailing day, once inspection fees and bedding-hire and rail fares were counted. Thirty pounds. The household tin held eleven. Her post-office account held six pounds, fourteen. Even with Odette's sewing money and even selling her own coat and her own future and her own name—

And none of it mattered, that was the elegant cruelty of it, none of the money mattered, because Vane had already been to the shipping office. Vane had already stood at that counter with his grey suit and his notebook and asked not *whether* but *who*. The moment a name touched that manifest — any name, right or forged — it passed into the machinery of comparison, and somewhere in Whitehall a man who did not let his ledger out of his hand would run his finger down the columns the way the clerk had run his finger down his, and stop.

She sat in the parked car and watched the river go by backwards, the way rivers do, carrying every boat toward the sea. Somewhere out on that grey water was the answer, thirty pounds and one miracle away.

Think. *Think the way Miriam thinks.* Miriam, who had built a refuge out of aliases and second suppers, who had taught Astrid without ever saying so that a rule is only a wall if everyone agrees it's solid. What would Miriam see in that clerk's careful rules?

*The manifest seals at dawn, Tuesday next. No name comes off and no name goes on.*

But a name could be *on* before it sealed. A name could be added early, quietly, paid for in deposit, sitting legitimate on the list days before Vane thought to look — and by the time anyone compared columns, the ship would be a speck and the name would be in Canada, where the circulars of Scotland Yard were so much wasted paper.

And a fourth berth—

Her breath caught. She hadn't meant to think it. She put the thought down as one puts down a knife, handle toward herself, blade away. Four berths cost forty pounds. Four berths meant the arithmetic failed entirely. But four berths also meant — and here the knife turned over, catching light — that no one had to be left behind, that the question she refused to look at directly (*if it comes to it, who*) could be answered in advance by simple arithmetic, by money, by the least romantic force on earth.

Forty pounds. She had seventeen pounds and change in the world. Odette might raise five, perhaps eight, at ruinous speed. Tomas had nothing — deserters travel light — unless he sold the one thing left to sell, and she would not ask him to sell that.

Twenty pounds short. Perhaps twenty-five. In nine days.

It was impossible, and therefore, according to the education of her childhood, it was merely expensive. The orphanage had taught her many things, but chief among them was this: impossibility is a price you haven't found the money for yet. Someone always had the money. The question was what they wanted in exchange, and whether you could live with paying it.

She started the engine. The motorcar grumbled awake, faithful old thing, and Astrid pulled out into the traffic of the Embankment with the cream form riding on the seat beside her like a sleeping cat, and she began, methodically, in the way of practical women, to make lists inside her head. What Odette's needle could earn in nine days. What the pawnshop on Clerkenwell Road gave for a silver hairbrush that had been her mother's, whoever her mother had been. What Miriam's solicitor could release without Miriam's signature and what he couldn't. What Mr. Pell had seen, and what Mr. Pell had written down, and whether the two were the same thing.

She was three streets along, stopped at the junction by Farringdon Road, when she saw the man across the way.

Grey suit. Still as a post while London streamed around him. He was not looking at her car — he was looking at the shipping office behind her, or had been; now, unhurried, he took out a notebook, wrote something with the air of a man completing a chore, and put it away. He did not cross the street. He did not follow. That was worse. Following meant you mattered enough to chase. This was cataloguing. This was a man adding a line to a page, and the page was hers.

The traffic moved. Astrid moved with it, because stopping would have been a confession, and her hands on the wheel were perfectly steady, and her mind was running the numbers one final time:

Seventeen pounds, six shillings, threepence. Nine days. Three berths — perhaps four — on a ship that seals its papers at dawn, and an inspector who has already asked the shipping lines to flag departures, who stands outside their offices with his little book, who is not hunting them at all, she understood suddenly, with a cold bright clarity that arrived like frost on glass — he is *farming* them. Letting them run, letting them spend, letting them gather themselves toward the water, because frightened animals run in straight lines, and straight lines end at gangways, and gangways are where the net is easiest to draw.

Very well. Then they would not run in a straight line. They would run the way her life had run — crookedly, by borrowed names and read letters and doors held open at cost — and they would be aboard that ship if she had to forge every name on the manifest with her own hand.

She turned the car toward home. Behind her, the inspector wrote one more line and closed his book, and ahead of her, over the rooftops of the city, the gulls were already flying west, toward Liverpool, toward the sea, toward Tuesday next at six o'clock in the morning — when the manifest would seal like a wound, and every name upon it would belong forever to whoever had the courage, or the desperation, to put it there.
