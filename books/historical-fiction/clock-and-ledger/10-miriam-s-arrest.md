## 10. Miriam's Arrest

The morning of the deputation, Miriam Halloway dressed as though for church.

Astrid watched her from the doorway of the bedroom, the motorcar keys cold in her hand, and understood — in the way one understands a thing a half-second before the mind can arrange it into words — that today would be different. Miriam had taken out the grey wool costume, the good one, with the velvet collar that Odette had re-stitched last autumn. She had pinned her hat at an angle that was neither vain nor careless but deliberate, a flag planted. She had put on gloves.

Miriam Halloway did not wear gloves to be arrested by accident.

"You've polished the bonnet," Miriam said, catching Astrid's eye in the spotted vanity mirror. "It shines like a beetle."

"It should shine. It's carrying you."

"Then it carries more than me." Miriam pressed a hairpin into place with her thumb, and in the glass her face was calm in the particular way that water is calm above a weir. "Caxton Hall at eleven. The deputation walks to Westminster. We present the resolution to whoever will receive it, and if no one receives it, we stand where we may lawfully stand, and we are seen."

"And if they take you?"

The question left Astrid before she could dress it in softer language. Miriam turned from the mirror. For a moment neither woman moved, and the house spoke around them in its small morning dialect — pipes ticking, Wren's chalk scraping somewhere below, the kettle beginning its low complaint in the kitchen.

"If they take me," Miriam said, "then I will have given testimony that no pamphlet can give. They can print ten thousand leaflets, Astrid. A leaflet is thrown away. A woman in a prison van — that, people remember. That follows them home."

"There are other women who could—"

"There are not other women with my name," Miriam said gently. "That is rather the point."

She rose and crossed the room and did something she almost never did: she took Astrid's face in her gloved hands, thumbs resting along the jaw, and looked at her the way one reads a difficult page.

"The house will need holding while I'm gone. However long the gone is."

"I'll hold it."

"I know. That's what frightens me, a little. You hold things so quietly I sometimes think you'd hold the whole world without telling anyone your arms were tired." She released her, briskly, and became again the woman of gloves and resolutions. "Odette knows the coal account. Tomas is not to go out — not to the corner, not for matches, not for anything. And Astrid—" at the door she paused, hand on the frame, not turning around, "—if it happens, don't fight them. Whatever you see. Don't get yourself written down. One name on their ledger is enough from this house."

But Astrid was already lost to that instruction, because she had learned long ago — in a dormitory where the matron's footsteps meant bread or meant punishment depending on nothing one could read — that instructions made to protect you were the first things to be broken.

* * *

November gave them a hard bright day, the kind that seems manufactured for photographs: light like struck flint off the wet pavements, plane leaves burning yellow along the Embankment railings. Astrid drove Miriam and Odette to Caxton Hall with the engine running sweetly and her own heart running less sweetly, and parked where she could watch the doors.

The hall was full — hats, banners in purple and green and white, the smell of wet wool and lavender water and the ghost of cigarette smoke. Astrid stayed near the car, cap pulled low, hands in her pockets, doing what she did best: watching, memorizing, standing at the edge of history with her collar up.

She had believed, until that hour, that history happened elsewhere. She had told herself this for thirty years — in the orphanage ledger where her name sat in careful copperplate between strangers' names; in the houses where she had served, invisible as dust; here, in Miriam's household, reading other people's letters and calling it prudence. History was for the named and the noted, for men in offices with ledgers of their own. She was only a driver. Her hands were for the wheel.

Then the deputation came out into the light.

There were perhaps three hundred of them, walking four abreast down the broad pavement toward Parliament, quiet as a congregation, and the strangeness of it was the quiet — no shouting, no stones, just women walking with their chins set, carrying petitions like brides carrying orange blossom. Miriam walked near the front in her grey wool and her deliberately pinned hat. Odette walked beside her, small and straight-backed, her seamstress's hands folded. The flint-light fell on all of them equally.

Astrid followed along the opposite pavement, moving when the crowd moved, stopping when it stopped. She saw the police before the marchers seemed to: constables thickening at the corners like weather coming in, inspectors on the kerbs with notebooks already open, and — her stomach turned over slowly, like a car taking a hill too fast — a plainclothes man she knew. Broad shoulders, bowler a size too careful, the stillness of a man who has spent his life watching doors. Pell had described him well enough that she'd known him before she'd ever seen him: Inspector Vane, arrived at the edge of her vision the way trouble always arrives, having been there some time already.

The order came without ceremony. It came as orders do — a whistle, a wall of blue shifting forward, and then the pavement broke like ice.

They went for the front rank first. Astrid saw the banner go down, purple silk trampled under boots; saw a grey-haired woman struck across the mouth with a rolled warrant; heard the sound the crowd made, which was not screaming at first but something worse, a great indrawn breath, three hundred people inhaling at once. Then the screaming started, and the grabbing, constables seizing wrists and collars, women linking arms and being peeled apart finger by finger like fruit.

She found Odette in the middle of it because Odette was wearing the coat Astrid had noticed that morning — dove-grey, mended at the cuff — and because Odette did not run. She stood over Miriam, who had gone down, hatless now, shielding her with her own body, and a constable had Odette's arm twisted behind her and was bending her toward the pavement, and Astrid's feet were moving before any part of her that kept accounts could vote on it.

Later she could never reconstruct the intervening steps. She remembered the cold iron smell of the railing; remembered her hand closing on the constable's sleeve — coarse blue wool, a brass button under her palm warm from his wrist; remembered saying, in a voice she did not recognize as her own, low and level as an engine idling:

"Take your hands off her."

He turned. He was young, and startled, and for one heartbeat he was only a boy in a uniform, and then training took him and he wrenched free and someone else seized Astrid's shoulder and the world became elbows and breath and the taste of smoke though nothing burned. Through it all she held to Odette — got an arm around her waist, dragged her sideways out of the closing grip, felt the crowd swallow them both. Somewhere behind, in a voice pitched to carry, Miriam Halloway was saying, distinctly, calmly, as though dictating a letter:

*I am Mrs. Miriam Halloway, of Number 9 Larkspur Row. I am not resisting. I wish that to be recorded.*

Recorded. Of course. Even now, even here, Miriam understood what Astrid had understood all her life in reverse: that being written down was power, if you chose the words yourself.

Astrid pushed Odette through the thinning edge of the crowd, past a shuttered tobacconist, into the mouth of a side street, and there they stopped, gasping, clinging to each other and to the wall. Behind them the pavement emptied of everything but hats and leaves and one abandoned shoe. Down at the corner, the black van waited with its doors open, and they watched — because they could not help watching — as Miriam Halloway was led to it, spine straight, hat gone, grey wool coat immaculate, and stepped up inside as though boarding a carriage she had ordered herself.

The door closed. The sound of it was very small for so large an ending.

"She let them," Odette said. Her accent had thickened; fear did that to her. "She could have walked away at the corner, I saw her look at the corner. She *looked* at it, Astrid. And she walked forward instead."

"Yes."

"Why? Why would anyone—"

"Because," Astrid said, and found the words arriving from somewhere she hadn't known she kept them, "a body in a cell says what a voice through a keyhole cannot."

Odette stared at her. "You've been reading her books."

"I've been reading everything," said Astrid, "for years," and then closed her eyes briefly, because the confession had come out sideways, in the wrong place, to the wrong audience, and there was no gathering it back.

Down the street, the van pulled away toward Cannon Row with its cargo of testimony, and Astrid watched it go with her hand pressed flat against cold brick, and understood that she was watching the exact moment her old life ended — the life where she stood at edges, where history was a thing in newspapers that happened to people whose names mattered. She was thirty-one years old and orphaned twice over now: once by a ledger, once by a prison van.

The constable had seen her face. The inspector's men took names. Somewhere in Vane's book, between lines about irregular persons and unregistered boarders, there might soon be a new entry, and it would be hers.

* * *

They did not speak much on the drive home. Odette sat pressed against the far door of the motorcar, gloved hands knotted in her lap, and the streets slid past — the same streets, the same costermongers and milk carts and clerks pouring out of the Strand — and the obscenity of it was how ordinary everything remained. A woman was in a cell for asking to be counted, and the city went on buttering its bread.

Tomas met them at the door, having heard — he would not say how; news ran through Larkspur Row faster than the post — and his face when he saw theirs was the face of a man who has been counting footsteps outside a sickroom. He asked nothing. He looked at Odette's torn glove, at Astrid's scraped knuckles, at the space beside them where a third person should have been and wasn't, and he stood aside to let them in.

Wren was on the stairs.

The child had heard something — children always hear; the deaf and the mute hear most of all — and had come down in her nightdress though it was nearly four o'clock, dragging her chalk in one fist. She looked from Odette to Astrid and then past them, at the empty doorway, and her face did something terrible and slow, the way a sky does before rain: comprehension arriving feature by feature.

Astrid crouched down to her level. There was nothing to say that words would carry, so she opened her arms, and Wren came into them like something falling, small and fierce, fingers gripping Astrid's collar with a strength that belonged to drowning.

Behind them, in the parlour, Tomas put the kettle on. Odette lit the lamp though the afternoon was barely failing. Small acts of siege economy; the household fortifying itself against evening without being asked. Astrid registered all of it from inside the child's embrace and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I am the one who answers questions now. I am the one the knock on the door comes to. She has left me the keys and the ledger and the fear, and I am expected to carry all three without dropping any.*

At the orphanage there had been a matron's phrase for the older girls who minded the little ones when staff fled or fell ill or simply stopped coming: *mother-girls*. Mother-girls got no wages and no thanks and slept nearest the door in case of fire. Astrid had sworn, aged twelve, that she would never be one again.

She held Wren until the shaking stopped, and then she stood, and brushed her skirt flat, and went to find paper.

* * *

The kitchen that night was lit low — lamp turned down to save oil, fire rationed to coals, the table scrubbed to bone-colour by Odette's endless scrubbing. Four places where there had lately been five. No one moved Miriam's chair; no one sat in it either. It stood at the head of the table like a monument to a country that had fallen.

"We go tomorrow," Odette said, when the tea was poured. Not arguing. Stating. "All of us. Tonight if I could pack it. While they are busy with their prisoners and their rallies. While the inspector's eye is on Whitehall and not on Larkspur Row."

"Tomas can't travel without papers that don't exist," Astrid said. "And Wren has no papers at all. And you—" she hesitated, and decided honesty was cheaper than kindness tonight, "—your reference letters name this house. If they're looking at this house, you walk into it."

"Then we find another way. Ships, trains, the boat train to—"

"Astrid." Tomas's voice, quiet, from the end of the table. He had not touched his tea. "What aren't you telling us."

The lamp hissed. Upstairs a floorboard settled. Astrid looked around the table at the three faces the world had misfiled — a deserter, a runaway, a child unclaimed by anyone — and felt the weight settle onto her shoulders with the terrible familiarity of something she had carried before, in another house, in another life, and sworn off forever.

"That constable saw my face," she said. "My name may already be written down. Which means this house has days, perhaps. Which means we do not run tomorrow like frightened people. We go properly, with berths and papers and a destination, or we do not go at all — because half-escaped is only caught, slower." She heard Miriam in her own voice as she said it and did not know whether that comforted or appalled her. "Tomorrow I go to the shipping office. Tomorrow I find out what passage costs and what it requires. Tomorrow I start building us a way out that holds weight."

"And tonight?" Odette asked.

Tonight there was a knock at the front door.

Not loud. That was the worst of it — not the fist of a raid but two soft raps, courteous, almost apologetic, the knock of a man who has all the time in the world and a notebook to fill it with. Tomas went white and did not move. Odette reached for Wren. The chair at the head of the table stood empty, and Astrid realized, rising, wiping her hands on her skirt, that for the first time in the history of Number 9 Larkspur Row, there was no one to answer the door but her.

Through the frosted glass of the fanlight, swaying slightly, hung the shape of a bowler hat.
