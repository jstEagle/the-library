## 11. The Empty Chair

The chair at the head of the table had been Miriam's — a high-backed thing of dark walnut, its arms worn smooth as river stones by twenty years of leaning elbows and decisive hands — and Odette could not stop looking at it.

Three days since the arrest. Three days, and the house had already begun to learn a new shape around that absence, the way water learns the shape of a stone pulled from a stream: rushing inward, filling, pretending nothing was ever there. But the chair was there, empty, catching the grey light from the window like an open mouth, and every morning Odette came down early to dust it, though no one had asked her to.

She was a seamstress. She understood garments. She understood that a house, like a dress, held its structure by its seams, and she could feel this one splitting — quietly, stitch by stitch, in the hours when no one was watching.

The coal scuttle told the story first. On Monday there had been perhaps two days' worth of small Welsh anthracite left, the good kind that burned long and clean, and Odette had made the decision without announcing it: fires in the kitchen only. The parlour grate went cold. The upstairs rooms went cold. Wren's room they kept warm because Wren was five years old and had night terrors that sent her padding down the stairs at all hours, small feet silent on the treads, appearing in doorways like something out of a folk tale — the child who came with the cold.

Odette sat now at the kitchen table with the last of the mending heaped before her and did not mend it. Her needle lay where she had set it down, threaded but idle, a thin bright line of nothing. Through the window above the sink she could see the street, wet with a rain that couldn't decide whether to be serious, and beyond it the plane trees of Russell Square dropping their leaves early, as if even the trees wanted to be elsewhere.

Astrid had gone out at seven with the motorcar and come back at nine without the motorcar — sold, or pawned, or hidden; no one had asked, and Astrid had offered nothing, only come into the kitchen smelling of petrol and November and set a paper sack of groceries on the table with a thump that said *don't*.

So Odette didn't. Not yet. But the question was in her, swelling, and she knew it would have to come out eventually, the way a seam under strain will give.

Tomas was in the cellar. He spent his days there now, among the coal dust and the shelved preserves, reading a newspaper so old its ink had gone soft, emerging only after dark like something nocturnal and ashamed. He was eating less. Odette noticed food the way other people noticed weather; it was her trade's close cousin, this business of how bodies were kept. Tomas was disappearing by inches, folding himself smaller so the house would have less of him to hide.

And Wren —

Wren was drawing on the kitchen floor again.

Odette saw it as she rose to put the kettle on: chalk marks on the flagstones by the door, half-scrubbed by small determined hands but not quite gone. Stars. A crooked row of them, five-pointed, uneven, and beneath them a shape that might have been a man or might have been a lamppost — tall, dark, hat bristling off the top of it like iron filings.

"Wren." Odette crouched, careful of her knees on the cold stone. "Sweetheart. Who is this?"

The child looked at her with those enormous dark eyes that gave away nothing and everything, and shook her head — not refusal exactly, but a closing, like a curtain drawn an inch.

"You can tell me," Odette said. "You can always tell me."

But Wren could not, or would not — no one knew which, and the not-knowing was itself a kind of wound the whole household carried — and she turned instead to the window, to the street, and pointed one small finger at the corner opposite, where the plane trees ended and the iron railing began.

Nothing there. A milk cart passing. A woman with a pram. An ordinary Tuesday in an ordinary London, going about its ordinary business.

Except that Odette had been a runaway for two years now, and runaways learned to read streets the way literate people read pages, and what she read on that corner — in the angle of a man's shoulders against the railing, in the way his newspaper had not turned a page in the quarter hour she'd been watching — was a single word, printed large:

*Waiting.*

* * *

The kettle sang. Odette made tea she diluted four ways because the tin was light and tomorrow was far away, and she carried a cup up to Tomas in the cellar because that was what she did now, apparently — she was twenty-three years old and she was keeping house for fugitives, which had not been in any of the futures she'd sewn for herself back in the workroom in Lyon, back when her ambitions ran to a forewoman's position and a room of her own with a real stove in it.

Futures shrank. That was the lesson of the age. You started wanting a room with a stove and ended grateful for a cellar with a candle.

Tomas took the cup with both hands, the way he took everything now, as if it might be taken back. He was sitting on an overturned crate with the dead newspaper on his knees, and in the candlelight his face had the grey translucence of a man slowly becoming a ghost ahead of schedule.

"Any word?" he asked. His voice had gone quiet, cellar-quiet, adapted to the acoustics of hiding.

"From Holloway? No. Solicitor's clerk came yesterday. Says the hearing's set for the twelfth." Odette lowered herself onto the second crate, the one she'd claimed as hers the first week, when the cellar had been a place they visited rather than inhabited. "Missus Halloway will make a speech of it, you'll see. She'll turn the dock into a platform. They've given her exactly what she wanted."

"And us?"

"Us we mind ourselves."

He nodded, drank, and asked the question she'd been dreading, in the mild way he asked everything, which somehow made it worse. "How long, Odette? Honestly. How long can this house hold me?"

She looked at him — at the hollows under his cheekbones, at the army boots he still wore because he owned nothing else, at the tremor in his left hand that appeared when he was tired and that he believed nobody noticed — and she lied to him, gently, the way you lied to the dying.

"As long as needs be."

He smiled, because he knew it was a lie, and let her keep it. That was the economy of kindness in this house: everyone owed everyone a comfortable fiction, and the debts mounted silently, and no one spoke of calling them in.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, came the sound of Wren's footsteps crossing the kitchen — then stopping — then the small scratch-scratch of chalk.

* * *

When Astrid came in at noon, Odette was waiting for her in the kitchen with her arms folded and the words already sharpened.

"We need to talk."

Astrid set down her satchel — heavier than it had been at nine; Odette noted these things; the woman had been to a bank or a broker or somewhere money changed forms — and looked at Odette with those pale, unreadable eyes that made people underestimate her until it was too late. "We're talking."

"Not here." Odette tipped her chin toward the ceiling, toward the listening floorboards and the child above them. "The scullery."

The scullery was cold and smelled of soap and standing water, and in its narrow brick confines the two women faced each other like duellists who had once been friends and weren't sure yet whether they still were.

"He's watching the house," Odette said. "Corner of the square. Grey coat. Since yesterday at least — probably longer; I only clocked him because Wren drew him first."

"I know."

"You *know.*" Odette heard her own voice rise and dragged it down to a whisper. "You know, and you didn't say?"

"There was nothing to say yet. I've seen him. I've also seen him relieved twice, mid-morning and dusk, regular as church bells. One man, one post, observation only. If Vane meant to come through that door he'd have done it with a warrant by now." Astrid unwound her scarf with maddening calm. "He's waiting for the hearing. Whatever Miriam says at the trial decides whether we're worth the paperwork."

"*Worth* the paperwork." Odette laughed, and it came out ugly. "Listen to yourself. There's a deserter in your cellar and an unregistered child drawing portraits of policemen on your kitchen floor, and you're calculating paperwork."

"I'm calculating survival," Astrid said, and for a moment — just a moment — the calm cracked and Odette saw the thing underneath it, which was not coldness but terror, held the way Astrid held everything: tightly, silently, behind a wall built in some childhood Odette could barely imagine. "Which is more than anyone in this house did before I—" She stopped. Started again. "The point is the plan is moving. Berths. Passage. It's arranged, mostly. We need—"

"We need to go *now.*" Odette stepped closer. In the scullery's dimness their breath made small ghosts between them. "Today. Tonight. Pack what we can carry, take Tomas out the back before his relief comes on at dusk, get to the coast while the roads are still just roads. Every day we stay is a day that man out there learns our habits. Our faces. When the lights go out. Which nights Wren cries."

"And go where?" Astrid's voice was low and terrible. "With what? The berths aren't paid. The names aren't fixed. You walk out tonight and by Friday you're three strangers sleeping in King's Cross with a child who can't call for help and a soldier whose face is in a regimental gazette. You wouldn't be running from Vane. You'd be running into him, or into someone like him, with none of this—" she gestured at the walls around them, the whole improbable shelter of the house— "*none* of this between you and the world."

"This house won't protect us forever, Astrid."

"Nothing protects anyone forever. This house protects us *tonight.* And tomorrow night. That's how staying alive works — one wall at a time."

"That's how *dying* works too!" The whisper tore. Odette pressed her knuckles to her mouth, forced herself lower, slower. "Miriam would go," she said. "If it were her choosing. She'd put us on the road tonight and stand here alone to answer for it, gladly, because that's what she built this place *for*. We're not holding the house. We're hiding in it and calling it courage."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere above them Wren's footsteps crossed and recrossed the kitchen floor, restless as a bird in a cage.

"You didn't know her before," Astrid said finally. "Before all this. Before the rallies and the arrests. She held this house together through worse than a man on a corner — through the year the fund collapsed, through the raid on the print shop, through—" A breath. "She'd tell you the same thing I'm telling you. Hold. A household that scatters is four funerals waiting to happen. A household that holds is a household."

"She isn't *here,* Astrid. That's my whole point. The person who knows how to hold things is in Holloway, and we are two young women, a frightened boy, and a baby, and we are improvising."

"We are not improvising." Something had changed in Astrid's voice — a hard, bright certainty Odette had never heard in her before, and it frightened her more than the anger had, because it sounded like faith, and faith in this house had always been Miriam's department. "There is a ship. There is a list. There is a way, and I have found it, and it needs eleven days, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you — to give me eleven days."

Odette searched her face. Underneath the certainty, the terror was still there; underneath the terror, something else — something that looked, astonishingly, like love, worn as awkwardly as a garment cut for someone else's body.

"Ten," Odette said. "And on the tenth day, if the way isn't open, I take Wren and I go, and God forgive me, I don't look back."

Something in Astrid's jaw shifted. For a moment Odette thought she'd pushed too far, broken the fragile accord past mending — and then Astrid nodded, once, sharply, like a woman signing a document she hated.

"Ten," she said. "Done."

They stood there another moment in the cold scullery, two conspirators shaking hands over the ruins of an argument, neither entirely trusting the other, both entirely bound.

* * *

Night came down early and mean, the fog off the river reaching even this far north, seeping yellow through the curtains. Odette heated soup that was more water than anything and they ate it in the kitchen — all four of them, because Astrid had insisted, had gone down to the cellar herself and stood in the doorway until Tomas climbed the stairs, and whatever passed between them in that doorway, Tomas came up changed, quieter and straighter at once, like a man who had been given orders he could live with.

Wren ate three spoonfuls and pushed the bowl away and began, with the intense solemnity she brought to everything, to draw on the tabletop's edge with her finger — tracing invisible lines, rehearsing shapes she'd soon commit to chalk.

"Stars," Odette guessed softly.

Wren nodded. Then she paused. Her finger stopped mid-air.

Slowly, deliberately, she looked up at the ceiling — at the crack that ran from the light fixture to the corner, the crack Odette had plastered over twice that autumn — and pointed. Then she pointed at the window. At the door. Back at the ceiling.

"What is it, little one?" Tomas asked. "What do you see?"

Wren frowned at their incomprehension, frustrated, and then did the most extraordinary thing. She slid off her chair, fetched her stub of chalk from the pocket of her pinafore where it lived like a talisman, and drew — right there on the kitchen flagstones, in full view of them all — a house. A simple house, square, with a door and two windows. Above it, stars. Beside it, a smaller shape: a boat, or the idea of a boat, a hull and a sail.

Then she took Astrid's hand, and Odette's, and laid them both flat on the drawing, one on the house, one on the boat.

"No," Odette said, understanding arriving cold as well-water. "No, sweetheart, we're not—"

But Wren was looking at Astrid, only at Astrid, with an urgency that needed no words at all, and Astrid had gone very still, her hand resting on the chalk house, her face suddenly the colour of the fog outside.

"The boat," Astrid said quietly. "She means the boat has to sail. She means time."

Outside, down at the corner of the square, a whistle blew — two notes, short and sharp, the signal for relief, or the signal for something else. Footsteps on wet pavement, unhurried, coming closer along the railings.

And above them, in the room where the fire was allowed, the empty chair creaked in a draught no one had made, and the house held its breath around them like a hand closing slowly into a fist.
