## 12. Vane's Offer

He was waiting for her at the motorcar.

Astrid saw him from the far end of Haverstock Hill, a dark shape leaning against the Daimler's running board as though the machine belonged to him, as though the whole street belonged to him — and in a way she supposed it did now, or enough of it that Inspector Vane could stand in the grey drizzle of an October afternoon with his hands folded over his ledger and let the world arrange itself around his patience.

She had left Miriam's solicitor's office ten minutes earlier with a sheaf of appeal papers buttoned inside her coat, and she had taken the long way back on foot because she had felt, for three days now, the particular pressure at the back of her neck that meant eyes. Wren had felt it too. The chalk stars had appeared on the pavement outside the house twice since the rally — once drawn small and frightened in the hour before dawn, once larger, defiant, redrawn after supper while Odette held the lamp. *A man watching*, the child had written without words, drawing his hat brim low over his chalk face, giving him chalk hands that curled like talons.

The man in the drawing had not been half so tall as the man at the motorcar.

"You walk," Vane said, by way of greeting. He did not straighten. Rain beaded on the brim of his hat and hung there, refusing to fall. "Miss Halloway's previous drivers all drove."

"The petrol ration is mine to manage," Astrid said. "I manage it."

"Economical." He turned the word over as if tasting it. "That's what Pell wrote. *Economical. Observant. Answers before the question finishes.*" His eyes came up, pale as dishwater, and settled on her with the flat weight of something that had been set down carefully and could not easily be lifted again. "He admired you, Miss Delacroix. Clerks rarely admire anyone. It worried me."

"Should it worry me?"

"It should interest you." He stepped away from the running board at last, unhurried, and opened the motorcar's door for her — a courtesy so unexpected it landed like a trap sprung shut. "Walk with me instead. Ten minutes. I'll return you to your household dry, employed, and unremarked upon. Or refuse, and I conduct this conversation at the house, on the step, where the seamstress keeps looking out through the lace curtains as though the curtains might save her."

Astrid looked at his hand on the doorframe. She calculated distances — to the house, four hundred yards; to the tram stop, two hundred; to anywhere at all, too far.

"Ten minutes," she said.

* * *

They walked toward the canal, which was where she should have known it would go. The water ran the colour of old pewter between its stone lips, and the gasworks on the far bank breathed their sweet tar smell across the towpath, and the rain thickened from mist into honest drops that ticked against Vane's umbrella when he raised it over them both. She noted that too — the umbrella held evenly, half for her, a small expenditure of care that cost him nothing and meant everything and nothing.

"You were at the orphanage on Coram Fields," he said. "Saint Agatha's Annex, properly. Admitted aged six. No surname on intake — they assigned you Delacroix from the baptismal register of a French chapel that burned the following year." He consulted nothing. He did not need to. "Emancipated at sixteen with a character reference and one pound four shillings. You have done remarkably well for a girl with no people."

The rain sounded very loud suddenly. Astrid kept her eyes on the water.

"Is this the part where you tell me my position is precarious? That you can take it from me?" Her voice came out level, and she was grateful to it. "I've heard it before. Matrons enjoy it too."

"Matrons enjoy power. I don't." He stopped walking. On the canal a barge slid past, low in the water with coal, the horse on the towpath throwing its head against the drizzle. "I want to show you something."

He took out his pocketbook first, and then, from behind it, a photograph — the photograph, she realized, the one she had glimpsed in his breast pocket during the interview, the corner of it worn soft and white as a moth's wing from handling. He held it out flat on his palm, sheltering it under the umbrella's edge, and for the first time since she had met him, Inspector Vane looked like a man standing in weather.

A girl. Perhaps eleven years old in the image, hair in two severe plaits, one boot resting on a garden roller. Behind her, a wall. On the wall — Astrid leaned closer despite herself — chalk marks. Stars, dozens of them, drawn in a child's ascending hand, climbing toward the corner of the frame as though the girl had been building a ladder out of the garden.

"Emily," Vane said. "My daughter. Eleven years and four months, which she would tell you precisely, because four months mattered to her." The photograph trembled. Not much. Enough. "Her mother died in '04. Emily went to her aunt in Bristol while I was seconded north. In June of '07 the aunt put her on a boat — an emigration scheme, respectable, church-chartered, perfectly documented. Canada. They called it a fresh start for orphan children." His voice did not change at all, which was somehow worse than if it had broken. "There was no aunt. There was a woman paid five shillings a head by the society to collect children off trains. My daughter stepped off a gangway in Quebec and into a household that worked her until her hands cracked, and then moved her on, and moved her on, and the files — Miss Delacroix, I have read every file the Dominions keep on home children, thousands of them, and they are ledgers of names crossed out."

Astrid thought of a ledger in a matron's office, her own name entered in copperplate and struck through with a single ruled line when the money for her keep ran out. She thought: *he knows*. And then, immediately, coldly: *of course he knows. That is how he hunts. He has read the same books I have, and we are both fluent in what a crossed-out name means.*

"I'm sorry," she said, and found that she meant it, which frightened her more than anything else he had done.

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." He put the photograph away, but slowly, the way one closes a door on a room one must leave. "Everything I do, I do because somewhere on some farm or in some factory cellar there is another Emily who has been made irregular on purpose — made irregular by people who mean well, who shelter, who forge, who lie beautifully to clerks like Pell. Every child hidden in a back bedroom without papers is a child who cannot be found by anyone, ever, including the people who love her. Irregularity sounds like mercy. It is a wilderness. I am trying to burn it back."

"And the deserter?" Astrid asked. "Does he fit your wilderness?"

"Tomas Halloway refused an order at Loos. Watched his brother's company go over and did not follow, and was sentenced, and ran before sentence was carried." Vane said it without heat, reciting. "He is not a child. He made his choice with his eyes open. But you see how the threads knot. A deserter hidden in a suffragist's house, a Frenchwoman sewing under a dead woman's name, a mute girl nobody will claim — and at the centre of it, a driver who reads letters, lies to census men, and walks home with appeal papers in her coat." He watched her flinch — she could not stop it — and continued gently, terribly. "Yes. I know about the papers. I know about the shipping line. I know you enquired at Cunard's offices nine days ago, and I know why the clerk smiled at you and quoted prices you cannot pay."

The canal horse passed out of earshot. Somewhere behind the gasworks a whistle blew, shift-change, thin and far.

"Here is the offer, and it is the only one, and I will make it once." Vane turned to face her fully, and up close his eyes were not cruel; they were exhausted, which was different and more dangerous. "Give me Tomas Halloway. Name, address of the recruiting depot where he may surrender himself quietly, no escort, no spectacle — I give you my word he goes into the army's hands alive, and deserters who surrender voluntarily are not shot in 1911, whatever he dreams at night. Give me the deserter." He held up one finger. "And in exchange: the child becomes regular. I will file Wren myself — foundling, ward of the Crown, placed under the guardianship of a suitable person of good standing. Miriam Halloway, even from Holloway prison, can be named. Papers. A name in a register that no one may strike through. A birthright, manufactured honestly out of my own pen." A second finger. "Odette keeps her alias; I lose her file. And your own employment, your own history, your one pound four shillings and everything you've built on top of it — none of it need ever appear in any record anywhere."

The rain had stopped. Neither of them had noticed. Water dripped from the umbrella's ribs, counting.

"One man," Vane said softly. "One grown man, who deserted, who knew the price when he ran. Against a child who will otherwise spend her whole life unfindable, uncrossable, belonging to no register on earth. You of all people know what that means. You were that child, and someone ruled a line through you, and you spent twenty years making yourself into a person the line couldn't reach." He paused. "I am offering to draw her inside the lines, Miss Delacroix. All you must do is let one soldier stand outside them, where he chose to stand."

Astrid stood very still.

She understood — with the terrible clarity that sometimes arrived in her, the clerk's clarity, the reader-of-letters' clarity — that he believed every word. That this was not corruption or bargain or trick. That Inspector Vane had taken his wound and built a cathedral on top of it, and the cathedral was procedure, and inside its vaults every lost child was a candle, and he would light as many as he had to, and the wax was other people. Tomas. Perhaps Odette. Perhaps, in the end, Astrid herself. He was not a man who broke things. He was a man who filed them.

And she understood the second thing too, the colder one: that the offer was real, and that refusing it would convert her from a subject into an obstacle, and that obstacles, in his ledger, got pages of their own.

"If I say yes," she said, "you'll still flag every berth out of Liverpool. You'll still be watching the house. The child's papers would arrive in an envelope with your return address, and every year of her childhood would arrive in another one."

"Yes," Vane said simply. "Guardianship requires oversight. That is what guardianship *is*."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the law proceeds as it proceeds." He closed the umbrella, shook it once, neat. "I take no pleasure in—"

"No," Astrid said.

He stopped.

"No," she said again, and heard her own voice as if from a little distance, steady and unfamiliar, the voice of someone she had been becoming without noticing. "You're wrong about what you're offering, and I'll tell you why, because you read files and I read people. Wren doesn't want a page in your register. She wants a chair at a table where second suppers exist, and a step where somebody repaints her stars when the rain takes them. Tomas isn't a thread to be knotted or cut. He is a man who tells ghost stories badly to make a child laugh, and he carries the washing up because Odette's wrists ache, and he is *ours*. You want me to trade a member of my family for paperwork describing a stranger with her face."

"She is not your family," Vane said. "She is a child you found. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire machinery of the state."

"Then let the machinery come," Astrid said. "It can queue at the door with everyone else. Miriam taught us that — you knock, and we decide who is kin, not you."

For a moment neither moved. Then Vane did something she had not expected: he took out the photograph again, just briefly, and looked at it, and she saw the trembling hand and understood at last that the trembling had never been grief alone. It was fury, held so long and so tightly that it had worn down to this — a fine continuous vibration, like a struck bell that would not finish sounding. His daughter's chalk stars climbed the wall of his lost garden, and somewhere across an ocean a young woman existed now who did not know his name, and every irregular person in London was, to him, a door hiding her.

"You will regret this," he said. Not a threat. A prognosis, delivered with clinical sorrow. "Not today. One day, when the house is empty and the choices are gone, you will stand exactly where I'm standing, holding exactly what I'm holding, and you will understand that I offered you the only mercy the world actually contains, which is a name that protects."

"A name that protects," Astrid said quietly, "is one given freely. Ask anyone whose name lives in your ledger."

She walked away from him along the towpath, and he let her go, and that was the worst of it — no hand on her shoulder, no summons, only the sound of his boots turning the opposite direction and the knowledge settling into her bones like the canal damp: *I have made an enemy, and his enemy-hood is made of love, and love does not tire, love does not blink, love checks the manifests every morning at dawn.*

Behind her, faintly, she heard him open his pocketbook and write.

* * *

She did not go straight home. She walked the long way, past the shipping office with its green lamps burning behind frosted glass, and stood across the street in the last light while a clerk inside locked a drawer — the drawer, she had no doubt, that held the passenger manifest sealing at dawn. Three berths. She had counted the money twice and it made two berths and half of a third, and half of a third bought no one anything but a longer list.

In her coat pocket her fingers found the stub of red chalk she carried now everywhere, since the night she'd understood what Wren's drawings were. Not warnings only. Addresses. A language for the findable.

When she finally came up the street to the house, the lamplight showed her the front step, and she stopped.

New chalk. Fresh, pressed hard, still crumbling at the edges. Not stars this time.

Wren had drawn three figures on the step — tall, tall, small — and beside them a fourth figure, separate, drawn in careful outline and then deliberately, thoroughly, rubbed out with the side of her fist, so that only a smudge remained where a fourth person should stand. Above the smudge, the child had written a single word in her round, urgent hand:

*TOMORROW.*

Astrid stood on the pavement in the cold and understood that the child had seen it before she had — had seen the arithmetic of the manifest, the shape of the choice coming toward them all like weather.

From inside the house came the sound of Odette's sewing machine, and Tomas laughing at something, and the kettle beginning its low climb toward singing. Everything ordinary. Everything about to end.

Astrid picked up the chalk stub, crouched on the step beside the smudge, and beneath Wren's word she drew one star. Then she went in to tell them the ship sailed at morning tide — and that she had until dawn to learn whether three berths could be made four, or whether tomorrow would ask them the question she could not yet bear to say aloud.
