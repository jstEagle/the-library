## 13. What the Letters Said

The kitchen at Hollis Row had never been so quiet, and it had known funerals.

Astrid stood at the scrubbed table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, the way she did when she was about to clean something — a carburettor, a wound, a conscience — but there was nothing in her hands. Only the lamp between them, its flame bending when the door draught came under the sill, and three faces turned toward her across the wood: Odette with her arms folded tight as bandages, Tomas standing by the range as if he might need to leave through any of its doors, and Wren perched on the stool in the corner, chalk stub in hand, drawing nothing.

Above them all, Miriam's chair stood empty. It had been eleven days since they had carried the letter from Holloway Prison home in Astrid's coat pocket, eleven days since she had read Miriam's voice aloud to this room — *Choose as a mother chooses, not as a servant* — and still none of them sat in that chair. It seemed wrong to. It seemed worse to let it stand empty forever.

"I have to tell you something," Astrid said, "and after I tell it, you may want me gone. I'd ask you to hear all of it first. The whole ledger, before you cross anything out."

Odette's chin lifted a fraction. "You frighten me when you talk like a solicitor."

"I'm not a solicitor. I'm worse." Astrid drew a breath that tasted of coal smoke and yesterday's tea leaves. "I read your letters."

The words fell into the room like coins down a well. She watched them go down and down, waiting for the splash.

"All of them," she went on, because stopping now would be a mercy and mercy was not what she deserved or needed. "Every letter that has come through my hands since I took the post as Miriam's driver, six years ago. I read them on the seat of the motorcar, in the mews, at dawn before she sees them. I read the ones from her sister in York and the ones from women whose names I've never learned. I read yours." She looked at Odette. "The one your mother sent from Calais, folded inside the pattern for a child's dress. And yours." Tomas, who had gone very still against the range. "The one from your brother's widow, that came in March. I know what he wrote to you the week before he died, because she copied it out for you herself."

Tomas's face did something she had never seen it do: it closed entirely, like a shop pulling down its shutters.

"You have no right," Odette said. Her voice was low and precise, the voice of a woman who sewed for a living and knew what a single pulled thread could do to a seam. "No right at all."

"No. None."

"Then why?" It came out of Odette half a cry, strangled at birth. "We trusted you. We gave you our names — the real ones, the dangerous ones — because we thought—" She stopped, pressed her knuckles to her mouth, started again. "I told you things in letters to my cousin. Things about my father. I never said them aloud in this house. I thought paper was private."

"Paper is never private," Astrid said. "That's the first thing an orphanage teaches you, if it teaches you anything. The matron reads the post. The board reads the files. Your whole life fits in a ledger, and men you'll never meet decide from it whether you're worth a winter coat." She flattened her hands on the table; they were steady, which surprised her, because everything behind her sternum was shaking. "So yes. I learned young that reading a thing first is the difference between being ready and being ruined. I kept the habit. I told myself it was protection — mine, then Miriam's, then yours. But that's only half true, and tonight I can't afford half truths."

Wren slid off the stool without sound and came to stand near the table, not touching it, watching Astrid with those grey eyes that missed nothing. The child had known. Astrid felt it with sick certainty. Wren, who spoke in chalk and silence, had probably understood the shape of this secret long before any of them.

"The other half," Astrid said, "is that I liked knowing things. Knowing made me feel — " she hunted for the word and hated the one that arrived — "*kept.* Like a person who matters enough to be informed. In the orphanage I once found the ledger page where my own name had been crossed out. Just a line through *Astrid Delacroix*, and beneath it, in different ink, nothing. No transfer, no discharge, no death recorded. Erased by accident, most likely. A clerk's slip of the pen. But I stood there aged nine and understood that the world could lose me on paper and not notice." Her thumb moved along the table's grain. "So I read. Everything. Because as long as I held everyone's letters, no one could erase me without going through me first."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Tomas had not moved from the range, and the fire behind the iron door painted one side of him copper and left the rest in shadow.

"And the shipping agent," he said quietly. "The loophole. The berth that doesn't exist on any public list. Was that in our letters too?"

Astrid met his eyes. "No. That was in theirs."

She crossed to the dresser, took down the biscuit tin where the household kept string and corks and other small useless treasures, and brought back the packet she had hidden there — not in her own room, where a search would look, but among the family's odds and ends, where no inspector would think to rummage. Inside were four letters, soft at the folds from handling.

"Miriam's correspondence with her solicitor, Mr. Fenn. Six years of it. He writes in a cramped hand and thinks himself clever with his discretion — 're: the Hartwell matter,' 're: the northern arrangement.' I cracked his code in my second year, out of boredom and spite, and after that I read everything. Including this." She laid the topmost letter flat and turned it so the lamp caught the ink. "Three years ago, Miriam asked Fenn how a person might move out of England quietly — not fugitives, just irregular people, people the census missed and the militia wants and the workhouses would take back. Fenn answered like a lawyer: dry as dust, all about manifests and berths and the difference between a passenger list and a crew list. And buried in the middle, in a sentence so dull I nearly skimmed it, was the fact that the Cunard line seals its third-class manifest forty-eight hours before sailing, but the purser's supplemental sheet — late additions, infants, transfers — stays open until the gangway goes up."

Tomas frowned. "Meaning?"

"Meaning a name added at the dock is a name the London offices never see. Vane can flag every departure in advance — and he has; I confirmed it at the shipping office, they have instructions about this address — but a name written onto the purser's sheet at the last hour slips past his flags like a fish through a net. It isn't a loophole in the law. It's a gap between two ledgers. And I only know it exists because I spent six years committing this household's post to memory."

Silence again, but a different quality of it. Odette had unfolded her arms. She looked at the letters as though they were fabric she was deciding whether to cut.

"So we owe our escape," she said slowly, "to your sin."

"To my sin. Yes. I didn't find the gap despite betraying you. I found it *by* betraying you, over years, in the mews, at dawn, with cold fingers." Astrid heard her own voice crack and did not smooth it over. "I won't insult you by saying I always meant to confess. I meant to confess the way one means to write to distant relatives. But Vane came here with Pell's notes and smiled at me across the hall, and I understood that if this house falls, it will fall because we were each holding our secrets separately instead of together. He counts on that. A household divided is just a row of solitary cells wearing a roof."

Odette pushed back her chair. For one wild moment Astrid thought the woman would strike her, and some stubborn orphanage corner of her braced for it — blows were legible, at least; blows meant you still counted enough to hit. Instead Odette picked up the packet of letters, weighed it in both palms like bread, and set it down again gently, deliberately, in front of Astrid.

"My mother's letter," she said. "The one inside the dress pattern. What did it say? Tell me what you remember. Exactly."

It was a test, and Astrid knew it, and she was grateful for it the way a drowning woman is grateful for rope.

"It began *Ma petite chou,* though she spelled it wrong, *choux.* She wrote that your father had come to the house twice more and that she had moved the good mirror to the neighbour's so he couldn't see his own face and remember he was handsome. She wrote that she forgave you for leaving without goodbye, but that she wanted it understood forgiveness was not the same as approval. She wrote — " Astrid closed her eyes; the words were stitched into her somewhere now, part of the lining of her — "she wrote that the dress pattern was for a girl of seven, and that she did not know why she was cutting it, only that her hands wanted to make something for a child connected to you, whoever that child turned out to be."

Odette made a sound that was almost laughter and almost grief, and put her hand over her eyes. "She has never met Wren. She doesn't know Wren exists."

"Now she does," Astrid said softly. "On paper. Which means she knows her the way I know all of us — imperfectly, intrusively, completely."

Odette lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet and furious and, beneath the fury, something moving like fish under ice. "You could have told me that. Years ago. You could have given me my mother's love secondhand instead of hoarding it."

"Yes. I could have. That's the shape of what I did wrong — not the reading. The keeping."

Tomas finally left the range. He crossed the kitchen slowly, pulled out a chair — not Miriam's, never that one — and sat, and put his scarred hands flat on the table the way Astrid had put hers, mirroring her without knowing it.

"In the army," he said, "we had a saying. You can sleep in a trench full of water, you can march on broken boots, but you cannot soldier with a man who lies about the rations. Not because the rations matter. Because you have to know that when he says the wire is cut, the wire is cut." He looked up at her. "You lied about the rations for six years, Miss Delacroix."

"I know."

"But." He exhaled through his nose, a soldier's exhale, rationing even his anger. "When you said the wire was cut — at the shipping office, at Vane's door, at the rally when you waded in for Odette — the wire was cut. Every time. So here is my question, and I want the ugly answer, not the comfortable one. If Vane had come to you privately and offered to leave the rest of us alone in exchange for me — would you have told us?"

The kitchen waited. Wren had gone very still beside the table, chalk suspended.

"Yes," Astrid said. "Six years ago, yes. Three years ago, perhaps. Tonight — no. But I want you to understand that the *yes* is not ancient history. It's a habit dying slowly, the way bad habits do, and I killed it a little more each time I chose this house. That's all any of us are, I think. Habits dying slowly, or growing."

Tomas studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded once, the smallest nod in the world, and said, "All right," and that *all right* contained more absolution than a church.

Wren reached up and tugged Astrid's sleeve. When Astrid bent down, the child pressed the chalk stub into her palm and pointed, imperiously, at the flagged floor by the hearth — the one place in the kitchen the matron of the house could never complain about, because there was no matron anymore, only them.

"You want me to write it?" Astrid asked. "Write it down?"

Wren nodded. Then, with her small finger, she drew in the air: a book. Then two hands opening it. Then — and here Astrid's throat closed — the finger drew a heart over the book, the way children do, careless and total.

*Read to us. It's fine. We're yours.*

"She's forgiven you faster than we have," Odette said, wiping her eyes with her wrist, laughing and crying at once. "The little tyrant."

"She's the only one of us without anything to hide," Tomas said. "Maybe that's why."

But Astrid shook her head, kneeling on the cold flagstones with the chalk in her hand. "No. She hides the most of anyone. She just hides it where none of us thought to look." And she drew, on the stone by the hearth, in careful white strokes, an open book, and two hands, and above it — because Wren tapped her shoulder and pointed until she did — a circle of small figures holding hands around the whole of it. Seven figures. One for each of them, plus one extra, drawn slightly apart but still within the circle, with a hat.

Miriam.

They sat late after that, the four of them and the ghost in the chair, and Astrid told them the rest — not the summary but the inventory, item by item, every secret she had ever lifted from their post. Odette's cousin in Manchester who sent money wrapped in tracing paper. Tomas's widow-sister-in-law who wrote twice a year and always enclosed a pressed flower from the grave. The letters Miriam received from women asking for help escaping husbands, fathers, factory foremen, and the addresses where help had been sent. Astrid poured it all out onto the table like coins counted into a creditor's palm, and with each item the room grew lighter rather than heavier, as though the house itself had been holding its breath for six years and could finally breathe.

By the time the candle had burned to its collar, Odette was sketching on the back of a laundry list — berths, dates, sums — and Tomas was doing the quiet arithmetic of a man planning to make a number come out honest, and Wren slept against Astrid's side with chalk dust on her fingers and one shoe off.

"There's just one thing left in the ledger," Astrid said at last, and her voice came out strange, and she understood she was about to say the hardest sentence of the night, harder than the confession itself. "The purser's supplemental sheet stays open until the gangway goes up. I checked the tide tables. The boat train from Euston, the dock gate, the manifest sealing at dawn. There are three of you and — " she made herself finish — "and I bought passage for four when I could only afford three. I forged the fourth name. Mine."

Odette's pencil stopped.

"So when the morning comes," Astrid said, "one of us is staying on this shore. And I want it known, here, in front of witnesses, that I have already chosen, and that none of you will like my choice, and that I will fight you on it with everything I have."

From the pocket of her waistcoat she drew the letter she had been carrying for eleven days, Miriam's letter, soft as cloth now from handling, and laid it in the centre of the table where the lamp could find it.

"Because tomorrow," she said, "we sail at morning tide — and Vane knows it too."

Outside, far down Hollis Row, a match flared briefly behind dark glass, and was gone.
