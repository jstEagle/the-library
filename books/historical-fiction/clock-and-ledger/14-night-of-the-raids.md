## 14. Night of the Raids

The lamps on Gower Street went out one by one, and Astrid counted them.

It was a habit from the orphanage, counting. Counting the candles before matron doused them, counting the stairs, counting the names read aloud at supper to see whose had been struck. She stood at the drawing-room window with her gloves still on — she had not bothered to take them off after locking up the motorcar — and watched the darkness come down the street like a lid closing over a box.

The first thing wrong was the dogs. There were no dogs on Gower Street worth mentioning, and yet somewhere near the corner of Keppel Row a dog began to howl, high and continuous, the sound an animal makes when it understands something before its masters do.

The second thing wrong was the boots. Boots on pavement make a particular music when there are many of them, and when they are in no hurry. Constables run; constables hurry to be anywhere at all. But men who know exactly what they have come for walk as though the street belongs to them already and they are merely collecting it.

"Tomas," Astrid said quietly, without turning. "The cellar."

She heard the chair legs scrape behind her, heard him freeze halfway to standing, the way a hare freezes when it has not yet decided which way the danger lies. Then Odette's voice, low and fast in French, and the quick pad of his feet across the carpet, down the service stair, the coal hatch groaning once before someone thought to muffle it.

Wren did not move at all. The child sat on the hearth-rug with a stub of chalk in her small fist, and she had been drawing stars on the slate board Miriam kept for her since before the lamps went out — stars, always stars, rows and rows of them, five-pointed and lopsided and utterly certain of themselves. Now Wren looked up at Astrid with those grey, unhurried eyes and set the chalk down deliberately, the way a soldier sets down a rifle at the end of a watch.

"I know," Astrid told her. "I see them too."

* * *

They had perhaps four minutes. She gave herself three and spent the fourth standing very still in the hall, listening to the house.

A house has a voice when you know how to hear it. For three years Astrid had listened to this one: Miriam's tread overhead, decisive even at seventy; Odette's needle clacking like a slow telegraph; the settling sighs of old pipes. Tonight the house held its breath. Somewhere below the scullery floor Tomas was folding himself into the dark between the coal sacks, pulling the canvas over his boots, becoming what he had been before he came to them — nothing, no one, a man the paperwork had never agreed to acknowledge.

That was the whole war now, Astrid understood. Not speeches, not deputations, not the great bright machinery of the movement that filled Caxton Hall and filled the newspapers and filled Holloway Prison with women singing through the walls. The real war was fought over paper. Who was written down. Who was counted. Mr. Pell with his census schedule and his too-many questions; Inspector Vane with his ledger of irregular persons, that little black book she had seen him stroke the way other men touched a woman's shoulder.

If they wanted a household made of paper, then paper was what she would give them.

She went to the sideboard drawer where Miriam kept the household accounts, and beneath the accounts, where she had hidden it a week ago with a forger's patience, she found the sheet she had been preparing without quite admitting to herself why. A census page. Genuine stock — Pell's own assistant had left a spare schedule on the hall table on collection night, and Astrid had pocketed it the way she pocketed everything, out of the old orphanage instinct that information unclaimed is information wasted. She had copied Pell's hand for hours by lamplight. His small, wet, self-satisfied letters; his abbreviations; his habit of pressing so hard the pen tore slightly at the downstrokes. She had aged the ink with tea and dried it against the radiator. And in the column for persons who slept in the house on the night of the second of April she had written exactly what the law required and nothing more:

Miriam Halloway, head, widow, 68. Astrid Delacroix, chauffeuse, domestic, 27. Odette Marchand, seamstress, 24. Three names. Three lawful, countable souls. No deserter. No child who belonged to nobody.

The forgery was good. She knew it was good, and knowing it made her feel ill and powerful in equal measure, the way she imagined Vane felt every time he opened his ledger.

"Astrid." Odette stood in the doorway, sewing apron still tied, hands pressed flat together as if in prayer or arrest. "There are torches in the street. They are stopping at every house."

"They're starting at the corner," Astrid said. "Keppel Row first. That buys us the length of the terrace."

"And if they search?"

"Then we are three women alone in a house belonging to a lady who is in prison for breaking a window, and we are frightened, and we let them see that we are frightened. Fear is credible, Odette. Calm would be suspicious."

Odette looked at her for a long moment. In the last two weeks they had quarrelled about running and staying until the words wore grooves between them, but just now Odette's face held something else — the appraising look of a woman discovering that the girl who drove the motorcar had a spine made of some material she had not previously catalogued.

"You have done this before," Odette said. It was not quite a question.

"No," Astrid said. "But I've been done to. It leaves marks you can read."

The knocker went. Not the polite double-tap of a neighbour. A single blow, struck with a truncheon's handle, hard enough to rattle the fanlight.

* * *

There were six of them on the step, and one of them was Vane.

He had dressed for the occasion, which was to say he had dressed exactly as always: dark overcoat, bowler straight as a verdict, the black ledger tucked under his left arm like a vicar's prayer book. Behind him two uniformed men shifted their weight and blew on their hands, and two more moved off toward the area steps at the side of the house, and Astrid's heart performed a long cold dive — the coal hatch, the coal hatch, had Tomas latched it —

— and then steadied, because she heard, faintly, beneath the knockers' echo, the small scrape of the hatch being drawn shut from within.

"Miss Delacroix." Vane removed his hat. He had a way of removing his hat that managed to be an accusation. "Forgive the hour."

"You'll forgive me if I don't light the parlour," Astrid said. "Coal's rationed and Miss Halloway is in Holloway. The irony is noted daily."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not amusement. Recognition, perhaps — the look of a chess player whose opponent has played an opening he did not expect her to know.

"We're searching the terrace tonight," he said. "Certain irregular persons have been reported moving after curfew. Deserted servicemen. Persons of no fixed abode. Minors unaccounted for on the census rolls." He let the last word sit between them like a coin on a counter. "May we come in?"

"You may not," Astrid said pleasantly, "until you show me your warrant, and you may search nothing until you show me your schedule of comparison. That's the regulation, isn't it? When a census return is disputed, the inspector must present the original schedule for comparison before any entry may be questioned."

She saw it land. She had spent two evenings in the reference room at the library copying out the census regulations in longhand, and she had learned what she had always secretly known: that the law was a house with a hundred doors, and most of its guardians never troubled to check the locks because they believed no one else could read the plans.

Vane's eyes narrowed — not in anger. In recalculation. "You've been studying."

"I've been driving," Astrid said. "It leaves a great deal of time for reading."

Behind him, on the pavement, Wren's chalk stars were still visible where the child had drawn them along the front step that morning — a spill of white five-pointed marks that the rain had softened but not erased. One of the young constables glanced down at them and away again, embarrassed, as though he had surprised the house in its nightclothes.

Vane produced the warrant. And then, because he was not a stupid man, only a wounded and thorough one, he produced also the original census schedule — Pell's true copy, folded once, and he unfolded it and held it up to the light of the constable's lantern.

"Curious document," he said. "Filed by Mr. Pell on the third of April. Four names in this household, Miss Delacroix. Four." His finger came down on the line, and even upside down she could read it, because she had memorised it within minutes of lying to Pell's face: *and one child, name unknown, said to be a niece.* Pell had written it anyway. Pell had written it after all her smoothness, all her warmth, all her perfectly pitched laugh about how children wander in and out of houses like cats, and he had carried it back to this man, and this man had carried it here.

"The schedule in the house," Vane continued softly, "the household's own copy, which I understand is customarily retained — does not say four names. It says three." He lowered the papers. "Someone has amended the record."

"The household copy was completed later," Astrid said. "From memory. Memory errs."

"Does it." Vane folded both documents together with terrible gentleness. "May we come in now, Miss Delacroix? The warrant, I believe, is in order. And it is cold."

* * *

They came in the way ruin comes in: politely, in boots.

Two constables took the ground floor, opening cupboards with an apologetic efficiency that was somehow worse than violence. One went up the stairs, and Astrid stood in the hall and listened to him go through Miriam's bedroom — the drawers, the wardrobe, the tin on the mantel where Miriam kept her cough lozenges and, until last week, her suffrage funds — and each small violation sounded in Astrid's chest like a knuckle cracking. Odette sat with her hands folded and her eyes down, playing the frightened seamstress, and it required no acting whatsoever, except for the part where she did not look at the scullery door.

Vane did not search. Vane stood in the parlour among Miriam's books and looked at things, which was different, and worse. He looked at the photograph of Miriam's late husband. He looked at the framed certificate from the WSPU. He looked, for a long moment, at the slate board lying on the hearth-rug with its rows of chalk stars, and something moved across his face like weather seen far off over water.

"My daughter drew stars," he said, to no one, it seemed. "Ceiling. Walls. All four corners of her room, so none of them would be lonely."

Astrid said nothing. There was nothing that could be said that would not be either cruelty or surrender.

The constable came down from upstairs and shook his head. Another appeared in the parlour doorway, stamping cold from the area steps, and shook his head also, and Astrid allowed herself one breath — shallow, careful, the breath of a woman keeping her face level while her lungs celebrate — before Vane turned to her.

"You will forgive me," he said, "if head-shakes are not conclusive. Cellars," he added mildly, to the room, "are notorious."

He walked toward the scullery himself. Astrid moved without deciding to move — one stride, placing herself in the narrow doorway, not touching him, simply occupying the space the way a locked gate occupies space.

"The coal is banked for the night," she said. "If you open that hatch you'll fill my kitchen with coal dust, and the dust will get into everything, and Miss Halloway's solicitor will hear how her household goods were fouled during a search that produced nothing. You've been through the whole house, Inspector. Every drawer, every cupboard. Your men want their beds. It's past midnight, and whatever you write in that book tonight will be read back to you under oath someday — these cases go to appeal now, they're going to appeal all over London. Is *'I pulled the coal hatch open and found coal'* the sentence you want waiting for you in 1913?"

The lantern light moved between them. Behind her she heard Odette rise, heard cloth rustle — Odette positioning herself, ready for whatever came through that door.

And Vane looked at her. Really looked, the way he had during the interview, that terrible patient inventorying gaze, and Astrid understood that he knew. Not proof — knowledge and proof were different rooms in his architecture, and he never confused them. He knew the cellar held a man the way you know a letter is sealed without opening it: by the weight, by the corners, by the refusal of it to lie flat.

"You are loyal," he said at last. Quietly. Almost kindly. "It is the most expensive virtue there is, Miss Delacroix. I paid for mine with—" He stopped. His hand went, briefly, unconsciously, to the inside pocket where the photograph lived. "Good night."

At the front door he paused, hat in hand, and looked down at the chalk stars on the step. He crouched — a man of his age and dignity, crouching on a servant's-side doorstep at one in the morning — and studied them with his head tipped, and the lantern light caught the lines of his face and hollowed them.

"She pressed too hard," he said. "With her chalk. It never wiped off. We repainted twice." He rose, settled his hat, and did not look at Astrid again. "Keep your step swept, miss. Chalk washes out. Names are harder."

The door closed. The boots receded down Gower Street, unhurried, collecting the next house.

* * *

They waited until the dogs stopped, and then ten minutes more, because Astrid had learned that safety is measured in minutes after the fear ends, not before.

Then she went out into the freezing yard in her stockinged feet and drew back the coal hatch, and the smell of coal and canvas and cold iron rose up around her like the smell of the earth itself.

"Tomas," she whispered.

For a moment, nothing. Then the canvas shifted and his face emerged from the dark — grey with it, powdered with it, eyes wide and wet — and he climbed out slowly, joints unfolding, a man returning from a very small death. He stood swaying in the yard, looking up at the strip of yellow window-light and the smoke-stained sky, and Astrid saw that he was holding something, and that he had been holding it down there in the dark for over an hour.

Her writing case. The little tin one she kept in the motorcar, the one with her nibs and her ink pencil and the sheets of good rag paper she used for the motoring logs.

"You took this?" she breathed.

"Before they came." His voice was ruined soft. "I heard the boots. I thought — if they take me, they take me. But they mustn't find the tools that made the papers. So I hid it with me." He pressed it back into her hands and closed her fingers over the lid with both of his, and his hands were shaking. "You made the passage list. I saw the name you wrote. Fourth berth." He swallowed. "Miss Delacroix. Astrid. Whose name is it? Truly?"

Above them, faint through the kitchen glass, they could hear Odette getting the kettle on, because Odette's answer to every apocalypse was strong tea, and Wren's small footsteps crossing above, and the ordinary sounds of a household reassembling itself out of the dark.

Astrid looked down at the tin in her hands, at the tools of her quiet crimes, and felt the night tilt beneath her — because tomorrow she had an appointment at the shipping office before the manifest sealed at dawn, and the manifest allowed three berths, and she had written four names, and the fourth name was written in her own hand and she had not yet answered even to herself the question Tomas had just asked.

"Go inside," she said. "Drink your tea. Don't ask me anything before sunrise."

But he stood there a moment longer, this gentle ruined man, and asked anyway, in a whisper:

"It's yours, isn't it?"

The kettle began to sing in the kitchen, thin and rising, like something reaching for a note it wasn't sure it could hold.
