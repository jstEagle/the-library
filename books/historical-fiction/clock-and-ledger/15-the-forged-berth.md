## 15. The Forged Berth

The shipping office on Fenchurch Street kept its hours the way a miser keeps his coins — grudgingly, and never after dark. Astrid had learned this on her third visit, standing beneath the gas lamp across the street with her collar turned up and the rain needling the back of her neck, watching Mr. Aldous Pimm, passage clerk, lock his door at six o'clock precisely and walk away whistling something from a music hall she was too tired to name.

Tonight she did not wait for him to leave. Tonight she had a key.

It had cost her eleven shillings and a lie about a lost latchkey belonging to her late aunt, and it had cost her something else besides — the small, clean feeling of being a person who did not do such things. She turned the key in the lock at twenty past midnight and let herself into a room that smelled of ink, cold tea, and other people's departures.

She struck no light. She knew the room now the way she knew the Halloway house — by touch, by memory, by the particular geography of things that mattered. The manifest ledger lived in the second drawer of Pimm's desk, beneath a blotter stained with rings from forty years of cups. She had watched him open that drawer four times. Once, when he'd gone to fetch a form for a widow emigrating to join her son in Toronto, he had left it unlocked for nearly three minutes. Astrid had counted. Astrid counted everything; it was the orphanage in her, the girl who had learned that survival was a matter of noticing which matron kept the keys in her left pocket and which in her right.

The drawer slid open with a whisper of old wood. The ledger came up heavy as a sleeping animal.

She laid it on the desk, spread it wide, and found the page: *SS Laurentic — Liverpool to Quebec — sailing Friday, morning tide.* Four names already entered in Pimm's careful clerk's hand:

*Mrs. Odette Marchand — seamstress.*
*Miss Wren Delacroix — ward.*
*Mr. Thomas Ellery — labourer.*
*Miss A. Grey — companion.*

Three true names and one borrowed one. Tomas Ellery was gone now — had been gone two days, since he'd walked into the recruiting office on Westminster Bridge Road and given himself back to the army that wanted to shoot him, buying with his surrender whatever hours of safety he could for the rest of them. But his name remained on the list, because Astrid had not yet had the heart or the courage to strike it through. Striking it through would make it real. It would mean the berth was empty. It would mean someone else might take it.

Or — and this was the thought that had been growing in her all day like a dark flower — it would mean *she* could take it.

She stood over the ledger in the dark, listening to the rain against the window, and told herself the truth slowly, the way you tell a child something frightening, in pieces, with pauses between.

Wren could not go alone. Odette would not leave without her. That was settled; that had been settled since the night of the raids, since Astrid had stood in the doorway with a forged census page trembling in her hand while Inspector Vane's lantern swept the hall. Two berths spoken for. The third —

The third belonged to whoever could use it most. And Astrid had spent the whole of her life believing she was the person who could be spared. It was an old arithmetic, learned young: the orphan is ballast; the orphan keeps the boat steady but weighs nothing; the orphan may be jettisoned without grief. She had carried that arithmetic out of the Foundling Hospital at sixteen like a coin sewn into her hem, and she had spent it every day since — spending it on Miriam's letters, on Odette's mending, on Wren's chalk stars, on Tomas's silence — always assuming that at the end of the account there would be nothing left for her, because there had never been anything meant for her in the first place.

But Miriam's letter from Holloway had said otherwise. *Choose as a mother chooses, not as a servant.* Astrid had read those words aloud to the household three nights ago, her voice steady until the last line, and then not steady at all. A mother chooses. Not the way a driver chooses a route. Not the way a servant chooses obedience. A mother chooses whom to keep and whom — God forgive the arithmetic — whom to give up, if giving up is the only way any of them live.

And so tonight, in a stranger's office that smelled of ink and cold tea, Astrid Delacroix took up Pimm's pen, dipped it in his inkwell, and wrote a fourth name onto the manifest in a hand she had practiced against the back of an envelope for a week: her own.

*Miss Astrid Delacroix.*

Four berths. Four lives. One ship, sailing on the morning tide, and a household in Camden that would wake to find its spine gone.

She blotted the name carefully, closed the ledger, and felt nothing at all for the space of ten heartbeats — and then everything, all at once, the way floodwater comes through a door.

* * *

She was still sitting in Pimm's chair with her hands flat on the desk, breathing the way Miriam had taught her to breathe before a deputation — in for four, hold for four, out for four — when the door opened.

Not Pimm. She knew Pimm's tread, the shuffle of a man whose feet had given up on ambition decades ago. This was a lighter step, quicker, and it stopped dead the moment it saw the shape of her in the dark office.

"Don't," Astrid said quietly. "Don't call out. I'm nearly finished."

A match flared. A young man held it up — clerical collar, ink-stained fingers, a face still soft with the last of boyhood. He looked from her to the open drawer to the ledger and back again, and Astrid watched him perform the whole calculation in a single breath: woman, night, locked office, crime.

"You're the one who's been coming," he said. "Asking about Canada. Asking about the *Laurentic*. Mr. Pimm said—" He stopped himself, glancing at the window as though the street might be listening. "He said a woman had come three times asking odd questions. About berths. About whether the line ever sailed under-booked."

"And does it?"

"Never." He set the match down and lit the desk lamp properly, and in its small yellow circle Astrid saw his face more clearly: earnest, frightened, kind. "That's why I'm here. I couldn't sleep for thinking about it." He pulled a paper from inside his coat — a telegraph form, creased from handling. "This came this afternoon, after you'd gone. From the head office in Liverpool. Mr. Pimm read it and went home grey as ash."

She took it. The message was brief, and she read it twice, because the first time her eyes refused the sense of it.

*RE LAURENTIC 14TH. INSPECTOR VANE METROPOLITAN POLICE HAS REQUESTED ALL DEPARTURES FLAGGED FOR IRREGULAR PERSONS STOP MANIFESTS TO BE SEALED 48 HOURS PRIOR STOP NO AMENDMENTS AFTER SEALING STOP ACKNOWLEDGE.*

Sealed forty-eight hours prior. The ship sailed Friday on the morning tide. Which meant the manifest would close Thursday at dawn — tomorrow, in fact, within hours — and after that, no amendments. No additions. No fourth name scratched in by a woman with a stolen key and a practiced hand.

"Am I too late?" she asked. Her voice sounded very far away, as though it belonged to someone standing outside in the rain.

"No." The young clerk sat down across from her, uninvited, as though his legs had decided the matter without consulting him. "That's why I came. The sealing isn't done here — it's done at the dock, by the chief purser, before boarding. Whatever's written in that book when the purser takes it up, that's what sails. Nothing added after." He swallowed. "I checked the regulations twice. There's no provision for adding passengers at the gangway. None. If your name isn't on the sealed list, you don't board. Not with money, not with begging, not with anything."

Astrid looked down at the ledger, at the four names lying there in their neat rows like figures on a tombstone. Three berths. She had known it in her bones before the telegram confirmed it — had felt it in the weight of the pen in her hand. The *Laurentic* would carry exactly the souls on its list and not one more, and the list would be nailed shut at dawn.

"How many berths were booked?" she asked. "For my party. When I paid."

"Three." He didn't look away, and she gave him credit for that. "Third class, forward section. Three berths, one cabin. The fare covered three." He hesitated. "There's a fourth berth in the cabin. Physically. They pack them tight in third class — six to a cabin sometimes, bunks stacked like—"

"But the manifest says three."

"The manifest says three." He spread his hands. "I'm sorry. I've been trying all evening to find a way around it. There isn't one. Unless—" He stopped.

"Unless what?"

"Unless one of the three names comes off." He said it gently, the way you'd tell a family a diagnosis. "Then another can go on. Before sealing. After sealing, nothing moves at all."

* * *

She walked home through the wet streets with the telegram folded against her heart like a letter she couldn't stop rereading. London at three in the morning was a city holding its breath — lamplighters long gone, milk carts not yet stirring, the houses dark and full of sleepers who believed, most of them, that the world was a reasonable place run by reasonable men. Astrid moved through it like a ghost with an errand, and the errand rang in her skull with every step.

*One of the three names must come off.*

Odette. Who had fled a master who beat her and a trade that owned her, who had taught Wren to braid hair and Astrid to laugh, who held the household together now with rationed coal and stubborn tenderness. Odette, who would put her fist through a wall before she'd let that child cross an ocean alone.

Wren. Six years old, mute as a held breath, who spoke in chalk — stars on pavements, warnings on walls, a whole language drawn in white dust that only the people who loved her had learned to read. Wren, who had no country, no papers, no claim on anyone except the three women in a house in Camden who had chosen her without ceremony, the way you choose breath.

Herself. Who had no one waiting anywhere, who had crossed the Atlantic once already as cargo in an orphanage scheme and been returned like damaged goods, who had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of being unnecessary.

The old arithmetic rose in her like a tide. *Take me off. Put Wren's name where mine was. Let the child have the cabin, the berth, the future. I am the one who can be spared.*

But Miriam's words stood in the way, planted in her like a stake. *Choose as a mother chooses.* And a mother — Astrid was learning this late, painfully, the way you learn a language as an adult — a mother does not choose the child who needs least. A mother does not perform sacrifice for the beauty of it. A mother looks at the whole living board and asks not *who deserves to stay* but *what will keep them all alive*, and sometimes the answer is ugly, and sometimes the answer is the mother herself walking away down a dock with her hands empty, and sometimes — this was the thought that made her stop on the empty Embankment and grip the cold stone balustrade — sometimes the answer is none of the ones you've imagined, because dawn hasn't come yet, and dawn has a way of changing the question.

The river ran black below her, carrying the city's secrets seaward. Somewhere down that water, past Greenwich, past the Nore, lay Liverpool, and the *Laurentic*, and a gangway where a purser would nail a manifest shut at first light.

She walked the rest of the way home.

* * *

The house in Camden was quiet in the way of houses that have learned to sleep lightly. She let herself in through the kitchen, and found Odette awake at the table, wrapped in a shawl, mending by candlelight — a coat that had belonged to Tomas, the tear at the shoulder sewn shut with thread the exact colour of grief.

"You went out," Odette said. Not a question. In this house, questions had become luxuries.

"I had an errand."

"At three in the morning."

"Some errands keep banker's hours." Astrid sat down across from her. The candle guttered between them, throwing their shadows large on the wall — two women and, behind them, faintly, the shapes of everyone who had ever slept under this roof and was now scattered to prisons, to barracks, to the sea. "Odette. The manifest seals at dawn tomorrow. At the dock. Whatever's written when the purser takes it up, that's what sails."

Odette's needle stopped. "And how many names are written?"

"Four."

The silence stretched. Odette set down the coat, folded her hands over it, and looked at Astrid with the flat, clear gaze of a woman who had survived worse than arithmetic. "The fare bought three."

"The fare bought three."

"Then one of us doesn't sail." Odette's voice didn't shake. "Say it plainly. We've earned plain speech, you and I."

"It's not settled."

"It's settled enough that you walked across London in the rain to write your own name on that list." Odette reached across the table and took Astrid's hand — the ink-stained right hand, the forger's hand — and turned it palm up, as though reading it. "I've watched you for two years, Astrid Delacroix. You think you're invisible. You think nobody sees you counting, planning, standing between this house and the world. But I see you. And I know what you're about to do, because I'd do the same thing, and that's exactly why neither of us can be allowed to do it."

Above them, faint through the ceiling, came a sound: small feet on floorboards. Then silence. Then the scrape of something soft against plaster.

They climbed the stairs together and opened Wren's door. The child stood at the wall beside her bed, in her nightdress, drawing. Chalk in her fist, tongue between her teeth, utterly absorbed. On the plaster, in wobbling white lines, she had drawn a ship — hull, funnel, smoke — and beside it three small figures holding hands on a dock, and above the ship, stars, dozens of them, the same stars she chalked on the front step every night so that, Odette believed, she could find her way back from anywhere in the world.

Three figures. Not four.

Astrid stared at the drawing until her eyes burned. The child who understood everything, who heard everything through the floors and walls of this house, who spoke only in chalk — Wren had done the arithmetic too. And she had drawn three.

"Wren," Astrid said softly.

The child turned. Her eyes were enormous in the candlelight, and they went from Astrid's face to Odette's and back, and then she did something she had never done before. She took Astrid's hand, pressed the chalk into it, closed Astrid's fingers around it, and pointed at the wall — at the empty space beside the three figures, the space where a fourth should stand.

Then she shook her head. Slowly. Deliberately. *No.*

"Not yet," Astrid whispered, and her voice broke on the second word. "It's not dawn yet, little one. Nothing is decided until dawn."

Outside, far off toward the east, the sky was still black. But somewhere beyond it, in Liverpool, a purser slept with a sealing wax and a stamp, and a clock was running, and the tide — indifferent, punctual, eternal — would turn whether they chose or not.

Astrid stood in the doorway with a child's chalk in her fist and four names on a manifest and three berths on a ship, and she thought: *there has to be another way. There has to be.*

She was still thinking it when the knock came at the front door — three slow blows, unmistakable, the knock of a man who had all the time in the world and a warrant in his pocket.
