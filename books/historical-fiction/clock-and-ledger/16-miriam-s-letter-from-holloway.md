## 16. Miriam's Letter from Holloway

Three slow blows. Astrid's hand found Wren's shoulder and pressed her backward, into the dark of the hallway, and the child went without a sound — she had learned that much here, in this house where silence was a currency and everyone paid their share.

The chalk fell from Astrid's fist and rolled along the floorboards, a small dry rattle like a beetle dying.

She crossed the hall. Through the frosted pane set into the front door, the gas lamp on the pavement made a smear of amber, and against the smear a shape — narrow, hooded, too short for Vane, too slight for any constable. Her heart was still hammering its absurd, animal rhythm, but her mind had begun to work again, coldly, the way it had worked in the orphanage when matron's footsteps came down the dormitory corridor after lights-out: *assess first; be afraid second.*

She opened the door on the chain.

It was Mrs. Quill — the widow from number eleven who took in washing and asked no questions, whose hands were permanently red to the wrist. She stood hunched in the cold with a shawl over her head, and she held out an envelope as though it were something dead she had found in her laundry basket.

"From my sister," she said. "Her husband's a warder at Holloway. He isn't supposed to. He did anyway."

Astrid unhooked the chain. The envelope was warm from the woman's hand, or hers had gone so cold it seemed so. The paper was cheap, thin as onion skin, and the direction written in pencil in a hand she knew better than her own — the firm downstrokes, the little hook Miriam put on her *M*s like a signature flourish even in captivity.

"Tell your sister's husband," Astrid heard herself say, "that there is bread and there is gratitude, and he may have which he likes."

Mrs. Quill's face did something complicated — fear and pride fighting for the same ground — and then she was gone up the steps and into the fog, and Astrid closed the door and turned the key in it, and stood a moment with her forehead against the wood.

*Choose,* she thought. But she did not yet know what.

* * *

They gathered in the kitchen because it was the only room still honest about what they were. The grate held embers rationed down to a red wink. Odette sat with her sewing abandoned in her lap, the needle still threaded, a shirt half-built for a boy who would never wear it. Tomas leaned against the dresser with his arms folded, and though he had shaved and done what he could with his hair, the deserter showed through him now the way a watermark shows through paper — visible only when you held him up to certain light. Wren climbed onto the third chair without being told, folded her small hands on the table exactly as Miriam used to fold hers, and waited.

Astrid broke the seal with her thumbnail. She had read ten thousand letters by lamplight in this kitchen, silently, swiftly, folding them back along their original creases so that no one would ever know. This one she would not steal. This one she owed aloud.

"It came out through a warder's wife," she said. "It will have been searched. It will say nothing it mustn't." And then, because lying to this household had become harder than lying to inspectors: "And before anyone asks — yes. I know how letters are searched. Yes. I've known how to read them since before I knew how to hide that I could."

Odette looked at her a long moment, needle flashing once between her fingers, and said nothing, which was Odette's way of saying *later.*

Astrid unfolded the page.

*My dear ones all,*

*I am instructed by circumstance to write briefly, and by nature I have never followed instruction well, so we shall see which wins.*

The handwriting wandered at the edges — fatigue, or the flat prison tables, or the cold. Astrid could see the ruled absence of lines; Miriam writing uphill against an unruled page, her sentences climbing slightly leftward like people leaning into weather.

*Holloway is not so bad as the newspapers promise, which is to say it is worse in ways they do not think to print and gentler in ways they would be scandalized by. My cell has a window. From it I can see a chimney and, above the chimney, some portion of sky, which changes. I mention this because I have decided the sky counts as correspondence.*

*The food is an insult and the company is magnificent. There are women here who threw bricks and women who threw nothing but their hats into the street, and women who are here for debts and sorrows having nothing whatever to do with the Cause, and I find I love nearly all of them, which I believe is the whole secret nobody tells you: prisons make strange families faster than churches do.*

Astrid paused. In the ember-light Odette had gone very still.

*Now to business, which I have delayed because I am a coward about the things that matter most.*

*I know about the ship.*

The kitchen seemed to lean inward. Tomas unfolded his arms.

*I know because I know Astrid, and Astrid does nothing halfway, and a girl who reads every letter that crosses our threshold was never going to let a manifest defeat her. You should understand something, my dears, and I want each of you to hear it plainly: I have known for years that Astrid reads the post. I have watched her do it across this very table, pretending to sort bills, her eyes moving twice over passages that had nothing to do with coal accounts. I never said a word. I never stopped her. I will tell you why, and then I will tell you the rest.*

*A house like ours runs on two fuels, coal and trust, and we ration both. I judged — rightly, I think — that a woman who reads everything and betrays no one is worth more than a woman who reads nothing and promises discretion. Astrid, if you are reading this aloud as I suspect you are, and if your voice has not failed you yet, look up now. Look around the table. That is your answer to the question you have been carrying like a stone.*

Astrid looked up. Odette met her eyes, and after a long moment the seamstress nodded once, slow, the way she nodded over finished work — *this will hold.* Tomas's mouth moved through something that was not quite a smile and not quite grief. Wren simply watched her with those grey eyes that missed nothing, that had never missed anything, and Astrid understood suddenly that the child had probably always known too, in the way children and animals know the architecture of a household without needing it explained.

Her throat hurt. She read on.

*Here is the rest, and I will write it small because it matters and the page is short.*

*I did not go to the deputation expecting to escape arrest. I went to be arrested. Someone must stand in the dock and say, calmly, before the law of this country, that sheltering the homeless is not a crime, and I am — I was going to write *well-placed*, but that is vanity — I am *willing*. That is my testimony and I chose it. Do not waste a single hour grieving the choice. Grieve instead the system that made it necessary, and then get back to work.*

*But hear me on the matter of the berths.*

*I know there are three and I know there are more than three of you, and I know that somewhere in this house a girl who grew up crossing names off ledgers is right now trying to invent a fourth berth out of arithmetic and stubbornness. Stop. Listen.*

*There is no version of this in which I ask one of you to stay behind for me. If I thought for one moment that Astrid intends to give up her own place on that ship — and I know her, so I know she is considering it, dressing it up as duty or strategy or fairness — then I want her to hear this from me across the walls they have built between us:*

*You are not a servant, Astrid. You never were. I gave you wages because the world insists on the fiction, but I hired a driver and God sent me a daughter. So choose as a mother chooses, not as a servant chooses. A mother puts the child in the boat. A mother puts the frightened one in the boat. A mother stands on the shore and waves until she cannot see them anymore, and then she goes home and gets up the next morning and keeps the light burning so they can find their way back. That is the whole of motherhood. Nobody promised it was fair, and everybody who has ever done it will tell you it costs more than you have.*

*Choose as a mother chooses, not as a servant.*

*Tomas — if you are reading over her shoulder, which you are, I can see you doing it — you brave, foolish, gentle man. Whatever you decide about yourself, decide it quickly and decide it clean, and do not make that girl carry your decision for you. She has carried enough of us. Your brother would say the same. I believe he says it every day.*

*Odette — the grey wool in the upstairs cupboard is yours. Take the scissors. Take the thimble. In Canada they will need dresses and they will need you, and I am told the winters require everything sewn double.*

*Wren, my love. My quiet one. You draw stars because you know, better than any of us, that navigation is a kind of speech. Speak to them across the water. They will be listening. I will be listening.*

*The kettle is on the hob and someone has forgotten to fill it. This is what happens when I leave. See that the house is fed, the fire banked, the chalk kept sharp. Houses remember. So do I.*

*Yours entirely, and in haste, and in hope,*

*M. H.*

*P.S. — The matron here confiscates letters, so this one is contraband and you are all accessories now. Welcome, at last, to the family.*

* * *

For a while nobody spoke. The embers ticked. Somewhere down the street a cab horse stamped and blew, and the ordinary sounds of London at night came through the window glass like news from another country.

Tomas moved first. He crossed to the stove, filled the kettle from the jug — *the kettle is on the hob and someone has forgotten to fill it* — and set it on the heat, and the small domestic clatter of it broke something open in the room, gently, the way a key breaks open a door.

"She knew," Odette said at last. It was not an accusation. It was almost wonder. "All those years. She knew, and she let you."

"I stole from her," Astrid said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Every day for six years. I told myself it was survival. It was. But it was also—"

"It was also how you saved us," Odette said. "The shipping agent's loophole. The flagged manifest. Every piece of this plan came out of a letter you weren't supposed to read." She wound the loose thread from her sewing around one finger, unwound it, wound it again. "Miriam is right. We are all accessories now." A pause, and then, softer: "We always were. She just made it official."

Wren slid off her chair. She went to the dresser, took up the stub of chalk that lived in the dish beside the salt, and tugged at Astrid's sleeve. When Astrid looked down, the child pointed to the letter in her hand, then to the floor, then made a swift gesture — fingers spread, moving upward like something rising.

*Read it again,* the gesture said. *The part about mothers.*

Astrid read it again. *Choose as a mother chooses, not as a servant chooses.* She read it slowly, and Wren watched her mouth form the words with the fierce concentration of a child memorizing scripture, and when it was done the little girl nodded once — Miriam's nod, Odette's nod, the nod of this whole household where everything important was ratified in silence — and returned the chalk to the dish as if returning a verdict.

The kettle began to sing.

* * *

Later, when the tea was drunk and Odette had taken Wren up to the attic room and Tomas had gone to sit with his boots off in the parlour like a man rehearsing being free, Astrid stood alone at the kitchen window with the letter in her hands.

The fog outside had thinned. The houses opposite emerged from it one window at a time, dark and dark and dark, except one — a high window she had never noticed lit before, where a lamp burned behind a curtain and a shadow crossed it, unhurried, and crossed back, and settled. Watching, or merely awake. At this hour, on this street, there was no longer any meaningful difference.

She looked down at the letter. Miriam's climbing sentences, leaning into weather. *I hired a driver and God sent me a daughter.*

Astrid had spent her whole life believing history happened elsewhere — in Parliament, in newspaper columns, in other people's burning houses. Now it happened in her kitchen, in pencil, in a smuggled page that smelled faintly of carbolic soap and Holloway's institutional disinfectant. It happened at a rally where her name had been taken. It was happening tomorrow at dawn, on a manifest, in ink that would not forgive mistakes.

*A mother puts the child in the boat.*

She had been composing arguments against it all evening — that Wren needed her less than Odette did, that the forged name would pass easier with her standing beside it, that sacrifice was a luxury the household couldn't afford. Good arguments. Servants' arguments. She saw that now. Every one of them was a way of staying in control, of managing the loss instead of enduring it, of keeping her hands on the wheel of a car that had already left the road.

A mother didn't get to drive.

Astrid folded the letter along Miriam's creases — out of six years of habit, and then, deliberately, tenderly, against them, making new folds, her own — and put it inside her bodice, over her heart, where it lay like a coal that would not cool.

Then the clock in the hall struck three, soft and silver, and she counted the hours to dawn on her fingers: four. Four hours until the manifest sealed. Four hours until the tide. Somewhere out there in the black streets between this kitchen and the docks, Inspector Vane was awake or asleep, and his ledger lay open or closed, and the photograph of his lost daughter waited in his pocket like a question he hadn't finished asking.

Astrid took her coat from the peg by the door.

"Where are you going?" Tomas stood in the parlour doorway, already reaching for his own.

"To settle accounts," she said. "Before the market opens."

She stepped out into the fog, and the fog closed behind her, and under the lamplight at the corner of the square, drawn small and precise on the pavement where any passer-by might see it, someone had chalked three stars and a boat.
