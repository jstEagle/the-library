## 17. Tomas Decides

The recruiting office on Westminster Bridge Road opened its doors at nine, and Tomas Havel stood across the street from half past seven, watching men go in the way other men watch the sea — counting waves, wondering which one would carry him under.

The morning was raw. Fog came off the river in slow grey tongues and lay along the pavement like something that had died there overnight. A lamplighter passed him, snuffing his lamp with a long brass pole, and did not look at him. Nobody looked at him. That was the mercy of London: a man could be invisible if he wore a coat that belonged to no one in particular and kept his eyes down. Tomas had been practising invisibility for two years now, first in the ports of Hull and Grimsby, then in Miriam Halloway's back bedroom with its too-short bed and its window that looked out on chimney pots and a sky the colour of dishwater.

Two years since Ypres. Two years since he had stood in the trench with the rifles of his own section at his back and the orders in his captain's mouth, and said *no* — not loudly, not bravely, only in the small stubborn voice that lives below the voice a man is given by his country. They had wanted him to stand against a wall and shoot a boy from Liège who had cut wire for the other side, and he had refused, and the boy had been shot anyway, by other hands, and Tomas had run before they could decide what to do with a Czech farm boy who would not kill children. His brother Jan had not run. Jan had stood where Tomas stood and taken the consequences, or perhaps simply stayed because someone had to bring the body home, and there was no body, and there was no home, and there was no Jan.

He had told Astrid all of it three nights ago, in the kitchen, with the banked fire breathing its last orange light. She had listened the way she did everything — completely, without flinching, her hands still on the table as though stillness were a kind of attention. He had asked her if the house could hold him longer and she had said yes, yes of course, and he had believed her, and he loved her for it, and that was precisely why he could not let her keep the promise.

Because he had seen the arithmetic.

He was not clever the way Astrid was clever, with her letters and her ledgers and her memory like a warehouse with every crate catalogued. But he could count. Three berths on the morning ship to Canada, and four names that needed them, and one name on an inspector's ledger that made every berth smaller, every door narrower, every day shorter. Vane wanted him. Every knock on the street door these past weeks had landed on his chest like a fist. And every day he stayed was a day Wren woke screaming at the sound of boots, a day Odette counted the coal scuttle twice, a day Astrid's shoulders climbed a little higher toward her ears.

There was another way to subtract himself from the sum. It required no forgery, no stowage in a coal cellar, no forged page turned against Inspector Vane. It required only that he walk across the street when the office opened and give them his name.

They were taking men now. Everyone knew it, even the ones who pretended not to; the war had learned to be hungry in a new register, polite about it, with posters instead of press-gangs. A man with an accent and no papers might raise questions in a line regiment, but the labour companies took anyone who could lift a spade, and Tomas could lift a spade. He had been lifting spades since he was seven years old in the beet fields outside Plzeň. The army would ask fewer questions than Inspector Vane, and the questions it asked would have answers, and the answers would buy time — weeks, maybe months — while the household thinned itself into respectability. One deserter surrendered and re-enlisted was a closed file. One deserter hidden in a cellar was an open wound, and Vane knew how to press wounds.

And there was the other thing, the thing he had not said aloud even to himself until this fog-bound morning: the berth question. Astrid had forged a fourth name onto a list that allowed three. She would stand on that gangway tomorrow and choose, and whatever she chose would carve itself into her the way the orphanage ledger had carved itself, he was sure of it — he had seen her once, only once, look at Wren's sleeping face with an expression like a woman reading her own sentence. If Tomas removed himself by choice, then the choice remaining was no choice at all, or less of one. Odette. Wren. Astrid's own name, which she would never use, but which existed now, written in her careful hand, a door she had built for someone else and left standing open.

He would not let her walk through it. Better a spade in France than that.

* * *

At ten minutes to nine he went into the pie shop on the corner and bought two penny pies he did not want, because a man standing idle on a corner for ninety minutes draws eyes, and a man eating breakfast draws none. He ate them standing at the counter, tasting nothing, watching the office door through the steamed glass. A sergeant emerged at five past nine, unlocked the door, propped it open, and lit a pipe with the air of a man opening a church. Within minutes two lads went in, laughing, elbowing each other, and Tomas felt the old familiar shame move through him like weather — these boys going in freely to a thing he had fled, and he going in freely to the thing they could not imagine fleeing. The war made strange mirrors of everyone.

He thought of Jan. Not the way he usually thought of him — the ambush of memory, sudden and total — but deliberately, the way you take a photograph from a drawer and hold it to the light. Jan had stayed at the wall. Jan had believed, perhaps, that staying was the price of being a man among men, that refusal purchased nothing. And Jan had been wrong about the purchase and right about the price: refusal had cost Tomas everything, home and name and brother and country, and had bought exactly one thing — the knowledge, unshakeable, bone-deep, that he was not the sort of man who could do that particular thing. Everything else had been taken. That one thing remained.

So it was not cowardice that walked him across the street when the pies were done and the fog was thinning to a pale wash. Nor was it courage, though he suspected the recruiting sergeant would write the word down somewhere, in the way of sergeants. It was bookkeeping. Astrid would understand that. It was choosing which column your life went into.

The warmth inside the office smelled of pipe smoke, boot polish, and wet wool. A long counter, a map of the Western Front pinned to the wall with flags like a child's game, a bench of waiting men. The sergeant behind the counter looked up.

"Morning. Enlisting?"

"Yes." His accent sat on the word as it sat on all his words, and the sergeant's eyebrows performed a small negotiation above his eyes.

"Name?"

Here was the moment. Two years of borrowed names — Tom Halloran, Tomas Hansen, the cousin from the north, the quiet lodger. He had worn them like coats. And here, in this warm shabby room with its childish map, he found he was tired of coats.

"Tomas Havel," he said. "Spelled H-A-V-E-L."

"Nationality?"

"Czech. Bohemian, the papers say." He laid his hands flat on the counter, palms down, the way Astrid did when she was telling the truth and wanted it believed. "Sergeant, I will say the rest before you ask it. I was conscripted into the Austrian army in the last war year. I deserted it. I have been in England two years without papers. I am surrendering myself to you and asking to enlist here, in whatever company will take a man who can dig and does not talk much. There are people who can explain the particulars to whoever needs them explained. I would rather say it once to you than twenty times to policemen."

The sergeant looked at him for a long moment. Behind Tomas, the two laughing lads had gone quiet, listening. Somewhere a clerk's pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

"That's a rum one," the sergeant said at last, but not unkindly. He pulled a form toward himself and squared it. "You'll wait. There'll be questions. Might be a cell in it before there's a uniform, and I'd be lying if I said otherwise."

"I know," Tomas said. "I have been in worse places. This one has a roof."

Something moved at the corner of the sergeant's mouth that was almost a smile. "Medical's through the back. You'll be called."

Tomas took the bench. The wood was warm from the boys who had sat there before him. Through the front window he could see the street, the fog burning off, the pie shop, the road running north toward Camden and the tall narrow house with the wobbly railings where six people had arranged themselves into a family without asking anyone's permission. He looked at it as long as he could, memorising the angle of its chimneys against the brightening sky, and then a corporal called his name — his true name, spoken aloud in England for the first time in two years — and he went through to be measured and weighed and pronounced fit for labour, and to answer, over the course of a long grey afternoon, the questions he had invited, in the order he had predicted, and to watch each answer close a little door behind him until at last there was only the future, narrow and plain, standing open.

* * *

They released him on his own recognizance pending transfer — a phrase the interviewing officer used with visible distaste, as though the paperwork of a voluntary deserter-enlistee had been designed personally to offend him — and it was full dark when Tomas came up out of the underground station near the house. He had one stop to make first, and it was the hardest thing on a day of hard things.

Astrid was in the kitchen. Of course she was; she lived in that kitchen the way a wick lives in a candle. She looked up when he came in and he watched the whole day arrive in her face in the space of a breath — his coat, his hands, his eyes, whatever it was that men carry in their eyes after such a day.

"You've done something," she said.

"I have done arithmetic." He hung his coat on its nail, straightening it longer than a coat needed. "I went to the recruiting office on Westminster Bridge Road. I gave them my name. My true one. I enlist Monday — a labour company, digging, not shooting. I surrendered as a deserter from the Austrian service and volunteered here, and there is procedure now, papers, an officer named Whitcombe who will confirm my account. While the procedure crawls, no one hunts me. A man in a queue is a man accounted for." He made himself turn around and face her. "It buys you the weeks. It empties the ledger. When the ship sails, the manifest has one irregular person fewer."

She had gone very still. Astrid still was a frightening thing, like a frozen river — you knew there was water moving underneath, fast and black.

"You didn't ask," she said finally.

"No. Because you would have argued, and you would have been right in every particular except the one that matters, which is that I cannot be the reason any of you drowns. I have stood at enough walls." His voice caught on the last word and he let it. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to say it back to me so I know it will be done."

"Anything."

"Wren. When she asks — and she will ask, she understands more than we give her credit for, you know that better than anyone — tell her I was brave." His throat worked. "Not that I sacrificed, not that I went away. Children are told people sacrifice and it means nothing to them. Tell her I was brave. Tell her I chose it the way she chooses not to speak — because some things are too big for words and must be done with the body instead." He smiled, and found to his surprise that the smile was real. "Tell her the chalk stars worked. I saw them on the step every morning and I always knew I was home."

Astrid crossed the kitchen and took his face in her two capable hands, this woman who guarded herself like a strongbox, and kissed his forehead the way his mother had, and he understood that Miriam's household had done to him what orphanages and armies had failed to do: it had taught him what he was worth.

"I'll tell her," Astrid said against his hair. "I'll tell her you were brave."

He believed her. That was the strangest gift of the whole long day — that he believed her completely.

* * *

But later, past midnight, sitting up in the back bedroom among his few things packed into one canvas bag, Tomas heard the kitchen clock strike two and heard, beneath it, the scratch of a pen.

He rose soft-footed and looked down through the stairwell dark. A line of lamplight lay along the hall floor like a drawn blade, and in it he could see Astrid at the kitchen table, hunched low, writing — not letters, he knew her letter hand, this was something else, quick and hard, page after page. And beside her elbow lay the shipping agent's paper, the manifest, the list with its three berths and its fourth name in her careful hand.

She was writing something new. Something with dates. And as he watched, she struck a single line through the fourth name — his name, the one he had freed her from — and wrote another in its place, and pressed the page flat with both hands, and sat looking at it the way a woman looks at a fuse she has just decided to light.

Tomas stood in the cold dark at the top of the stairs and understood, with a lurch like the ground giving way, that removing himself from the sum had changed nothing at all — that Astrid Delacroix had simply carried the arithmetic one step further, and the name she had written in the berth was her own.

He put his hand on the banister to go down to her, and stopped, because below, very softly, Astrid began to speak aloud to the empty kitchen — rehearsing, in a steady clear voice, the speech she would make to the household in the morning, and the first words of it were: *"You have all trusted me with your secrets. Now I must trust you with mine."*
