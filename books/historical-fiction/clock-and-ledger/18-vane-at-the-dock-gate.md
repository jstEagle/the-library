## 18. Vane at the Dock Gate

The Thames at half past four in the morning was a country of its own, ruled by fog and gulls and the groaning of ships that had crossed more of the world than any man who walked these docks would ever see. Inspector Edmund Vane stood at the gate of the Royal Albert Dock with his collar turned up and his ledger in its oilcloth satchel, and he watched the *Maiden's Promise* breathe at her moorings like a great sleeping animal — three thousand tons of iron and intention, bound for Liverpool and thence the wide cold Atlantic, and beyond that, Canada.

He had been awake all night. That was not unusual; what was unusual was that he could not have said, if pressed, precisely when this case had stopped being a case. Somewhere between Pell's smug little notes and the coal cellar he had not searched as thoroughly as he might have, somewhere between the forged census page that a driver named Astrid Delacroix had held up to lamplight with steady hands while he stood dripping on the doorstep — somewhere in there, the ledger had begun to feel less like an instrument and more like a wound.

Fourteen years of service. Fourteen years of names.

That was the trouble with ledgers: they only went one direction. You wrote a name down and it stayed written. You did not get to unwrite the ones that haunted you.

*Clara Vane. Age nine. Removed from the register of living persons, March 1897.*

His wife thought he worked too much. His superiors thought he worked exactly enough. Neither of them knew that he worked because the alternative was a quiet house in Camberwell where a chalk drawing still clung to the garden wall eleven years after rain should have taken it — stars, dozens of them, drawn by a small hand in white and yellow, climbing the brickwork toward a door she had never come home through.

He checked his watch against the dock clock. Half past four. The manifest for the *Maiden's Promise* would seal at six; the tide waited for no inspector and no grief. He had received the shipping line's reply to his flag request yesterday evening, and it had been courteous and useless: *three berths booked under the name Halloway, paid in full, documentation in order, no irregularities observed.* The word *irregularities* had been underlined twice, in someone else's hand, which told him the line had been leaned on from above his head or beside it. Someone wanted these people gone. Perhaps the same someone who had wanted Miriam Halloway quieted in Holloway Prison. The irony of that name had not escaped him.

But flags and underlinings were not why he had come down to the docks before dawn with cold seeping through his boot soles.

He had come because of the fourth name.

Pell — diligent, humourless Pell, whom Vane privately thought of as a man shaped like his own handwriting — had copied the passage list from the shipping office clerk who owed him a favour. Three berths, three names. But the clerk, nervous as a wet cat, had let slip that a woman had come asking about berths for *four*, weeks ago, and been turned away, and had come back, and kept coming back, patient as tide itself, until somehow — he didn't know how, he swore he didn't know how — a fourth name had appeared on the working copy of the manifest. A name that matched no birth certificate, no parish record, nothing.

*Miss A. Delacroix.*

The driver.

Vane had spent two evenings trying to make the arithmetic work. Three berths, four souls — Odette Marchand the seamstress, the child called Wren, the deserter Tomas Halloway who had surrendered himself at a recruiting office ten days ago like a man settling a debt, and the driver herself. Three berths, four people. Unless one of them was not going.

Unless one of them had already decided, days ago, who would be left standing on the dock.

He found he could not stop thinking about it. He had interviewed hundreds of fugitives and harbourers in his career and he had a nose for self-preservation; it was the commonest scent in London, stronger than coal smoke. But this woman — this chauffeur with her ink-stained cuffs and her orphanage vowels worn smooth by imitation — this woman had arranged her own exclusion the way other people arranged flowers. And she had done it with his own paperwork. He still woke at night with the image of that forged census page held up between them, his official stamp reproduced so perfectly that for one vertiginous moment he had doubted which of them was the forger and which the instrument.

She had beaten him. She had beaten him using the rules, or near enough to the rules that no magistrate would ever see the seam. And instead of anger he felt something he refused for a long time to name, walking the fog-bound road along the dock wall, and finally named, because he was too tired not to: *recognition.* It was the feeling of meeting, in a police court, the one honest witness among a dozen liars. You knew them instantly. They looked at you the way no one else dared.

A gull screamed. The gate guard, blue-lipped, touched his cap and asked if the Inspector wanted the office kettle on, and Vane shook his head and took up his post where the foot passengers would pass, between the gate and the gangway, close enough to smell the tar and the river's old iron breath.

They would come soon. Ships sealing their manifests at dawn meant goodbyes at dawn, and goodbyes at dawn meant people arriving in the grey dark with too many parcels and not enough words. He had seen it before — emigration mornings, whole families poured out onto the dock like the contents of a drawer, weeping and laughing and arguing over trunks. He had always watched those scenes from behind glass, from ledgers, from the safe distance of other people's lives.

Today he intended to stand in front of it.

*One name,* he told himself. *You are here for one name.* The child, Wren. No record of birth, no record of anything, a silence in the shape of a girl — and a guardianship question that, properly pursued, could hold the whole departure at the dock gates until the lawyers and the Boards got their hands on her. He told himself this was procedure. He told himself the law required it. He told himself a great many things, and the fog took each one and blurred it, and gave it back to him looking less certain than before.

* * *

They came at twenty past five, materialising out of the fog the way people do at docks, as if the whiteness had simply decided to give them up.

He saw the driver first. He would always, afterwards, see the driver first. Astrid Delacroix wore her good coat and her hair pinned with severe competence, and she carried two valises and a canvas bag slung across her body, and she moved through the dock crowd the way a needle moves through cloth — finding the grain, following it, unhurried. Behind her came Odette Marchand, a hatpin flashing dull silver, and in her arms —

In her arms was the child.

Vane had never seen Wren except in the photograph Pell had taken covertly through the area railings, a smudge of pinafore at a window. Now he saw her clearly for the first time: small for seven, perhaps eight, with a face as serious as a Sunday and eyes that were already moving over the dock, the crane, the ship, him — resting on him — staying on him a moment longer than a child's eyes should.

Then she lifted her hand, just slightly, and drew one finger through the air. A small motion. Up, out, down. A star.

He felt it in his chest like a hand reaching through eleven years of brickwork.

Odette turned her shoulders to shield the girl, an instinctive, animal movement, and the little procession passed within twelve feet of him. Astrid Delacroix saw him. Of course she saw him; she had been expecting him since the shipping clerk's nervous telephone call reached whoever it reached. Their eyes met across the tar-smelling air, and she did not falter, did not speed up, did not look away. She gave him a single nod — the nod of one professional acknowledging another at a funeral — and walked on toward the gangway with her two charges and her canvas bag.

It was the bag that stopped him.

The flap had come loose in the jostle of the crowd, and from inside it protruded a piece of paper, curled and soft with handling, and on that paper, in coloured chalk ground into fibre as if the drawing had lived on a pavement and been loved enough to be rescued from the rain — stars. Dozens of stars. White and yellow, climbing the page from bottom corner to top, some five-pointed and careful, some mere bursts of light, ascending toward a door drawn at the top of the sheet with a small figure standing before it, waiting.

Vane stood very still in the moving crowd, and the dock noise receded from him like water down a drain.

Clara had drawn stars like that. Not similar — the same. The same compulsion upward, the same crowding of sky, the same sense that a child had looked at the night and thought *there is not room on the paper for all of it, so I will draw the wanting instead.* He had a folder in his desk at Scotland Yard, bottom drawer, beneath the warrants, containing four sheets of paper he had rescued from the bin when the house was cleared. Chalk drawings. Stars, climbing a page toward a door. Eleven years old now, those papers, soft as cloth at the folds.

The crowd flowed around him. Somewhere ahead, the gangway clanked. A steward's bell rang the first call. And Inspector Vane, who had crossed London in the dark to stand at this gate and invoke the full weight of the census laws upon an undocumented child, discovered that his feet would not move.

*Here is the moment,* he thought, with terrible clarity. *Here is where you are made.*

Because he understood, suddenly, standing in the fog with the ledger heavy against his ribs, what he had actually been doing for fourteen years. Not enforcing order. Not even, though he had dressed it in that language, finding answers. He had been conducting a search — a search for the person who had failed Clara, who had let a nine-year-old walk out of the world through a gap in the attention of everyone who loved her — and he had known, in the place where he kept things he did not read aloud, that the search would end at his own door. That the man responsible was the one who had responded by making lists. By writing down every irregular person in London, every soul outside the walls, every name that fell through the ledger's lines — because if he catalogued them all, guarded them all with suspicion, then no other Clara could slip through. It had felt like penance. It had felt like protection.

It had felt like love. God help him, it had felt like love, and it had been neither penance nor protection but only fear wearing an official coat, and this morning it stood at a dock gate with its hand on a warrant it could not make itself serve.

The child had drawn him a star from across the crowd. She had looked at a strange man in a policeman's coat — a man whose face she had every reason to know, whose visits had brought soldiers of paperwork to her door — and she had drawn him a star, the way children do when they have decided the world contains more kinds of people than the dangerous kind.

Or perhaps — and this thought arrived like cold water — she had drawn it the way her mother used to, at the top of letters. *Watch for the stars, Papa. Count them till I'm home.* A signal. A blessing. Or a warning: *I see you.*

The bell rang again. Down the dock, he could see Astrid Delacroix at the foot of the gangway, handing the valises to a porter, speaking low and fast to Odette Marchand — and he saw the shape of it now, unmistakable, the geometry he had deduced from the manifest playing itself out in flesh. The seamstress gathering the child close. The driver stepping back. One step. Two. Her hands emptying themselves deliberately, folding shut, becoming the hands of a woman who has finished carrying.

Three berths. Four names. One left on the shore.

And the choice — he saw it land on Astrid Delacroix's face even across fifty feet of foggy morning, saw it arrive and settle and be answered, all in the space of a breath. She put her arms around the child, said something into her ear that made the small solemn face crumple and then set itself hard, and then she lifted Wren clean off the ground and placed her in Odette Marchand's arms as carefully as if she were setting a lamp in a window.

Vane opened the satchel. His fingers found the ledger. The warrant was folded inside the cover, correct in every particular, signed by a magistrate who trusted him. All he had to do was raise his voice — one word, *Stop* — and the machinery he had served for fourteen years would grind forward, and the child would be taken into care, and the departure would collapse into hearings, and the irregular persons of number fourteen Larkspur Row would become regular at last, documented, filed, safe, destroyed.

His mouth opened.

What came out — what he discovered he had chosen, in the held breath between the bell and the word, without ever feeling the choosing — was quieter than the fog.

"Inspector?" The gate guard was at his elbow. "Shall I fetch the boarding officer, sir? Is there a problem at the gangway?"

Vane looked down at the ledger open in his hands. His own handwriting marched up the page, neat and merciless: *Halloway. Marchand. Minor female, no registration.* Names he had pursued across a winter of the city. And below them, unwritten yet, the space where a fourth entry belonged, the space he had come to fill.

Down the dock, the steward at the gangway head was checking papers, ticking names, and Astrid Delacroix stood alone on the cobbles with her empty hands and her canvas bag, watching her family climb toward the ship, and she had not yet seen him again — or she had seen him and understood, somehow, that for this one moment the man at the gate was not the danger, and the true danger was coming from another direction entirely, because Vane, turning his head along the line of her fixed gaze, saw what she saw:

A constable at the far gate. Then two. And behind them, walking with the unhurried confidence of a man who has bought his outcome in advance, a broad-shouldered figure in a good overcoat that Vane did not recognise — not police, not Home Office, not anyone from his division — with a leather folio under one arm and the look of someone who has come to collect a debt.

Someone else wanted the manifest sealed with questions unanswered. Someone above the shipping line, above the underlining, someone who had leaned on clerks and prisons and now, at six o'clock on a spring morning, had sent men to the dock.

The bell rang for the last call. The child, high on the gangway in Odette's arms, turned and looked back — at the ship, at the shore, at the driver — and raised her chalk-drawn star once more into the fog.

Edmund Vane closed the ledger, left it shut, and started to run.
