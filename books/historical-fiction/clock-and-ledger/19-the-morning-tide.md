## 19. The Morning Tide

The Thames at four in the morning was a grey animal breathing in its sleep. Astrid stood at the dock gate with her hand on the cold iron of it and listened to the river slap against the pilings — that wet, patient sound, like something counting. The *Montcalm* rode low at her berth, black-hulled, her funnels exhaling a thin coal smoke that the fog would not let rise. Gulls argued overhead in the dark that was not yet morning but no longer night.

In the cab behind her — Miriam's Daimler, sold yesterday for less than it was worth, driven here for the last time by Astrid's own hands — Odette held Wren on her lap and did not speak. Tomas was gone since Tuesday, since the recruiting office on the Westminster Bridge Road, since his hand had pressed Astrid's once, hard, and let go. Since he had said: *Tell Wren I was brave.* She had not yet found the words for it. Perhaps there were none in English. Perhaps none anywhere.

The tickets were in her coat pocket, sewn there now because pockets could be picked and papers could fall and she had learned at eight years old that everything you carried you carried as though someone wanted to take it. Three berths. Steerage to Canada — Quebec City by way of the St. Lawrence, eleven days if the weather was kind. The manifest sealed at dawn. The steward at the gangway with his list and his pencil, already checking names.

And in Astrid's bag, folded twice, the forged page.

She had written it at the kitchen table at two o'clock, by candlelight, with Miriam's good pen that leaked. *A. Delacroix.* Her own name, in her own hand, in the third berth beside Odette's and Wren's — a fourth name where only three were permitted, because the line had flagged the sailing and the purser's office had cut the party back without warning, and dawn was coming whether the arithmetic agreed or not. She had blotted it carefully. She had looked at it for a long time. It had seemed, in the candle's small circle, almost like a decision rather than a wish.

Now, at the gate, she took it out and read it again under the yellow spill of the dock lamp, and the ink looked different in the open air. Colder. More like what it was.

"Astrid." Odette's voice through the cab window, very quiet. "The man is looking."

The steward had lifted his head. A thin man with a green eyeshade and the particular boredom of someone who has watched ten thousand people leave England and expects nothing from any of them. Beyond him, at the far end of the pier, another figure: a tall shape in a dark overcoat, hatless, standing where the lamplight thinned out. Inspector Vane. He had been there when they arrived. He had made no move to stop them. That was somehow worse than if he had.

"Give me Wren," Astrid said. "Then give me your hands, and don't let go of either of us."

* * *

They crossed the pier together, the three of them, Astrid between the other two with Wren's small fist closed around two of her fingers and Odette's grip light and trembling on her sleeve. The boards were slick with river damp. Somewhere aft, a winch woke and groaned, hauling the last of the mail sacks aboard, and the whole ship shivered as though waking from the same long sleep as the river.

Wren stopped walking.

It was so sudden that Odette nearly stumbled. The child planted her feet on the wet planking, turned, and looked up at Astrid with those dark unblinking eyes that had never needed words to say anything — eyes that had said *man watching the house* in chalk stars and crooked figures on the pavement outside number fourteen for weeks before any of the adults understood. Now they said something else. They said: *you are not getting on.*

Children always knew. That was the cruelty of children; they could not be lied to with faces.

"In the ship," Astrid said, crouching, making her voice into the warm nonsense-voice she used for the child, the one about ships and gulls and the great white mountains of ice that floated like swans. "You and Odette, all the way across the water, and then a farm with cows—"

Wren pulled her hand free. Deliberately. Then she reached into Astrid's coat pocket — the left one, the one without the tickets — and took out the stub of chalk she kept there, because Astrid had learned to carry chalk the way other people carried smelling salts. And on the wet board of the pier, in quick sure strokes while the gulls screamed and the winch groaned, the child drew three stars, and beneath them a stick figure with its hand raised, and then — pressing hard enough to snap the chalk — a second figure, apart from the others, standing still.

Then she pointed at Astrid. And then at the figure standing still.

"No," Astrid said, and heard how it cracked in the middle. "No, that's not — Wren, look at me. Look at me."

But the child only looked, with the terrible patience of the ones who cannot lie and therefore cannot be lied away, and Astrid understood that Wren had known since the kitchen table, since the candle, since the moment the pen scratched the third name — perhaps since the dock gate, reading Astrid's spine the way she read streets — exactly what the forged page was for. Exactly whom it was meant to save. And exactly what it would cost.

Odette had her hand at her mouth. "We can draw lots," she said rapidly, in French first and then in her careful English, as though speed would make it fair. "Truly, Astrid, we can — or you take her and I stay, I am nobody, I have no one, my papers are false anyway, they'd send me back within the month—"

"And I'd spend the rest of my life knowing it," Astrid said. She stood. Her knees ached; she was twenty-six and her knees ached. "Odette. Listen to me. Vane offered me a trade two nights ago — Tomas for the paperwork, remember? And I refused him, and he wrote something down in that ledger, and this morning he is standing at the end of this pier letting us walk, which means either he has decided to lose or he is losing on purpose, and I do not know which, and I cannot know, and that is precisely why it must be me. If anything unravels — the forged name, the census page, any thread of it — it should unravel onto me. My name is true. My papers are true. There is nothing they can send me back *from*."

"That is not brave," Odette said, and her voice broke on it. "That is only arithmetic."

"Yes," Astrid said. "I know. I've always been good at ledgers."

She knelt again and gathered Wren in, both arms, the child's cheek against her collarbone, the smell of coal smoke and soap and sleep. She held her the way she had once been held by nobody, for no length of time at all, and she spoke into the small ear with the wind pulling her words out over the water.

"You went to the orphanage ledger once," she said. "I showed you. My name was in it, and then it was crossed out, and I told you that meant no one wanted me. That was a lie, Wren. Listen. Being crossed out is not the same as being erased. You will cross me out of your house and I will still be in your hand. Every star you ever draw. Do you understand me? Every single one."

Wren's fingers curled into her coat. Held. Released. And then the child did something she had done only twice before in all the months: she put both palms flat on either side of Astrid's face, the way you hold something you are memorizing, and she pressed her forehead to Astrid's forehead, and stayed.

The ship's bell rang out, brazen and enormous, once, twice.

"The manifest," the steward called down the gangway, bored as God. "Manifest closes in five minutes. All passengers aboard."

* * *

The gangway was narrow and steep and it moved, very slightly, with the swell — a thing alive, breathing with the ship. Astrid went first, carrying Wren, because if Odette went first Astrid might follow and if she followed she might keep following all the way to Quebec, and some choices you had to make with your back already turned.

At the head of the gangway the steward stood with his list.

"Party of three? Delacroix?"

"Delacroix," Astrid said. "Two traveling, one — " and here her voice did the thing she had rehearsed in the cab, in front of the cracked mirror, saying it to her own reflection until it stopped sounding like a wound — "one seeing us off. The berth under A. Delacroix goes to the child. Wren Delacroix." A breath. "Family name. We thought it best, for the crossing."

The steward's pencil hovered. Down the pier, at the edge of the light, Inspector Vane had taken one step forward. One. His hands were at his sides; in one of them, she could see even at this distance, was the ledger. His thumb moved along its edge the way a man touches a scar.

The pencil came down. Struck through *Astrid*. Wrote *Wren*. The scratch of it was the loudest sound in the world.

"Steward," Astrid said, before she could stop herself, "there will be a letter forwarded to this office, from a Mrs. Halloway, regarding the child's — arrangements in Canada. See it reaches the matron at the Quebec farm school. Will you do that?"

"There's a war coming," the steward said, which was what everyone said now, about everything. But he nodded.

She handed Wren to Odette at the top of the gangway, and Odette took her and held her and could not speak, and Astrid stepped backward down the slope of the plank with her eyes fixed on them both — Odette's white face, Wren's dark eyes over Odette's shoulder — one step, another, the ship breathing under her feet and then not under her feet, the solid pier rising to meet her heels, and the gap opening between her life and her family by eighteen inches, then three feet, then a fathom of black water.

Wren twisted around. Raised her arm. Not waving — drawing. Her finger traced in the air, slow and certain, three stars.

Astrid raised her hand and drew them back. Three stars, in the grey air, over the Thames, where no one would ever see them but the two people who needed to.

Then the ropes came off, the funnels bellowed, and the *Montcalm* leaned out from the wall like a woman turning away in a doorway, and the water between them widened, and widened, and did not stop widening.

* * *

She stayed until the hull was a shadow and the shadow was a suggestion and the suggestion dissolved into the fog off Greenwich, and the gulls gave up, and the winches slept, and the dockmen began their clattering morning around her as though the world had not just divided itself in half. Her legs held. That surprised her. She had expected, at the moment of casting-off, some collapse — the dramatics the orphanage matrons had always punished. Instead there was only a strange steadiness, an awful clarity, the feeling of having set down a weight so heavy she had forgotten she was carrying it, and discovering that the weight had been holding her upright.

History happened elsewhere. She had believed that once, at dawn, polishing a motorcar, memorizing other people's letters. History happened in Parliament, in newspapers, to women named in the papers. It did not happen to a driver with cold hands on a wet pier.

But she had driven history to Caxton Hall in a Daimler. She had hidden it in a biscuit tin. She had lied to it on census night and forged it in Miriam's leaking pen and stalled it at her own front door with its own paperwork, and this morning she had put it on a boat to Canada with chalk dust on its sleeve, and now it was steaming toward the St. Lawrence carrying her whole heart in steerage.

So it had never happened elsewhere at all. She simply hadn't been looking.

She turned toward the end of the pier.

Vane had not moved. The lamp behind him had died with the dawn — the lamplighter snuffing it, the day taking over — and so he stood in the flat early grey like something unfinished. He was watching the water where the ship had been. When Astrid came down the pier toward him her footsteps sounded enormous and he turned, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the river counted its patience between them.

"She drew them for you too," Astrid said. "Didn't she. Once."

He looked down at her. Up close, in the honest light, he looked older than the ledger made him look, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. His hand went to his breast pocket — the photograph, the girl with the stars — and stopped halfway, and came away.

"The manifest is sealed," he said. His voice was rough gravel. "Names verified. No irregular persons aboard the *Montcalm*."

"I see."

"Do you." He opened the ledger, then — slowly, deliberately, the way a man opens a door he has been standing outside of for years — and looked at the page, and looked at the water, and closed it again without writing anything at all. "My daughter," he said, and stopped, and started again. "There was a crossing. Years ago. I chose the shore." He looked at her then with something that was not kindness and not mercy and not defeat but some bare, unaccommodated thing underneath all three. "I have regretted it every day since in a language I did not know I spoke."

Astrid said nothing. There was nothing to say that the morning had not already said.

Vane tucked the ledger under his arm, turned, and walked away down the emptying pier, his footsteps keeping time with the water, growing smaller, until the fog took him the way it had taken the ship.

Astrid stood alone on the dock with her empty bag and her ticket-less pocket and the tide going out, and reached inside her coat for the chalk, meaning to mark something — a star, a line, anything — on the grey boards of England.

Her fingers found paper instead. Folded paper, slipped into the pocket beside the chalk that had not been there before, in a hand she did not know, addressed in block capitals:

*MRS. A. DELACROIX — TO BE READ AT ONCE.*

She broke the seal with cold fingers, and the first line made the pier tilt beneath her feet.
