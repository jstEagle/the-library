## 20. What the Inspector Wrote

The ledger had a smell to it, and after eleven years Reginald Vane could no longer separate that smell from himself: leather gone sour with damp, ink gone brown where the iron-gall had oxidised, the faint ghost of coal smoke that lived in everything in this city, in his coat, in his hair, in the pages he turned each morning at the desk by the window of his rooms on Gower Street. He had begun the ledger in 1900, in the first flush of his appointment, when the world had seemed a place of categories and every category a kind of protection. *Irregular persons*, the cover said, stamped in gilt that had flaked away until only the ghosts of letters remained. Irregular persons. Persons whose papers did not agree with their faces. Persons who existed in the seams.

He opened it now to the page marked **H — Halloway, M., Camden**, and read down through his own handwriting as though it belonged to a stranger. Eleven entries. The first dated April of the previous year: a boarding-house licence irregularity, dismissed. Then Pell's report from census night, copied out in Vane's careful hand because Pell's own hand was a spider's argument and Vane did not trust arguments he could not read. *Female, French, occupation seamstress, no papers produced.* *Male, claims kinship, no regimental record found under given name.* *Child, female, approx. six years, no birth certificate, no school registration, no name but one given informally.*

No name but one given informally. Wren. A bird named for hiding in walls.

Vane sat with the ledger open for a long time while the gas hissed in its bracket and the lamplight made a small yellow country on the desk and left the rest of the room in dusk. Across the street a woman was beating a rug against a railing, three slow blows, a pause, three more, and the sound came through the glass muffled and patient, like a heart heard through a stethoscope. He thought — not for the first time — that his whole profession was built on the belief that a person could be known by what was written about them, and that this belief had never once, in eleven years, been proven true. The ledger knew everything about the house on Delancey Street and understood nothing about it.

He turned back two pages, to where he had pasted the photograph.

It was a small thing, cabinet-size once, trimmed down to fit between the ruled lines because he had not wanted it loose, had not wanted it falling out of the book in some police station or records office where any clerk might pick it up off a floor and file it under lost property and forget it. A girl of seven, standing on a pebble beach at Whitstable with her hem lifted in one fist, squinting into a sun that is not visible in the picture but is visible anyway, always, in the way she holds her face. Alice. She had drawn stars on everything that year — on the fogged window of the train, on the slate above the fireplace, on the back of his hand once in pencil while he slept in his chair, five-pointed, slightly lopsided, drawn the way children draw stars when they love them: not accurately, but devotedly.

She had been dead nine years this June. Scarlet fever, four days, the fourth day the worst, and he had been in Manchester on the Fenwick case because the Fenwick case mattered, because irregular persons mattered, because the ledger mattered — and the ledger had never once held her name. There was no page for Alice Vane. There was no category for her. That was the joke, if he had let himself laugh at it, which he did not: the man who spent his life cataloguing the living had no place in his book for the only person whose loss had made all the rest of it merely paper.

The rug-beating across the street had stopped. In the silence the clock on the mantel ticked like something being counted.

* * *

He went back to Delancey Street twice more before he closed the matter, and both times he told himself it was procedure.

The first time was a Tuesday, grey and smelling of rain that would not commit, and he came at ten in the morning expecting an empty house — servants out, mistress in Holloway awaiting appeal, the place sealed and quiet as a held breath. Instead the door was answered by the child.

She stood in the gap with the chain still on, so that only half of her was visible in the dark hallway, and she looked up at him the way children look at large animals: without fear exactly, but with an assessment, a weighing. He saw her eyes go to the warrant card in his hand, then past it, to his face, and stay there.

'I'm not coming in,' he said, which he had not planned to say. 'I wanted to ask—' And here his prepared questions, his eleven years of prepared questions, arranged themselves in his mouth and then walked out of it one by one and left him empty. 'I wanted to ask whether anyone has been troubling the house.'

The child studied him. Behind her, deeper in the dark, he could see the shape of the Frenchwoman, Odette, motionless at the foot of the stairs, a rolling pin in her right hand held low against her skirt, and he pretended not to see it and she pretended not to be holding it, and this mutual pretence struck him as the most honest transaction he had witnessed in months.

Then the child did something extraordinary. She reached up — still behind the chain, still only half-visible — and drew in the air with one finger, slowly and deliberately, the shape of a star. Five points. Slightly lopsided. Drawn the way children draw stars when they love them.

Vane stood on the step with his hat in his hand and felt the ground shift somewhere beneath the foundations of himself.

'That's very well done,' he managed.

The door closed. Not slammed — closed, with a care that somehow undid him more than slamming would have, as though even the shutting of doors in that house was done deliberately, by people who chose their actions. He walked back down Delancey Street with his collar feeling two sizes too small, and at the corner he stopped and looked back, and there in the dust of the front step, faintly, he could see chalk marks rubbed nearly away by feet and weather — stars, dozens of them, a whole sky trodden under the boots of postmen and milk-boys and inspectors of irregular persons who had never once looked down.

* * *

The second visit he made in plain clothes, on the Thursday, to the shipping office on Leadenhall Street, and this was not procedure and he knew it.

He had asked the line, weeks ago, to flag departures to Canada — any berths booked in haste, any names paid in mixed coin, any passenger lists with more hope than paperwork on them. It had been a routine request; he had made forty such requests over the years. But he had gone in person this time, unannounced, and the junior clerk had fetched the manifest book with the eagerness of a man glad to surrender responsibility for it, and Vane had stood at the counter turning pages with his finger while rain ran down the office window in silver threads, and there they were: three berths, the *Melusine*, sailing Saturday on the morning tide, bound for Halifax. Paid in full, in notes, by a woman who had given her name as *A. Merrick* and whose signature he recognised instantly, because he had taken down her statement after the Caxton Hall rally and she wrote her capital A the way some people slam doors — hard, final, with a flourish that dared you to doubt it.

Delacroix. Astrid Delacroix, driver of motors, reader of other people's mail — yes, he knew about that, Pell had told him about the letters, and Vane had filed it away not as evidence but as character, which was a different kind of filing altogether.

And below the third name, in the same hand, a fourth entry, freshly inked, the ink still carrying a faint shine where the clerk's thumb had smudged it: *E. Vane.*

His own name. Or near enough. He stared at it for a long moment while the rain stitched its silver threads and the clerk waited, respectful, bored.

It was a forgery, obviously. Clumsy in its audacity and perfect in its effect: a berth purchased under the name of the very man hunting them, so that if he flagged it, he would be flagging himself; if he questioned it, he would have to explain why his own signature appeared on a Canadian manifest; if he struck it, he would strike a name that was, on paper, indistinguishable from his own. She had used his own machinery against him. She had looked at the ledger — at everything the ledger meant — and answered it with a joke written in his name.

He should have been furious. He sat down instead, on the stool the clerk offered, and asked for a glass of water, and drank it slowly, and thought about the fact that in nine years no one had written Alice's name anywhere. No manifest. No ledger. No list of passengers bound anywhere. The world had simply closed over her like water, and he, who believed in records, had been left with nothing but a photograph pasted between ruled lines.

'Is anything wrong, sir?' the clerk asked.

'No,' Vane said. 'Everything here appears to be in order.' And he closed the manifest book himself, gently, as though it were a door.

* * *

Saturday came up cold and clean, the Thames running pewter under a sky scoured thin, and Vane stood at the dock gate at half past six in the morning because he had told himself he would come and because, having told himself, he discovered he had no excuse left.

He did not wear the warrant card. He wore his good grey coat and carried the ledger under his arm the way he had carried it for eleven years, close to the ribs, and he watched the third-class passengers funnel down toward the gangway — trunks and birdcages and a man carrying a sewing machine like a child, families moving in that tight silent formation of people who have decided something together and are afraid the decision will scatter if they speak.

He saw them from fifty yards. The Frenchwoman first, tall, a carpet bag in one hand and the child's hand in the other, her chin set at the angle he had learned to read as *I will bite you if required*. The child wore a coat too big for her and carried, clutched to her chest with both arms, a piece of cardboard on which someone had drawn — he could see it even at that distance, because he had learned to look for it now — a constellation of lopsided stars.

And beside them, just at the foot of the gangway, in a plain coat and no gloves, stood Astrid Delacroix. Not boarding. He understood that before he understood anything else, the way one understands weather: she was not boarding. Her hands were empty. She had put the child in the other woman's arms — he watched her do it, watched her lift Wren up and hand her over with a steadiness that cost her something enormous, he could see the cost in her shoulders, in the set of her jaw — and then she had stepped back onto the cobbles, and the gulf of six feet between her and the gangway might as well have been the Atlantic itself.

She saw him then. Of course she did; she had been watching for him since the gate, he realised, watching every uniformed back, every official cap, and when their eyes met across the crowd she did not flinch and she did not run. She simply stood, with the ship's smoke beginning to smear the clean sky behind her, and waited to learn what kind of man she had been forging names against.

Vane walked toward her. Under his arm, the ledger pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

At twenty feet she spoke first. 'Inspector.' Level. Respectful, in the way of a duellist.

'Miss Delacroix.' He stopped. Around them the crowd flowed and parted, porters and farewells and a band somewhere playing something jaunty and horrible. 'You have a very fine hand,' he said. 'Your forgery. The E is particularly convincing.'

Something moved in her face — fear, and under the fear, astonishingly, humour. 'I had a good teacher. Eleven years of reading other people's signatures.'

'Nine years ago,' Vane said, and heard his own voice go somewhere quiet and strange, 'my daughter died of scarlet fever while I was in Manchester on a case. I have spent the time since keeping a book of persons whose papers do not agree with their faces. I have never once asked myself what my papers say about mine.' He shifted the ledger. 'Yesterday I reviewed the file on the Halloway house. Census returns, boarders' statements, the Pell report. Do you know what I found, when I read it properly, at the end?'

She said nothing. The ship's whistle blew, long and low, a sound that went through the chest rather than the ears.

'I found a household,' he said. 'That is all the file contains, if one reads it honestly. A seamstress working sixty hours a week at honest work. A young man who could not make his hands do what his conscience forbade — and who, I am told, presented himself at a recruiting office on Wednesday, of his own free will, God help him. And a child who draws stars on pavements so that people can find their way home.' His voice caught, briefly, on the last word, and he let it. 'There are no irregular persons, Miss Delacroix. There are only persons, and the regular cruelty of the world, and whatever shelter people build against it with their bare hands. I have been inspecting shelters for eleven years and calling them offences.'

Astrid Delacroix looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, quietly: 'Her name is Wren. She chose it herself. She'll want you to know that, someday, if she ever knows.'

'I know it,' Vane said. 'It's in my book.'

On the gangway, the stewards were calling the final boarding; the Frenchwoman had turned at the top, the child high on her hip, and Astrid raised one hand to them, steady as a lamp, and Vane stepped back — out of the sightline, out of the moment, out of the photograph of it that he knew she would keep all her life — because some mercies are only mercies if they are unwitnessed.

The mooring lines went into the water. The *Melusine* leaned away from the dock like a slow animal deciding, and the gap of green water widened, and the child at the rail became a speck of coat, and the speck raised one small arm, and then there was only the wake unrolling toward him like a page being written and erased at once.

Astrid Delacroix stood on the dock until the ship cleared the bend. So did he. They did not speak again, and he thought afterward that this was correct; there are transactions complete precisely because nothing further is said.

* * *

That evening, in his rooms on Gower Street, with the gas low and the rug-beating woman's window dark across the street, Reginald Vane opened the ledger for the last time.

He turned to the page marked **H — Halloway, M., Camden**. Eleven entries, his own careful hand, eleven years of suspicion rendered in iron-gall brown. He uncapped the pen, dipped it, wiped the nib against the bottle's lip, and beneath the last entry he wrote a single line:

*No irregular persons at this address.*

He looked at it for a while. It was not enough and it was everything; most true sentences are like that. Then he took his penknife and worked the photograph carefully up from the page where it had been pasted — the glue gave reluctantly, taking a shred of paper with it, leaving a pale rectangle like a scar where Alice had lived in the book for nine years — and he sat holding it under the lamp. Whitstable. The lifted hem. The sun she was squinting into, invisible and everywhere.

He found a plain envelope and addressed it in the hand he used for nothing official, the loose hand, the private one: *Miss A. Delacroix, Delancey Street, Camden.* Inside, with the photograph, he enclosed a slip of paper, and on it, after several false starts and two discarded drafts burned in the grate, he wrote:

*She drew these too. Thank you for drawing them back. — R.V.*

He posted it that night himself, walking through the wet streets to the pillar box on the corner, and when he dropped the envelope in and heard it land among the other envelopes — bills, apologies, love letters, all the ordinary freight of ordinary lives — he stood for a moment with his hand still resting on the cold iron of the box, and felt the ledger's absence under his arm like the phantom weight of an arm lost at sea.

The hunt was over. It had ended, as he had somehow always known it must, not in victory but in recognition: the inspector and the forger, the keeper of names and the giver of them, standing on the same dock looking at the same departing child, wanting — he understood it now, finally, absurdly, completely — exactly the same thing.

Across the sea, in a house in Camden with chalk stars fading on its front step, a letter was already three days into its journey. And in a cell in Holloway, awaiting the outcome of an appeal, a woman named Miriam Halloway was writing lines that would reach Delancey Street before the postman's knock on any morning Vane could predict — lines that would ask Astrid Delacroix, in her turn, to choose.
