## 21. The House That Kept Its Names

The house was quieter now, but it was not empty. Astrid understood the difference the way she understood engines — by feel, by the particular hum a thing made when it was running true.

She had come back to Wilbraham Place on a grey Tuesday in March, three weeks after the appeal, and stood on the pavement for a full minute before going in. The railings needed paint. One of the window boxes had tipped in a February gale and lay on its side in the areaway, its dead geraniums reaching out like hands. From the street it looked like any house that had outlasted a hard winter. Only she knew how much it had outlasted.

The key turned with the same reluctant click it always had. Inside, the hall smelled of cold ash and beeswax and something underneath both — the smell of a place that had been lived in hard and then left alone. Miriam's hat stand stood bare by the door, its hooks empty since Holloway had claimed them. The coat rack where Tomas's one good jacket had hung showed a pale rectangle of wall, the paint around it unfaded, the shape of his shoulders printed in absence.

Astrid set down her bag and did not cry. She had done her crying at the dock, alone, after the ship had become a smudge and then a rumor and then nothing at all. What she felt now was quieter and stranger: the sensation of walking through a body's memory of itself.

"Astrid?"

Miriam's voice came from the kitchen, and hearing it still startled her, even after three weeks of freedom. There was something about Miriam Halloway being audible in her own house again — the rattle of a lid, the thump of coal in the scuttle — that made the world seem briefly to have righted itself, like a picture rehung slightly crooked so that you kept looking at it.

"In here," Astrid said, which was foolish, because Miriam knew whose footsteps those were. She had known them for years, apparently, the way Astrid had known the weight of her letters.

The kitchen was warm. Miriam had lit the range, and the kettle was beginning its low gathering murmur, and she herself looked thinner than the woman Astrid had driven to rallies and deputations and, once, away from a magistrate's court at twenty miles an hour with the hood of the car steaming. Her hair had gone white at the temples in prison and she wore the white like an ornament. She was slicing bread, of all ordinary things, and the sight of her doing it nearly undid Astrid more than the emptied coat rack had.

"There's a letter came this morning," Miriam said, not turning round. "I didn't open it. It's yours."

"Mine?"

"It's addressed to *the lady who drives*, care of this house." Miriam's mouth curved. "Odette has your measure."

Astrid took the envelope from the table. The postmark was Halifax, smudged and faint, and the paper inside had gone soft at the folds as if it had been read many times before being sent — or before being surrendered. Through the paper she could feel the ghost of something else: a smaller enclosure, stiff and rectangular.

"You'll want to be alone," Miriam said, and passed her the bread knife handle-first, an old courtesy between them that meant: *you are family, and families let each other leave the room.*

But Astrid stayed. That was the whole of it, wasn't it — she stayed. She slit the envelope at the table while Miriam made the tea, and read aloud, because that was what they did now.

*Dear Astrid —*

*Wren has words now. Not many, and she spends them like a miser, but she has them. She said her first one to a steward on the ship — "star" — pointing up through the porthole, and I cried into my apron in front of God and the third class. Since then: "bread," "no," and yesterday, looking at a photograph I brought of you and Miriam at the step, "home."*

*Canada is wide and cold and nobody asks a seamstress for her papers. I have work. The child has school. We have a cow in the next field and I have learned to hate it personally.*

*Tomas is in France. He writes to me through the YMCA and the letters arrive late and cut about, but they arrive. He asked me to tell Wren he was brave, and I have told her, and she drew him a picture of stars that I forwarded on. Whether he gets it, only God and the Army postal service know.*

*Now the thing I enclose. Don't lose your temper at me across an ocean; it wastes breath.*

*I met a woman on the ship. She had a daughter. The daughter died last winter — fever, quick, terrible. The mother was emigrating because staying near the grave was killing her. We talked every day for eleven days and by Liverpool she had said something I have turned over ever since: that grief needs somewhere to put its hands. That it will break whatever it's given, or it will build.*

*Astrid. You and Miriam kept a house for people who could not keep themselves. The house is standing. The rooms are there. There are more Wrens in London than there were berths on that ship, and you know exactly where they sleep, because you read the letters — you always read the letters. Open the house again. Not as shelter, worse luck, the police would have opinions — but as a boarding house, respectable, registered, every name in its proper ledger where Inspector Vane can admire it. Take in girls off the boats and women out of prison and the ones nobody claims. Charge rent for some and not for others and never explain the system, because there isn't one.*

*Grief needs somewhere to put its hands. Put yours on this.*

*Your loving friend,*
*Odette*

*P.S. Wren sends her love in the enclosed. She would only do it once. She says chalk doesn't travel.*

Astrid lifted out the second thing. It was a sheet of paper, folded twice, and on it, in careful grey strokes, Wren had drawn the front step of the house in Wilbraham Place — the step itself, the iron railing, the number — and above the step, scattered across the paper's sky, the stars. Five-pointed, uneven, drawn from memory by a child who had not spoken until the sea and had chosen these as her first word.

Astrid put the paper face-down on the table, because if she looked at it a moment longer she was going to come apart in front of the bread.

"Well," Miriam said at last, setting down the teapot with a click. "She's become a general in exile, hasn't she. Odette."

"She's become us," Astrid said. "That's worse."

* * *

They argued about it for nine days. It was, Astrid thought later, the most honest argument they'd ever had, because neither of them was keeping anything back anymore — there were no letters between them left unread in secret, no loyalties untested. They argued across the kitchen table, in the motorcar on the way to the solicitor's, standing in the emptied back bedroom where Tomas's bed frame still showed four bright squares on the wallpaper where his photographs had been.

Miriam was against it first, on principle, which surprised Astrid until she understood the principle. "I built that house on lies, Astrid. Good lies, necessary lies, but I lied to every official who walked through that door and I did it for years, and I told myself it was war. Then they took me, and I had eighteen months in Holloway to consider whether the house justified itself or merely survived." Her hands were flat on the table. "If we open it again, even respectably, we do it in daylight. Every name written down. No borrowed names. If a girl comes to us, the census man can count her and welcome."

"And the ones who can't be counted?" Astrid said. "You know they exist. You've fed them."

Miriam was quiet a long moment. Outside, a milk cart went by, the bottles singing.

"Then we find out," she said finally, "what a house can hold without lying. That's the experiment. That's the whole of the rest of my life, I think — finding out whether everything I believed survives being done legally."

So they opened it in daylight. The solicitor drew up papers; the register went on the hall table, bound in green cloth, its lines ruled and patient. A BOARDING HOUSE FOR WOMEN, the little brass plaque would eventually say, though the plaque was weeks out. First came a girl named Constance, nineteen, off the boat from Ireland with a bruise going yellow around one eye and no luggage but a hymn book; then Mrs. Okafor, a widow from Lagos by way of Peckham who paid her rent weekly and exact, in coins counted onto the hall table like offerings; then two sisters, the Misses Fenn, who quarreled beautifully and sewed for a theatrical costumier and filled the kitchen with laughter that had no business surviving the war news but did.

Astrid drove Miriam nowhere in particular anymore — Miriam's public life had contracted to committees and letters to newspapers — and so the motorcar sat in its shed, and Astrid found she did not miss it. Her hands wanted other work. She cooked badly and grew better. She learned the names of everyone's people and wrote them in the green ledger in her own upright hand, and at night she checked the door locks and listened to the house settle, and understood that this too was driving: keeping a thing moving, watching the road, holding the wheel.

And every evening, coming home up the street in the dark, she saw it: the front step, and the chalk.

* * *

The chalk had started as an accident. One of the Fenn sisters used it to mark a hem on the pavement and left the stub on the railing, and Astrid, coming home past midnight, had knelt down almost without deciding to and drawn five stars on the front step. Uneven ones. Wren's stars — copied from memory, from a thousand mornings of sweeping around them, from the drawing that still hung above the kitchen dresser in a frame Odette had sent from a junk shop in Halifax.

She hadn't explained it to anyone. But by the end of the week, small stars had begun appearing elsewhere — on the cellar door, on the gatepost, one brave one on the lamppost opposite, drawn by hands unknown. Constance denied it with a face that confessed everything. Mrs. Okafor added one drawn with suspicious skill. Even Miriam, passing the step with her cane one morning, paused, bent with a wince, and touched chalk to stone for a moment; Astrid never saw what she drew and Miriam never said, but the star was there at evening, slightly crooked, like handwriting.

It became the house's signature. The fish boy left a star on a wrapping paper scrap as a joke. The vicar, calling about the roof fund, looked at the step a long moment and said only, "Lovely, that. My sister used to do the same on our garden wall. Children know things about marks, don't they? How a mark means *someone was here.*"

Someone was here. Someone is here. Come back by this sign.

On a bright cold morning in April, Astrid swept the step and found the chalk half worn away by feet and weather, and felt a pang so sharp and specific that she laughed at herself — grief for chalk, of all things. But that was the truth of it: the stars would fade. Chalk didn't travel; Wren had said so, in her seven words. And if the house was going to keep its promise to a child across an ocean, the promise would need better materials than weather would allow.

She bought the paint on Saturday. White enamel, a small tin, and a brush with a fine point, and she waited until the household was at supper and the light had gone blue and gold at the edges of the day, and she knelt on the front step with the tin open and began.

She traced the faded chalk first, each star, following the lines she knew by heart. Then she filled them in, slow, the brush whispering on the stone. White on grey. When she finished one she moved to the next, and the smell of the enamel rose sharp and clean in the cold air, and behind her the kitchen window glowed yellow and she could hear the Misses Fenn arguing about whether a bodice could structurally survive the second act, and Mrs. Okafor's laugh, low as a cello, and Miriam reading aloud something from the newspaper in the voice she used for absurdities.

Her knees ached on the stone. She let them.

Halfway through the fifth star, the front door opened above her and Miriam came out, cane in hand, and stood looking down.

"You'll ruin your dress," Miriam said.

"It's already ruined."

"Mm." Miriam lowered herself, with dignity and some difficulty, onto the step beside the wet paint, well clear of it, and gazed out at the street. Lamps were coming on. Somewhere a piano practiced scales, badly, faithfully. "Do you know what I thought about, in Holloway? Not the cause. Not the speeches. I thought about this step. About sweeping it. About who had stood on it last." She turned her head. "I thought I might come out of prison unable to bear this house — that it would smell of the raid, of Vane's men's boots. And instead I came home and within a week I was arguing with you about ledgers, and it was the happiest I had been in two years." She looked at the stars, wet and shining. "He closed his ledger, you know. I heard it from her ladyship's secretary. 'No irregular persons at this address.' Written in his own hand."

"I know. He sent me—" Astrid stopped.

"The photograph," Miriam said gently. "Yes. Odette told me. A strange mercy, from a strange man." She was quiet a moment. "His daughter drew stars too, you said. Perhaps — and I offer this only as a possibility, Astrid, not a certainty — perhaps every house on every street is holding someone against somebody's ledger. And perhaps the whole of morality is which side of the door you stand on when the knocking starts."

Astrid finished the fifth star. She sat back on her heels and looked at the row of them, gleaming faintly already as the enamel began its long cure, permanent now, rain-proof, winter-proof. Not chalk. Paint. A mark that meant *someone was here* and intended to remain.

"Wren will come back someday," Astrid said. "When she's grown, or when Odette brings her, or when it suits them. Ships run both ways. And she'll walk up this street, and she'll see the stars, and she'll know we kept them. That we kept everything."

"That's a large promise to make in enamel."

"I'm a large promiser," Astrid said. "I got it from reading other people's mail."

Miriam laughed — the real laugh, the one the committee never heard — and reached over and took Astrid's paint-stained hand and held it, and they sat together on the painted step while the lamps came fully on and the piano found its scale at last and the house behind them breathed with all its borrowed, lawful, chosen lives.

Inside, the hall clock struck eight. On the hall table the green ledger lay open, waiting for tomorrow's names, and pinned above it — where the inspector's list had once threatened to hang their fates — was Wren's drawing, framed, the paper sky full of stars.

Astrid had just risen to brush the paint flecks from her skirt when the sound came: footsteps on the pavement, unhurried, and then stopping at the gate. A figure stood under the lamppost in the young dark, a woman with a traveling coat and a canvas bag, looking down at the step — counting the stars, Astrid thought, the way you count your own fingers in the dark to be sure of them.

Then the woman raised her head to the lighted windows, and Astrid's heart turned over, because beneath the travel dust and the thinness and the years she knew that face — and the woman said, in a voice hoarse with distance and decision:

"I saw the stars from the end of the road. Are you taking lodgers?"
