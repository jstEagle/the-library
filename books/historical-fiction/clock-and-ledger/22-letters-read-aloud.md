## 22. Letters, Read Aloud

The kitchen of No. 14 Marlowe Street smelled, on the first morning of April 1912, of toast and coal-smoke and the particular sweetness of a house that had learned to breathe again. A year ago I would have told you that smell was impossible — that houses did not recover, that people did not either, that history was a flood which left nothing standing but the ledger lines recording what it had swept away. A year ago I had believed, with the whole force of my orphanage-forged heart, that history was a thing that happened to other people, in other places, at a comfortable remove from my polished motorcar and my memorized letters.

I knew better now. I had stepped off a dock with an empty berth behind me and a family scattered across an ocean ahead of me, and I had learned the lesson that no institution ever taught me: that belonging is not given. It is chosen. And having chosen it, one must go on choosing it, every morning, at the cost of every comfort one ever hoarded.

But this morning there was toast. And there was Miriam Halloway, freed on appeal since Michaelmas, sitting at the head of the kitchen table in a shawl the colour of wet slate, her hands — still marked by the forcible feeding, two fingers on the right hand that would never again grip fully — cradling a cup of tea she had let go cold because she was watching me.

"You're smiling at the post," she said.

"I am not smiling at the post."

"You are. You've done it every morning for three weeks. You hold each envelope up to the window light like a jeweller assessing a stone, and then you smile as though you've found a flaw only you could love."

I set the small stack of envelopes down beside the marmalade and looked at them honestly. Three bills. A circular from the Taximen's Union, addressed to me now, since I drove for hire out of a garage on Ladbroke Grove and no longer only for her. A letter from the suffrage society with Miriam's name in the corner, which she took without ceremony and slid into her pocket to open later, in her own time, as was her right. And at the bottom, thin as a wafer and travelled soft at the corners, an envelope bearing a Canadian stamp and a return address written in Odette's careful French-inflected hand: *Prairie Rose District, Saskatchewan.*

"It's from them," I said, though Miriam had already seen it. Saying it aloud made it truer. "It's finally come. Six weeks crossing, they said the post might take as long again."

"Then open it," said Miriam. But she said it in the old way, the way she had said everything since Holloway — not as an instruction but as a permission, offered gently, watching what I did with it.

Because here is what had changed, and it was the change that mattered more than any act of Parliament or any verdict of any court: I opened the letter at the table. In front of her. With the blade of the butter knife, because we owned no letter-opener worth the name, and I read it aloud, in my plain driver's voice, while the kettle ticked on the hob and the morning came grey and kind through the window over the sink.

There had been a time — and both of us knew it, and neither of us pretended otherwise anymore — when every letter in this house passed through my hands twice: once in fact and once in secret. I had read Miriam's correspondence for years before I ever confessed to it. I had read it the way a child in an orphanage reads the matron's ledger — not from wickedness but from the bone-deep conviction that survival belonged to those who knew what was written about them before it was acted upon. My own name had been crossed out of such a ledger once, aged seven, with a single stroke of a pen that smelled of ink and indifference, and I had sworn I would never again be the last to know anything.

And then, last spring, that habit — that ugly, useful, cowardly habit — had been the very thing that saved us. The shipping agent's loophole, the flagged departures, Vane's quiet inquiries at the office of the line: I had known all of it because I read what was not mine to read. Confessing that had nearly broken the household. Odette had not spoken to me for four days; Tomas had looked at me as though re-counting everything he'd ever said in my hearing, which was precisely what I deserved. And then Odette had come down to the kitchen on the fifth day with red eyes and said, in her flat, practical way: *You read our letters so you could find us a ship. All right. Then from now on you read them at the table, where we can hear you.* It was not quite forgiveness. It was better. It was terms.

So I read them at the table. Every letter, aloud, whether it flattered anyone or not. It had become the household's strange new sacrament, this daily unsealing, and Miriam — who had known all along, who had let me steal her secrets for years as some terrible test or some terrible kindness, she had never told me which — Miriam said once that it was the only good custom the census crisis had left us, and that most customs cost more and gave less.

I slit the envelope. Two sheets fell out, and something else besides — a pressed flower, flattened nearly to translucency, some prairie bloom the colour of weak wine, its petals gone papery as moth wings. And beneath the sheets, a smaller folded paper covered, in wobbling grey strokes, with stars.

My hands stopped working for a moment. I will admit that. There are things a body does before the mind consents.

"Read," said Miriam softly. "Whatever it is. Read."

* * *

*My dear Astrid — and dear Miriam, whom I hope will make Astrid read this portion twice, because I know how she skims when she thinks no one is checking.*

Miriam laughed, a real laugh, low in her chest, and I shot her a look over the page that only made her laugh harder.

*I write to you from a kitchen that is larger than ours at Marlowe Street and colder, which I did not think God would permit in any country He claimed to have finished making. But the stove is iron and honest and Tomas — yes, Tomas — writes that he has fixed it twice and swears he can fix it a third time if it will only agree to break for him. You will want to know about Tomas first, because I know you, and so: he is well. He is farming. He writes his own letters now, to a cousin in Manitoba, under his own name, his true name, and no one here has ever heard of Inspector Vane or a ledger of irregular persons. The war everyone whispers about has not come, and if it comes, he says, it will come too late to take him, and he says it with his chin up but his hand shaking, and I hold the hand, and that is the whole of our arrangement and it suits us both.*

"He and Odette—" I began.

"Married in June, by special licence, before the ship even cleared Liverpool," Miriam said. "You knew that. You arranged half of it."

"I arranged the licence. I wasn't sure about the rest. People say things on gangways."

"People mean things on gangways," said Miriam. "Go on. I want the part about the wheat. She promised me wheat."

There was wheat. There were eleven paragraphs about wheat, because Odette wrote as she sewed, stitch by meticulous stitch, and the wheat came in for more description than the wedding. The soil was black and went down forever; the sky was so large it frightened Wren for the first week and then delighted her; their nearest neighbours were six miles off and considered this close. The house had two rooms and a roof that leaked over exactly the bed, so they slept diagonal, "which Tomas claims is romantic and which is in truth a matter of arithmetic." The town was called something unpronounceable and the district Prairie Rose, and there was a school opening in the autumn, and Wren would be enrolled in it, under the name Odette had given her on the manifest — the fourth name, the forged name, the name I had written in my own hand at midnight with the lamp turned low and my heart going like a driven engine.

Wren Delacroix. My name, given to a child nobody claimed, so that she could cross an ocean and belong to somebody. I had put it on the paper thinking I was spending myself, and it had turned out to be the opposite of spending. You cannot use up a name by giving it away. It multiplies.

*She goes to school in September,* Odette wrote. *The teacher is a Scotswoman with no patience and enormous kindness, and Wren has already charmed her out of two biscuits and a pencil. As you know — as we all finally know — our girl does not speak. We have stopped waiting for her to, and I think that is why, lately, she has begun trying. She makes sounds at the cows. The cows are unmoved. Last week she laughed — a proper laugh, out loud, at Tomas falling in the creek — and the sound of it frightened her at first, as though she had found some machine in her own throat she didn't remember owning. But she laughed again the next day. Astrid: she laughed again the next day.*

I read that sentence twice, as instructed, and my voice cracked on the second pass, and Miriam reached across the table and put her ruined fingers over my knuckles and left them there, and neither of us said anything cheap about it, because we were English women of a certain sort and we had been through a raid together, and there are things one does not need to narrate.

*Now the enclosure,* Odette wrote. *She made it for you specifically. She asked — in chalk, on the pantry door — for paper and a pencil, and I gave them, and she worked at it for two evenings by lamplight and would not let me see. I believe you will understand it. I believe you are perhaps the only person who will understand it entirely. Tell Miriam the chalk on your front step has an answer now, even across the sea. Especially across the sea.*

*We are well. We are happy, and I use the word carefully because I did not trust it for thirty years and find that it holds weight after all, like good cloth. Come and see us when the fares allow and the season is kind. There is room. There is always room; that is the whole discovery of this country and of this life.*

*Your friend, in wheat and weather,*
*Odette*

* * *

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked itself into a boil and nobody moved to lift it. Outside, April rain had started, fine as sifted flour, silvering the window and darkening the flagstones of the area steps — and the front step above them, where the chalk stars lived.

Because that was the other thing we had done, Miriam and I, in the emptied weeks after the ship sailed. The house had been hollowed — Odette gone, Wren gone, Tomas surrendered and then pardoned and then emigrated inside eight months, a record I still credited to the recruiting sergeant's boredom rather than to any mercy of the Crown. Miriam had come back to us in October, thinner, whiter, absolutely unbowed, delivered home in a cab like a parcel of important documents. And the house had stood around us, too large, full of second suppers with no second mouths, and one grey evening Miriam had found me on the front step with a stub of chalk in my hand, doing a thing I could not have explained to a stranger and did not need to explain to her.

"Stars," she had said, crouching beside me, her coat hem in the damp.

"She drew them on the pavement to warn us," I said. "A man watching the house. She couldn't say it, so she drew it. She understood everything, Miriam, that whole time — everything — and her only voice was chalk." I had drawn another star, five-pointed, lopsided, next to the first. "So I thought — when she looks back, from wherever she is, whenever she thinks of this street — let there be something she recognises. Let the house say it plainly, in her language. *You were understood. You are remembered. Come back by the stars.*"

Miriam had taken the chalk from me and drawn a third star, worse than mine, and then a fourth, correcting herself, and we had stayed there until dark, two grown women on our knees on a London step, chalking a message to a mute child four thousand miles away, and I do not believe either of us has ever done a saner thing.

Now I unfolded the small enclosed paper with both hands, the way one handles a moth.

It was a night sky. That was the first reading of it — a deep paper-blackened field — no; I saw then it was ordinary white paper covered entirely in pencil strokes, hundreds of them, laid so close together the whole sheet had gone grey-dark, and scattered through the darkness, scratched out bright with the bare paper showing through, were stars. Dozens of stars. Some were five-pointed and careful, drawn the way she had drawn them on our pavement. Others were mere jabs and bursts, wild ones, a whole sky's worth, as if she had not been able to stop once she started — as if two years of silence about everything had all gone into this one sheet at once.

And at the bottom of the page, in the corner, in strokes that wobbled like a foal standing for the first time, was a shape I knew. Two figures, small and tall, side by side beneath the largest star. One had hair drawn in scribbled curls — Odette. The other wore something boxy and square at the shoulders.

A motorcoat. Mine.

Beneath them, printed in enormous, painstaking capitals that filled the width of the page — letters learned, I realised, in a Canadian schoolroom already, or taught by lamplight by a patient seamstress with a pencil — was a single word:

**S O O N**

"She can write," Miriam said. Her voice had gone somewhere low and unsteady. "Astrid. The child can *write.*"

"She can write," I agreed, and found I could not say anything else for a moment, because the word *soon* sat there on the table between the teacups and the marmalade like a summons, like a promise, like a hand held out across an ocean, and I am forty-one years old and was crossed out of a ledger before I lost my milk teeth, and I had believed for thirty years that I would go through the world unwritten-down, unclaimed, a person to whom things happened and about whom nothing was recorded except the fare paid and the letters carried.

I looked around my kitchen — at the cold cup of tea, at the woman I drove and followed and chose, at the rain coming down kindly on a street where a census taker had once asked one question too many and set the whole world tilting. I had been the last to know everything, once. Now I was the first to know this: that somewhere under an enormous sky, a girl who did not speak had spent two evenings by lamplight making me a sky full of stars, and had ended it with a word, her first word on paper, and the word was *soon.*

I got up, took the chalk pot down from the shelf where it had lived since autumn, and went out into the April rain without my coat.

"Astrid?" Miriam called after me. "What on earth—"

"The post isn't finished," I said. "There's a reply to send."

On the front step, under the awning's dry edge, the old stars waited, faint from winter, needing renewal as all messages do. I refreshed every one of them. Then, beside them, in capitals as large as the page allowed and my numb fingers permitted, I answered her — the only answer there had ever been to a word like hers, the same word returned, doubled, sent back across the sea in a language any passing neighbour could read and only one child in Saskatchewan would understand:

**SOON**

Behind me, through the open kitchen door, I heard Miriam laughing and crying at once, and putting the kettle on again, and beginning — as she did every year on the anniversary of nothing in particular, merely of being alive and unarrested — to count chairs.

"How many for supper tonight?" she called out. "Shall we lay for four? Five?"

I stood in the rain with chalk dust on my fingers and the letter against my heart, and I found — to my astonishment, and entirely without warning — that I was smiling at the post.

"I don't know yet," I called back, which was the truest thing I had ever said in that house. "But lay the extra seats anyway. This family has a way of growing."
