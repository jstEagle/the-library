## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

The ink was going pale in the November light, and Cyrus Vane did not stop to trim the lamp.

He had learned the coast of Marrow's Bend so thoroughly by now that he could draw it blind — the way a man knows his own mouth, the way he can find his own teeth with his tongue. The estuary opened its crooked fingers into the sea, and the little town sat in the elbow of the western arm like a seed caught in the joint of a hand. He had drawn it forty times. He could draw it in his sleep, and some mornings he woke with his right hand cramped as though he had.

The atlas was Aldous Crane's, though. That was the trouble of it. Cyrus only worked at the edges now, inking what the old man had pencilled, filling in the hachures on headlands Crane had left bare because he meant to come back to them and never did. Six weeks dead, and Cyrus still caught himself turning to speak before remembering the chair by the south window stood empty, that the coat still hanging behind the door would hang there until the moths took it, because Cyrus could not bring himself to move it and could not have said why.

He worked the coastline because it asked nothing of him. Coastlines were patient. A river changed its course in a lifetime; a wood was felled or grew; but the meeting of land and water held its line across generations of men who drew it, and died, and were drawn no more. There was comfort in that. History happened elsewhere — in the capital, where the parliament sat, in the newspapers that arrived four days late smelling of other cities' smoke. Here in Marrow's Bend the great events passed over like weather, bending a few trees, drowning no one. Cyrus had built his whole quiet life inside that belief, the way a snail builds inside its shell, and he had never once tested whether the shell would hold.

He set his pen down and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. The lamp guttered. Outside, the first snow of the year came down fine as flour, sifting through the light from the print-shop windows along Cooper's Row, and somewhere down the street a cart complained over the cobbles and went quiet again.

The atlas lay open before him: sheet eleven, the eastern coast, Crane's pencil ghosting beneath Cyrus's fresh ink. In the margin, in Crane's upright, deliberate hand, a note: *Soundings unfinished. Return in spring.* Cyrus looked at those four words for a long time. *Return in spring.* The old man had written them in March. He had been dead by June. It seemed suddenly obscene, the confidence of that note, the assumption that the seasons owed him anything. Cyrus pulled the sheet away from himself as if it had grown warm.

That was when he noticed the desk.

Crane's drafting desk stood against the north wall, oak, older than either of them, scarred with a century of compass points and pin-holes. Cyrus knew it as he knew his own hands. Every drawer. Every false bottom — for there was one, under the plane of the drawing surface, where Crane kept his better gravers and a bottle of brandy he called *medicinal* with a straight face. Cyrus had gone into every drawer a dozen times since the funeral, cataloguing plates and tools for the estate lawyer, and he would have sworn on his mother's grave that he had found everything the desk contained.

But the candle-flame, leaning in the draft from the ill-fitted window, threw light along the underside of the desk's right flank, and the light caught something that should not have been there: a seam. A hairline shadow running where no join ought to run, low against the floor, below the deepest drawer.

Cyrus knelt. The stone flags were cold through his trousers. He ran his thumb along the shadow and felt the truth of it at once — a joint, planed flush, almost invisible, made invisible deliberately, by a man who understood how things fit together. There was no handle. There was no keyhole that he could find. Only when he pressed the panel did it give, faintly, with the dry click of a spring latch, and a shallow tray slid out into his hands like a tongue from a closed mouth.

It held three things.

A key, small and brass, to a lock Cyrus could not find.

A folded letter, sealed, with no direction on it but his own name — *For C. Vane* — in Crane's hand.

And between them, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger. Not an account book, quite, though it had the look of one: leather boards gone soft at the corners, the spine cracked and mended twice with paste and linen tape. It weighed more than it seemed it should, the way books do when they are filled edge to edge.

Cyrus sat back on his heels on the cold floor and did not touch any of it for a long moment. The snow ticked faintly against the glass. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked and settled. He thought — absurdly, he would think later — that the room had become very crowded without anyone else entering it.

*For C. Vane.*

He broke the seal with his thumbnail.

The letter was short. Crane's writing had begun to fail him that last winter; the strokes wavered like a man walking against wind, and Cyrus had watched it happen and said nothing, because what was there to say. Now the wavering seemed unbearable, each shaky letter a small confession of mortality:

*Cyrus — If you are reading this you have found the drawer, which means I am past explaining myself, and means also that you were cleverer about my desk than I gave you credit for, which will please me wherever I am. The ledger is not mine. I mean that plainly: I did not write it, though my hand is in parts of it, and I will answer for every word in it. What it contains is not survey work and is not to be published, sold, copied, or shown, until such time as you understand it, and I am sorry to leave you to understand it alone. Do not take it to the authorities. That is the whole of my advice and it cost me a great deal to learn. Burn this. — A.C.*

Cyrus read it three times. Then he folded it along Crane's creases and did not burn it, because burning it felt like a second death, a tidying-away, and he was not ready to tidy anything tonight.

The ledger he unwrapped slowly, the oilcloth crackling loud in the quiet shop. He carried it back to the lamplight and opened it at random, expecting accounts, inventories, perhaps debts — some dull arithmetic that would explain the secrecy in some disappointing human way.

It was names. Page upon page of names, in several hands, arranged not alphabetically nor by trade nor by anything Cyrus could immediately determine, though there was a columnar discipline to it, ruled lines in red, entries numbered. Beside each name: a place. *Row Nine. The Quarry Road. Mill Lot, east side.* And beside each place, further columns, mostly filled, sometimes blank, sometimes marked only with a dash where a figure or date should have been, and those dashes disturbed him more than the writing did. A dash was a refusal. A dash was a thing someone had decided not to know.

He turned back to the first page. At the top, in the same wavering hand as the letter — Crane's hand, but older, harder — a single heading:

*Orchard Clearances. Dispossessed & Dead. As witnessed.*

And beneath it, smaller: *The record office holds none of this.*

Cyrus sat with that. The lamp hissed. Snow accumulated on the sill outside in a soft grey ridge like ash.

He knew about the clearances the way everyone in the county knew about them — that is, hardly at all, and sideways. Something done in the hill country nine, ten years ago. Orchards grubbed out, smallholdings consolidated, folk moved on to make room for larger concerns. It had all been done lawfully, presumably; everything was done lawfully; the words *clearance* and *improvement* had appeared in the gazette and then stopped appearing, and the hill road had carried wagons of furniture and families down toward the coast towns for one long autumn, and then it hadn't. History, happening elsewhere. Even when it happened twenty miles uphill, it had somehow happened elsewhere. He had been seventeen. He had thought about it approximately never.

But here it was, happening on paper, in his master's hand, in his master's locked drawer, hidden under lock and spring and oilcloth and a lie of joinery — and hidden, Cyrus understood now, not from strangers. From anyone who might open a drawer. Which, six weeks ago, had included Cyrus himself.

Why hide it from me? The question arrived with a small cold sting. Then its answer, colder: Because I was young. Because I talked. Because I was the sort of man to whom history happened elsewhere, and Crane had known it, and loved him anyway, and waited for him to be otherwise.

He turned pages. The names went on. Some entries had small annotations in the margin, cramped sideways: *refused compensation, evicted by force.* *Died on the road near Harrow Ford, February.* *Infant, name unknown, Row Nine.* And here and there a third column Cyrus had not at first distinguished from the rest — names of another kind entirely, names with titles attached, magistrates and commissioners and men whose signatures Cyrus had seen on grain notices and toll receipts all his life without ever seeing them. Men who signed things. Men whose signatures were everywhere and faces nowhere.

One of the names appeared again and again, in that third column, in a firm commercial hand entirely unlike Crane's. Cyrus could not have said why the sight of it made him sit up straighter, made the room seem to shift half a degree, as a ship shifts when the swell changes. He wrote it down mentally the way he memorized bearings: *Voss.* No title yet attached. Just the name, recurring, patient, threaded through the ledger like a dark vein through marble.

Outside, the snow had thickened. The lane below the window lay unmarked and white, and down toward the records office — the squat granite building where the county kept its deeds and registers, its births and burials and purchases — no lamps burned. Of course they didn't. It was night. But Cyrus looked at it a long while anyway, this building he had passed ten thousand times without a thought, and thought: *The record office holds none of this.*

Which meant the ledger in his hands might be the only place on earth where these people — the dispossessed and the dead, the infant with no name — existed at all.

He closed the book gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and wrapped it again in its oilcloth, and sat in his dead master's chair with his dead master's letter in his pocket and the snow coming down on Marrow's Bend like something being buried, or something being kept.

In the morning, he decided, he would begin at the first page and go through the whole of it, entry by entry, name by name, and he would not stop until he understood what Aldous Crane had spent his last years understanding.

He did not know — could not have known, standing at the window with the ledger heavy under his arm — that within the week, soldiers would be marching up the coast road in the early light, with requisition papers for the records office and orders concerning the estate of Aldous Crane, deceased.
