## 2. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the river fog had not yet decided whether to lift or settle in for the day. Cyrus heard them before he saw them — the particular wet percussion of boots on cobblestone, too many boots, moving in unison — and he set down his pen with the careful deliberation of a man who has learned that sudden movements attract attention.

From the window of Crane's old workroom he watched them come up Anchor Street four abreast, forty of them at least, their greatcoats dark as spilled ink against the whitewashed houses. They did not march so much as flow, the way water flows into a cellar through a cracked foundation: without malice, without haste, and entirely without any intention of leaving.

Marrow's Bend had seen soldiers before, of course. There was the garrison on the hill, mostly ceremonial these last years, its complement grown fat and bored on garrison wages. But these were different. These men carried themselves like weather. Their bayonets caught the early light and threw it back in small cold pieces, and the townspeople who had come out to stand in doorways — Mrs. Aldridge from the bakery, the Kessler boys, old Tam Wren with his bad hip — all of them wore the same expression, the one Cyrus had been trying not to look at directly for weeks now since the funeral: the look of people watching something arrive that they had been told would never come.

A mounted officer rode at the column's head. He sat his horse very straight, very still, in the way of a man who has made stillness into a discipline rather than a temperament. Even at that distance Cyrus could see the quality of him — the immaculate uniform, the face like a coin worn smooth by handling. The horse's breath steamed. The officer's did not seem to.

Later, everyone in Marrow's Bend would know his name. That morning, Cyrus only knew that the man looked at the town the way a surveyor looks at ground: measuring it, assessing it, deciding what would have to be removed.

* * *

By noon there were proclamations pasted to every public wall, printed on cheap paper in a hand too fine for the message it carried. Cyrus read one outside the records office, where a corporal stood with a rifle and the fixed stare of a man pretending not to hear the crowd muttering behind him.

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL COMMISSION FOR THE DISTRICT OF MARROW'S BEND, it began, and Cyrus felt something turn over in his chest at the word *provisional*, because provisional meant temporary, and temporary meant they intended, eventually, for things to be otherwise than this — which meant they knew this was not otherwise yet, which meant they knew what had actually happened here.

The proclamation was long and full of words like *stability* and *reconciliation* and *the closing of a regrettable chapter*. Its operative clause was short. All official documents, registers, maps, correspondence, and records of every description held within the district were hereby requisitioned for review by the Commission. Citizens possessing materials of an official or semi-official nature — including, the clause specified, papers belonging to the estates of deceased officials — were instructed to surrender them to the Commission's representatives within three days.

*Deceased officials.* Cyrus read the phrase twice. Around him the crowd read it too, and he heard the silence move through them like a draft through a house.

Three days. He walked home slowly, past the shuttered offices of the *Bend Advertiser*, past the chapel where the minister was already taking down the memorial plaques from the clearance years — he could see him through the open door, standing on a chair, working screws — and he thought about the locked drawer, and about what a man does with a thing he does not understand but cannot, somehow, put down.

He had not opened the drawer again since that first night. He had told himself it was grief, that he needed time, that Crane deserved better than to have his private papers rifled by an apprentice still learning which end of a scale bar pointed toward truth. These were good reasons. They were also, he was beginning to suspect, reasons the way a fence is a reason — something built after the fact, to mark a boundary one has already chosen.

The ledger lay where he had left it, wrapped in oilcloth at the back of the plan chest, beneath the unfinished sheets of the coastal atlas. He unwrapped it now and looked at it properly for the first time in daylight. It was an ordinary ledger, the kind sold by the stationer on Cordwainer Lane for sixpence, quarter-bound in scuffed leather, its corners soft with handling. Crane's name was embossed on the spine in his own order, in his own hand-press. Inside, the columns ran on and on. Names. Dates. Amounts. And in the margins, in that small severe script Cyrus knew better than his own — had imitated, had been corrected in, had loved — annotations in a shorthand Cyrus could not yet read.

He had counted, that first night, four hundred and eleven entries. He had stopped counting because counting felt like a kind of complicity, though he could not have said why.

Now he wrapped it again, and stood holding it, and listened to the boots on the cobbles outside, and understood that the choice he had been deferring had just been made for him, or rather taken out of his hands entirely, which is how most choices arrive.

* * *

They came to Crane's house on the second day.

Cyrus heard the knock and knew before he reached the door what it would be, and he took the time anyway — thirty seconds, no more — to do the thing he had spent the previous night deciding how to do. The atlas plates went back under their dust cover. The ledger went into the survey map.

It was a map of the lower valley, six feet of paper drawn in Crane's own hand five years earlier, commissioned by the county and therefore, by the proclamation's own logic, official material — material to be surrendered, not hidden. Cyrus rolled the map loosely around the ledger, wrapping the oilcloth bundle in the map's center like a stone in dough, and tied the roll with the plain cord Crane had always used, and leaned it in the corner of the hall among the other rolls, the honest ones, the ones with nothing inside them but rivers and contours and the patient geometry of land.

There were two of them at the door. A sergeant with a list, and behind him, in the street, the officer from the morning of arrival — Voss, the sergeant called him, Captain Ellery Voss — sitting his horse again, watching the house with that surveyor's patience.

"Apprentice to the late Aldous Crane," the sergeant said, reading from his list. "You'll have papers of the estate."

"He was my master," Cyrus said. "I'm finishing his atlas. The maps are mine to complete — the contract is with the Geographical Society." He heard his own voice and was astonished by it: level, unhurried, almost bored. Somewhere beneath the boredom a heart was going like a trip-hammer, but the voice belonged to someone else, someone competent, someone who had already decided to lie and was simply executing the decision.

"The atlas will be reviewed," the sergeant said. "All materials."

"Of course." Cyrus stepped aside. "I'll show you what's here."

They went through the workroom first, because he wanted them to. He wanted their hands on the atlas plates, their thumbs smudging the margins, their eyes sliding over the beautiful useless coastlines and the contour lines of hills no army would ever need to take. Let them find the atlas. Let the atlas be the thing they found. He stood with his hands folded while the sergeant unrolled sheet after sheet and glanced at each with the quick contempt of a man who cannot read drawings, and Captain Voss remained in the street, and Cyrus found he was grateful for that, and then immediately afraid of his gratitude, because a man who is grateful for the absence of danger has begun to believe danger can be managed.

In the hall, the sergeant paused at the roll of maps in the corner.

"Surveys," Cyrus said. "County commissions. Those go to the records office, I'd have thought, not the Commission. They're ten years old."

The sergeant hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a hesitation of jurisdiction, and Cyrus watched it work in the man's face — the fear of taking the wrong thing, of being blamed upstairs for a surplus of diligence. Ten-year-old surveys. Who would want them?

"Leave those," said a voice from the doorway, and Cyrus turned and saw Captain Voss standing in the entrance hall, having dismounted without Cyrus hearing, and the sight of him at close range was worse than the sight of him at distance, because at close range the smoothness of his face resolved into something more specific: attention. Total, economical attention, distributed across the room like light.

Voss crossed to the roll, lifted it, weighed it in one hand. Cyrus kept his breathing even and his eyes on the man's collar and thought, absurdly, of the weight of the ledger, of whether paper had a sound when it was rolled tight inside other paper, of whether a heartbeat could be heard through oilcloth.

"You worked for Crane how long?" Voss asked.

"Six years."

"And you're completing his atlas."

"Yes, sir."

Voss looked at him then, fully, and Cyrus understood that he was being measured — not for guilt, which Voss could not yet suspect, but for capacity. For what he might be worth later, or what he might cost. The captain's gaze moved once, briefly, to the workroom doorway, to the spread of atlas sheets visible beyond it, and came back.

"Crane was a thorough man," Voss said. "Thorough men keep thorough records. If anything surfaces — anything at all, a scrap, a note in a margin — you'll bring it to me personally. Not to the office. To me." He set the roll down exactly where it had stood, aligned its edge with the others, a small precise courtesy that frightened Cyrus more than any threat could have. "Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good." At the door he paused, half-turned. "He died suddenly, your master."

"Heart failure. The doctor signed it."

"Mm," said Voss, in a tone that contained nothing whatsoever, and left.

* * *

That night Cyrus did not sleep. He sat in the workroom with a single lamp lit low, and he unrolled the survey map, and he took out the ledger, and he laid both flat on the drafting table side by side — the official valley, all gentle gradients and named fords, and whatever the other thing was, with its four hundred and eleven entries and its locked margins.

Outside, the fog had come down off the river again, and through it he could see the new sentry posted at the corner of Anchor Street, a shape like a question mark, present even in darkness. The town had gone quiet in the way towns go quiet when they are deciding what they are allowed to say.

Cyrus dipped his pen. On a fresh sheet of tracing paper he wrote out the first entry of the ledger in full, copying Crane's cramped hand as faithfully as he could, and beside it he wrote the date, and beside the date he began, letter by letter, to work out the shorthand — because Crane had taught him that shorthand, years ago, as a game, as a discipline, on long winter evenings; had drilled him in it until Cyrus could read it faster than longhand; and Crane could not have known, could not possibly have known, that he was arming his apprentice for this.

The first name resolved itself out of the marginal cipher at a little past two in the morning.

Cyrus stared at it for a long time. Then he looked at the proclamation still folded in his coat pocket, at the names of the Commission's members printed beneath the seal, and he found the same name there, third from the top, styled *Honorary Commissioner*.

He sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere across the town a dog began to bark and was silenced, abruptly, by a voice or a boot or something worse.

Tomorrow, he thought, he would go and ask questions of people who had lived here longer than he had, people who remembered what the ledger remembered. And he understood, with the clarity that arrives only at the bottom of a sleepless night, that the moment he knocked on the first of those doors, he would stop being a man who had survived something and become a man who intended to say so — and that Captain Voss, who aligned map rolls with such exquisite care, would be waiting for exactly that.
