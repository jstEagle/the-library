## 3. Names in the Margin

The candle had burned to a thumb's width when Cyrus found the cipher, and he laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh that startled him more than the soldiers' boots had done all week.

It was in the atlas. It had been in the atlas for eleven years, sitting under his eyes every working day of his apprenticeship: the survey of the Vane River estuary, the first plate Aldous Crane had ever let him ink. In the lower margin, where most cartographers wrote their scale and bearing, Crane had written instead a string of figures that Cyrus had always taken for a printer's note — *4/9/12, 7/3/11, 4/16/9* — and beneath it, in letters so small they seemed an apology, the words *by the orchard road*.

Numbers and a road. A key and a direction. The ledger's entries were not names at all, not at first glance; they were columns of paired numerals, page references and line numbers, as dry as anything the records office had ever produced. But turned against the atlas — against Crane's own plates, his own meticulous hand — each pair resolved into a letter, and each letter assembled itself into words, and the words were these:

*Ostler, Maren, b. 1871. Row Nine, third holding east of the millrace. Evicted 14 October 1892 under Writ of Clearance 44. Orchard: forty-one trees, thirty years' stand. Burned on the order of —*

And then a name.

Cyrus read it three times because the first two times he assumed he had made an error in the cipher. He had not made an error. The name belonged to a man who had dined at Crane's table. The name belonged, as of nine days ago, to the Commission of Inquiry seated at the King's Arms, five men appointed to establish *the true and impartial history of the Marrow's Bend clearances*, and one of them — Cyrus set down the pencil because his hand had begun to shake — one of them had ordered Maren Ostler's orchard burned with the family still standing in the dooryard.

He did not know yet that her name was Maren. The ledger knew. The ledger knew everything, in its crabbed disguised hand, and it went on knowing, entry after entry, row after row, holding after holding, through the whole of the autumn of 1892 and into the frozen weeks after, when the deaths began to be recorded not as evictions but as something else entirely. *Died of exposure*, the ledger said, and then, in a second column Crane had headed *cause actual*: *turned back at the ferry; gate locked; child in arms*. *Fever*, said the official cause. *No physic permitted within the cordon*, said the truth.

Cyrus sat in the cold workroom among the ghosts of coastlines and understood, with the slow sick certainty of ice forming, that he had spent his whole life believing history was a thing that happened to other people — to capitals, to armies, to men whose names were printed in the gazette in bold type. He had believed the mapmaker's creed Crane himself had taught him: *we record, we do not participate.* The world happened elsewhere and they drew it afterward, faithful and neutral as glass. And here was the proof that neutrality was a story men told themselves while other men burned orchards — that Crane, faithful, neutral Crane, had looked at what was done and could not bear it, and had done the only thing his craft allowed: he had written it down, hidden it inside beauty, buried testimony in the margins of an estuary like seed corn in winter.

* * *

Three days later, sleepless again, Cyrus found himself speaking aloud to the empty chair across the desk, and found that the voice that answered in his memory was not his own but Crane's — the master's low, patient voice, the voice he had used for correcting a botched contour line, which was somehow worse than any anger. It came back to Cyrus now in fragments, in the cadences of lessons given years ago that had meant nothing at the time and meant everything now.

*"You will mark this well, boy."* That had been a Tuesday in November, four years past. Cyrus remembered the frost ferns on the window, remembered being irritated, seventeen and impatient, at being stopped over a detail. *"A map is never a picture of the world. It is an argument about the world. Every choice — what to include, what to leave at the edge, what to name and what to leave blank — is an argument. The man who tells you his map has no argument in it is either a fool or is selling you one so quietly you don't hear yourself agreeing."*

And another evening — later, much later, the spring before his death, when Crane had come back from a trip to the county seat looking ten years older and had drunk more brandy than usual and stood a long while at the window watching the river. *"There are maps,"* he said, apparently to the water, *"that are drawn to make you forget. They are the most dangerous ones, because they look exactly like the honest kind. Same paper. Same hand. And a hundred years on, no one alive can say which version was true — unless someone kept the other copy."*

Cyrus had asked, half-listening, what other copy. Crane had only said, *"Never mind,"* and turned up the lamp and set him to ruling parallel lines until midnight.

*Never mind.* The old man had been carrying this alone for seven years. Had been dining with the men who signed the writs, taking their commissions, drawing their estates with a steady hand — and every night going home to add another name to the ledger, another cause *actual*, hiding the century's crimes in plain sight inside the most beautiful atlas the province would ever see. What had it cost him? Cyrus thought of Crane's last year: the way he had stopped accepting dinner invitations, the way he'd checked the street before lighting the workroom lamp. He had thought it was age. It had been fear, worn so long it had settled into the bones like weather.

And Crane had died anyway — thrown from his horse on the Orchard Road, the inquest said, though Cyrus had never been able to make the geography of that accident agree with itself, and now he knew why he kept trying. Men who carried ledgers died on roads. Men who carried ledgers were found with their skulls broken a mile from anywhere, and the magistrates wrote *misadventure* because the alternative required effort.

* * *

By the fourth night Cyrus had built a system: the ledger open, the relevant plates pulled from the flat cabinet, a sheet of scratch paper for the transposition. He worked by the shaded lamp with its hood turned toward the wall, the habit of smugglers learned in a week. Outside, the garrison's patrols passed at fixed intervals — he had timed them, another thing he had never imagined himself doing — and between patrols the town lay quiet in the way of occupied places, quiet as held breath.

Row Nine. Row Ten. The Ostler family, the Beck family, the Hallorans of the lower terrace — he copied them all out fair, name by name, holding by holding, and beside each the column Crane had headed simply *who profited*. Not the soldiers who swung the axes; they were named too, poor devils, but in smaller entries, almost pitying. The profiting names repeated. They grew familiar the way names grow familiar in a parish register, and that familiarity was the horror of it, because Cyrus knew these men. He had bought paper from one. Another's coachman had once retrieved Cyrus's dropped glove outside the church. Three of them sat now on the Commission of Inquiry, charged with determining *what in fact occurred* during the clearances, and the answer sat in a locked drawer that had been under Cyrus's own elbow for eleven days.

One name appeared more than any other. Beside evictions, beside the burnings, beside the worst entry of all — the Halloran infant, February 1893, *died of exposure / cause actual: turned back at the ferry; gate locked* — the same signature recurred like a refrain. A captain then. Major now. And presiding over the inquiry, with the authority of the Crown behind him:

Captain Ellery Voss.

Cyrus put down the pen. His mouth was dry and his heart was going hard and steady, like a man walking fast without wanting to run. So that was why the soldiers had come. Not for paperwork, not for the requisition's tidy fiction. The army had burned the records office to ash and then come straight to Crane's house, polite as tax collectors, asking after the estate's papers, because somewhere in the capital a man with a new title needed a certain list of names to stop existing before the century turned and took his career with it.

The ledger was not a record, Cyrus understood then. It was a fuse. It had always been a fuse, and Crane had spent seven years keeping it dry, and now it was in the hands of an apprentice who had never in his life done anything braver than argue with a customs officer over a duty stamp.

He should burn it. The thought arrived honestly, wearing the clothes of prudence: burn it, keep the atlas, keep his hands, keep his life. No one would ever know he had failed to act, because failing to act left no trace — that was the whole genius of doing nothing, the philosophy he had lived by for twenty-three years.

But the ledger had a child's name in it. *Halloran, infant, b. November 1892, d. February 1893.* Eleven pounds of flesh at the start, less than that at the end, and a gate locked against its mother at the ferry, and somewhere out in this occupied town — he was almost sure, he had begun making inquiries almost sure — a woman had survived that winter and was still living, and the official record said her child had died of fever as fevers did, an act of God, no one's hand in it at all.

Cyrus trimmed the candle, squared the ledger on the desk, and began, carefully, in his best hand — Crane's hand, really; everything he had was Crane's — to copy the key.

When the knock came at the door, soft, twice, and then once more, in the small hours, he blew out the light and stood in the dark with the pages against his chest, listening to a woman's voice say his master's name through the wood.

"I saw your lamp," she said. "I know that handwriting. I want to know what he wrote about Row Nine."
