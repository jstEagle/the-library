## 4. The Widow of Row Nine

Maren Ostler knew every footstep on Cordwainer Lane the way other women knew psalms. Eleven years in the house at the end of it, since the army had turned her out of the orchard rows and the town had turned its face away, and in that time she had learned to read sound the way her mother had read weather. The cobbler's heavy tread at six. The milk cart's iron-rimmed groan at seven. And now, on a morning thin with river fog, a lighter step — hesitant, stopping twice between the baker's and her gate, as if the walker kept changing his mind.

She put down her knife and the half-peeled apple and watched the lane through the window's warped glass.

A young man stood at her gate. Too young for the coat he wore, which hung like a borrowed opinion. He carried a leather tube such as surveyors used, and he stood the way people stood outside her door: apologizing to the gatepost before he'd touched it.

Cyrus Vane. She knew him by sight only — Crane's apprentice, the quiet one who inked coastlines and was said to speak to no one but his master. And Crane eleven weeks dead, drowned or dropped or however the town chose to tell it that day, because Marrow's Bend had never settled on a story it could live with.

Maren did not go to the door. She let him knock, and knock again, and she counted the knocks the way you count a man's conscience. Four was respectable. Six meant he had something to hide. He knocked five times, and she decided that was honest enough — a man who wanted something and hated wanting it.

She opened the door with the chain still on.

"Mrs. Ostler." His breath made a small ghost in the cold. "Forgive me. I'm Cyrus Vane. I worked for Aldous Crane."

"I know what you worked."

He blinked. Fog had gotten into his hair; he looked like a boy who'd run through a field of spiderwebs. "I'm finishing his atlas," he said. "The *Gazetteer of the Bend and Basin*. It was to be his great work. There are — gaps. Parcels along the east bank, the old orchard lands, that were never surveyed to completion. I thought perhaps someone who lived there might—"

"Someone who lived there," Maren repeated.

"Someone who remembered it."

*Remembered.* The word had a smell to it, like meat gone over. She unhooked the chain, because curiosity was the one hunger age had never dulled, and stood aside to let him into the kitchen, where the stove ticked and the apple lay browning on the board.

"Sit," she said. "Don't touch anything."

* * *

He sat where she pointed and put the leather tube across his knees like a child holding a cat it wasn't sure it was allowed to keep.

"You'll take tea," she said, not asking, and poured it because her hands wanted occupation. She studied him while he cupped his palms around the mug. Long fingers. Ink in the creases of his knuckles that never washed out — she remembered that from her father, who had signed what he was told to sign. This boy had hands made for careful work, and eyes that hadn't yet learned to lie with them, which was either a mercy or a liability depending on what he'd come to ask.

"The east bank parcels," she said. "Rows one through fourteen. What is it Crane wanted there? A map needs distances, not memories."

"He wanted—" The boy stopped. Started again, more carefully, and she saw him choosing each word the way you cross a frozen river. "His notes reference field names. Which fields bore which fruit. Who held which row. For the historical appendix."

"Historical." She set her own cup down harder than necessary. "There's a word they've been polishing all year. The commission uses it. *Historical record shows the lands were surrendered.* Surrendered, mind you. As if we laid our orchards at their feet with ribbons on."

"I didn't say—"

"You didn't come all this way through the fog to not say. So say it, boy."

He looked at his tea. Then, to her surprise, he reached down and drew from the tube not a map but a slim book bound in grey cloth, and laid it on the table between them without opening it, the way a man lays down cards he hasn't looked at yet.

"I found this in my master's desk," he said. "Behind a locked drawer, in a hollow I didn't know was there until the wood swelled with the damp. It isn't in his hand entirely — some of it is, the headings and corrections. Most of it is entries. Names." He paused. "Row Nine."

The stove ticked. Somewhere down the lane the cobbler's hammer started up, patient as ever.

"What about Row Nine?" Maren asked, and heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used at market when the price of flour changed for no reason anyone would admit.

"It lists a family. Ostler. Seven names. And beside them, in a different ink, dates. And a word I can't make sense of." He turned the book around, opened it at last, and slid it toward her. "*Fevered*, it says. Three of them marked fevered, two marked—*" He stopped reading aloud. She watched his eyes travel the line and understood he had just met her dead.

She did not touch the ledger. Her hands stayed flat on the table on either side of it, framing it, the way you frame a thing you're afraid will move.

"That's Crane's hand," she said quietly. "The headings. I'd know it anywhere."

Vane looked up sharply. "You knew my master?"

"My father knew him better than he knew God, and consulted him more often. Aldous Crane came to Row Nine four times before the clearances. Measuring. Asking. Writing." She tapped the grey cloth once, with one finger. "I watched him write those very letters. *Ostler*. He had a way of crossing his capitals like he was shutting a gate."

* * *

Afterward she could not have said why she told him. Perhaps because he had carried the book through the fog to her door instead of carrying it to the commission, which was where any sensible man would have carried anything worth money in Marrow's Bend these days. Or perhaps because the kitchen had been silent eleven years and silence, like a well, will hold only so much before it starts to rise.

She told him about the spring of '88, how the requisition notices came printed on paper so fine the words showed through like bruises. How her father went to the assize hall with the deed his grandfather had carried and was shown a copy of a copy of a document that gave the company title back three generations. How they were given six days.

"They came on the seventh," she said. "Not soldiers first — that's what nobody believes. Soldiers came after, to keep the peace, they said. First came the company men with the wagons and the tally books, and they took the harvest. Everything bearing fruit, they took. Then the bailiffs came with the kerosene."

"Fevered," Vane said softly. "The ledger says fevered."

"We were moved to the relief camp past the tannery, forty souls from our row alone, in tents in October. My grandmother went first, then the little twins inside a week of each other. The camp surgeon wrote *gastric fever* on their cards because that was the card he had pre-printed and there was a shortage of everything but stamps." She found her hands had begun shaking and pressed them together in her lap. "Two others didn't die of fever. They died of being old and put out in the wet. But *fever* went on all four cards, because fever is an act of God, and acts of God don't come to trial."

Vane's face had gone the colour of the fog outside.

"There were eleven rows," she went on, because once the well rises you let it come. "One hundred and six souls displaced off the east bank, and nine dead within the year, and every name written in that book of yours is written nowhere else, because the records office burned last month — oh yes, I know about the fire, everyone knows about the fire that leaves the tax ledgers dry and eats only the inconvenient pages." She leaned forward. "Now you tell me why an apprentice mapmaker brings that book to a widow's kitchen instead of losing it in a river."

He held her gaze, and to his credit did not look away, though she could see it cost him.

"Because Captain Voss requisitioned everything in Crane's estate two days ago," he said. "And I told him that was everything. I hid the ledger in a rolled survey map and handed it to him and lied to an officer of the army, Mrs. Ostler, and I've been sick with it since, and the only thing worse than having lied is thinking I should have lied sooner." He swallowed. "My master spent years on this. I thought it was an atlas. I thought history happened somewhere else, to other people, and my job was to draw the coastlines. Then I read the names in the margins and found your family sitting in mine."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down the lane, the cobbler's hammer stopped, and the silence felt sudden, like a held breath.

Maren looked at the ledger — at the gate-crashed capitals, at her father's name in the hand of a man who had measured her world and, it seemed, mourned it in ink. Aldous Crane had known. Had written it all down knowing, and locked it away, and died with it unfinished. That was its own kind of cowardice, and she was not feeling generous enough to forgive it yet. But the apprentice had brought it through the fog. That counted. It counted the way a single plank counts to a drowning woman — not as rescue, but as direction.

"If you finish this," she said slowly, "if you print these names under some fine atlas heading, Voss will bury it or burn it or bury you with it. He didn't requisition a records office for the good of posterity. He was at the east bank in '88 — a lieutenant then, riding escort for the tally wagons. I've seen his face every night for eleven years."

"I know." The boy's voice cracked and steadied. "There's a clerk. Army. He's seen the commission rewriting the death registers — changing causes, changing dates. He wants to come over. He says there's proof."

"A clerk." She almost laughed. "A mapmaker, a widow, and a clerk. Against a captain and the whole victor's commission."

"Yes," said Cyrus Vane, and did not smile.

Maren rose, took down the third cup she never used, and poured. Then she pulled her chair to the table, opened the ledger to Row Nine, and put her finger beneath her grandmother's name.

"Then you'll want the parts Crane never wrote down," she said. "Because I remember every soul on these rows, and I was there when the kerosene came — and if we're going to spend the truth, boy, we'll spend it all."

Outside, the fog was lifting off Cordwainer Lane, and somewhere toward the garrison a bugle called the hour — the same hour, she thought, that it had always called, as if the town had learned nothing and forgotten nothing and would need telling again.
