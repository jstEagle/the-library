## 5. A Clerk Without a Country

The Commission's temporary offices occupied the old wool exchange, and Tobias Finch had come to know its silences the way other men came to know a wife's moods. There was the silence of the vault below, where the requisitioned records slept in their iron cabinets like patients who no longer answered to their names. There was the silence of Captain Voss's office above, where the floorboards creaked in patterns Finch had memorized: two steps to the window, four back to the desk, a long stillness at the ledger stand. And there was this third silence, the worst one, which lived in Finch's own chest and had lived there for eleven days now — ever since Major Halloway had set the fair copy of the clearance schedule in front of him and said, in the mild voice of a man discussing weather, *Make it clean, Clerk. The old dates are confused. Confusion is unhelpful to the inquiry.*

Eleven days of copying. He had been an army clerk for nineteen years and he had never once refused an order, because refusing was a thing that happened to other men, braver and stupider men, and Finch had built his whole life on the principle that he was neither. His hands were good hands. Neat hands. Hands that could produce a column of figures so regular it looked printed. All his superiors had said so.

Now those hands moved across the page and changed the date of a death.

It was a small thing. That was what nobody understood about evil — or whatever word one used when one could not yet bring oneself to say evil. It was small. A widow named Ostler had lost her husband on the fourteenth of March; with three strokes of Finch's pen the man died on the twenty-second instead, which mattered, because the twenty-second fell after the date the commission claimed the evictions had concluded peacefully, and therefore the death became unfortunate rather than consequential. Three strokes. The ink dried in four seconds. Somewhere in the town a woman was walking around with a grief that no longer officially existed, and Finch's pen had done it as smoothly as a plane shaves wood.

He wrote the next line. *Cause of death:* The original return said *fever, following exposure*. The new copy would say *fever, pre-existing*. He knew the difference was nothing and he knew the difference was everything, and his pen did not hesitate, because pens do not have consciences; only men do, and Finch had misplaced his somewhere around 1897 and was only now going about the business of finding it.

That evening he stayed late under pretense of reconciling the stationery returns. The vault was not locked — locking things implied they might be wanted by someone else, and the commission had already decided that nothing below stairs belonged to anyone else — and Finch went down among the iron cabinets with a candle and stood in the cold air that smelled of dust and mouse and old glue. Row Nine. The Ostler holdings were filed under the parish returns, and he found them where he expected, tied in buff ribbon, Aldous Crane's hand all through them in the margins: small, precise annotations, dates and initials, a mapmaker's obsession with accuracy even in another man's paperwork. *Verified against parish register.* *Discrepancy — see witness statement, J. Quill.* And beside one entry, in red: *This man was hanged from his own gate. I saw it done.*

The commission's fair copy said Ostler died of fever in his bed.

Finch stood in the vault until the candle guttered, and something shifted in him the way ice shifts on a river — not breaking, not yet, but groaning, announcing that it would break, that it was only waiting for one more cold night or one warm morning.

* * *

He told himself for three more days that there was nothing useful he could do. He was a clerk. Clerks did not act; clerks were acted upon. But he began, without quite admitting it, to memorize. He memorized which returns had been altered and how. He kept a tally inside his cuff of the deaths reassigned to fever, to misadventure, to *cause not recorded*. On Thursday he copied, in a hand deliberately unlike his own, the original eviction order bearing the signature of a man now elevated to the victory commission — the same order Crane's marginal note called *backdated, see survey 41-C* — and folded it into the lining of his coat, where it lay against his ribs like a coal.

Friday morning, Major Halloway praised the fair copy. *Clean work, Clerk. The Captain will be pleased.* And Finch looked at his neat hands on the desk and understood that they were not good hands at all, that they had never been good hands, that goodness was not a matter of regularity, and that if he wrote one more clean line he would be the kind of man whose obituary would be accurate and unbearable.

That night he did not go home to his lodgings. He went out the delivery gate with his greatcoat buttoned to the throat and the papers against his ribs, and he walked through Marrow's Bend in the rain, past the burned shell of the records office where the wet char smelled like a doused hearth the size of a cathedral, past the garrison sentries who did not look twice at a clerk's grey silhouette, down lanes he did not know, following directions he had extracted over two weeks of careful listening from a woman at the market who sold apples and hated the commission with the specific hatred of someone who had lost a row of her life to it.

The house behind the disused orchard nursery had one lit window.

* * *

Cyrus Vane opened the door with the bar already lifted out of its brackets, and behind him the room was candlelit chaos: atlas plates laid edge to edge on the floor, the ledger open under a weight of glass, Maren Ostler seated at the table with a mending basket she was not using. Finch stood dripping on the step and heard himself say, in the flat voice of a man reciting his own indictment:

"My name is Tobias Finch. I am fourth clerk to the victor's commission. I have spent eleven days falsifying the record of the clearances, and I have brought you proof of it, and I would like — before you decide what to do with me — to write down what I remember of the true ones."

Nobody spoke. Rain ticked in the eaves. Cyrus looked at Maren; Maren looked at Finch the way one looks at a snake and tries to determine whether it is venomous or merely frightened.

"It was your hand," she said at last. "On the fair copies. I've seen the requisition notices. You press too hard on your downstrokes."

"Yes," Finch said. "I do."

"Come in, then," she said, "before you drown on my doorstep. But understand, clerk — you'll get no absolution here. We're not in the business of it."

"No," Finch said, coming inside, working off his wet gloves finger by finger. "I didn't come for that. I'm fairly certain there isn't any."

They worked until dawn. It went easier than any of them expected, which was itself unsettling — treason, Finch discovered, felt almost identical to ordinary clerical diligence, the same lamplight, the same columns, the same care taken not to smudge. He reproduced from memory the alterations: fourteen deaths reassigned, nine dates moved, three causes struck entirely. He described the original returns still stored in the vault below the wool exchange, uncatalogued because cataloguing them would require acknowledging them. He drew, freehand, from a memory honed by nineteen years of filing, the layout of the cabinets, and Cyrus transcribed it onto the margin of a survey plate, turning the vault into geography, and there was a moment — Finch would remember it all his life — when the young mapmaker looked up and said quietly, "You've made them into a map. Everything they've hidden is just coordinates now." And Finch felt something in his chest loosen that had been clenched since 1897, some knot whose name he supposed was shame.

Maren made tea at three in the morning. They drank it standing, like conspirators in a cheap novel, and Cyrus said, "If they catch us, you'll hang. You know that. You're not a displaced farmer they can call a troublemaker. You're one of theirs."

"I was never one of theirs," Finch said. "I was adjacent to theirs. It's a different country, and I've just renounced my citizenship." He surprised himself by almost smiling. "Besides, hanging requires a trial, and trials require a record, and we intend to make records very expensive for them."

But it was Maren, folding the copied pages into oilcloth with a farmwife's economy, who gave the conspiracy its shape and its warning both. "The vault won't keep," she said. "Voss will burn it like he burned the records office, once the inquiry closes. If the originals matter, they have to come out before the century does. And if we take them—" she tied the cloth, "—we should assume he already knows someone will try."

None of them knew yet how right she was. None of them knew that in Captain Voss's office that same Friday, a requisition clerk had reported a fourth set of boot-prints in the mud behind the nursery wall, grey army leather, narrow heel, size eight — or that Voss, hearing it, had said only, "Then we know where all three of them sleep," and reached for his hat.
