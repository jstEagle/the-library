## 6. What the River Keeps

The frost came early that year, silvering the print shop windows on the morning the soldiers found us.

I had been awake since before dawn, working by lamplight at Crane's old press, inking a proof of the river plate — the Marrow itself, drawn from his surveys and corrected by my own hand. There is a particular silence to a print shop before it wakes: the smell of oil and lampblack, the damp wool weight of paper stacked in the corner, the press standing patient as a beast that knows its work will come. I had loved that silence once. That morning I understood it for what it was — the quiet of a thing about to be broken.

Maren heard them first. She was sleeping in the loft above the type cases, wrapped in Crane's old coat, and she came down the stairs without a sound, one hand on the rail, her face pale in the grey light.

"Cyrus." Just my name. That was enough.

I killed the lamp. Through the window I could see them coming up Tannery Lane — six soldiers in the blue-grey of the garrison, breath steaming, bayonets fixed, and behind them a man on foot in a captain's greatcoat who did not need to raise his voice to be obeyed. Voss walked like a man entering his own property.

"The back," Maren whispered.

There was no back. There was the yard, and beyond the yard the low stone wall, and beyond the wall the drop to the towpath and the river, brown and slow with autumn rain. The plates were already packed — I had done it two nights before, unable to say why, some animal instinct telling me that the atlas must never sleep far from its escape. Twelve copper plates wrapped in sacking, the ledger sewn into an oilskin wallet against my chest, and Finch's stolen commission papers rolled inside a spare roller.

We went over the wall as the front door came down. I heard the crash of it, the splinter of good oak, and then boots in the shop, and then — this is the part I cannot make peace with, even now — the sound of the press being broken. A deliberate, methodical destruction. Iron pried from iron. Crane's press, which had printed parish notices and land bills and forty years of honest maps, dismantled with crowbars while we crouched in the frozen mud of the yard with our arms full of other people's deaths.

"Move," Finch said. He had come around from the coal merchant's slip where he'd been watching the lane, and his face was the colour of tallow. "They'll post men on the bridge within the hour. We take the barge."

* * *

Ostler's barge — Maren's late husband's barge, though she never called it that — lay at the coal wharf below the mill race, half-loaded with firebrick, her hold smelling of tar and wet grain. Maren went aboard first and lit no lamps. Cyrus followed with the plates; Finch cast off the stern line with hands that shook so badly he dropped the coil twice.

We slid out on the ebb with the mist still on the water, past the gasworks, past the drowned orchard terraces where the stumps stood black in the shallows like teeth. Nobody spoke until the bend took the town from view. Then Maren sat down on a firebrick and put her face in her hands, and I understood that she was not weeping for the shop. She was weeping because she had known, the way the displaced always know, that leaving would be the easy part.

"They'll burn it," she said finally. "They burned the records office. They'll burn the shop."

"They already have the press apart," Finch said. His voice had the flatness of a man reciting inventory. "Voss won't burn what he can use. He'll want witnesses. He'll want someone to have seen us flee — flight is nearly a confession, in his arithmetic."

"Then let him have his confession," Maren said, and there was something in her voice I had not heard before, something with iron filings in it. "Let him chase it all the way to the capital."

I wanted to believe her. I stood at the tiller with the ledger against my chest and told myself that we had saved the truth, that truth was a cargo like any other, that it would keep. The river carried us past reed beds and the skeletons of fish traps, past the ruined weir at Hollis Bend where the water made a sound like distant applause. The plates rode in their sacking like a coffin's contents. I kept thinking: he built these. He cut every line of the coast with his own burin, and they broke his press with crowbars, and I ran.

Grief, I learned that day, is not only sorrow. It is arithmetic. It is the sum of what you carried away weighed against what you left burning behind you.

* * *

We made the lock at Ferrow's Cut by dark, and that was where the boy found us.

He came along the towpath barefoot despite the cold, a miller's son with flour to his elbows, and he would not come aboard until Maren promised him bread. Then he crouched on the gunwale and told us what we already feared: soldiers in the town square, a wagon, a list being read aloud. Names. Ostler — no, not Ostler, she was gone, but her sister-in-law. The coal merchant who had sold us lamp oil. Old Prewitt, who kept the toll book at the north gate and had once, years ago, carried a message from Aldous Crane to a magistrate and never asked what was in it.

"They're saying," the boy said, chewing, "that nobody comes home till the map-thief does. Captain's orders. They're holding them at the drill hall." He said it the way children say terrible things, lightly, with bread in his mouth, and then he was gone down the towpath, running, flour-white in the dusk.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The lock keeper's lantern swung somewhere upstream. The water in the lock rose around us, lifting the barge inch by inch, patient as everything else that night.

"Hostages," Finch said at last. He did not look at me. "That's the word for it. He's taken hostages."

"He wants the ledger," I said. "He doesn't care about me. He cares about the names."

"He cares about both," Finch said. "The ledger proves the names, and you prove the ledger exists. And now—" he stopped, and started again, and I watched a man choose honesty over kindness in real time, and admired him for it even as it cut me. "Now he has something better than warrants. Now he has leverage. Every mile we go downriver, someone in Marrow's Bend goes hungry in a drill hall. Every day we run, they stay. He knows you'll hear about it. He's counting on your conscience doing what his soldiers couldn't."

Maren stood up. In the dark her voice came from somewhere above me, steady and terrible.

"My sister-in-law has three children."

"I know," I said.

"Do you? Do you know what a drill hall is in winter, Cyrus Vane? Straw on stone. One stove for two hundred people. Do you know what the army feeds prisoners of its own town?" She was not shouting. That was the worst of it. She had been evicted once already; she knew the grammar of it. "You are carrying a book of names, and he is making new ones. Faster than you can print."

The lock gates opened. The water levelled. Somewhere ahead the river ran on through the dark toward the capital, wide and cold and indifferent, keeping whatever was given to it.

"We go back," I said.

Finch turned sharply. "No."

"We go back, or we send it forward without us—"

"And how?" Finch's composure cracked at last, and beneath it I saw the clerk who had spent ten years falsifying other men's cruelty and hated himself for it. "Send it with whom? The boy? The lock keeper? You'd hang a stranger for every mile gained. Listen to me. I served under men like Voss. Leverage is a door that only opens one way. Give him the ledger and he burns it and takes the hostages anyway, because hostages who've seen your face are liabilities. You don't buy people back from a man like that. You make him afraid to hold them."

"How?" Maren asked. "How do you make a captain afraid?"

Finch was quiet a moment. When he answered, he was looking at the dark water, but his voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice of a man reading from a document he wished did not exist.

"You don't frighten him with the truth. Truth is slow; he'll be promoted before it arrives. You frighten him with exposure — with the possibility that the record escapes him. Right now he believes the ledger is the last copy of the truth in the world. So long as he believes that, the hostages are worth taking." He looked up at me. "So we make him believe there are copies. Letters already posted. A second ledger, held elsewhere. Make the hostages worthless as leverage and dangerous as scandal. A captain who tortures a town to recover a document that no longer matters — that's a report his superiors will read."

"It's a bluff," I said.

"It's a forgery," Finch said, and for the first time since I'd known him, he smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "It's the one honest use I ever found for my trade."

* * *

We tied up that night in the willows below the derelict ferry stage, and by the light of a shielded candle Finch wrote the letters while I forged — there is no gentler word — the second ledger: thirty pages in an imitation of Crane's hand, copied from memory and invention both, real names mixed with plausible ones, dates shuffled, a false cache named in the hills above Kelder's Ford. My master taught me lettering for eleven years. I never thought to thank him that the lesson would one day be used to lie to a murderer.

Maren sat apart, mending a torn corner of sacking over the plates, and did not help and did not object. Once she said, quietly, to no one: "Aldous would have hated this." And then, after a while: "Aldous would have done it anyway."

Toward morning I climbed the bank to see if the road was clear, and looked back the way we had come. Far off, at the limit of sight, Marrow's Bend lay under woodsmoke — ordinary smoke, chimney smoke, perhaps, or perhaps the records office still smouldering, or the shop. From this distance I could not tell guilt from weather. I thought of the boy with flour on his arms, of Prewitt with his toll book, of Maren's sister-in-law's three children learning what straw on stone smells like, all of it held hostage to a book sewn in oilskin against my ribs.

The river kept moving. That was its whole nature; it kept what sank and moved what floated, and it did not distinguish between them. I had spent my life believing history happened elsewhere, to other people, in other rooms. Now it happened here, on a barge, in my own hands, and its price was paid in a currency I did not carry — paid by the baker, the toll keeper, the children of Row Nine.

I went back down the bank and told them the road was clear, and we pushed off into the mist, downstream, toward the capital, carrying the truth and the lie together in the same hull.

Behind us, faint on the wind, I thought I heard the drill hall bell ringing the hour — though it may only have been the weir at Hollis Bend, applauding.
