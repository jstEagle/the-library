## 7. The Commission Sits

The inquiry room had been the assize court once, before the town needed it for something more useful than justice. Captain Ellery Voss stood at the head of the long table and watched the light come through the tall windows — light the colour of weak tea, filtered through the smoke still rising from the records office two streets away — and thought, as he thought every morning now, that a room was only ever an instrument. It could be tuned. It could be played. The men seated along it this morning were not judges; they were keys on a keyboard, and he had learned all their weights.

At his right hand sat Colonel Haskin, whose gout was worse in winter and whose opinions therefore travelled no farther than the end of his own boot. At his left, Alderman Prew, whose signature appeared in certain documents Voss had personally committed to the fire, and who understood with the clarity of a man permanently under threat exactly how cooperative he intended to be. Then the others: Major Sleight, grey and punctual as weather; the magistrate's delegate, young Corrigan, who took notes nobody would read; and Dr. Fenwick, the commission's medical officer, whose reports on causes of death had been drafted twice already and would be drafted again.

Voss did not sit. Sitting invited discussion, and discussion invited the record, and the record — the true one, the one being assembled here out of whole cloth and half-truths — was his to build.

"Call the first witness," he said.

* * *

The mechanics of it were almost beautiful, if you had the stomach to see beauty in them. Voss had the stomach. He had discovered years ago, somewhere in the mud of the border campaigns, that most men did not want to lie. They wanted to be given something they could say instead of the truth — a phrase, a shape, a small respectable container — and then they would carry it gratefully, like a soldier carries a rifle he doesn't have to fire.

The first witness was a millhand named Daker, called regarding the evictions at Row Nine. He came in wringing his cap and looking at the floor as though the answers were written there.

"You were present," Voss said, "at the clearance of Row Nine in March of '97."

"Present, sir, yes."

"And you saw no violence."

It was not a question. Voss let it hang there, weightless, and watched Daker find the shape of it.

"No violence, sir. Not as such. There was... pushing. There was always pushing, moving folk along. But I wouldn't call it violence."

"Thank you." Voss turned to Corrigan. "Note: witness confirms orderly conduct throughout."

Corrigan's pen moved. Somewhere behind them, in the gallery set aside for townsfolk, someone shifted, and a woman's voice said nothing loudly — the particular silence of a person biting down on words. Voss did not turn to look at her. He knew who she was without looking. Half the town knew Maren Ostler by sight now; grief had made her famous in the way that floods and fires are famous.

He had reviewed the list of witnesses himself, struck four names, added nine. He had moved the hearings from the schoolhouse to this room so that the acoustics would favour the table and swallow the gallery. He had arranged for testimony about the winter of '97 to be heard after the Christmas recess, when memories soften like fruit. None of this required cruelty. That was what the colonels and the ministers never understood, sitting in the capital with their clean hands: corruption was not a passion. It was bookkeeping. It was schedules. It was knowing which clerk owed you which favour and calling it in on a Tuesday rather than a Friday, because Fridays the alehouses were loud and clerks grew sentimental.

"Next witness."

* * *

Through the middle of the day the machinery ran smooth. A farmer testified that the orchard land had been "mostly derelict." A constable swore the deaths recorded in November of '97 — three names, an old man, a woman in childbed, a boy of eleven — had been "unconnected to military action." Fenwick presented his revised medical summary, in which fever appeared where exposure had been, and misadventure where fever should have stood, and the boy of eleven became a drowning, regrettable, unattributed, closed.

Prew asked no questions. Haskin dozed by degrees, his chin descending toward his chest like a slow tide.

It was during the afternoon session — a surveyor, summoned to establish that the new boundary markers followed "natural features" rather than property lines — that the day cracked.

The surveyor's name was Loomis. He was an old man, half-deaf, and he had been Crane's sometime colleague. He answered the questions put to him correctly enough, but between questions he kept doing something with his hands, folding and unfolding a scrap of paper, and Voss, who had spent a career reading men's hands the way other men read faces, marked it.

"Loomis," he said, when the formal examination ended. "A moment. You worked alongside Aldous Crane on the parish surveys."

"I did, sir. Thirty years, near enough."

"And in your professional judgement, his work was sound."

Loomis looked up. His eyes were milky but they were not stupid. "Sound as any man's, sir. Better than most. He kept everything, Mr. Crane. Everything down to the last fence post and the last—" He stopped. The folded paper stopped too, mid-fold, in his fingers.

"The last what?"

"Fence post, sir." Too quick. Too smooth. Old men are not smooth; old men ramble. Voss felt the small cold click inside him that he had learned to trust above rank or evidence — the click of a door opening onto a dark stair.

"Did Mr. Crane keep papers?" Voss asked lightly. "Notes, surveys, correspondence?"

"Everyone keeps papers, sir."

"Where is his estate now?"

"With his apprentice, they say. Young Vane. Was his apprentice since—"

"That will be all. Thank the witness."

Loomis shuffled out clutching his scrap of paper, and Voss sat very still for a moment at the head of the table while Corrigan read back the previous testimony in his flat student's voice, and inside Voss the cold click began to spread, patient as frost through a wall.

He had burned the records office. He had burned the parish registers, the eviction orders, the requisition dockets, the commissary ledgers showing where the cleared grain had gone and into whose granaries. Nine days of burning, done properly, chimney by chimney, and the commission convened to produce the official account — his account — of what had happened at Marrow's Bend. And now it appeared that a dead mapmaker had built his own archive out of the very documents Voss was erasing, and left it, like a trap or a testament, to an apprentice.

"Aldous Crane," Voss said suddenly, into Corrigan's drone. "Enter into the record: the estate of the late Aldous Crane, surveyor, is to be considered material to the commission's inquiry. All papers, maps, instruments, and effects to be surrendered for examination."

Corrigan's pen paused. "All of it, sir? Even the personal—"

"All of it, Corrigan. A map is a document. A document is evidence. We cannot make findings on incomplete materials." He let the irony sit in his mouth like a coin. "Send soldiers to the print shop on Chandler's Lane. Today."

* * *

They came back at dusk with ash on their sleeves.

Not from the print shop — from the yard behind it, where the raiders had found the forge banked and the place empty, the plates gone from their racks, the strongbox standing open and cleaned out. The sergeant reported it in the tone men use for weather, and Voss listened with his hands folded, and made him repeat it twice, and then dismissed him and stood alone at the window while the lamplighter worked his way up the far side of the square with his pole and his little flame, lighting lamps for a town that was quietly emptying itself of truth.

Empty shop. Empty racks. An open strongbox.

An open strongbox meant a key, and a key meant preparation, and preparation meant the apprentice had known — had perhaps known since before the soldiers came — that this day would arrive and had planned for it the way Crane must have taught him: carefully, precisely, with everything drawn to scale.

And there was more. The sergeant, embarrassed, had brought back a deposition from the ostler at the coaching inn: a rolled survey map, requisitioned weeks ago among Crane's papers, returned by the army clerk Finch and found, when unrolled, to be heavier than a map had any business being. The ostler couldn't say why. The map itself was gone now — taken to divisional headquarters, logged, misfiled, lost in the ordinary way of things — but the ostler remembered the clerk's face when he handed it over. Remembered him sweating.

Finch. Tobias Finch, fourth clerk, records office, presumed dead in the fire until he turned up alive and asking awkward questions about transport manifests. Voss had let him run, thinking him vermin and beneath the boot. Vermin did not sweat over maps. Vermin did not conspire with apprentices and widows.

Widows. Ostler. Row Nine.

Voss assembled it in his mind the way he assembled anything: piece by piece, coldly, without appetite. An apprentice with the dead man's papers. A clerk with access to the requisition logs. A widow whom half the gallery had watched bite down on her tongue that morning. And somewhere in the middle of it, wrapped in oilcloth or sewn into a coat lining or rolled inside a survey map — the ledger. The names. Every eviction, every death, every official signature, preserved in the handwriting of a man who had known he was making a record and not merely keeping accounts.

He could feel the century turning beneath him like a tide. In nineteen days the year died, and with it, by quiet ministerial arrangement, the commission's mandate — its findings sealed, printed, shelved, done. Whatever entered the record before midnight on the thirty-first would stand forever. Whatever missed it would dissolve.

Voss picked up his cap.

"Corrigan."

The young man started. "Sir?"

"Witness lists for tomorrow. Strike the rest of the local names — we have sufficient testimony for our purposes. Adjournment pending review of documentary evidence." He settled the cap on his head. "And find me the duty roster for the north road and the river telegraph stations. Every station between here and the capital."

"Yes, sir." A hesitation, the pen hovering. "Sir — shall I note the reason for the adjournment?"

Voss looked at him. Outside, the last lamp caught and steadied, and the square lay bright and hard under it, and beyond the roofs the river ran black toward the sea, carrying — he was suddenly certain of it, with the certainty of the click and the frost and the turning tide — carrying his problem downstream even now.

"Note," Voss said, "that the commission awaits materials essential to a just and complete finding."

Corrigan wrote it down. Of course he did. They always wrote it down; that was the whole genius and horror of it — every lie wanted a ledger of its own.

Voss went out into the cold to give his orders. Nineteen days. One river. One apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a dead man's truth in his keeping.

He would be at the capital before the year turned. He would burn the last page of the old century himself, and no one — not Crane's ghost, not the widow, not the whole river — would stop him.
