## 8. Winter Road

The road north of Marrow's Bend had no name on any map Cyrus Vane had ever drawn, and he resented it for that. It ran between hedgerows crusted with rime, climbing through country that grew steadily sterner — soft orchard ground giving way to grey stone and black pine — as if the land itself were hardening against them the farther they went from the river.

Maren Ostler walked three paces ahead, as she had done every day since Finch's arrest, her breath pluming white, her bundle of belongings strapped across her back like a soldier's pack. She did not look back to see if he followed. She never did. She simply assumed he would, and so far he always had.

The ledger rode against his own chest, sewn into the false bottom of the map case Crane had carried for thirty years. He could feel it there even through wool and oilcloth — a small rectangular weight, lighter than a brick, heavier than a coffin ought to be. Eleven hundred names. He had counted them by candlelight in a barn outside Harrow's Cross while Maren slept with her boots on, and he had wept over perhaps forty of them before his eyes failed him. He did not tell her that in the morning.

They made nine miles that day, which was not enough. The capital lay better than two hundred miles north, and every mile of railway was now watched; Finch's capture at the ticket office of Wetherby Junction had seen to that. So they walked, two figures in a winter landscape, moving by lanes and drovers' roads, sleeping where hay was sold cheaply and questions were asked slowly.

At dusk they came down into a village strung along a frozen stream, its chimneys smoking like slow thoughts. Maren stopped at the edge of it and swayed, just slightly — a motion so small Cyrus might have imagined it — and then straightened and walked on.

"You're limping," he said.

"I'm walking," she said. "There's a difference."

He let it go. He was learning, with Maren, which silences were doors and which were walls.

* * *

Maren Ostler had buried two children and a husband, and she knew the geography of illness the way other women knew the geography of market towns. She felt this one arriving days before it landed: a heat behind the eyes, an ache in the hinge of the jaw, a tiredness that sleep did not touch but deepened, like water finding a cellar. Her mother had called it *the visitor*. Her mother had died of it, one February, in a cart on the road out of Row Nine while soldiers burned the family's papers on the verge.

She said nothing. Saying something would make it real, and there were still a hundred miles between here and the capital, and Cyrus already watched her too closely.

That night they lodged above a wheelwright's shop, paying with the last of Finch's careful hoard of notes. The room was narrow and smelled of linseed and cold iron. Maren sat on the pallet nearest the window while Cyrus spread the atlas plates on the floor and studied them by candle stub, the way he did every night, tracing their route with a blunt pencil as though the right line drawn often enough might carry them there.

"The northern road joins the coach way at Stony Ford," he murmured. "Two days if the snow holds off. From the ford there are carriers who'll take passengers as far as Thornhill without asking names."

"And after Thornhill?"

"After Thornhill," he said, "I haven't the faintest idea."

She almost smiled. It was one of the things she had come, reluctantly, to trust about him — that he admitted ignorance the way other men admitted nothing at all. Aldous Crane had been the same. She remembered the old mapmaker coming to Row Nine in the year before the clearances, measuring fields with his chain and his theodolite, kind in the absentminded way of men who did not yet know what their measurements would be used for.

"Cyrus." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how thin it sounded. "If I slow you down—"

"You won't."

"If I do. You must take the ledger and go."

He looked up from the plates. The candlelight moved on his face and made him seem very young, which he was, and very stubborn, which he also was. "Maren. The ledger is half yours. More than half. Those are your people in its pages. If you think I'd carry your dead to the capital and leave you bleeding in a ditch beside them, then you've understood nothing about me these eight weeks, and I've wasted my breath on every word."

She turned to the window because there was nowhere else to put her face. Snow was beginning again, fine as sifted flour. Behind her she heard him roll the plates and blow out the candle, and then the creak of the second pallet taking his weight, and then nothing but wind.

*The visitor,* her mother had called it. Maren lay awake and felt it settling in her chest like a tenant who intended to stay.

* * *

Cyrus woke to the sound of coughing.

It came from the window pallet, rhythmic and deep, the cough of someone hauling something heavy up out of themselves. By the time he got the candle lit Maren was sitting upright, grey-faced, her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding a door shut from within.

"How long," he said. Not *are you ill* — that question had been answered. "How long."

"Since Harrow's Cross. Perhaps before." Her voice was scraped raw. "It will pass. It passed for my sister."

"It didn't pass for everyone."

"No," she agreed quietly. "Not for everyone."

He dressed and went down into the blue morning and found the wheelwright's wife, and paid too much for what she had: a jar of goose fat, a twist of paper containing some bitter green powder, a bottle of vinegar, half a loaf. He asked about a doctor and saw the answer in her face before she spoke it — none nearer than Stony Ford, and the roads snowed under.

When he came back up Maren had risen and dressed and packed her bundle, standing by the door with her chin up like a woman reporting for work. Something in Cyrus broke a little at the sight of it.

"We rest today," he said.

"We lose a day."

"Yes. We lose a day."

"And if a day is the difference—"

"Then we lose it anyway." He set the provisions on the table between them, more roughly than he meant to. "Maren, listen to me. I spent four years learning that a map is only true if the surveyor lives long enough to finish it. You are carrying me to the capital as surely as I'm carrying that ledger. If you fall in the road, it doesn't arrive. Nothing arrives. So we rest, and we eat, and we walk slower, and we get there. Both of us. That is the whole of the plan now."

For a long moment she looked at him, and the coughing had stopped, and in the silence something passed between them that neither of them named — a thing too dangerous to name on a winter road with soldiers behind them and no certain future ahead, a thing that lived in the space between *I need you* and *I love you* and kept its own counsel there.

"All right," she said at last. "One day."

* * *

They lost more than one day. The fever climbed through the afternoon and by evening she could not stand without his arm under hers, and Cyrus sat through the night in the wheelwright's room boiling vinegar and water in a pot borrowed from downstairs, holding the steam to her face, changing cloths soaked cool on her forehead, listening to her breathe. The wheeze had moved into her lungs. He knew what that meant without wanting to know it. His mother had died of the same sound when he was nine; he had lain in the next bed listening to it all night, helpless, and here he was again, twenty-six years old and helpless in exactly the same way, with the century's greatest atlas of human knowledge folded on the floor at his feet and not one page of it worth anything at four in the morning in a sickroom.

Somewhere past dawn she slept — truly slept, not the shallow burning doze — and Cyrus sat by the window and let himself think the thought he had forbidden himself for days:

*Go now. Alone. Fast marches. You could be at Stony Ford by nightfall and on a carrier's cart tomorrow and in the capital in nine days, and she would forgive you, because she told you to go, she has told you twice.*

And underneath it, quieter, the counter-thought, which was not really an argument but a fact, the way a bearing is a fact: *There is no version of the man I intend to be who does that.*

He did not choose between speed and care. Care chose itself, the way water chooses the low ground, and he surrendered to it with something close to relief.

* * *

On the third morning she could walk. Slowly, stopping often, leaning on his arm along the frozen lane — but walking. The fever had broken in the night and left her weak as a new foal and furious about it, and her fury, Cyrus thought, was the best medicine in the wheelwright's entire inventory.

They reached Stony Ford at dusk on the fourth day, and there learned two things. The first was that the carrier Jem Holt would still take two quiet passengers to Thornhill, for triple the fare and no questions, leaving before light.

The second came from a newspaper blowing against the inn fence, plastered wet against the pickets. Cyrus peeled it free. On the third page, in print no larger than ants, was a notice: the Commission of Inquiry into the Orchard Clearances, sitting in the capital, had announced it would receive final evidence until the last day of the year. After which the record would be closed. Sealed. Settled.

*The last day of the year.* Nine days hence. Voss had not needed to chase them after all; he had only needed to wait behind a deadline.

Cyrus read it twice and handed it to Maren, whose hands were still unsteady, and watched her read it, and saw her jaw set against the coughing.

"Nine days," she said.

"Six hundred miles of it ours," Cyrus said. "Holt goes as far as Thornhill. After that—" He looked up the darkening road, toward the north, where somewhere beyond the pines and the snowfields and the watched stations the capital was lighting its lamps for the end of the century. "After that, we spend everything."

Behind them, far down the valley, a train whistle sounded — long, lonely, and going the wrong way. And on the inn's door, freshly pasted, Cyrus noticed a bill he had missed on entering: a sketch of a young man with a mapmaker's case, and beneath it a reward figure large enough to buy the inn itself.

*Wanted for questioning. Last seen heading north.*

Maren saw it too. She read it calmly, pulled her scarf higher, and took his arm.

"Well," she said. "Best not keep the gentleman waiting."
