## 9. The Century Turns

The capital smelled of tallow and river fog, of horse dung and roasting chestnuts, and beneath it all — faint as a watermark — of ink. Cyrus Vane had been in the city four days and he had not once slept through the night, but he had learned its streets the way he had once learned coastlines: by their bearing, their hazards, their hidden channels. Quill Street ran downhill toward the press-works like a river finding the sea, and at its bottom, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered chapel, stood the shop of Hesper & Dunn, Printers, whose sign hung by one chain and turned slowly in the cold wind off the water.

Maren Ostler stood beside him under the sign, her breath silvering the air, her cough finally quieted after three days of fever that had aged her ten years on the winter road. She still wore Finch's army greatcoat, dyed a bad black to hide the buttons.

"You're certain," she said. It was not a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere outside Duncarrow, when Cyrus had laid the ledger open on a tavern table and shown her Row Nine written in Aldous Crane's small, exact hand.

"No," Cyrus said. "But it's New Year's Eve, and the commission adjourns tonight, and after midnight every clerk in this city will have a fresh year's worth of excuses." He looked up at the swaying sign. "We print it now, or we never print it at all."

Inside, the shop was warm and thunderous. Presses ran even on holidays — the capital's appetite for broadsheets and almanacs did not observe the calendar — and the men who worked them were broad-handed, ink-stained, and suspicious of strangers until Hesper himself, a squat man with spectacles thick as coach windows, read the letter of introduction Cyrus carried and went very still.

"This is Crane," Hesper said. "Aldous Crane. I bought type from this man twenty years ago. He argued with me about kerning for an hour."

"He died in October," Cyrus said. "I was his apprentice. The atlas is his. The appendix—" He put his hand flat on the satchel, on the shape of the ledger beneath the canvas. "The appendix is his too. He just didn't live long enough to bind it."

Hesper looked from the satchel to Cyrus's face, and whatever he saw there decided him. He took off his apron and put it back on again, as if girding himself.

"Set it out," he said. "All of it. And you'll tell me what we're printing while I read it, because if soldiers come through that door I want to know exactly what I'm going to hell for."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the city outside grew louder and drunker. The atlas plates came out of their oilcloth wrappings one at a time — Marrow's Bend and the river country, drawn in Crane's patient line over thirty years, corrected and re-corrected by Cyrus's own hand on the road north. Hesper's compositors set the appendix last: the ledger entire, every name, every date, every cause of death transcribed without amendment. *Infant, exposure, Row Eleven. Hanged, adjudged riotous; no inquest held. Shot by escort, listed as fever.* Page after page of it, the official omissions made flesh in ink.

Maren read the proofs standing at the compositor's elbow, her lips moving. When she reached her husband's name she did not weep. She touched the letters once, lightly, the way one touches a sleeping child to confirm the breath, and then she rolled up her sleeves and asked where they needed hands.

Cyrus found he could not stop checking the windows.

Every hour or so a bell rang somewhere in the city, and each time his heart lurched. Voss would have arrived by now — Voss had resources, warrants, and a soldier's contempt for distance. Cyrus had left Duncarrow with a two-day lead and spent half of it doubling back for Maren when she collapsed on the towpath, and he knew — he knew the way one knows weather coming — that the lead was gone. What remained was only whether the thing could be finished before the door came open.

At dusk, Hesper's boy ran in with news from the corner: soldiers at the Magistrate's Court, six streets up, and a captain asking questions about print shops.

"How many shops on this street?" Cyrus asked.

Hesper smiled without warmth. "Nine. And every one of them owes me money or I owe them, and none of them loves the army." He blew out the lamp above the door. "It'll take him a while to knock on nine doors on the loudest night of the year."

* * *

The riot began, as such things do, over almost nothing.

A customs officer had come at eight o'clock to collect a holiday levy from the taverns on Quill Street — a new assessment, tripled since autumn, part of the victor's commission's quiet program of rewarding itself. By nine there were arguments. By ten there were fiddlers playing defiantly out of tune and a crowd of printers' men, dockhands, and year-end drunks shoulder to shoulder in the street, and someone threw a bottle, and someone else threw a chair, and by half past ten the officer's watchmen had waded in with staves and the whole street had become one heaving, singing, cursing animal.

From the press-room window Cyrus watched torchlight run along the rooftops like fire along a fuse.

"It's not for us," Maren said beside him. Her face was lit orange and blue by the flames below. "They don't know what we're carrying."

"No," Cyrus said. "But they're buying us the hour anyway." He thought of Crane, who had believed history happened elsewhere, in capitals, to other people — and here it was happening on this street, made by people who would never appear in any ledger, furious over a tax and a hard year and a hundred grievances with no names attached. Perhaps that was how history always actually happened. Not by design. By accumulation. By pressure finding its hour.

Hesper appeared behind them, wiping his hands. "Last signature is drying. Binding crew's at the wire-stitcher now. Forty copies tonight — the plates can run more when it's safe, but forty is what fits before the bells." He paused. "There's a question you should answer before they're done, lad. Who's name goes on the title page?"

"Aldous Crane," Cyrus said. It was the easiest decision he had ever made. "It's his atlas. His testimony. I finished it; I didn't write it."

"And the appendix?"

"His name also. He gathered the dead. Let him carry them."

Maren made a small sound beside him, and Cyrus did not look at her, because if he did he would not be able to keep working.

* * *

The magistrate came at eleven.

She came unannounced, in plain clothes, through the riot's backwash — a narrow woman of sixty named Ilse Verhoeven, whom Hesper greeted with the wary courtesy of a man greeting a debt collector. She sat in the office among the ink smell and the noise and read the appendix proofs for a full twenty minutes without speaking, while Maren stood rigid in the corner and Cyrus's pulse counted down the century.

Then she set the pages down, aligned their edges precisely, and looked up.

"I sit on the circuit bench at Assizes," she said. "Three of my clearance-era rulings were reversed by the commission this year. Reversed on dates, Mr. Vane. On *dates*. Deaths moved backward into years when the parties were already amenable to settlement." She tapped the proof. "This ledger says what my own records used to say before the records office burned. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I need a magistrate more than I need a printer," Cyrus said.

"That you have one." She rose, buttoning her coat. "The statute requires deposition before publication for matters touching public record. I will take yours, the widow's, and the apprentice-clerk's, here and now, witnessed and sealed. Then, when the bells ring, your atlas enters the world lawfully — deposited, catalogued, and copy-filed with the Crown Archive by morning cart. They can burn a bookshop, Captain Voss. They cannot burn a deposit."

"The archive closes at ten on holidays," Maren said sharply.

Verhoeven allowed herself the thinnest smile. "The night clerk owes me a ruling on his brother's tenancy. It closes when I say it closes."

* * *

They gave their depositions by lamplight while the presses groaned below like something alive trying to be born. Cyrus spoke his name, his master's name, the provenance of the ledger, the burning of the records office, the requisition, the hostages Voss had taken at Marrow's Bend — and heard his own voice steady in a way that surprised him. Maren spoke of Row Nine in a voice like a blade being drawn slowly. Finch's deposition, written in his precise clerk's hand before his capture and smuggled out sewn into Maren's coat lining, Verhoeven read aloud into the record herself, her mouth tightening around each falsified date.

At eleven-forty, the last stitched copy came up from the press-room floor: dark boards, cloth spine, the title stamped in gold — *An Atlas of the River Country and Its Orchards: Being the Survey of Aldous Crane, Completed by His Apprentice* — and bound into its appendix, forty pages deep, the ledger entire.

Verhoeven signed the deposition, sealed it, and wrote the deposit docket. "Cart leaves for the archive at midnight sharp," she said. "It goes in the first load."

That was when the knocking started.

Not on the street door — the riot had seen to it that no door on Quill Street opened quietly — but at the yard entrance, hard and rhythmic and military, boot butts on wood. Hesper's boy came tumbling up the stairs white-faced. "Soldiers in the yard! A captain — he's got the yard gate down—"

Cyrus took the atlas off the table.

"What are you doing?" Maren's hand closed on his sleeve.

"There's a back stair to the alley. Hesper—"

"I know my own shop, lad. Go."

He went. Maren with him, Verhoeven sweeping the deposition into her satchel, down the narrow stair into the frozen alley where their breath hung like signal smoke. Behind them the yard door splintered. Ahead, at the alley's mouth, the riot still burned and sang, torches and silhouettes, a whole street refusing to go quietly into the next hundred years.

They were halfway to the alley's mouth when the voice came behind them, carrying over the noise with parade-ground clarity:

"VANE!"

Cyrus turned. Captain Ellery Voss filled the broken doorway, sword drawn, two soldiers behind him, his face lit from below by the fires on the street — and it was not rage in that face, Cyrus saw. It was arithmetic. A man calculating whether forty copies existed yet, whether a deposition existed at all, whether one sprint down an alley could still undo everything.

"Drop it," Voss called. "Drop the case, and the widow walks home free, and we discuss this like men of property."

"The property isn't yours," Cyrus said — and ran.

They burst into the crowd on Quill Street and the crowd closed around them like water. Torches, song, the crush of bodies; Maren's grip on his wrist; the atlas hugged to his chest under Finch's stolen coat. Behind them Voss's soldiers shoved into the throng and the throng, primed all night, shoved back with interest. A bottle sailed overhead. A watchman's whistle shrieked. Somewhere a fiddle struck up faster.

Then the bells began.

Not one bell — all of them, every tower in the capital, ragged at first and then together, a wall of bronze rolling over the rooftops, drowning thought, drowning fear, drowning the century itself as it died. Twelve strokes. Cyrus stood still in the middle of the roaring street, pressed against Maren, the atlas between their bodies like a shared heartbeat, and counted them in.

On the twelfth stroke, the midnight cart pulled away from the head of Quill Street toward the Crown Archive, Verhoeven aboard, the deposit docket in her lap, the depositions sealed, the first copies wrapped and loaded.

Behind them, in the sudden hush between the last bell and the cheering, Cyrus heard a new sound: boots in formation, many of them, coming down the hill. Officers' voices. An order given in a tone he recognized — not Voss's.

Through the crowd he glimpsed them: a file of military police with lanterns, moving with terrible unhurried purpose straight toward the broken yard door where Ellery Voss stood alone, sword still drawn, his soldiers scattered in the mob. A colonel at their head held papers. Even at forty yards, Cyrus could guess what the papers were: word had traveled faster than any cart. A burned records office, falsified dates, a hunted ledger — and now a public deposit naming names, which meant inquiry, which meant the commission's superiors needing someone small and expendable to stand in front of the scandal like a sandbag against a flood.

Voss saw them coming. Cyrus watched the arithmetic leave his face all at once, like light going out of a window.

The captain turned to run — and the crowd, which did not know what he had done but knew a soldier's coat and a drawn sword on a night of fires, closed around him too.

Maren's fingers tightened on Cyrus's wrist. "It's done," she said, and her voice broke on it, and the bells' echo was still trembling in the stones. "Cyrus. It's done."

He looked east, where the cart lamps shrank toward the archive through the smoke, and found he could not answer. Because it was done — the truth was in motion now, beyond any captain or colonel to recall — and yet standing in the dying year with the atlas safe against his chest, Cyrus felt no triumph at all. Only the strange lightness of a man who has set down a weight he has carried so long he cannot remember his own posture without it.

Somewhere in the crowd, Voss was shouting his own rank at men who had stopped listening. Somewhere across the river, the archive clerk was breaking his seal. And somewhere in Cyrus's breast pocket lay Finch's last letter from Duncarrow, unopened since the arrest — the one line folded outward, the one sentence Finch had underlined twice:

*Whatever happens to me, finish it — and then come back. There are witnesses in Marrow's Bend who will only speak to a face they know.*

The new century's first fireworks went up over the river, gold and green against the smoke, and Cyrus turned to Maren with the words already forming: *we go home.*
