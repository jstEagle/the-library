## 10. Scale and Bearing

The apprentices arrive in autumn, when the light through the workshop windows goes the colour of weak tea and the smell of ink settles into everything like a second climate. There are four of them this year — three boys and a girl named Sable who asks more questions in an hour than the other three ask in a month. Cyrus likes her immediately and refuses to show it.

"Master Vane," she says on her first morning, holding up a survey plate of the eastern coast as though it had personally offended her, "this shoreline is wrong. The chart in the schoolroom shows a bay here."

"The chart in the schoolroom is eleven years old," Cyrus says, "and was drawn by a man who never saw the coast."

"Then which one is true?"

Cyrus takes the plate from her, turns it to the window, lets the light fall through the engraved lines. "Neither. A map is not a mirror, girl. It is an argument. Every line it draws, it draws for a reason, and every reason belongs to somebody. Your first lesson is to find out whose."

She writes that down. He watches her do it, her pen moving with the fierce diligence of someone who has decided, in advance, that everything will matter. He was like that once. In some ways he still is. But there are things he knows now that cannot be taught at a bench, and he spends most days deciding how much of them to teach anyway.

* * *

The atlas circulates. That is the plain fact of it now, ordinary as weather: *The Marrow's Bend Atlas of Aldous Crane*, second printing, third, fifth, sold in shops from the capital's university quarter to river towns whose names Cyrus once drew without ever expecting to stand in them. The appendix — forty pages of ledger transcribed into Crane's careful, upright hand, or rather into Cyrus's best imitation of it — is bound into every copy, unremarkable as an index until someone reads it.

And people read it. Not everyone. Not even most. But a clerk here, a magistrate there, a widow in a distant town who finds her own family name on page thirty-one and writes Cyrus a letter with a hand that shakes so badly the address is barely legible. Some officials fell — two commissioners resigned within the year, one was tried, one drowned himself in the very river Cyrus and Maren had come down in the dark with the plates wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. Most did not. Most grew old comfortably, attended funerals, were spoken of as difficult men but men nonetheless. Justice, Cyrus learned, is a cartographer too: it surveys what it can reach and leaves the rest blank, and calls the blank space mercy, or pragmatism, depending on who is holding the pen.

Voss did not hang. Cyrus had wanted — he will admit it only to himself, in the hour before sleep, in the honest dark — something operatic, some public accounting with the man standing in the dock while the names were read aloud. Instead there had been tribunals, quiet ones, and a dishonourable discharge printed on paper no thicker than a love letter, and Voss walking out of the capital garrison with his coat over his arm and his head high, the way men walk when they have decided that history will judge them differently and intend to wait it out. Cyrus heard years later that he kept a shop somewhere east, selling surveying instruments. It figured. He had always been good with instruments and bad with what they measured.

* * *

Maren plants the orchard in the third spring after the turn of the century, on land the restitution board finally — grudgingly, with paperwork that took fourteen months and cost her half her savings — returned to Row Nine's survivors. She sends word by letter, short as all her letters are, and Cyrus travels out to see it because there are things a letter cannot carry.

The land is poor at first glance. That is its deception; Maren tells him the soil under the scrub is blacker than any in the county, that the old trees' roots went deep before the clearances, that what grows from ground where orchards stood remembers, somehow, that it is meant to be an orchard again. She has planted forty saplings in rows that follow no surveyor's grid but the ghost-lines of the original nine rows, and she walks him along them in the evening with her shawl pulled tight, naming each tree for a family. Row One for the Ostlers. Row Nine for the children, eleven of them, none older than twelve, whose deaths the official record had listed as *fever, various*.

"Fever," Maren says, stopping at the last sapling. She does not spit. She stopped spitting years ago; she says contempt ages a woman faster than grief. "I could almost admire it, you know. The nerve of it. Fever, various. As though death itself could be made vague if you wrote it small enough."

"It wasn't small," Cyrus says. "You made sure."

"We made sure." She looks at him then, and her eyes have not softened exactly — Maren Ostler does not soften, she weathers — but there is something in them that took eleven years to grow and could not have grown alone. "Say it properly, mapmaker. You were there."

"We made sure." The words feel strange and right in his mouth, like a bearing finally fixed.

They stand together while the light fails over her rows. He wants to say the thing neither of them has said — has never said, not on the winter road when her fever burned and he sat up three nights deciding whether to spend their lead on medicine or distance, not in the capital doorway on the last morning when she shook his hand like a colleague and held it one second longer than a colleague would. He opens his mouth. She reaches over and adjusts his collar, briskly, the way a sister would, or a comrade, or something that has no clean name in any language either of them speaks.

"You'll come back in spring," she says. It is not a question. "When it blossoms. Somebody should see it who knows what it cost."

"Yes," Cyrus says. "I'll come back in spring."

He does not say the other thing. Some arguments are better left mapped than spoken.

* * *

In the workshop, winter coming on, Sable finds the drawer.

It is the old desk — Crane's desk, which Cyrus inherited with the lease and the tools and the debt, and which he has kept all these years with its locked drawer intact, less from reverence than from a superstitious unwillingness to force anything open that his master had chosen to close. He told the apprentices nothing about it. He told them about Crane the way the atlas told about Crane: the great surveyor, the meticulous hand, the man who died before his masterpiece was done. The rest — the ledger hidden inside the drawer, the night Cyrus spent with a lamp and a cold cup of tea learning what his quiet, exacting master had actually been doing all those evenings he claimed to be reworking coastal soundings — the rest he folded away, and locked, and kept.

But locks mean nothing to a girl like Sable. She comes to him at closing time with the little brass key she found taped beneath the middle drawer, held out on her palm, and her face full of the particular shame of a person caught being curious.

"I didn't open it," she says. "I want you to know that. I found the key and I brought it to you instead. But I want to know why it's locked. Master Vane — I've read your appendix. I've read it four times. I know what he hid in ledgers. So what's in this one?"

Cyrus takes the key. It is warm from her hand. Through the window behind her the town is lighting its lamps one by one, and beyond the town the hills go dark, and beyond the hills the road runs south toward Marrow's Bend, where he has not been in eleven years and where, he heard just last month from a travelling bookseller, the commission's replacement is finally reopening the inquiry — new witnesses coming forward, old documents surfacing, a clerk's testimony, decades late, being taken seriously at last. The record stands. That was always the point. And yet.

"There's a copy in there," Cyrus says. "Of everything. My master's original went into the world wearing his name, and it did its work. This is my hand — my transcription, made the year I was twenty-two and afraid of my own shadow. I kept it because I thought someday I'd need proof of what I'd done." He weighs the key in his palm. "Turns out nobody ever demanded proof. The book itself was enough. So the drawer stayed shut, and every year I thought I'd open it, and every year I didn't, and now—"

"Now?" Sable says.

"Now there's an inquiry sitting in Marrow's Bend again, and they're asking for original documentation, and my hand is the oldest living hand on that ledger." He slides the key into the lock. It turns with a small, clean sound, like a sentence ending — or beginning. "Your second lesson, apprentice: a record isn't finished when it's published. It's finished when the last person who can vouch for it stops breathing. I'm not done yet."

He draws the drawer open. Inside, flat and pale in the lamplight, lies the ledger, exactly as he left it, waiting the way paper waits — patiently, implacably, for hands.

Sable leans in. "What is it?"

Cyrus lifts it out and sets it on the bench between them, opens the cover, and turns to the first page of names.

"That," he says, "is an argument. And tomorrow we're going to answer it."

* * *

That night he writes to Maren. Four lines only, in the hand she once teased him for — *a surveyor's fist*, she called it, *all bearings and no tenderness*. He tells her the inquiry is reopened. He tells her they are asking for originals. He tells her he will travel south within the month, ledger and atlas both packed in the same case, the way they always should have travelled.

Then he sits a long while with the pen dry in his fist, looking at the fourth line he has not yet written, the one that says *come with me* — and at last, deliberately, he writes instead: *Row Nine should be represented. Blossom or no.*

Her reply arrives eight days later, brief as all her letters, in a hand that shakes only slightly:

*I was always going to. Pack for two roads — theirs and ours.*
