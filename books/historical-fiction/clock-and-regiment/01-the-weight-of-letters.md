## 1. The Weight of Letters

The parcels came to Orsen Winterbourne the way confessions come to a priest: sealed, trusting, and never meant for their eyes.

They stood in the Marchmonts' morning room with a brass scale and a ledger of postage, weighing the day's correspondence in the grey light that came through windows which had not been washed since Michaelmas because Mrs. Hobbes, the housekeeper, held that washing windows before Easter invited misfortune. Orsen held that it invited nothing but clearer views of the street, which was its own kind of misfortune, but they kept this opinion where they kept everything else—folded small and carried inward.

Three pounds, four ounces. The parcel was bound in brown paper and tied with hemp string, addressed to a Miss E. Whitlock in Clerkenwell in handwriting that pretended very hard to be a clerk's hand. Orsen set it on the scale, watched the needle settle, wrote the figure in the ledger with their left hand though they were naturally right-handed—a small deliberate crookedness, so that no investigator could ever match their script to anything—and slid the parcel to the bottom of the mail basket.

Then they did what they always did.

Not to all of it. Not carelessly, not greedily. A letter from a cousin about a legacy could pass unread; Orsen had learned to know them by the shape of their envelopes, the franking, the fat little confidence of family news. But anything with a second seal beneath the first, anything addressed in a hand that didn't match the return direction, anything that arrived on a Tuesday when it might as easily have come on a Friday—those went into Orsen's coat pocket and down the back stairs to the room behind the coal store, where a lamp burned behind a curtain of sacking and no one asked why.

It had begun, three years ago, as an act of housekeeping. That was how Orsen thought of it still. The Marchmont household kept dangerous company—not dangerously in the way of scandal, which the family would have relished, but dangerously in the way of prison sentences and broken windows and men with notebooks loitering outside the gate. Mrs. Leonora Marchmont wrote and received letters that, if read by the wrong eyes in the wrong order, could assemble themselves into a case. The cause she served—the vote, the franchise, the plain and infuriating proposition that women were citizens—had enemies who did not argue; they collected. And a household that did not know what its own letters said was a household walking through a field with its eyes shut, waiting for the wire.

So Orsen read. Steam over a kettle for the adhesives that resisted a thin blade; patience for the rest. They read every word twice, once as an enemy would and once as a friend, and then they resealed what they opened so cleanly that even Mrs. Hobbes, who could detect a teaspoon moved an inch from its place, never once suspected.

In three years, Orsen had betrayed nothing. Not to the police who called, politely, twice a year; not to the cousin in Shoreditch whose loyalty was for sale at the price of a pub lunch; not even to the Marchmonts themselves, who believed—Orsen needed them to believe—that their driver was a quiet young person of no curiosity whatsoever, hired on Mr. Pettifer's recommendation, paid thirty shillings a week plus board, and grateful.

Grateful was true. Quiet was true. No curiosity was the only lie in the ledger, and Orsen had made peace with it the way one makes peace with a limp: it was theirs, it cost them something daily, and it let them walk.

* * *

Below stairs, past the kitchen where Mrs. Hobbes ruled with a wooden spoon and a memory like a bailiff's book, there was a door that most of the household had forgotten and the rest pretended to.

It looked like nothing: a green-painted door with a hinge that shrieked if you didn't lift it as you opened, opening onto a short brick passage that smelled of coke and damp, ending in the old wine cellar that Mr. Marchmont had emptied during his brief and disastrous enthusiasm for teetotalism. He had never thought to ask what became of the room afterward. It was easier not to know, and the household had arranged itself around that easiness the way water arranges itself around a stone.

Orsen lifted the shrieking hinge, ducked under the lintel, and went down.

The lamp was already lit—Nell Tripp was down here, then—and the cellar resolved out of the dark in its familiar pieces: the small hand-press they'd bought from a bankrupt jobbing printer in Southwark, bolted to a slab of slate so its thumping would read, to anyone listening overhead, as pipes settling. The cabinet of type in its shallow trays, each compartment a little city of lead letters. The ream of cheap paper, the pots of ink, the drying line where pamphlets hung like strange laundry. And Nell herself, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up with two pencils, setting type with her lip caught between her teeth in the concentration of a woman doing surgery by candlelight.

"You're late," Nell said, without looking up. "Mrs. M's been down since quarter past."

"Postage took longer. Somebody's discovered registered parcels."

"Somebody'll discover a slap if she keeps me waiting." But there was no edge in it. There was never any edge in it, not down here. Orsen hung their cap on the nail that had held a ham hook in some previous life of the room and came to stand at Nell's shoulder, reading the composed stick of type upside down and backwards, as fluently now as they read a newspaper forwards.

*THE VOTE IS A TOOL. A TOOL MUST BE SHARED OR IT RUSTS IN THE HAND THAT HOARDS IT.*

"Mrs. Marchmont's?"

"Her own words. She rang down the change herself." Nell tilted her head. "Well—Cressida rang it down, pretending to be her mother, which I told her is a hanging offence in some counties, and she said good, something ought to be."

Orsen smiled despite themselves. Cressida Marchmont, twenty-two years old and impatient as a kettle, chafed against every wall of her life including the ones built to protect her. She wanted to be at the front of the marches; her mother wanted her nowhere near them; the compromise was that Cressida ran messages and made speeches to audiences of trusted women in drawing rooms, and wrote inflammatory prose under a man's name that fooled nobody, which was rather the point.

The press thumped. The room smelled of ink and oil and the cold earth smell that cellars keep no matter what you do with them. Above them, faintly, the household moved through its morning—doors, footsteps, the muffled thunder of Cressida on the stairs, a person entirely incapable of descending quietly—and Orsen felt the particular contentment that always caught them unawares down here: the feeling of being inside a thing rather than beside it.

They had been beside things all their life. Workhouses and delivery yards and the backs of other people's houses; always the servant entrance, always the trade entrance, always the door that exists for people who carry things. They had learned to be furniture, and furniture is safe and furniture is nothing, and there were years—younger years, thinner years—when being nothing had seemed the whole available ambition.

And then Mrs. Marchmont had looked at them across the hall of this house, three years ago, on the day they were hired, and said: *You will see a great deal coming and going, Mr.—?*

*Winterbourne,* they had said. *Orsen Winterbourne.*

*You will see a great deal, Mr. Winterbourne, and I must ask you to forget it.*

*I've a good memory for forgetting, ma'am.*

She had laughed—one short syllable, surprised out of her—and something in Orsen had quietly changed address that day, moving from lodging to residence without giving notice.

* * *

By eleven the morning post was sorted, weighed, entered, and ready for the afternoon collection, except for the one letter that wasn't.

It sat in Orsen's coat pocket now, riding against their ribs like a small cold animal. Cream paper, expensive, addressed to Mrs. Marchmont in a copperplate hand that was too beautiful to be natural—no one's everyday writing looked like that; hands like that belonged to engravers and forgers. The seal was wax, deep red, stamped with a device Orsen didn't recognize: not a crest they knew, not the monogram of any of the family's correspondents. Something geometric. A circle within a square, or a square within a circle; the impression was slightly smudged, as if the stamp had been pressed by someone unfamiliar with it.

New seals were rare. New seals in copperplate were rarer. New seals, copperplate, and a Tuesday arrival combined into a small arithmetic that Orsen had learned to do without wanting to.

They waited until the household sat down to luncheon—cold mutton, and Mrs. Hobbes in a rare temper about a butcher's bill—and then they went down the back stairs with the letter and the lamp and the little tin of tools they kept wrapped in an oilcloth behind the sacking: the flat blade, the tongs, the beeswax stick coloured to match.

Steam first. The flap gave reluctantly, as if the glue resented being hurried. Orsen unfolded the single sheet and read.

*Madam,—*

*Certain parties have taken an interest in your establishment and its connections. We are aware of the presses you believe concealed. We are aware of the persons who frequent your house under colour of employment. Within the month, an event will occur in London upon which public attention will fix, and when it does, certain documents will come to light placing your name and your daughter's at the centre of it. These documents are already prepared. Whether they are used depends entirely upon whether your presses fall silent and your associations dissolve before that date.*

*Consider what a court of law makes of pamphlets and printers. Consider what a jury makes of a daughter.*

*No reply is expected. None would be read.*

There was no signature. The seal was pressed again below the fold—the same geometric device, cleaner this time, and Orsen bent close and understood, with a lurch, that it wasn't smudged at all. It was deliberately imperfect. A seal made to look amateurish, so that any future inquiry would conclude it was the work of a crank, a hoaxer, a malicious correspondent unworthy of investigation.

Which meant whoever wrote it had thought about inquiries. Had planned for them. Had prepared, in advance, the explanation that would make this letter worthless as evidence.

Orsen read it again. Then a third time, while the lamp hissed and the coal shifted somewhere in the scuttle above and the household ate its cold mutton twenty feet overhead, laughing at something—Mr. Marchmont's laugh, warm and booming, a man who still believed the world was fundamentally reasonable.

An event will occur. Documents already prepared. Your name and your daughter's.

They were not threatening to expose the pamphlets. Anyone could expose the pamphlets; Mrs. Marchmont half-wished they would, she'd say her piece in any dock in England. This was different. This was a promise to *place* the Marchmonts at the centre of something—something that would fix public attention, something violent enough, presumably, to warrant a court and a jury and the word *documents* spoken in that careful plural.

A bombing, Orsen thought, and their own mind supplied it unasked, the way it supplied routes and timetables and the faces of strangers who stood too long outside gates. There had been bombs. There would be more; the papers were hungry for them, the government hungrier still. And a frame needed no bomb at all—only paper, only ink, only a prepared narrative and a household too proud to run.

The letter lay on the slate slab in the lamplight, cream and elegant and utterly poisonous.

Orsen's hands were steady. That surprised them. Inside, something was moving fast, running down corridors, checking doors—but the hands were steady, the breath was even, the face they showed the empty room was calm, because a face is a habit like any other and they had practiced this one for years.

Options presented themselves in order, like clerks at a counter.

Burn it. Tell no one. The threat depended on silence working both ways; perhaps it was a bluff; perhaps the presses could go quiet for a month and resume after; perhaps—

Perhaps Cressida's name on a charge. Perhaps Mrs. Marchmont in the dock, her life's work converted by a prosecutor into evidence of conspiracy. Perhaps the household scattered, the workshop raided, Nell arrested at her sewing machine, the whole small warm architecture of Orsen's one belonging dismantled in a courtroom while they stood at the back with their cap in their hands, furniture again, nothing again, watching.

No.

The word arrived without drama. It simply arrived, the way dawn arrives, and Orsen found they had already begun thinking practically: the letter could not be destroyed—it might someday matter, its paper and watermark and postmark, all of it mattered—but neither could it go upstairs steamed and resealed and untouched, because to carry this knowledge alone across the threshold of that dining-room laughter was more than invisibility required. Invisibility had rules. It protected the family from the world. It had never before been asked to protect the family from the world *and* keep secrets *from* them in the same hour.

They copied the letter out anyway—in the crooked left hand, twice, one copy to go into the lining of their coat where Nell's needles could make a proper pocket of it—and then they resealed the original with the tinted wax, pressing it flat with the tongs warmed at the lamp, matching the amateur imperfection as best they could, and stood a long moment in the lamplight holding the envelope that weighed, on the brass scale of the world, almost nothing at all.

Above, chairs scraped. Luncheon was ending. Cressida's footsteps crossed the landing above the coal store, quick and light, pausing—Orsen froze—pausing, then moving on.

They climbed the back stairs with the letter in their pocket and their heart going like the press below, thump and draw, thump and draw, and they understood, standing in the passage with the shrieking hinge silent behind them, that a line had been crossed that could not be uncrossed. Three years of reading had been housekeeping. This was something else. This was enlistment.

The afternoon post went out at two o'clock, forty-one items, none of them remarkable. Orsen drove the trap to the district office through a fine drizzle, handed the basket over the counter, touched their cap to the sorter, and drove home the long way, along the river, because the long way bought ten minutes to think and thinking had become urgent work.

At the printer's alley—their last call, collecting a proof from old Wexford's shop—they noticed the man.

He stood at the mouth of the alley in a rain-darkened coat, too still for idleness, reading nothing. When Orsen clattered past he turned his head with the unhurried precision of a clock hand, and his eyes did not follow the horse or the trap.

They followed Orsen.

Orsen did not look back. They had a rule about looking back, and besides, they had memorized him already: heavy brows, soft build, boots too good for his coat, a notebook-shaped bulge in the left pocket. They turned at the corner, unhurried, and began composing in their head the sentence they would say that evening, down the shrieking-hinged stairs, to the two people below who had to be told and had to be told carefully:

Something has changed. Someone has posted a date to our ruin. And we have weeks—not months, not seasons—weeks to find out who is forging a noose for this family, and cut the rope before it reaches any neck.

The drizzle thickened. The lamps of London came on early, one by one, like a slow decision. Orsen drove into the gathering dusk with the weight of letters on their lap and the weight of everything else in their chest, invisible as ever to every eye on the street—and for the first time in three years, entirely unsure whether invisibility was still protection, or had become, instead, the perfect place from which to begin a war.
