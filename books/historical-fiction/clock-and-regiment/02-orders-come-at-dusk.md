## 2. Orders Come at Dusk

The Daimler drank petrol the way old men drank porter—slowly, expensively, and with a great deal of complaint—and Orsen Winterbourne knew its moods better than they knew their own. By four o'clock the light had begun to fail over Belgravia, sliding off the stucco fronts in long copper sheets, and Orsen was under the bonnet of the car with a rag and a spanner, tightening a fuel line that had developed a whisper of a leak. Not dangerous yet. Dangerous by Saturday, if left. Orsen did not leave things until they were dangerous. That was, in a sense, the whole of their employment.

"You'll ruin your gloves."

Mrs. Marchmont stood on the terrace step with her hands folded into one another, which was how she held herself when she had something to say that she preferred to deliver sideways rather than straight. She was fifty-one years old and dressed in the grey-violet of half-mourning she had never entirely abandoned since a sister's death eight years past, and she watched her driver work with an expression that gave away precisely nothing—except, perhaps, to someone who had spent three years learning to read the household's faces the way other people read newspapers.

"Gloves are for church and funerals, ma'am," Orsen said, sitting back on their heels. "Petrol doesn't respect either."

A breath of laughter, quickly folded away. Mrs. Marchmont came down the steps with the careful economy of a woman who had decided long ago that appearing frail was occasionally useful and never true. "You'll be going out this evening, then. The usual errand."

"The usual errand." It was how they spoke about it, the two of them, in a code so thin it was nearly transparent: parcels for the shop, papers to collect, nothing that would interest a magistrate.

"There's more than the usual tonight." She produced an envelope from her sleeve—not sealed, merely tucked—and held it without offering it. "Two stops before the printer's. And Orsen—" Her eyes came up, grey and level, and rested on their face with a weight that felt like being measured for a coat. "You take a long time with some letters. I've noticed you reading the post by lamplight in the morning room when you think the house is asleep."

The spanner went very still in Orsen's hand. Somewhere down the mews a cat knocked over a bottle, and the sound cracked the silence like a shot.

"I check for damage," Orsen said. "Water stains. Tampering. A driver who hands over a torn letter is a driver who gets blamed for it."

It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. Mrs. Marchmont looked at them for three full seconds longer than comfort allowed, and then she handed over the envelope and said, in exactly the tone she used for ordering flowers, "See that the second parcel goes round the back, as before," and turned and walked into the house, leaving Orsen kneeling on the gravel with their heart hammering like a misfiring cylinder.

She knew. Not everything—perhaps not even much—but she knew there was something to know, and she had chosen, this evening, to say so without saying it. Orsen turned the envelope over twice, found it ordinary, and understood that its entire purpose had been the sentence attached to it. *I see you. I have not stopped you.* In the Marchmont household, that was very nearly a decoration for valour.

* * *

The light was almost gone by the time the Daimler rolled south of the river, lamps lit, engine running smooth now that the line was tight. Orsen drove the way they did everything: unremarkably, precisely, like a person whose business was nobody's. That was the first rule of invisibility, learned young and paid for dearly—be dull, be prompt, be forgettable. A driver is furniture with hands. Furniture is not questioned.

The envelope in the breast pocket contained instructions in Mr. Marchmont's cramped hand: collect pamphlet proofs from Wren & Daughter in Cordwainer's Alley, off Blackfriars; deliver the enclosed packet to the second address; return by ten. Ordinary. Except that Mr. Marchmont had written *two* addresses where he usually wrote one, and the second was in Lambeth, in a street Orsen associated with a certain kind of lodging house and a certain quality of silence.

Cordwainer's Alley announced itself by smell before sight—ink and oil and coal smoke, the particular sharpness of molten lead from the type foundry two doors down. At this hour the alley should have been emptying, the last apprentices sent home, shutters half-drawn. Instead every window seemed to have an eye in it, and at the mouth of the alley, beneath the gas lamp whose mantel had been broken for a month (Orsen had reported it twice; the report itself had gone unanswered, which now seemed less like municipal neglect and more like an omen), stood two men in plain clothes doing an extremely poor impression of men not in plain clothes.

They were talking to each other, which constables on the beat never did—they walked alone or in pairs but always moving, always with somewhere to be. These two were planted. One was heavy, ruddy, with the collapsed-shoulder stance of a man who'd been in the ranks before the force made him a sergeant; his boots were wrong for plainclothes, polished to parade standard. The other was younger, narrow, and kept his right hand in his coat pocket in a way that suggested either cold or a notebook, and his eyes never stopped moving across the alley's mouths and windows. He had a small scar through one eyebrow, pale as a pencil mark. The older one chewed something steadily and spat into the gutter every forty seconds or so, regular as a clock.

Orsen brought the car to the kerb at a crawl, killed the lamp for the turn—perfectly legal at dusk, perfectly ordinary—and used those six seconds of dimness to fix both faces in place the way one memorises a route: landmarks, distances, relationships. *Ruddy, army boots, chews. Scar-left-brow, hand-in-pocket, watches high.* Names would come later, if names existed. Faces first. Faces could be matched to doorways later.

Wren & Daughter occupied the third premises on the left, behind a brass plate gone green with weather. Orsen carried in the empty crate for the pamphlet proofs and found old Wren himself—or what passed for himself these days, a stooped man of seventy-odd with ink worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like bruising—standing at the doorway instead of at his press, arms crossed, watching the street.

"Evening," Orsen said. "Collecting for Marchmont."

"Are you indeed." Wren didn't take his eyes off the alley mouth. "You'll have seen our visitors, then."

"Two. One ex-army, one with a scar through the left brow. They're watching the alley, not your door specifically."

"That's what worries me." Wren turned, finally, and his eyes were milky at the rims but sharp in the middle. "Watching is how it starts. First they watch, then they stop watching because they've already written it all down, then one morning there's a warrant and everyone wonders how they knew. My father set this shop up in sixty-eight. We've had presses seized twice, and both times it began with men standing under a broken lamp pretending to admire architecture." He limped to the back and returned with a crate weighted in proofs, wrapped in plain brown paper, tied with string in a pattern any railway porter would recognise as innocent. Then, underneath the proofs, wrapped separately and smelling of fresh ink: a smaller bundle. "Tell Mrs. Marchmont the order's ready. All of it. She'll know what I mean, and if you don't, don't ask me and don't open it."

"I never open anything," Orsen said.

Wren looked at them for a long moment, the way Mrs. Marchmont had looked at them on the terrace, and Orsen felt the strange vertigo of being seen twice in one day after three years of successful blindness.

"No," Wren said at last, dry as tinder. "I don't expect you do."

* * *

The Lambeth address took Orsen down roads that narrowed and leaned, where the houses wore their soot like mourning bands and children ran barefoot in October. The second packet went, per instruction, around the back—through an alley that smelled of cabbage and tallow, to a door painted the exact green of every other door in the row, answered by a woman with flour on her forearms who took the packet without a word and closed the door before Orsen could decide whether they'd seen her face before. Perhaps at the workshop below stairs. Perhaps at a Sunday dinner Orsen had served but not attended. The network was like that: a web of half-familiar faces, each thread knowing only its nearest neighbours. It was supposed to keep everyone safe. Tonight it only made everyone look like a question.

The tail appeared on Westminster Bridge Road.

Orsen noticed it the way one notices a wrong note—not immediately, but as an accumulation. A cart horse shying at nothing. A man under a lamp who had been under a different lamp two streets back, same coat, same uneven gait, left leg shorter than the right. And then, half a mile on, the crowning proof: a motorcar keeping an impossible distance, a Humber tourer with its side lamps hooded, holding thirty yards back no matter what Orsen did with the throttle.

Not police procedure. Police followed in relays, clumsily but by the book. This was one vehicle, persistent, patient—a private tail, or worse, an amateur one, which was more dangerous because amateurs had something to prove.

Orsen's mind went cool and quick, the way it did in fog. *Options.* Lead them to Lambeth again? No—the green door was still warm in memory. Back to Belgravia? Never; you don't bring hounds to the den. So: theatre. Orsen signalled left with elaborate correctness, took the turn slowly, and settled into a route home that happened, by accident of course, to pass through the market streets around the Borough, where at seven o'clock in the evening the traffic of costers' barrows and drays thickened into a brown tide that no motorcar could push through without announcing itself.

At the corner of Bedale Street Orsen swung the Daimler down an alley between two warehouses—a passage learned years ago delivering a piano, of all things, wide enough for a car by six inches, smelling of salt and rope—and killed the engine. Sat in the dark. Listened.

Thirty seconds. A minute. The Humber's engine note rose somewhere to the north, hesitated, circled, faded. The driver had lost them in a lane that didn't exist on any map a stranger would buy, because Orsen had bought London street by street, delivery by delivery, and kept the surplus in their head like a hoard.

Orsen sat another minute in the dark of the parked car, hands on the wheel, breathing.

Three years of invisible service, and tonight the street itself had changed temperature. Men under broken lamps. Tails on the road home. Old Wren saying *watching is how it starts*. The household printed pamphlets and moved papers and called it parcels-for-the-shop, and everyone involved had lived for years inside the comfortable fiction that the authorities were too busy, too stupid, or too indifferent to care. Tonight suggested otherwise. Tonight suggested that someone, somewhere, had opened a file.

And underneath the fear, quieter but sharper, sat the other thing: Mrs. Marchmont on the terrace step. *I've noticed you reading the post by lamplight.* Three years of stolen reading, done for love, done to protect them—and the woman who owned those letters had known, and had said nothing, until tonight, when the stakes had grown teeth.

Orsen started the engine and drove home the long way, by streets with names, arriving at the mews at nine-forty exactly, punctual as furniture.

* * *

The house was quiet in the way it got after eleven, when Cressida's lamp went out above the library and the cook's radio murmured itself to sleep in the basement. But the morning room lamp was burning.

Orsen came in through the side door with the empty crates and found Mrs. Marchmont at the table, not reading, not writing—simply sitting, hands folded, facing the door as though she had been waiting, which she had. On the table between her hands lay the evening's second envelope, the one with Mr. Marchmont's instructions, unopened. Beside it lay a sheet of paper covered in her upright, unhurried hand.

"Sit down, Orsen."

Orsen sat. The chair was hard. Everything in the room was suddenly hard and detailed: the tick of the mantel clock, the smell of cold tea, the blue shade of the lamp throwing Mrs. Marchmont's shadow long across the carpet.

"Wren telephoned while you were on the bridge," she said. "He has a telephone now, which he swore he'd never have, which tells you how frightened he is. Two men at the alley mouth. He described them. An older man who chews, and a younger one with a scar." She let that sit. "You saw them too. You'll want to tell me whether they followed you after you left."

"They tried. I lost them in the Borough."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite approval; recognition. As if a suspicion she'd been carrying had been confirmed and she didn't know whether to be glad.

"Here is my difficulty, Orsen." She slid the handwritten sheet across the table. Orsen looked down at it and felt the floor tilt. It was a list. Dates, in her hand, going back fourteen months. Against each date, initials and a word or two. *17 Feb — O.W. — Holland St letter, read & resealed, wax slightly lifted.* *3 June — O.W. — Ashworth correspondence, opened at flap.* Fourteen months of observations, compiled by servants' intelligence, by a maid who dusted the morning room at odd hours, by a mistress who missed nothing.

"I have known for a long time," Mrs. Marchmont said quietly. "I did not stop it, for reasons I will come to, if you're willing to hear them. But the ground has shifted under us tonight. Men outside Wren's. A car on your road home. Whatever we are doing, someone has begun to take it seriously, and when that happens, secrets between allies become more dangerous than the secrets themselves." She folded her hands again. "So I am going to ask you plainly, once, and whatever you answer will settle what happens next. Why do you read my post?"

The clock ticked. Down in the basement the radio murmured. Orsen found that their mouth was dry and their voice, when it came, was steadier than they had any right to expect.

"Because in 1911 a letter came to this house addressed to Miss Cressida from a cousin in Manchester, and inside it was a warning that a landlord's man meant to accuse her of incitement over a meeting she never attended. I read it at the flap. I resealed it. Because of what was in it, the family stayed away from the Albert Hall that night, and a girl who was arrested in her seat was not my girl—was not your daughter." Orsen stopped. Took a breath. "I've read every letter since. Not to betray you. Never that. To stand between you and whatever the post might carry. I'm a driver, ma'am. It's the only border I can guard."

Silence. The clock ticked five times, six.

Mrs. Marchmont reached across the table and turned the list of dates face-down, closing it like a book.

"Then you will understand," she said, "why I am not angry." She rose, took the cold tea to the window, and stood looking out at the dark mews where the Daimler sat with its lamps cold. "Tomorrow there is a letter coming that frightens me more than policemen do. It should arrive by the afternoon post. When it comes, I give you permission—no. I ask you, openly, as one keeper of this house asks another—to do what you have been doing all along, but this time with my knowledge and my blessing." She turned. The lamplight caught the grey-violet at her throat. "Read it before anyone else does. And if it is what I fear it is, come find me. Wake me if you must."

She left then, unhurried, her footsteps fading up the stairs, and Orsen sat alone in the morning room with the overturned list and the racing certainty that invisibility, the whole careful architecture of it, had just come down—and that something was coming by the afternoon post that frightened even her.

The clock ticked toward midnight. Fourteen hours, perhaps fifteen, until the letter.

Orsen put out the lamp and did not sleep.
