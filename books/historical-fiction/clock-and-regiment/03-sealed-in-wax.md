## 3. Sealed in Wax

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing at the time and would mean everything afterward.

Orsen had learned long ago that Tuesdays were the quietest post days—fewer invitations, fewer demands, the household's correspondence settling into its midweek lull like water finding its level. Mrs. Harkness the cook had gone to market; Cressida was at her French lesson with Madame Vasseur, whose accent could be heard through the study window on still mornings, correcting *r*'s with patient severity; Mr. Marchmont was at his chambers. The house held only Orsen, the maids upstairs turning mattresses, and the morning's letters laid out on the hall table like a hand of cards waiting to be dealt.

Orsen sorted them as always. Bills first—the gas company, the vintner, the stationer whose invoices Mr. Marchmont paid promptly but grumbled about in equal measure. Then the political correspondence: two envelopes bearing the return address of the Women's Social and Political Union, one from a barrister named Whitlock whose handwriting Orsen knew by now better than their own mother's, if they'd ever had a mother's handwriting to know. Then the personal notes, the invitations, the circular asking for subscriptions to something charitable that would never be subscribed to.

And then, beneath all of it, an envelope that did not belong.

It was heavier than it should have been. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the seal—not the Marchmonts' own crest, not the WSPU's belt-and-arrows, not any of the dozen seals Orsen had come to recognize the way a postman recognizes streets. This one was plain wax, black as a beetle's back, stamped with nothing but a single initial pressed deep enough to nearly pierce the paper.

*H.*

Orsen stood in the hall with the letter flat across both palms and let themselves feel, for exactly three seconds, what it would be easy to feel: nothing. Ignorance. The comfortable blindness of a driver who carried parcels and did not read them.

Three seconds. Then they turned toward the narrow stairs that led down past the kitchen to the workshop below stairs, where the light was good in the afternoons and the door locked from the inside.

* * *

The workshop had been a coal cellar once, before Mr. Marchmont had the floor raised and the walls papered over with cork to deaden sound. Now it held the small Albion press that Nell's brother had bought at auction from a bankrupt newspaper in Croydon, the cases of type arranged by size in wooden drawers worn smooth by thumbs, the ream wrappers stacked against the wall, the smell of ink and oil and damp stone that Orsen associated more strongly than anything else with the word *home*—though they would have bitten through their own tongue before saying so aloud.

They lit the lamp, set the letter on the composing stone, and looked at it for a long moment the way one looks at a dog that may or may not be mad.

There were rules. Orsen had made them themselves, in the first months of their employment, when the habit of reading had begun as caution and hardened into ritual. Rule the first: read everything, because what you do not know cannot protect anyone. Rule the second: change nothing. Fold each letter along its original creases. Warm the wax seal with a knife heated to the precise degree that softens without smearing—Nell had shown them, years ago, how a seamstress lifts a hem without a trace of the needle, and the principle was the same. Rule the third, the one that mattered: tell no one. Not Mrs. Marchmont, who suspected already—Orsen had seen the suspicion in her eyes on the evening of the printer's alley, that flick of attention like a dressmaker measuring a stranger for a coat she hadn't ordered yet. Not Cressida, who watched everyone with her father's lawyer's eyes. No one. Invisibility was not merely a condition. It was a discipline, maintained daily, like pressing one's collar or winding one's watch.

Orsen heated the knife blade over the lamp flame, counted under their breath, and touched it to the black wax.

The seal lifted clean. Beneath it, the flap of good rag paper—too good, Orsen noted absently, for whatever this was about to say—and inside, a single sheet folded twice.

The handwriting was copperplate. Not disguised, exactly, but perfected—the way a forged banknote is drawn with too much love, every stroke immaculate, no human hesitation anywhere in it. Orsen read it once quickly, the way one reads anything frightening, wanting it finished. Then they made themselves read it slowly.

*To the household at Number 9, Cavendish Row. It has come to our attention that certain persons resident at that address are implicated in the affair at the Admiralty annex. Matters will come to light within the month. Evidence exists and is in keeping. Those wise enough to remove themselves from London before the twelfth of November will find the law less attentive to them thereafter. Consider this the only courtesy extended.*

There was no signature. Only the initial again, pressed into the bottom corner without wax—a blind stamp, the kind stationers used, the kind Orsen had seen on the wrapping paper of official dispatches.

They sat down on the type case stool, which was the wrong height for sitting and right for everything else, and did not move for some time.

*The affair at the Admiralty annex.* Everyone in London knew of the bombing—or thought they did. Three weeks past, a device had gone out the ground-floor windows of the annex on Horseferry Road, shattering glass across two streets, injuring a constable's arm, scorching the front steps black. The newspapers had done their work thoroughly: SUFFRAGIST OUTRAGE in three-inch letters, editorials fulminating, a country already half-convinced that every woman in a green-and-white sash was carrying matches in her reticule. No arrests yet. The police spoke of inquiries proceeding. And inquiries, Orsen knew from four years of reading other people's mail, proceeded wherever they were pointed.

*Evidence exists and is in keeping.* As though evidence were a commodity, ordered up like coal. And perhaps it was. Perhaps somewhere in a police archive or a private office, statements were being taken and exhibits photographed and a narrative assembled with the same meticulous care that had produced the copperplate perfection of this letter.

And the Marchmonts—Mrs. Marchmont with her committee meetings and her speeches, Mr. Marchmont with his chambers and his quiet funding of the press below stairs, Cressida with her French lessons and her lockpicking hands—were to be the point of the spear. Framed for it. *Discovered* within weeks, the letter said, which was a strange verb for discovery, unless discovery was something one scheduled in advance like a dinner party.

The twelfth of November. Orsen counted without meaning to. Nineteen days.

* * *

The knife was still warm. Orsen looked at it, and at the letter, and understood with terrible clarity the choice lying on the composing stone beside them.

Burn it. That was rule-following. That was invisibility. Burn the letter, forget the initial *H*, and let whatever machinery was turning elsewhere turn without them. The Marchmonts were grown people, resourceful, well-connected, warned in a hundred small ways they might or might not hear. If the blow fell, it fell on them as a family, together, with lawyers and friends and the whole architecture of their class to absorb it. What could a driver possibly add? A letter with no signature, no provenance, nothing a court would look at twice. Worse than nothing—it was a thing that, once spoken of, made the speaker either suspect or fool. If Halloway's people—if whoever's people—learned the letter had been opened, read, kept, then the invisible man in the driver's coat became a witness, and witnesses to this kind of work had a way of ending up in the river with their pockets full of stones.

Burn it, and stay invisible, and live.

Orsen rose, crossed to the small iron stove that took the chill off the cellar in winter, and opened its door. The coals glowed. The draft pulled at their sleeve.

They stood there for perhaps a full minute, the letter in hand, feeling the heat push against their face, and found that their body would not perform the simple motion of letting go.

It was not courage. Orsen examined the feeling honestly, because dishonesty even with oneself seemed suddenly dangerous, and found it was something older and stubborner than courage. It was Mrs. Marchmont setting an extra shilling in the envelope of the girl who'd been dismissed for attending a rally, unasked and unremarked. It was Mr. Marchmont standing in the workshop with ink to his wrists, setting type himself on the night the regular compositor was ill, laughing at his own crooked lines. It was Nell sewing hems by lamplight and Cressida arguing politics with the milkman and the whole improbable, fragile, deliberate family of the place—a family that had, without ceremony or announcement, folded a driver with no surname worth using into its number, so gradually that Orsen had not noticed it happening until long after it was true.

They had protected this family with silence for four years. Silence had felt like service. But silence, Orsen saw now, looking into the stove's orange throat, had always had a limit written into it, a clause neither party had spoken aloud: *so long as silence is enough*. Tonight a sealed black disc of wax had informed them it was not.

If the Marchmonts went to prison for a bomb they had not built, they would go there partly because their driver had valued his own invisibility above their liberty. And Orsen would have to live in the wreckage of that arithmetic for the rest of a life that would deserve no better name than *coward's*.

They closed the stove door.

* * *

Afterward came method, and method was steadying. Dread, Orsen had found, was bearable only when converted inch by inch into procedure, the way fear of a dark road became knowledge of the road's every bend.

First: copy. There was good thin paper in the drawer and a steel-nibbed pen that made a fair imitation of a clerk's hand. Orsen copied the letter twice, once in full and once abbreviated to its essentials, working slowly, checking each line against the original the way they checked a delivery route. The copies went into the oilcloth packet sewn into the lining of their coat—an old habit from the early days, when carrying pamphlets had required such precautions—which hung on its nail by the cellar door. One copy would go, in time, somewhere safer than a coat. They had not yet decided where. That problem could keep.

Second: preserve. The original letter must survive. It was the only physical object connecting the threat to anything real—the paper stock, the blind stamp, the copperplate hand. If it vanished, there would be nothing to compare against whatever forged evidence was being manufactured. Orsen wrapped it in waxed paper and considered the workshop's hiding places and rejected them all; the workshop was the most obvious target, whenever the hammer fell, and anything hidden there was hidden in the fire. It would need to leave the house entirely, in hands that could pass unquestioned. That was a problem for tomorrow, and it had a shape already: Nell. Nell, who sewed documents into coat linings as casually as she sewed buttons, and who had never in eight years asked a question whose answer she wasn't entitled to.

Third: understand. Orsen sat back down on the type-case stool and made themselves think about *H*. The initial told them little—half the men in London began with H—but the manner of the letter told them more. Good paper. Perfected hand. A blind stamp of the kind used on official wrappings. Knowledge of the investigation's direction before the newspapers had it. Whoever wrote this was not a blackmailer; blackmailers ask for money, and this letter asked for nothing except flight. It was a courtesy, it said—the courtesy extended to useful instruments being given notice before the building comes down. Which meant the writer believed Orsen might be an instrument too. Someone had sent a warning into the Marchmont household addressed to no one, confident that someone in it would read between the lines and save their skin.

The thought turned Orsen's stomach cold. *They know someone reads the mail.* Or they guessed. Either way, the letter was aimed not merely at the family but at the household's invisible circulatory system—at Orsen specifically, though the writer could not know the reader's face.

Unless they could. The evening in the printer's alley returned to them: the two men by the shuttered shopfront, the way the taller one's eyes had tracked not the deliveries but the deliverer. Orsen had memorized their faces and dismissed the incident as surveillance of the street. Now it looked different. Now it looked like inventory.

Fourth, and hardest: decide what to tell, and to whom, and when. Rule the third said *no one*, and rules kept you alive, and yet a rule that got its keepers transported was no rule at all but a trap wearing a rule's clothes. Mrs. Marchmont, then. Not yet—not tonight, when suspicion was still fresh and a confession would land as accusation. Soon. Within days. And Pell—

But that thought stopped short. Ambrose Pell was a name Orsen had heard only in fragments, passed across the workshop table in the low voice reserved for damaged goods: a police clerk, dismissed for refusing to falsify a report, drifting now around the edges of the trade. If anyone knew how evidence was manufactured and archived and pointed like a gun, it would be a man who had been asked to make it and would not. If anyone could look at a copperplate forgery-in-progress and say *I know that hand, I know that firm, I know whose desk it crosses*, it was he.

That was tomorrow's business too. Tomorrow, or the day after. Tonight there was only the last item of method, the oldest one:

Orsen restored the letter. The fold, crease by crease, remembering which edge lay over which. The flap warmed and pressed down onto the softened wax, the black disc reseated with the knife's flat until the seam showed only the faintest hairline, visible to no one who had not lifted it before. The letter went back to the hall table, back to the bottom of the morning's stack, where it had lain all day like a stone at the bottom of a river pretending to be a riverbed.

Orsen banked the lamp, checked the stove, climbed the narrow stairs, and paused at the top with a hand on the rail.

Above, the house breathed its nighttime sounds—the tick of the hall clock, water moving in pipes, a maid's laugh muffled behind a door. Somewhere up there Cressida was asleep with a French novel face-down on her counterpane. Somewhere Mrs. Marchmont slept the hard sleep of a woman who had been arrested twice and knew precisely what she was risking every time she stood at a podium, and slept anyway. They trusted the world to be dangerous in the ways they had chosen and safe in the ways they hadn't. They did not know that danger had taken an interest in them specifically, had bought good paper for the purpose, had set a date.

Nineteen days.

Orsen stood in the dark of the stairwell and felt the dread finish its slow transformation—not into calm, which would have been a lie, but into resolve, which is only dread that has been given work to do. Tomorrow they would speak to Nell. Tomorrow they would begin the long, careful business of becoming visible.

From above, faint through the floors, the hall clock struck eleven, and Orsen counted the strokes, and went quietly up toward the light left burning in the servants' passage—past the coat on its nail with its sewn secrets, past the hall table with its harmless-looking stack of Tuesday's post, past the letter that had ended one life and, though Orsen could not yet see its shape, had begun another.

Down in the cellar, in the dark, the type lay in its cases: thousands of small leaden letters, silent and orderly, each one meaningless alone and incendiary in combination, waiting—as Orsen now was—for someone to arrange them into words.
