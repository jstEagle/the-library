## 4. The Household That Isn't Mine

Cressida Marchmont had learned to read people the way her mother read newspapers: thoroughly, sceptically, and with an eye for what had been left out. It was a skill cultivated at dinner tables where cabinet ministers' wives said one thing and meant another, where aunts spoke of the Cause in code as light as drawing-room chatter, where the truth was a currency spent carefully or not at all. She could tell when her father's law clients were lying about their finances. She could tell when Mrs. Prewitt, the cook, was concealing a burned trifle under a sauce. And she could tell—had been telling herself for three days now, with increasing alarm—that something was badly wrong with Orsen Winterbourne.

It showed in the smallest places. The way Orsen carried the coal scuttle up the back stairs: three days ago the trips had doubled, four in an evening instead of two, as though the extra weight of walking past the landing mirror had become unbearable. The way their hands moved when they polished the motorcar's bonnet—the same small circles, always the same circles, but faster now, urgent, like a heart beating somewhere outside the body. The way they stood in hallways. Orsen had a genius for standing in hallways without occupying them; Cressida had never quite worked out how a person could be so entirely present and so completely invisible at once. But lately the invisibility had frayed. Orsen stood *in* rooms now, not around them, and their eyes did something Cressida recognised from her own mirror: they counted exits.

She noticed it most acutely on Thursday evening, at supper. The household ate in shifts—the servants' hall first, then the family—and Cressida had made a habit, which she told no one about, of lingering near the servery door to exchange a word with whichever of the staff was passing through. It was her own small rebellion against the architecture of the house, which insisted that people who washed her linens should not also be her friends. On Thursday, Orsen came through carrying a tray of cleared dishes, and Cressida watched them set the tray down with a precision that was slightly too careful, the way a drunkard walks too straight.

"Orsen." She said it quietly, so it wouldn't carry to the kitchen. "Have you slept this week?"

Orsen looked up. Their face did the thing it always did—a pleasant blankness arriving like a curtain drawn across a window—but the curtain snagged this time, caught on something raw underneath. "Well enough, miss."

"That's a lie," Cressida said, not unkindly. "You've got shadows under your eyes deep enough to post letters into."

Something flickered at the corner of Orsen's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgement that the joke had been heard and filed away. "I'll take care of it, miss."

*Miss.* Cressida wanted to seize the word and shake it until it confessed. Three years Orsen had been in the household, driver and courier and general quiet factotum, and in all that time Cressida had never once been invited past *miss*. She knew almost nothing about them. No history, no people, no town of origin anyone could name. References had come through some acquaintance of her father's, papers signed, and Orsen had simply appeared one grey morning at the mews door with a kit bag and steady hands and a way of looking at the motorcar as though apologising to it for not being a horse. The household had absorbed them the way sand absorbs rain. Even her mother, who missed nothing, referred to Orsen only as *Winterbourne*, as though a surname were a complete biography.

But Cressida had seen the letters. Not their contents—she wasn't her mother, whose radicalism extended to reading everything that crossed the threshold, including other people's correspondence, on the principle that the State already did it worse. But she'd seen how Orsen handled them: the little pause before sealing, the tilt of the head, the way certain envelopes were weighed in the palm as though the paper itself might testify. She had decided long ago that Orsen Winterbourne read the post, and she had decided, just as firmly, that she didn't mind. Whoever Orsen was protecting the family *from*, the protection seemed genuine. You cannot fake the kind of care Orsen took with Mrs. Marchmont's parcels—checking knots, noting seals, memorising return addresses the way other people memorised hymns.

Now that same care had turned inward and was eating its owner alive.

* * *

The opportunity came on Saturday. Her parents were out—the Marchmonts kept a standing engagement on Saturday afternoons that involved, officially, a lecture on municipal reform, and unofficially, a meeting of the WSPU finance committee held above a hat shop in Marylebone. Cressida had pleaded a headache and stayed home. This was itself becoming a habit; she had things to do in empty houses that she couldn't do in full ones, chiefly writing letters she would deny under oath and consulting maps of railway timetables spread across her bed like scandal sheets. But today, instead of going down the back stairs to retrieve her hidden notebook, she went out through the conservatory and across the wet garden to the mews.

The garage smelled of petrol and leather and cold iron. The motorcar sat in state, gleaming even in the dimness, and beside it, at a workbench under the one small window, sat Orsen, cleaning parts laid out in rows like surgical instruments. They didn't look up, but their hands stopped moving, which was how Cressida knew they'd heard her come in.

"You should be resting, miss. Big dinner tomorrow."

"It's my house," Cressida said. "I'll stand in the garage if I like."

"Your father's house."

"My father's house," she conceded, and pulled the stool from the corner and sat down on it directly in Orsen's line of sight, which forced them either to look at her or to perform the elaborate fiction of being absorbed in a valve. They chose the valve. Cressida waited. She was better at waiting than people assumed; her mother called it stubbornness and her father called it patience, and both were wrong—it was simply that Cressida Marchmont did not retreat from things.

The rain ticked against the roof. Somewhere down the mews a cart horse shifted and stamped.

"I'm not going anywhere," Cressida said finally, "until you look at me."

Orsen's hands stilled again. Then, with the air of someone conceding a point in chess, they set down the rag and looked up. Up close, in the grey light, the exhaustion was worse than she'd thought—not merely sleeplessness but the particular hollowness of someone who has been frightened for days and has run out of ways to hide it. Their eyes were dark and very steady.

"There," Orsen said. "You're looked at."

"You're frightened."

"I'm careful. There's a difference."

"Not this much difference. I grew up in a house full of suffragettes; I know the difference between careful and frightened. Careful checks the locks. Frightened counts them four times." She leaned forward. "And you've been counting, Orsen. I've watched you do it. Doors, windows, the gate into the alley. You did it at breakfast yesterday while Father was talking about the rates."

For a moment Cressida thought Orsen would laugh it off, and she braced for it, rehearsing her counterattack. But instead something in Orsen's shoulders simply let go—a fraction, no more than an inch of surrender—and they looked down at their hands, which were stained black to the knuckle with grease, as though the hands might answer for them.

"If I told you there was nothing," Orsen said quietly, "you wouldn't believe me."

"No."

"And if I told you it was nothing to do with you, you'd only worry more."

"Almost certainly."

Orsen exhaled. It might have been a laugh in a different life. "You argue like your mother."

"Everyone says that. It's meant to be a compliment."

"It is one." Orsen picked up the rag again, folded it, unfolded it—a person needing occupation. "Miss Marchmont. There are things in this house that aren't yours to carry. Your mother's work puts your family in a certain kind of light, and that light draws a certain kind of attention, and part of my employment—" a hesitation, so brief anyone else would have missed it, "—is managing what attention finds you."

Cressida sat very still. *Part of my employment.* Not *my duties include driving*. Something in the phrasing, in the deliberate vagueness, opened a door she had only ever pressed her ear against.

"You read the letters," she said.

The silence stretched long enough that she heard the rain change tempo. Orsen didn't deny it. Didn't confirm it either, but the denial's absence rang through the garage like a struck pipe.

"I thought so," Cressida said. "For about two years, actually. You hold the envelopes too long. And last spring you refused a registered parcel from Brussels until you'd checked the wax, and you don't check wax unless you know what wax can hide." She paused. "Mother doesn't know. Does she?"

"Your mother suspects everyone of everything. It's why she's never surprised." Orsen's voice was dry, and for a moment they sounded like a different person—younger, wryer, someone Cressida had never met but instantly wanted to know. "No. She doesn't know. Or she knows and considers it useful. With your mother those are often the same thing."

"Why?"

"Why what?"

"Any of it. Why does a driver read the post? Who taught you to check wax?" Cressida shook her head. "Three years, Orsen. I've defended you at the dinner table when Aunt Winifred called you sullen. I've covered for your half-days. And I don't know one true thing about you, except that right now you are afraid of something more than you're afraid of me, and that is *new*."

Orsen looked at her then—really looked, the way one looks at a locked door one has walked past a thousand times and only now thought to try. Cressida felt the examination and bore it, chin up, because whatever else was happening, this was the closest thing to honesty the two of them had ever exchanged.

"You've been followed," Orsen said at last.

It was not what she expected. "What?"

"You heard me." The wryness was gone; the voice had gone flat and quick, the voice of someone doing arithmetic. "Have you, or haven't you. A man on the pavement by the chemist's on Wigmore Street, perhaps. Grey coat. Or someone who was on your omnibus Tuesday morning and again Thursday. Someone who is always reading a newspaper he never turns the pages of."

The garage tilted, very slightly. Cressida gripped the edge of the workbench. Because there had been a man by the chemist's, grey coat, and she had filed him under coincidence, and there had been a newspaper that never turned, and she had filed him under paranoia, and here was Orsen Winterbourne holding both files open at once.

"Yes," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Both of those. How do you—"

"Because I've been followed too. Different streets, same weather." Orsen stood, moving to the window, and looked out at the mews with the counting-eyes again. When they spoke, their back was to her, and the words came reluctantly, dragged up like stones from a well. "There's a letter. It came Tuesday. It wasn't addressed to your family, which is why your mother never saw it. It was addressed to a printer in Clerkenwell, and it passed through my hands because everything passes through my hands, and I read it, because that is what I do, and I wish to God I hadn't."

"What did it say?"

"I can't—"

"Don't you dare say *it isn't my place*," Cressida said, and stood up, and found that her legs were entirely steady now, steadier than they'd been all week. "Someone is following me, Orsen. Me. Down Wigmore Street, past the chemist's, close enough that if I stopped and asked him the time he'd have to decide whether to run. If it concerns me, it is my place, and I will find out what it is with you or without you, and I will be considerably stupider about it without you."

Orsen turned. In the flat light their face was unreadable for a long moment—and then it changed, the way ice changes, all at once and invisibly, solidity becoming water with no visible line between. What Cressida saw afterwards, trying to describe it to herself later that night, was recognition. One guarded creature seeing another across a garage, and understanding that the guards were facing the wrong direction—that they had each been watching the front door while something circled round the back.

"The letter named your family," Orsen said. Each word placed separately, like type set by hand. "In connection with an explosion. One that hasn't happened yet. It's planned to happen, and it's planned to be blamed on your mother's people, and there are weeks, not months, in which to stop it." A breath. "That is nearly all of it. The rest isn't mine to give, because it belongs to other people whose safety depends on my silence, and you may hate me for that, but I won't spend lives that aren't mine to spend."

Cressida discovered she was shaking—not with fear, or not only fear, but with the furious relief of a person who has known, *known* in her bones, that something was wrong and has finally been believed. "Then we stop it," she said. "Together. You and I."

"No." Orsen came away from the window, and their expression had closed again, though not all the way; a crack of light remained, and through it Cressida saw something that startled her more than the whole confession had. Orsen was afraid *for her*. "You stay out of it. You go where there are people, you vary your routes, you tell no one—not your mother especially—and if a grey coat appears on Wigmore Street again, you come to me before you do anything brave."

"Brave is the whole family business."

"Cressida." It was the first time Orsen had ever used her name. It landed strangely, warmly, like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was hers. "Please."

The rain thickened against the roof. Down the mews, the cart horse stamped again, and Cressida thought of her mother upstairs in a hat shop in Marylebone dividing money that might soon be evidence against her, and her father drafting briefs for men whose secrets were safer than his own, and the great blind house behind them all, warm and lit and utterly unaware.

"Allies, then," she said. "Not partners. Allies. You keep your other people's secrets; I keep yours. But you tell me what touches this family, the moment it touches this family. And Orsen—" she held out her hand, grease-stained palm up, an absurd gesture in a garage, a perfectly serious one, "—when you need help that can't come from servants or drivers or couriers, remember that nobody watches a daughter."

Orsen looked at the hand. Then they took it, and their grip was calloused and careful, and neither of them smiled, because it wasn't that kind of moment. It was the other kind—the kind where two people sign something no paper records.

"Nobody watches a daughter," Orsen agreed quietly. "Which is why you must tell me at once if anyone starts."

They were still standing there, hands clasped over the workbench like conspirators over a treaty, when the sound came: footsteps in the mews, unhurried, stopping just short of the open door. Orsen's hand tightened once—a signal, clear as telegraph—and let go, and by the time the figure filled the doorway, blocking the grey afternoon light, Orsen was bent over the valve again, all invisible, all driver, and Cressida was a young lady who had come to complain about a rattle in the wheel.

The man in the doorway wore a grey coat, and carried a newspaper he did not open, and looked at neither of them as he said, to the street in general:

"Lovely day for it."

Then he walked on, and the rain closed behind him like a curtain, and Orsen's eyes met Cressida's over the engine, and Cressida understood that the weeks had just gotten shorter.
