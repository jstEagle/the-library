## 5. What Can Be Carried

The workshop smelled of ink and lamp oil and, beneath both, the cold mineral breath of the brick itself — a cellar smell, a kept-secret smell. Orsen came down the narrow stair at half past six in the morning, before the household stirred, before the kettle sang in the scullery, carrying a candle in one hand and a carpenter's pencil behind one ear like a woman who meant business, which was precisely the impression Orsen wished to give the empty room.

Nobody was there to receive the impression. That was rather the point. Orsen had learned long ago that rehearsing competence in front of witnesses was performance, but rehearsing it alone was preparation, and only one of those could be trusted.

The candle threw its small gold circle across the floor. Orsen set it on the workbench beside the hand-press — the Albion, older than Orsen and probably older than Mrs. Marchmont, its iron frame painted a green that had faded toward grey the way all things in that room faded toward grey except the ink, which stayed stubbornly black. Then Orsen took out a notebook, cheap cloth-bound, the kind a grocer's assistant might carry, and opened it to the first blank page, and wrote at the top in a hand disguised as boredom:

*Inventory. What can be carried.*

The words sat there looking more ominous than they needed to. Orsen looked at them for a while anyway.

Three nights had passed since the sealed letter with the unfamiliar wax. Three nights of lying in the narrow attic room above the stables, listening to the house settle, running the letter's phrases over and over like a cloth over a stain: *the Marchmont name will answer for what is discovered.* Discovered. Not done — *discovered*, as though the bombing were a relic waiting to be unearthed, planted like a coin in an excavation. Whoever wrote it had not been threatening violence. They had been promising theatre.

And theatre required props. Props could be moved.

Orsen licked the pencil and began.

*One Albion press, c. 8 cwt.* The number stopped the pencil dead. Eight hundredweight of cast iron and hope. It had come down those stairs in pieces, Orsen knew from the story Nell told, carried by four men over two nights in 1909 when the first press in another cellar had gotten too warm for comfort. It could go back up in pieces too. But it would take time, and hands, and above all it would take *warning* — and warning was the one commodity the sealed letter had declined to supply.

*Two cases of long primer type. One case brevier.* Orsen lifted the lids as they passed, checking the little lead armies in their compartments, each letter standing shoulder to shoulder in its cell. Type was heavy in aggregate but light in portions; a case could be carried by one determined person at a shuffling walk. Type was also dangerous beyond its weight. Every letter was a signature. Compositors left fingerprints in their spacing, their kerning, their habits of hyphenation, the way handwriting left loops and slants. Pell would have said that — no. Orsen corrected themself. Pell was a name in Cressida's mouth, not yet a person; that introduction was still to be survived. But whoever had set the type for the Marchmont pamphlets could be identified by the type itself, if the police were clever, and some of them were cleverer than their uniforms suggested.

*Ledgers.* Orsen wrote the word and then, after a moment, underlined it twice.

There were three. The subscription ledger, dull as a parish register — names of sympathisers who paid a shilling a month toward paper and ink, schoolmistresses and clerks and two actresses and a widowed countess who insisted her contributions be recorded as "repairs to greenhouse." The production ledger: dates, titles, print runs, distribution routes written in a cipher of Orsen's own devising that looked, to an untrained eye, like a dairyman's milk counts. And the third ledger, unnumbered, kept wrapped in oilcloth inside a false bottom of the type cabinet — the one with everything in it. Names of the network. Addresses of safehouses. The whole living architecture of the thing, drawn in Orsen's careful invisible hand.

If the raid came and the police found the third ledger, it would not matter whether the Marchmonts were innocent of the bombing. Half the quiet people in London who helped keep the suffrage presses running would go down in the same net, and the net would be hauled by men looking for someone to hang the explosion on, and they would not be careful with the hauling.

Orsen sat down on the bench, candle guttering, and let the fear have its moment. It came every morning now, punctual as a mail train: a cold sliding sensation under the ribs, the body's honest arithmetic of danger. Orsen had learned not to argue with it. Arguing wasted time. Better to shake its hand, thank it for attending, and put it to work. Fear noticed things. Fear counted doors.

So: inventory. What can be carried.

The press — disassembled, four nights' work minimum, needs four strong backs and a cart. The type — portable tonight if necessary. Pamphlet stock, three reams of paper — burnable, thank God; paper made its own alibi. The ledgers — the first two copyable, the third not copyable fast enough, therefore the third must simply *leave*, and soon, and never be found, because a thing that could not be explained was worse than a thing destroyed.

And the distribution list sewn into garments — that was where the thought arrived, the way solutions did, sideways, while the mind was busy elsewhere. Orsen paused with the pencil hovering and let the thought finish itself.

Nell.

* * *

Nell Quill — not her birth name, which nobody used and which Orsen suspected involved a great many syllables — kept a workroom on the floor above the alley print shop, reached by a staircase so narrow that two people meeting upon it had to negotiate like ships in a canal. She was a seamstress by trade and by temperament: a small, quick, sharp-eyed woman of fifty-odd whose fingers were always moving even when the rest of her was still, worrying a thread, rolling a pin between thumb and forefinger, mending something that didn't look broken until you saw how much better she'd made it.

Orsen climbed those stairs at nine o'clock, after delivering Mr. Marchmont to his chambers and Mrs. Marchmont to a committee meeting at which she would say nothing seditious aloud and a great deal of it between the lines. The morning was grey and soft, the alley quiet, the coal-smoke hanging low enough to taste.

Nell looked up from a coat spread across her table like a patient on an operating slab, took in Orsen's face in one glance, and put down her needle.

"Shut the door," she said. "And sit on the stool, you hover like a moth."

"Nell—"

"Sit."

Orsen sat. There was no known method of resisting Nell once she deployed that tone; it was the voice of a woman who had raised four younger brothers and buried two of them and was not going to be managed by anyone's nerves, including her own.

"Now," she said, taking up her needle again, because her hands could not be idle while her mind worked — Orsen had watched this often enough to understand the needle was how she thought — "you've had trouble since Tuesday, and it isn't the kind a person can fix alone, and you've spent three days deciding whether to tell me. Have I got the shape of it?"

"You have the shape of it exactly."

"Good. Then save us both the overture." She threaded the needle in one pass, without looking, an act Orsen had witnessed a hundred times and still found faintly miraculous. "Tell me what has to move."

Not *what's wrong*. Not *are you in danger*. Nell went straight to logistics the way other people went straight to prayer. Orsen felt something in their chest unknot slightly — the specific relief of being understood by someone who shared your grammar of worry.

"There may be a raid," Orsen said quietly. "I can't say when. Weeks, possibly less. If it comes, everything in the cellar has to vanish or be made harmless, and I've been making lists since dawn, and every list ends in the same problem." They leaned forward. "Paper, Nell. Ledgers. Documents that can't be burned because we need them, and can't be found because they'd kill us."

Nell stitched in silence for a moment. Then she smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile; it was the smile of a craftsman shown a problem squarely within her trade.

"Do you know," she said, "in thirty years of sewing for other people, the single most useful thing I ever learned is this: nobody searches a seam properly. Not police, not customs men, not husbands looking for betting slips. They pat the lining, they feel along the hem, and their fingers tell them *cloth*. Because cloth is what linings are supposed to feel like." She turned the coat on the table, opening it flat with both hands, exposing its inner architecture — the canvas, the padding, the folded seams at shoulder and skirt. "Now. A document folded small — eighth-folding, edges creased sharp — laid flat inside a lining seam and stitched over with fine thread, matched thread, thread chosen for the garment..." She demonstrated with quick movements against the fabric, miming the surgery. "Done right, you could wear the entire subscription ledger to a garden party and no man alive would know it. Done *very* right, you could wear it through a police station."

"How right can you do it?"

Nell gave Orsen a look over the top of her spectacles. It was the look of an artist asked whether she could manage a masterpiece.

"Give me the ledgers a few pages at a time," she said, "and give me coats. Coats belonging to people who have reason to own good coats and no reason to be searched — and I'll tell you now, dear, they mustn't all go to one place or one person, because six coats in one closet is a pattern, and patterns are what they look for even when they miss seams." She was already pulling pins from her wrist cushion, planning, allocating. "The Marchmonts' own winter things, for a start. Nobody raids a lady's wardrobe on suspicion of sedition sewn into a pelisse. And I'll do a set for myself and a set for young Cressida, though if you tell her I said so I'll deny it."

"She knows something's wrong," Orsen said. "She's started watching me the way I watch everyone else."

"Then she's her mother's daughter, and you'd best decide soon how much watching you can afford." Nell bit off a thread. "What else is on your famous list?"

"The press. Eight hundredweight."

Nell winced audibly. "The Albion. Oh, that's a heartbreaker, that is. Four men, two nights, coming *down* — and coming down is always easier than going up, because gravity takes a side." She chewed her lip. "There's the undertaker on Mallow Street. Mr. Prewitt. He owes the cause a favour he's never named and we've never asked about, and he has a hearse with a compartment built for coffins that no constable on earth will ask to inspect. When the day comes, iron goes to Prewitt and lies among the dead till it's wanted. Dead men make excellent accomplices — they never talk and nobody suspects them of anything."

Orsen wrote it in the notebook. *Prewitt. Hearse.* It looked absurd next to the arithmetic of weight, and it was the most valuable line on the page.

"And the third ledger?" Nell asked, too casually.

Orsen met her eyes. "That one doesn't get sewn anywhere. That one stops existing. New book, new cipher, old book gone — dissolved, burnt, scattered in the river, I don't care, so long as it can never be read whole again."

"Mm." Nell bent back over her coat. "You'll want to be careful burning it. Paper flies when it burns, dear. Ask any woman who's burnt letters. Little corners take wing like moths and land behind the grate where you can't reach, and ten years later a sweep pulls out a corner with a name on it." She pulled the stitch taut. "Tear it first. Tear every page in half, then in quarters, wet your fingers, roll the pieces, burn them loose and stirring. And don't burn it here. Burn it somewhere a fire is expected. Bakeries are good. Nobody questions a chimney at a bakery."

Orsen looked at her — really looked, the way one looks at a tool one has owned for years and suddenly sees the maker's mark on. Nell Quill, who mended hems and took in waists and let out seams for half of respectable Islington, and who had apparently spent those same thirty years learning how documents died and how they hid and how they travelled, all in the domestic, disregarded, invisible language of women's work. Stitching. Folding. Burning safely. Keeping secrets in cloth.

"Nell," Orsen said slowly, "has anyone ever asked you to sew anything unusual before? Before this week?"

The needle paused.

"Once or twice," Nell said, in a voice that closed the subject like a door. Then, gentler: "You're not the first frightened courier to find my stairs, love. You're just the politest. Now — coats. How many can you bring me by Friday?"

* * *

Orsen walked back through the streets with the notebook inside their coat, against the breast, where for years they had carried other people's letters and read them all, and where today, for the first time, lay a page of their own composition that mattered more than anything they had ever ferried.

Fear had not left; Orsen had not expected it to. But it had changed state overnight, the way water changes to ice and becomes something you can build on. All night the dread had lain on their chest like a stone; this morning it had become ballast. A list. A plan. A seamstress with a needle and thirty years of invisible warfare in her fingertips. A hearse waiting on Mallow Street among the respectable dead.

By Friday there would be coats. By the following week, perhaps, a dismantled press travelling under mourning drapery. And somewhere ahead, unknown, the day itself — boots on the cellar stairs, lanterns, Inspector Somebody reading a warrant — and on that day Orsen intended to open the door with clean hands, an empty cellar, and tea already on the tray, and watch the searchers find nothing but brickwork and disappointment.

It was a strange kind of courage, Orsen reflected, turning up Fetter Lane into the wind. Not the kind that charged. The kind that inventoried. The kind that counted hundredweights and chose thread colours and tore paper into quarters with wet fingers. The courage of people who expected the worst and prepared for it in installments, calmly, before breakfast.

They were crossing the yard behind the Marchmont house, gravel crunching, when the sound reached them: voices from the direction of the mews, low and urgent. Cressida's voice, pitched in the particular key she used when she was trying to sound calm and fooling no one. And beneath it, a second voice — male, clipped, apologetic in the aggressive way of a man delivering bad news by instruction.

Orsen rounded the corner in time to see a stranger in a dark coat handing Cressida a printed notice — a large red-bordered rectangle of paper, official as a death certificate.

The stranger touched his hat, walked past Orsen without a glance, and was gone through the arch.

Cressida stood very still, the notice in both hands, her face drained to the colour of the paper. When she looked up at Orsen, her eyes were bright and furious and afraid.

"It's the workshop," she said. "They've posted the demolition order."
