## 6. A Clerk Without a Badge

The rain came down on Clerkenwell like an apology no one had asked for—thin, grey, and too late to matter. Ambrose Pell stood beneath the awning of a shuttered chandler's shop with his collar turned up and his hands in the pockets of a coat that had been good wool once, before three winters of unemployment had pressed it into something nearer to sackcloth. Across the road, the Divisional offices glowed behind their frosted windows, and he watched them the way a man watches a house where he once lived and is no longer welcome.

Eleven years, four months. He could still recite his own service record the way other men recited psalms. Entered as a junior copyist at nineteen. Promoted to second clerk at twenty-four. Commended—actually commended, there was a letter with a seal on it somewhere in his mother's effects—for the reorganisation of the warrant registries, which had cut the average retrieval time from two days to four hours. Two days to four hours. It was not the kind of achievement that got a man statues. It was the kind that got a man trusted with things.

That had been the trouble, in the end. Trusted with things.

He had not set out to be principled. He wanted that understood, if anyone ever bothered to understand him at all. Principles were expensive luxuries for men with private incomes; Pell had a mother in Pimlico and a landlady in Pentonville and neither of them accepted convictions in lieu of rent. But when Superintendent Brayling had slid the report across his desk last March—the report on the arrest of the boy Wilcox, seventeen years old, taken up outside a dockworkers' meeting—and said, in that mild, reasonable voice, *just amend the time entry, Pell, it's a matter of half an hour*, Pell had looked at the amendment, and then at the boy's statement, which he happened to have read, and the two documents disagreed by exactly the half hour required to make the arrest lawful.

Half an hour. A small thing. A clerical thing. His whole life had been made of small clerical things, and he knew better than most men how empires of harm could be built out of them, one amended entry at a time.

"I won't sign it," he had said. And Brayling, who had expected compliance the way one expects a machine to turn, had looked at him with genuine surprise, and then—this was the part Pell thought about at three in the morning—with a kind of disappointment, as though Pell had failed some examination he hadn't known he was sitting.

They had not dismissed him for refusing. That would have been awkward, possibly actionable. They had dismissed him for irregularities in his filing, for a pattern of "administrative negligence," for the late submission of eleven forms over eighteen months, each one trivial, each one documented, each one—Pell realised only later—filed away and hoarded against just this eventuality. They had been keeping a drawer of him, ready to be opened when needed. That was the thing about institutions: they did not punish principle. They waited for it, catalogued it, and buried it under paperwork.

Since April he had lived on savings that were nearly gone and on piecework—copying for solicitors' clerks, addressing envelopes, once a fortnight of inventory work at a stationer's that smelled of gum arabic and despair. He read the newspapers more carefully than he had ever read them as a serving officer. He found he could not stop reading them the way he had been trained to read everything: for inconsistencies, for the seam where two accounts had been stitched together by hands in a hurry.

It was in the newspaper accounts of the Clerkenwell bombing that he first saw the seam.

* * *

He had gone back to it five times before he let himself believe what he was seeing. The *Gazette*'s report, then the *Chronicle*'s, then the coroner's preliminary remarks quoted in the *Standard*. The official account held that the device had been a pipe bomb packed with black powder and nails, timed with a cheap watch-movement alarm of the sort sold at any ironmonger's. Fine. Ordinary. Except that the blast pattern described in the coroner's remarks—the scorching, the directionality, the way the shopfront had failed outward rather than upward—did not sit right with a black-powder pipe bomb in a confined space, and Pell had spent enough dull years transcribing explosives reports to know what sat right and what didn't.

But it was the case notes that decided it. Those came to him by accident, the way everything important seemed to come to clerks: through the copying work. A junior barrister's chambers had hired him to produce fair copies of deposition summaries ahead of committal proceedings, and among the pages—carelessly collated, carelessly confidential—had been extracts of police memoranda relating to the bombing inquiry. And there, in the fourth memorandum, in a description of physical evidence recovered from a lodging house in Somers Town, Pell's eye snagged on a sentence and refused to move past it.

*Paper fragments recovered from the hearth consistent with printed pamphlet material; ink analysis pending.*

Ink analysis pending. On paper fragments. Six weeks after the fact.

Pell sat back from the copying desk in the chambers' dim anteroom and stared at the gas bracket until the flame doubled in his vision. Because you did not send paper fragments for ink analysis unless you already knew what you were looking for. Ink analysis was a confirmation procedure, not a discovery one—it told you whether a document matched a known source, a known press, a known batch of type-ink. Someone had submitted those fragments against a reference sample. Which meant someone had, weeks before the arrest of the Marchmonts' associates, already possessed a theory connecting printed pamphlets to the bombing—and had been quietly building the evidentiary bridge to carry that theory into court.

And there was something else. Something smaller. The memorandum described the recovered fragments as bearing "partial letterforms in a heavy grotesque face." Pell had copied ten thousand evidence descriptions in his career and he had never once seen letterforms described that way, because that was not how policemen talked. That was how *printers'* apprentices talked, or typesetters, or—his mind went down the corridor of his memory, past the warrant registries and the form indexes and the long grey years of transcription—to a very particular kind of expert witness, the kind retained privately by firms that prepared exhibits for prosecution counsel. Describing a fragmentary forgery by its *typeface* was the habit of a man whose business was making documents look authentic. It was the tell of the craftsman admiring—or condemning—his materials.

Someone had manufactured evidence. Not bungled it, not stretched it—manufactured it, with craft, with the specific professional vanity of a trained forger describing his trade goods. And the Marchmonts, whose names appeared in every column now beside the words *anarchist outrage* and *the bombing conspiracy*, were going to be fitted for that manufacture like a woman being fitted for a coat.

Pell finished his fair copies in perfect hand, returned them to the junior barrister with a bow, and walked out into the rain thinking, with a sensation like a key turning in a lock he had believed rusted shut: *I know this work. I have seen this work before. I was dismissed for refusing to do a small version of this work.*

* * *

He did not go to the Marchmonts directly. Eleven years in an institution taught a man that the direct approach was the approach that got people watched. Instead he went to the printer's alley off Sekforde Street, because he had done his homework—he was, whatever else they had taken from him, still a man who did his homework—and the homework told him that if anything true was going to move in this affair, it would move through the little world of jobbing printers, pamphleteers, and the women who carried folded papers in their hatbands as casually as other women carried violets.

The alley smelled of ink, steam, and horse. Print shops leaned against one another along its length like tired old men sharing a secret, their windows fogged, their doors propped open regardless of weather because compositors ran hot. Pell walked it twice on successive days without stopping, learning it the way he had once learned registry indexes—noting which door had two bells, which cellar hatch stood ajar, which window on the upper floor of the corner premises kept a lamp burning until ungodly hours. That lamp interested him. Above a print shop, above what he took to be a workshop of some kind, someone worked late and did not want it generally known.

On the third day he saw the driver.

He knew the man—or knew *of* him—from the same careful homework. Winterbourne. The Marchmonts' driver and courier, a quiet figure who moved through the fringes of the movement like a fish through weedy water, noticed by no one, remembered by everyone vaguely. Pell had put out discreet feelers—a question here, a shilling there—and the answers came back consistent: reliable, silent, always where he was meant to be. In Pell's experience, *always where he was meant to be* described either the most trustworthy man in an organisation or its best-placed traitor, and the difference could usually be read in the eyes.

So he positioned himself at the mouth of the alley in the late afternoon drizzle, where a man waiting for a cab looks like any other man waiting for a cab, and let Winterbourne come to him.

The driver came at the pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be and total awareness of everywhere else. Pell watched him register the alley—left, right, the cellar hatch, the upper window—without appearing to look at any of it, and revised his assessment upward. Not a courier who drove. A courier who happened, at present, to drive.

"Mr. Winterbourne," Pell said, stepping just far enough under the eave to be heard and no further. "My name is Ambrose Pell. Late of the Divisional offices, second clerk, eleven years' service. Dismissed in April for refusing to amend a report. I have something you need to hear, and roughly ninety seconds in which to say it, after which you may walk away and I will never trouble you again."

Winterbourne stopped. He did not startle—that was the first notable thing—but turned with the unhurried economy of a man who had already noted the exits and found Pell to be the least dangerous object in view. Up close he was younger than his stillness suggested, with the pale, unlined face of someone who lived indoors behind other people's windows, and eyes that went briefly, professionally, over Pell's coat, boots, and hands.

"Your hands," Winterbourne said. Not a greeting. An observation. "Ink under the nails. You've been copying."

"Solicitors' chambers. Piecework." Pell allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "You read people the way I read documents. We'll get on, or we won't. Ninety seconds."

Winterbourne said nothing, which was not a refusal. Pell took it for permission.

"There is a man—I don't yet have his name—who prepares forged exhibits for prosecutions. Paper stock, inks, letterforms. The memorandum describing the paper fragments from the Somers Town lodging house describes them the way such a man would describe them: by typeface, by impression weight. That language does not belong in a police memorandum. It belongs in a tradesman's invoice. Somebody's contractor has been admiring his own work in an official document, and the work is being built around your employers." He paused, precisely, the way he had learned to pause in testimony. "I cannot prove any of this yet. But I can read a forged file faster than the man who forged it, because I spent eleven years learning what genuine ones smell like. I am offering you my eyes. I am not offering you my trust, and I'm not asking for yours. Institutions burned us both; I merely suggest we compare scars."

The rain ticked on the awning. Down the alley, a press began its rhythmic clatter, muffled like a heart heard through a wall.

Winterbourne regarded him for a long moment. Then: "If you're what you say, why come to me and not to the Marchmonts directly?"

"Because Mrs. Marchmont is watched, Mr. Winterbourne, and you are not—or are not yet—and because if I am wrong about you, I'd rather discover it at arm's length than inside her drawing room." He drew a breath. The next part cost him; he paid it anyway. "And because I have been six months without a purpose, and a man without a purpose begins to believe he deserved what he got. I need this to be true. Check on that. Doubt me thoroughly. Only doubt me quickly."

Something shifted in Winterbourne's expression—nothing so legible as sympathy; more like recognition, the look of a man finding a coin in his pocket he'd forgotten he owned. "There's a public house on Compton Passage," Winterbourne said. "The Green Man. Thursday, seven o'clock. Ask at the bar for correspondence addressed to Mr. Loxley. If I decide to meet you, there'll be an answer waiting."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then there isn't." Winterbourne lifted his chin toward the street. "You chose the alley well, Mr. Pell. But you should know the walls here have opinions. The lamp upstairs watches this corner."

"I know," Pell said. "I've been watching it watching me since Tuesday."

For the first time, the driver almost smiled. Then he was past, unhurried, folding himself into the traffic of the alley as though the alley had arranged itself for his convenience, and Pell stood alone under the awning with his heart going harder than ninety seconds of speech could account for.

* * *

Thursday came slowly. Pell spent Wednesday rereading his copied extracts by candlelight, annotating them in pencil on separate sheets so the originals stayed clean, building—as he had once built warrant indexes—a structure out of scattered facts. Fragment descriptions in tradesman's idiom. Ink analysis initiated impossibly early. The arrest timeline of the Somers Town lodgers preceding, not following, the recovery of the fragments they supposedly produced. Each item thin as onionskin. Together, a dossier with weight.

He dressed in his best remaining coat, which was to say his least worst, and presented himself at the Green Man at five minutes to seven. The public house was low-beamed and honest, smelling of sawdust and beer, and the barman—receiving the name *Loxley* with the flawless blankness of a man who had received many names—directed him to a corner table where a single envelope lay, unlabeled, face down.

Inside, in a small precise hand: *Back yard. Door on the left.*

The yard was cobbled and dark, walled high against the London sky, and Winterbourne was leaning against the wall beside a door that opened—when he pushed it—onto warmth, lamplight, and the unmistakable iron-and-oil breath of a printing press. Steps descended below street level into a workshop: a platen press, cabinets of type, a woman at a long table with needles threaded through the lapel of her apron like a general's medals, and a girl of perhaps nineteen setting type with the absorbed ferocity of a surgeon.

"This is Nell," Winterbourne said, indicating the needle-woman. "Cressida you'll meet when it's safe, which tonight it isn't. The workshop has no name and no owner we'll admit to. Sit down, Mr. Pell."

Pell sat. The chair was hard; the room was warm; and from a shelf above the press a stack of printed pamphlets regarded him with their heavy grotesque letterforms—letterforms he had last met in a leaked memorandum, described in a forger's idiom.

"You've brought us something," Winterbourne said, taking the opposite chair. It was not quite a question.

"A beginning," Pell said, and laid his annotated sheets on the table between them. "A memorandum that talks like a tradesman. An investigation that started before its evidence. And a question nobody official is asking: *who hired the hand that made the papers?*" He looked round the lamplit room—at Nell's steady gaze, at the type cases, at the press that had printed the very pamphlets now being forged into evidence of murder—and felt six months of ash shift into something that might, with care, be coaxed back into flame.

"Find me that contractor's name," he said, "and I will show you how to break him."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the doorbell of the Green Man rang—and Winterbourne's head came up sharply, because it was ringing a second time, and the second ring was wrong.
