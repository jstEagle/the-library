## 7. Ink Under the Fingernails

Ambrose Pell had spent eleven years learning to read paper.

Not words—anyone could read words. Paper. The weight of it, the rattle of it, the way it took ink and gave back a confession of its own manufacture. In the records office at Scotland Yard he had handled forty thousand documents at least, and somewhere in those years his fingertips had become an instrument more honest than any witness on the stand. Paper did not lie about its age. Ink did not lie about its chemistry. A signature did not lie about the hand that made it—not to a man who had spent a decade measuring the angle of clerks' flourishes the way a tailor measures shoulders.

They had dismissed him for refusing to falsify a report. The charge on the record was insubordination; the truth was that Inspector Dane had asked him to move a date, one date, three days, so that a search warrant would appear properly sequenced, and Pell had looked at the form and said, *no sir, that would be forgery*, in the flat voice he used for facts, and Dane's face had gone the colour of cold mutton. Eleven years, gone between one word and the next. He had walked out with his hat, his umbrella, and nothing else, because they kept your pen when they dismissed you. They kept your pen as if the pen were theirs, as if everything you had ever written on their paper belonged to them.

He still dreamed in forms. Wakeful at four in the morning in his lodgings above the tobacconist on Clerkenwell Road, he would lie still and mentally file the day's observations: * memorandum, undated; subject, forgery; classification, pending.* It was a habit he could not break, this filing of the world, and he had stopped trying.

The case notes had come to him folded inside a copy of the *Police Gazette* left at his usual table in the coffee house on Leather Lane, and whoever had left them—and he suspected now it was the Winterbourne fellow, the driver, with his careful eyes and his parcel-measuring hands—had chosen well, because Pell could not look at a document described in another man's words without needing the document itself. What he had instead were three pages copied by someone with a decent but not expert hand: an inventory of evidence against the Marchmont household in the matter of the Cannon Street bombing. A threatening letter said to be in Mrs. Marchmont's hand. A delivery ledger said to bear her husband's initials. A receipt for chemicals said to have been found sewn into a coat.

Said. Said. Every third sentence rested on *said*, and Pell had built his whole career on the difference between what was said and what could be proven.

He spread them on the table in his lodgings with the gas turned low, weighted at the corners with a candlestick, a shoe, the tobacco tin, and read them the way other men read scripture—slowly, twice, hunting the seams.

The copies themselves told him little; a copy is a mirror, and you cannot weigh a reflection. But the copier had been conscientious, and had noted things. The letter was said to be on cream-laid paper. The ledger on ruled foolscap, blue-ruled. The receipt on wove stock, watermarked.

Pell sat back and looked at the ceiling for a long time.

Three documents. Three different papers. Now, in a genuine conspiracy—a real one, the desperate amateur kind—the paper would be consistent. Guilty people steal whatever is nearest: the household's own stationery, the office pad, the back of an envelope. Consistency is the fingerprint of haste. But these three documents came from three separate stocks, each one correct for its purpose—the letter personal, the ledger official, the receipt commercial. That was not haste. That was procurement. Somebody had *sourced* those papers, deliberately, the way a forger of means sources them, which meant money, which meant time, which meant an organisation.

And there was something else, the thing that had kept him awake until dawn and got him up before it: the watermark note. *Found receipt bears partial watermark, illegible.* Illegible, in the inventory—but the copier had sketched what could be seen of it in the margin, a few curved strokes like the top of a letter or a device.

Pell knew that curve. He had filed invoices against it for years without ever thinking about it, because filing is a trade of the hands and the hands remember what the mind dismisses. A rising curve, broken, with a serifed foot. Part of a crest. Part of a name.

*Halloway.*

*

The firm of Halloway & Sons occupied three floors above a stationer's on Fetter Lane, which was the sort of address that explained itself: near enough to Fleet Street to smell the newsprint, far enough away never to be named in print. They were commercial agents—Pell had processed enough of their paperwork to know the shape of them. They prepared documents for other people. Contracts, attestations, certified copies, translations, verifications. A firm whose entire business was making paper say things with authority.

He did not go to the front door. Eleven years of police work had taught him that front doors are where investigations go to die; you walk through a front door and every clock in the building starts. Instead he spent four days being a man out of work, which he was, and therefore beyond suspicion: he drank coffee at the corner of Fetter Lane and Holborn at half past eight, at noon, at four. He watched the clerks come and go, thin young men with cardboard collars and satchels, and older men with the particular gait of those who have sat too long on chairs built for shorter spines. He watched the stationer's below, and on the second morning he went in and bought a penny notebook and asked, in the idle tone of a man furnishing a home office, whether they stocked good laid paper, cream, for correspondence, and the shopkeeper—a Welshman with ink-stained cuffs and the loneliness of small tradesmen—was delighted to talk.

Cream-laid, yes, very fine, though most of his custom ran to the firms upstairs. Halloway's bought heavy. Bought the same stock year after year, wouldn't take variations, fussy as a bride about it. And the foolscap, blue-ruled? Oh, that was a special order, that was. Ruled to Halloway's own specification. Most offices ruled their own headings after purchase, but Halloway had the mill rule the sheets—headings printed in, the whole thing done at source. Very tidy. Very hard to match.

Very hard to match, Pell thought, and impossible to explain by accident. If a forged ledger page appeared in a police inventory ruled exactly to Halloway's private specification, then either Halloway's paper had walked out of Halloway's door into hostile hands—or Halloway's door had opened from the inside.

And the watermark. He drew it from memory on the penny notebook's cover that night: the rising curve, the serifed foot, and beside it, from the depths of eleven years of filing, he wrote the full device as it appeared on Halloway & Sons' invoice paper—an anchor, old-fashioned, with a coiled cable, the curve of the fluke matching precisely the strokes in the margin of the copied inventory. A partial watermark. Illegible, if you didn't know. Legible as a signature, if you did.

Paper does not lie about its manufacture. And a watermark is the manufacturer's own hand, set into the sheet while it was still pulp and rags, before anyone thought to make it speak.

*

He found Orsen Winterbourne on the fourth evening, in the printer's alley off Clerkenwell, which surprised him less than it should have. He had reasoned it out over the coffee-house days: the driver who read letters, the driver who had passed case notes to a disgraced clerk rather than to a lawyer or a journalist—that driver needed a place where paper was made and moved, and there was only one such place within the geometry of the routes Pell had watched him drive. So Pell simply stood at the mouth of the alley at dusk, hat brim low, hands in his pockets, and waited to be noticed, because a man who reads letters notices watchers the way sailors notice weather.

It took eleven minutes. Then the driver came up the alley with a parcel under one arm, saw him, stopped, and stood very still in the way of a man calculating distances to doors.

"Mr. Pell," Orsen said. "You shouldn't be here."

"Neither should you," Pell said, "and yet here we both are, which suggests we have the same bad judgment." He held up the penny notebook. "I've been reading paper instead of people. It's the only kind of reading I'm good at. May I show you something?"

For a moment there was no sound but the far-off rattle of a press running late somewhere below the street, and the two of them regarded each other across six feet of wet cobbles—one dismissed, one invisible, each carrying the particular wariness of people whom institutions had failed. Then Orsen tilted their head toward the alley's depth. Not trust. An audit. Pell recognised it and respected it; he would have done the same.

Inside the workshop it was warm and smelled of oil, ink, hot metal, and the dust that paper sheds forever, all its life, like dandruff. A platen press. A cabinet of type with the drawers half-open in the way of working furniture. Two women at a long bench—one broad-shouldered, quick-fingered, threading a needle with thread the colour of nothing at all; one younger, setting type with the speed of someone who thinks faster than her hands can follow. Neither looked up, which was itself a kind of discipline.

"Say it plain," Orsen said, setting down the parcel.

"The evidence against the Marchmonts is forged, and I can prove how, and I think I know who made it." Pell laid the copied pages on the bench, weighted the corners with a typecase and a tin of pins borrowed without asking from the seamstress, who flicked him a glance of mild, eternal female contempt for the theft and returned to her needle. "Three documents, three papers, each correct for its purpose. That's procurement, not opportunity. Someone bought those sheets to order. The foolscap is ruled to a private specification—mill-ruled, headings in, sold to one customer only. The receipt carries a partial watermark." He drew the curve in the air with one finger. "An anchor with a coiled cable. The device of Halloway & Sons, commercial agents, Fetter Lane."

The younger woman at the type cabinet had stopped setting type. The seamstress's needle hung motionless in the light.

"Halloway," Orsen repeated.

"Documents of all kinds, prepared to order, discretion guaranteed—I've filed their attestations for years. A firm like that doesn't forge for its own sake; it forges on commission. Which means someone paid them, and someone senior, because the forged items are already inside a police inventory, and evidence doesn't enter an inventory without a chain of signatures." Pell paused, because the next part was the part that had cost him his career once already, and he found he was willing to pay it again, which was an interesting discovery about himself at fifty-one. "I signed inventories behind Dane for eleven years. I know his clerks' hands, and I know the countersignature format at the Home Office division that handles political cases. The paper trail runs through Fetter Lane and ends at a desk with a government lock on it. Someone inside the Home Office commissioned this. Halloway is the hand. Someone above him is the arm."

"And the demolition order," Orsen said quietly, "that arrived yesterday."

"What demolition order?"

Orsen told him. The notice served on the leaseholder of the alley properties—structural survey, compulsory purchase, clearance scheduled. Dated, stamped, entirely proper, and timed to the week. Pell listened with his filing-mind working and felt the pieces settle into place with the ugly click of a mechanism closing.

"It's not demolition," he said. "It's erasure. You clear the workshop, you scatter the network, you destroy the presses that could print a defence, and you do it under a council stamp so no one asks questions. The bombing frame takes the Marchmonts; the demolition order takes everyone who might help them. Two operations, one timetable." He looked around the warm room, at the press, at the type, at the needle and the pins and the two women who had gone very still, and understood that he was looking at the thing he had spent eleven years failing to protect from inside the institution: people made of paper, and paper made of people, and all of it burnable.

"Then we have two clocks," Orsen said. "Their trial, and their wreckers."

"One clock," Pell corrected. "The trial is the deadline for everything else. And Mr. Halloway's firm works to commission, which means it works to schedule, which means—" he tapped the copied inventory, the sketch of the anchor in its margin, "—somewhere in Fetter Lane there is a job book with dates in it. Forgers keep records, Mr. Winterbourne. Vanity, invoicing, habit. Eleven years of filing taught me that no one throws away the proof of what they were paid."

The seamstress spoke for the first time, without looking up, in the voice of a woman stating a fact about cloth: "If they're coming for this place, I'll need to know how many coats we're talking about."

"As many as it takes," Orsen said.

"Then someone find me calico and a strong back," said Nell, and threaded her needle.

Pell gathered his copied pages, and at the door he stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in his chest since the coffee house like a swallowed stone. "There's a man used to work for Halloway," he said. "A journeyman forger—good, better than whoever cut the Marchmont letter, actually; the letter has tells, the spacing goes wrong after line six. He left the firm some years ago under circumstances nobody will discuss. His name is Wren. If anyone alive can testify to how Halloway's workshop operates, to commissions and customers and the job book—it's him. I want to find him before the trial. Before the raid. Before anything else happens."

Orsen looked at him for a moment, and Pell saw the driver do the thing drivers do—recalculate a route.

"Then we'd best start tonight," Orsen said. "Because I checked the demolition notice against the court calendar while you were talking. They're not even pretending anymore, Mr. Pell."

"How so?"

Orsen told him the dates, and Pell stood in the doorway with the wet wind coming up the alley behind him, and filed it where such things are filed forever:

*Memorandum. Subject: two clocks discovered to be one. Time remaining: less than anyone believes.*

* * *

That night, in his lodgings above the tobacconist, Ambrose Pell lit the gas, laid out his penny notebook, and began, in a small precise hand, the first affidavit of his new life—unofficial, unsanctioned, belonging to no institution but himself.

At the top of the page, before anything else, he drew the anchor. Full device. Coiled cable, serifed foot, the fluke's curve exactly as it rose through the rag pulp of every sheet that had ever passed through Halloway & Sons' hands.

Then, underneath, he wrote: *In the matter of the framing of the Marchmont household, I, Ambrose Pell, late clerk of the Metropolitan Police, being of sound mind and ruined career, do depose as follows—*

Outside, rain moved up Clerkenwell Road from the direction of the river, and three floors away in Fetter Lane, in a dark office smelling of gum and ruling ink, a job book lay open on a desk with dates in it, waiting for morning.
