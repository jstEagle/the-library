## 8. Sunday Dinner, Loaded Silence

The Marchmonts kept Sunday dinner the way other families kept a Bible: reverently, and without much thought as to why. It had been her mother's invention, Cressida suspected—this weekly gathering of the household's scattered sympathisers under the pretext of roast mutton and a decent sherry—and it had survived every arrest, every raid in the papers, every winter when the cause seemed to be losing. One did not cancel Sunday dinner. One simply made sure the servants' hall ate well too, and that the curtains were drawn before anyone said anything worth remembering.

Cressida dressed for it slowly. She chose grey wool, plain at the collar, the sort of dress that said *I am a serious young woman and not worth watching*, and she caught herself in the glass doing what she had done all week: looking over her own shoulder in the mirror, checking the window behind her reflection. Twice on Thursday she had noticed the same man outside the post office on Camden Road—a stocky fellow with a bowler too new for his coat, who read the same page of the same paper for twenty minutes. On Friday he was there again. She had not told her mother. She had not told Orsen either, though Orsen was the one she most wanted to tell, which was its own strange development. A week ago she had thought of the driver as furniture with excellent posture. Now she catalogued their small competences the way she had once catalogued a certain girl's opinions on Ibsen: the way Orsen memorised routes, the way their eyes moved across an envelope's seal without seeming to, the way they stood at parade rest beside the motorcar as though the whole street were a document they were quietly proofreading.

Someone was following them both. That much she knew. What she did not know—what had kept her awake since Tuesday, listening to the house tick and settle—was whether Orsen knew it too.

She heard the kitchen before she reached the stairs: the percussion of a good dinner being assembled by people who understood that food could be an alibi. And beneath it, faintly, the smell of ink. Her mother would have preferred no pamphlets printed below stairs on the Sabbath, but Mrs. Ferro from the workshop had come up early to "help with the gravy," bringing with her the sharp vinegar-and-iron scent that clung to everything she touched, and no one had asked any questions because no one wanted the answers.

Downstairs, the dining room was being made ready for eight.

* * *

Her father arrived last, as he always did, coming in from his chambers with his collar loosened and his briefcase held like a shield he had forgotten to put down. Gerald Marchmont was a barrister of the old, exhausted variety—brilliant, principled, and perpetually surprised that the world kept requiring him to be both—and he greeted the table with the distracted affection of a man who loved everyone present and could name perhaps half of them.

"Miss Ferro," he said, bowing over the printer's ink-stained hand. "Mrs. Ashworth. Dr.—forgive me—"

"Kellerman," said the tall woman in the unfashionable suit.

"Dr. Kellerman, of course. My memory is a filing cabinet with the drawers pulled out." He took his seat at the head of the table, and Cressida watched her mother perform the seating with the precision of a chess problem: Nell the seamstress placed beside Mr. Marchmont where her soft voice could steady him; Mrs. Ferro angled toward the sideboard; Dr. Kellerman—the coroner's friend, the woman who could make a death certificate say things—given the chair nearest the door. Every arrangement a sentence. Every course a paragraph.

Orsen served.

That was the thing Cressida could not stop noticing. Orsen moved through the room like weather, soup ladled, wine poured, plates cleared, present everywhere and acknowledged nowhere. Guests talked straight through them, over them, around them, as though invisibility were a uniform the household issued. But tonight there was something different in the set of those shoulders, something coiled. When Orsen leaned between Mrs. Ferro and Cressida to set down the mutton, Cressida smelled coal smoke and lamp oil and, very faintly, sealing wax.

She had spent three years learning to read a table. Tonight she read this one like evidence.

* * *

The code began with the soup.

"So," said her mother, breaking her bread with unnecessary concentration, "I hear the Hendersons are thinking of leaving town."

"They're wise," said Mrs. Ferro. "The air in their quarter has gone bad. There was talk of it at the shop only yesterday—talk of a fire, if you can credit such gossip in autumn."

"A fire?" Her mother's spoon did not pause. "Where?"

"Nowhere yet. But the landlord's been round with papers. The whole alley is to be pulled down before Christmas, they say. New warehouses." Mrs. Ferro's eyes found Cressida's, just for a moment, and Cressida understood that she was being included deliberately, that this was a lesson conducted in front of her for her benefit. *Demolition orders.* The workshop. So it had begun.

"Ghastly," said Dr. Kellerman mildly. "One hears of so many premises being emptied these days. Cleared out overnight, sometimes. The authorities are thorough when they choose to be." She sipped her wine. "Though thoroughness depends on knowing where to look. And on the paperwork being genuine. I've seen documents lately—" she arranged her napkin with terrible care— "that would not survive a careful reading."

"No?" said Mr. Marchmont, from the far end, only half attending. He was sketching something on his cuff-link box, some point of law from Monday's brief.

"Signatures in the wrong hand. Dates that don't agree with themselves. The sort of thing a first-year clerk would catch." Kellerman let the silence sit exactly long enough. "If it mattered. If someone cared enough to look closely at, say, a matter involving a certain family."

The word *family* landed on the tablecloth like a dropped fork.

Cressida watched her father's pen stop moving. Watched him look up, slowly, the way a man looks up when he realises the conversation has been happening about him the entire time. His eyes went from Kellerman to his wife to Mrs. Ferro's carefully blank face, and then—to Cressida's horror and something else, something warm—they went to Orsen, standing motionless at the sideboard with the claret decanter in hand.

"I think," said Gerald Marchmont, laying down his pen with great exactness, "that we might dispense with the weather. Cressida, my dear, would you ask for the doors to be closed?"

"The doors are closed, Papa," said Cressida. "They've been closed since the fish."

He absorbed that. Nodded, once, like a judge accepting a stipulation. "Then tell me plainly. Someone has manufactured evidence against this household. Am I right?"

Nobody spoke. Orsen poured the claret—one glass, two—with hands that did not tremble, and Cressida thought: *they know the shape of this. They've known longer than any of us.* The realisation arrived with a small, cold shock, followed immediately by an anger that was mostly fear wearing better clothes. All those letters. All those nights she'd seen lamplight under Orsen's door at two in the morning and assumed insomnia.

"You are right," her mother said at last, into the silence. "There is a plot. It concerns a bombing—an explosion planned, or rather *arranged to be discovered*, somewhere it can be pinned upon us. The evidence is being built now. We have perhaps weeks."

"Weeks." He repeated it flatly, testing the weight.

"And there is more," her mother continued, and here her voice did something Cressida had never heard it do: it gentled. "Gerald. There are options. Geneva is not so far. Your practice has correspondents in Edinburgh; a sudden northern engagement would be unremarkable. If we remove ourselves—if the household disperses for a season—there is nothing for them to aim at. No family, no target. The frame requires subjects."

The room held its breath. Cressida realised she was gripping her napkin hard enough to crease the linen permanently.

Her father looked down the length of his own table. At Mrs. Ferro, whose press had turned out four thousand pamphlets from beneath this house while he'd been writing closing arguments upstairs. At Nell, whose needlework he had praised at breakfast without ever asking what the hidden seams were for. At Dr. Kellerman, who had sat in this chair eleven times and never once mentioned that she'd testified against two of his clients' enemies pro bono. At Orsen, who stood with the decanter still in hand, invisible and utterly load-bearing, the way a keel is invisible under a boat.

"No," he said.

"Gerald—"

"No, Beatrice. Hear me." He set both hands flat on the table, and for a moment he looked less like an exhausted barrister and more like whatever he had been at twenty-three, before the world had worn him smooth. "I have spent thirty years defending people against exactly this. Against paper armies. Against men in offices who decide what happened and then arrange the proof of it. If I run—if *we* run—then every person who ever trusted this house learns that trust stops at the border of our comfort." He looked at his wife, and his voice softened without weakening. "You built this network. You fed it and sheltered it and taught half of London's radical printers their trade. Do you truly imagine I'll be the man who dismantled it by packing a trunk?"

"It isn't dismantling," her mother said. "It's survival. Networks survive by scattering, Gerald. That's what makes them networks."

"And what survives of us afterward?" He shook his head. "No. If they want to build a case, let them build it. I will answer it. In court, in the press, wherever they care to meet me. I have cross-examined better liars than whoever is forging these papers, and I promise you—" the ghost of his courtroom smile appeared, thin and dangerous— "signatures in the wrong hand cut both ways."

Cressida felt something loosen in her chest that she had been holding tight since Tuesday. Not relief exactly. Something fiercer. Her father was not a brave man in the ordinary ways—he flinched at thunder, disliked motorcars, crossed the street to avoid arguments—but he was brave in exactly the one way this moment required, and she loved him for it with a suddenness that stung her eyes.

* * *

The conversation resumed at a lower register after that, all business now, the pretence of dinner abandoned except as cover. Mrs. Ferro described, in the language of tenancy and rent, how the workshop's contents might be inventoried and thinned. Kellerman laid out, in the language of medical records, which documents needed copying and by whom. Nell, in the language of alterations, offered her needles. Cressida contributed what she had—the bowler-hatted man, the post office, Friday—and watched the information travel around the table like a dish being passed, each person adding to it.

Only Orsen said nothing. Orsen moved.

Plates appeared and vanished. Wine rose and fell in glasses. And twice—twice—Cressida caught it: a small adjustment of a serving dish, a half-second pause in pouring, positioned precisely at the edge of someone's sentence. When Orsen refilled Kellerman's glass just as the doctor said *"the tenth at the earliest,"* and Kellerman, without looking up, amended to *"the twelfth, more likely,"* Cressida understood that the driver was not merely overhearing the plans.

Orsen was editing them.

Later, clearing the sweets, Orsen passed behind Cressida's chair, and something was set down beside her dessert fork—a folded napkin, nothing more, except that napkins did not usually weigh quite so much or smell of lamp oil, and inside it, when she palmed it under the table, she found a scrap of paper bearing five words in a small, disciplined hand:

*I know about the man.*

She read it three times. When she looked up, Orsen was at the sideboard, face composed into perfect serviceability, decanter in hand, entirely a piece of furniture. But their eyes met for one bare instant over the candlelight, and what was in them was not apology.

It was an offer. *We are being watched, you and I. Which means we watch back together, or not at all.*

Cressida folded the note into her cuff and finished her pudding, and had never felt less alone at her own family's table.

* * *

The guests left in ones and twos, staggered like a well-run delivery route—Ferro first with her empty gravy tureen, Kellerman walking Mrs. Ashworth to the bus as though they were merely two tired women discussing a charity committee. By half past ten the house was shut up, the curtains drawn, the candles pinched out one by one until only the study lamp remained, and behind its yellow circle Cressida could hear her parents' voices, low and continuous, arguing architecture: what to keep, what to move, what to defend.

She should have gone up. Instead she stood in the hall, pulling on her gloves, and found Orsen waiting in the doorway with her coat over one arm—holding it out, helping her into it, performing the small theatre of service while speaking in a voice pitched for nobody.

"The man in the bowler," Orsen said, smoothing her collar with two efficient tugs. "New boots, government shine, left-handed cigarette. He watches the front of the house. There's another, older, who takes the alley behind the workshop at dusk. You should use the scullery door when you go out evenings."

"You've been counting them." Not a question.

"Counting is my trade." Orsen stepped back, hands folding neatly, the servant's mask sliding home—but not before Cressida saw the exhaustion underneath it, the bruised look of someone carrying a weight in a place no one could see. "Your father is a braver man than this household gave him credit for."

"He's a Marchmont," Cressida said, and heard, with some surprise, that she sounded like her mother. "We stand."

"Yes, miss." A pause. The clock on the mantel counted out the silence, patient and brass-hearted, and somewhere below the floorboards Cressida fancied she could hear the great press waiting in the dark like a sleeping animal. "Then tomorrow," Orsen said quietly, "we had best begin teaching the whole family to count."

Through the study door, her father's voice rose briefly—*"the ledgers stay with me"*—and Orsen's expression flickered, once, like a lamp in a draft, before going perfectly still again.

Cressida did not yet know what a ledger could cost. She would learn.
