## 9. The Long Road to Birmingham

Orsen had learned the roads of England the way other people learned their prayers: repetitively, in the dark, and with a quiet desperation that had nothing to do with piety.

Six years of deliveries for the Marchmont household had carved the country into them—not the country of railway maps and county guides, but the working country, the one made of lane widths and gradient, of which village bridge would take a motorcar's weight and which would groan like a dying man, of where a driver could water a radiator without being remembered by the woman at the pump. They knew that the road out of Barnet forked twice before Potters Bar and that the second fork was easy to miss if you took it at speed. They knew the Coventry road was watched by constables who noted plates because the Chief Constable liked his lists tidy. They knew that between Northampton and Leicester the land went soft and fog pooled in the valleys like milk poured into a bowl, and that a man who drove into that fog without knowing which way it bent would end his journey in a hedge, or a canal.

All of this lived in Orsen Winterbourne's hands and feet and spine, and on the third morning after Pell named Halloway, they rose before the household stirred, checked the oil and the tyres and the spare canister strapped under the running board, and prepared to drive two hundred miles to Birmingham to put all that knowledge between themselves and anyone who might follow.

The journalist's name was Edmund Fairleigh. Pell had given it to them written in pencil on the back of a tram ticket—*Birmingham *Gazette*, ask not for him but for the man who mends his boots*—and Cressida had copied the route onto a scrap she pressed into Orsen's palm at the door with fingers that were colder than the morning justified.

"You'll come back," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Thursday," Orsen said. "Two days. If I'm not back by Thursday evening—"

"I know what to do if you're not back." She did know; they had drilled it. "That's not the same as believing you will."

"Cressida."

"Drive carefully," she said, which was her way of ending an argument she had decided not to have, and shut the door, and through the glass Orsen watched her silhouette stand in the hall until the car turned out of the square.

* * *

The first hundred miles were ordinary, and Orsen distrusted them thoroughly.

Ordinary was what trouble looked like when it was lying. They stopped at St Albans only long enough to buy tea they did not want, standing where they could see both the car and the road behind it, and counted vehicles by habit: a brewers' dray, a doctor's little Napier going too fast, a farmer's cart with a frightened pig. Nothing else. At Luton a boy sold them apples and told them his brother had gone to London to be a mechanic and his mother said he'd ruin his hands. Orsen paid him a penny extra to watch the car while they used the privy, not because the car needed watching but because boys remembered faces that paid them well, and a face remembered in Luton might be a face accounted for.

They ate the apple driving. They did not let themselves think about the letter folded in oilcloth inside their coat—the original, the one with the seal and the promise of a bombing *discovered* rather than committed—and they thought about it anyway, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Halloway wanted the ledgers. Halloway wanted the workshop. And if Orsen was stopped on this road and searched—

They had considered leaving the letter behind. Nell had refused to take it. "It travels with you or it travels sewn into something bound for a place you trust," she'd said, threading her needle without looking up, "but it doesn't sit in that house waiting for Dane's lot to kick the door. Things that sit get found." So it rode inside Orsen's coat, over their heart, absurdly light for the weight of it.

Near Rugby the traffic thinned and the sky lowered, and it was somewhere past the last turnpike before Dunchurch that Orsen saw the car for the second time.

The first time could have been anything: a dark saloon holding a steady half-mile back since Crick, overtaken at a crossroads, forgotten. But then it was behind them again on the long straight after the canal bridge, same shape, same two silhouettes in front seats, and when Orsen slowed to pass a hay wagon the saloon slowed too, a half-beat late, the hesitation of a driver watching rather than travelling.

Years of invisibility had taught Orsen a discipline most people never need: the discipline of not looking at what you've noticed. Their eyes stayed on the road. Their hands stayed loose on the wheel. Inside, everything went very quiet and very fast, the way it had the night they'd first read the sealed letter by lamplight and understood what the wax and the words meant.

*Don't run yet.* Running confirmed. A tail followed best what fled from it. Orsen did what they always did at Dunchurch, which was nothing at all: took the Coventry turn at a lawful pace, kept to thirty through the village where an ancient constable raised two fingers from beside the smithy, and let the miles accumulate evidence instead of speed.

The saloon held its half-mile. Then, on the stretch where the hedges grew high enough to swallow a car whole, it lost courage and came closer—a quarter mile, close enough that Orsen could read nothing of its plate but everything of its intention. Two men. The passenger had turned once, checking behind them, which no honest traveller does three times in ten minutes.

Coventry offered choices, and Orsen took none of them. Straight through, past the cathedral's half-built bones and the watch factories, out the Nuneaton road, unhurried, a courier on legitimate business with a schedule to keep. Let them report a grey Marchmont motorcar going sensibly toward Birmingham. Let them wonder. Wondering was cheap; certainty cost men and motorcars, and whoever sat in that passenger seat was spending neither yet.

At Nuneaton Orsen stopped for petrol they half needed, and while the attendant cranked the handle, Orsen wrote in their notebook—a courier's notebook, full of delivery times and signatures—*dark saloon, 2 men, since Crick, no plate read.* Then, beneath it, the decision they had already made: *He won't follow the road. He'll follow the car.*

So the car would stop following the road.

* * *

Beyond Atherstone the land began to fold, and the weather, which had been threatening all morning, finally made up its mind. Fog came off the fields the way Orsen knew it would—not drifting in but rising, born of warm ground under cold air, thickening from wisps to walls between one village and the next. By the time they passed Fenny Drayford the world had contracted to forty yards of white-grey; by Tamworth, twenty.

Most drivers slowed. That was the sensible thing, the thing the motoring journals advised in their careful columns, and it was exactly wrong.

Orsen stopped in Tamworth's market square long enough to douse both headlamps and buy a sack of oats they didn't need, and while the boy carried it out they studied the road north with ears instead of eyes: the distant grind of the saloon changing down, still there, patient, half a mile of white curtain behind. Then they pulled away at walking speed, took the last turning before the Lichfield road—and vanished.

There was a lane out of Tamworth that existed for no purpose the map acknowledged. It ran behind the tannery, across a ford shallow enough for wheels in October, and along the top of a drainage ditch to rejoin the main road two miles on, beyond a bend where no follower could see what had come out of the fog until it was past. Orsen had found it four years earlier delivering a parcel whose recipient preferred not to appear at any house with a name. They had driven it perhaps a dozen times since, in better light, and once at night with Cressida laughing beside them at the sheer criminal joy of a secret road. In fog it was suicide or salvation, and the difference was entirely a matter of knowing where the ditch began.

Orsen knew where the ditch began.

They drove it at fifteen miles an hour with the window down so they could hear the water under the ford, counting fence posts, feeling the camber of the ground through the steering the way a blind hand reads print. The tannery smell came through the mist, sharp and animal. A heron exploded out of the ditch not six feet from the bonnet and Orsen's heart went with it, but their foot stayed light on the brake and their hands stayed gentle on the wheel, and the lane delivered them, as it had always delivered them, onto the main road with the bend and the embankment hiding everything behind.

They stopped just past the junction, engine idling, and listened.

Nothing. Fog. The far complaint of a train. Somewhere ahead, the muffled bell of a church marking three o'clock, though the sky was too blind to show whether the hour was honest.

For eleven minutes—they counted them against their watch, because panic is a liar and watches are not—Orsen sat in the white silence and waited for the sound of another engine taking the ford, or the bend, or the lane's muddy mouth. The saloon's driver would do what every driver did at that turning: continue north on the obvious road, fast now, needing to make up the time his quarry had seemingly squandered, expecting to find them again at Lichfield or beyond. He would wait at Whittington, perhaps, or crawl through Lichfield itself looking for a parked grey car among the cathedral city's respectable traffic.

There was no grey car in Lichfield. There was no car on the Lichfield road at all, because Orsen Winterbourne, invisible for six years and intending to stay so, had turned south at the embankment instead of north, dropped again onto a farm track that was barely a rumour, and begun the long loop east that would bring them to Birmingham by a direction no sane traveller would choose.

It cost them ninety minutes. It bought them everything.

* * *

They crossed into Birmingham as the gas lamps were being lit against the murk, entering the city from the east through Small Heath on roads that smelled of coal smoke and rolling mills, and the relief of arriving was so physical it was almost unpleasant—a loosening in the chest, a sudden awareness of how tightly their jaw had been set since Crick. Their shoulders ached. Their eyes burned from the strain of reading fog for six hours.

The *Gazette*'s bootmaker operated out of a cellar workshop off Digbeth where the last of the city's hand cobblers still worked amid the roar of the machine trade. Orsen left the car two streets away, walked a deliberate wandering line—past a mission hall, through a passage between warehouses, out by a yard where women were stacking crates—and presented themselves at the cellar door with Pell's tram ticket.

The man who opened it was seventy if he was a day, with forearms like mooring rope and spectacles thick as paperweights. He read the ticket, looked at Orsen's face, looked past them at the empty street, and stood aside.

Fairleigh was upstairs in a room that smelled of pipe smoke and newsprint, a heavy man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular stillness of someone who had learned to listen for a living. He heard Orsen out—the letter, Halloway, Pell's findings, the watermark, the demolition order, the tail on the Coventry road—without interrupting once, which unnerved Orsen more than pursuit had. When they finished he was quiet a long moment, drawing on his pipe though it had gone out.

"A Home Office man forging sedition to hang a suffragist family," he said at last. "You understand what you're asking. This isn't a paragraph. It's a war."

"It's a delivery," Orsen said. "I'm a courier. I don't write the letters, Mr. Fairleigh. I carry them."

"And what happens to couriers when the letters are worth killing for?"

Orsen thought of Cressida's cold fingers at the door. Of Nell's needle flashing in lamplight. Of Pell, who had lost a career rather than sign his name to a lie, and who had come to a printer's alley anyway, hat in hand, trusting strangers. Of Mrs. Marchmont, who perhaps had known for years that her driver read the post, and had said nothing, because whatever else a household is, it is also a conspiracy of mercies.

"They get the letters there," Orsen said.

Fairleigh smiled then, briefly, tiredly, and it transformed his heavy face into something almost young. "Pell's affidavit will need corroboration I can print—names, dates, the paper stock ledger from Halloway's own supplier. Can your people get me that within a week?"

"Yes." Orsen believed it the way they believed in the fog lane: not because it was safe but because they knew the ground.

"Then we understand each other. Go home by a different road than you came." He rose, extended a hand, and then paused, holding Orsen's grip a moment longer than business required. "The man following you. Describe the passenger again."

Orsen described him again: heavyset, fair-haired, gloved, turning to check behind.

Fairleigh nodded slowly. "I've seen that face. Not in London. Here." He knocked his dead pipe out against the grate. "Three weeks ago, outside a magistrate's court in this city, watching a case collapse the way cases collapse when witnesses forget things. He wasn't local police and he wasn't press. My note-taker sketched him. I'll send you the sketch by the usual channel."

"The usual channel being?"

"A parcel of boots," said Fairleigh, "for the Marchmont household. Your mistress's cobbler owes me favours. Mind the left heel."

* * *

Orsen did not leave Birmingham until dark, and they left by the Wolverhampton road heading, impossibly, west, because Fairleigh's parting words had rearranged the geography of the case: the man hunting the Marchmonts had been seen in Birmingham, which meant his reach was longer than Pell had guessed, which meant the net was drawn tighter around everyone Orsen loved, which meant every mile of the drive home was now a mile driven for them as much as by them.

The fog had lifted with the dusk, replaced by a hard clear cold, stars sharp as nails over the black countryside. On some deep country lane west of Walsall, stopped once more to check a wheel nut they had already checked, Orsen found themselves doing something they had not done in six years of solitary roads: talking aloud.

"He'll send it in a boot heel," they told the darkness, the engine ticking as it cooled. "A sketch of a man neither of us has met, hidden in a shoe, delivered to a house where I read the post. It's madness. It's the whole mad shape of us—Nell and her needles, Pell and his pencil, boots with secrets in the heels." They tightened the nut that didn't need tightening. "And it worked. Today it actually—" Their voice did something unfamiliar, and they stopped, embarrassed even before the stars, before the sleeping hedgerows, before nobody.

But the word had been said, quietly, into the cold: *worked.*

They drove through the small hours with the throttle steady and their mind moving faster than the car, laying plans the way Nell laid seams: the supplier's ledger to be obtained—Pell would know how; the sketch to be awaited; Thursday's return to keep, because Cressida was counting hours and Orsen had promised, and promises to Cressida Marchmont were the only documents Orsen had ever signed without reading first.

They came into London with the dawn, delivered the car to the mews, and walked the last streets through the milk-carts and shutter-clatter of a city waking. In the square, their square, a figure stood by the railings in the grey light—not Cressida, who would have been abed, but Ambrose Pell, hatless, collar up, a newspaper folded under one arm with the stiffness of a thing carrying more than news.

He saw Orsen and crossed the street at a pace no clerk should manage.

"Thank God," he said, breathless. "The witness. The forger from Halloway's firm—he was to give his deposition tomorrow." Pell's face in the early light was the colour of the fog that had nearly swallowed them yesterday. "He's gone, Orsen. Vanished last night from his lodgings. Room cold, supper untouched, and this—" he thrust the newspaper forward, folded to a brief column— "is what they're saying happened to the printer's alley."

Orsen took the paper. Read the headline. And felt the road drop out from under them for the second time in two days.

*Smoke Over Clerkenwell: Fire guts printers' row; arson suspected.*
