## 10. Nell's Needles

The letter arrived at half past six in the morning, folded inside a bolt of green serge that had no business being delivered before the shops opened.

Nell Quill—she had kept the name even after the marriage ended, because it was hers by trade if not by blood, and a needlewoman ought to have a name with an edge to it—took the bolt from the boy at the door, counted out his penny, and carried it down the narrow stairs to her workroom as though it were nothing but cloth. In a sense it was. The serge was real, good weight, good nap; Mrs. Marchmont had chosen it herself for a winter coat that would be seen at three separate meetings and one funeral. But sewn into the fold at the centre of the bolt, flat as a pressed flower, lay an envelope in Orsen Winterbourne's careful hand.

She did not open it at once. That was the first discipline of the craft: never hurry. A hurried seam showed like a scar. She filled the kettle, set the iron to warming on the little spirit stove, laid out her tools on the baize—the shears, the beeswax, the fine needles rolled in flannel, the thimble worn concave at the crown from twenty years of the same pressure—and only when the room had settled into its familiar order did she slit the envelope with a single pass of the sewing scissors and read what Orsen had copied in a hand so plain it might have been set in type.

The original letter was described page by page. The wax seal, unfamiliar, pressed with a crest belonging to nobody: a tower above three feathers. The wording of the threat, transcribed exactly, commas and all. The claim that evidence against the Marchmonts would be *discovered*—the word underlined twice in the transcription, once by the threatener and once, furiously, by Orsen—and that the discovery would come within weeks, timed to the trial that had not yet been announced but which everyone in the network could feel approaching like weather in a bad knee.

And beneath it, in Orsen's smaller hand, the copies Pell had made of the case notes. Forgery upon forgery, documented. Ink that behaved wrongly for its stated age. Handwriting that matched itself too perfectly, the way handwriting only matches when it has been traced.

Nell read it all twice, then folded the pages along their existing creases and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the table, looking at nothing.

Outside, Clerkenwell was waking up. Carts on the lane, the clang of the printworks two doors down, someone's radio—no, not a radio, they hadn't reached that miracle yet—someone's gramophone playing a music-hall tune with more enthusiasm than accuracy. The workshop below the Marchmints'—below the Marchmonts', she corrected herself, though in her head the possessive always stumbled—was quiet this early, Orsen having driven out on some errand, and the alley behind carrying only the smell of ink and horse and the river's distant salt.

*Evidence will be discovered.* As though evidence were a mushroom, springing up overnight where it had not been planted.

She rose, shook out the green serge, and began to work.

* * *

Her mother had taught her to sew before she had taught her letters, and Nell had sometimes thought since that this ordering of things explained a great deal about the world. Letters were how other people told you what was true. Stitches were how you made things hold. Any woman who had spent a life at the needle knew which of the two kinds of knowledge kept a family fed through a hard winter.

Sewing was invisible work. That was the whole of it, the beginning and end. Nobody saw the seams that held a coat together through ten years of weather; nobody saw the tacking, the interlining, the thousand small decisions that kept a collar from wilting and a hem from swinging crooked. A garment was judged by how it hung and whether it wore well, and the labour that achieved this was as good as nonexistent—as good as a woman's opinion in a courtroom, as good as a charwoman's name on any document ever filed.

Invisible work. And so, when the network had needed a way to move papers past police eyes—papers that would hang a household if found in a pocket or a portmanteau—it had been Orsen who thought of Nell, and Nell who had thought of linings.

A coat lining was the most unexamined square of fabric in the Empire. Men were searched, parcels were searched, luggage at stations was searched with increasing thoroughness now that the newspapers had everyone convinced an anarchist crouched behind every hedgerow. But a lady's coat? A gentleman's greatcoat handed over with a courteous request to *feel the shoulders, constable, go on, mind the tailoring*? No hand that had ever been paid a policeman's wage paused over a lining. Linings rustled. Linings were meant to crinkle. And beneath a lining, between the shell and the sleeve's silk face, a sheet of paper folded to the size of a playing card sat as safe as scripture in a church wall.

She had developed the method over months of private experiment, working alone in this room while her landlady believed her to be taking in ordinary mending. First the paper itself: it must be thin stock, rag-based, that folded without cracking along the grain. Then the fold: never quarters, which made a ridge a knowing finger could catch, but an offset roll, each layer shifted a hair's breadth from the last, until the document became a sliver no thicker than a pencil lead and nearly as flexible. Then the pocket: a channel of silk habotai, selvedge-finished, stitched to the interlining with running stitches in thread dyed to match the lining exactly—match it *under gaslight*, because searches happened under gaslight, and a colour honest by daylight could betray itself after dark. Then the closing: the channel left open at neither end but at a point midway, closed with a single line of slip-stitch so fine that opening it required knowing it existed. And finally the pressing: warm iron, damp cloth, gentle weight, so that the lining remembered its new shape as naturally as if the pocket had been born there.

Twelve minutes for a single sheet, start to finish, if the light was good. She could do four garments an evening. She had done eleven once, the night the first pamphlets went north, and had not slept for thinking about slip-stitches.

But this consignment was different. This was not pamphlets, which were cheap and replaceable and mourned by no one. This was the threatening letter itself—or rather Orsen's certified transcription, witnessed by Pell, who had signed the copy with his old badge number in a gesture Nell understood to be either gallantry or despair—and the copied forgeries that proved the frame. If these were found, they did not merely embarrass. They named names. They pointed at Halloway. Whoever carried them carried a noose.

So the hiding place must be better than clever. It must be true.

* * *

She chose the garments the way a general chooses ground.

There were five pieces bound for the safehouse in Highgate: two ladies' coats, a gentleman's ulster, a child's cape—for the safehouse was a widow's house with a widowed daughter and a grandson, and a household that looked incomplete invited questions—and a mourning cloak that belonged, nominally, to a cousin in Hampstead who did not exist. Nell had invented the cousin herself, right down to the black-edged handkerchief tucked in the cloak's pocket, because grief was the one luggage no customs man wished to handle.

To the ulster—a heavy thing, brown herringbone, built for a man of substance—went the transcription of the threat. A man's coat was searched more often than a woman's, but searched carelessly, by men who assumed other men's clothes held nothing they needed to imagine. To the child's cape went nothing at all; that was decoy, its linings deliberately ordinary, its pockets holding sweets and a tin soldier, because a searcher who found contraband stopped searching, and a searcher who found a tin soldier smiled and waved the whole family through. She had learned that trick from a smuggler's memoir bought for a penny at a stall, and had improved on it: the sweets were wrapped in papers printed with a pattern that, folded a certain way and held to the light, produced a map of the alley behind the workshop. Not that she expected the grandson to know. Children were excellent couriers precisely because they could not betray what they did not carry knowingly.

To the mourning cloak went the copied forgeries, and here she allowed herself her one extravagance of craft.

The cloak was bombazine over a lining of black silk, and into the lining's interlining she sewed not one pocket but three, distributed along the garment's length—one at the shoulder, one at the hip, one in the trailing hem—so that even if a searcher were somehow moved to unpick a seam, he would find the first empty and conclude himself mistaken before reaching the second. The papers themselves she split, the forgery copies cut and interleaved, so that no single hiding place held a complete document. Reassembly required knowledge of a key she sewed nowhere: the order of the fragments followed the sequence of stitches in the cloak's buttonholes, long-short-long, which only someone who had been taught to read her buttonholes—which meant Orsen, and Pell, and no one else breathing—could parse.

It was, she admitted to the room, the finest work of her life. Better than the trousseau she had made at nineteen for a marriage that lasted four years and produced no children. Better than the mourning clothes she had sewn for her mother, every seam straight and every stitch true while her hands shook. Those pieces had been made with skill and love. These were made with skill and purpose, and purpose, it turned out, steadied the hand even better than grief.

She worked through the morning, and as she worked she thought about her sister-in-wives—all those women across London, across the country, bent over needles at this very hour, making the world's holding-together invisible.

They said women had no head for conspiracy. Nell had heard it said at dinner tables and inquests and once, memorably, at a magistrate's bench, by a man whose own wig was held together with basting threads she could see from eight feet away. No head for conspiracy. Every household in England ran on a conspiracy of women—the withheld shilling, the unmentioned debt, the quiet arithmetic that stretched Sunday's roast to Thursday, the illness concealed so no employer would hear of it, the pregnancy hidden until it could be hidden no longer, the letters read and resealed, the savings buried in a garden under a rosebush whose planting was itself a code. Women had run conspiracies since Eve, most of them in service of keeping somebody alive, and the only reason men called them simple was that the conspiracies succeeded.

Well. Here was a conspiracy in service of the same old object: keeping people alive. Keeping the Marchmonts out of the dock, keeping the workshop's people out of prison, keeping the truth moving through the veins of the city the way a lining moved invisibly under a coat's shell—unseen, structural, essential.

She pressed the last seam, lifted the iron, and stood a moment with the warmth rising against her face.

* * *

The boy came back at noon for the wrapped parcel—garments travel boxed, tied with string, addressed in a round hand to *Mrs. Ferris, Widow's Walk, Highgate*—and Nell added the final touch: a laundry mark chalked inside each box flap, meaningless to any stranger, a route instruction to whoever received them. She tipped the boy extra to take the longer way to the station, avoiding the alley where the police still loitered some days, and watched him from the window until he turned the corner.

Then she sat down at her table and let her hands be empty, which was the part she liked least.

Empty hands thought. Empty hands remembered that the parcel was out of her sight now and into the world's, that twelve minutes of stitchery per sheet was all that stood between those pages and a cell, that the whole ingenious system depended on the infinite incuriosity of men in uniform—and that systems, however fine, were made of stitches, and every stitch was a hole.

Her mother had a saying: *a seam is just a tear that hasn't decided yet.* Nell had laughed at it once. She did not laugh at it now. Somewhere between her table and Highgate, in a station queue or a porter's trolley or a constable's bored glance, a seam might decide.

She was still sitting thus, thimble still on, when footsteps sounded on the stair—Orsen's tread, she knew it by its evenness, a driver's walk, weight balanced for motion—and a knock at her door that came in the particular rhythm they had agreed on: two, pause, three, like a waltz with a limp.

She opened it. Orsen stood in the passage with rain on their shoulders and something wrong behind their eyes, holding a newspaper folded to a column on the inside page.

"Nell," they said. "Have you seen this morning's Standard?"

"I've seen nothing but serge since dawn."

Orsen stepped inside and unfolded the paper on her cutting table, smoothing it flat with both hands as though it might behave better for being handled gently. A column, short, buried below a notice about dock strikes. *Demolition Order Served on Printworks Property in Clerkenwell Alley — Structures Condemned Following Survey — Occupants Given Fourteen Days.*

Fourteen days. She read it three times. The workshop below the Marchmonts'. The presses, the type, the ledgers, the darkroom where Pell examined his documents, the whole warm underground heart of everything they had built—condemned by survey, by paperwork, by some office where a pen had scratched and a building had died.

"That's not accident," Nell said. It was not a question, and Orsen did not treat it as one.

"Pell thinks the demolition is to destroy the ledgers legally. Fire next, if we don't clear them ourselves." Orsen's voice was level, but Nell had spent a lifetime reading what hands did during speech, and Orsen's hands were flat on the table the way a person flattens them to keep them from shaking. "They're coming for everything. And there's something else. Someone's been asking after me. At the livery stable. A gentleman, the hostler said. Well-dressed. Wanted to know my routes."

Nell looked at the newspaper, and at the door through which her parcel had gone not two hours since, riding toward Highgate with the truth sewn into its bones, and felt the peculiar clarity that came to her only at the worst moments—the clarity of the needle's eye, narrow and bright.

"Then we've fourteen days," she said, "and I've five coats' worth of pockets left in me. Tell me everything, love. Start at the beginning and don't leave out the parts you're afraid of."

Orsen sat down. Outside, the printworks clanged on, oblivious, counting out its own remaining time in sheets.
