## 11. Halloway Makes an Offer

The rain came in off the river that Tuesday like something with an errand to run—sideways, insistent, rattling the lamp-glass at the Marchmont gate. Orsen had learned long ago to read weather the way they read letters: not for what it said but for what it would cost. This weather meant wet wool, fogged windscreen, slow crossings on the Embankment. It also meant fewer eyes on the street. Rain was a kind of mercy, if you knew how to use it.

They were wiping down the Darracq's bonnet when the stranger's car drew up opposite the house.

It was a Humber, black and immaculate, too fine for a tradesman and too sober for a pleasure-seeker. The man who stepped out of it wore his overcoat the way certain men wore authority—not as clothing but as office—and he stood a moment under the plane tree, letting the rain strike his umbrella rather than his shoulders, and looked up at the Marchmont house as though taking its measure for a coffin.

Orsen had never seen him before. Orsen knew at once who he was.

Halloway. The name Pell had spoken three nights ago across a scarred table in the back of the printing works, in the voice of a man laying out an autopsy: *the trail points here*. A face now attached to it—broad, clean-shaven, ordinary in the way of men who did terrible things between luncheon and dinner.

"Driver," Halloway said pleasantly, when Orsen made no move to retreat. "Just the man I hoped to see. Might we speak privately? Ten minutes of your time." He produced a card case, opened it, and showed a plain white card with a single name engraved upon it, then closed it again before Orsen could read anything but the shape of the letters. "You know who I am. That saves us both some theater."

"Mrs. Marchmont is not at home."

"I haven't come for Mrs. Marchmont." His smile was small and dry, like paper left too near a flame. "I've come for you."

* * *

There is a particular terror that visits people who have spent years being invisible: the terror of being seen. Orsen felt it arrive in the chest like a hand closing around something soft. Every discipline they had built—the flat expression, the servant's half-step-back deference, the name spoken only in their own head—all of it strained against the fact that this man had come through the rain specifically, deliberately, for *them*.

Which meant he knew. Not everything—he could not know everything—but enough. Enough to make the offer he was about to make. And if he knew that much, then the household was already closer to ruin than even Pell feared.

"Private," Orsen said carefully. "Not here."

"The tea room at the Criterion is private enough at four o'clock. No one looks at anyone there." Halloway glanced upward at the grey sky with mild distaste. "Unless you'd prefer to stand in this. I'm told drivers are used to worse."

The insult was gentle, almost kind, and precisely aimed. It said: *I know your station. I know exactly how small you are permitted to be.* He thought he was speaking to someone whose price could be measured in shillings.

Orsen thought of Nell's needles pulling thread through coat linings by candlelight. Of Pell's ink-stained fingers flattening a copied page. Of Cressida's face in lamplight saying, *you haven't slept either.* They put all of it away in the locked room behind their eyes where every dangerous letter lived until it could be moved safely, and let their face go smooth as the Darracq's bonnet.

"Four o'clock," they said. "I'll be there after the errands."

"Sensible fellow." Halloway tipped his umbrella just enough to show the whole of his smile, and it was worse than the small one. "I do admire practical people. They're so much easier to reason with."

* * *

The Criterion at four was gaslight and gilt gone slightly brown with steam and cigar smoke, marble tables crowded close, conversation running in the low continuous roar that swallowed individual words the way the sea swallows footprints. Halloway had taken a corner table with its back to the wall—of course he had; men in his trade always sat with their backs to walls—and he rose halfway from his chair in a parody of courtesy that no one watching could have faulted.

He ordered for both of them without asking. Coffee for himself, chocolate for Orsen, which was either thoughtfulness or another small demonstration of how thoroughly he had researched them. With Halloway, Orsen suspected, the two were indistinguishable.

"You've been with the Marchmonts how long?" he began, stirring in sugar he didn't need. "Three years? Four? Long enough, certainly, to become furniture. The best servants are furniture, my father used to say. Present, useful, and unobserved." He smiled at Orsen over the spoon. "But you're not furniture, are you, Mr.—"

"Winterbourne."

"Winterbourne." He tasted the name as though checking it against a list. "No. You're the sort who reads."

The word landed like a dropped glass. Orsen kept their hands still around the cup and their breathing even, and inside the locked room behind their eyes, they began very calmly to move the furniture.

"Everyone reads," they said. "It's not illegal yet."

Halloway laughed—a real laugh, brief and genuine, and somehow more frightening than the smile. "Oh, I like you. I confess I expected sullenness, or that dreadful trembling deference they teach at service registers. Instead I get wit." He set the spoon down and leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight caught the fine grain of his skin, the immaculate collar, the wedding ring worn thin with age. A family man. A churchgoer, possibly. They were always the worst ones. "Let me be direct, since you appreciate directness. There is going to be trouble. Public trouble, involving people you may have heard mentioned—people you drive about town. When it comes, there will be inquiries, and inquiries are blunt instruments; they break whatever they touch, guilty or innocent. You will be touched. Your employers will certainly be touched. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless certain documents in certain hands find their way to me before the inquiry needs them." From his breast pocket he drew a folded paper—not a document, only a newspaper clipping, but he handled it like one—and laid it on the table between them, squared to the edge. "The workshop below the Marchmonts' old print premises in Clerkenwell. The presses, the type, and above all the ledgers. Names, dates, subscriptions, deliveries. Everything your Mrs. Marchmont has so industriously collected in her little kingdom of ink."

He said it plainly, without heat, and the plainness was the horror of it. He had not needed to describe the workshop. He had not needed to prove he knew about the ledgers, or their contents, or the cellar door with the loose brick beside it. He simply knew, the way a surgeon knows anatomy.

"In exchange," he continued, "you receive two hundred pounds—five hundred, if the ledgers are complete—and immunity. Real immunity, Winterbourne, not the whispered kind. Whatever the inquiry turns up about parcels driven, letters carried, pamphlets printed—you will be a witness, not a defendant. A young person of good character, deceived by clever people above your station. Juries weep for those." He sat back, letting the offer settle on the table like the bill. "You lose nothing but loyalty, and loyalty, I find, is the one commodity that never pays its own fare."

* * *

Orsen drank the chocolate slowly. It was good chocolate, thick and bitter-sweet, and they hated it.

The terror had not gone. It sat in them like swallowed stone, cold and heavy, and beneath it ran a second current, faster and stranger: *he is telling us things.* Every sentence was a confession wearing the clothes of a bribe. The workshop. The ledgers. An inquiry coming. A timeline implied in every *when it comes*. Ambrose Pell had spent weeks prying these facts loose one splinter at a time, and here was the man himself arranging them over hot chocolate like dishes at a buffet, because he believed Orsen was already bought, or cheaply buyable, and because powerful men cannot resist demonstrating their power to the furniture.

So Orsen listened. And while listening, they did the thing they did best in all the world: they read.

His cufflinks were plain gold, but the left one was scratched in a way the right wasn't—he wore gloves for most work but removed them for paperwork. His fingernails carried a faint grey shadow at the quick, the ghost of graphite and printer's dust; he had recently handled documents fresh from a press, and recently meant hours, not days. He glanced twice toward the doorway, not nervously but with the habit of a man timing something—or someone. He was late to meet whoever came next. Which meant his window with Orsen was short, and a short window made a man generous with details.

And when he spoke of the inquiry, his eyes went briefly to the window, to the street, doing arithmetic with days.

"How long," Orsen asked quietly, "do I have to decide?"

Halloway considered them over the rim of his cup. "Ten days," he said. "I would want the ledgers within ten days. After that, events proceed whether you've chosen sides or not, and I'm afraid I couldn't protect you from events. No one can."

Ten days. Said as easily as a train timetable. Pell had guessed weeks; Cressida had feared days; and here was the number itself, handed across a café table to a driver, wrapped in the fiction of generosity. Ten days until whatever manufactured catastrophe he and his patrons had assembled came down on the Marchmonts like the demolition ball waiting for Clerkenwell.

Two hundred pounds. Orsen turned the figure over and found, to their surprise, that it was almost funny. Two hundred pounds for the names of Nell and her girls, of Pell, of the two apprentice compositors who slept above the press, of the widow in Somers Town who stored paper in her coal hole. Two hundred pounds for forty lives, itemized in a ledger in Mrs. Marchmont's careful hand. He might as well have offered threepence.

"It's a great deal of money," Orsen said, and let the voice go humble, go uncertain, go exactly as small as he wanted them to be. "A person could get out of London on that. Start somewhere the trains don't know their name."

"One could." Satisfaction moved through Halloway like light through water. He believed it. That was the miracle of him; he wanted so badly to believe that everyone could be priced that he read hunger in a face that held only contempt. "I'll call here Friday—no, Saturday, I'm engaged Friday. You needn't give an answer. Simply tell me you'll keep the option open. Practical people keep options open."

"I'm practical," Orsen agreed.

They let him believe it. They watched him rise, settle his account with a sovereign left casually over the change, press his hat to his head, and pause at the table just long enough to lay one gloved finger beside the newspaper clipping he had never actually given them—a joke, a leash, a demonstration.

"Saturday, then, Winterbourne. Do think of your future." A beat, and the smile returned, smaller than ever. "It has such potential."

Then he was gone into the smoke and the murmur, his umbrella collected, his Humber pulling away from the kerst into the wet dark of the early evening, and Orsen sat alone with two cooling cups and the sound of their own heart working like a pump-handle in a mine.

* * *

They did not go straight home. Home—that was a word now, though no document in England acknowledged it—they did not go straight home, because home was being watched, and they would not bring Halloway's shadow to Nell's door. Instead they walked. Down Haymarket in the gathering dark, through Covent Garden where the porters shouted over flower crates, east along High Holborn with the gas lamps coming on one by one like a slow argument being won, until the streets narrowed and the smell of ink and oilcloth rose familiar as bread, and the alley took them in.

The workshop lights burned low. Inside, the press stood dismantled down to its iron bones, half-crated already; Nell's sewing table had migrated to the corner; Pell sat hunched over borrowed candlelight with a copied page pinned under his elbow, and he looked up when the door shut, and whatever was in Orsen's face made him set down his pen.

"He came to me," Orsen said. No preamble; there was no time left for preambles. "Halloway. Today, at the house. He offered me five hundred pounds for the ledgers, and immunity, and he told me—" Their voice held. They made it hold. "He gave me the date, Pell. Ten days. He wants his answer Saturday."

Silence, except the candle guttering. Nell had stopped threading her needle mid-stitch, the thread hanging from it like a single hair.

Pell took off his spectacles, polished them on his cuff with the deliberation of a man buying himself three seconds, and put them back on. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet and very steady, the voice of a clerk reading a verdict he has written himself.

"Then he's frightened," Pell said. "Men don't buy furniture unless they've discovered the furniture has been counting the silver." He pulled a fresh sheet toward him and uncapped the ink. "Sit down, Winterbourne. Tell me every word he said, in order, exactly as he said it. We have ten days."

Outside, the rain had found the alley again, running down the gutter past the door, carrying the city's filth toward the river—and Orsen sat, and began to talk, and understood with a strange calm that they had just lied to a powerful man with their whole face, and that the lie had been the easiest thing they had ever done.

Nine of the ten days were already spoken for. What remained would have to be enough.
