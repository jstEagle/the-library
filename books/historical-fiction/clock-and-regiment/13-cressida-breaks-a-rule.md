## 13. Cressida Breaks a Rule

The waiting, Cressida had decided, was the worst invention of the twentieth century.

Not the corsetry, which her mother's generation had suffered and which Cressida, in her second year at Bedford College, had already begun to quietly abandon in favor of a looser, plainer cut that scandalized the aunts. Not the lectures, delivered in that droning Cambridge cadence by men who spoke about women as one might speak about a geological formation—interesting, ancient, best left undisturbed. Not even the trial, which hung over the house like weather, darkening every room, its date circled in her father's diary in red ink as though a circle could hold it back.

It was the waiting. The sitting still. The being *managed*.

For three weeks now—since the Sunday dinner where her father had learned everything and refused, with that mild, immovable voice of his, to flee—Cressida had been permitted to know the shape of the danger and forbidden to touch it. She was to keep her routine. She was to walk to college by the same streets, at the same hour, and to note without acknowledging the man in the grey cap who had taken up a post near the corner of Torrington Square, and the woman who read the same newspaper on the same bench outside the chemist's, and who never, ever turned a page.

*You are the bait we must not spring*, her mother had said, not unkindly, holding Cressida's face in both cool hands. *If you bolt, they know we know. If you run, they follow you to everyone we love.*

So Cressida walked. She sat in lectures. She came home. She wound the hall clock. She was nineteen years old and being asked to be furniture, and every hour of it curdled something in her that she did not have a name for yet.

And all the while, the household moved around her in whispers.

She saw it in fragments. Orsen leaving at odd hours in the motorcar, returning with the headlights dark and the bonnet still ticking. Her mother descending to the workshop below stairs and coming up again with ink on her fingertips she made no effort to explain. The seamstress Nell arriving with garment bags that clinked—*clinked*, like a haberdashery had been liquefied and poured into them—and leaving with coats that hung wrong, too heavy at the seam. Pell came on Thursdays, sat in the kitchen with his back to the wall where he could see both doors, and drank tea he never finished, and left with his collar damp and his eyes on the street.

They were building something. She could feel it assembling itself around her, around all of them, brick by invisible brick—and she was not allowed to lay a single stone.

*You're the daughter*, Orsen had said, when she'd demanded a role. *You're what they're watching for. Every risk you take doubles for the rest of us.*

*And what am I supposed to do? Embroider?*

*If it comes to it. Nell will show you how.*

She had laughed then, and Orsen had not, and the silence between them had lasted two days.

The journalist's name was Edmund Fairweather, and she had it from overheard conversation, which she did not pretend to regret. He wrote for the *Northern Star*—a provincial paper, deliberately, because London editors could be bought and Birmingham editors apparently could not, or could not cheaply enough. He had met with her father once, in a railway refreshment room in the fog of that long drive north, and something had been arranged, and since then the arrangement had gone into the same silence that swallowed everything else in the house.

Cressida had a journalist's address. She had a fountain pen. She had, as of Tuesday, a free afternoon that her mother believed she was spending in the library.

She told herself it was not recklessness. She told herself it was *initiative*, which was a word from her father's side of the family, and that the suffrage movement had not been built by women who sat still and waited to be managed. She told herself that if the men of the Home Office could manufacture a bombing out of ink and spite, then a nineteen-year-old could certainly manufacture a letter out of honesty.

She wrote the letter three times. The first was too passionate. The second was too cautious. The third she posted from a pillar-box in Bloomsbury rather than the one on their own street, wearing a hat her mother would not recognize her in, and she felt, walking away from it, the specific lightness of a person who has done something irreversible and found it agrees with her.

The reply came on Saturday.

It came to the house—of course it came to the house, she had given the Marchmont address because she had not thought, because she had been in too much of a hurry to invent a poste restante, and the mistake sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone when she saw the envelope on the hall tray, squared neatly beside the bills, addressed in a small urgent hand: *Miss C. Marchmont*.

Fairweather had written: *Your letter reached me. Your father and I have an understanding which I will not compromise, but I will hear you. Not by post. Come to the British Museum reading room, Tuesday, half past two. Ask at the desk for the slip on Assyrian irrigation. Do not be followed. — E.F.*

She read it standing in the hall with her gloves still on, and she heard, behind her, the particular silence of a house in which someone has stopped moving.

Orsen stood at the end of the corridor with a silver tray under one arm and an expression Cressida had never seen on that careful face before—not anger, not yet, but the flat pale thing that comes before it.

"That's mine," Cressida said, which was stupid, and they both knew it.

"I read the post." Orsen's voice was very quiet. It was always very quiet; that was the thing about Orsen, everything about them ran quiet, the footsteps, the engine, the voice, as though the whole person had been engineered to pass through a room without disturbing the air. "You know I read the post."

"You read it to *protect* us. That's what you always say."

"Yes." Orsen set the tray down on the hall table, precisely, the way they did everything, and Cressida understood suddenly that the precision was the anger—that when Orsen was most furious they became most exact, like a clock wound past its tolerance. "And this morning, reading the post to protect you, I learned that you have arranged to meet a newspaperman in a public reading room, alone, three days after a man in a grey cap watched you buy chestnuts on Tottenham Court Road."

"You've been following *me*?"

"Someone has to. Since you won't tell anyone where you're going."

The hall clock ticked into the silence. Cressida became aware that her mother was somewhere in the house, and her father, and that the walls of the Marchmont residence, which had always seemed to her so solid, were in fact paper-thin with listening.

"I'm not a child," she said.

"No," Orsen agreed. "You're bait. There's a difference, and it matters."

* * *

She did not go on Tuesday. That was the compromise, extracted in her mother's study in an interview Cressida would remember for years: her mother, seated, very still, hands folded, listening to the whole of it—the letter, the reply, the reading room—without once interrupting, which was somehow worse than shouting.

"You will not go," Mrs. Marchmont said at last, "because if you are followed, they will not arrest you. Arrest is a courtesy they extend to people they're afraid of. They will simply *watch* you, and note whom you speak to, and within a fortnight every name we have protected will be in a file in Whitehall." She unfolded her hands. "You are not wrong to want to act, Cressida. You are wrong to want to act *alone*, which is the one way that has never once worked in the history of anything."

It was, Cressida thought later, the most infuriating thing about her mother: that she was nearly always right, and never once gentle about it.

So Tuesday came and went, and Cressida sat in a lecture on the Reform Acts and copied out Assyrian irrigation facts she did not need, and the grey-cap man bought chestnuts again, and the house went on whispering without her.

And then on Thursday, Nell did not come.

It was small at first. Nell was reliable the way tides were reliable; Thursday was her day, had been her day since before the trial loomed, since before the letters, since Cressida was a girl watching the seamstress kneel with pins in her mouth. But the workshop below stairs had entered its final days—Pell had said so at dinner, in the coded grammar they all now spoke: *the lease is short; the landlord is keen; we should have everything out by the weekend*—and there was packing, and hauling, and perhaps Nell had simply been kept elsewhere.

Except that at four o'clock Orsen went out in the motorcar without a word, and did not come back, and at six the hall telephone rang and her mother answered it and said, in a voice Cressida had never heard her use, "Where," and then, "I see," and then nothing at all for a long moment, and then, "Keep the line open," and rang off, and stood in the hall with her hand still on the receiver as though it were a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

"Cressida." Her mother's voice was level. That was how Cressida knew it was bad; her mother only went level in extremity, the way deep water goes still. "Nell was stopped this afternoon. Not arrested—*stopped*. Two men, plain clothes, asked her what she was carrying. She talked her way clear, but they'd already searched the garment bags once, last week, at the market. They're closing in on the workshop. Your father has gone to help move the press. And you—" her mother turned, and Cressida saw that she was afraid, actually afraid, in a way the trial had not made her, "—you will stay in this house tonight. Whatever you hear. Whatever you think of. *Stay*."

Cressida stayed. For four hours she stayed, sitting in the drawing room with a book she did not read while the house filled and emptied with men's voices and the scrape of crates, while her father came in grey-faced with dust in his hair and went down again, while Pell arrived and left and arrived again, while somewhere below the floorboards the great press that had printed ten thousand pamphlets was taken apart bolt by bolt and carried out into the London night like a body being smuggled out of a besieged city.

And at half past nine, alone in the drawing room with the lamps turned low, Cressida did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all evening.

The workshop was being emptied because it was lost. The network was scattering because it had to. Fairweather was the one thread that ran out of the house into the world beyond it—the thread that could carry the truth to print, to Parliament, to anyone who mattered—and that thread currently ran through her father, through Orsen, through men in railway refreshment rooms, through everyone but her.

Nell had been stopped. Tomorrow it might be Pell. The day after, the driver.

Cressida went upstairs, changed out of her house dress into the plain grey walking costume she kept for exactly this kind of disobedience, took the envelope with Fairweather's second letter—which had arrived that morning, which she had intercepted from the tray before Orsen could, which she had read by the light of her bedroom candle and then hidden inside a copy of *Jane Eyre*—and went out through the kitchen.

The kitchen window had a loose catch. She had known that since she was eleven.

* * *

The British Museum was closed at this hour; she had not been fool enough to suggest it. Fairweather's second letter had named a different place: a chophouse off Chancery Lane called the Bell & Anchor, nine o'clock, a booth at the back, *come by the alley door and sit with your back to the wall*. He had written, at the bottom, in the same small urgent hand: *Your father will forgive me. The truth does not keep office hours.*

She took a hansom from the rank on Gray's Inn Road because her nerve failed at the last moment about walking, and the whole ride she watched the street behind them through the little window, and saw nothing, and told herself that seeing nothing meant something, and knew even as she thought it that it meant nothing at all.

The Bell & Anchor was loud and brown and smelled of mustard and spilled porter. She found the alley door, and the booth at the back, and a man already sitting in it with his back to the wall—a lean, rumpled man of perhaps forty with ink on his right hand and the particular hungry patience of a man who has waited his whole career for a story and cannot quite believe it has sat down across from him.

"Miss Marchmont." He did not offer his hand. "You look like your father when he's about to do something inadvisable."

"I'm told it's a family trait."

"Sit. We have perhaps twenty minutes before the regulars notice a young woman alone, and then we have perhaps five more." He turned his beer glass a quarter-turn, a fidget, a tell. "Your father and I have an arrangement. He provides corroboration; I provide a page that cannot be bought. It is a good arrangement. It is slow, because slow is safe. And you have decided slow is a luxury."

"I've decided it's a coffin."

Fairweather looked at her for a moment, and something in his face shifted—the reporter's mask slipping just enough to show the man underneath, tired and earnest and a little afraid. "Tell me what you know that your father doesn't think I should have yet."

So she told him. She told him about the letter with the unfamiliar seal, or as much as she had been permitted to know of it; about the demolition order and the raid that was coming; about Halloway, the name that passed around the dinner table like a hot coal; about the watermark, which Pell had found and which she was not supposed to know existed and told him anyway, because what was the use of a watermark if it sat in a drawer. Fairweather listened without writing anything down, which she understood, even at nineteen, was the discipline of a man who had learned what notebooks cost a man in court.

And it was going *well*. That was the thing she could not stop thinking about afterward. It was going well—he was nodding, he was asking the right questions, he was saying *that corroborates, that corroborates, God help me, that corroborates*—and she had done it, she had *acted*, she had laid her single stone in the wall, when the chophouse door opened and the noise of the room changed.

It changed the way a room changes when a draught comes in. Not dramatically. Just—the sound bending, heads not quite turning, two men in the middle distance who had been talking to each other suddenly talking *past* each other, their eyes on the alley door.

Cressida's mouth went dry.

"That's my cue," Fairweather said quietly. He did not stand. He reached into his coat and slid a card across the table, face down, and covered it with his hand, and said, "Booth by the window, the Lamb and Flag, Covent Garden, Thursday, eight o'clock. Bring nothing. Tell no one." And then, louder, in the bored voice of a man ending an unsatisfactory conversation: "As I said, Miss Ashworth, the Assyrian material is quite beyond my remit. Good evening."

He stood and walked out through the front, unhurried, a man going home to his supper.

Cressida sat in the booth with her heart going like a sewing machine and made herself count to sixty. The two men had not approached her. They were by the door, conferring, one of them holding the alley door half open and looking out into the dark of it, and she understood with a cold, clarifying clarity that the alley door was the only door she had, and that it was currently being held open *for* her, like a trap with its mouth arranged.

She stood. She walked toward it, because hesitating would look like hesitating. The alley was black and smelled of cabbage water and coal smoke, and the man holding the door said, pleasantly, "Evening, miss. Bit dark down here," and did not move out of her way, and behind her she heard the other one's boots come off the floorboards onto the flagstones.

"Evening," Cressida said, and her voice came out steady, which astonished her, and then the alley door at the *far* end—the one she hadn't known was there, the one that must have been there because everything old had two doors—banged open, and headlamps swept the wet flagstones, and a motorcar came up the alley at a walking pace with its engine idling low and its near door already swinging wide.

"Miss Marchmont," said Orsen's voice out of the dark. "You're late for your father."

She got in. She would never know afterward how she got in—whether her legs decided or whether Orsen's hand at her elbow simply made the decision for her—but she was in, and the door shut, and the motorcar moved off at the pace of a funeral procession, which was, she understood, exactly the pace that would not attract attention, while behind them the two men stood in the alley mouth and watched the tail-lamps go, and one of them, she was nearly sure, wrote something down.

They drove in silence for six streets. Seven. Eight. Orsen's hands were perfect on the wheel, perfectly calm, and it was the calm that undid her, the terrible engineered calm of a person who had planned for this, who had *expected* this, who had perhaps been sitting outside the chophouse in the dark for the last hour waiting for the daughter to do exactly the thing everyone had told her not to do.

"You followed me," Cressida said.

"I followed the hansom."

"You *followed* me. The whole way. You knew."

"I read the second letter too," Orsen said. "Tuesday. It came to the house."

"You let me go. You sat outside and you *let me walk into that*—"

"I let you get close enough to learn what it costs." Orsen's voice was still quiet. Still level. And that was the thing that finally broke something open in Cressida, that terrible gentleness, that patience, as though she were a pupil and not a person. "The men in the alley. The door held open. That's what it costs, and you paid it in an alley instead of a cell, and I would rather you hated me tonight than—"

"Hated you?" It came out of her at full volume and she did not care, they were crossing Blackfriars Road and the night was loud and she was louder. "You think this is about *you*? You think I'm angry because I was frightened? I'm angry because I *succeeded*—do you understand? Fairweather corroborated everything. The watermark, Halloway, all of it—he has it now, he has it *because of me*, and not one of you would have given it to him for another month, another *year*, while you all sat in this house being careful, being slow, being *safe*—" her voice cracked and she hated that most of all, "—while they close in on Nell, while they burn the workshop, while my father circles a date in red ink and waits for the trial to *happen to* us—"

"Stop the self-pity," Orsen said, and it was the first harsh thing Cressida had ever heard them say, and it landed like a slap. "You were followed from your own front door. You led two of Halloway's men to a journalist. You gave them his face, his booth, his *paper*—and if they had taken you, you would have given them everything else inside an hour, because you are nineteen and brave and you have never once been questioned by men who do it professionally. Fairweather may hang for what you gave him tonight. Did you think of that? Did you think of *him*, for one moment, in your hurry to stop being furniture?"

The motorcar turned into the mews behind the Marchmont house and stopped, engine ticking, and neither of them moved.

Cressida found that she was crying, which she had not permitted herself to do in three weeks, and that the crying did not feel like weakness; it felt like a dam going, like three weeks of being managed and watched and *loved so carefully* finally finding the one crack in her they hadn't braced.

"I did it because no one would let me do anything," she said, smaller now. "You all keep me like a vase. Something to protect. Something that stays on the shelf."

For a long moment Orsen said nothing. The engine ticked. Somewhere a cat crossed the mews, and the lamplight through the windscreen caught the ink stains on Orsen's fingers—Cressida had never noticed before how permanent they were, how the work had got into the skin itself.

"I was a vase once," Orsen said at last, so quietly she almost lost it. "In a house like this one. I read the letters because no one would let me carry anything heavier." The hands on the wheel tightened, relaxed. "I know exactly what it is to be kept. That's why I'm hard on you. Not because I think you're fragile. Because I know what it does to a person, being fragile in other people's eyes, and I didn't want it to happen to you before you'd chosen what to be."

The anger went out of Cressida the way air goes out of a room when a window opens—not all at once, but enough to breathe.

"I'm not apologizing," she said.

"I know."

"I'd do it again. I'd do it *better*."

"I know that too." Orsen turned, finally, and in the lamplight their face was tired and young and, for the first time since Cressida had known them, unguarded. "Thursday. The Lamb and Flag, eight o'clock. Fairweather left a card. You're going to give it to me, and I'm going to drive you there myself, and we are going to sit in that booth together, and you are going to introduce me to the man whose life you nearly ended—because he'll need to know your face is known, and there's no one he'll believe faster than you."

Cressida stared. "You're—rewarding me?"

"I'm *using* you," Orsen said. "There's a difference, and it matters. From tonight you're not bait and you're not a vase. You're a courier who has already been seen with him, which makes you the only person in this house he can meet without raising fresh questions. Congratulations. You're now the most dangerous person we have."

It was, Cressida thought, the strangest compliment of her life, and the first one she had ever entirely believed.

They sat a moment longer in the ticking dark of the mews. Then Orsen said, in a voice that had gone carefully, deliberately light, "Your mother is going to ask where we were."

"Tell her the truth."

"She'll be furious."

"She'll be *proud*," Cressida said, opening the door, "and she'll never once say so. It's a family trait."

But as they came up the garden path, the kitchen window stood open a hand's width, and the lamp inside was still burning, and her mother's shadow was long across the floor, waiting up—and on the hall table, where Orsen would find it in the morning, a third envelope had already arrived, hand-delivered, no stamp, no seal at all.

Just her name, and inside, in block capitals:

*WE KNOW ABOUT THE JOURNALIST.*
