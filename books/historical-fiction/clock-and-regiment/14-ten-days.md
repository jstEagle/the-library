## 14. Ten Days

The third envelope sat on the hall table like a stone dropped into still water, and the water had not yet finished rippling when Orsen carried it, on a salver, into Mrs. Marchmont's study at seven o'clock precisely.

Mrs. Marchmont read it twice. She did not pale—Orsen had never seen her pale—but she set the paper down flat upon the blotter and smoothed it once with two fingers, the way she might smooth a crease from linen, and said: "Then we have less time than I thought. Ten days, if Halloway keeps to form. Perhaps fewer."

"You know his name."

"I know a great many names, Orsen. That is rather the trouble." She looked up then, and her eyes were not angry, which was somehow worse than fury would have been. "You read my letters. You have done for years. I have known since the winter of '11, when you began steaming the envelopes over the kitchen lamp instead of slitting them with your knife—a knife leaves a telltale nick, dear; you never did learn that." She folded the threat along its crease. "I have never once minded. Do you understand? Not once. But whatever you are doing now—and I can see that it is *something*, because Cressida has started humming when she lies, exactly as her father does—you will not do it alone in this house. Do we agree?"

Orsen agreed.

It was the first conversation they had ever finished without feeling smaller afterward, and it should have been a comfort. It was not. It was a door opening onto weather, and the weather was coming whether or not anyone stood ready on the step.

* * *

**Ten days remaining.**

Orsen copied by night.

The ledgers came out of the workshop after midnight, three of them, bound in grey board so ordinary that any constable glancing through the coal-hole would take them for grocer's accounts—which was, in a sense, what they were. Accounts of ink bought and pamphlets printed and journeys made, each entry a small confession written in Nell's neat hand or Pell's cramped one or Orsen's own careful capitals. Every name in the network lived between those covers. If Halloway's men seized them, the Marchmonts would be the least of the fallen.

So: copying. The household slept; Orsen did not, or slept in instalments, an hour here, forty minutes there, dozing upright in the driver's seat of the Daimler with their coat buttoned to the throat like a man waiting out a rain that would not stop. The copying was done by lamplight in the scullery, page by page, in a hand deliberately altered—looser, rounder, a clerk's hand, nobody's hand—so that even the copies could betray no one. It was slow work. It was maddening work. The lamp guttered. The nib caught and spat. Somewhere past three each morning the letters began to swim, and Orsen learned to stop before the swimming started, because a copyist's error could hang a person as surely as a perjurer's oath.

Nell came on the second night, unannounced, letting herself down the area steps with the soft tread of a woman who had spent twenty years moving through other people's houses unheard. She set her sewing basket on the scrubbed table, pushed aside the bread tin, and began threading needles by touch.

"You'll ruin your eyes," Orsen said.

"I'll ruin them faster standing at a machine for eleven hours," said Nell. "Read me the figures. My eyes are fresher than yours and I've a better memory for numbers, always did. You write; I'll hold the numbers." And this was how they worked until dawn, one reading, one writing, the ledger's quiet inventory of sedition passing between them in low voices while the house above breathed in its sleep, and it occurred to Orsen—not for the first time, but for the first time without bitterness—that invisibility shared was not invisibility at all. It was something else. It had no name yet. They were building it anyway.

**Nine days remaining.**

The hearse.

There was only one way to move a printing press through London without being stopped, and that was inside a vehicle that no policeman cared to look at twice. Mr. Tredgar of Tredgar & Sons, Undertakers, was a client of the Marchmonts'—he had voted for the cause with his rates, in his fashion, taking pamphlets alongside his order forms for coffins and wreaths—and he lent the second-hearse, the older one, its paint gone the colour of wet slate, its curtains faded to a grey that swallowed light. He asked no questions. He handed over the key with the gravity of a man surrendering a relative to the care of physicians.

"It wants a new spring on the left rear," he said. "Carry nothing living in her, nothing that breathes."

"Nothing that breathes," Orsen promised, and drove it back through morning traffic at a funereal twelve miles an hour, and discovered that Tredgar had undersold the vehicle's genius. Coaches parted before it. Constables saluted it. A newsboy fell respectfully silent as it passed. Grief, Orsen reflected, steering with two fingers, was the last great right of way, and London yielded to it as to a royal procession.

They rehearsed the route that afternoon and again at dusk: workshop to a lock-up under the railway arches at Vauxhall, thence—if the worst came—north. Orsen drove it four times, varying the turns, memorising the points where a tail could be lost: the market street where carts choked the carriageway by nine, the bridge with its two exits, the cul-de-sac behind the chapel that was not truly a cul-de-sac at all but opened, if one knew the gate, onto the towpath. Roads learned across six years of deliveries, every shortcut a sentence in a language only Orsen spoke fluently.

Cressida rode along on the last run, sitting very straight among the coffin-rests, her hands folded in her lap like a mourner.

"You're smiling," she accused.

"I'm rehearsing grief."

"You're enjoying yourself."

"A little," Orsen admitted. The fog was coming up off the river, and the streets went gold and blind under the lamps, and for one mile, one turn of one wheel, the fear let go. Then it returned, punctual as a debt.

**Eight days remaining.**

Pell brought word that the raid would include the alley premises as well—the printer's shop proper, not merely the workshop below the Marchmonts'—and that Halloway had engaged two men from the firm to act as 'expert witnesses' in the matter of the recovered documents. Experts who would swear, under oath, to the authenticity of forgeries Pell could identify by their very flaws.

"He taught me half of what I know about forgery," Pell said, meaning Halloway, though he said the name as if it tasted of copper. "The other half I learned catching him at it. His people never get the serifs right. On a capital T, the crossbar—look for the crossbar, it tapers wrong, thick at the left end. Machine-made type doesn't taper. Hands do."

So they practised forgery-spotting in the scullery, Pell manufacturing specimens with a fountain pen and a steady conscience, the others learning to see. Nell had the eye of a born detective; Cressida had the stubbornness; Orsen had neither talent nor patience for it and contented themselves with memorising instead—faces, routes, times, the geography of the next ten days laid out in the head like a map drawn on the inside of the skull.

That night the press came apart.

It was a Wheland hand-press, old and honest, cast iron painted green, and it weighed as much as sin. Orsen and Pell unbolted it in the dead hours—the platen, the bed, the frame in its pieces—wrapping each part in sacking and old curtain, lowering them down the coal chute on a rope into the waiting dark of the hearse. The type went into drawers, the drawers into crates, the crates into false coffin-shells Tredgar supplied, and there was something in the sight of a printing press resting where a body should lie that made Nell laugh, briefly, terribly, into her apron.

"They'll carry us all to our graves eventually," she said. "Might as well give the machinery a preview."

**Seven days remaining.**

A week gone, five hundred pages copied, and Orsen's handwriting—both hands, both disguises—had begun to deteriorate into something a graphologist could have hanged them with. They slept four hours and dreamed of envelopes. In the dreams the envelopes were all addressed to *them*, in block capitals, and every seal was wax, and the wax was blood-warm.

Mrs. Marchmont summoned them at eleven and asked, without preamble, what remained to be done.

"The ledgers dispersed tomorrow. The last of the type Wednesday. The press is already gone. The workshop will be empty by Friday, a day ahead of schedule, if the schedule holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then it empties faster."

She considered this. From somewhere in the house came the sound of Cressida at the piano, playing badly and with great commitment, which was her way of announcing that she was awake and unobserved and listening at doors.

"Orsen." Mrs. Marchmont turned her ring once upon her finger. "When this is finished—if it is finished well—I intend to offer you something more permanent than wages. I want you to know that now, in advance, so that you are not fighting these ten days as an employee. Fight them as what you actually are."

"What is that?" It came out rougher than intended.

"Ours," said Mrs. Marchmont, and rang the bell for tea.

**Six days remaining.**

The dispersal. Ledgers copied thrice, originals sewn—actually sewn, Nell's needle flying through coat linings and hat bands and the stiff interlining of a collar—into garments that left the house on six different bodies headed for six different addresses. A copy to the journalist's solicitor. A copy to Pell, lodged in a Left Luggage office at Paddington under a name Pell invented on the spot and immediately forgot, so that he had to write it on his cuff, which Orsen told him was the single most incriminating cuff in London. A copy nowhere, held only in Nell's memory, recited nightly like a rosary.

The rehearsal of routes continued. Fog came off the river again and stayed, and driving through it Orsen felt the city become navigable in a new way—every street a corridor, every corridor an exit, the whole of London reduced to a problem of sequence. *If stopped at A, proceed to B. If followed past B, abandon the vehicle at C.* They wrote none of it down. It lived in the skull-map now, complete.

**Five days remaining.**

Nothing happened.

Somehow this was worse. The house held its breath. The empty workshop below stairs echoed—or seemed to; an emptied room makes a particular silence, a silence with edges. Orsen polished the Daimler that did not need polishing, checked the hearse's suspect spring, walked the coal chute twice with a lantern looking for anything forgotten. Found a single piece of type, a lowercase *e*, rolled into a corner, and put it in their pocket without thinking, and did not take it out for months.

**Four days remaining.**

Cressida, chafing, was given work at last: she copied the address list in her own hand, in cipher, and memorised it, and burned her draft in the kitchen range, watching the ash with the expression of a woman who has been allowed, finally, to carry something heavier than a teapot.

"You're giving me this because you pity me," she said.

"I'm giving you this because you're the best liar in the house," said Orsen, "and I mean that as the compliment it is."

She smiled then, quick and true, and for a moment the kitchen was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the range.

**Three days remaining.**

Pell came at dusk, fast, hatless, which from Pell was a siren. He had heard—from a cousin still inside the force—that the raid had been moved forward. Not ten days. Not Friday. Sooner. How much sooner, the cousin could not or would not say; warrants were being drawn that week; the Inspector assigned was named Dane, and Dane was thorough.

"So it may be tomorrow," Mrs. Marchmont said, when the household gathered in the study, "or it may be Thursday, or it may be a rumour designed to make us bolt and be taken running. We cannot know. Therefore we behave as though it is tomorrow, and we do it calmly, and nobody runs." Her eye passed around the room—her daughter, the seamstress, the disgraced clerk, the driver—and rested on no one longer than another. "We are, I believe, ready. Which is an unusual sensation. I recommend it."

**Two days remaining.**

The final crate went down the coal chute at two in the morning, and the hearse pulled away at half past with Orsen at the wheel and the fog thick as wool, and no one followed them—or no one that Orsen could see, which through that fog amounted to the same faith. The lock-up door closed. The padlock clicked. The key went on a string around Orsen's neck, under the shirt, against the skin, where it lay like a small cold heartbeat.

Coming back through the sleeping city at four, the streets utterly empty, Orsen realised they were counting aloud—not the turns, not the miles, but the hours. *Two days. Two days.* Ritual, Nell called it, when Orsen confessed it later. Ritual is just fear that has learned to keep time.

**One day remaining.**

Everything that could be moved had been moved. Everything that could be copied had been copied and hidden and copied again. The workshop was a swept floor and four paler rectangles on the walls where shelves had hung. There was nothing left to prepare, and so Orsen prepared anyway—checked, rechecked, walked the route in the mind's eye a hundredth time—because the alternative to preparation was imagination, and imagination these days showed only one picture: the door coming open, the boots on the stair, the light swung round the emptied room.

On the last evening, Mrs. Marchmont sent up a plate from dinner—cold tongue, bread, a glass of sherry—to the scullery where Orsen sat over the final page of nothing in particular. Beneath the plate, a note, in her hand:

*Ten days ago you fought this as ours. Tomorrow, fight it as yours. —E.M.*

Orsen read it three times, and put it in their pocket beside the piece of type, and blew out the lamp.

One day. Perhaps less. Perhaps—

The bell at the area door rang, once, softly—the signal, but early, hours early, the wrong hour entirely—and through the scullery window, down the steps through the fog, came a shape Orsen knew, and a limp Orsen knew better, and Ambrose Pell stood dripping at the door with his collar up and his face gone the colour of paper, saying only:

"They've come for the witness. She's gone. And Dane's men are already asking at the alley."
