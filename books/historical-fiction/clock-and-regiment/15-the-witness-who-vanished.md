## 15. The Witness Who Vanished

Ambrose Pell had spent eleven years in the police service learning to read rooms the way other men read newspapers—quickly, hungrily, and with an eye for the lie buried three paragraphs down. The room he read now was the back parlour of Mrs. Fearnley's lodging house on Clerkenwell Road, and it told him nothing good.

The teacup was still full. The chair was pushed back at an angle that suggested haste rather than comfort. On the table lay a half-finished letter, the ink dry for hours by the look of it, addressed to *My dear sister Agnes* and stopping mid-sentence after four lines about the price of coal. The window sash was locked from inside. The landlady swore she had heard nothing in the night, though she slept like the dead and admitted it freely, as if it were a virtue.

The man who had sat in that chair was Josiah Crake. For nine years a journeyman forger in the employ of Halloway & Sons, Commercial and Legal Stationers. For the past six weeks, under Pell's careful coaxing and Orsen Winterbourne's quiet money, their witness.

And as of this morning—the seventeenth of October, a Wednesday, eleven days before Halloway's raid and two before Crake was to swear his deposition before a solicitor who owed Pell an old favour—Josiah Crake had vanished like smoke up a chimney.

Pell stood in the middle of the parlour and did not touch anything. That was habit. Evidence, even evidence nobody would ever collect, deserved respect. He looked at the teacup again. Full. Cold, when he bent close enough to feel the air above it. A man did not pour himself tea, drink none of it, write four lines to his sister, and then walk out of the world voluntarily. Not Crake. Crake was a nervous man, yes—a man whose hands shook so badly at their first meeting that Pell had made him hold them flat on the table like a schoolboy at prayers—but nervous men who agreed to testify were nervous in a particular direction. They flinched toward safety. They did not leave behind cold tea and unfinished letters.

They were taken.

He let himself out through the scullery, nodded to the landlady, and stood a moment on the step, breathing the grey morning air of Clerkenwell, which smelled of iron filings and horse dung and yesterday's fog. Then he set off at a clerk's unhurried pace—he had never quite lost the walk—toward the printer's alley, and all the way there he made himself do the thing he dreaded most.

He made himself count the people who had known where Josiah Crake was sleeping.

* * *

There were five. Himself; Orsen; Nell Quill, who had sewn Crake's rent money into the lining of his own coat so he could not drink it; Cressida Marchmont, who had insisted on knowing everything once she learned anything; and old Wetherall the compositor, who technically knew only that "a friend of the cause" lodged on Clerkenwell Road but who missed nothing and forgot less.

Five people. And Pell trusted four of them with his life, which was precisely why the arithmetic frightened him. Because one of five was not a large number. One of five was the width of a doorway. He had seen cases turn on less—had watched, in his years at the Yard, entire prosecutions collapse because some junior man had mentioned a name over a pint, or a landlady had been paid in sovereigns instead of silence. Institutions leaked. It was what they did. The question that had ended his career—*who asked you to change the report?*—had never been answered, and here it came round again wearing new faces.

He found Orsen in the alley behind the workshop, stacking type trays into a crate lined with felt, working with the methodical economy Pell had come to admire and, lately, to find slightly unnerving. There were people who moved through the world loudly and people who moved through it quietly, and Orsen Winterbourne belonged so thoroughly to the second category that Pell sometimes wondered whether the quiet itself was the disguise. He put the thought away. He was getting ahead of himself, and ahead of himself was exactly where paranoia wanted him.

"Crake's gone," he said, without preamble, because preamble was a luxury they no longer owned.

Orsen's hands stopped on the tray. Did not drop it, did not fumble it—simply stopped, held level, as if the news were a weight to be measured before it was borne. "Gone how?"

"Gone gone. Window latched from inside, tea untouched, letter to his sister abandoned mid-line. Either he climbed out through the flue or someone persuaded him to leave quietly." Pell kept his voice low; sound carried strangely in the alley. "I went there at seven. Landlady says he came home at half past eight last night, said good evening, went up. She heard nothing after."

"He wouldn't run." It was not a question. Orsen set the tray down with exaggerated care. "He wanted this too much. Nine years of doing Halloway's dirty work—he talked about it the way a man talks about a debt he's been carrying. He wanted to be clean."

"That's my reading. Which leaves us with the other reading." Pell glanced up the alley toward the street, where the morning traffic rattled past, indifferent. "Somebody knew where he was. Somebody either followed him home last night or was told."

He watched Orsen take that in. Watched the small muscles around the eyes do their work. He was not a cruel man, Ambrose Pell—or he had tried not to be, since the day they took his badge and he discovered how much cruelty institutions excuse—and he took no pleasure in what he said next. But he had spent eleven years watching guilty men go free because decent people declined to suspect each other soon enough.

"Five of us knew," he said. "You. Me. Nell. Miss Cressida. Wetherall, in a fashion."

The silence that followed lasted perhaps four seconds and cost Pell more than any silence of his career.

"You don't think—" Orsen began.

"I don't think anything yet. That's the point. I'm telling you what I know, which is that Crake is gone, and that the number of people who could have made him gone is short." Pell rubbed the bridge of his nose. His eyes ached; they had ached since the fire in the alley, though he had told no one. "I'm also telling you that I intend to ask questions of everyone on that list, including myself, and that you should let me. If we start protecting each other from awkward questions now, we're finished. Halloway won't need forgers. He'll only need patience."

Orsen studied him for a long moment. In the grey light of the alley, with the felt-lined crate between them, Pell thought they looked very young and very old at once—which, he had gathered, was roughly accurate. Then Orsen nodded, once, the nod of a driver accepting a route.

"Ask your questions," they said. "But Pell. Be careful what you ask Nell. She's carried more of this than any of us, and she doesn't rattle easy, but there's a difference between not rattling and not bleeding."

"There always is," said Pell. "That's why I'll be careful with everyone. Including her."

* * *

He started with Wetherall, because Wetherall was easiest and because Pell believed in warming up the way boxers did. The old compositor received him in the workshop among the dismantled skeleton of the press, each piece wrapped in oilcloth and labelled in Wetherall's copperplate hand, as though disassembly were simply printing conducted in reverse. He listened to Pell's account with his thumbs hooked in his waistcoat, and when it was done he snorted.

"Clerkenwell Road? Never knew the street till this minute." He turned to the shelf where his ledgers lived and produced, without hesitation, a small notebook. "Here. Every errand I've run since Michaelmas. Look for yourself. I've been to Farringdon twice, the Angel once, and a public house in Saffron Hill I won't defend. No Clerkenwell Road. You can check the dates against the tram timetables if you've a mind to insult me properly."

Pell checked anyway, because he had promised himself he would insult everyone equally, and Wetherall watched him do it with something very like satisfaction, as though being suspected rigorously were a courtesy the young no longer extended. When Pell closed the notebook, the old man fixed him with eyes gone pale as weak tea.

"You're right to look," Wetherall said. "Just make sure you're looking for the hole and not hunting for somewhere to hang your fear. Fear wants a hanger, boy. Don't give it one before it's earned."

Pell thanked him and went out feeling neither better nor worse, which he recognized as the honest result of a thorough inquiry.

Nell he found at her lodgings in a court off Rosebery Avenue, bent over her work-table with thirty garments in various states of strategic surgery, seams opened and waiting. She heard him out with her needle still moving, threading a hem that was mostly lead shot and partly hope, and her face while he spoke gave away nothing—which was itself informative, because Pell remembered what her face had done when the alley burned. It had given away grief, plain and enormous. A face that could do that could do guilt, and it was doing nothing now except concentration.

"You're asking whether I sold him," she said at last, tying off. "Say it plain, Mr. Pell. We're past wrapping things."

"I'm asking whether anyone could have followed you to him. You carried his coat. You carried the money."

"And I walked that coat to Clerkenwell Road the long way round, changed omnibuses at the Angel without needing to, and looked in every window on the street for a man standing still." She bit her thread. "Nobody was standing still. Nobody has ever been standing still behind me, Mr. Pell, because I learned that trick before I learned to sew a straight seam. You can believe me or not, but if you don't, ask yourself what I'd gain. I've got no wages in this but clean hands at the end."

"That's more than most people get," Pell said quietly.

"Yes," said Nell. "It is."

She let him look at her ledger of deliveries—kept in a cipher of her own devising, knots and dashes that meant nothing until translated—and he copied out the relevant entries into his own book, and left with his suspicion of her no smaller than his suspicion of anyone else, which was to say: present, weighed, unresolved. It was the most uncomfortable he had felt since the dismissal. He noticed, with professional detachment, that he would have to become comfortable with it.

* * *

Cressida Marchmont refused to be interviewed in her own house.

"Not with my mother's footman listening at doors," she said, meeting him instead at the Reading Room of a library in Bloomsbury where, she had established over weeks of experiment, no watcher could follow without becoming conspicuous. She sat opposite him with an open periodical as shield and spoke barely above the sound of turning pages. Her colour was high. He had prepared himself for tears or fury and got neither; what he got was something colder and more alarming.

"I've been making a list since you sent word," she said, and produced from her muff a sheet folded to the size of a visiting card. "Everyone who knew. Everyone who could have known. Times. Routes. Who was where when Crake was moved, when the rent money went, when the deposition was arranged. I've been doing it for three days, Mr. Pell—not since your note, since *before* your note, because I've been followed myself since September and I've had time to think about what following looks like."

Pell unfolded the paper. It was immaculate. Dates in columns, initials in margins, arrows connecting events he had not known were connected. He read it twice and felt the floor of his certainty tilt gently.

"This entry," he said. "The eighth. 'P. to C., via J.' What's that?"

"Myself, via Josiah." She met his eyes steadily. "I wrote to him once. Before the arrangement, when Papa and I were still deciding whether to stand. I wanted to hear from his own mouth what Halloway's firm had done for him—what they'd made him do. So I wrote care of the lodging house, unsigned, and he wrote back, and I burned both letters. But the letters existed, Mr. Pell. Two letters, passing through a lodging house on Clerkenwell Road, in the weeks before he vanished."

The library's quiet pressed against Pell's ears. He became aware of his own pulse.

"You didn't tell me this."

"No," said Cressida. "Because I judged, correctly, that you'd tell me off for taking the risk, and I judged incorrectly that the risk was finished. That's my fault and I own it." Her chin came up, and for a moment she looked so much like her mother that Pell nearly smiled despite everything. "But you should know that I've been asking myself ever since whether my letter is what brought someone to that house. Whether I drew the map and someone else walked it. So before you turn your suspicion on me, Mr. Pell, understand that I've already turned it on myself, and I've examined the matter thoroughly, and I am *not* your leak—but I might be your mistake, and those aren't the same thing, and you'd better be able to tell them apart."

For a long moment Pell could not speak. When he could, he folded her paper along its original creases and put it in his breast pocket.

"Miss Marchmont," he said, "you've just done in ten minutes what the Yard failed to do in the whole of the Fenwick affair. May I keep this?"

"You may. And Mr. Pell—" she rose, tucking her periodical under her arm— "when you've finished suspecting everyone, including yourself, do come and tell us what you've concluded. Because Orsen hasn't slept in four nights, and Nell is sewing herself into a wound, and if this alliance breaks over suspicion, Halloway will not need to lift a finger. We'll have done it for him. He is, I imagine, rather counting on it."

* * *

He did not conclude anything that night. He sat in his rooms over the shuttered shop in Saffron Hill with his case notes spread out like a patient on a table, and he performed the examination he had promised himself, beginning with the man he knew best.

Ambrose Pell. Dismissed clerk. Knew Crake's address. Had visited it alone, four times, at varying hours, once after dark. Had walked routes that could be memorized. Had, on the second visit, been nearly certain he was not followed, and merely certain on the third. *Nearly certain*, written out in his own hand, looked different from *certain*. He stared at the words a long time.

He went back through the others in the same fashion, coldly, giving each the same charity and the same rigour: Orsen, who had driven Crake twice and lost no tails that Pell could identify but who moved through London like water finding cracks, untraceable almost by nature, and therefore unverifiable; Nell, cleared on method but never on motive; Wetherall, cleared on paper; Cressida, whose single careless kindness might have lit the fuse regardless of who struck the match.

And underneath all of it, patient as sediment, the alternative hypothesis he had been refusing to look at directly, the way one refuses to look at the sun.

That no one had leaked at all. That Crake had been followed the simple, stupid, old-fashioned way—by professionals, hired early, who had marked every visitor to the lodging house for weeks and needed no traitor's help whatsoever. That Halloway's firm had simply done what firms like it existed to do: watched, waited, and removed.

It was, Pell had to admit, the most likely explanation. It required no betrayal. It required only money, and Halloway had plenty.

So why, then, sitting up alone at two in the morning with the candle guttering, did the likeliest explanation frighten him less than the unlikely one?

Because—if Crake had been taken by watchers who had done diligent, competent surveillance—then those watchers had seen everything. Every visit. Every face. The comings and goings of a network that believed itself invisible. And if that were true, then the leak was not a person among the five.

The leak was the pattern itself. Their whole method of meeting, moving, trusting—the clockwork of their little regiment, regular as prayer. Anyone who had observed it long enough could reconstruct it, and what could be reconstructed could be anticipated, and what could be anticipated—

Pell sat up so suddenly the candle went out.

In the dark, his heart hammering, he ran it forward: if the watchers had mapped the network's habits, then they knew not only where Crake had slept. They knew the alley. They knew the workshop. They knew, or could deduce, the routes by which the ledgers and the press and the scorched watermark page were being moved piece by piece toward safety—and the raid, when it came, would not fall on an empty building at all. It would fall on the road between buildings. On the crates. On whoever was carrying them.

On the borrowed hearse.

He dressed in the dark, hands steady by main force, and let himself out into the Saffron Hill night, heading for the Marchmonts' mews, rehearsing as he went the words that would shatter whatever remained of the group's fragile peace: *Stop the move. Change everything. We are not betrayed—we are read.*

Two streets from the mews, at the corner where the gaslight failed and the darkness pooled like spilled ink, he saw the carriage.

It stood with its lamps hooded, horses breathing steam, positioned precisely where a vehicle could wait unseen and watch the entrance to the Marchmont stables. As Pell froze in the shadow of a wall, a figure stepped down from the box, crossed to the area door of the mews house, and slipped inside without knocking—as though it had a key, as though it had done it before.

And by the cut of the coat, the roll of the shoulder, the terrible economy of the movement, Ambrose Pell knew the silhouette as well as he had ever known anything in eleven years of reading rooms.

It was Orsen Winterbourne.
