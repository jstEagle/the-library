## 16. Smoke Over the Alley

Nell Quill had always known fire would come for her eventually.

She was a seamstress by trade and by temperament, and a seamstress lived her whole life in the shadow of flame. Candles at the cutting table. Heated irons for pressing seams flat. The gas jets they weren't supposed to use after dark but used anyway because eyes failed faster than fingers and there was never enough light in a room that belonged to someone else's house. Every woman she'd trained with carried the same small dread, tucked behind the pincushion: one knocked candle, one dropped iron, and thirty years of work gone up in the time it took to scream.

So when she came around the corner of Cutter's Lane at half past four in the morning and saw the glow above the rooftops—orange, pulsing, greedy—her body understood before her mind did. Her feet stopped. Her hands, cold inside her gloves, went to her throat the way they did when a pin slipped and drew blood.

"No," she said, to nobody. "No, no, no."

The alley was burning.

* * *

She had been out because of a hem. That was the absurdity she would carry afterward, the detail that made her laugh at strange moments for the rest of her life: she had been awake since two o'clock over a *hem*. A grey wool coat bound for the safehouse in Kentish Town, one of seven garments leaving the city that day, each of them lined with paper that wasn't paper—copies of the ledgers, pages Pell had drafted and Orsen had checked, folded to the width of a pencil and sewn into the interlining where no customs man or police inspector had ever thought to look. Nell had found a flaw in her own work at two in the morning. A puckering along the left pocket seam, invisible to any eye but hers, the kind of imperfection that whispered *cut me open here.* She could not leave it. She did not leave flaws. Flaws were how women got hanged.

So she had threaded a needle by lamplight, unpicked six inches of stitching, resewn it flatter than the original, pressed it with the iron she kept hot for exactly such purposes, and only then—only then, at half past three, in a coat that weighed perhaps four ounces more than God intended—had she wrapped the garment in brown paper, put on her boots and shawl, and set out through the empty streets to deliver it early rather than let it sit in her rooms a moment longer.

The package was under her arm now. The coat that could hang forty people was pressed against her ribs while the alley that had sheltered them all turned into a lantern against the night sky.

She ran.

* * *

Cutter's Lane was not properly a lane; it was a crooked gutter of a street behind Fetterbrook, where the buildings leaned toward one another like conspirators and the workshops stacked on top of the shops three and four storeys high, timber-framed, tar-black, dry as a sermon in August. The Marchmonts' workshop—the press room, the type cabinets, the drying lines where pamphlets hung like washing—occupied the second floor of number nine, above a stationer who sold pencils to schoolboys and knew nothing and was left alone precisely because he knew nothing.

By the time Nell reached the mouth of the alley the stationer's ground floor was already lost, flames standing in the windows like choirboys in a pew, singing. The heat came off the street in waves she felt through her shawl. Somewhere above, glass burst and rained down in bright crumbs.

"Number nine." She caught the sleeve of a bystander, a night-soil man still in his harness, soot already settling on his shoulders like snow. "The upper floor. Is anyone up there?"

"Weren't nobody living in it, miss. Watchman raised the cry from the far end. Fire brigade's coming—" and indeed she could hear the bells now, distant, doubled, trebled—"but it's gone up like paper, hasn't it? These old places."

Like paper. Yes. Like paper, like ink, like type cast in lead that would run in the heat and pool in the shapes of letters nobody would ever read again.

Nell stood in the road and did arithmetic. The presses—two of them, the big Albion and the little hand-press Mr. Marchmont called the Widow—were scheduled for removal Thursday. Today was Tuesday. Ten days, Halloway had told Orsen, and everyone had built their calendar on those ten days, and the raid was to come first anyway, and they had rehearsed and rehearsed. But the type—the type was still in its cabinets. Three cases of founder's type, some of it fifty years old, plus the display faces and the brass rules and the composing stone. The ledgers were meant to be moved Wednesday. The stock of paper, a fortnight's worth, still sat wrapped in the storeroom.

All of it, everything, was two days early dying.

Unless—

"The back," she said. "Is there a way round the back?"

"Coal yard, miss, but the fence—"

She was already running.

* * *

The coal yard backed onto the lane's rear yards through a fence of vertical boards that had been weathering since Victoria's coronation. Nell went at it sideways, got a boot in a gap between boards, and tore a hole large enough to admit a woman who had spent twenty years fitting herself through spaces never meant for her—attics, cellars, the gaps between what was said and what was meant. She landed in coal dust, coughed once, and looked up.

Number nine's rear wall stood black against the orange. The fire hadn't taken the back yet—it was eating forward from the front stairwell, the way fire eats, hungry mouths first—but smoke poured along the eaves in a low grey tide, and the window of the storeroom glowed dull red at the corners like a poker left in the grate too long.

There was a door. There was always a door; the workshop had been built by men who smuggled things, and they had built as smugglers build. Nell put her shoulder to it. It gave on the second shove, and the heat rolled out to meet her, and the smoke came low across the floor like water finding its level.

Do not breathe deeply. Stay low. Count your steps.

She knew the room blind. She had sewn in it, eaten in it, argued with Orsen in it about whether a straight stitch or a backstitch held paper better (backstitch, obviously; Orsen had conceded with the grace of a person who had never truly disagreed). Twelve steps from the rear door to the storeroom partition. Fourteen to the type cabinet against the north wall, the old case with the loose top drawer that everyone remembered to lift rather than pull.

She counted eleven steps and hit the partition with her hands out. The wood was hot but not burning. Not yet. She turned along it, found the cabinet by touch, and dragged open the top drawer—one of the display faces, big heavy sorts—and then the lower drawers, the body text, the bread and butter of every pamphlet they'd ever printed. Too much. Far too much. A cabinet of type weighed more than a horse. She could not carry a cabinet. She could carry a drawer. Perhaps two, if she ruined herself doing it.

And the ledger page.

That was the thing, wasn't it. That was why her feet had carried her here instead of away, why some part of her had known before her mind said it aloud: the page. Pell's scorched treasure, recovered from nowhere, the sheet of paper pulled from Halloway's own wastebasket that proved—by watermark, by stock, by the smug little fleur-de-lis embedded in the rag content—that the forged evidence against the Marchmonts had been produced on paper sold exclusively to Halloway's office. It lived in the flat tin box behind the third shelf of the storeroom, because Pell trusted locks less than he trusted shelves, and shelves less than he trusted Nell, and Nell least of all, which was the highest compliment he knew how to pay anyone.

Smoke was at her waist now, curling, thickening. The window above the storeroom shelf cracked—a sound like a knuckle cracking, enormous—and a finger of flame licked down the inside of the wall and touched the edge of the paper stock.

Wrapped paper. A fortnight's worth. Dry as a sermon.

Nell did not think about it. Thinking was for people with time. She wrenched the tin box from the shelf—hot, oh, it was hot, her gloves sizzled faintly against the metal—thrust it inside her shawl against her chest, seized the top drawer of type with both hands, and turned back into the smoke.

Twelve steps back. Eleven. Ten. Her eyes streamed. The smoke found the seam between her lips and teeth and coated her tongue with the taste of burnt ink, which was, she thought absurdly, the taste of every word she'd ever helped make free. Nine. Eight. Something collapsed upstairs with a roar like a train and the whole world went orange and raining embers. Seven. Six. Her shin struck something—the leg of the worktable—and she went down on one knee among the falling sparks and rose without deciding to rise, because women of her profession learned long ago that falling was permitted only if rising followed within the same heartbeat. Five. Four. The door. Air. Cold air, sweet as communion wine, and she stumbled out into the coal yard with the drawer in her arms and the box against her heart and her lungs full of knives.

Behind her, with a sound like a great book closing, the roof of number nine came down.

* * *

She sat in the coal dust with her back against the fence and coughed until she saw stars that had nothing to do with the sky. The night-soil man appeared at the hole she'd torn, his face grey with more than soot.

"Miss! Miss, you're bleeding—"

"It's my hands." They were. The palms, through the ruined gloves; the tin box had branded her gently, a map of red already rising. "The type. Take the drawer—mind, don't tip it, the letters will spill and mix and they're no good mixed—take it to the woman sitting on the step across the yard, tell her Nell says hide it under the sacking."

"There isn't a woman sitting—"

"There is now," said Nell, and began, very slowly, to laugh, because shock is a joker and grief is worse, and somewhere in the middle of the laugh the tears came, and the two of them together tasted like smoke.

Because it was gone. All of it. Twenty years of the trade compressed into one street—the Widow press that Mr. Marchmont had coaxed back to life letter by letter, the drying lines, the cutting tables scarred with the initials of apprentices dead before Nell was born, the kettle in the corner that was strictly for tea and secretly for steaming open nothing, ever, they'd have fired anyone who steamed anything open. Gone in an hour. And not by accident. She knew arson the way she knew a bad seam: the fire had started at the stairwell, the single point that would block escape upward and guarantee consumption downward, in a building with no lodgers and no watchman on the upper floors, hours before any inspection would bring help. Someone had wanted this particular fire, in this particular place, tonight.

Two days early. Which meant their ten days were a lie, which meant Halloway—or whoever moved Halloway's hand—had never intended to wait, which meant everything they had rehearsed for Thursday was happening now, tonight, while they slept.

Which meant Orsen didn't know yet.

Nell closed her eyes and saw the network laid out in her mind the way she saw a pattern laid on cloth: the Marchmonts in their square-cut house, Orsen sleeping badly in the loft over the stable, Pell in his rooms with his unbought conscience, the safehouses strung across the city like beads on thread she herself had sewn. Every bead now in danger of the same flame. If they would burn Cutter's Lane two days early, what would they not burn?

She opened her eyes. Across the yard, the night-soil man was setting the type drawer carefully into the sacking as instructed, handling it, she noticed with a distant fondness, better than most printers' devils handled type. The fire brigade had arrived at last and was performing the ancient English ceremony of saving the neighbouring buildings while the target building finished its business. Flames stood up out of number nine's skull and licked at the belly of the sky. A small crowd had gathered at the lane's mouth, as crowds do, silent and warm-faced and grateful it wasn't their street.

Nell got to her feet. Everything hurt in the ordinary way, which meant nothing was broken. The tin box hung against her chest inside the shawl, and when she pressed her palm to it she felt the warmth of it still, and beneath the warmth, through the metal, the hard rectangular certainty of what it held.

One scorched page. Watermark and all. The one piece of proof that had survived the night, saved by a tin box and a seamstress's memory of where shelves keep their secrets.

Loss, she thought, is just fabric before it's cut. You weep over the yardage and then you cut anyway, because the garment doesn't make itself, and grief that doesn't get cut and sewn into something is only cloth rotting in a chest.

Her purpose had never been sharper. It fit her hand like a needle.

She took the type drawer back from the night-soil man with thanks and a sovereign pressed into his palm—"and you never saw me, mind"—wrapped it in sacking, and walked out of the coal yard into streets going grey with the first light, past the crowd, past the firemen, past the black and smoking skeleton of everything she had helped build, without looking back more than twice.

Then, at the corner, she looked back once more, properly, the way you look at a grave you intend to visit again.

And it was there, in that last look, that she saw him: a man standing apart from the crowd, on the far pavement, not watching the fire at all. Watching the people. His hat was wrong for his coat and his coat was wrong for the hour, and he held a small notebook, and every time a soot-blackened figure emerged from the alley's mouth his pencil moved.

He was counting who came. He was writing down who cared.

Nell turned up her collar, shifted the sacking bundle to her hip like a woman carrying washing, and walked in the opposite direction from home—because home was where Orsen was, and home was where she was going, but no trail worth its salt ran straight.

Behind her the sky over Cutter's Lane turned the colour of a bruise healing, and the smoke rose all morning, visible from the bridges, visible from the parks, a black flag flown by nobody over a war that had, in the space of one night, stopped being a matter of documents and become a matter of survival.

She had six garments left to deliver. She had a message to carry that no coat lining could hold.

Two days early. God help them, they had two days.
