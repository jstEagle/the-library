## 18. The Raid

The knock came at nine minutes past seven in the morning, and Orsen knew before their hand touched the latch that it was the police.

There was a quality to certain knocks—a patience in them, an official weight. A tradesman rapped twice and waited as though the door owed him money. A friend scratched. But this knock fell four times, evenly spaced, like the ticking of something that had all morning and intended to use it. Orsen had spent eleven years learning the language of doors, of parcels, of footsteps on pavement, and this knock spoke only one word: *warrant*.

They had expected it two days from now, on the schedule Halloway's man had let slip over brandy and false consideration. The fire in the alley had changed the arithmetic. Fire was impatience; impatience meant someone upstairs had grown nervous; nervousness meant the raid would come early, and it had.

Orsen smoothed their apron, checked that the kettle sat within easy reach of the stove, and opened the door.

Inspector Dane stood on the step with the grey, unhurried air of a man who had been woken early and had decided the world would pay for it. He was not large, but he occupied space the way ledgers occupy shelves—completely, and without apology. Behind him waited two constables and, further back at the mouth of the alley, a third figure Orsen did not recognise: heavyset, gloved, standing where he could see both the workshop door and the street. Not police. Police stood differently.

"Morning," Dane said. "Orsen Winterbourne. Driver and general man to the Marchmont household." He said it the way a man reads charges, even when he isn't. "We're here to search the premises."

"Good morning, Inspector." Orsen stepped back and held the door wide, the gesture of a man with nothing to hide and every intention of making the hiding take as long as possible. "You'll want tea. It's bitter out."

"We'll want the cellar open."

"The cellar's yours after the tea's on. My mother raised me never to offer a policeman a cold floor and a hot temper in the same visit." A lie about the mother—Orsen had no memory of any mother, only a laundry basket on a vicarage step and a name given by a curate who liked alliteration—but lies worked best when they came dressed in domestic detail.

Dane's eyes did not soften, but something in them flickered, the way a lamp gutters when a draught finds it. He had been to this workshop before; Orsen remembered him from two years ago, a noise complaint that had been no complaint at all, only a test. He had drunk tea then too. He had drunk it slowly, watching the room over the rim, and he had left saying nothing, which was its own kind of message.

"Two cups," Dane said now, to one of the constables. "Mr. Winterbourne doesn't drink with suspects."

"I'm not a suspect, Inspector. I'm staff."

"Everyone's staff until the paperwork says otherwise."

* * *

The cellar door stood at the back of the scullery, behind the coal range, its lock old and its hinges older. Orsen had oiled those hinges three nights ago and then, deliberately, let them gather a film of dust—not enough to look staged, just enough to creak authentically. When Dane pulled the door open, it complained exactly as a neglected door should, and Orsen permitted themselves one small internal note of satisfaction, filed away with all the other small satisfactions that made up a life spent invisible.

The cellar was empty.

Not innocent-empty—the emptiness of a place that has always held nothing—but *cleared*-empty, which is a different texture entirely. There were pale rectangles on the brickwork where shelving had stood for years. Drag-marks in the coal dust, swept imperfectly. A single brass tack glinting near the far wall where Nell's brother-in-law had dropped it while carrying the second press out through the coal chute at four in the morning, swearing under his breath in a way that would have scandalised his Methodist mother.

Dane saw all of it. Orsen watched him see all of it, and understood that the inspection had already succeeded or failed before a single drawer was opened, and that what happened next was not investigation but negotiation, conducted without a word of negotiation in it.

"You've moved house," Dane said.

"We've lost our lease." Orsen kept their voice level, pleasant, faintly bored. "Demolition order came through last month. You'll have seen the notice—it was posted on the alley gate for anyone to read. Whole row's coming down. The landlord gave us a fortnight to clear out, and my employer does not pay storage fees for other people's property."

"Convenient."

"Demolition usually is, for somebody. Rarely for the tenants."

Dane descended the steps. The constable with the lamps followed, and the two of them moved through the cellar with the thoroughness of men who had done this many times—tapping walls, checking the coal chute's iron cover, running fingers along shelf brackets looking for hollows. Orsen remained at the top of the stairs with the second constable, hands folded over the apron, wearing the expression of a servant obliged to wait upon his betters: respectful, transparent, faintly put-upon.

Invisibility, Orsen had learned, was not absence. It was presence so carefully modulated that no one thought to look twice. The trick was to be exactly what people expected down to the seams—and then, crucially, to be slightly duller than they expected. A driver who read letters was dangerous. A driver who talked about horse liniment and the price of oats was furniture.

Upstairs, the kettle began its low gathering murmur.

"The chute," Dane called up. "It opens onto what?"

"The yard behind, Inspector. Shared with the tailor's. There's a wall and a gate and then the mews."

"And you've used it recently."

"For coaling, yes. For moving out, also yes. It's remarkable what a man will carry up a chute rather than down a staircase when the staircase has a turn in it like a corkscrew." Orsen smiled, and let the smile carry just enough weariness to be true. "You're welcome to check the yard. Mind the puddle by the gate; the drain's been blocked since the frost."

Dane climbed back into the scullery light, dust on his knees, and looked at Orsen for a long moment. He had eyes the colour of dishwater and the habit of holding still while he thought, which most men found unnerving. Orsen found it restful, in a way—a man who thought before speaking was a man who could be given time to think.

"Tea," Dane said at last.

* * *

They sat in the workshop proper, at the long trestle table where pamphlets had been collated by lamplight for six years, where Cressida had learned to set type at fourteen and cut her thumb doing it and bled cheerfully all over a proof sheet about the Franchise Bill. The presses were gone, but the room remembered them. There were ink ghosts on the table's grain, black crescents worn into the wood by decades of elbows; there was the smell, faint but unmistakable, of oil and lead and paper, soaked so deep into the plaster that no demolition would ever fully get it out.

Orsen poured. Dane took the cup without thanks, drank, and set it down.

"Paperwork," he said. "You mentioned a lease. I'll see it."

"Certainly." Orsen produced the folder they had prepared two nights ago, sitting up with Pell until midnight while the clerk turned documents over in his careful, ruined-career hands, hunting for anything a policeman might smell. Pell had found two things: a date inconsistency in a delivery receipt, which they corrected, and a stamp on the lease renewal that was a shade too red, which they aged with cold tea and a warm iron. *A forgery is only ever caught by another forgery,* Pell had said, half-laughing, half-grieving. *I know because I used to catch mine.*

Dane read the lease the way he did everything—slowly, completely. Then the inventory of removed goods, signed by the landlord's agent (a fiction with excellent penmanship; Nell's sister had a beautiful hand and no politics whatsoever). Then the demolition notice, its creases genuine because it had genuinely hung on the gate for three weeks while everyone pretended not to see it.

"Marchmont," Dane said. "That name comes across my desk more than you'd credit, for a family of nobody radicals."

"They'd be heartbroken to hear the *nobody*, sir. Mrs. Marchmont works very hard at being somebody."

"I don't doubt it." He set the papers down and squared their edges, tap tap tap against the tabletop, a small metronome of a man deciding something. "Mr. Winterbourne. I've been a policeman twenty-two years. I know what a cleared cellar smells like, and it doesn't smell like coaldust and excuses."

"Then you know what a legal eviction smells like too."

"What I know—" and here Dane leaned forward, and his voice dropped into a register meant for no one else, "—is that somebody very high up wants this address searched, catalogued, and reported, and that the report has already been drafted. I've seen the shape of it. I was handed this job on a Tuesday with the paperwork pre-written, which is not how jobs are handed to honest men. So you'll understand if I don't take your lease at face value."

Orsen's heart was a fist against the ribs. They let none of it reach the surface. Eleven years of practice: the pulse is private, the face is public.

"What will you write, Inspector?"

For the first time, Dane almost smiled. It was a thin thing, quickly folded away, but it had been real. "I'll write what's true. That the premises are vacated pursuant to demolition, that nothing of evidentiary value was recovered, and that the tenant's man"—he tapped the inventory—"keeps better paperwork than half the Home Office, which I intend to note, since it seems to annoy them." He straightened. "I can't protect you from what comes next. Whatever's aimed at the Marchmonts isn't going to be deflected by an empty room."

"No, sir."

"But I'll tell you this for nothing, and it's worth nothing because you didn't hear it from me. The warrant named the cellar specifically. Somebody believes there are ledgers in that cellar, and they believe it strongly enough to have written the belief down before ever setting foot here." He stood, buttoned his coat. "Men who write conclusions before evidence don't stop at one conclusion. Watch the people around you, Mr. Winterbourne. And mind the third fellow at the end of the alley—he's not mine, and he's been counting the windows since we arrived."

* * *

The search continued for another hour anyway, because procedure is procedure and Dane was nothing if not procedurally impeccable. Drawers were opened and found to contain thread, receipts, a tin of biscuits, Cressida's abandoned French exercises. The loft above the workshop yielded pigeons, hay, and a broken perambulator. One constable tested the coal chute cover, hauled it open, and peered down into the yard where, if he had looked thirty degrees to the left, he would have seen the fresh scrape on the tailors' gatepost where the last crate—the ledgers themselves, copied and dispersed now, but the originals carried out regardless because Pell insisted originals mattered—had caught on its way through at half past four that morning.

Orsen made more tea. Answered questions. Produced, upon request, the key to every door including the door that no longer existed. Kept the voice level, the hands busy, the face furniture-plain, while inside, some animal part of them counted seconds against the memory of a crate's weight on a rope, a chute's iron lip, Nell's brother-in-law's sworn whisper: *last one, that's the last one, God help us if it's not.*

At ten minutes past nine, Dane pocketed his notebook.

"We're finished here." At the front step he paused, and lowered his voice once more. "A word of advice from a man who lost his career once already this year—no, don't ask, it's not my story to tell and the man it belongs to wouldn't thank me. Advice is this: when the paper trail runs out, they come for people. Papers can't testify. People can." His dishwater eyes held Orsen's, and in them, unexpectedly, was something that might have been pity, or might have been its cousin, warning. "Whatever you're carrying, Mr. Winterbourne—carry it fast."

He walked down the alley toward the waiting car. The heavyset man at the corner peeled away from the wall and followed him—not with him, Orsen noted, but after him, at a distance, in the manner of a man paid to watch the watcher.

Orsen stood in the doorway until all of them were gone. Then, and only then, they allowed their hands to shake—one long, silent tremor, knuckles white against the doorframe, the whole body briefly confessing what the face had refused to all morning.

Nine days, Halloway's man had said. The raid had come in two. Which meant the schedule had collapsed entirely, and collapsed schedules made careless men, and careless men made mistakes, and mistakes were the raw ore of truth. Pell would want to know. The journalist would need to know. Somewhere in the wreckage of the printer's alley, under Nell's scorched and stubborn hands, a watermark waited to say its single devastating sentence.

Orsen went inside, washed the teacups—because a household must be left as one found it, that was the first law of invisibility—and reached for their coat.

There was a card in the pocket that had not been there at dawn. Thick stock, expensive, unmarked except for a single embossed initial:

**H.**

And beneath it, in ink: *We should talk again before Friday.*
