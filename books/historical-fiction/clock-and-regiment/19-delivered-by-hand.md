## 19. Delivered by Hand

Cressida Marchmont had spent her whole life learning to read rooms the way her mother read people—quickly, silently, and with an eye for what was being concealed. On the morning the *Clarion* hit the stands, she read the breakfast room and found it transformed.

Her father sat at the head of the table with the newspaper open before him, and he was not reading it so much as holding it, the way a man holds a letter that has arrived from someone he had given up for dead. Her mother stood at the window with a cup of tea gone cold in her hands. Orsen was by the sideboard, ostensibly arranging the toast rack, and Cressida watched them pretend, with tremendous discipline, not to look at the paper at all.

"Well," her father said at last, into the ringing quiet. "Well."

He turned the paper around so the room could see it. The headline ran across five columns in type so heavy it seemed to have been driven into the page like nails:

**HOME OFFICE SUSPENDS PROCEEDINGS IN REGENT'S PARK CASE — FORGERIES LAID BARE — MP DEMANDS FULL INQUIRY**

Beneath it, a photograph she did not recognise: a narrow-faced man with careful hair and the flat, unhurried eyes of someone who had never once expected to be photographed under such circumstances. The caption identified him as Mr. Edmund Halloway, principal of Halloway & Verrall, commissioning agents and document specialists, whose premises had been searched the previous evening on warrant.

"They've printed the watermark," her mother said softly. "Look—the comparison, side by side. The stock sold exclusively to his office against the paper in the alleged evidence." She set down her cup with a small, decisive click. "Anyone can see it. Anyone."

"That was rather the point," said Ambrose Pell, from the corner where he had been permitted, for the first time in his professional life, to take breakfast in a Marchmont breakfast room. He looked as though he had not slept in three days and as though he would happily not sleep for three more. He had the paper's second edition folded inside his coat, and he produced it now like a man producing a child. "The affidavit runs in full. My name and all."

"Your name," Mrs. Marchmont repeated. "Pell, you understand what you've done. There will be no going back to them now. Not ever."

"I understand precisely what I've done, ma'am." He smoothed the paper on the table, and Cressida saw that his hands were not entirely steady, and that he did not seem to mind. "For eleven years I filed other men's lies under my own name. It seemed only fair that the reverse should happen once."

Cressida reached past her father and took the first edition. She read it standing up, because sitting felt impossible, because her body kept insisting that this could not be real and required her to be moving, ready, braced for the trick of it. But there was no trick visible anywhere in the dense grey columns. Their journalist—Finch, of Birmingham, who had ridden back to London in Orsen's borrowed hearse with forty pounds of dismantled printing press rattling behind his knees—had done exactly what he had promised. He had printed everything. The threatening letter, transcribed. The forged witness statements, reproduced with their inconsistencies marked in the margins. Pell's affidavit, sworn before a solicitor at seven o'clock yesterday morning. And above all of it, laid out like an anatomy lesson, the watermark: a small crowned fleur-de-lis that appeared in the Regent's Park evidence but could be bought, per the stationer's own ledger, by one client only.

*The one person who can stop it,* Orsen had said, weeks ago, in the dark of the mews, and Cressida had thought then that they meant a judge, a barrister, some great instrument of the law. Instead it had come to this: a newspaper, an MP named Aldous Crane with a grudge against the Home Office and a voice like a church organ, and four hundred thousand breakfast tables doing the jury's work before any courtroom could close its doors.

She read on. Halfway down column four she stopped, and read the paragraph again, and read it aloud without deciding to.

"*It has further emerged that a former employee of the firm, a compositor and forger of considerable skill who cannot at present be named for his own protection, came forward on Tuesday last to give testimony regarding the practices of Halloway & Verrall. This witness—*" her voice caught "*—having narrowly escaped the fire which gutted premises in Clerkenwell on Thursday night, is now said to be in protective custody and prepared to identify documents, signatures, and methods.*"

"He's alive," she said. "Wexford is alive."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Wexford—their vanished witness, the man who had disappeared the night before his deposition, whose disappearance had very nearly cracked the whole network apart with suspicion. Nell, arriving through the service door with her coat still buttoned wrong, froze mid-step.

"Say that again," she demanded.

Cressida read it to her twice. Nell pressed both hands flat against the doorframe as though the house had begun to tilt.

"He fled the fire himself, then," she said. "He wasn't taken. He ran, and he hid, and when the smoke cleared he came forward anyway." She laughed—a short, wet, disbelieving sound. "All week I've been sewing his name into hems like a memorial stitch, and the man was walking about Islington eating pies."

* * *

The day unfolded with the strange, slow-motion quality of weather breaking after a long storm. Cressida went out with Orsen at half nine—not driving, merely walking, because both of them needed to walk—and found London already arguing about them.

They heard it in the queue outside the newsagent on the corner, where a woman in a green coat was reading the front page aloud to anyone who would stand still for it. They heard it in two clerks outside the tobacconist, one saying to the other, *"—no evidence at all, manufactured, the whole thing, and they'd have hanged her mother for it—"*, though of course no one had proposed hanging; the case rested on conspiracy and seditious publication charges, and Cressida corrected this in her head with the pedantic precision that was her inheritance from her father, and then let the correction go, because the woman in the green coat had the essential truth of it and the law's exact shape hardly mattered now.

What mattered was that the machinery had seized. At eleven, Finch sent a boy round with a note: *Crane rose in the House at question time. Home Office Under-Secretary, visibly unprepared, undertook to "review the basis of the proceedings pending inquiry." In Whitehall language that means they are running. Proceedings suspended by end of week if we keep the pressure on. Send more of everything.*

*Send more of everything.* Cressida folded the note and put it in her glove and thought: all those years of letters passing through this household, all those sealed envelopes she had never been allowed to open, and now she was writing them herself, and every one of them was ammunition.

By afternoon they were doing exactly that, in the workroom above the reconstituted press—Nell at the table with her needles and her stacks of copied pages, sewing bundles into the linings of coats bound for sympathetic solicitors; Pell drafting follow-up memoranda in a hand so beautiful it ought to have been illegal; Orsen at the door receiving messengers, sorting them, dispatching answers, running the whole improvised operation with the calm of a stationmaster in fog. Cressida worked beside her mother addressing envelopes, and somewhere between the fortieth and fiftieth she realised she had stopped being afraid.

Not that the danger was over—she was not naive enough, any longer, to think in terms of *over*. Halloway had patrons, and patrons had reach, and men like him did not go quietly into resignation. But the fear had changed its chemistry. For weeks it had been the fear of the trap closing: the raid date, the demolition order, the trial calendar advancing like a regiment. Now it was only ordinary risk, the risk of people fighting in the open. She found she could breathe inside it.

At four o'clock, word came from Finch's office that the witness had given his deposition—not at Scotland Yard, but before a stipendiary magistrate convened privately, with Crane's solicitor present, because nobody trusted any room the Home Office controlled. His statement ran eleven pages. He described the commission: how it had come to the firm in September through an intermediary, how specifications had been dictated verbally, how samples of the Marchmonts' correspondence had been supplied so that hands could be matched, how he had done the work himself—the letterforms, the ink ageing, the seals—until the conscience that had apparently been sleeping for twenty years finally woke up, and he began keeping copies.

"He kept copies," Pell repeated that evening, when the household gathered in the drawing room and Cressida relayed Finch's account. He shook his head slowly. "That's the part I can't get over. Every forger knows the rule—you destroy your practice work or it destroys you. He kept it. Hid it in the roof of the alley shop, which is presumably why our friend with the matches failed so completely. You can't burn what isn't where you think it is." A thin smile. "There's a moral there about invisible work, but I'm too tired to preach it."

"The fire," Mrs. Marchmont said. "Have they established—"

"Established? No. But the insurance man has already been asking why the firm renewed its policy on the premises three days before the blaze, and increased the cover." Pell shrugged. "Halloway's world is starting to eat itself. The patron will cut him loose within the fortnight—that's how it works. One morning he'll arrive at the office and find his name already off the brass plate."

Cressida looked at Orsen, across the room, in the chair nearest the door where they always sat. They had not spoken alone since the news broke; there had been too much to carry. But Orsen met her eyes now, and something passed between them that needed no coding and no cipher: the memory of the mews, the argument, the bitter fracture of Chapter thirteen's making—*you don't get to decide what I'm brave enough for*, Cressida had shouted, and Orsen had answered, low and terrible, *I decide what I can bear to lose*—and the reconciliation afterward that had not been words at all but simply the two of them, working, side by side, refusing to be parted again.

Orsen raised their chin slightly. *Later*, the gesture said. *We'll talk later.* And Cressida nodded, patient at last, which was perhaps the greatest change the whole affair had worked in her.

* * *

Later turned out to be nearly midnight, when the house had gone quiet except for the tick of the hall clock and the distant clatter of the night presses three streets away—*their* presses now, or nearly; the deed was being drawn. Cressida found Orsen in the kitchen, as she had known she would, sitting where the range still gave warmth, with a cup of tea untouched beside them and the day's final edition open but unread.

"You haven't celebrated once," Cressida said, sitting down opposite. "All day. Not even Nell could make you laugh, and she was on magnificent form."

Orsen considered the question honestly, the way they considered most things—as something owed full attention. "I keep thinking about the moment it almost went the other way," they said at last. "The raid. Inspector Dane in the workshop with his tea going cold, looking at me, knowing—" They stopped. "We won by hours, Cressida. Hours. If Wexford hadn't run from the fire. If Pell's affidavit had been in the shop when it burned. If Dane had been one day less tired, or one degree more ambitious." They turned the teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "Vindication should feel like a wall built between us and them. It feels like a rope bridge."

"Then we'll keep building the wall," Cressida said. "That's what the press is for. That's what all of this is for." She reached across the table and, deliberately, turned the newspaper toward Orsen so the headline faced them. "Look at it properly. You've spent weeks reading things in secret that were meant to destroy us. Read this one out loud. It was meant to save us, and it did."

Something moved in Orsen's face—something old and guarded beginning, very slowly, to stand down. They looked at the page. Then, quietly, in the voice they used for reading letters by lamplight, they read: "*Home Office suspends proceedings.*"

"And again."

"*—forgeries laid bare.*"

"And the rest."

"*—MP demands full inquiry.*" Orsen set the paper down. Their eyes were bright and wet and they did not hide it, which was its own kind of milestone. "You know what I keep seeing? Not the headline. The delivery route. Finch in the passenger seat of the hearse with the affidavit inside his coat, sewn in by Nell, and me driving through the fog with the tail two cars back, thinking: this either works or everyone I love goes to prison. And now it's printed in four hundred thousand copies and none of them know the half of it."

"They don't need to know the half of it," Cressida said. "They need the truth, and they got it. Delivered by hand." She smiled. "You've always been the one who carries things, Orsen. Well—it arrived. Everything you carried. It arrived."

For a long moment Orsen said nothing. Then they picked up the cold tea and drank it anyway, which was, Cressida had learned, what Orsen did instead of crying.

* * *

The end of the week brought the formal confirmation, delivered with bureaucratic flatness into the very house that had been its target: notice that proceedings against Mrs. Eleanor Marchmont and Mr. Charles Marchmont were discontinued sine die, pending the public inquiry announced for the spring. Her father read it at the breakfast table and then read it again and then, with great dignity, folded it into quarters and put it in his waistcoat pocket, where Cressida suspected it would remain for the rest of his life.

The same post brought two other things. The first was a note from Finch: *Wexford deposed for six hours today. Names, dates, specimens. Halloway's solicitors withdrew their appearance this morning. Draw your own conclusions.* The second was a copy of the evening edition, marked in Pell's careful hand, with a single paragraph ringed at the bottom of the inside page:

*Mr. Edmund Halloway, it is understood, has tendered his resignation as principal of the firm which bears his name, ahead of the announced inquiry. Questions are now being asked in Whitehall as to who commissioned the work of Halloway & Verrall, and on whose authority the resulting material entered official channels.*

"On whose authority," Mrs. Marchmont murmured, reading over Cressida's shoulder. "Which means the trail doesn't stop at Halloway. Someone inside—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

Cressida folded the paper. Outside, the streetlamps were coming on along the square, and somewhere below stairs she could hear the familiar sounds of the household in motion—Nell's laugh, the clink of the press crates, Orsen's measured footsteps checking a door. The enemy had lost its face, but not yet its hand; somewhere behind Halloway, someone with a title and a department still believed the Marchmonts were safer destroyed than heard. The inquiry in the spring would find them, Pell said. Inquiry witnesses had a way of finding things out.

But that was spring. Tonight, the presses were running, the family was whole, and the truth had gone out into the world in ink that would not wash off.

Cressida took her coat from the hook and went downstairs to help load the evening's parcels, and found Orsen waiting by the car with the engine already warm.
