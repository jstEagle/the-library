## 20. Halloway's Quiet End

Ambrose Pell had spent eleven years reading other men's endings. In the records office at Scotland Yard, before they took his badge and asked him to leave by the service stair, he had filed the resignations of men brought low—superintendents caught with their hands in the till, inspectors whose informants turned out to be figments sustained on expense accounts, one commissioner's brother-in-law who had sold protection to the West End gambling houses with a frankness that bordered on the artisanal. Pell had catalogued their departures with the neutral hand of a man who believed, then, that institutions caught their own. That belief was the first thing the Yard had taken from him. It had not been the last.

So when the notice came through the network on the third Tuesday of October—a single sheet, printed in the *Clarion*'s small type and carried to the workshop by a boy who did not know what he carried—Pell read it standing at Nell's cutting table with his coat still damp from the alley fog, and he felt none of the triumph he had imagined feeling for months.

HOME OFFICE CLERK RESIGNS PENDING INQUIRY, the headline said, and beneath it, in a paragraph no longer than a man's thumb: *Mr. Julian Halloway, formerly of the Home Office registry and more recently principal of Halloway & Sons, documentary agents, has tendered his resignation from all public appointments. A spokesman declined to comment on reports of an internal inquiry into contracting irregularities.*

*Documentary agents.* Pell almost admired it. Somewhere in the last fortnight, someone had taught Halloway's trade its polite name.

"You've read it four times," Nell said from the doorway. She had pins in her mouth and a half-finished coat over her arm—the grey one, the one with the lining that could hold twenty pages of affidavit and still press flat under a tailor's iron. "It doesn't change however many times you read it."

"I'm checking for the trick," Pell said. "There's always a trick."

She took the pins from her mouth one at a time and set them in the saucer she kept for the purpose, a blue willow-pattern thing chipped at the rim that had survived, as far as Pell could tell, three moves, one fire, and any number of catastrophes that never quite reached her hands. "And is there?"

"Not yet." He set the clipping down and smoothed it flat against the table, as though it might rearrange itself if he looked away. "That's what troubles me. Men like Halloway don't resign. They're resigned. There's a hand on the collar we haven't seen. Someone above him decided he'd become expensive, and I'd very much like to know who's holding the collar."

Nell considered this while she draped the grey coat over her stand. "Does it matter? He's finished either way."

"It matters," Pell said, "because a man who is pushed knows things about the hand that pushed him. And a man who jumps—" he tapped the clipping— "takes what he knows down into the dark with him. Read the paragraph again. *Declined to comment on reports of an internal inquiry.* Internal. Not parliamentary, not police. They mean to bury him in-house, Nell. Quietly. The way you'd put down a horse with a broken leg behind the stable so the neighbours don't hear the shot."

The workshop was quiet around them. Since the fire in the printer's alley, since the raid that hadn't been, since Dane's warning delivered over an untouched cup of tea, the place had felt hollowed—not empty, because the presses were back, smaller ones, and the type trays were full again, and Nell's table held its scatter of chalk and shears—but hollowed the way a recovered lung is hollowed, tender with use. Orsen was out on a run. Mrs. Marchmont had gone north for two days of meetings that were certainly meetings and would be described, if anyone asked, as a visit to her sister. The household had learned to speak in these substitutions, and Pell, who had once drafted reports in a language designed to conceal everything including its own purpose, had come to find the fluency beautiful.

"He'll get a pension," Nell said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He'll get a pension and a house somewhere respectable and a silence bought at a price neither of us will ever see itemised." Pell straightened the clipping again. "The Marchmonts walk free. The charges are withdrawn. The witness came out of hiding with a solicitor paid by someone who wanted him talking to the right people. On paper, we've won."

"But?"

Pell looked at her. Eleven years of reading endings, and here was a woman who could hear the unsaid clause in a sentence the way she heard a dropped stitch across a room. "But Halloway never sat in a dock," he said. "No jury heard the watermark explained. No barrister stood up and said *this evidence was manufactured, gentlemen, forged in a private office on paper sold to exactly one buyer*. The truth came out in a newspaper, which is better than nothing, but newspapers have the memory of mayflies. In six months, *Halloway & Sons* will be some other name doing the same work for some other office, and everyone involved will agree it was always thus."

Nell threaded a needle without looking at it, a quick economical motion Pell had watched a hundred times and never tired of studying. "You sound like a man who wanted a trial."

"I wanted," Pell said carefully, "a record."

* * *

The summons came the next morning, which was fast even by the standards of men who wanted things buried quickly.

It arrived at the workshop not by post but by hand—a card, cream stock, good weight, the kind of card that expected to be answered within the day. *Mr. Ambrose Pell. The favour of your company is requested by Sir Edmund Vane, GCB, at his chambers, King's Bench Walk, at four o'clock.* Nothing more. No reason given. A card like that was less an invitation than a demonstration: *we know where you are, we know what you are calling yourself these days, and we can reach you whenever it suits us.*

Pell showed it to Orsen first. They read it the way they read everything, twice, the second time slower, and then set it face-down on the table between them.

"Vane," Orsen said. "He's the patron. The one whose name kept coming up in the expense ledgers—the subscriptions, the charitable donations that routed through Halloway's firm like water through a culvert."

"The same. Under-secretary until the reshuffle, now comfortably on the back benches with a knighthood and a finger in every committee that touches Home Office contracts." Pell had built the file himself, over weeks, out of fragments: a subscription to a hunting club, a donation to a regimental fund, a payment marked only as *consultancy — historical research*, which was the sort of euphemism that made forgery look like archaeology. "He was Halloway's cover. Halloway was his instrument. And now the instrument has snapped in his hand, and he wants to know how sharp the pieces are."

Orsen's face did the thing it always did when they were thinking—went very still, very watchful, the eyes settling on middle distance like a driver judging a road ahead in failing light. "Don't go."

"If I don't go, he comes to us. And I'd rather meet him on ground with more than one door."

"That's not a no."

"No," Pell admitted. "It isn't."

Orsen was quiet a moment longer. Then: "Wear the brown suit. The one Nell lined. If he offers you anything—and he will offer you something—I want whatever he gives you to travel home sewn where he can't imagine looking." A pause, and something moved behind the stillness, wry and warm at once. "We've learned not to trust envelopes in this family."

*Family.* The word landed in Pell's chest with a weight he hadn't expected. He had been expelled from an institution that had been, in its cold way, a family too—uniform instead of blood, ranks instead of siblings—and he had spent two years telling himself he needed nothing of the kind. He nodded, and went to find Nell and the brown suit.

* * *

King's Bench Walk at four o'clock in late October was all bronze light and falling leaves, the Temple gardens going gold past the windows, the quiet inside the chambers thick enough to muffle footsteps. Pell was shown upstairs by a clerk who did not announce him and did not offer a chair until Sir Edmund Vane, standing at the window with his back turned, lifted one finger.

Vane was not what Pell had expected, though Pell could not have said what he had expected—somebody louder, somebody coarser. This man was silver-haired and precise, dressed in the kind of understatement that cost more than display, and when he turned, his face had the terrible mildness of a person who had never in his life needed to raise his voice to be obeyed.

"Mr. Pell." He pronounced the name correctly, which meant he had made enquiries. "Thank you for coming. Sit down. May I offer you anything? No? Sensible." He remained standing himself, hands loosely clasped, a man who preferred to keep his exits unencumbered. "I'll be brief, because I know your time is valuable and mine certainly is. You were a clerk at the Yard for eleven years. Dismissed—let us say dismissed—for refusing to amend a report regarding certain licensing irregularities in the docks. Your superiors considered you obstinate. I have read the file. I consider you something rather different."

"Then you've read further than my superiors did," Pell said.

Something flickered at the corner of Vane's mouth—not quite a smile; an acknowledgement that a point had been scored and would be permitted to stand. "Quite. Mr. Pell, this country runs on paperwork. It always has. Wars are declared in triplicate, famines are denied in ledger columns, and men are ruined or rescued entirely by what is written about them and by whom. The public imagines justice happens in courtrooms. Those of us who actually administer the state know it happens in offices like yours and mine, in the space between what occurred and what is recorded. Julian Halloway understood this. Perhaps he understood it too well, or pursued it too far beyond the boundaries of taste. Either way, the situation has been resolved."

"Resolved," Pell repeated. "That's a word for it."

"It is the correct word." Vane moved to the desk at last and rested two fingertips on a leather folder that lay there, squared to the desk's edge with a precision Pell found more menacing than any gesture of anger could have been. "Here is what I imagine you want to hear. The proceedings against the Marchmont family are withdrawn. The material evidence has been discredited. The informant who fabricated portions of it has recanted and left the country. Mr. Halloway has retired from public life with his health, such as it is, and will trouble no one. Justice, Mr. Pell, has been served—quietly, efficiently, and without a circus. I should have thought a man of your temperament would appreciate exactly that."

"A man of my temperament," Pell said, "spent eleven years watching quiet efficiency used as a shovel. With respect, sir, what happened to the Marchmonts wasn't resolved. It was abandoned. There's a difference, and the difference is that nobody was ever accountable. Halloway resigns. His firm dissolves and re-forms under another name. And the next time someone needs a bombing invented and pinned to inconvenient people—suffragists, strikers, Irishmen, whoever the season's inconvenience happens to be—there'll be a new Halloway, and the paper stock will come from a different mill, and there'll be no clerk with a grudge and a good eye for watermarks to catch it."

The mildness did not change, but something behind it did—a recalculation, Pell thought, the way a chess player revises an assessment of an opponent assumed to be a novice.

"You want a trial," Vane said. "A public reckoning. Very American of you, if you'll forgive the observation. Tell me, Mr. Pell—what would it accomplish? A scandal. Headlines for a fortnight. Questions in the House, answered with the customary skill. And at the end of it, the machinery of government continues precisely as before, except that every office in Whitehall becomes slightly more careful about whom it employs to handle its awkward paperwork, and slightly more vigilant about clerks with long memories." He paused. "Which brings me, delicately, to the reason I asked you here."

"There it is," Pell said quietly.

"I'm told the new Commissioner holds a favourable view of men who refuse to falsify reports, once the political weather has changed. Reinstatement is available. Full restoration of rank and pension arrears—two years' salary, Mr. Pell, which I calculate is not a sum you have earned honestly since your departure. In exchange, I would ask only one thing, and I ask it as a kindness: that you stop digging. The inquiry into Halloway's affairs is concluded. The documents you have collected—affidavits, watermarked pages, correspondence—are relics of a closed matter. Retaining them serves no purpose and exposes you to a charge of theft of official papers, which even your friends in the printing trade could not embroider into heroism." Vane lifted his hand from the folder and stepped back, and the gesture somehow made the room colder. "Take the badge, Mr. Pell. Take the pension. Take the respectable ending. It is being offered to you by someone who does not usually offer anything twice."

Pell sat very still. He was aware of his own heartbeat, slow and hard, and of the brown suit's lining lying flat and secret against his chest, and of the extraordinary fact that this man—this silver, precise, terrible man—believed the offer was generous. That was the part Pell knew he would remember longest. Not the threat. The sincerity.

"May I tell you what I saw in the files, sir?" Pell said. "Not the Halloway files. Mine. The day they dismissed me, the report of my dismissal was drafted before the hearing was finished. I know because I recognised the typing—it came off the registry machine, and I'd filed enough of that machine's work to know its habits, the way the lowercase *e* rides high. My ending was written before my trial. That's what quiet efficiency means, sir. It means the outcome precedes the process. So when you tell me justice has been served quietly, I hear you saying the paperwork has been tidied, and I decline, respectfully, to mistake tidiness for justice."

He stood. His legs held, which surprised him pleasantly.

"The answer to the Commissioner's generosity is no. I have employment. It pays badly and the hours are worse and last week I spent six of them hauling scorched type out of a burned alley with my bare hands. But every name in our ledgers is a living person who chose to be there, and every document we move is moved by consent, and no one's ending gets written before their trial." He took his hat from the chair. "Tell Mr. Halloway, if you ever speak to him—which I suspect you won't—that the coldest thing I ever learned in the Yard is that a man can vanish completely without dying, provided enough important people agree he was never really there. You've done that to him today. I hope you enjoy the view from behind the collar."

At the door, Vane spoke once more, and the mildness had gone all the way through, leaving something Pell was glad not to see fully.

"You understand," Vane said, "that declining makes you a permanent inconvenience."

"Yes, sir," Pell said. "I've been one before. It's honest work."

* * *

He walked back across the river in the dusk, over Westminster Bridge, the lamplights coming on along the Embankment one by one like a slow vote being counted. The brown suit's lining lay flat against him. Vane had offered nothing to carry—only the offer itself—and Pell found he was sorry; he had rather wanted the man's terms to exist in writing, sewn where the sun didn't reach, so that someday, when the season turned and inconvenient clerks became convenient witnesses, there would be a thread to pull.

But perhaps it was cleaner this way. Perhaps some endings deserved no record at all—only the fact of themselves, unwitnessed, unwritten, a resignation printed in a paragraph the length of a man's thumb, and then the long administrative dark.

Halloway's end, when Pell imagined it—and he imagined it often, in the years after, though he rarely said so—was a study in coldness. Not disgrace; disgrace required an audience, and Vane had starved him of one. Just a house in some respectable suburb, a pension paid by a firm that no longer existed, letters written to old colleagues and returned unopened, and everywhere the soft, total silence of men who had agreed he was never really there. Pell had wanted a dock, a jury, a public accounting with the watermark displayed under glass. What he had helped build instead was stranger and crueler: Halloway erased not by law but by the same machinery he had served, turned at last upon its servant. The forger forged out of the record. There was a symmetry to it that no courtroom would have managed, and no mercy either.

The lamps of the bridge gave way to the narrower lights of Southwark. He smelled coal smoke and river mud and, faintly, ink—the particular smell of the workshop now, worked into his coats, his hair, his hands, so that Nell teased him that he no longer needed a press to make an impression. Two years ago he had walked out of the Yard carrying a cardboard box of personal effects and the certainty that he would never again belong anywhere with a door he could lock behind him.

He let himself into the workshop at half past six. Warmth, lamplight, the tick of the small press running—Orsen at it, sleeves rolled, ink to the wrists, and Cressida Marchmont cross-legged on the floor collating sheets with the fierce concentration she brought to everything. Nell looked up from the grey coat, needle poised.

"Well?" Orsen asked. They didn't stop the press. They simply asked, the way family asks, already braced for either answer.

"They offered me my badge back," Pell said. "Full arrears. All I had to do was stop."

Cressida's head came up. Nell's needle stayed exactly where it was. And Pell hung his hat on the peg that had, without anyone ever proposing it, become his peg, and rolled his sleeves, and took his place at the folding table.

"And?" Orsen said.

"I told him I already had employment." Pell picked up the first stack of fresh-printed sheets—the new press's second edition, warm from the rollers—and read the top line by lamplight, slowly, the way he had once read other men's endings. This one began differently. This one began: *Our first duty is to the record.*

Orsen smiled, and the press ran on.
