## 21. A Name of One's Own

The document arrived the way documents always had—by hand, sealed, carried into the house on a Tuesday when rain came down in grey ropes and the gutters of Woburn Square ran like small rivers. Orsen took it from the postman's wet glove, felt its weight, and knew before turning it over that it was meant for them. There was no name written on the front. There was no name needed.

They had read other people's letters for six years. They had stood in hallways with envelopes warmed against their palms, holding them to lamplight until the wax gave up its secrets, and they had done it not for gain but for guardianship—because a household that printed sedition below stairs needed a driver who could see trouble coming folded inside a bill of lading. Every secret Orsen Winterbourne had ever carried had been taken, not given. Stolen kindly, perhaps, but stolen all the same.

This envelope was addressed to *Orsen Winterbourne* in Mrs. Marchmont's upright hand, and the seal was unbroken, and it was theirs.

They stood in the service corridor a long moment, rain drumming at the area steps, and did not open it. Not because they feared what was inside. Because opening it felt like a threshold, and thresholds deserved ceremony, and Orsen had never in their twenty-nine years been permitted ceremony of any kind.

*Later,* they told themselves, tucking it inside their coat—the left breast, over the heart, where important things went—and went on to see to the motorcar as though nothing in the world had changed.

But everything had changed, and they both knew it, and the car's crank fought them twice before it caught.

* * *

The reconstituted press lived now in a disused chapel schoolroom off Camden Road, a long room with tall windows and a floor that sloped gently toward the altar-end where the big press stood. It was not the old workshop. The old workshop was ash and blackened joists, a smell of char that lingered in Nell's hair for weeks afterward, a scorched page with Halloway's watermark pressed flat between two boards like a butterfly pinned for study. That place had sheltered them and then tried, in the end, to burn them out. This place asked less of memory. Its walls were whitewashed. Its windows let in honest light. Children's chalk ghosts still haunted the wainscoting—letters, sums, a crooked drawing of a dog—and Orsen found this fitting. A room built for teaching people to read their own names seemed the right home for what they were becoming.

Nell had claimed the corner near the stove, where her worktable held needles, thimbles, and an innocent-looking stack of finished garments whose linings could carry more truth than a mailbag. Pell worked at a desk he'd rescued from a skip, his police-trained hand producing copy so clean it looked typeset, his badgeless life worn now like a coat that finally fit. Cressida came and went with galley proofs and arguments, mostly arguments, delivered with the radiant confidence of a young woman who had nearly been arrested twice and considered it education.

And Mrs. Marchmont came on Thursdays, because Thursday was the day the type was distributed and the week's plan laid, and she came dressed plainly and sat on a plain chair and was, in that room, not Lady-Who-Lunched but simply Beatrice Marchmont, chairman of nothing, employer of no one, partner among equals—which was, Orsen had learned, precisely how she preferred it.

On this particular Thursday the rain followed Orsen even to Camden Road, hammering the schoolroom windows while the press clacked its patient rhythm and the smell of ink and oilcloth made a warmth the stove only began. Orsen had carried the sealed letter all day. It rode against their chest now like a second heartbeat. They half expected Mrs. Marchmont to pretend it didn't exist—to let the thing arrive through ordinary channels, unwitnessed, unremarked. That was the English way. That was the way of households like hers, where the truest transactions happened in the pauses.

Instead she called them across the room by name.

"Orsen. Would you sit down, please?"

Sit down. In six years, Orsen could count on one hand the times anyone in the Marchmont employ had asked them to sit: once after the Birmingham run, when fog had swallowed the road and they'd come back white-faced and shaking; once after the raid, when Inspector Dane's tea had gone cold in Orsen's hands and the last crate had cleared the coal chute by four minutes. Both times it had been an offering of rest. This was something else. Mrs. Marchmont stood beside the altar-end of the press, and on the flat of the stone where once a Bible had lain there lay a folder, and beside the folder, deliberately, a second chair.

Not her chair. A second chair, set facing hers.

Orsen crossed the room and sat, and the chair received their weight without complaint, and somehow that small sturdiness undid them more than anything else that day.

"You've read it," Mrs. Marchmont said. It wasn't a question. "I assume you've read it. You've read everything I've sent or received since 1907."

The room did not go quiet—presses never do—but Orsen felt the sound of it recede, as if the machine itself were leaning in.

"Yes, ma'am," they said. "Since the spring of '07. The Corbett correspondence first. There was a name in the third letter that would have put two organizers in prison if it had gone where it was addressed." They stopped. Six years of confession compressed into silence, waiting to see if honesty was about to cost them everything.

Mrs. Marchmont only nodded, slow, unsurprised. "I know. I wrote the third letter. I put the name in to see whether it would travel or whether someone would stop it." She folded her hands. "It never arrived. And I have known ever since exactly who my postman really was."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way it does when a wall you've leaned on all your life turns out to have been leaning back.

"You—" Orsen began, and found their voice had gone somewhere. They started again. "You used me as a test."

"I used you as a safeguard," she said, "and then, when you proved to be one, I trusted you with everything else and let you believe you were invisible. That was wrong of me. It was convenient, and it was wrong. Invisible people are not protected by their invisibility, Orsen. They are merely easier to lose." Her gaze went, briefly, to the scorched ledger page framed now behind glass on the chapel wall—a relic Nell had insisted upon, proof that watermarks could hang men. "We lost enough this year. I am done pretending the people I depend upon are furniture."

She slid the folder across the stone.

"Read it aloud," she said. "That is the whole point of it. Read it where everyone can hear."

* * *

The document was three pages, drafted in Pell's impeccable clerk's hand, and it was, of all things, simple. *The Camden Road Press, constituted jointly.* The parties named were Beatrice Mary Marchmont; Cressida Anne Marchmont; Ambrose Pell; Eleanor Sallow—for Nell, it turned out, had a surname, which she had guarded longer than Orsen had guarded anything; and Orsen Winterbourne, driver, courier, reader of letters, listed fifth and equal, with a share expressed not in pounds but in words: *in labour, in judgment, and in profit alike.*

There was a line for signature beneath each name. Four of the five lines were already filled.

"We waited," Cressida said from behind them—Orsen hadn't heard her come in, dripping, her collar dark with rain. "Pell signed Monday. Nell signed yesterday and pretended she hadn't cried, which fooled nobody." She came to stand at Orsen's shoulder. "Yours was the only one that mattered, so we saved it. Mama says you should do it properly, with witnesses, and Mama is usually right, and it's unbearable."

"Cressida," her mother said mildly.

"It is unbearable," Cressida repeated, unrepentant, and Orsen heard in her voice the girl who had stood in a doorway at midnight asking why they weren't sleeping, the girl who had been followed down Portland Road and never said so until she couldn't bear it, the girl who had broken every rule her mother kept and forged, out of the wreckage, this strange alliance. Orsen had nearly told her everything that night. Now there was nothing left to tell. Everything hidden had surfaced or burned or been sewn into a coat lining and delivered.

Nell appeared at the edge of Orsen's vision, wiping her hands on her apron, and Pell rose from his desk and came too, and for a moment the five of them stood around the press like people around a hearth, which was, Orsen supposed, exactly what they were.

"The ink's here somewhere," Nell said, patting pockets. "Somebody moved my ink."

"Somebody," Pell said, "is holding it," and produced the little pot from beside his blotter with the dry satisfaction of a man who still, after everything, enjoyed being right about small things.

Orsen looked at the page again. Their own name, printed in Pell's careful capitals, looked back—*ORSEN WINTERBOURNE*—and Orsen thought about names. They had been christened Orsen by a mother barely able to afford the registrar's fee, and surnameless in practice for most of their childhood, *the boy* and *the girl* and *that one*, depending on the household and the hour, until Winterbourne had been chosen off a gravestone in a country churchyard at sixteen because a person needed something to write on a reference letter. A borrowed name. A dead man's name. It had served. But it had never quite belonged, the way the envelope in their coat had not belonged until today—not because it was false, but because no one had ever offered them the dignity of addressing it directly.

"Dip it properly," Nell instructed. "Not a skim. A proper dip. You're signing your life, not a laundry list."

"It's not my life," Orsen said quietly, dipping the pen. "My life was the part before. This is the part after."

"That's exactly what I mean," said Nell.

The pen touched paper.

Orsen had signed things before—ledgers, delivery books, a statement for Pell's affidavit that had helped end Halloway's career without ever bearing Orsen's true role in gathering it. Every signature until now had been an act of concealment or compliance. This one was neither. The nib caught briefly on the fibre, then flowed, and Orsen wrote their name slowly, feeling each letter as a small decision, and when it was done they understood that they had never once in their life read a document and then signed it in the same hour, openly, among witnesses, as a person with standing to refuse.

Four signatures and a fifth. The press clacked on. Rain silvered the tall windows. Somewhere outside a cart went by, wheels hissing through water.

"Well," said Mrs. Marchmont, and her voice, for just a moment, was not entirely steady. "That's a company, then. Illegally opinionated, frequently seditious, and legally unimpeachable—thank you, Mr. Pell, for seeing to that last. I have spent thirty years being spoken about in newspapers. I find I should like, at last, to own one."

"One-eighth of one," said Cressida.

"One-eighth of one," her mother agreed, "which is five-eighths more than Lord Northcliffe has ever given me credit for."

There was laughter then, brief and bright, the kind that comes after long fear—the sound the group made now sometimes, tentatively, like people learning a language. Pell shook Orsen's hand with a grip that managed to convey both congratulations and a full risk assessment. Nell hugged them hard enough to creak ribs and whispered, "Told you the coat would fit eventually," which made no sense and complete sense at once.

And Mrs. Marchmont, last, took Orsen's hand in both of hers and held it, and said, not formally at all, "Thank you for reading my letters. All these years. Thank you for keeping us."

Orsen, who had rehearsed a dozen dignified replies on the drive over, found they could only say, "It was the best work I ever did," and discovered it was true, and discovered also that saying it aloud cost nothing—that secrets, once surrendered willingly, weigh nothing at all.

* * *

They walked home late, Orsen and Cressida, because the motorcar was in for brake adjustment and because Cressida declared she wanted to walk and because Orsen, who had spent years alone in the dark between houses, no longer minded admitting they wanted company on the wet streets. The rain had eased to a fine drifting mist. Gas lamps doubled themselves in the shining pavement. Camden Road smelled of coal smoke and chestnut carts and the river far off, the ordinary perfume of a city that had tried to destroy them and failed.

Cressida talked the whole way—about the first edition, set for the following week; about the masthead, argued over for days (*The Regiment* had won, over Orsen's quiet preference for *The Clock*, on the grounds that a newspaper ought to march rather than merely tick); about whether the journalist Aldous Renwick would keep his promise to hold the Halloway exposé for their second issue so it would be *their* scoop and not a gift. She talked, Orsen realized, the way people talk when they are happy and trust the silence not to swallow them.

At the square she stopped under the plane tree, mist beading in her dark hair, and looked at Orsen with sudden seriousness.

"You'll be here," she said. "Tomorrow. And the day after. It's not—it isn't a position anymore. You understand that. You're not staff."

"I understand," Orsen said.

"Because Mama will still call you 'our driver' at dinner parties, purely to horrify people, and you mustn't take it seriously—"

"Cressida."

"—and Pell will try to make you keep minutes, and Nell will try to make you eat breakfast, and none of it is optional, that's what belonging is, apparently, it's people imposing porridge on you—"

"*Cressida.*"

Her mouth closed. Her eyes were bright. Orsen understood that she had needed to say all of it badly because she could not yet say the true part well, and so Orsen said it for her.

"I'll be here," they said. "I live here now. That's rather the point of the whole document."

Cressida laughed, dashed a hand across her eyes, blamed the mist, and went up the steps two at a time, calling back something about porridge that Orsen chose not to hear.

Alone on the pavement, Orsen took out the morning's envelope—still unopened, still theirs, carried all day like a talisman—and broke the seal at last under the plane tree, by gaslight, in public, hiding from no one. Inside was a single card in the same upright hand:

*The first edition goes to press Tuesday at six o'clock. I cannot imagine the room without you in it. —B.M.*

Below it, in a different, loopier script—Cressida's—someone had added: *Bring the good pen. Mama will deny needing it. She will need it.*

Orsen stood a moment longer, letting the mist gather on their shoulders, watching the lit windows of the Marchmont house—the house that had never legally been theirs and was now, in every way that mattered, home. Six years of measured parcels and memorized routes and letters read by lamplight with the shade drawn. Six years of being a shadow with excellent reflexes.

Not anymore. On Tuesday the press would run, and their name would be on the imprint, in type, public, permanent, legible to anyone who cared to look—the most visible thing Orsen Winterbourne had ever done, and the least frightening.

They folded the card into their breast pocket, over the heart, and went inside.
