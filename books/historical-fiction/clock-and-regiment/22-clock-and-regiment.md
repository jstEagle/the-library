## 22. Clock and Regiment

The press had a voice Orsen had never heard before.

The old workshop's machine—God rest its rusted heart—had spoken in a kind of asthmatic clatter, half complaint, half cough, the sound of a thing doing its work against its own better judgment. This new one, bought secondhand from a defunct newspaper in Croydon and rebuilt bolt by bolt under Ambrose Pell's exacting eye, spoke differently. It spoke in rhythm. In certainty. *Ka-chunk, ka-chunk, ka-chunk*—the sound of a clock that intended to keep time until the end of the world.

Orsen stood in the doorway of the converted coach-house on Fetter's Lane and listened to it run, the first edition sliding out beneath its rollers like bread from an oven: *The Open Hand*, Vol. I, No. 1. Four pages. Eight hundred copies. The lead article was Mrs. Marchmont's, on the franchise; the second was Pell's, a dry, devastating accounting of how evidence was manufactured in the back rooms of private firms and sold to men in public office. No names had needed inventing. Halloway's had been given freely, by history, in print.

Orsen picked up a copy while the ink was still faintly warm and read the masthead for perhaps the hundredth time since the type had been set:

*Printed and published by the Winterbourne & Marchmont Press.*

*Winterbourne.* Their own name. Set in type. Not hidden in a courier's ledger or buried under a false initial, but standing at the front of the line where anyone might see it, where everyone was meant to.

They had signed their name eleven months ago on a partnership document, the first document they had ever read openly, by lamplight, with witnesses—and the strangeness of it still hadn't worn off. For twenty-six years Orsen Winterbourne had been a name used only on railway manifests and rent receipts, a name written by other people about them. Now it was set in twelve-point Caslon, and eight hundred people who would never meet them would know it by breakfast.

"Stop fondling the first copy," Nell said, coming through from the composing room with a tray of type-cleaning solvent and the particular walk of a woman whose hands knew forty ways to hide things and had finally, blessedly, run out of reasons to use most of them. "You'll smudge the legend."

"I'm checking quality."

"You're preening."

"Both can be true." Orsen set the paper down on the stone bench with the reverence other people reserved for Bibles. "Is the binding thread the new linen?"

"The very same. Left over from—" Nell stopped, and her mouth did something complicated. "From the coat linings. Last of it."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. The coat linings. Eleven months ago that thread had carried a stolen affidavit across London sewn flat against a man's ribs, invisible as breath, and delivered justice into the hands of a journalist named Croft and through him into the chamber of Parliament itself, where it detonated more cleanly than any bomb the Marchmonts had ever been accused of making.

"It holds well," Orsen said at last.

"It holds forever," said Nell. "That's what it's for."

* * *

By four o'clock the coach-house had filled the way it always did now on printing days—not with conspirators, whatever the Home Office might once have imagined, but with family. That was a word Orsen had spent a lifetime using carefully, sparingly, the way a miser spends a coin of doubtful metal. They spent it freely now. It kept coming back to them with interest.

Mrs. Marchmont arrived first, of course; she had a genius for arriving first at everything except her own surprise parties, which was why they no longer attempted surprise parties. She came in still wearing gloves, reviewed the first edition with the eye of a general reviewing troops, pronounced the kerning in the second column "a small scandal," and kissed Orsen on both cheeks anyway.

Mr. Marchmont followed, carrying a hamper that smelled gloriously of his cook's pork pie, and behind him Cressida—Cressida, who had argued her way into writing a column of her own over Mrs. Marchmont's formal objection and everyone else's silent delight. She came in arguing, in fact, mid-sentence, a pencil behind each ear and a proof sheet in her hand.

"—and if the Home Secretary won't answer the question about women's factory inspections, then the question goes in the headline, Mother, and *he* can be the one who looks unreasonable in print—"

"In my day," said Mr. Marchmont mildly, unpacking the hamper, "we settled disagreements at dinner."

"In your day, Father, you didn't have a press."

"We had a cellar full of pamphlets and a driver who read all our post. I'd call that a press with wheels."

Cressida caught Orsen's eye across the room, and the look that passed between them was the whole of chapter thirteen compressed into a glance—the shouting match in the rain outside Croft's office, the week of not speaking, the slow repair, the eventual and slightly astonishing discovery that trust, once broken and reset, sets stronger at the fracture than anywhere else along the bone.

"Your column's good," Orsen told her. "I read the proof this morning."

"You're supposed to say that."

"I'm supposed to *think* it. Saying it is voluntary."

Cressida laughed, and Mrs. Marchmont said something about children and presses, and Pell appeared at Orsen's elbow the way he always did—quietly, at an angle, a man whose instincts for entering rooms unremarked had never quite left him even though he no longer needed them.

Ambrose Pell wore no badge these days because he wore nothing at all of the old life. He dressed like what he was: a clerk turned investigator turned columnist, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and a memory that the Home Office still, on bad days, gave him nightmares about. He had declined reinstatement twice—once formally, once by simply not answering a letter—and when Orsen had asked him why, he'd said, "Because the last institution that employed me taught me exactly one useful thing, which is that institutions keep records. And now I keep records instead. It turns out I prefer being the record."

He looked at the stack of *The Open Hand*, issue one, and his throat moved.

"Well," he said.

"Well," Orsen agreed.

Pell picked up a copy and held it at arm's length, the way he held everything he was afraid of loving. His eyes found the third page, where his article began—a plain, unadorned, merciless recitation of watermarks, invoices, and paper stock ledgers, ending with a single sentence that had already made its way into two rival newspapers by quotation: *A forgery is only ever as honest as the man who profits by it.*

"They'll try to sue," Pell said. "Halloway's people, what's left of them."

"Let them. Discovery works both ways. You taught me that."

"I taught you that by ruining myself."

"You taught me that by *unruining* yourself," Orsen said. "Eventually. With help."

Pell almost smiled. On his face it looked like weather breaking. He set the paper down with exaggerated care, aligning its edge with the stack, and Orsen understood—it was the gesture of a clerk, of a man who had once believed order was the same thing as justice and had learned otherwise at the cost of everything, now building a new order where the two were, at least some of the time, actually acquainted.

"The witness deposes Thursday," Pell said quietly, for Orsen alone. "Granger. The one who vanished before the fire. Protective custody suits him; he's put on weight and taken up whist. He'll testify clean."

"And Halloway?"

"Halloway is in Boulogne, writing letters nobody answers." Pell straightened his cuffs. "His patron has a new protégé and a shorter memory, which is the kindest thing I could say about either. There won't be a trial, Orsen. There'll just be a quiet man in France getting quieter, and a firm dissolved, and a clause in next year's bill about the certification of evidentiary documents that will bore everyone who reads it and protect everyone who doesn't."

"That's not justice," Orsen said. "As it's usually drawn."

"No. It's better. Trials end. Print doesn't." Pell nodded at the stack. "Every issue, we'll run the ledger column. Names, dates, invoices. As long as this press runs, it runs."

* * *

They ate at the long table by the loading doors, because there was nowhere else big enough and because Mrs. Marchmont declared that a press's first edition ought to be inaugurated like a ship—broken bread over, properly christened, none of your champagne nonsense, pork pie and pickled onions and Mrs. Marchmont's smuggled bottle of the good sherry. Mr. Marchmont carved. Nell told the story of the fire for perhaps the dozenth time, and it was no less harrowing for the dozenth telling: smoke rolling down the alley at two in the morning, the heat through the workshop door, and Nell going *in*, because the type was in there, because the scorched ledger page with Halloway's watermark was in there, because some things are worth lungs and eyebrows and a scar along the left forearm she now showed at parties.

"You went in for the *type*," Mr. Marchmont said, for the dozenth time.

"I went in for the truth," said Nell, for the dozenth time, "and the type happened to weigh less."

Cressida proposed a toast to Inspector Dane—who had stood in the empty workshop eleven months ago sipping tea Orsen had poured with hands that did not shake until afterward, surveying bare shelves and swept floors, and who had known, *known*, and let them go anyway with a warning delivered like a benediction: *"I'll read about it in the papers, I expect. Make it good reading."* Dane had retired in the spring. The first copy of *The Open Hand* was already en route to his cottage in Surrey, marked *With respect, and thanks we cannot publish.*

"To absent friends and reformed inspectors," Mrs. Marchmont said, lifting the sherry.

"To the ones who aren't absent anymore," Cressida said, looking around the table.

And then, because someone had to and because it sat in Orsen's chest like a coal asking to be set down, Orsen raised a glass and said: "To the letters."

The table went quiet, the good kind.

To the letters. To every envelope steamed open by lamplight in a servants' hall, every threat memorized, every secret carried so the family above stairs could sleep. To the sealed dispatch with the unfamiliar wax that had started everything—the letter naming a bombing plot that never existed except on paper, in the hands of men who dealt exclusively in paper, and who had never considered that their courier might read. To the threatening letter itself, sewn by Nell's needles into a coat lining and produced, intact and damning, before a journalist and a barrister and a room full of men who had never imagined that women's work could be ammunition.

"To the letters," the table echoed, and drank, and Mr. Marchmont said, "Though I maintain I'd rather my correspondence weren't literature," and Mrs. Marchmont said, "Darling, your correspondence was *always* literature; we merely had it edited," and the laughter went around the table like the sherry.

Orsen laughed too, and underneath the laughter thought: *this*. Not the press, not the vindication, not even the name in the masthead—but this table, these people, the improbable arithmetic by which a household that hired a driver had become a household that contained one.

* * *

Evening came down over Fetter's Lane the way it does in autumn, early and gold-edged, and one by one they left. The Marchmonts first, driven home by Orsen in the new motorcar—which Orsen drove with the same competence and rather more joy than the old horse and trap, though the horses were not forgotten; there was a picture of Bucephalus, retired and fat in a Hertfordshire field, pinned above the composing stone. Then Pell, who shook hands like a man closing a successful transaction, and paused at the door to say, "Thursday's deposition transcript—I'll want it typeset by Saturday," and was gone into the lamplight. Then Nell, who did not shake hands but embraced, briefly and hard, the embrace of a woman who sewed things shut for a living and knew the value of holding them closed.

Which left Cressida, lingering over the last proofs.

"You could lock up," she said. "I could stay."

"You have a column due."

"I have a column *drafted*. Due is a state of mind."

"Cressida."

She sighed, and grinned, and surrendered the way she surrendered everything—loudly and without meaning it. At the door she stopped and looked back, and for a moment Orsen saw her as she had been that first night in the Marchmont house: another guarded person in a house full of guards, recognizing a fellow sentry across the parapet.

"We were good at it," Cressida said. "Weren't we? The whole wretched business. We were *good*."

"We were adequate," said Orsen, "under pressure."

"Don't you dare be modest in your own masthead. Goodnight, Orsen Winterbourne, printer and publisher and terror of the Home Office."

"Goodnight, Cressida Marchmont, columnist and menace to ministers."

The door closed. The press ticked as it cooled, clock-regular. Orsen stood alone in the coach-house among the smell of ink and oil and cold tea, and did not move for a while, and was not lonely.

* * *

Locking up took longer than it used to, because there was more to lock. The coal chute was bricked now, and had been since the month after the raid—Orsen had paid for the bricks personally, and had laid the last one themselves, and had felt no ceremony about it whatsoever, or rather had felt all of it at once and told no one. The windows were shuttered. The type was cabinetted, sorted, every letter in its bed: a whole alphabet of small lead soldiers standing down at last from active service.

Orsen walked the room once, as they did every night. Past the press, ticking toward silence. Past the stone bench where the first edition lay stacked, eight hundred copies of a world slightly amended. Past the wall where three things hung in a row, and had hung there since the day the frames went up:

A photograph of the old workshop in the printer's alley, taken before the fire, everybody squinting into the sun.

A single scorched page under glass, the watermark showing through the burn like a scar showing through skin—*the truth keeps*, Nell had said when she framed it, *even when you burn it. Especially then.*

And a letter. The original. The one with the unfamiliar seal, the one that had named a bombing and a frame and a date, the one Orsen had carried to the edge of the fireplace intending to burn and had turned away from instead, breaking every rule of invisibility they had ever lived by. It hung framed beside the watermark page like a confession and a rebuttal sharing a cell. Visitors asked about it sometimes. Orsen always gave the same answer: *It's there to remind us what paper can do. Both kinds.*

At the door, Orsen took down the key ring from its hook.

It was not a heavy ring. Two keys—the coach-house, the type cabinet—and a third, newer, that opened a drawer in the Marchmont house that had been formally assigned to them last spring in a small ceremony consisting entirely of Mrs. Marchmont saying "This is yours" and meaning approximately seven larger things. Three keys. Once, Orsen had carried the keys to other people's houses, other people's carriages, other people's secrets, and owned nothing but the carrying.

Now the carrying *was* the owning. Every key on the ring opened a door Orsen had a right to stand behind.

Orsen stepped out into the lane, locked the coach-house door, and tested it—once, twice, the old habit, the courier's habit, the habit of a person whose job had been to make sure that what was entrusted arrived and that what mustn't be lost wasn't. The street was quiet. Lamplight lay along the wet cobbles in long gold seams. Somewhere north, a train whistle sounded, bound somewhere with somebody's letters, and Orsen wished it, as always, safe passage.

The clock over the lane struck nine—old, slow, accurate—and Orsen stood and listened to it count, all the way to the end, the way one listens to a regiment passing: not because each beat is different, but because together they are a promise that the hours will go on being kept.

Memory kept. Justice kept. Home kept.

Orsen Winterbourne, driver no longer, reader of letters still, printer and partner and son-of-no-one-and-everyone, turned up their collar, pocketed the keys, and walked home through the lamplit streets of the city they had helped, in a small and stubborn and printed way, to make a little more honest—whistling, for no reason anyone would ever need to know, the sound a press makes when it is running well and means to keep on running:

*Ka-chunk. Ka-chunk. Ka-chunk.*

**THE END**
