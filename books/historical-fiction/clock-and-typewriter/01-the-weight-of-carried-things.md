## 1. The Weight of Carried Things

There were three hundred and eleven steps between the attic dormitory and the counting room of Meridian House, and Odile Whitlock had never once taken them at a run.

Not because she couldn't. Because running drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing her whole life was arranged to avoid. She had learned this early, the way other children learned not to touch hot stoves: the girl who runs gets asked why she runs, and the girl who gets asked questions is a girl who has already begun to disappear from the ledger of useful people.

So she walked. Every morning, down the three hundred and eleven steps — well, two hundred and six steps and one hundred and five stair-treads; she counted both, because counting was free and hers — with her hands folded and her face arranged into that particular expression of pleasant nothingness that the Meridian Trading Company found so reassuring in its indentured staff. It was not quite a smile. Smiles invited conversation. It was more the look of a person who has been reliably informed that nothing interesting will ever happen to her, and who has made her peace with it.

The port city of Greyhaven did fog the way other cities did rain: thoroughly, professionally, as though under contract. On the morning this story properly begins, the fog lay against the windows of Meridian House so thick that the harbor bells sounded like they were being rung underwater, and Odile stood at her station beside the counting desk with her pencil sharpened to a point you could have performed surgery with, and waited for Mr. Calloway to begin.

"Morning, Miss Whitlock."

"Morning, Mr. Calloway."

"Crates came in on the *Peregrine* overnight. Forty-one, by the dockmaster's tally."

"Forty-two," said Odile. "The dockmaster can't count past his own boots."

Mr. Calloway, factor of Meridian House, a man built rather like a teapot and about as easy to read, paused with his cup halfway to his mouth. "Forty-two?"

"There's one listed as damp-damaged on the manifest but there's no damage entry, which means somebody knocked it off a handcart and hoped nobody would notice before the tally was signed." She said all this in exactly the tone she used for reporting weather. "It's behind the pilings at berth nine if he went back for it. If he didn't, it'll still be there tonight, and I'd suggest we send someone before the night men find it."

There was a silence in which Mr. Calloway looked at her the way he occasionally did — a long, considering look, like a man reading the last page of a book first to see whether it's worth starting. Odile endured it by examining her pencil.

"You noticed an unlisted crate," he said, "from the counting room. Without going to the docks."

"The light comes through the east window at quarter past six, sir. You can see berth nine if you stand on the second stair. Someone moved something heavy off the *Peregrine* at half four, because the gulls started up, and gulls only start like that when there's fish or spilled cargo, and the *Peregrine* doesn't carry fish."

"Hm," said Mr. Calloway.

That was all he ever said, usually. *Hm.* But he sent a porter to berth nine, and the porter came back with a crate, and forty-two was written in the day book in Odile's small, even, entirely forgettable hand, and nobody thanked her, which was how she preferred things. Thanks created obligations. Obligations created attention. And attention, in Odile's long experience of being owned by a trading company since the age of nine, was the first symptom of trouble.

* * *

Here is what it means to be indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, in case you should ever wonder, and Odile recommended wondering about it often, because wondering was also free:

You slept in the attic with four other girls, beneath a roof that leaked in precisely one spot, directly onto Marta's bed, which Marta had made peace with by the simple method of moving her bed every season and complaining every night. You rose at half five. You ate porridge that could have been used to patch the harbor wall. You worked from six until six, counting crates, copying manifests, entering weights and duties and fees into ledgers whose contents you were expected to know and whose existence you were expected to forget the moment you left the room. And at the end of seven years — or ten, or twelve, depending on how the company chose to calculate your "education costs," a sum that grew with impressive vigor whenever anyone checked it — you would be released into the world with a reference letter, a set of clothes, and the accumulated wages of nothing whatsoever.

Odile was eleven years into hers. Four years remained, by the company's arithmetic. Fewer, possibly, if the war kept eating the clerks and they needed to stretch the ones they had.

She did not mind it, mostly. That was the trick she had built, the architecture of her survival: she had made herself so good at the small, precise, invisible work that the work itself became a kind of home. Numbers did not lie to her, or if they lied, they lied in patterns, and patterns could be caught. Ledgers were honest in aggregate even when men were dishonest in particular. A crate weighed what it weighed. A debt accrued at the rate it accrued. There was a comfort in that which Odile suspected people with families and houses of their own could not understand — the comfort of a world where, if you paid close enough attention, everything added up.

Her mother would have understood it. Her mother had been a pearl diver, back before the war, back before the coughing sickness, back before Odile was a ward of anybody's anything. Divers lived by counting too — breaths, fathoms, shells, the slow arithmetic of what the sea would give and what it would take. Her mother used to say the sea kept the most meticulous ledger of any creditor alive, and it always collected, and the only wisdom was to know exactly what you owed before it came asking.

Odile thought about her mother most often at the oilcloth bundle in the bottom of her trunk, which she unwrapped once a month, on the first Sunday, when the attic was empty.

The knife was a diver's knife — short, curved, wickedly practical, its wooden handle worn smooth and pale where a woman's grip had polished it over ten thousand dives. There was no ornament on it anywhere. That was what Odile loved about it. Everything else she had from her mother was gone — sold, eaten, lost in the move to the company orphan wing — but a pearl diver's knife survived because it was too plain to be worth stealing and too useful to be thrown away. Her mother's whole philosophy, in an object.

She kept it wrapped in oilcloth against the salt air, and she kept it at all against company rules, which forbade staff to hold weapons, valuables, or "items of unaccounted provenance." Odile had accounted for its provenance very carefully, actually, in a private ledger she kept in her head, which no inspector could ever seize. *One knife. My mother's. Value: incalculable. Debt owed: none.* It balanced perfectly.

* * *

On fog-bound afternoons, when the crates were counted and the ink was drying, Odile permitted herself her one indulgence, which was cataloging.

Not the company's catalogs — hers. The private inventory. The one she maintained against the day, always possible, never scheduled, when orders came and she was transferred, dismissed, sold on to some other concern, or otherwise disposed of like surplus stock. Indentured girls got moved around. It happened. A company's discretion was absolute, and discretion meant that any morning could be her last at Meridian House. So she kept ready. She kept, in the strictest sense, portable.

What could be carried, in the event of orders:

One diver's knife, oilcloth-wrapped, one pound.
Two dresses, one good, one everyday, three pounds.
One pair of boots, resoled twice, two pounds.
One hairbrush, one comb, one tin cup, one candle stub, collectively one pound.
Savings hidden in the false seam of the trunk lining: eleven pounds, six shillings, threepence. (Four pounds.)
And the ledgers — not the paper ones, those belonged to the company, but the ones in her head. The shipping routes. The fee schedules. The names of every ship, every skipper, every clerk who took his cut in rum instead of coin. Thousands of pounds of knowledge, weighing nothing at all.

That last entry was the one she dwelled on. It was, she understood, the only wealth she would ever truly own, and she had assembled it deliberately, entry by entry, year by year, the way a poor woman builds a coat out of rags nobody wants. When the tally-keepers dozed, she memorized. When corrections were burned, she remembered what they'd corrected. The company thought she was quiet because she had nothing to say. In truth she was quiet because she was busy.

She was aware, in a detached way, that this might be considered theft. She had decided long ago that it wasn't. They had bought her hands and hours; the contents of her own head had never appeared on any bill of sale, and she'd checked. She'd checked every document relating to herself that the company held, during an afternoon four years ago when the filing room was empty and Mrs. Brand had a toothache. Her indenture papers. Her mother's death certificate, misfiled under W for *Whitlock, deceased, cause: pulmonary*. And a slim folder labeled *Staff Assessment* that contained, among other observations, a single line in Calloway's handwriting that she had carried with her ever since, the way other people carry a stone in the shoe:

*W. notices everything. Reports almost nothing. Ideal.*

Ideal. She still didn't know whether it had been a compliment or an invoice.

* * *

That evening, the fog thickened until even the house seemed to squint. Supper was barley and onions and a long argument between Marta and the cook about the leaking roof, conducted at volumes suitable for reaching each other across a harbor. Odile ate steadily, listened to the wind worry at the shutters, and reviewed the day's entries in her head the way other people said prayers.

It was on the way back upstairs — step two hundred and ninety-one, the loose one, third from the landing — that she heard the knock.

Not at the front door. Front-door knocks rang through the hall and brought the porter. This was at the service door, down the scullery stairs, soft and doubled, the knock of someone who did not want to be answered by anyone important. And underneath the sound of it, thin and furious and utterly impossible, coming through the fog from the direction of the sea-steps:

A cry. Small. New. The kind of sound that had exactly one source in the world.

Odile stood on the stairs with her candle, and eleven years of practiced invisibility told her, clearly and kindly, to keep walking. Attic. Door. Bed. Morning. Counting room. Nothing interesting ever happens to girls who keep walking.

The knock came again. Then the cry again, closer now, muffled through oak and fog — and angry, the way only something brand new can be angry, furious at the whole cold arrangement of the world.

Odile looked down at her own feet on the stairs, and thought, absurdly, of her catalog. Of the neat list of what could be carried. Two dresses, one knife, eleven pounds six and threepence, and everything she knew.

There was no column in the ledger for what came next. Nobody had taught her an entry for it. But her feet had already turned, and she was going down — down toward the service door, down against every rule she'd ever made for herself — and the strange thing, the thing she would remember all her life, was that she was not frightened yet. She was only curious, in the way of a woman who has just discovered an error in a balance that has always, until tonight, come out even.

She lifted the latch.

The fog poured in around the door like something with intentions, and there on the sea-steps, in a basket meant for fish, was the reason the gulls had gone quiet.
