## 2. Instructions to Forget

The rain came to Grayharbor the way debts did — gradually, then all at once.

Odile Whitlock had learned to read weather the way other girls read faces. Her mother had taught her, back when there was a mother and a boat and a sea that gave as often as it took: *the gulls go quiet before a squall, little fish, and men go quiet before they lie.* The gulls had gone quiet at dusk. By nine o'clock the rain was coming down on Meridian House like a verdict, hammering the slate roof, drowning the gutters, turning the cobbled yard into a shallow grey sea of its own.

She was in the counting room, where she was not strictly supposed to be after hours, doing what she was not strictly supposed to do — which was nothing at all, visibly. Invisibility, Odile had found, required maintenance. A girl who was too diligent drew notice; a girl who was too idle drew more. The trick was to be exactly as useful as furniture: present in every room, remarked upon by no one, indispensable in the way that a good chair is indispensable, which is to say only when it is missing.

So she sat with the ration ledger open before her and her pen moving in small, honest strokes, recording three dozen tins of condensed milk received from the *Cormorant* and dispatched to the hospital stores, and she let her mind do what it always did while her hands worked, which was everything else.

It was a talent, though she'd never have called it that aloud. Mr. Calloway, the factor, once said — kindly, in his way that felt like being weighed on a fishmonger's scale — "You have a remarkable memory for figures, Miss Whitlock."

"Only for the ones that matter, sir," she'd said, which was true, and also a lie, because she remembered all of them. She remembered the weight of every crate that had passed through Meridian House in four years. She remembered that Mrs. Brand watered the ferns in the front parlor on Tuesdays and Thursdays but not Sundays, because Sunday was for God and ferns could wait. She remembered that the company's private correspondence stock — the heavy cream paper with the seal pressed into it like a thumbprint in wax — lived in the second drawer of the cabinet in Calloway's study, and that the drawer stuck unless you lifted the handle as you pulled.

She remembered these things the way her mother had remembered the sea floor. Not because anyone asked her to. Because knowing where things were had once been the difference between surfacing and not.

The clock over the mantel said half past eleven. Rain, rain, rain. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged its loose rhythm against the frame, and nobody would fix it until spring, because fixing things cost money and Meridian House believed, with a piety it applied to everything, that money should flow inward like a tide and never out.

Odile finished the milk entry, blotted it, squared the ledger's corners so they aligned with the desk's edge, and was reaching for her shawl when she heard the knock.

* * *

It was not much of a knock. That was the first strange thing about it. The service door of Meridian House had an iron knocker the size of a fist, and the people who used it — carters, fishwives, the occasional customs man with bad news — used it accordingly. This sound was soft. Almost apologetic. Two taps, like something falling against wood rather than someone choosing to strike it.

She stood very still and listened, the way you listen when you've been taught that listening is cheaper than looking.

Rain. Wind. The shutter. Then, beneath all of it, thin as a wire drawn through cloth:

A cry.

Not a cat. Odile knew cats; the yard cats of Grayharbor had voices like rusted hinges and no shame about using them. This was smaller, angrier, more astonished. It was the sound of a creature that had recently discovered the world was cold and wished to register a formal complaint.

She took the lamp down from its hook and went to the door, and she told herself — firmly, in the voice she used for ledgers — that it would be a crate. A crate left late, with something alive inside it, a goose perhaps, or one of the smugglers' ferrets that occasionally escaped their sacks and caused a week of chaos. Crates were manageable. Crates went on manifests.

She lifted the latch.

There was no crate. There was a basket — willow, lined with a blanket that had once been fine and was now damp through, the kind of blanket with embroidered edges that belonged in a house where people said *parlor* without irony. And in the basket was a baby.

It was red-faced and furious and wrapped to the chin, and it had stopped crying the moment the door opened, as if it had been waiting for exactly this and had prepared remarks. Its eyes were open. That was the second strange thing. Newborns, she thought — she had no experience of newborns beyond a cousin's christening and one disastrous afternoon minding the ferryman's twins — newborns mostly slept or screamed. This one looked at her. Looked at her with the flat, unblinking assessment of a customs officer, and Odile felt, absurdly, that she was the one being inventoried.

"There's been a mistake," she told it. Her breath fogged in the doorway's cold. "I'm not who you want."

The baby blinked, unimpressed, and began to cry again.

That was when she saw the note. It was pinned to the blanket with a pearl-headed pin — a pearl pin, she noted, and some deep diver's part of her registered the luster of it even now, even soaked, even in this — and the paper was cream-colored and heavy, and the seal at its foot had been pressed into it like a thumbprint in wax.

She knew that seal. Everyone in Grayharbor knew that seal: the meridian line, the star above it, the motto worn nearly smooth by a century of stamping — *OMNIA SUB LIBRA*, All Things Under the Scale. She had seen it ten thousand times on ten thousand documents. She had never once seen it on anything meant for her.

She did not touch the note. Not yet. Some instinct older than sense told her that touching it made it hers, and she already had enough things that were hers, most of them heavy. Instead she crouched, lamp held high, and looked up and down the lane.

Nothing. Rain sheeting through the light of the lamp, the lane running with water, the warehouses across the way black and blind. Whoever had set the basket down had been quick about it, and dry about it, and gone. But the flagstone under the basket was dry. Dry, in that rain, meant minutes. One minute, maybe two. Someone had stood exactly here, in the lee of the door, and listened to the same crying she had heard, and had walked away anyway.

Something turned over in Odile's chest. She examined the feeling with suspicion, because feelings were expensive and she had never been able to afford many.

"Right," she said to the baby, because there was no one else to say it to. "Well. You can't stay here."

The baby cried harder, as if to point out that neither of them had any better ideas.

* * *

Mrs. Brand's room was behind the kitchen, and the rule of the house — unwritten, like all the important rules — was that you did not knock on Mrs. Brand's door after ten o'clock unless the house was burning, and even then you considered whether Mrs. Brand might prefer to be informed in the morning.

Odile knocked.

It took a long time. She heard the shuffle of slippers, the scrape of a chair, the mutter of a woman assembling herself out of sleep into authority. Then the door opened six inches and Mrs. Brand's face appeared in the gap, gray hair pinned badly, spectacles nowhere, eyes sharp as tacks despite everything.

"It's near midnight, girl."

"I know, ma'am." Odile stepped back so the lamplight fell full on the basket. "There's a baby at the service door."

For a moment — just a moment, a crack no wider than a hair — something moved behind Mrs. Brand's eyes. Odile saw it and filed it away, because filing things away was what she did. Then the shutters came down, smooth as a shopfront at closing time.

"A baby." Mrs. Brand said the word the way she said the word *mice*: an infestation, a matter for traps. "Whose?"

"No one's, ma'am. That is — I don't know whose. There's a note."

"Then give the note to Mr. Calloway in the morning and let men sort it out." Mrs. Brand's gaze dropped to the basket, to the small furious face inside it, and Odile watched her look away from it deliberately, the way you look away from a beggar you've decided not to see. "Babies are not linen, Miss Whitlock. They are not my department."

"The blanket's soaked through, ma'am. It'll take a chill before morning."

"Then it will take a chill." Mrs. Brand's jaw tightened. Something in her voice went quieter and harder at once, like a rope drawn taut. "I have been in this house thirty-one years, girl, and I will tell you what I tell every new maid: the company pays us to see what we're shown and nothing else. Whatever that is—" she nodded at the basket without quite looking at it "—it is not ours. Put it back on the step if you must, and lock the door."

And she closed the door. Gently. That was the worst of it, Odile thought later. Not slammed — closed, with the care of a woman setting down something breakable, and the click of the latch sounded almost like regret.

Odile stood in the dark passage with the lamp and the basket and the rain still hammering somewhere far above, and she understood that she had just been given an instruction. Not in words. Words were never how this house instructed. But the instruction was clear as print: *this did not happen.*

She had followed instructions her whole life. It was practically her trade. See nothing, keep nothing, be nothing, and in exchange be fed and housed and permitted to continue existing, which was more than her mother had gotten, in the end, from the sea or from anyone else.

The baby hiccupped. A small, defeated sound.

"Oh, stop that," Odile muttered. "It won't work on me. I invented it."

But her hands were already moving, because her hands had never once in her life waited for permission, and that, she suspected, was going to be the death of her someday.

* * *

The linen room was on the third floor, at the end of the corridor where the roof sloped low and the heat from the boiler chimney kept the pipes from freezing. It smelled of lavender and starch and, underneath, of warm dust — the smell, Odile had always thought, of a house pretending to be cleaner than it was. Nobody came here after the evening round. Mrs. Brand counted the linens on Fridays, and today was Thursday, which gave Odile a margin of one day, and one day, she had learned, was usually enough.

She set the basket on the broad shelf between the stacked sheets and the winter blankets, and the baby, sensing perhaps that negotiations had entered a new phase, went abruptly silent and stared up at her again with those disconcerting, uninfantlike eyes.

"You understand," Odile said, keeping her voice low, "that this is theft. Technically. Of company warmth."

The baby kicked one foot free of the blanket. The foot was astonishingly small. Everything about it was astonishingly small — the fists, the ears, the nose like a comma — and Odile felt that turning-over thing again, stronger now, and recognized it at last for what it was, because her mother had named it once, sitting on the gunwale with the pearls wet in her lap: *That's what caring feels like, little fish. Like something breaking. Don't mistake the breaking for damage.*

She unwrapped the blanket far enough to check the child properly, because if she was committing a crime she intended to commit it thoroughly. A girl, she thought — the swaddling was neutral but the face was fine-boned, and fine bones ran in certain families the way debt ran in others. Warm enough, fed recently — there was a smell of milk about her — dry except where the rain had reached. Somebody had cared for this child, right up until the flagstones of the service step. Somebody had dressed her well and carried her carefully and stood in the rain for a minute listening to her cry, and had walked away anyway, and had left a note sealed with the company's mark.

Which meant the note was not a plea. Pleas were written on scraps, in pencil, by desperate people. Notes on cream paper with seals were orders. This had not been abandoned. This had been *filed*.

Odile dried the change of dampness with a corner of pillowcase, rewrapped the baby in the driest of the winter blankets — the good wool ones, the ones Mrs. Brand guarded like state secrets — and then, finally, with a reluctance she refused to examine, she unpinned the note.

The paper was exactly what she'd known it would be. Heavy cream stock, deckled edge, the watermark faint under the lamplight when she held it up: a meridian line and star, ghosted into the fibers. Company correspondence stock. Second drawer, Calloway's study, lift the handle as you pull.

Three words, in a hand she didn't recognize — a man's hand, she thought, hurried but educated, the letters leaning forward as if impatient to be done:

*Forget the face.*

No name. No date. No instruction as to feeding or care or what should become of the child come morning. Just the command, as if forgetting were a task like any other, something a person could be assigned, something that could be entered in a ledger and marked complete.

Odile read it twice. Then she folded it along its original creases and put it inside her bodice, against her skin, where she kept the few things that were truly hers — and where, she reflected grimly, it would sit directly over her heart, an organ with a documented history of insubordination.

"Forget the face," she repeated softly.

The baby, hearing herself discussed, made a small imperious noise and grabbed Odile's finger with one hand, and her grip was shockingly strong, stronger than seemed possible from something so new, and she held on the way a drowning person holds, the way a diver holds when the shell comes loose from the rock and there is one chance and one only.

Odile looked down at the small furious face in the lamplight, and she did the one thing she had been instructed not to do, the one thing she had spent twenty-two years training herself never, ever to do.

She memorized it.

The crease between the brows. The milk-blister on the upper lip. The exact grey of the eyes — grey, she was almost sure, both of them grey, though the lamp was poor and she would check again tomorrow, because she checked everything, because remembering was the only inheritance her mother had left her and she had never yet let a single item of it be lost.

"Well," she said to the baby, tucking the blanket to her chin. "Now we're both in trouble."

Above them the rain went on falling on Meridian House, patient as arithmetic, and downstairs the clock counted toward morning, and somewhere in the dark of the city the person who had written *forget the face* in the company's own ink was sleeping the sleep of a job well done.

They would come back for her. Odile understood that with the certainty of tide tables. Orders like that got collected, sooner or later, by somebody.

Her plan, therefore, was simple: learn everything, owe nothing, and be ready.

It was the same plan she'd had since she was nine years old. It had simply never, until tonight, included anyone else.
