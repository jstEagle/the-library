## 3. Ledger of Small Debts

The trouble with feeding a ghost, Odile discovered by the second morning, was arithmetic.

A baby — this baby especially, who had a lung capacity that suggested a future in the shipyards — required milk. Milk required money or requisition. Requisition required paper, and paper at Meridian House was never merely paper; it was testimony. Every chit, every entry, every half-penny of tallow and flour passed through ledgers that were checked twice monthly by men from the head office who had the faces of herons and the patience of them too.

Odile Whitlock had kept herself alive for eleven years by being beneath notice. She was not going to be undone by a pint of milk.

So she did what she always did with a problem too large to fight: she made it small, and then she made it accurate.

The house received forty-two mouths' worth of provisions weekly — staff rations, entered in Mrs. Brand's hand, countersigned by the factor's clerk. But forty-two mouths were counted at the door and fed at the table, and between those two points there existed, as there exists in every large household since the invention of households, a certain fog. A sack of onions listed but not fully delivered. A cheese cut down for "spoilage" that had spoiled nothing but someone's dinner portion. Odile did not invent these discrepancies; she had only ever noticed them, quietly, the way she noticed everything, and filed them away against a day when noticing might be worth something.

The day, it turned out, smelled of sour milk and slept in a laundry basket lined with dish towels.

She began with butter. Wednesday's delivery was logged at fourteen pounds; fourteen arrived; twelve went to the kitchen. Two pounds of butter, entering the fog. She adjusted nothing in the main ledger — Mrs. Brand's territory, guarded like a border province — but the kitchen tally was kept by a girl named Peg who owed Odile for three nights of copied love letters to a stoker in Gdansk, and Peg's arithmetic had always been willing to be spoken to.

Then there was the matter of milk itself, which could not be smuggled in pats. This required the grocer's boy, Tommy Finch, who delivered on Thursdays and who had been in love with Odile since he was fourteen and she had once, in a moment of weakness she still regretted, told him his hat suited him. Tommy now believed himself engaged in a courtship of long silence, conducted across crates of condensed milk, and Odile let him believe it because belief was cheaper than bribery and twice as reliable. One tin per week would go missing from his cart. One tin per week would appear in the linen room, its label soaked off, its provenance untraceable.

"You're very quiet lately," Peg said on Thursday, watching Odile count cans of condensed milk with more attention than any sane person gave condensed milk. "Quieter," Peg amended. "You've gone from quiet as furniture to quiet as *hidden* furniture."

"I'm practicing," Odile said, "for my career in espionage."

Peg laughed. Odile did not, because the joke had the disadvantage of being true, and because Mr. Finch was coming up the steps with his crate, and because the tin she wanted was third from the left and she needed to lift it without looking at it, which was a skill she had taught herself over four days and which she performed now with the smoothness of long practice.

Tommy touched his hat. His hat did suit him. She said so, and hated herself, and took the milk upstairs.

* * *

The linen room had become, in five days, a country with two citizens.

Its sovereign was small, red in the face, and loud about policy. Its other inhabitant was learning the rhythms of a life she had not asked to govern: feed at six, feed at ten, feed whenever the lungs announced session. Sleep in the basket between the good sheets and the mended ones, where the draft couldn't reach. Change everything the moment anything required changing, which was constantly, and bury the evidence in the rag bag, because a wet nursemaid's story ended badly for everyone including the wet nursemaid.

Odile had named her nothing. Naming, she understood, was a form of keeping, and keeping was a debt, and she was already up to her ears in small debts — two pounds of butter, one tin of milk, half a loaf that Peg thought went to the rats. The child remained *the child*, or, in Odile's private hours, when the candle was low and the arithmetic done, *little one*, which was not a name, she told herself firmly. It was a pronoun with pretensions.

She fed the child now, propped against her knee in the light of a shuttered lamp, and studied the note again while the small mouth worked at the tin's spout.

*Forget the face.*

Five words in a careful, upright hand. Company ink — she'd confirmed that the first night, holding it against the blotter samples in the copying room until her eyes ached. But ink was common to every desk in the building. It was the paper that had been nagging at her, the way a wrong note nags at someone who cannot read music.

The note's paper was thick, cream-colored, with a faint laid pattern visible when she held it to the flame-light — the kind of watermark that doesn't advertise itself. Ordinary company stock, the stuff in the copying room, was thin and grey and shed fibers like a nervous animal. This paper came from somewhere better.

It took her two evenings of authorized snooping to find it. Authorized, because nobody notices a copyist fetching files; a copyist fetching files is a broom sweeping itself. She carried stacks in and stacks out, and between the stacks she opened cabinets she should not have opened, and on the second evening, in the correspondence cabinet marked *PRIVATE — A.M. ONLY*, she found it: cream paper, laid finish, the private stock reserved for Augustus Meridian's own hand and, apparently, for whoever else felt entitled to reach into his stationery.

That was the finding that made her sit down on the file room stool for longer than any efficient person would have sat.

Whoever left the baby had not used scraps. Had not written on the back of a delivery docket like any normal conspirator in a hurry. They had gone to the private cabinet, taken a single sheet of the owner's personal correspondence stock, and written *forget the face* in the company ink — meaning either that they had keys to everything, or that they didn't think of it as taking at all.

Someone inside. Someone senior. Someone whose hand she might even know.

She copied the note — of course she copied it; she copied everything, it was nearer to breathing than habit — and folded her copy into the oilcloth packet where her mother's knife lived, because that packet was the one place in the world where she kept things that were hers, and this, whatever it was, was hers now. The problem was hers. The debt was hers.

"Don't look at me like that," she told the child, who had stopped feeding to regard her with an expression of grave, milky judgment. "I'm excellent at this. I've been invisible for eleven years. You're just the first thing that's ever been invisible *with* me."

The child resumed drinking, which Odile chose to take as endorsement.

* * *

Mr. Calloway found her on Monday.

Later she would replay that corridor encounter the way one probes a tooth, and every replaying would yield the same unbearable ambiguity. But living forward through it, all she knew was: she was carrying a stack of shipping abstracts she had no reason to carry past the east stairwell, and Mr. Calloway was standing at the bottom of the stairs with his hat in his hands like a man who had forgotten why he came downstairs, and he said, "Miss Whitlock."

He knew her name. That was the first wrong note. Eleven years in this house and the factor — the *factor*, who signed everything, who answered to Meridian alone, whose office smelled of pipe smoke and other people's futures — had never once addressed her. Copyists were furniture. She had worked hard to be furniture.

"Sir."

"You're the one who copies the Vane letters." It wasn't a question. He consulted a small notebook, which unsettled her more than anything else that morning; factors did not consult notebooks about copyists. "Elias Vane. Correspondence retained under the wartime routing order. You've handled the file for — how long?"

"Three years, sir."

"Neat work, I'm told." He put the notebook away. He had grey eyes set close together, giving him the look of a man perpetually focusing on something just behind your shoulder, and a kindness around the mouth that Odile trusted precisely as far as she could throw the whole building. "My compliments. And my apologies — you'll have noticed the routing orders changed last week?"

Her heart performed a maneuver she was grateful he couldn't see. "The overseas files go to London now, sir. I re-shelved them Thursday."

"Mm." He looked at the shipping abstracts in her arms. Looked at her. There was a silence of perhaps two seconds in which Odile became aware of every fact about herself that could be known, arranged them in order of incrimination, and presented her face. Blankness was a craft like any other; she had served her apprenticeship.

"There's a girl in the kitchen," Calloway said at last, apropos of nothing, "telling anyone who'll listen that the butter count is short again. Two pounds, she says. Thinks it's mice." He smiled — the kind smile, the surveillance smile, they were the same smile, that was the horror of it. "Mice don't take butter in pat form. Whatever you tell her, tell her something better."

"Yes, sir."

"Good night, Miss Whitlock."

He went down the stairs and out, and Odile stood in the corridor with her irrelevant abstracts and her galloping pulse and conducted the inquiry she would conduct, in one form or another, for the rest of the week.

He knew about the butter. Or he knew about *a* discrepancy, and had chosen to describe it to the one person in the house who would understand both halves of the sentence — the theft and the instruction. Tell her something better. That was either a warning or a lesson. It was either *I see you, run* or *I see you, improve*.

Kindness, in Odile's experience, was rarely free, and the price was generally revealed after delivery. Mrs. Brand was kind the way winter is mild — as an anomaly you'd be a fool to rely on. The priest at the mission had been kind until she'd declined to confess anything worth confessing. Kindness at Meridian House arrived like a surveyor: it measured you before it built anything on you.

But — she climbed back toward the linen room, one slow stair at a time — if he meant it as a warning, he had warned instead of reporting. And if he meant it as a lesson, then he was teaching. Neither branch of the tree led anywhere comfortable, but both of them, she noted, led somewhere other than the tribunal, the magistrate, the gate.

She got the child up, changed her, fed her the Thursday tin stretched with a little water and rather a lot of hope, and settled into the rocking posture that had appeared in her body from nowhere, some ancestral memory of pearl divers soothing babies between dives.

"He knows something," she told the small solemn face. "Maybe everything. Maybe less than we fear and more than we'd like. So here's what happens now: I get faster, and quieter, and cleverer, and if he comes for us, we will owe him nothing, because owing is how they hold you."

The child seized her finger with the astonishing strength of the very young and held on.

"Yes," Odile agreed. "That's what I thought too."

* * *

She worked late that night, because working late was expected of copyists during inventory season and therefore the safest place in the world to hide. By lamplight she reconciled the ration tallies — Peg's corrected figures, butter reborn as legitimate kitchen waste, milk unmentioned and thus nonexistent — and she traced, for the fourth time, the route of the note's paper. Private cabinet. Owner's stock. Careful hand. Company ink.

And because the file was open anyway, and because no one watches a copyist copy, she turned the pages of the Vane file she had re-shelved on Thursday — the file she knew better than its author did, line by line, in her own fair hand over his dictated voice.

Three years of Elias Vane's letters home to a mother in the north. Three years of mud and rations and jokes that got darker each winter. She had copied them all, and withheld three pages, and the withholding had seemed at the time like such a small rebellion — pages mentioning a package moving through the port, a name attached to the route, and the name had been *Whitlock*, and the world had tilted sideways at her own desk and righted itself before anyone saw.

She had never learned whether the name meant her family or simply her surname, worn by strangers. She had never dared trace it further. Some doors you feel through the wood, she thought, and leave shut.

Tonight the file fell open to one of the withheld pages on its own, as though it had been waiting, and the lamplight lay across the familiar lines — *the route runs through the Meridian warehouse, tell them the child travels under*—

Under the door, footsteps stopped.

Not Mrs. Brand's uneven tread. Not the clerk's scurry. A pause, deliberate, the weight of a man standing in the corridor outside the copying room at nearly midnight, listening to a lamp burn.

Odile closed the file without sound, put both hands flat on the ledger as though reconciling figures were the only thing she had ever done or would ever do, and did not breathe.

After a long moment, the footsteps moved on — unhurried, almost polite — down the corridor toward the factor's office, and a key turned softly in a lock that Odile had never heard turned at midnight before.

Mr. Calloway, working late. In an office connected to a private stationery cabinet. On the same week a baby had been left on the step with instructions to forget.

She sat very still in the lamplight, one hand resting on the closed file, and understood that the tide she had been reading all week had turned, and that everything — the note, the butter, the man with the kind terrible smile — was about to come in fast.
