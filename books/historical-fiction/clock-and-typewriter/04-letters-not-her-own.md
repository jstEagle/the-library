## 4. Letters Not Her Own

The rain had found its way through three layers of tarpaulin, a greatcoat, and one woolen sock, which Elias Vane considered a fairly impressive achievement for something with no mind of its own. He sat propped against a duckboard wall in a dugout that smelled of wet earth, wet tobacco, and the particular wet-wool melancholy of forty men who had stopped believing in dry things, and he was trying to compose a letter to a woman he had not thought about in eleven months, which was a lie, because he thought about her every time anyone said the word *carbon*.

"Vane." Sergeant Hollis ducked under the beam, dripping. "Post's going out at six. You dictating or writing?"

"Dictating."

"Still that hand?"

Elias flexed his right fingers inside his glove. They moved, mostly, in the way that a rusted gate moves — eventually, and with complaints. Shrapnel at Bullecourt had done the initial work; a month of field dressings had finished the argument. "Still that hand. Come sit, you're letting the weather in."

Hollis settled onto the bench, produced his stub of pencil, and licked the point with the enthusiasm of a man who considered literacy a form of cardio. "Who's it to, then? Your mum?"

"My mum gets one page and falls asleep. This is — " Elias paused. How did one explain it? "This is to a copying clerk at a trading company."

Hollis looked at him the way men look at other men who have just confessed to naming their rifle. "A *clerk*."

"She copies things. Beautifully. With the margins of a cathedral architect and the spelling of a bishop." He had been composing that sentence, in various drafts, since spring. "I do business correspondence through Meridian House. Shipping invoices, insurance claims, my aunt's estate. She's the copyist. Before I came out here, I'd dictate and she'd take it down at something like a hundred words a minute, and then she'd copy it out fair, twice, once for the file and once for whoever deserved it."

"And you're writing to her because — "

"Because," Elias said, with dignity, "she is the only person alive who reads my handwriting without phoning for assistance."

What he did not say, because Hollis would have made it into a song and taught it to the entire signals section, was this: that Odile Whitlock took dictation the way other people took confessions. That she never looked up while you spoke, so that you found yourself telling her true things just to fill the silence — how your father had died, how you'd once cried over a dead dog named Admiral, how scared you were, actually, if they wanted the whole truth of it. She'd write all of it down in that swift, level hand, and then, when you asked for the letter back, most of the true parts would be gone, trimmed away like fat off a cutlet, and the letter would be *fine*, would be *correct*, and you'd feel both relieved and robbed.

Once — he had only seen it once — he'd caught her keeping a page.

* * *

It was the autumn of 1914, and he had come into Meridian House straight from the recruiting office, still wearing the smile of a man who has been assured the war will be over by Christmas. The fog outside had the specific gravity of soup. Inside, the copying room smelled of ink, cold tea, and beeswax, and there she was at the second desk from the window, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a sense of grievance.

"Meridian House, good afternoon." She said it without looking up, in the voice of a woman who had said it eleven thousand times and intended to say it eleven thousand more.

"I've an invoice to copy and a letter to dictate, and possibly a confession, depending on how the invoice goes."

That got him a look. Grey eyes, quick and measuring, the look of someone counting the contents of your pockets without appearing to. "Mr. Vane. The shipping one. Sit down."

"You know me?"

"You're the one who signs his name like he's stabbing it." A ghost of a smile, quickly suppressed, as though smiles were company property and subject to audit. "Sit. Dictate. I haven't got all afternoon. I've got until five, which is less."

He dictated the invoice. She transcribed it at a speed that seemed frankly disrespectful of the English language, and then he dictated the letter to his solicitor about his aunt's estate, and somewhere in the middle of it — because she never looked up, because the pen never stopped, because the room was warm and the fog outside made the world small — he heard himself say:

"— and tell him I don't care what the will says about the furniture, my aunt promised me the typewriter, she promised it on her deathbed, or near enough, she was in the kitchen making scones at the time, but a deathbed is a state of mind —"

Odile's pen stopped.

For one moment the whole machinery of her went still, and Elias had the sensation of having thrown a stone into a very deep well and waiting, longer than expected, for the splash.

"The typewriter," she said. "Which make?"

"An Empire. A monstrosity. It weighs more than the aunt did."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It might have been a smile escaping through a gap in the fencing. "I'll include the typewriter clause," she said, "in the strongest terms," and her pen began again, and the well closed over.

Afterward, gathering the fair copies, she dropped a page. He bent to retrieve it — reflex, courtesy — and she got there first, or nearly; their hands met on the paper, and for half a second Elias saw it upside down and read, in her own smaller, tighter hand, entirely unlike the flowing script of the fair copies:

*child — girl — came in with the tide run — ask no further —*

Odile took the page out of his fingers with the smoothness of a conjuror, folded it once, and put it up her sleeve.

"That one's mine," she said. "Not yours. Yours is finished." She held out his letter, correct and complete and typed-clean in its way, every true thing trimmed away. "Sign where the cross is."

And because Elias Vane was twenty-six and polite and already, in a way he had not yet admitted to himself, entirely lost, he signed where the cross was, and went out into the fog, and did not think about the page in her sleeve for at least four hours.

* * *

Now it was 1917, and the rain was coming through the tarpaulin again, and Hollis was reading the letter back to him in the voice of a town crier with a grudge.

"'Dear Miss Whitlock. Enclosed find the third instalment of my aunt's interminable estate, which my solicitor swears is the last, and which I have decided to disbelieve on principle, as the estate appears to regenerate like a starfish —'"

"Skip to the end."

"'— the hand improves. The doctors say I may play the piano again, which is a mercy, as I never could before and shall enjoy beginning from ignorance. Keep well. Keep the margins wide. Yours etc., E. Vane.' Etc.? You put *etc.*?"

"It's a term of deep respect."

Hollis folded the letter. "You know," he said, with the tactlessness of the genuinely kind, "you could just court her. Like a normal man. With visits and chocolates and that."

"One doesn't court a woman by arriving at her place of employment with chocolates."

"Why not?"

"Because she'd count them," Elias said, "log them, enter them in a ledger, and bill me for the wrapping paper," and Hollis laughed, and the letter went into the mailbag, and Elias lay back and let the memory have him, because it was going to anyway.

* * *

Here is what he remembered, laid out now in the dark like tools on a cloth.

He remembered that Odile Whitlock was the best-informed person in the port, and pretended harder than anyone he'd ever met to be the worst. She counted crates on the quay with her lips moving. She memorized ledgers the way monks memorized psalms. Once, at the harbor office, a customs man had misquoted a tonnage figure, and Odile — who was supposedly nobody, a pearl diver's daughter, an indentured girl, furniture — had said, quietly, to the floor, "*Fourteen hundred and sixty*" and then looked appalled at herself, as though the number had escaped from some inner pocket she kept locked.

He remembered that she carried something wrapped in oilcloth in her satchel, always, and that when Mrs. Brand the housekeeper once reached for the satchel to store it, Odile had moved — fast, fluid, a diver's motion, there and gone — so that the satchel was behind her leg and her face was pleasant and empty, and no one but Elias had noticed anything at all.

He remembered that she was indentured — that the word was stamped in her file, that her wages went mostly to Meridian & Sons against the cost of her keep, that she was nineteen years old and legally a kind of property, and that the first time he understood this properly he had gone out and stood in the fog by the harbor rail for an hour feeling ashamed of every joke he'd ever made about his own obligations, and had come back in and dictated a letter to his solicitor that included, unprompted, a clause about trust funds for unnamed beneficiaries, and had watched her write it down without a flicker, and had wanted, absurdly and permanently, to give her everything he had ever owned including the monstrosity of an Empire typewriter.

And he remembered — this was the part he turned over and over now, in the dark, in France, like a coin worn smooth — the day the carbon papers were changed.

Company procedure. Every letter copied twice: once for the file, once for dispatch, carbons between the leaves. When the carbon stock ran low, a fresh box came up from the stores, and the used sheets were supposed to be burned. He had been in the copying room waiting on a bill of lading when Calloway the factor called Odile down to check a discrepancy, and she went, and Elias, alone, idle, and possessed of exactly the curiosity that would someday get him promoted and killed in roughly equal measure, lifted the lid of the burn-box.

Sheets and sheets of carbon. Ghost-writing, faint and reversed, the mirror-world of the company's correspondence. Most of it was dull as scripture: freight, insurance, freight, tea, freight. But near the top, several sheets together, all in the same session, all recent —

He read them upside down, which after two years of her files he could do almost as fast as right-way up.

*...berth paid at B—, no manifest entry, the child to travel as cargo, marked "samples"...*

*...the Whitlock woman asks too many questions at the quayside. Advise her contract be reviewed. Divers drown reaching for two shells...*

*...the girl keeps nothing, reports less. Her file confirms. Safe.*

His name. Not his name — but the letters were his. His letters, the ones he dictated to her, the ones about his aunt and the typewriter and the piano he couldn't play — routed, copied, carboned, read by other eyes. And threaded among them, in sessions he had no memory of dictating, entries about a child brought in by boat. About a woman named Whitlock who asked questions. About *himself*, filed and weighed and pronounced safe.

He heard footsteps on the stair and put the lid down and was examining the wallpaper by the time Odile came in, and she looked at him — that grey measuring look, that counting-his-pockets look — and said, "Your bill of lading isn't ready," and nothing else, and he took his hat and went.

He told himself a hundred explanations on the walk home. Misread it. Dreamed it. Carbon ghosts ran and doubled in bad light. The name Whitlock was common enough on that coast; half the fishing fleet was related to itself. And yet the phrase followed him down the foggy streets like a cat deciding whether to adopt him:

*Divers drown reaching for two shells.*

He hadn't known, then, that her mother had drowned. He knew it now — had learned it in a secondhand conversation, an officer's wife at a canteen in Rouen whose brother worked the same port: *"Oh, Meridian House, the factor there, yes — terrible business years ago, a pearl diver lost, left a daughter, the company took her on, indentured, nine years old, can you imagine."*

Nine years old. Signed. Filed. Pronounced safe.

* * *

"Elias." Hollis's voice, from very far away, then nearer. "Vane. You've gone somewhere."

"France," Elias said. "I hear it's lovely this time of year."

"You were smiling and then you weren't. It was like watching weather." Hollis handed him a mug of something that claimed to be tea and had clearly won the claim through intimidation. "Bad letter, is it? From the clerk?"

"No letter. That's rather the point." Eleven months of letters sent, and answers that came thin and rare and wrong — correct, courteous, *trimmed*. Yours is finished, he heard, in her level voice. He had begun, lately, to wonder what was happening to the rest.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd been doing in the trenches, where a man has time for arithmetic: his letters went through the company post. Meridian & Sons handled coastal freight; his solicitor's office banked with the firm; the whole correspondence of his small, dull, orphaned life passed through that fog-bound house like cargo through a hold. And somewhere in that house sat a woman who forgot nothing and reported less — her own file said so, he'd read it in a carbon ghost, *safe* — and who kept certain pages up her sleeve.

A child come in with the tide run. A woman named Whitlock asking questions at the quayside. Two shells.

"You're doing it again," Hollis said.

"Doing what?"

"Weather."

Elias drank the intimidated tea and looked at the seam of light under the tarpaulin where the shelling had gone quiet and the year was turning toward whatever came next, and he thought: when this hand heals enough to hold a train ticket, I am going back to that port. I am going to walk into Meridian House, and I am going to sit down at the second desk from the window, and I am going to say, *Miss Whitlock, take a letter,* and I am going to dictate to her every single thing I have spent eleven months not saying, and this time I am going to watch which pages go into the fair copy and which ones go up her sleeve.

And then, three days later, the orders came through — discharge, convalescent, home passage booked via the northern route, and the last line of the routing slip named, among its ports of call, the fog-bound one, the one with the harbor and the house and the copying room that smelled of ink and cold tea.

Elias read it twice, and smiled, and then stopped smiling, because beneath the routing slip, tucked carelessly by some clerk's careless hand, was a single carbon sheet — wrong file, wrong folder, ghost-faint, reversed — and one line of it legible in the lamplight:

*...the last berth sails with the armistice. One name only. See it is not hers.*
