## 5. The Face She Was Told to Forget

The trouble with a secret, Odile Whitlock discovered over the following week, was that it required a second person to keep it properly. A secret kept entirely alone had a way of growing heavier without ever changing hands, like a crate that no one else would help you lift and that you could not, for reasons of pride or terror, set down.

She carried it up three flights of stairs twice a night.

The linen room at Meridian House occupied the awkward space under the eaves where the roofline bent like an apology, and it smelled of lavender and lye and, increasingly, of infant. Odile had established a routine with the precision she brought to everything: feed at six, feed at eleven, feed at four, the hours measured against the housekeeper's rounds the way she measured ledger columns against their totals. Mrs. Brand slept like a woman who had trained herself to sleep — deliberately, out of duty — and Odile had learned to time the baby's complaints to the groan of the water pipes, which ran through the wall at all hours and excused a great deal of noise.

"You are," Odile informed the child one grey Thursday, warming milk in a chipped cup set in a basin of water drawn from the boiler tap, "the most expensive thing I have ever stolen."

The baby regarded her from the nest of folded table linens. She had concluded, after a fortnight's close observation, that the child was a girl, on grounds that felt scientific at the time but would not have survived cross-examination. She was perhaps four months old. She had a temper that arrived and departed like weather, a startling grip, and eyes that —

Odile stopped stirring.

She had been avoiding the eyes. She understood this now, in retrospect, the way one understands one has been walking around a hole in the floor. She fed the baby by lamplight turned low. She checked her ears and her gums and the astonishing tenacity of her fists. But the note had said *forget the face*, and Odile Whitlock, whose file at the company office said — she knew because Calloway had let it slip once, in his soft way — *retains particulars; reports little*, had made a discipline of not looking at faces she was instructed to forget. It was the same discipline that kept her from studying the factor's correspondence when it crossed her desk, though she read every word of it anyway, sideways, in the geography of envelopes.

The baby chose that moment to fix her with both eyes at once, the way infants do, as if demanding an accounting.

One eye was grey. Not blue-grey, not the washed-out grey of the harbor in January, but a deep slate grey, the exact color of a pearl before it is polished, the color Odile had spent her entire childhood diving toward.

The other eye was brown. Warm brown. Chestnut, with a ring of darker brown at the edge of the iris, like wood grain, like tea left steeping, like nothing else in the fog-bound palette of this city.

"Oh," Odile said softly. "Oh, you're not meant to be hidden. You're meant to be *unseen*. There's a difference, isn't there."

The baby blew a bubble of milk and looked extremely pleased with herself.

Odile sat down slowly on the crate she used for a stool and did arithmetic, because arithmetic was what she did when the world tilted. A face that must be forgotten rather than merely unremarked. A note under the company seal, meaning someone inside these walls had arranged the delivery. And now two mismatched eyes, which were not common — she knew this; she had catalogued ten thousand faces coming and going through Meridian House's doors, dockhands and clerks and captains and wives, and she could count the mismatched eyes on one hand — and which therefore constituted a signature. A face like that was a signature written on skin.

Somewhere in this building or its orbit was another pair of those eyes, and someone very much did not want the two pairs seen side by side.

* * *

Calloway's study occupied the front corner of the ground floor, with windows on two sides that admitted fog and refused light, and it was locked. This in itself told a story: the counting house was locked at night, naturally, where the strongbox lived, but Mr. Calloway locked the study itself even when he stepped out to the yard, even at luncheon, even — Odile had tested this once, years ago, and never risked it again — during the quarter-hour on Sundays when he attended early service and returned with the regularity of a tide.

What she knew of the room's contents she knew from inventory lists, which passed through her hands: a desk, a safe, four shelves of ledgers, two chairs, a globe with a dent in it that had been written up as damaged in transit in 1909 and never repaired, and a portrait in a frame, entered on the inventory without description. Just *portrait, oil, framed*. Every other item in that inventory received its particulars — *globe, dented*; *chairs, six, leather, worn* — but the portrait got only its category, as if whoever compiled the list had also been instructed to forget a face.

Odile dusted the study twice weekly. This was officially Mrs. Brand's domain, but Mrs. Brand's knees had opinions about stairs and corners, and Odile had long ago absorbed the task without either of them remarking on it. Twice weekly, then, she stood in the study with her rag and her polish while Mr. Calloway hovered, and twice weekly the portrait faced the wall.

It had faced the wall for as long as she had worked in the house. Nine years. She had dusted its back. She knew it was oil on canvas, that the frame was gilt gone dull, that a hairline crack ran down the lower left corner, and that behind it, on the wall, the wallpaper had stayed dark where the picture hung, proving it had hung there facing out once, for years, long enough for the sun to make its mark on everything around it.

On Friday, Mr. Calloway was called away to the customs house unexpectedly — a shipment held, voices raised in the yard — and he went out with his coat half-buttoned and his keys in the door, which he did, in his distraction, three times a year.

Odile had planned for none of this. She would like to report, later, to whichever tribunal of her own conscience convened on the matter, that she hesitated. She would like it entered in the record that she stood in the corridor with her cleaning box for a full minute, listening to the foghorn, telling herself that curiosity was a luxury for people whose contracts weren't held in escrow by their employers.

Then she went in, and she turned the portrait around, and she understood that her whole life had just changed weight.

* * *

The girl in the painting was perhaps nineteen. She sat angled toward the light with a book closed on one finger, and she had been painted by someone good — good enough to catch the impatience in the mouth, the sense that the sitter had better things to do than hold still, that stillness was being imposed on her by money and would not survive contact with her will. Her dress was pale silk. Her hair was dark, pinned loosely, escaping.

And her eyes — the painter had rendered them both together, front-lit, unmistakable, because a commission is a commission and a painter paints what is there — were two different colors. Grey and brown. Slate and chestnut.

Odile stood very still. Downstairs, faintly, the business of the yard went on: a crane's chain, a man's shout, the gulls conducting their eternal grievance. The fog pressed at the windows. The girl in the portrait looked past Odile's shoulder at something outside the frame, wearing the expression of a person who has been promised the world and has begun, only recently, to have doubts.

"Whitlock," said a voice behind her, and Odile's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

Mr. Calloway stood in the doorway with fog-beads on his shoulders and his hat in his hand, and he was looking not at her but at the painting, and his face had gone carefully, professionally blank — which was how Odile knew that underneath the blankness he felt a great deal.

"I was dusting, sir," Odile said. "The frame was moved." Which was true, and insufficient, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so. That was the strange grammar of Meridian House: everyone spoke a language of true-but-insufficient sentences, and fluency in it was survival.

"Were you," said Calloway. He came into the room and set his hat on the desk, and for a moment Odile thought he would turn the portrait back to the wall himself, and instead he stood beside her and looked at it, and something in his shoulders lowered, like a man setting down a load he had forgotten he was carrying.

"She was painted before the war," he said, which was not an answer to any question, because Odile had asked none. "Before everything. Her father wanted her painted by Halvorsen and Halvorsen was expensive and slow and worth every shilling, and she complained about the sittings so magnificently that he extended them twice."

Odile said nothing. Reporting little was her craft, but listening was her gift, and Calloway, standing in his own study with his hat off, appeared to have misplaced the habit of discretion.

"The frame goes back against the wall, Whitlock," he said at last, gently, and it was not quite a threat. It had the tone of a man closing a window before weather comes in. "Some things are put away for the sake of the thing, not the viewer."

"Yes, sir," said Odile. And then — because she was twenty-four years old and had spent her whole life calculating exactly how much courage each moment could afford, and because she heard, in the linen room upstairs, in memory, the small wet sound of a baby blowing milk bubbles — she spent more than usual.

"And the eyes, sir?" she said. "Are they put away for the sake of the eyes?"

The silence that followed lasted precisely as long as it takes a man to decide whether to lie to a servant. Odile watched him decide. She watched him decide *not* to, which frightened her more than a lie would have, because men like Calloway only told the truth to servants when the truth had already stopped mattering — when the game was lost, or won, or moved beyond the servants' reach.

"The eyes," he said quietly, "are the reason there is a wall between us and London, Whitlock, and a seal on the correspondence, and a shipping schedule I would alter if I could. Mind the frame doesn't scratch the plaster."

He took up his hat and left, and his footsteps went down the corridor, and Odile stood alone with the painted girl and her impossible eyes, and did her arithmetic again, slower this time.

Meridian. The name arrived in her mind not as a deduction but the way a ship arrives in fog — suddenly, fully formed, having been there all along making sound. The owner. Augustus Meridian, whom she had seen twice in nine years, once at Christmas and once at a funeral. He had daughters. This was known the way everything was known in the house: incompletely, through doors left ajar. The eldest was married to some baronet in Surrey. There had been a younger one. There had been a scandal — Odile had been fourteen, and the word *Paris* had circulated through the kitchen for a season along with a great deal of meaningful silence, and then the silence had closed over it like water, and the portrait had acquired its new position facing the wall, and the inventory had acquired its entry with no particulars.

A daughter who had gone to Paris and come home wrong. A daughter with one grey eye and one brown. A grandchild, then, arriving nine months or more after whatever happened in Paris, and a family that owned ships and warehouses and manifests and people, and that could lose a daughter to disgrace but could not afford to lose a bloodline to gossip.

*Forget the face.* Not: abandon the child. Not: see it provided for. *Forget the face* — an instruction aimed at the one employee in the house known for retaining particulars. Someone had chosen Odile deliberately, knowing her memory, expecting her obedience to override it. The thought should have frightened her. What frightened her instead was how much it sounded like flattery.

* * *

Upstairs, the linen room had developed amenities. Odile surveyed them that evening with the critical eye of a quartermaster: the crate-cot lined with a petticoat retired from service; the chipped cup; the hot-water bottle that was officially broken and unofficially reassigned; a rattle improvised from a wooden thread spool and three buttons, which the baby considered beneath her. A tin of milk hidden behind the damask napkins, its cost dissolved into the ration ledgers across eleven days of entries so small and so plausible that Odile almost admired them. *Spoilage, condens'd, 2 oz.* The company had taught her arithmetic; it had never taught her what it was for.

"You," she told the baby, lifting her, "belong to people who own the docks and the customs house and, as of Tuesday, my wages for the next four years, which I am given to understand they consider generosity."

The baby grabbed her collar with both fists and pulled, and fixed her with the grey eye first and then the brown one, and Odile felt the specific vertigo of holding something whose true weight she had only just learned. All week she had believed she was hiding a foundling — a sad, ordinary parcel of the war, of which the port had many. Now she understood she was holding a Meridian. Holding, in fact, the single object in the world that Augustus Meridian's money could not buy openly and could not afford to have seen. The child weighed perhaps twelve pounds. It was the heaviest thing Odile had ever carried, and she had spent her childhood hauling crates.

Because here was the arithmetic that mattered, and it would not balance: nobody leaves such a child at a service door with a sealed note unless they cannot be seen near it — and nobody gets it *to* that door without help, without a driver, without a ship or a cart or a compartment in a crate marked *spare parts*. The baby had come through the company's machinery the same as everything else in this city. Somewhere upstream of that doorstep was a route, and routes have runners, and runners have paymasters, and somewhere in the last month a line of entry in some ledger — maybe one of hers, maybe one she had copied with her careful invisible hand — recorded the freight that had ended up in her arms smelling of salt and lavender.

And here was the second line of the sum, the one she kept turning over like a shell in the palm: if the family wanted the child erased, why was the child alive? Erasure was not difficult, in ports, in wartime. Boats went down. Fever happened. A note saying *forget the face* was not the note of a family disposing of a problem. It was the note of somebody saving the problem from disposal — smuggling her *out* of her own family's reach, the way anything valuable gets smuggled: at night, through the small doors, past the people paid to watch.

Which meant the danger was not behind the child. It was ahead of her, following her trail inland, one doorstep behind.

The baby fell asleep mid-grip, one fist still knotted in Odile's collar, the grey eye and the brown eye shuttered. Odile sat in the eaves' crooked light and did not put her down for a long time.

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the house settling into its evening: Mrs. Brand locking the pantry, Calloway's pen scratching in the study, the pipes groaning their midnight argument. A house full of people keeping secrets from each other in shifts, like lamp-trimmers.

"I ought to give you up," she told the sleeping child conversationally. "I want that noted. Any reasonable person would. I've a contract, not a conscience budget."

The baby sighed and burrowed.

"Well," said Odile Whitlock, pearl-diver's daughter, indentured, unremarkable, keeper of a face she had been ordered to forget and now could not have forgotten with a hammer. "Then we'll have to be unreasonable."

She laid the child down among the table linens of a family that would use such linens to wrap smuggled goods, took up her candle, and let herself out into the corridor — where she stopped, one hand on the banister, because down in the entrance hall, muffled through two floors and the fog outside, she could hear the iron knocker on the front door sounding at an hour when no honest knock had ever come to Meridian House, and Mrs. Brand's feet crossing the tiles below, slow as a funeral bell.

Odile stood in the dark with her candle guttering and counted the knocks. Three. Evenly spaced. Deliberate. The knock of someone who knew the door would be answered and wanted the house to know he had knocked.

She had memorized enough military correspondence to know what soldiers' letters sounded like. And she knew — the knowledge arriving whole, the way the ship had arrived in fog — that whatever was beginning tonight had begun with a knock, and that the face she'd been told to forget was only the first face this affair was going to put in front of her.
