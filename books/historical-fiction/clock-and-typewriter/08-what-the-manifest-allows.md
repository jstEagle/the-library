## 8. What the Manifest Allows

The notice went up on the morning board on a Tuesday, pinned between the tide tables and a memorandum about weevils in the flour shipment, written in the same flat administrative hand as everything else at Meridian House, as though the end of a war and the presence of insects were matters of equal urgency.

*By order of A. Meridian, Esq.: In anticipation of forthcoming developments on the Continent, the Company will charter one civilian vessel, the *Corvid*, for departure no later than month's end. Berth allocations at Company discretion. Enquiries will not be entertained.*

Odile read it twice, standing in the corridor with her ledger under her arm, while around her the other staff drifted past with their heads down, the way everyone moved through Meridian House — quickly, quietly, as if the walls were listening, which they probably were.

One berth.

She did not let her face do anything. She had gotten very good at that, over fourteen years. Her face could be a closed ledger, ruled and balanced, no entry visible from the outside. But underneath it she felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth.

Berth allocations at Company discretion.

Discretion was the company's favorite word. It meant: *we decide, and you pretend to agree.*

She went on to the counting room and counted crates.

* * *

The strange thing about hope, Odile thought that afternoon, crouched in the linen room with a tin of condensed milk and a spoon, was how it arrived without being asked for. She had spent her whole life keeping her wants small enough to fit through the eye of a needle — a warm corner to sleep in, a full meal on Sundays, her mother's knife kept dry in its oilcloth. Wants that size couldn't be used against you. But the notice on the board had put a large want in her chest, fully grown, kicking, and now it would not lie down again.

"You're a great deal of trouble," she told the baby, who was gnawing on the spoon with the solemn determination of a person doing important work. "I want you to know that I knew that before I took you in. I want it on the record."

The baby's mismatched eyes regarded her — one grey, one brown, the eyes from the portrait locked in Calloway's study — and produced a bubble of milk and approval. On the record, then: forgiven instantly, the way babies forgive, which is to say they have never once considered blaming anyone.

Odile fed her, changed her, wound her into a clean towel that she would enter in her falsified ration column as *toweling, wear and tear*, and settled her back in the linen hamper with the false bottom, which Mrs. Brand had pretented not to notice so thoroughly that she had also, apparently accidentally, left a folded blanket beside it three days running.

Then Odile washed her hands, straightened her collar, and went down to be unremarkable in the corridor, where Mr. Calloway was waiting for her.

He had a way of appearing that was never quite a surprise. He stood by the stairs with his hands clasped behind him, a thin man going grey at the temples like ink spreading through paper, wearing the expression he wore when he wanted to seem harmless, which was most expressions.

"Miss Whitlock."

"Mr. Calloway."

"The tide ledgers, when you have a moment." He paused. "And a moment besides, if you can spare it. Walk with me to the harbor office."

It was not a request. Nothing at Meridian House was ever a request; even the kindnesses came with an invisible *by order of* stamped somewhere along the edge. Odile fell into step beside him and they went out through the side door into the fog, which lay over the port that November thick as wool, softening the cranes and the warehouse roofs into suggestions of themselves.

They walked without speaking until the house was behind them. Calloway breathed the sea air like a man tasting something he'd been denied.

"Do you know the *Corvid*?" he said at last.

"I've seen her name on the manifests."

"She's a tramp steamer, older than sin and uglier, but sound. The company chartered her this morning. One civilian crossing, before whatever's coming closes the port altogether." He looked at her sideways. "You read the board."

"I count what's on it, sir. Reading is incidental."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might keep its coat. "There is one berth," he said. "Allocated by discretion."

The fog seemed to press closer. Odile kept her eyes on the cobbles, on the wet shine of them, on anything but his face, because her own face was supposed to be a closed ledger and Calloway had a way of reading over shoulders.

"Company discretion," she said carefully, "generally favors people whose linen rooms are empty."

* * *

She heard herself say it before she could stop it — the words simply walking out the door of her mouth with her signature already forged on them — and for one long moment the fog held its breath around them both.

Calloway stopped walking.

He did not look shocked. That was the terrible part. He looked like a man who has set out a saucer of milk and finally heard the small feet come to it.

"Does it," he said quietly.

Odile's heart was going hard against her ribs, but her mother's voice was in her ear, cool as deep water: *A diver who panics drops her catch.* So she stood still, and let the silence do its work, and watched this man who watched everything decide how much of his hand to show her.

"I couldn't say, sir," she said, "what you mean by linen rooms."

"No," Calloway agreed. "You couldn't. And yet."

They walked on. The harbor office rose out of the fog ahead of them, a squat brick building with a light burning yellow in one window. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanged, patient and half-heard, counting the swells.

"Fourteen years ago," Calloway said, "I signed the indenture papers for a girl of nine whose mother had just drowned. Your file says — and I quote, because I have an excellent memory for files — 'quiet, exact, reports nothing, forgets nothing.' Do you know how rare that second quality is? Forgetting nothing? Most people forget as a courtesy. To themselves, to their masters. It keeps everyone comfortable."

"And the reporting nothing?"

"That keeps *me* comfortable," said Calloway, and now he did almost smile. "I am not a sentimental man, Miss Whitlock. I have been party to things in this house that I will answer for someday, if there is any justice, and I hold no illusions that there is. But I find, lately, that I would like one thing I've done to turn out well. Just one. I'm not greedy."

"And the berth is the thing."

"The berth is the thing," he said. "The last ship out of this port before God knows what. There are people in this city who would cut a throat for it. And I am authorized to give it away with a stroke of my pen, to whomever I judge deserving." He turned to face her fully, and the fog beaded grey in his eyebrows, and his eyes were kind in the way that ledgers are kind — accurate, and cold at the bottom. "Or I am authorized to withhold it. Discretion runs both ways."

* * *

In the harbor office, the clerk pretended to be busy with charts while Calloway pulled the manifest from a locked drawer and spread it on the table. Odile looked at it the way she looked at all documents — automatically, hungrily, cataloguing. Ship's name. Tonnage. Destination: a neutral port, south, past the blockade line. Crew complement. Passenger accommodation: one berth, first class, marked with a small penciled asterisk.

Everything in her wanted to reach out and touch the paper. She kept her hands folded.

"Provisions for one passenger," Calloway said. "Name to be entered before sailing. Afterward—" he tapped the line, gently, with one finger, "—the manifest goes to the registry, and what's written is written. The company cannot alter a lodged manifest without scandal, and scandal is the one cargo Mr. Meridian will not carry." He glanced up. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

That the paper mattered more than the promise. That promises could be revoked in a hallway and papers could not, once lodged. Yes. She understood paper better than anyone in this house except perhaps Calloway himself.

"I understand, sir."

"There's a condition attached, of course." He began rolling the manifest back up, unhurried, a man wrapping up something delicate. "Conditions always attach. You know that better than most. The berth is available to a person of good conduct. A person who causes no trouble. A person whose presence in this house raises no questions whatsoever between now and the sailing." The roll was finished; he held it loosely, like a baton he hadn't decided to pass. "The company values tidiness, Miss Whitlock. Tidy ledgers. Tidy rooms. Tidy silences. A berth is a small price for tidiness, I think. Don't you?"

The bell buoy clanged out on the water. Somewhere above the fog, unseen, a gull cried once and gave up.

"Yes, sir," Odile said. "I think the company gets the better end of that trade."

Calloway laughed — briefly, softly, as if the laugh might be reported to someone — and set the rolled manifest back in its drawer, and locked it, and Odile watched the key turn and thought: *there it is. Hope, with a leash and a lock.*

"Good day, Miss Whitlock. Mind the fog on your way back."

* * *

She minded the fog. She walked back through it slowly, letting it hide her face while it decided what expression to wear, and by the time Meridian House materialized out of the grey — tall, narrow, its windows lit unevenly, like a face with some eyes open and some shut — she had settled on blankness, which was her best and oldest garment.

Inside, the house smelled of beeswax and boiled mutton. Mrs. Brand was polishing the hall clock with the grim devotion she brought to all machinery that measured time, and Odile slowed beside her, ostensibly to shake the damp from her shawl.

"Well?" said Mrs. Brand, not looking up. The word was pitched for exactly two listeners, and one of them was asleep upstairs in a hamper.

"He offered me the berth," Odile murmured. "If I'm tidy."

Mrs. Brand's cloth stopped on the brass. She resumed polishing. "And did he say what happens to untidy girls?"

"He didn't need to."

"No," said Mrs. Brand. "They never do. That's the elegance of it. They don't have to threaten you — they only have to show you the door out, and let you remember yourself how many doors there aren't." She buffed the brass until her own reflection wavered in it, small and warped. "I've seen that offer made before, girl. Different year, different ship. The offer isn't charity. It's a hook baited with the thing you want most, and they reel you in slow, and by the time you feel the pull you've already taught yourself it was your own idea to swim."

Odile leaned close, as though steadying herself on the clock.

"It worked," she whispered.

"Mm," said Mrs. Brand. "Of course it did. That's what makes it cruel." She snapped the cloth once, sharp, like a woman closing a subject. "Supper's at six. The factor dines out. Go up and be tidy, and mind—" and here her voice dropped to nearly nothing, worn smooth as river stone, "—mind you decide soon whether you're a girl who swims, or a girl who cuts line."

* * *

Upstairs, in the linen room, the baby was awake and watching the ceiling with the intense suspicion infants reserve for ceilings. Odile lifted her out and sat with her on the floor between the shelves of folded sheets, in the little cave of lavender and clean cotton that had become, without anyone deciding it, the only room in the world where Odile's face was allowed to do what it liked.

"So here is the arithmetic," she said, because saying things aloud to someone too young to repeat them had become her one extravagance. "One berth. Two of us, if you count me, and one Elias Vane besides, wherever he is, if he still lives, which I have no evidence of either way and refuse to investigate because investigating is how you get caught wanting things." The baby seized her thumb with astonishing strength. "Precisely. And the berth goes to whoever stays tidy. Which means you, my girl, must become a rumor. A draft under the door. Less than a draft. And I must become what I have always pretended to be — nobody, nothing, a girl who counts crates and forgets faces."

The baby yawned, vast and unbothered, one grey eye and one brown closing by degrees.

"They told me to forget yours," Odile said quietly. "Did you know that? There's a note with a seal on it. Forget the face. And here's the joke of it — I have forgotten every face they ever told me to. Every debtor, every drunk captain, every disgraced daughter in a gilt frame. My memory is a very obedient servant." She rested her cheek on the small warm head. "But I find it disobeys them exactly once, for you, and I can't make it stop."

Below, faintly, the front bell rang — two rings, brisk, the ring reserved for official correspondence — and then voices in the hall, and Mrs. Brand's voice rising in a register Odile had never heard from her, high and careful at once, the voice of a woman standing in front of something.

Boots on the stairs. More than one pair.

Odile gathered the baby against her shoulder and listened, every sense narrowed to the door, as the boots passed the linen room without pausing and continued up toward the factor's empty office, and a man's voice said, clear and carrying through the old walls:

"Then he can explain to Mr. Meridian why his private correspondence stock is short four sheets. Wake him. I'll wait."

The boots settled in to wait. Odile sat in the lavender dark with the child's heartbeat against her collarbone, and understood, with the cold clarity of deep water, that the tide had just gone out from under somebody — and that the somebody might be her.
