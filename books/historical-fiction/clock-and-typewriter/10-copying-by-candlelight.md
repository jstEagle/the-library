## 10. Copying by Candlelight

Odile had a theory about Elias Vane, and the theory was this: he could not sit still in a chair like an honest man.

He had been at Meridian House four days now — discharged, half-healed, and possessed of a cane he used mostly as a pointer, a doorstopper, and once, memorably, to retrieve a dropped spool of thread from under the copying desk without bending down. He sat with one leg hooked over the arm of his chair like a man perpetually about to be somewhere else. Mrs. Brand disapproved of him thoroughly and fed him second helpings anyway, which Odile had learned was the highest compliment the woman paid.

Tonight the copying room was theirs alone. The rest of the house slept — or pretended to. Rain worked at the windows the way it always did in this port, patient and without ambition. Two candles burned on the desk between them, and between the candles they had laid out their evidence like a pair of gamblers showing hands.

His letters, forty-one of them, arranged by date.

Her ledgers, three volumes, open at the pages that didn't balance.

"You understand," Odile said, "that if anyone asks, we are doing correspondence backfill for the London office."

"Are we not?"

"We are absolutely not."

"Good." He pulled the first letter toward him with one finger. "I'd hate to have spent four years writing things worth hiding only to hide nothing now."

* * *

Elias had thought, coming home, that the worst of it would be his leg. He'd budgeted for the leg. The leg was manageable; the leg and he had reached an arrangement, the way two men sharing a narrow bunk do.

What he had not budgeted for was Odile Whitlock reading his own handwriting back to him.

He had never seen her face while she worked. Four years ago she had been a girl at the corner desk with ink on her cuff, head down, invisible — he'd signed the requisition for her herself, he remembered that now, remembered thinking *there's a girl who will still be here when the building falls down.* Then the war had eaten the years whole, and when he came back through the service door with a cane and a discharge paper, there she was at the same desk, same posture, same ink — and looking up at him with eyes that said, quite plainly, *I know what you wrote.*

Nobody had ever known what he wrote. That had been rather the point of dictating letters to a copyist three hundred miles from everyone he knew.

"The seventh of March, 1916," Odile read now, in the flat careful voice she probably used for grocery lists. "'Tell C. the consignment went north as planned. Tell her the child was warm when I left it.' Period. Then your usual paragraph about mud."

"I remember the mud paragraph," Elias said. "I was proud of the mud paragraph."

"It's very good mud."

"It won me exactly nothing. Go on."

She turned the letter so they could both read it, and their heads came close over the paper, close enough that he could see the candle flame doubled in her eyes, and he made himself look at the page instead because there were only so many new things a recovering man could survive in one week.

"C.," he said. "Clare Meridian. The daughter. She was sent away in disgrace two years ago — before my time at the front, but Brand still sets an extra place at Christmas and then puts it back. I thought she'd gone to an aunt in Edinburgh."

"She went north," Odile said. "The letter says north."

"And the child was warm," Elias said slowly, "when you left it."

They looked at each other. Then, in the manner of two people who have discovered they've been carrying opposite halves of the same map, they both began pulling papers at once.

* * *

Here is what Odile had: dates.

She had kept a private ledger in her head since she was nine years old, because a girl who owns nothing learns to own arithmetic instead. And the company's shipping ledger, the one she balanced nightly, had gaps in it like a mouth missing teeth. Crates marked *textiles* that weighed wrong. A wagon that left on the quarter-tide six times in two years, always on nights the harbor master's log showed no inspection. And each time, within a day, one of Elias's letters to "C." mentioning a consignment gone north.

"Your handwriting changed on those paragraphs," she said. "Tighter. You pressed harder."

"Because I was dictating them standing up in a dugout with someone's boot on my neck, most likely." He rubbed his jaw. "No — I'll tell you honestly. Because those weren't mine. Clare wrote to me direct, twice, before she disappeared. Her hand was cramped and slanted like that. I copied the phrasing off her. It felt — loyal. Like carrying a tune for someone who can't sing it themselves."

"You smuggled her messages inside your own letters home."

"I smuggled them inside letters nobody read," Elias said, and then heard himself, and winced. "That came out wrong."

"It came out exactly right," Odile said quietly. "Nobody read them. Except the person paid to copy them."

And there it was, the vertigo again, arriving right on schedule. Four years of a stranger's war passing through her pen. She had copied his mud, his frostbite, his jokes about army tea, his careful cheerful lies to a mother whose address, Odile now knew from the file room, had been a forwarding office all along — because Elias Vane's mother had died in 1915 and he had never once stopped writing to her. Every letter home had gone into a drawer in London that no one opened.

Except the smuggling paragraphs. Those, someone *had* opened. Someone had read them, understood them, and let them keep traveling.

"Who collected your letters?" she asked. "At this end."

"The company post room. Standard practice for employees abroad." He paused. "You're in the post room."

"I'm in every room," Odile said. "That's the entire trick of me."

* * *

By midnight they had it spread across the floor, because a copying desk built for one cannot hold a conspiracy, and Elias sat on the floor with his bad leg straight out like a drawbridge and refused all assistance on principle.

The route assembled itself out of dates and freight weights, the way a face assembles out of a description. Goods north — wool, mostly, wool being the most forgiving cargo in the world for padding — and coming back south, in the same crates, weighted differently: infants. Company records called them *returns.* Six returns in two years. Five accounted for in the household books as foundlings apprenticed out, wet-nursed, dispersed. Names Odile recognized, because she had entered them herself, believing them to be bookkeeping.

The sixth had come to the service door in the rain, with a note under the company seal: *forget the face.*

"But why bring it *here*?" Elias said. "If you're running babies north to safety, the last place you leave one is on the owner's doorstep. Unless—"

"Unless it isn't leaving it," Odile finished. "Unless it's *returning* it. To the grandmother."

They sat with that. The rain filled the pause.

Augustus Meridian's granddaughter, smuggled out by her disgraced mother, brought back by whoever ran the route — left at the door like an unpaid invoice. Not rescued. Delivered.

"Someone wanted Augustus to have it," Elias said. "Or wanted Augustus to owe for it. Either way, they used the seal. Who has access to the seal?"

Odile did not answer immediately, because the honest answer was a list of three names, and one of them belonged to Mr. Calloway, whose kindness she had been unable to classify for eight months, and whose study contained a locked drawer she had never managed to feel indifferent about.

"The factor has access to everything," she said, which was true, and a coward's answer, and she knew it.

"Calloway." Elias said the name the way he'd once described weather. "He signed my discharge processing. Shook my hand like he was checking it for rings." He looked up at her across the paper-scattered floor. "You don't trust him."

"He's kind to me."

"That wasn't my question."

"No," Odile said. "It wasn't."

She gathered the letters into date order again, and her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady, but Elias watched them and saw — he was learning to see her — that the steadiness itself was the effort. A woman balancing shells.

"What happens to you," he asked, "if Augustus finds out what's sleeping down the hall?"

Odile folded the oilcloth thought of her mother's knife, wrapped and waiting, and gave the question its true weight before answering.

"The same thing that happens to the baby," she said. "Discretion. It's the company's favorite word. It's in my contract twice."

* * *

They blew out one candle to be economical, which put them closer to the other, which neither of them remarked upon, both being people who catalogued everything and mentioned almost nothing.

"I used to wonder about you," Elias said. "At the copying desk. Four years ago. You never looked up. I used to invent lives for you while I waited for my letters. Spy. Nun. Champion swimmer."

"My mother was a pearl diver."

"A champion swimmer, then. One for one."

"She drowned," Odile said. It came out easily; grief does, after enough years of being carried. "Reaching for two shells at once. That's what she told me never to do, and then she did it, and I was nine, and the company bought my contract the same month. So." She smoothed a ledger page that didn't need smoothing. "You may strike spy and nun."

Elias was quiet a moment. "I'm sorry. About her."

"So am I. But it was useful information, in the end." She looked at the baby's blanket folded over the chair back — Mrs. Brand's knitting, absurdly yellow. "Everything I know about choosing, I learned from her drowning."

"That's a terrible curriculum."

"It's the only school that admitted me."

He laughed — surprised into it, a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and Odile found she liked the sound more than was strategically wise. Then he sobered and tapped the manifest page with his cane, gently, like knocking on a door.

"There's talk at the harbor," he said. "A civilian ship taking on passengers before month's end. Last one out before whatever's coming. And a berth allocated by company discretion." He glanced up. "Calloway mentioned it to me. Casually. Calloway is never casual."

Odile felt it arrive in her chest — hope, that heavy imported goods, no crate mark, no ledger line, nothing to say where it came from or what it really weighed.

"One berth," she said.

"One berth."

Outside, down the corridor, faint through two doors, a baby stirred and settled. Both of them listened without meaning to.

"Odile," Elias said carefully, "who were you planning to ask it for?"

And before she could construct an answer — before either of them could — the hallway clock struck three, and beneath its chime they both heard another sound: the front latch, turning softly, deliberately, from the outside. Someone coming *in* at three in the morning, quiet as fog.

Footsteps in the hall. Pausing — Odile counted — precisely at the linen room door.

Then moving on toward the factor's study, where a match flared behind the frosted glass, and Mr. Calloway's shadow sat down at his desk and did not light a lamp.
