## 11. Calloway's Kindness

There were three ways to leave a child at a door, Nathaniel Calloway had decided, and he had spent the whole of Tuesday evening failing at all of them.

The first way was quickly — a man walking briskly, a bundle deposited, no backward glance. He had attempted this four times between the gasworks and Meridian House and had aborted each attempt on account of his knees, which had opinions, and his conscience, which had worse ones. The second way was anonymously: through an intermediary. But every intermediary in the port could be bought, and Augustus Meridian bought in bulk, wholesale rates, the way he bought everything.

The third way was to knock.

He did not knock. He was not, whatever Mrs. Brand said of him behind her tea towel, a coward, but he understood arithmetic well enough to know that knocking meant witnesses, and witnesses meant statements, and statements meant that within the week the child would be fished out of the harbor wearing new chains. So instead he set the basket down on the wet stone of the service step, arranged the blanket with the fussiness of a man who had never once in fifty-eight years arranged anything for an infant, and stood there in the rain feeling like a great sentimental fool.

The note he had written three times. *Forget the face.* It sounded monstrous written down. He had tried softer phrasings — *do not inquire after the child*, *no questions will be necessary* — but softness invited inquiry, and inquiry was death. The company seal he pressed into the wax with a thumb that shook only a little. The seal would do what kindness could not: it would frighten whoever found the bundle into silence, and silence, in that house, was the only reliable form of shelter.

He rang the service bell twice and walked into the fog like a man leaving a crime scene, which, he reflected, was not entirely inaccurate. A crime against whom, exactly, was the question he intended to spend the rest of his life not answering.

* * *

In his office the next morning, with the ledgers open before him and the rain still working patiently at the window, Calloway performed his usual morning accounting. This was a private ritual and had been for thirty-one years: not the company's ledger, which was a work of fiction in fine binding, but the true one, kept in his head, where ink could be subpoenaed by no one.

Item: eleven years he had signed manifests for crates marked as machinery that clinked when lifted. Item: four names struck from the wage books at Mr. Meridian's instruction, men who had asked after cargo that was not cargo. Item: one letter, dictated by a soldier too wounded to write, copied by the Whitlock girl and returned to the sender's family with two pages missing — pages Calloway himself had removed from the copying book before they ever reached her desk, because he read everything, because reading everything was his job, because his job was to be the machine's memory so the machine's hands could stay clean.

He had not managed it. His hands were filthy. They had been filthy since 1886, when he'd taken the factor's position at twenty-seven and told himself, as every man in such a position tells himself, that he would reform the place from within.

Thirty-one years of reforming from within. The place remained un-reformed. He remained within.

And now there was a basket on the service step containing the granddaughter of the man who owned Calloway's contract, his wages, his small rented rooms, and — though this last was not written anywhere — his remaining conscience.

Because here was the shape of it, the true ledger entry he could never record: Margaret Meridian. Nineteen years old last spring, sent away in disgrace to her aunt in the north with a trunk of mourning clothes she had no right to, since nobody had died yet. She had come back once, in March, thinner and furious and carrying a secret under her coat that was doing a poor job of staying secret. She had come to the office after hours and put the child on his desk — actually on the desk, among the shipping invoices, as if presenting a peculiar invoice — and said, "Grandfather knows. He's decided. I'm to be sent abroad again and the baby is to be 'placed.' You know what placed means. You've signed the papers for placed."

He had known. God help him, he had known precisely.

"And you'll sign those too," she had said. Not a question. She had her grandfather's eyes, which in her were still capable of disappointment, and she had aimed them at him like a lamp.

"I'll see the child safe," Calloway had said, which was the sort of sentence a man says when he has not yet checked whether he intends to keep it.

She had left before dawn on the northern boat. He had spent six weeks discovering what he intended.

* * *

The difficulty was that Augustus Meridian did not want the child drowned or lost or given to a fisherman's wife up the coast. Any of those might surface someday, might walk into some office somewhere with grey-and-brown eyes and a claim. What Meridian wanted — what Meridian had instructed, in writing, in his own hand, in a letter Calloway had burned and then wished he hadn't, because burned evidence was its own confession — was for the child to pass through the world without a name attached to any Meridian. Placement abroad. Through channels. The channels being the same channels that carried the unlabeled crates, which was to say: the child was to become cargo, and cargo of that particular kind did not, in Calloway's long experience of reading manifests, arrive anywhere at all.

So the choice had narrowed itself while he slept, the way choices do. He could obey, and the child would vanish cleanly, and Margaret would grieve something she couldn't prove, and Calloway would add one more item to the true ledger and die eventually of having lived.

Or he could defy the owner of everything he had.

He was not brave. Let that stand in the record. He was a fat, careful, nearsighted man who took his tea weak and his risks never. But he had spent thirty-one years watching the machinery work, and there comes a point — he had reached it sometime around the sixth week, holding the child in the copying room while her mother slept in a borrowed bed upstairs, watching the girl grab his finger with the ferocious entitlement of someone who has not yet learned that the world rations things — there comes a point when a man understands that the machine does not need him to oil it. It needs only his signature. And signatures, unlike fingers, can refuse.

He could not keep her. That was plain. A factor's rooms above a counting house; a child with Meridian blood and Meridian eyes; the household staffed by people whose loyalty was purchased annually. She would be discovered by Thursday. No — she had to go out of the port entirely, out of the country, quietly, soon, on legitimate papers, to someone with no connection to any of it. He had a cousin in Bristol. There had been letters. It could be arranged inside a fortnight.

But a fortnight is a long time to hide a baby in a house that inspects itself like a ship.

Which brought him, on Wednesday morning, to the matter of Odile Whitlock.

* * *

Her file was thin, as files went, and Calloway knew it better than most, because he had written most of it. *Indentured 1908, age nine, bond transferred from father's debt.* Her father had been a borrower; her mother had been the sea's, and then the harbor's, and then nobody's. The girl herself had entered the house quiet and had never seen reason to revise the strategy. Eleven years of service. Not one reprimand. Not one compliment either — compliments drew attention, and attention, in Meridian House, was a tax.

But files are summaries, and Calloway dealt daily in summaries, so he knew how much they left out.

He knew that the ration entries for the linen room had gone strange in the past fortnight — a half-pint of milk here, a heel of bread there, entered against "breakage" and "spoilage" with the neat, unimprovable hand of a girl who had evidently concluded that if falsification was going to happen in that house, it ought at least to be done properly. He knew that the errors were beautiful. That was what had caught him, more than the milk. Every other discrepancy in the company books was ugly — greed leaves fingerprints everywhere. Hers were invisible unless you already knew to look, and even then he had needed three evenings and a candle stub to confirm the pattern. She fed the child on debts so small the company would never feel them and never miss them, and she wrote the lies in a hand indistinguishable from her truths.

*Forgets nothing,* ran the note in his own file, in his own writing, dated three years back, when he had first begun keeping private assessments of the staff the way Meridian kept private assessments of rivals. *Reports less.*

That was the whole recommendation, and it was sufficient. A girl who noticed the milk, noticed the seals, noticed the paper stock — he had traced the note's stationery to the private correspondence drawer within days of planting it, of course she had, he'd have been insulted otherwise — and who nonetheless reported nothing, wanted nothing, asked nothing. Not loyal to the company. Not loyal to anyone, apparently, except whatever private economy she ran behind her flat grey gaze.

He had watched her for eleven years the way a man watches a lock he hasn't yet needed to open.

Now he needed it open. The child could not stay in a linen room for a fortnight on breakage and spoilage; the arithmetic of crumbs failed eventually, always. The girl had to choose to protect the child — knowingly, deliberately, with her eyes open — because a secret kept unwillingly leaks, and a secret kept willingly can be kept through fire.

So he began, in his careful fashion, to be kind to her.

* * *

It went badly, at first, in the way that his kindnesses generally went. He was out of practice. His warmth came out shaped like supervision, because that was the only mold he owned.

"Miss Whitlock." He intercepted her in the corridor outside the copying room, holding out a mended umbrella. "The ribs were loose. Mrs. Brand had it seen to. Take it; your coat's soaked through and sick girls copy slowly."

She took it the way she took everything — with a nod that acknowledged receipt and promised nothing further — and he watched her file the gesture somewhere internal, cross-referenced, suspicious. Of course she was suspicious. Kindness in that house arrived like weather from an unusual quarter, and unusual weather meant a storm coming. He could see her doing sums behind her eyes: *what does he want, what has he seen, what will it cost.*

Everything, he thought, glumly, straightening his cuffs. It will cost you everything, Miss Whitlock, or near enough, and the worst of it is I cannot warn you properly, because a warning is a confession, and my confessions are worth more to certain parties than yours.

He improved with practice, or believed he did. He adjusted the coal allotment for the copying room upward — "the damp gets into the ribbons," he said, technically true. He let slip, over the ledger desk, that the manifest clerk in Bristol was a drunk and the manifests went out unchecked, which was true and useful and which she received with a stillness he had learned to read as interest. He praised her penmanship once, out loud, and had the pleasure of watching her flinch as if struck. Poor girl. Praise in Meridian House was either bait or a bill.

The terrible thing — the thing he turned over at night in his narrow bed like a coin worn smooth — was that he liked her. Genuinely. Without strategy, in the hours when strategy slept. She reminded him of no one so much as his younger self, the twenty-seven-year-old who had arrived with reformation in his briefcase, except that she had never had the luxury of illusions and had built her competence directly on top of their absence, which made her stronger than he had ever been. He was complicit in the machine and hated himself in the ordinary, manageable way; she had simply learned the machine's habits in order to survive it, and hated nothing, having priced hatred beyond her budget. He admired her enormously. He was fairly sure that between them, one of these dispositions was going to get the other one destroyed.

* * *

Thursday, he heard the news that rearranged all his careful calendars: the *Corinthia*'s sailing moved up. Armistice talk was running through the port like gin through a wake — rumors arriving faster than dispatches, bells primed, whistles ready — and Meridian, from London, had wired instructions to accelerate everything. All departures advanced. All accounts settled early. All promises subject to revision, though that last was unwritten; it was merely the grammar of the man.

Calloway sat with the wire for a long time.

A fortnight had been comfortable. A fortnight had allowed for the fiction that he controlled events. Now the calendar was shrinking around the child like ice around a drowning man's sleeve, and Meridian himself was coming home ahead of it — Calloway knew the man's habits; accelerated sailings meant the owner wanted his hands on the tiller personally, and the tiller he would want his hands on was the smuggling schedule before any amnesty could make evidence of it.

And Meridian coming home meant interviews. Staff, one by one, in the study, door closed. Calloway had sat through enough of them across the desk to know the procedure: the mild voice, the seemingly idle questions, the way a man's entire employment could turn on the pause before an answer. Meridian did not need proof. Proof was for tribunals. Meridian needed only suspicion, and suspicion he manufactured locally, from raw materials.

Odile Whitlock, feeding a hidden infant on falsified rations, with a note in the company seal folded in her apron pocket — he was nearly certain she still had it; she was the sort to keep evidence even knowing it was a noose, because evidence was also a lever — would sit in that study, and Meridian would look at her, and Calloway's whole clumsy architecture of kindness would be tested by a man far better at cruelty than Calloway had ever managed to be at gentleness.

He should take the child tonight. Tonight, to Bristol, consequences be hanged.

Instead he unlocked the correspondence drawer, took out the manifest of the *Corinthia*, and looked at the line he had been trying not to look at for a week. Berth one. Single civilian allocation, discretionary, at the factor's nomination. A kindness built into the ship's paperwork by some distant committee that had never imagined what discretion would be used for in a port like this.

One berth. One name.

He had told himself he was keeping it for the child — that when the hour came he would enter *infant, ward of company* and dare anyone to question it. But he had watched the girl in the copying room these past weeks, and he had seen what happened to her face when she looked at the fog where the ships went out, and he understood, with the miserable clarity available only to men reading other people's hearts because their own were too painful to audit, that she was counting exits too. That she had counted every crate, memorized every ledger, catalogued everything that could be carried — and that a berth on the last ship out was the single item on her list she could not carry, only be carried by.

His protection had an expiration date. He had known it from the beginning; he had chosen her partly *because* of it, though he'd dressed that choice up as prudence. When Meridian walked through the door — and the wire said Friday, which meant Wednesday, because Meridian's trains ran on rumor — Calloway's usefulness as a shield would end and his exposure as a traitor would begin, and everything he had set in motion would be in the hands of a girl of twenty and a soldier he hadn't yet accounted for and a housekeeper who had survived this house longer than any of them, which was its own ominous credential.

He dipped his pen. In the margin of the manifest, in a shorthand only he used, he wrote a single word: *Whitlock?*

Then he crossed it out. Then he looked at the crossing-out for a long time, because a crossed-out word is still legible, and in that house legible margins had hanged men.

Below the stairs, faintly, he could hear the child crying, and Odile's low voice doing whatever it was Odile did to make the world tolerable — and beneath both, at the far end of the drive, the crunch of gravel under motor tires, two days early, getting louder.

Mr. Meridian, Calloway thought, setting down his pen with a hand that had finally earned its shake, is home.
