## 12. The Owner Comes Home

The telegram came on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day Odile Whitlock did the silver.

She learned of it the way she learned of most things at Meridian House — sideways, through the seams. Mrs. Brand received the flimsy yellow slip from the boy at the service door, read it twice, and then stood very still in the corridor with her hands folded, the way she did when the news was bad but not yet official. Odile was polishing a fork. She did not look up. She counted the seconds instead — one, two, three — and at four Mrs. Brand said, in a voice sanded flat, "Mr. Meridian arrives on the evening train. Thursday."

"Thursday," Odile repeated, as though confirming a delivery of lamp oil.

"He was not expected until the new year."

"No," Odile agreed, and buffed the tines of the fork until they threw back a small, honest light.

* * *

Here is what everyone in Meridian House understood by Wednesday noon, without anyone saying it aloud: Augustus Meridian had come home early because something was leaking.

Not water. Water they could have handled; there were buckets for water. This was paper leaking — correspondence leaking — and paper, in that house, was more dangerous than any flood. The company's letters moved through Meridian House the way blood moves through a body, and somewhere in all that circulation there was a cut, and Augustus Meridian was coming home to find it with his thumb.

Odile spent Wednesday doing exactly what she always did, which was the point. She inventoried the cellar stores. She entered the coal deliveries in her neat, upright hand. She fed the baby — *her* baby now, though no document in the building would admit it — mashed pear and bread soaked in milk, and the child gripped her thumb with one fist and looked up at her with those two mismatched eyes, grey and brown, like weather arguing over a field.

"You're going to be very quiet tomorrow," Odile told her. "Quieter than you've ever been. Quieter than furniture."

The baby blew a raspberry into the pear.

"Yes," Odile said. "That's the spirit. Aim for slightly less than that."

* * *

Thursday came down out of the sky like a dropped anvil. The fog was thick enough to chew, and through it the train announced itself with a long, mournful hoot, and by four o'clock the whole house had been scoured, straightened, and arranged into its best version of itself — which is to say, its most obedient version. Mrs. Brand walked the corridors with a candle checking for dust the way other people checked for intruders. The boots were blacked. The brass was blinding.

And Odile? Odile put on her plainest apron, smoothed her hair into its usual unremarkable state, and rehearsed nothing at all.

This was the trick, and she had learned it young: you did not rehearse blankness. Rehearsed faces cracked under pressure like river ice. You simply became, genuinely and completely, a person who had noticed nothing, wondered nothing, kept nothing — and the way you became that person was to give your mind something dull and true to hold onto while the danger passed over you. Counting. Her mother's old habit, from the diving boats. When the water went dark and the breath ran short, you counted your strokes, because a counting mind could not also be a panicking one.

So when the front door opened at half past five and Augustus Meridian came into his own hall trailing fog and London and displeasure, Odile was in the corner of the corridor with a basket of linens, counting folds. Eleven. Twelve. She glanced up, gave him the look servants give owners — brief, deferential, entirely empty — and looked back down. Thirteen.

He stopped anyway.

She felt the stop the way divers feel a change in current. He was a tall man built along severe lines, with a face like a ledger's spine — closed, ruled, expensive — and eyes that did arithmetic on whatever they landed on. She heard him set down his case. She heard him say, to the empty air more than to her, "You're the copyist."

"I do copying and ledgers, sir, yes." Fourteen.

"Hm." It was not a question. It was a mark in a margin. Then he moved on, and Odile let out a breath so small it wouldn't have stirred a candle, and folded linen number fifteen.

* * *

The interviews began after supper.

They were conducted in the study, one soul at a time, and the household waited its turn in the kitchen like patients outside a surgeon's door. Mrs. Brand went first and came out with her face set in its usual granite, though Odile noticed her knuckles were white around the tea tray she carried back down. Then the bootboy, who came out looking as though he'd been personally audited. Then the gardener, the cook, the second housemaid.

Each interview lasted between ten minutes and half an hour. Each interviewee returned to the kitchen slightly grayer than they'd left it. And each one, within moments of sitting down, was asked the same question by their fellows, in the same hushed tone:

"What did he ask *you*?"

And the answers were all variations on a theme. *Did you handle post. Did you see anything unusual. Have you ever been approached. Do you know what happens to people who steal from this company.* The cook reported that Mr. Meridian had asked whether she could read, and when she said only prices and recipes, he had seemed almost disappointed.

"It's the copying desk he's worried about," Mrs. Brand said quietly, drying a plate. "All the letters go through it. Everything out, everything in."

Odile scrubbed a pot and thought about Elias Vane's letters, hidden these many months inside the false bottom of her mother's sea chest, and about how a person could drown reaching for two shells at once, and about how the water above her at this particular moment was very deep indeed.

"Odile," Mrs. Brand said. "You're last."

"Am I?" said Odile, who had known she was last since Tuesday.

* * *

They made her wait, which was itself a technique. The kitchen clock crawled past ten, past eleven. The fire burned down. Mrs. Brand went up to bed and paused at the door and looked back at Odile with an expression that contained, compressed into a single line between the eyebrows, everything the old woman could not safely say aloud — which Odile chose to read as *keep your hands still and your face shut*, because that was the advice she herself would have given.

At quarter past eleven, the study bell rang.

She climbed the stairs counting them. Nineteen steps; she knew every one. At the top, the corridor smelled of pipe smoke and lamp oil, and the study door stood open, and Augustus Meridian sat behind his desk with a single green-shaded lamp turned on the chair opposite him like an interrogation performed by furniture.

"Sit down, Whitlock."

She sat. She folded her hands in her lap. She let her gaze rest on a point approximately six inches to the left of his shoulder, which was respectful without being riveted.

He studied her for a long moment. She let him. Under the table, out of sight, her heart was going like a hammer on a hull, but her face was a locked room, and inside the locked room she was counting strokes in dark water — one, two, three — and the counting held.

"Do you know why I've come home early?"

"No, sir."

"Someone has been interfering with my correspondence." He said it mildly, conversationally, the way a man might mention weather. "Letters delayed. Letters missing pages. A letter I wrote in September arrived in London in November with two sheets gone and a grocer's bill substituted in their place." He leaned back. "Grocers' bills are not my stationery, Whitlock. I know my own stationery."

"Of course, sir."

"So." He opened a drawer and took out a slim file — her file, she realized, her name on the tab in someone's clerk-hand — and turned it toward the lamp but did not open it. "Your file says you were indentured at nine. Mother a pearl diver, drowned. Father unrecorded. It says you have been at this house eight years without a single reprimand." He tapped the file once. "It also says — and I quote the factor's own assessment — *forgets nothing, reports less.*"

The phrase hung in the lamplight. Calloway's handwriting. Calloway's words, meant once as protection or prophecy, now lying in a drawer waiting to be used against her.

"What does that mean to you, Whitlock?"

"It means I do my work, sir, and keep my opinions to myself."

"Mm." He opened the file at last and drew out a single sheet — a copied letter, she saw with a distant, underwater lurch; one of the routine ones, harmless, months old, in her careful hand. "This is yours?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good hand. Very good hand." He set it down and aligned its edge with the desk's edge, precisely, a man who liked the world squared away. "A good hand can copy anything. A good hand can also *alter* anything. Did you know that in the last year, three of my private letters passed through this house and never arrived?"

Three. Not one. Odile filed the number away in the counting place, alongside the strokes and the folds and the nineteen stairs.

"That must be very troubling, sir."

"It is." He regarded her across the lamp, and for a moment something moved behind the ledger-spine face — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but interest, the cold bright interest of a man examining a lock he suspects someone else has already picked. "You were interviewed by the police as a girl, weren't you. After your mother drowned. You were nine years old and you sat in a room full of grown men asking you questions, and according to the record you answered every one correctly and volunteered nothing whatsoever. The magistrate wrote that you were 'composed beyond her years.'"

"My mother taught me to hold my breath, sir."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement; it might have been indigestion. "Did she."

"Divers learn it early, sir. You don't waste air on things that don't matter."

"And what matters, Whitlock?"

The question came soft, and it was the sharpest thing in the room. She felt it slide past her guard like a blade finding the gap in armor — not *did you take the letters*, not *where is the child*, but *what matters to you*, which was a question with no safe answer, because any answer revealed a shape, and any shape could be grabbed.

She let three seconds pass. One, two, three. Then she lifted her eyes to meet his — directly, for the first time, because a perfectly blank servant who suddenly met your gaze was either insolent or honest, and honesty was the better disguise tonight — and said:

"Wages, sir. And a dry bed. And being left to do my work."

It was, she reflected later, the most dangerous sentence she had ever spoken, because every word of it was true, and truth delivered plainly is the one coin even a suspicious man will accept at face value.

Meridian held her gaze a moment longer. Then he closed the file, squared it with the desk's edge, and said, "Thank you. Send Mrs. Brand up on your way down — no. Never mind. Go to bed."

She rose, curtsied the exact correct depth, and crossed to the door. Her hand was on the latch when he spoke again, and his voice had changed — lost its conversational mildness, gained something with weight behind it, a stone rolled to the top of a hill.

"Whitlock. The linen room on the third floor. It's been locked since summer. Tell me — has it stayed empty?"

The corridor behind her was dark and silent. The house was asleep, all except the third floor, where a baby slept in a laundry hamper lined with flannel, breathing the small, damp, trusting breaths of the utterly innocent.

One stroke. Two. Three.

"The linen room, sir?" Odile turned her head just enough to offer him her profile, her plainest, emptiest, most practiced profile, and heard her own voice come out level as a shelf. "I couldn't say. Linen isn't my department. That would be Mrs. Brand's."

A silence. Long enough to count five strokes in black water.

"Goodnight, Whitlock," said Augustus Meridian.

"Goodnight, sir."

She went down the nineteen stairs without hurrying, which was the hardest thing she had ever done, and let herself into the linen-sorted dark of the kitchen, and stood with both hands pressed flat against the cold deal table until they stopped shaking.

Then she banked the fire, took up the covered pail of warm milk she'd prepared hours ago, and climbed the back stairs to the third floor — because whatever Augustus Meridian suspected, whatever traps he was setting in locked rooms and mild questions, the baby would wake hungry by dawn, and some things mattered more than fear.

She had just lifted the child, warm and heavy with sleep, out of the hamper when she heard, below her, the front door of Meridian House open and close softly — though no visitor had been admitted all night, and no member of the household had gone out.

Someone else, it seemed, was awake. And moving.
