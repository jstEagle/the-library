## 13. Two Shells

The milk was still warm in the pail when the knock came at the linen room door — three measured raps, unhurried, the knock of a man who had never once in his life had to wait for anything he wanted.

Odile did not pretend she wasn't there. Pretending not to be there only worked on people who hadn't spent thirty years buying buildings. She set the child gently back into the hamper among the towels, drew the lid down to within an inch of closed, and opened the door.

Augustus Meridian stood in the corridor with a candle in one hand and no expression at all on his face. He was smaller than the portrait in the study, which Odile found privately reassuring; it was difficult to be properly terrified of a man whose chin her own eyes came level with. The London tailoring helped him. It could not help everything.

"Miss Whitlock," he said. "You're up late."

"The baby wakes hungry," she said. "Babies don't consult the clock."

"No," he agreed. "They don't consult anything. That is rather their defining feature." He looked past her shoulder into the small room, and she understood that he already knew exactly what was in the hamper, and had known for some time, and that this entire conversation was a formality conducted for his pleasure rather than her confusion. "You will come down to the study. Bring nothing."

She brought nothing. She also memorized, on the way down, that the housekeeper's door stood open a hand's width with lamplight behind it, that Mr. Calloway's office was dark, and that the front door's bolt was drawn back — explaining, at least, the soft opening and closing she'd heard. A man letting himself into his own house. Or a man who had been let in by someone who shouldn't have.

In the study, the fire was laid but unlit, and Meridian did not light it. He sat behind Calloway's borrowed desk — even his factor's furniture requisitioned for the evening — and indicated the chair opposite as though offering her a sweetmeat.

"Your mother," he said, "was called Maren. Maren Whitlock, of the Ceylon boats. Pearl diver. Remarkable lungs, by all accounts. Died reaching for a shell in forty feet of water off Meridian-chartered vessel *Coralline*, year 1908." He folded his hands. "I have read your file, Miss Whitlock. All of it. I read everyone's file; it is the least tedious part of owning people."

"She drowned reaching for two shells," Odile said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "That's what they told me. One in each hand. Neither one worth enough to buy back her contract."

Something moved at the corner of Meridian's mouth — not quite approval, but the ancestor of it. "And yet you've remembered the moral better than most men twice your age would have. Two hands. Two shells. Divers drown because they will not let go of the first shell to take the second." He leaned forward a fraction. "There is a berth on the *Providence Belle*. Last civilian ship out of this port before whatever comes — armistice, amnesty, or neither. One berth, allocated at company discretion, and I am the discretion. I am prepared to allocate it to you. Passage, papers under a name we choose together, funds sufficient to establish yourself anywhere the ship will stop. In exchange—"

"The linen room stays empty," Odile said.

"The linen room stays empty," he agreed pleasantly, "and the child goes where children without names go, which is nowhere anyone will ever trouble to look. You will not be asked to harm it. The company does not ask its people to do ugly things; it asks them to look away from ugly things, which you have done expertly for eleven years. Your file says so." He paused. "Your file says you forget nothing and report less. I find I admire that in a person. Most people forget everything and report constantly."

Odile looked at the unlit fire. In the glass of the bookcase behind him she could see the doubled reflection of the candle — two flames, burning in the same place, occupying it perfectly without crowding each other. She thought: that is what he wants me to believe about myself and the child. Both of us out. Both of us safe. Two flames, one berth.

Except one of the flames was made of glass.

"You knew my mother's file," she said. "Do you know what else was in it?"

"Debts. Ordinary ones."

"A letter," Odile said. "She wrote it before her last dive. I've never seen it, but I know it existed, because Mrs. Brand told me once, years ago, when she thought I wasn't listening — which is how Mrs. Brand tells me everything. My mother wrote that if anything happened to her, the company should send for her daughter, and teach her a trade on dry land." She looked up at him. "The company kept the letter. The company sent for me anyway, because a nine-year-old indenture costs less than a trade. So you'll forgive me, Mr. Meridian, if I don't take the company's word on what my mother would have wanted. The company has been speaking for her since 1908, and every word has been a lie."

Meridian received this without visible offense, the way a seawall receives weather. "Sentiment," he said at last, almost fondly. "Very well. You have until tomorrow night. The *Belle* sails on the morning tide after, armistice or no armistice — I will not have my manifests caught under whatever amnesty the politicians are cooking up. If the linen room is empty when the tide turns, the berth is yours. If it is not—" he rose, straightened a cuff, "—then I will conclude that my discretion has been wasted on you, and exercise it elsewhere. Good night, Miss Whitlock. Do sleep. You'll want your steadiness, whichever way you choose. Forgers always do."

He was at the door before she realized what he'd said — before she could arrange her face into the blankness that was supposed to be her whole fortune — and then he was gone, taking the candle with him, leaving her in the dark with the sound of the bolt sliding home and her own heart going like a diver's pulse at depth.

*Forgers always do.* He knew. He had probably known before he walked in. And still he'd offered her the berth — which meant the offer itself was the trap, or the test, or both, and she had just sat in a dark room and learned that Augustus Meridian didn't need her confession. He needed her choice.

*Choose,* her mother might have said. *Or be chosen for.*

Upstairs, through the floors, faint as a heartbeat under a coat, the baby cried.

* * *

Mrs. Brand was waiting outside the linen room door, arms crossed over her apron, which meant she had heard the bolt and drawn her own conclusions. She was seventy if she was a day and had forearms like a stevedore, and she had once, in Odile's first year, knocked a drunken tally-clerk the length of the scullery for touching a scullery maid, and had reported it as "the clerk slipped" with a face of such perfect innocence that Odile had privately modeled her entire method of survival upon it.

"Well?" Mrs. Brand said.

"He offered me the berth."

"And?"

"For the child."

Mrs. Brand said nothing for a moment. Somewhere below, the tide was turning; you could hear it in the walls of Meridian House if you'd lived in them long enough, a long slow exhale under the stones. Then she held out her arms, palms up, in the universal gesture of women who have work to do and no patience for men standing about.

"Give her here. You go and sit somewhere and be frightened properly. Frightened in front of that baby makes her frightened, and she's got enough against her without learning fear from you."

"I'm not frightened," Odile said, and heard how false it rang.

"No," Mrs. Brand agreed, taking the hamper-lid off and lifting the child with the competence of long practice. "Neither was her ladyship, the daughter of the house. Not frightened at all, right up until she was on a boat to wherever disgraced daughters go, and the company had the baby, and the company decided babies are inventory." She settled the infant against her shoulder, where she quieted at once, traitorously comforted. "Go on. Sit. I'll mind her till dawn."

Odile went and sat. She did not sleep.

* * *

This is what the night was made of:

At two o'clock, she thought about the berth. Not abstractly — concretely, obscenely, in detail. She thought about dry land that belonged to no ledger. About waking in the morning and having no one's count of her. About salt air that was not the port's fog-and-coal smell but real sea, clean sea, the sea her mother had died in and loved besides, because divers love the water even when it kills them; her mother used to say the sea was honest, which was more than could be said for anyone on land.

At half past two, she thought: the child won't remember. That was the terrible arithmetic of it, the thing Meridian had counted on. The baby would not remember being surrendered. Would grow up wherever the company put such children — a foundling home, a farm, a factory floor with good ventilation and bad odds — and would never know that a woman with steady hands had traded her away for a ticket. No memory means no wound. Whereas Odile knew exactly what she'd be giving up: the berth, the sea, the whole horizon of her own unbought life. She'd feel it every day. The child would feel nothing at all.

*That's how they get you,* she thought, sitting very straight on the edge of the cot. *They make the cruelty quiet. They make it cost the guilty one everything and the innocent one nothing, and they call it mercy.*

At three, she thought about her mother's hands. She had been nine, and she remembered the hands better than the face now — scarred across the knuckles from shell edges, nails permanently rimmed with salt. Two shells. Everyone always told the story wrong, she realized suddenly, sitting in the dark. Everyone said her mother drowned because she was greedy, because she wouldn't let go. But Odile had been on the beach that day. Odile had seen the boat come in. And the shells her mother surfaced with were both empty — worthless, rejected, tossed back by the buyer without a penny offered. Her mother had reached for two shells precisely because the first one alone wasn't worth surfacing for. She hadn't been greedy. She'd been doing sums, the same desperate sums Odile did every day of her life in the counting room, and the sea had simply refused to honor the calculation.

*Loyalty to blood is fatal,* Meridian had said. But it hadn't been blood her mother was loyal to. It had been a daughter ashore who needed feeding. Her mother had gone down for the second shell because of love, and love does not make you stupid, it makes you accurate — it tells you exactly what things are worth, and the answer is usually: more than safety.

At four, she got up and went upstairs, because she was done reasoning and wanted only to look at the thing itself.

Mrs. Brand was asleep upright in the rocking chair with the baby on her chest, both of them breathing in the same slow rhythm, the lamp turned low. Odile stood in the doorway a long time. The child's fist was closed around a fold of Mrs. Brand's apron, holding on with the whole furious grip of someone who has decided, on no evidence whatsoever, that the world is worth trusting.

One grey eye, one brown. When she woke briefly — babies surface and sink like divers, Odile thought, and the image went through her like cold water — the two eyes found Odile's face in the doorway and considered it, without alarm, without judgment, with the frank accounting of the very young.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. Eleven years of practice at forgetting faces, and she had failed at it in the first hour, and she understood now that this was the whole shape of her life arriving at once: she had made herself forgettable so that no one could ever make her leave anything behind. Disposability as armor. And here was the flaw in the armor, ten pounds of it, breathing against Mrs. Brand's shoulder.

She could take the berth. She could stand on the deck of the *Providence Belle* and watch this fog-bound country fall astern and be free, truly free, free the way she'd imagined freedom at nine years old when the company carriage took her away from the last piece of her mother. And every mile of that sea would be bought with a child who would never know what she'd cost, and Odile would know, and knowing was the curse her file had named. *Forgets nothing.* She would carry it the way her mother carried water in her lungs — quietly, completely, fatally.

Or she could stay. Stay and face whatever Meridian's tribunal was, with Elias's bargain hanging over them both like weather, with nothing in her hands but a knife wrapped in oilcloth and a talent for invisible precision.

It was nearly five when she crossed the room, knelt by the rocking chair, and eased the fold of apron out of the small fist — gently, gently, the way you take a shell from a diver's hand — and lifted the child. Mrs. Brand woke instantly, said nothing, watched.

"I have to think about it honestly," Odile whispered. "Even the coward's version. Especially that one."

"Thinking it and doing it aren't the same animal," Mrs. Brand murmured. "But mind you keep them apart tonight. Things get confused in the dark."

Odile carried the baby to the window. Below, the harbor lay black and flat, and at the far end of the quay, faint with distance, she could make out the riding lights of the *Providence Belle* — her berth already assigned, her manifest already drafted, her tide already turning. Somewhere on that ship was a line with a name on it, waiting to be written.

The baby yawned enormously, unimpressed by harbors, and put her fist against Odile's collarbone, and held on.

And that was when Odile saw the second light on the quay — not the ship's, but smaller, moving, a lantern traveling fast along the waterfront toward the company offices, pausing at the door of the counting room. Someone with a key. Someone with business at five in the morning, on the day the manifest would close.

The lantern stopped. The counting-room door opened. And in the narrow wedge of light that fell out onto the stones, Odile saw the silhouette raise one hand — not to the door, but toward Meridian House, toward her window, as if he knew exactly where she stood.

It was Elias.
