## 14. Elias's Bargain

Elias Vane had learned in France that the worst decisions rarely announced themselves as decisions at all. They arrived dressed as small practicalities. A trench to be crossed. A letter to be written. A man named Meridian to be visited on a grey morning when the fog hung over the port like wet wool and every bell buoy in the harbor seemed to toll for someone else.

He woke before dawn in his rented room above the tobacconist's, and he lay still for a while listening to the gulls argue about nothing, and he understood — the way you understand a thing in the dark before your reason can talk you out of it — that he was going to do it.

The thought did not feel heroic. It felt like putting on boots.

* * *

He had been a man made of postmarks once — he'd said that to Odile, half joking, and she had looked at him with those careful grey eyes and said, "Then I suppose I'm a woman made of other people's margins," and they had both laughed, because laughing was easier than saying what they were actually circling.

Three weeks now since he'd come down the steps of Meridian House with his cane and his half-empty file of letters, and found her waiting at the bottom as though she'd been sent there by the narrative of some better novel than either of them deserved. Three weeks of stolen hours: walks along the seawall where the wind scrubbed the smell of ink out of him, an afternoon in the chandlery where she'd held his mother's compass and pronounced it "sentimentally overpacked," two evenings in the company's back parlor comparing documents by candlelight like conspirators, which, he supposed, was precisely what they were.

They had reconstructed it together, piece by piece. His letters, the ones she'd kept back — the ones mentioning the child smuggled aboard the *Cormorant*, the name *Whitlock* passed between men who should never have known it. Her ledgers, the small falsified ration entries, the note with the company seal. The route assembled itself on paper the way wreckage assembles itself on a beach: ugly, complete, undeniable.

And somewhere in the middle of all that evidence, without either of them signing anything, they had become something to each other. Elias could not have named it. He only knew that when she handed him a page, their fingers touched, and neither of them hurried the moment along.

That was the desire part. He was clear-eyed enough about himself to know the duty part had gotten there first and would not yield its seat.

* * *

The trouble — and Elias had spent two nights lying awake cataloguing troubles the way he'd once catalogued artillery placements — the trouble was arithmetic.

There was one berth. One name permitted on the manifest of the last civilian ship out of the port before whatever came next came. Calloway had let it slip in that maddening sideways way of his, offering it to Odile like a fisherman offers bait: *the linen room stays empty, Miss Whitlock, and we'll see what discretion allows.* Odile had repeated the conversation to Elias on the seawall, flat-voiced, and then she had said the thing that had kept him up ever since:

"He thinks buying me is as simple as showing me the exit."

"And is it?" Elias had asked, and hated himself for asking.

She'd turned to look at him then, fog beading in her hair, and smiled the smallest smile he'd ever seen on a human face. "I've been sold once already, Captain Vane. I remember how the paperwork felt."

So no. She would not take the berth. Not while the baby stayed hidden in the linen room, not while the smuggling ledger sat half-reconstructed in his coat lining, not while *he* — and this was the part that undid him — not while Elias stood in the same port with a target painted on him by everything he knew.

Because here was the arithmetic. Odile knew things. Elias knew things. And Augustus Meridian was coming home — Calloway had warned them of it, the owner summoned back early by the stink of leaking correspondence — and when a man like Meridian hunted a leak, he did not distinguish carefully between the girl who copied letters and the soldier who wrote them. One of them was indentured and expendable by contract. The other was merely a civilian with a discharge and a limp and no institutional protection whatsoever.

Elias had done the sums a hundred times and they always came out the same. If it came to a choice between them, Meridian would break Odile, because breaking her cost less. Unless Elias gave him something more expensive to break instead.

A witness. A serving officer — former, but still; a man who had carried dispatches through Ypres and could swear to names, dates, hull numbers, paymasters. A man whose testimony, offered freely against the ring, would be worth more to Meridian as a thing to control or destroy than a copyist girl ever could be.

Trade myself for her berth. Trade my danger for her safety. Simple.

Except that she would never agree to it, which meant he must do it without telling her, which meant — and here Elias paused, pen hovering over the blank page, honest with himself at last — which meant he intended to make a decision about both their lives unilaterally, in secret, exactly the sort of thing he'd have called unforgivable if she did it to him.

"Well," he said aloud to the ceiling, "nobody ever drowned from reaching for one shell."

It didn't help. Her mother's proverb, which Odile had given him like a key to her whole character — *choose, or be chosen for* — cut both ways, and Elias suspected that choosing *for* someone else was just drowning with extra steps. But there are decisions you make not because they're wise but because the alternative is living in a world where you didn't, and he had watched enough men die in France to know which regrets rot you slowest.

He got up. He dressed. He polished his boots, absurdly, because some occasions demanded polished boots even if the occasion was selling yourself.

* * *

Meridian House at nine in the morning was a different animal than at midnight. By candlelight it had seemed a place of secrets — hushed, warm, conspiratorial. In daylight it was simply large and cold and full of accounting, which Elias supposed was the truer horror. Clerks scratched in the counting room like a rookery of very tidy birds. The portrait in the study — the disgraced daughter, the heterochromatic eyes, the whole terrible genealogy of the child upstairs among the linens — was covered now, or moved; he didn't ask.

Augustus Meridian received him in the study, standing, because sitting would have conceded something.

Meridian was not what Elias expected, though he couldn't have said what he'd expected instead. A big man gone quietly to seed, silver-haired, with the unhurried voice of someone who had never in his life needed to raise it to be obeyed. His eyes did the arithmetic of Elias's appearance in four seconds flat: the cane, the discharge pin, the coat a size too good for its current owner.

"Captain Vane." Not a question. Of course not a question; the man owned files on everyone. "You've been reading my mail."

"Copying it, sir. There's a difference, though I take your point."

Something flickered at the corner of Meridian's mouth. Recognition, maybe, of a certain species of stubbornness. "Most men in your position would stay away from this house."

"Yes, well." Elias set his cane against the chair and remained standing too; two could play at posture. "I've spent three years learning that staying away from dangerous places doesn't keep you safe, it just means the danger gets there before you do."

"France?"

"Ypres. Then a hospital in Rouen, then a dockyard in this fog-bound purgatory. You'll find it all in my file, I imagine, right beside Miss Whitlock's employment record."

The name landed between them like a dropped glass. Meridian did not bend to pick it up. He walked instead to the window, hands clasped behind him, and looked out at the harbor where the last ship waited with its single unallocated berth like a loaded chamber.

"I'll be direct," Meridian said. "You know what you know. The question is what it costs to make you forget it."

"Actually," Elias said, heart going hard and steady, the old artillery-heart, the one that beat properly only when the shells were real, "I've come to sell you the opposite."

He laid it out plainly, because plainness was all he had and, he suspected, the only currency Meridian respected. Everything Elias knew — the *Cormorant*, the run through the northern shallows, the paymaster's false bottoms, the children, God help them, the *children* — every name and date, sworn, signed, witnessed, delivered into Meridian's hands as a completed dossier. In exchange: the berth on the manifest. Allocated to Odile Whitlock. In writing, today, sealed, irrevocable.

Silence. A clerk's pen scratched faintly through the wall, absurdly loud.

"You'd hand me the rope," Meridian said at last, slowly, "to hang the ring. Knowing I might use it on you."

"I know you'll use it on *someone*," Elias said. "I'd just rather choose who. That's the whole of my offer, Mr. Meridian. I'm told you respect discretion. This is the most discreet transaction of my life — including the ones conducted under fire."

Meridian studied him for a long moment, and Elias had the unsettling sense of being weighed not as an enemy but as inventory. Then the older man turned back to the window.

"The girl," Meridian said. "She knows you're here?"

"No."

"She wouldn't thank you."

"No," Elias agreed. "She'll be furious. She'll possibly never speak to me again. She has her mother's knife wrapped in oilcloth and I have no doubt whatsoever she could use it." He heard his own voice go quieter. "But fury is survivable, sir. I've checked. The other thing isn't."

For a moment — one strange, unwelcome moment — something almost human moved behind Meridian's face, some old ledger-entry of grief or memory, quickly balanced and closed. Perhaps he had once traded something for someone too. Perhaps the trade had not gone as advertised. Men like him usually had one such entry, buried deep, initialed in regret.

"You understand," Meridian said, "that a berth is not absolution. If the tribunal sits, testimony will be taken from everyone in this house. Including her."

"That's why I'm giving you the dossier first," Elias said. "Control the record, control the tribunal. Isn't that the company creed? I've read enough of your correspondence to quote it."

Another silence. Then Meridian crossed to the desk, drew a sheet of the private correspondence stock — the good paper, the sealed stock, Odile had traced its watermark herself — and began to write.

Watching his own safety being purchased for another person, Elias discovered, felt less like sacrifice than like setting down a pack he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had straps. His shoulders actually dropped. He nearly laughed.

*Don't,* he told himself. *You haven't survived the invoice yet.*

Meridian sanded the signature, held the paper out — then, at the last instant, withdrew it an inch. Old habit. Old power.

"One condition," he said. "You don't leave this port until the ship sails."

"I hadn't planned to."

"People say that." Meridian released the paper. "Fewer mean it."

* * *

Elias walked out through the counting room with the folded manifest allocation inside his coat, over his heart like the world's most bureaucratic locket, and stopped dead in the corridor because Odile was standing by the stairs with a stack of ledgers in her arms and a question already forming on her face.

She always caught him at doors. It was becoming their tradition.

"You're pale," she said. "Paler than usual, which for you is practically translucent."

"Long stairs," he managed.

Her eyes went to his coat — the slight wrongness of the fold there, the weight of paper where no paper had been at breakfast. She was too sharp by half; he watched suspicion assemble itself behind her face like weather. He thought, wildly, absurdly, of telling her everything right there between the ledgers and the stairs: *I've bought your ticket out, I've sold them my testimony, I've done it without asking because asking meant you'd refuse.*

Instead he said, "Odile. If a man arranged something for you — something good — and didn't tell you until it was finished, would you forgive him?"

She considered him for a long moment, head tilted, ledgers hugged to her chest like armor. Fog-grey eyes, one of them catching the window light differently than the other, and Elias thought with a lurch that he would remember this exact arrangement of light on her face for the rest of his life, however long Meridian allowed it to be.

"My mother used to say," she said at last, "that divers who reach for two shells drown. So I suppose it depends whether what he arranged was the second shell — or whether he was pushing *me* toward the water while calling it a gift."

"It's a gift," Elias said, and hated how much the sentence trembled.

"Then he shouldn't mind telling me," said Odile Whitlock, in the mild, reasonable, absolutely immovable voice of a woman who had forged ration entries to feed a stolen baby and memorized a factor's kindness like a threat, "what it cost. Because in my experience, Captain Vane, gifts from careful men always cost somebody. And I should like to know, before the tide turns—" she shifted the ledgers, stepped closer, lowered her voice to something fierce and soft at once— "*who is holding the bill.*"

Behind them, faintly, through the leaded glass of the corridor's end, came the sound of the harbor bell — and beneath it, unmistakable now, the first ragged cheer of celebration drifting up from the quays, rumor running ahead of truth the way it always did.

The armistice bells. Almost.
