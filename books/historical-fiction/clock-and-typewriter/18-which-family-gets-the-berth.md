## 18. Which Family Gets the Berth

I found Elias in the counting room at half past four in the morning, sitting where I had sat for six years, his hands flat on the copying desk as though the desk might confess to him if he pressed hard enough.

The manifest was open between us. I had left it there deliberately — my forgery, still wet at the edges of the ink, the berth entered under a name that would pass any clerk's eye: *Meridian ward, infant, per company discretion.* A name no one questions because no one has ever questioned what belongs to the company.

He looked up when I came in, and his face did the thing it always does — the small, complicated arithmetic of a man deciding which truth to show first.

"You've seen it," I said.

"Odile—"

"The draft manifests. Your bargain." I kept my voice level, the way I keep all my voices, but inside me something was moving like water finding a crack. "You offered yourself to Meridian. As a witness against the ring. In exchange for the berth going to *me*."

He didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He didn't even have the decency to look ashamed. He looked *resolved*, which is what men look like when they've decided to be noble without consulting anyone.

"It was done before you—" he began.

"Before I what? Before I had a say?" I crossed the room and put both hands flat on the desk, mirroring him, so that the manifest lay between our palms like a border. "Elias Vane, you have been dictating your life to strangers since 1914. You dictated letters home and let a copyist decide which pages arrived. And now you've dictated *this* — you've signed yourself over to Augustus Meridian's tribunal like a line item, and you never once thought to ask me."

"Because you'd have refused."

"Of course I'd have refused! That is not a flaw in the plan, that is the entire point of asking!"

Somewhere above us a floorboard creaked — Mrs. Brand, awake, or pretending to be asleep, which in that house amounted to the same courtesy. The lamps were turned low. Outside, the fog had come in off the harbor the way it always did before a sailing, thick and white and smelling of coal and cold salt, and through it we could hear the *Corvina*'s crew working her moorings, the muffled percussion of a ship preparing to leave.

"Sit down," Elias said.

"I'd rather stand. Standing is how I do my best refusing."

He almost smiled. Even now, even at half past four in the morning with a forged manifest between us and Meridian's men due at dawn, he almost smiled, and I hated how much it cost me not to smile back.

"Odile. Listen to me." He stood too, and we were close enough that I could see the new scar at his temple, the one the war had given him along with his discharge and his limp. "One of us gets on that ship. That was always the arithmetic. One berth. You said it yourself — one name on the manifest. If it's you, you live. If it's me, I live, and you go to the tribunal, and Meridian will make an example of you, and I will spend whatever years I have left knowing I traded you for myself. I can't do that. I won't. So I made the trade first. That's all it was. Speed."

"That's not all it was." My voice cracked on it, and I let it crack, because I was tired of performing calm for men who mistook it for agreement. "It was also you deciding that your life is worth less than mine. It was you deciding that love means choosing *for* someone. My mother drowned reaching for two shells at once, Elias. Do you know what she told me before she died? *Choose, or be chosen for.* She said it like a warning. She said it like a will. And every man in this city — Meridian, Calloway, and now you — keeps trying to choose for me, and call it kindness."

The words came out harder than I meant them, and truer than I'd planned. Elias went very still.

"I wasn't trying to be kind," he said quietly. "I was trying to keep you."

"And I am trying to keep *everybody*, which is how you end up keeping nothing." I pulled the manifest toward me and held it up between us. "Look at it. Really look. Whose name is on this berth?"

His eyes went down to the page. I watched him read it — watched the moment the ink resolved itself for him, the moment he understood.

"Not yours," he said slowly.

"Not mine."

"Not mine."

"*Hers.*" I set the manifest down and pressed my thumb flat against the entry, as if I could hold the ink in place by pressure alone. "The child. Entered as a company ward, per company discretion. Because here is the thing neither of us saw, Elias, though it was sitting in front of us the whole time, wearing two different colored eyes: the berth was never ours to fight over. We were two soldiers squabbling over a medal while the actual war walked past us on unsteady legs. Meridian offered the berth to buy my silence. You offered yourself to buy my safety. And the whole time, the only person aboard that ship who can't testify, can't bargain, can't be held to a tribunal — the only person whose freedom costs the company *nothing* — is a baby who was left on a doorstep with instructions to forget her face."

Elias sat down again. Not because he wanted to. His legs simply ended the argument for him.

"They'll check," he said finally. "Meridian will check the manifest before she sails."

"He'll check it *after*," I said. "That's the beauty of it. Calloway signs tonight, the harbormaster countersigns at first light, and Meridian — Meridian is spending this last night burning papers and bribing customs men, because he thinks the leak is in his correspondence, not his cargo manifest. By the time he reads the name, the gangplank will be up." I paused. "Probably."

"*Probably* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence."

"Probably is the only word I own outright. Everything else in my life has been leased."

* * *

We worked until the windows went from black to the color of dishwater. There was a great deal to do, and it is strange, looking back, how gentle those hours felt — two people who had been circling each other for weeks finally standing shoulder to shoulder at the same desk, passing the same pen, our elbows knocking in the small courtesies of shared work. He knew the smuggling route from his letters; I knew the ledgers; together we made sure the forgery would survive scrutiny, adjusting a date here, a weight there, until the lie was indistinguishable from the house style of Meridian Trading Company, which is to say: tidy, confident, and rotten three layers down.

At some point he took my hand — not romantically, not dramatically, just took it, the way you take hold of a rope in fog — and said, "I'm sorry I chose for you."

"I'm sorry I shouted."

"You're not."

"No," I admitted. "But it seemed polite to offer."

And then, because the light was coming and there was no more time for politeness or anything else, I kissed him. It was not a good kiss. It was hurried and tasted of ink and we were both crying a little, which I mention only because novels leave that part out and someone ought to correct the record. But it was *ours* — chosen, mutual, nobody's bargain — and I held onto that fact the way my mother held onto the diver's rope: not because it would save me, but because letting go without it was unthinkable.

* * *

The fog on the quay was so thick that the *Corvina* existed mostly as sound — the groan of her timbers, the slap of water, a bosun's whistle somewhere above like a bird trapped in wool. Mrs. Brand was already waiting at the foot of the gangplank, wrapped in her good shawl, holding a carpet bag that contained, I later learned, three changes of clothes for the baby, a tin of condensed milk, and a letter she had written and sealed and addressed to herself, to be posted across the sea, so that the child would someday have proof of who had loved her out of this port.

Mrs. Brand had been losing things to the company's discretion for forty years. This morning she was collecting one debt back.

"She'll need a name for the crossing," Mrs. Brand said. "The purser won't take 'ward.'"

I had thought about this. I had thought about little else for three days. "Brand," I said. "Her name is Brand. Company wards are entered under the surname of the household staff — it's in the indenture conventions, section nine. No clerk will question it, because no clerk has ever read section nine." I looked at the old woman. "And it's true, isn't it? She's yours now. I'm only handing over what's already been decided."

Mrs. Brand's chin trembled once, and then steadied, the way it had steadied through every storm this house had thrown at her. "You could come," she said. "There might be room in the steerage count. Calloway could—"

"Calloway's kindness expires at sunrise. We both know it." I turned to the bundle in her arms. The baby was awake, watching the fog with her mismatched eyes — one grey, one brown, the exact colors of a portrait locked behind Calloway's study door, the exact colors of everything she'd never be told about. "Hello," I said to her, because I had never once said it aloud, and I found I couldn't leave this quay without having said it. "You were left at a door with instructions to forget your face. Well. I forget nothing. It's in my file. So wherever you go, some part of your face goes on being remembered by a pearl diver's daughter in a fog-bound port, and that is more family than most people ever get."

Then I lifted her out of Mrs. Brand's arms and carried her up three steps of the gangplank — just three, just far enough that the ship's deck was beneath her future and the quay was beneath mine — and handed her back, and stepped down.

Elias was beside me. He had not argued. When I'd told him, an hour earlier, what I intended, he had opened his mouth to refuse my refusal, and then closed it again, and said only: "Two shells."

"One shell," I corrected. "We're choosing the same one. That's the trick my mother never got to show me — sometimes the choice isn't between people. Sometimes it's between the people you are."

The whistle sounded. Long, low, mournful, gorgeous. Somewhere across the harbor, faint as memory, a church bell began to ring — early, eager, wrong — and men on the quay started laughing and shouting that the war was over, the war was over, though no armistice had been signed and the bells were jumping the gun by hours or days. History, accelerating past all of us.

Mrs. Brand climbed the gangplank with the baby high on her shoulder, and at the top she turned, and she did not wave — Mrs. Brand did not wave, waving was for people with time — but she touched two fingers to her heart, then held them out toward us, and that was better than waving. That was a signature.

The mooring lines came off. The *Corvina* gathered herself, sighed, and began to move, and the fog closed around her stern like a ledger closing over a finished page.

Elias's hand found mine. "Now what?" he asked.

"Now," I said, watching the white where the ship had been, "we go back into the house, and we wait for Augustus Meridian to read his manifest."

Because he would. Any minute now. And when he did, the reckoning wouldn't stay on the water.

It would come looking for us.
