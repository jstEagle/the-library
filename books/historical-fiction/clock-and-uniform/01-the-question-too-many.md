## 1. The Question Too Many

The census came to the Halvard Trading Company every year on the first Tuesday of the frost, and every year Paloma Oyelaran made themselves so thoroughly dull that the takers' eyes slid off them like rain off oiled canvas. This was a skill. This was, in its way, an art. Nine years of practice had refined it to something like musicianship: the slightly stooped posture that suggested a man who thought only of freight, the flat accent scrubbed of river vowels, the answers delivered in the same tone one used for inventory — *forty barrels of tar, yes, all accounted for.*

This year's taker was different.

She arrived at half past nine, when the ledger room was at its stillest and the radiators clanked like a ship in mild distress. She was small, precise, and wore a gray coat buttoned to the throat as though the weather were a personal insult she had chosen to endure rather than negotiate with. Her census satchel was not the usual cracked leather affair but a stiff new case with brass clasps, and when she set it on Paloma's desk, she set it down *squarely*, corners aligned with the desk's edges, and Paloma felt something in their chest tilt a degree out of true.

"Odile Brac," she said, showing a tin badge. "Annual enumeration. I'll need you to confirm the household register for this address of employment."

"Of course." Blandness, deployed like a folded napkin.

She worked through the clerks alphabetically, which meant she reached Oyelaran fourth, after three men who answered her questions about their mothers' birthplaces with the bewildered honesty of dogs being asked to recite. Paloma watched her write their names down without comment and thought, *good, she's tired now,* and then thought, *no, she isn't, look at her pen.*

"Oyelaran," she said, not looking up. "Paloma. Clerk, second grade. Nine years' continuous service under indenture to Halvard Trading."

"That's right."

"Mm." She turned the register around so he could see it — *they*, though no one in Vessany had ever used the word for them aloud; they had chosen it privately, the way one chooses a name for a boat — and tapped one line with a fingernail. "Your registration of record lists your father as unknown and your mother as *deceased, occupation unrecorded.* Yet your initial intake papers, filed nine years ago, list her occupation as *mariner.* Which is it?"

"Both, probably," Paloma said, with the small apologetic laugh that had defused a hundred awkward moments. "Whoever filled out the intake papers guessed, I expect. She kept a shop later. Haberdashery."

"I see." Odile Brac wrote *haberdashery* in a hand so neat it looked typeset. "And your mother's name?"

"Serafine."

"Serafine *what?*"

"Oyelaran."

"And your father?"

"Unknown," Paloma said pleasantly. "As stated."

"As stated," she agreed. Her pen hovered. "Birthplace?"

"The fishing quarter." True, technically. The fishing quarter stretched along the south bank of the Vess like a wet sock, and people were born there in back rooms and rowboats with cheerful regularity, and no one ever asked which fifty feet of mud a person had arrived on.

"No. Before that."

Paloma blinked. "Before that?"

"You have an accent," she said, "that appears only on certain words. *River.* *Water.* You pronounce them like someone who learned speech on open water, not at a wharf. And your intake papers note you swim — unusual for a clerk, noted here as relevant to insurance premiums — and your swimming certificate examiner wrote in the margin, and I quote, 'swims like a born diver, ask them where.'" She closed the register with a soft, terrible click. "So. Where?"

Somewhere across the room a radiator banged, and Paloma used the noise to arrange their face into mild, honest confusion. It was a good face. They had maintained it through customs inspections, through a company doctor who once asked why their shoulders were scarred in patterns consistent with harness rope, through nine years of Duret's Christmas toasts. "I couldn't say, madam. My mother didn't talk much about before. People from the fishing quarter move around, boats and so on."

"Mm," said Odile Brac again. She seemed to collect *mms* the way other people collected debts. Then she opened the register once more, turned to the last column, and asked the question too many.

"One further item. New directive this year, from the Ministry. *Other allegiances.* Family abroad, business connections across the border, correspondents in foreign territories. Anything of that nature?"

The room went very quiet, or perhaps Paloma simply stopped hearing it. On the shelf above their desk sat the ledgers they had filed for nine years, and behind those ledgers, in the company warehouse, in a crate marked SPARE CANVAS, there had once been letters — though *once* was doing careful work in that sentence, and Paloma made themselves think of the crate as empty, had trained themselves to think of it as empty, the way one trains oneself not to touch a bruise.

"No," they said.

"No foreign correspondence?"

"No."

"No family across the river?"

"No one worth counting."

Odile Brac looked up at last, and her eyes were pale gray and unhurried, the eyes of someone who counted things for a living and was good at it. "Everyone is worth counting," she said. "That's rather the point of a census." She signed the page, blotted it, aligned her satchel's corners with the desk's edge again, and stood. "Thank you for your cooperation, Clerk Oyelaran. I may have follow-up questions. The Ministry is thorough this year."

"We're a thorough company," Paloma said, and gave her the bland smile that had carried them through everything, and watched her carry her square satchel out past the filing cabinets like a small gray ship making way against current.

They did not exhale until the street door closed behind her.

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the chandlery where they had lived for six years without once decorating it — decoration implied permanence, permanence implied history, history implied questions — Paloma took up floorboard and lifted out the tin.

Not the crate; the crate was gone, seized or cleared years ago in some administrative shudder they'd never been told about. The tin was smaller, and closer, and worse. Inside: a diving weight no bigger than an egg, their mother's; two pearls wrapped in flannel, worthless on any market that mattered because they'd been pulled by hand from the Vess shallows and therefore had no papers; and one letter.

One letter. Ilse Marchetti's handwriting, slantwise and impatient, ink gone brown at the folds. Three years old. The last one to make it across before the border posts went up on the bridge, before the Marchetti firm's steamers stopped coming, before *war* stopped being a word men said loudly in cafés and started being a thing with a date attached.

*P.,* it began, because Ilse never could be bothered with whole names. *Father says we're leaving by month's end. He says it will be temporary, which is what fathers say. If you don't write back I'll assume you've finally learned to be sensible and I'll be devastated. The regatta next Saturday. Come. Wear the ridiculous hat. — I.*

Paloma had read it perhaps four hundred times. They knew it the way their mother had known the river's moods — by weight, by season, by the particular silence it made. They had kept it through inspections and rumors and one memorable night when Duret himself had toured the boarding house on a charity pretext. Keeping it had been, until today, manageable risk. A calculated thing. Like everything else in their life: weighed, measured, held below the surface where the light couldn't reach it.

But *other allegiances.* A new directive. Follow-up questions. A woman whose pen did not hover by accident.

Paloma knelt at the little stove, struck a match, and found that their hands — steady hands, hands that could thread a manifest ribbon in the dark, hands trained underwater since before they could write — were shaking.

"Not yet," they told the letter, absurdly, quietly, the way one argues with a tide. "Not yet, I'm not ready—"

But readiness was a luxury the census had just revoked. The letter's edge caught, and Ilse's voice curled upward in flame — *wear the ridiculous hat* — and Paloma watched nine years of hidden life go to smoke and ash in a stove built for coal, and felt the strangest thing underneath the grief.

Relief. Terrible, guilty relief. As if a strap they'd worn so long they'd mistaken it for skin had finally been cut, and the wound hurt, and the air hurt, and they were — for the first time since the last peacetime regatta, since a girl with ink-stained fingers had gripped their sleeve on a dock and made them promise — *unhidden*. Even if only from themselves. Even if only by fire.

They sat on the cold floor a long while, watching the stove tick.

Then, because grief and duty braid tight in a practical person, they got up, dressed warmly, and did what clerks do with unbearable feelings: they went to check the files. Specifically, the old intake registry, specifically their own entry, specifically the word *haberdashery* they had invented at nineteen and never corrected.

The entry read: *mother's occupation, mariner.*

Someone had already flagged it. A thin red line ran beneath the word, and beside it, in handwriting Paloma did not know but was beginning, with a lurch, to recognize — neat, typeset-perfect, patient — a single notation:

*Query pending.*
