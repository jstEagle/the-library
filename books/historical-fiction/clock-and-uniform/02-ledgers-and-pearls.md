## 2. Ledgers and Pearls

The ledger room of the Halvard Trading Company smelled of lamp oil, cold tea, and nine years of Paloma Oyelaran's life.

They had long ago stopped noticing the smell, the way a person stops noticing their own name. What they noticed instead was the rhythm: the scratch of the pen nib, the soft *thock* of the stamp, the particular sigh the third drawer made when it stuck in damp weather. Order was a comfort. Order was a place to hide. A clerk who filed everything correctly was a clerk nobody looked at twice, and Paloma had built a whole career on not being looked at twice.

The manifests that morning were munitions—casing shells, percussion caps, forty crates of something the manifest could only describe as "machined components, per attachment." There was no attachment. There never was. Paloma copied the figures into the master ledger with a steady hand and did not think about what the figures meant, which was a skill, like diving, that improved with practice.

"Oyelaran." Master Clerk Fenn's head appeared around the shelving. "Duret wants the Marchetti correspondence boxed up. All of it. Don't ask me why."

"When?"

"Yesterday, ideally. He's in one of his moods." Fenn vanished again, then reappeared. "And don't read any of it while you're boxing it. I know you, Oyelaran. You've got reading eyes."

"I have working eyes," Paloma said, with dignity.

"You've got *curious* eyes. It's going to get you in trouble someday." Fenn departed, entirely unaware of the accuracy of his prophecy, and Paloma sat for a moment with their hands flat on the desk, listening to their own heartbeat.

The Marchetti correspondence. Nine years of letters between the Halvard Company and its rival across the river, all passing through Paloma's hands at one time or another, because Paloma handled everything, because Paloma was reliable, because reliability was the best disguise ever invented.

They rose, retrieved the empty crate, and began at the first cabinet, pulling files with hands that only trembled a little.

* * *

Thirty years earlier, or so it felt—the sea kept no calendar—a girl named Sefa Oyelaran stood at the bow of a diving boat no longer than a kitchen and told her child to breathe.

"Breathe," she said. "Not fast. Not deep. Just *boring*. Boring breath is what keeps you alive down there."

Little Paloma, six years old and furious about it, breathed boringly. The boat rocked. Their mother's pearl knife gleamed at her belt, worn smooth where her thumb rubbed it the way other women worried a coin.

Below them lay the shallows off the Vessany coast, green-gold in the morning light, and somewhere down there, oysters with secrets in their bellies. Paloma had been diving since before they could properly swim, which their mother insisted was the correct order of things. *You learn to hold your breath first. The swimming is just falling over slowly.*

"The pearl doesn't care if you're scared," Sefa said, tying off the anchor line. "The pearl cares if your hands are steady. So give your fear to the water and keep your hands for yourself."

It was the closest thing to scripture Paloma ever received. They thought about it decades later, filing manifests for artillery fuses, and wondered what their mother would have made of a world where the water itself had learned to be afraid.

Those years aboard the boat were the ones Paloma kept folded smallest, brought out rarely, like a garment too fine for ordinary days. Mornings of salt and rope-burn. The taste of their mother's fried dough on feast days, when she'd buy it from a dockside woman and pretend she hadn't sweetened the bargain by fixing the woman's nets. Other diving families, moored together in the shallows like a floating village, everyone's children everyone's children. And always, always, the work: down into the cold and the green, fingers finding the ridged shells, the long boring breath, the slow ascent with the day's livelihood clutched to the chest.

Their mother held hereditary rights to those waters. It was a strip of river-mouth and coastline older than anyone's paperwork, confirmed by documents Sefa kept in an oilcloth packet under her bunk, and she treated them with the casual pride of a woman who owned something simply because her grandmother had dived it before anyone thought to ask whose it was. "The water knows us," she'd say. "The paper is just for people who can't hear the water."

Paloma was eleven when the fever came through the anchorage. It took three children from the neighboring boats and then, with the terrible democracy of illness, it took Sefa Oyelaran, who had faced sharks and squalls and the customs office without flinching, and who died asking whether the tarp over the cargo was tied down.

It was.

* * *

The indenture was signed, as all the worst decisions of Paloma's life were signed, in triplicate.

There had been no other option, though the lawyer from the trading company had presented it as a kindness. A diver's rights, he explained gently, could not pass to a minor; the girl—or boy, the lawyer had hedged, peering at Paloma with the confusion Paloma would spend a lifetime making convenient—the child had no standing, no registration, no papers but a dead mother's oilcloth packet, which the lawyer examined the way a man examines a fish he suspects has gone off. Without citizenship papers—and where, exactly, were the parents registered? Ah. Unregistered coastal divers. Well.

The Halvard Trading Company, he said, was prepared to be generous. Indenture the child as a clerk-apprentice for a term of twelve years—room, board, instruction—and the company would hold the question of the diving rights *in trust*, pending the child's majority and proper registration. Very generous. Practically philanthropic.

Paloma, eleven years old and smelling of fever and salt, understood two things clearly: that the man was stealing from them, and that he was the only person in the room offering them breakfast. They signed. The nib scratched. Somewhere in the company's vast archives, a folder closed over their name like water.

What was surrendered: the boat, sold for salvage. The rights, held "in trust" until such time as Paloma produced papers proving who they were—which was precisely the thing they couldn't do without becoming visible, and visibility, their mother's ghost whispered, was how divers lost their waters to men with lawyers. What remained: a bunk in the clerks' house, a stipend of coppers, and a set of skills. Reading. Figures. A hand that could copy anything, forge anything, file anything into perfect, invisible order.

The company thought it had bought a clerk. In truth it had bought a pearl-diver's child, which is a different animal entirely. Divers know that the valuable things are hidden under plain surfaces, and that getting to them means holding your breath past the point where breathing seems possible.

* * *

The last peacetime regatta fell on a hot blue afternoon, and Paloma had spent it exactly as intended: badly, and happily.

Ilse Marchetti had entered the rowing heats purely to annoy her father, finished fourth out of four, and celebrated anyway. She found Paloma afterward at the edge of the crowd, eating fried dough with the guilty concentration of someone who has promised themselves only one and is already negotiating for a second.

"You have sugar on your collar," Ilse said.

"It's decorative."

"You're a disgrace to clerks everywhere." She dropped onto the bench beside them, still breathing hard from the rowing, her face bright with sunburn and mischief. Below the seawall the boats knocked gently against their moorings, and somewhere a band was playing the kind of music that makes everyone believe the summer will last forever.

They had been friends the way people from rival houses are sometimes friends: secretly, thoroughly, and with an appetite for each other's company that neither of them had ever put a proper name to. Ilse talked; Paloma listened; occasionally Paloma said something dry enough that Ilse laughed the big unguarded laugh she never used at home. Between the Halvard offices and the Marchetti warehouses, between her father's ambitions and Paloma's careful invisibility, there was this bench, this festival light, these stolen hours.

"I heard my father's men talking," Ilse said, quieter now. "Tariffs going up. Border permits harder to get. He says it'll mean war before it means profit." She pulled a piece of fried dough from Paloma's hand and ate it, which was her way of saying she needed steadying. "Stupid. Who wars over river tolls?"

"People who've never held their breath," Paloma said.

Ilse looked at them then—not laughing, not teasing, just looking, the way she did maybe twice a year when the mischief drained out and left something truer showing underneath. "If it comes to that," she said. "If it actually turns bad. You'd tell me, wouldn't you? You wouldn't just vanish into your ledger room and let me find out from a proclamation?"

The band swelled. The boats knocked. Paloma, twenty-two years old and certain of almost nothing except the weight of Ilse's shoulder against theirs, heard the words come out of them as if someone else were diving and they were only watching from the surface.

"If it ever turns bad," they said, "I'll get you across."

It sounded simple. Promises usually do, spoken in daylight, with sugar on your collar and music playing. It was only later—much later, on the wrong side of a border and the right side of a war—that Paloma understood what they had done: they had tied themselves to another person the way their mother had tied off an anchor line. Casually. Competently. With no idea how deep the water underneath was going to be.

Ilse hadn't laughed, either. She'd pressed her sunburned shoulder harder against Paloma's, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

* * *

Present-day Paloma boxed the Marchetti correspondence with the care of a person handling relics.

Every letter with Ilse's handwriting in it had been routed through company channels, of course—business correspondence only, her father's office rules—but there had been others, private ones, smuggled inside invoice folders for years now, and Paloma's chest went tight every time they lifted a file and saw the small slanted hand in the margin. *Did the Halvards ever pay for those barrels? Ask your Fenn.* Business notes. Innocuous. Precious beyond reckoning.

Into the crate, one by one. The machinery of history required it. War had not yet been declared—that was still rumor, still a growl on the far side of the border—but everyone in the company offices behaved like servants moving furniture before an earthquake, and Duret's order had come down with unusual speed.

Paloma worked through the afternoon. At the bottom of the second-to-last drawer, wedged behind a stack of freight claims, they found a folder they had missed in an earlier purge: a single letter, undelivered, returned months ago marked *addressee unreachable — border restrictions*. Ilse's handwriting. Dated the week the restrictions began.

Paloma did not read it. That was the discipline, the boring breath, the steady hands. They had read it once already, the night it arrived, and once was locked away somewhere safe. But they stood there holding it, feeling the paper's small weight, thinking: *this may be the last thing she ever wrote me,* and thinking: *if war is declared tomorrow, this letter becomes evidence.*

Evidence of what? Of a friendship. Of a promise. Of an unregistered clerk with a rival's daughter's handwriting in their keeping, in a city that was about to start asking very carefully who loved whom.

Fenn's voice drifted from the front office, complaining about the price of lamp oil. The stamp thocked somewhere. Ordinary sounds. The world pretending, for a few more weeks, to be the old world.

Paloma slid the letter into their shirt, against their chest, where it lay over their heartbeat like a second, smaller heart. Then they picked up the crate of contraband-to-be and carried it down the corridor toward Duret's office, walking at their usual unhurried pace, wearing their usual bland face, invisible as ever.

They had just set the crate down outside the manager's door when Duret himself came around the corner with a courier at his elbow, and Paloma heard him say, in the mild tone he reserved for catastrophe:

"Wake the telegraph office. If the declaration's come through, I want the seizure orders drafted tonight."
