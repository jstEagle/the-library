## 3. Enemy Property

Aurel Duret had been manager of the Halvard Trading Company's Vessany office for eleven years, and in that time he had developed a private taxonomy of bad mornings. There were the ordinary ones—a barge late, a clerk sick, the coffee burnt in the bottom pot. There were the moderately bad, involving insurance men or inspectors from the capital. And then there was this morning, when he arrived at his desk to find a telegram with a triple red border lying on top of his blotter like a dead bird, and knew before opening it that the world had decided to become simpler and worse at the same time.

*Hostilities declared effective 0600. All assets of enemy firms within city jurisdiction to be seized and inventoried forthwith. Marchetti Frères listed first schedule.*

He read it twice, because the first time his eyes had slid off the word *hostilities* the way a boot slides on river ice.

"Well," he said to the empty office, in the tone of a man addressing a particularly stupid junior partner. "There it is."

The company had been preparing for war the way respectable people prepare for floods: with sandbags nobody believed in and an evacuation plan folded into a drawer. Duret himself had drafted the seizure protocols two months ago, at the request of the board, and had felt vaguely ill doing so, the way one feels ill signing a will—not because of what was written, but because of what writing it admitted.

Now the flood had come, and the sandbags were his to arrange.

He called for his senior clerks. Three came; the fourth, Paloma Oyelaran, was already there, standing by the filing cabinets with a manifest in hand, having evidently heard the telegram delivered and read it over the boy's—over the clerk's—shoulder before Duret had even sat down. That was Paloma all over. Nine years in the ledger room and they had developed the uncanny habit of being precisely where information was, slightly ahead of everyone else.

Duret had never entirely decided what he thought about that.

"Marchetti Frères," he said. "Everything. The offices on Quai des Orfèvres, the warehouse on Tanner's Row, all vessels under their flag, all accounts. Inventory by Thursday."

"Yes, sir," said Rennet, the eldest clerk, who had the gift of saying *yes, sir* in a way that meant nothing whatsoever.

Paloma said nothing at all. Duret noticed the silence the way you notice a missing stair.

"Oyelaran. You handled some of the Marchetti correspondence last season. The grain swaps."

"Some of it, sir."

"Then you're on the warehouse. Tanner's Row. You know where everything was filed." He hesitated, and then added, because he was not a cruel man and because something in the clerk's stillness pricked at him, "Take Rennet with you. Two sets of initials on every inventory page. Protocol."

"Of course, sir."

Paloma's voice was perfectly level. Duret had spent eleven years listening to clerks' voices for the tremors underneath them, and he heard none now—which either meant there were none to hear, or that he was getting old and deaf, and he did not care for either possibility.

"That's all. Except—" He stopped them at the door. "One more thing, and I'll say it once, to all of you, so nobody can claim they weren't told." He waited until he had three faces and one very composed profile. "As of six o'clock this morning, association with enemy names is a chargeable offense. Not a fine. A charge. Letters, debts, keepsakes, acquaintances—if it has a Marchetti name on it or in it, it is now contraband, and holding it is holding evidence against yourself." He let that sit. "I would advise everyone to look through their desks. Their lodgings. Their coats. Thoroughly."

Rennet went pale in the way of a man mentally reviewing a card game with a Marchetti cousin. The youngest clerk, Fossier, simply looked confused. And Paloma—

Paloma nodded. Just once, small, like someone accepting a diagnosis they'd already suspected.

"Thank you, sir," they said, and went to collect their coat.

Duret watched them go and thought, not for the first time, that there was something about that clerk he had never gotten to the bottom of. Nine years of indenture and no visitors, no lodgings he could name, no history that didn't start at the signing of the contract. It was, he had long ago decided, none of his business. This morning, for the first time, he wondered whether the state had begun to disagree.

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled the way it had always smelled—tallow and wet rope and the river underneath everything—but the Marchetti warehouse wore its new ownership badly. The seals on the doors were fresh, waxy red, official; the building itself seemed embarrassed by them, the way a gentlewoman looks embarrassed in borrowed uniform.

Paloma worked through the morning in a silence Rennet kept trying to fill.

"Bit grim, isn't it," Rennet offered, around noon, prying open a crate of brass fittings. "Old Marchetti used to stand me a drink at the regatta, you know. Every year. 'To healthy competition,' he'd say. Now I suppose I'll have to arrest him if I see him." He laughed, a thin laugh. "Not arrest him. You know what I mean."

"Mm," said Paloma.

They worked the letter room last—the small inner chamber, windowless, where firm correspondence was archived. Paloma had asked for it themselves, which Duret would learn later and find significant, though at the time Rennet only grumbled about the cold in there.

What Rennet saw: a clerk methodically listing boxes, dating them, initialing pages in a neat hand.

What Rennet did not see, because it happened in the four minutes Paloma was alone in the inner room while Rennet searched for a straightedge: a clerk kneeling before a particular shelf, third from the floor, where a loose panel behind the C-crate had opened onto a gap between walls—a gap where, for nearly a year during the tariff disputes, a certain correspondence had lived, wrapped in oilcloth, safe from both firms' prying auditors.

The gap was empty. Of course it was empty. Paloma had emptied it the previous evening, in the dark, with hands steadier than they had any right to be, and burned the contents one letter at a time over a washbasin, watching nine years of Ilse Marchetti's handwriting curl into ash that smelled, absurdly, of violets and tallow.

All but one. But that is not this chapter of events.

Paloma closed the panel, stood, and called out, "This box is water-damaged, Mr. Rennet. We should note it."

"Note it, note it," Rennet said cheerfully, returning with his straightedge. "You're a machine, Oyelaran. Don't you feel anything? These people fed half this quarter's dockhands."

Paloma considered the crate in front of them—the neat stenciled M, the wax seal, the whole apparatus of a life reduced to inventory.

"I feel it," they said quietly. "That's why I'm fast. Slow is where it gets in."

Rennet blinked at that, decided it was philosophy, and moved on.

* * *

Duret came to Tanner's Row at four o'clock, mostly to escape his own office, where the telegrams had begun arriving like hail. He found his clerks working by lamplight among crates, and something about the scene—the dust, the seals, Paloma's bent head—struck him with an old memory he hadn't consulted in years: his own father, a customs man, sealing a widow's shop during the border troubles of Duret's boyhood. The widow had thanked him. That was the part that had kept Duret awake even then. She had *thanked* him, because what else does a person have left, facing paperwork with a face on it?

He dismissed Rennet to the front office to finish the vessel manifests, and stood a while among the crates.

"You knew this warehouse well," he said at last. It wasn't quite a question.

"I filed here. During the tariff season."

"During the tariff season," Duret repeated. "When both firms were hiding things from each other's auditors." He picked up a wax seal fragment from a crate lid and turned it over. "You know what strikes me about this job, Oyelaran? Twenty-six years in trade, and I have never once seen a war make anything cheaper except people." He set the fragment down. "The board wants inventories. The government wants names. Somewhere in between, ordinary history gets confiscated."

Paloma looked up at him then—really looked, which they rarely did—and Duret had the uncomfortable sense of being weighed.

"Are you warning me, sir, or confessing?"

Duret laughed despite himself, a short bark that echoed oddly among the crates. "Both. Neither. Listen to me." He lowered his voice, not because anyone could hear—Rennet was forty feet away behind brick—but because some sentences are too heavy for a normal volume. "A man came by my office this morning. Census bureau liaison. They're cross-checking registrations against the enemy-property rolls. Names, parentage, places of birth. Everyone's papers are going to be looked at now, not just the Marchettis'. Everyone's." He watched the clerk's face. Nothing moved in it, and that stillness itself was an answer, of a kind. "I'm telling you because you're the best clerk I have and because I signed your indenture papers nine years ago and I'd rather this company didn't lose you to a filing error. Look through your own history, Oyelaran. Whatever's in it. Decide what the state needs to know before the state decides for you."

For a moment—one long breath—something surfaced behind Paloma's composure, quick as a fish turning in dark water. Grief, maybe. Or calculation. With that one, Duret suspected they had always been the same thing.

"Is that legal advice, sir?"

"It's grandfatherly advice, and I'm fifty-eight and tired. Legal advice costs more." He put on his hat. "Finish the letter room tomorrow. Leave tonight while the lamplighters are still out—you don't want to be walking past the patrol posts after eight with Marchetti dust on your sleeves."

He got as far as the door.

"Mr. Duret." Paloma's voice behind him was even, careful, and carried something underneath it that made him turn. "The letters that used to come through this warehouse. During the tariff season. If a person had... held correspondence from that time. For legitimate reasons. Would burning it now be suspicious? Or sensible?"

Duret considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, and with the seriousness it didn't.

"Burning is always sensible," he said. "Suspicion is only ever about what you failed to burn." He settled his hat. "Goodnight, Oyelaran. Go home. Whatever home is."

He walked back along the quay in the early dark, past the patrol post newly doubled in size, past the ferry landing where the lanterns burned sullen and low, and thought about the widow who had thanked his father, and about how the worst thing a bureaucracy ever did was not cruelty—it was making people grateful to be overlooked.

Behind him, in the warehouse on Tanner's Row, a lamp went out.

And in the ledger room of the Halvard Trading Company, come morning, the clerks would notice nothing amiss except that Oyelaran's desk had been cleaned to a shine—every drawer emptied, every paper squared—neat as a cell prepared for inspection, or a bag packed for a journey.

Which, though no one could yet say so, it was.
