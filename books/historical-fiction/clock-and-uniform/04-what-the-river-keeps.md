## 4. What the River Keeps

The fishmonger's stall smelled of brine and old money changing hands, which was to say it smelled like every honest business in Vessany. Paloma had been buying eels from Old Sefa for six years without once looking at her longer than courtesy required. That was the arrangement. You did not look too long at people in Vessany; you bought your fish, you praised the weather, and everyone's grandmother stayed where grandmothers belonged.

But on the fourth Tuesday of the war, Sefa wrapped Paloma's eels in two layers of paper instead of one, and said, "Extra for the cold nights. Keeps the flavor in."

Paloma felt the second layer with their thumb and knew before unwrapping it at home that it was not paper.

They waited until dark. They locked the ledger room door even though the house was empty, then unlocked it because locking it looked suspicious, then locked it again out of spite toward their own nerves. By candlelight they peeled back the oilskin and found a page torn from a hymnal—Ilse's family was nothing if not ironic—and across it, in a cipher they had invented at thirteen on the back of a regatta scorecard, a message no longer than a confession:

*STILL HERE. OCCUPIED SIDE. GRANDMOTHER'S HOUSE. THE PROMISE—DOES IT HOLD? —I.*

Paloma read it four times. Then they laughed, once, badly, the way a person laughs when a rope they've been gripping goes taut after months of slack.

*Does it hold.* As if Ilse had asked about a knot. As if it were a mooring line that might have rotted in nine years of weather.

They sat down on the floor with their back against the filing cabinet—the same cabinet, Paloma realized, that now held manifests for shell casings—and answered the question aloud to the empty room, in the voice they'd used only twice in their life: once at the regatta, once at the dock when the trains left.

"It holds," they said. "Of course it holds. What else do I own?"

* * *

Preparation, it turned out, looked almost exactly like ordinary life, which was either a mercy or an indictment of how ordinary Paloma's life had become.

In the mornings they filed manifests. In the afternoons they learned, with the patience of a woman defusing herself, the anatomy of a transit pass.

The Halvard Company issued its clerks real authority in one narrow strip of the world: goods moving under company seal between the docks and the garrison depots needed escort papers, and the escort papers were filled out by clerks. The blanks were familiar as prayer. *Name of bearer. Purpose of transit. Authorized crossing point. Duration.* What was not familiar was the registrar's stamp in the corner—a fat, ugly thing in violet ink, applied by Duret himself or by Ferro the assistant manager, who pressed too hard and always left a halo of excess ink around the rim, like a bruise around a wound.

Paloma had been copying ledgers for nine years. Nine years of reproducing other people's hands: Duret's cramped capitals, Ferro's looping descenders, the shipping master's drunken slant. It had begun as survival—your work is your handwriting when you're nobody—and it had become, without their noticing, the single most dangerous skill in Vessany.

They practiced the stamp first. A raw potato, a penknife, three ruined attempts. The fourth impression on scrap paper made them sit very still, heart hammering, because it looked *real*, and things that looked real were the beginning of every bad story the census office told.

"You're getting strange, Oyelaran," said Tobbik, the junior clerk, leaning over the partition wall the next day. "You've filed the Kessler manifests in date order. *Date* order. You're never efficient."

"I'm dying inside," Paloma said pleasantly. "It's improved me."

Tobbik laughed and went away, and Paloma pulled the Kessler manifests back out and misfiled them properly, and understood that they had just done something new: they had lied casually, competently, for cover. The hidden life was no longer hiding. It was building.

The ferryman took longer. There was only one man who ran the winter crossing at the western shallows—Raske, whose boat had a name so obscene that everyone called it *the Argument*—and Raske could not be bribed with money, because money was traceable. He could, however, be bribed with pearls.

Paloma had exactly three left from their mother's last season, sewn into the hem of a coat they had not worn since the indenture was signed. Taking them out at night, rolling them across their palm in candlelight, was like holding three small moons, three cold opinions. Their mother had said pearls kept the grief from settling. *Grief is sediment,* she used to say, spitting over the side of the boat. *You keep the water moving.*

"All right," Paloma whispered to the pearls, to the coat, to the woman dead eleven years. "I'm moving."

Raske turned one pearl in his yellow teeth-flecked fingers and named his price anyway, because he was a ferryman and therefore a philosopher of despair. "Two," he said. "One for the crossing, one for my silence after."

"And what does silence cost if someone asks you directly?"

"More than you have." But his eyes flicked to the river, gray and locked under ice, and something in his face suggested his price included a private debt of his own. "Midnight, Thursday week. Western shallows. And clerk—if the ice groans, don't stop to apologize to it."

* * *

Odile Brac found Paloma on the docks on Wednesday, which was wrong, because census takers worked from registers and registers lived in offices, and the docks were where the wind stripped all illusions of privacy off a person like flags off a mast.

"Clerk Oyelaran." She had a notebook, small and black, and held it the way a priest holds a book she no longer believes in. "A follow-up. Routine."

"Routine has been redefined lately," Paloma said. "I've read the new forms."

"Then you'll know why I'm here." She did not open the notebook. That was somehow worse than opening it. "Your mother's name appears in the harbor registry of 3908. A diving permit. Oyelaran, O.—no given name recorded, which is irregular. No death record filed in Vessany. No departure record either. Your file says 'orphaned, circumstances unrecorded.' Circumstances are my business now, I'm afraid. The war has made everything a circumstance."

The river breathed beneath its ice. Paloma thought of the forged pass hidden inside the binding of a hymnal, of all places, in their lodgings; thought of Raske's teeth; thought of Ilse asking *does it hold*.

"My mother dove the western stretch," Paloma said. "She held water rights under the old harbor law. She died in 3911. The company absorbed her permit when I signed my indenture. You can find all of it if you look."

The honesty came out of them like a swallowed hook coming back up—painful, and leaving them strangely lighter. Odile Brac stared. For a moment her professional stillness cracked and something younger looked through it, something tired.

"Why," she said quietly, "did you just tell me that? I gave you three chances to deflect. You've never deflected before. It was almost artistic."

Because I need the census to know my mother's name, Paloma did not say. Because a lie keeps you invisible and invisibility is a kind of drowning. Because there is a girl in an occupied house asking if a promise holds and I have decided, this week, that fear makes a poor compass.

"I'm tired of circumstances," Paloma said instead. "Will it go in your report?"

Odile looked at the river for a long moment. "Reports require certainty," she said at last, closing the notebook. "I'll write down what I'm certain of." She turned to go, then paused. "The harbor law of 3901, Clerk Oyelaran. Most people think it was repealed. It wasn't. It was *amended*. There's a difference, and the difference has a section number, and lawyers get paid to know it."

Then she walked away up the frozen quay, small and upright against the wind, and Paloma stood on the dock where their mother had once moored her boat, feeling the ground shift under the ice of their whole careful life—not cracking, not yet, but groaning, the way Raske had warned.

Thursday week. Midnight. Western shallows.

Between now and then stood a census report, a forged stamp, two pearls' worth of silence, and a lawyer they didn't yet have. Paloma turned up their collar and went, whistling, to find one.
