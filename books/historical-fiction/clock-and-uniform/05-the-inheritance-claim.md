## 5. The Inheritance Claim

The lawyer arrived on a Tuesday, which Paloma would later consider the only suspicious thing about him. Tuesdays at the Halvard Trading Company were ledger days, and ledger days made a person invisible in the most reliable way: everyone was too busy to look up.

He did not look up either, at first. He stood at the counter of the records room with his hat dripping snow onto the floorboards, reading the posted notice about wartime filing hours as though it contained scripture. When Paloma finally cleared their throat, he produced a card from inside his coat with the ceremony of a man producing a dove.

*Mevane & Kessel, Advocates. Riverine Law, Inheritance, Salvage.*

"Mr. Kessel," Paloma read aloud.

"Advocate," he corrected gently. "There's a difference, though I couldn't tell you what it is without billing you for the explanation." He smiled. He had the kind of face that made smiles seem like weather rather than intention — something that happened to him, not something he chose. "I'm looking for the clerk who handles the Oyelaran file."

Paloma's pen stopped mid-numeral.

"There isn't an Oyelaran file," they said, with nine years of practiced blandness.

"No," agreed Advocate Kessel. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The letter had come first, actually — three weeks earlier, folded into a bale of river-cloth from downriver, smelling of tar and dried fish. Aunt Bisi had never learned to write a letter that began anywhere other than the middle of her own thought, as if the reader had been standing beside her all along and merely stepped out for a moment.

> —and so I told the ferryman he could keep his opinion, because the last time anyone in this family paid for an opinion we got your mother married to a man who thought pearls grew on trees. They do not. I have checked.
>
> Now to business, which I know you dread coming from me, since my business has historically been scolding you for not eating enough. The old woman Tano died, peace on her, and among her papers was your mother's diving charter. Not the company copy. The family one, signed by the harbour-master the year you were born, with the seal still on it like a wax plum. Rights to the shallows from the Stone Pilgrim down to the Broken Jetty. Hereditary. Hers, and after her yours.
>
> Before you fold this letter into one of your tidy little squares and pretend you never received it, understand what I am telling you. Those rights are not a keepsake. They are a *legal instrument*. A person who holds property on this river is a person the census cannot simply erase. Your mother knew that. It's why she signed it. She said, "Bisi, if anything happens to me, the paper will say what I was even when nobody else will."
>
> Come see me before the ice breaks. Bring your appetite. This is not negotiable.
>
> — Bisi

Paloma had read the letter four times and then done what they always did with dangerous things: filed it. There was a hollow space behind the third drawer of the ledger cabinet where the wood had warped just wide enough for a folded letter, and Ilse's letters had lived there once, and now only this one did, sitting alone like a widow.

But the letter did not stay filed in Paloma's mind. It walked around during inventory. It sat beside them at supper. It asked questions in Aunt Bisi's voice: *What are you, if no paper says so?*

* * *

"I represent the estate interests of the late Sari Oyelaran," Kessel said, once they had retreated to the back of the records room among the smell of dust and cold glue. "Diving charter, registered under harbour authority, year of issue—" he consulted a card, unnecessarily; Paloma suspected he knew the number the way priests know psalms— "the year of your birth."

"My mother has been dead nine years."

"Yes. And dead persons' rights don't drown with them, whatever the Company might wish." He glanced around the records room with the frank appraisal of a man who spent his life among shelves and liked them. "The Halvard firm has been dredging your mother's shallows under a salvage license for six years. A salvage license, clerk, is what you take out when nobody can prove the water belongs to anyone. But somebody can. Somebody always can, eventually. It's why I eat."

Paloma kept their hands flat on the counter, which took effort. "Why come to me?"

Kessler looked at them then — really looked, the way Odile Brac had looked at them during the census, but warmer, and somehow more dangerous for it.

"Because the charter names an heir. Because the heir appears nowhere in the civic registry, which means the Company's lawyers will find the heir before I do unless I move quickly, and when they do, they'll discover a person with no standing, no registration, and no rights, and they will bury the claim under that person instead of under the paperwork." He paused. "I'd rather not watch them do it. I've seen it done. It takes eleven minutes and costs nothing."

The radiator in the corner knocked twice, like a fist on a door.

"You're saying I have no case."

"I'm saying you have no *self*, in the eyes of the law, and that is a different and more interesting problem. Fix the second and the first fixes itself." Kessel set a slim folder on the counter. Its cover was the grey of river ice. "A claim of inheritance requires the claimant to register truthfully with the census. Name, parentage, birthplace. All of it. In writing. Under seal."

Paloma stared at the folder the way one stares at a dog that may or may not be rabid.

"That's all?" Their voice came out thinner than intended. "Just — write it down?"

"Just write it down," Kessel agreed pleasantly. Then, because he was not a fool, he added, quieter: "It's never *just* anything in this city anymore, is it."

* * *

> You haven't written back. I know you haven't because the ferryman tells me everything, the gossip, and also because I raised half of you and know exactly how long you can sit on a decision like a hen on a stone egg.
>
> Your mother did not hide because she was ashamed. Say it out loud sometime, in private, where the walls belong to you. She hid because hiding let her work, and love her family, and dive her shallows, and be left in peace by men whose grandfathers had drowned women like her for luck before a launch. Hiding was her *tool*, child, not her face. Don't go mistaking the chisel for the hand.
>
> The pearl-boat days: you were four, maybe five, riding on the deck with your feet in a rope basket so you wouldn't roll overboard, and every time your mother came up gasping with her hands full of shell you clapped like the tide itself had performed for you. And she'd say, "Paloma, the river doesn't give gifts. It makes trades." Remember that? You were too small to remember, I expect, but I remember for both of us, that's what aunts are, we're the memory the children didn't know they needed.
>
> The river makes trades. Here is yours: a piece of paper that says who you are, in exchange for being who you are where everyone can see it.
>
> I know what I'm asking. I watched you sign that indenture at fourteen with your chin up and your name in small careful letters, trading yourself away to be safe, and I said nothing, because safe seemed like enough, back then, before things turned bad. Things have turned bad, Paloma. Safe is shrinking every day like ice in spring.
>
> Come downriver. Bring your appetite. I mean it about the appetite, you look like a filing cabinet that's been locked too long.

* * *

Paloma went home through streets where new notices bloomed on the pillars like white fungi: *REGISTRATION OF FOREIGN PERSONS. DECLARATION OF ALLEGIANCES.* The war was three weeks old and already the city had learned its new grammar, nouns sorted into *ours* and *theirs*, everything else suspect.

They climbed to their room, lit the lamp low, and laid the two documents side by side on the table: Kessel's grey folder, and — taken from behind the third drawer — Aunt Bisi's letter, and beneath it, where it had migrated against all sense, a scrap of paper on which Paloma had once copied out, and then could not bring themselves to burn, three lines of Ilse's handwriting. *If it ever turns bad, I'll get you across,* the promise ran, in Paloma's own hand, dated the day of the last regatta. Below it, in Ilse's slanted script: *I'm holding you to that, Oyelaran. In writing. Under seal.*

In writing. Under seal.

Paloma laughed, once, alone, and it startled them — a sound the room hadn't heard before.

Because here was the trap and here was the key and they were, of course, the same object, the way a door is. To claim the shallows was to write their mother's name, their own birthplace, their pearl-diver blood, into the census ledger where Odile Brac — meticulous Odile Brac, who had noticed the discrepancy in the first place and been sent away with blandness — would surely find it. Registration was a lighthouse: it showed you the rocks and it showed you to everyone else.

And yet. A person of standing could hold property. Could hold a *contract*. Could, possibly, apply for a transit pass in their own name instead of a forged one — could stand in front of a magistrate and say *this person across the river is owed a debt by me, legally, verifiably* instead of smuggling love across the ice like contraband. Could exist hard enough that deporting them meant tearing something official, something with a seal on it.

Hiding had been Mother's tool, not her face.

Paloma thought of the warehouse seized last month, Duret's careful warning — *association with enemy names is now a chargeable offense* — and thought: yes. Charged. Charged requires a charge sheet, and a charge sheet requires a name, and a name —

— a name was what they'd spent nine years not having.

They took out a fresh sheet of paper and began, in small careful letters, the way they had at fourteen, to draft the claim. Name. Parentage. Birthplace. Each word felt like lifting a stone off their chest and discovering it had been holding them down and anchoring them both at once.

They were halfway through the birthplace line — pen hovering over the word *riverborne* — when someone knocked. Three soft knocks, patient, unhurried.

Paloma opened the door on the landing and the lamplight fell on a familiar coat, a familiar clipboard, and Odile Brac, stamping snow off her boots, who said:

"Don't worry. I'm not here in any official capacity." A pause. Her breath clouded between them. "Although — I saw the flag on the registry this morning. Someone's filed a preliminary inheritance inquiry under the name Oyelaran. And I thought you should know that inquiries get assigned." She met Paloma's eyes. "This one has been assigned to me."
