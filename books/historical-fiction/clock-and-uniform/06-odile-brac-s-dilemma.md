## 6. Odile Brac's Dilemma

The trouble with a well-kept ledger, Odile Brac's mother used to say, is that it tells you everything except what you went looking for.

Odile had been thinking about that all week, because Paloma Oyelaran's file was a well-kept ledger. Too well kept. Every year for nine years the census forms came back immaculate: occupation, clerk; employer, Halvard Trading Company; residence, the company dormitory on Quay Street; status, indentured, term renewable. Nine years of tidy ink in a hand so consistent it could have been printed. And around that tidy ink, like water around a stone, nothing at all. No parentage listed beyond a first name — *Maren, diver, deceased*. No birthplace beyond *river districts*. No siblings. No religion, no society memberships, no debts, no loves, no letters. A person reduced to their usefulness and filed accordingly.

Most people's forms were messy. They misspelled streets, forgot birthdays, claimed ages ten years younger or fifteen older. Messiness was how Odile knew a form was honest. Oyelaran's was not messy. It was maintained.

And maintained, she had learned over eleven years of taking the census of Vessany, was a different word than true.

She sat now in the census office on Candlemakers' Row with the files spread out before her and the radiator clanking like a small angry engine, and she did what she always did when a form troubled her: she made a list.

*Item one.* The Halvard Company's indenture register, which she had requested under her statutory authority two weeks ago and received only after a delay long enough to be its own kind of answer. The register recorded the signing of one Paloma Oyelaran, age fourteen, ward of no one, guarantor — the company itself. Parentage: *mother deceased, father unrecorded.* But in the margin of the original contract, in faded pencil, someone had written a word Odile had not been able to stop seeing since: *Ondarri.*

It wasn't a Vessany word. It was a boat-word, a river-people's word, from the diving families who worked the shallows before the trade companies bought up the water rights and called the divers *unregistered labor* until the divers stopped existing at all. *Ondarri* meant, as best Odile could reconstruct it from a dialect dictionary three shelves out of reach of the radiator, *one who belongs to the water's edge.* It also meant, in the eyes of the Bureau of Population and Allegiance, *stateless.*

*Item two.* The regatta records. This had taken longer, because the regattas were civilian and cheerful and nobody had thought to index them for war. But there it was in the spring program of nine years past: the last peacetime regatta, before the border closed. A doubles rowing event, and among the entrants, two names side by side in the junior division. *I. Marchetti* and *P. Oyelaran.* Rowing together. In matching sashes, if the engraving in the festival supplement could be believed, and Odile had looked at the engraving rather longer than an investigator strictly needed to.

Marchetti. Enemy property. Enemy name. Enemy daughter, presumably, stranded across the frozen river with the rest of the Marchetti family when the borders slammed shut in October.

*Item three.* The fishmonger. This was thinner, and Odile disliked how much weight she found herself putting on something thin. But twice in November she had watched, from across the market square in the course of perfectly legitimate door-to-door verification work, a fishmonger named Ansel Ferric pause at the service entrance of the Halvard counting house and pass something small to a clerk with dark watchful eyes. Not fish. Fish smell, yes — everything in Vessany had fish smell in winter — but the object passed had been paper-shaped.

And item four, the one that sat on her desk like a stone on a hymnal: the regulation itself, black-bordered since the declaration of war. *Knowingly failing to report an unregistered person of foreign or indeterminate allegiance is an offense equal to harboring.* She had read it forty times. She could recite it in her sleep. Some nights she did.

Reporting was simple. She filled out Form 11-C, she described the anomaly, she surrendered her notes, and by week's end men with better coats and worse manners would come to the counting house on Quay Street and take Paloma Oyelaran away to be sorted into one of the state's neat categories: citizen, enemy, alien, contraband. Her duty would be discharged. Her record would show diligence. And somewhere across the river, a girl who had once rowed in a matching sash would never know why the letters stopped.

Odile took off her glasses, polished them on her scarf, put them back on, and looked at the list again, as though a fifth option might have grown there overnight.

It hadn't.

* * *

Her brother Tomas had been invisible too, in his way. Nobody ever said so — the state saw Tomas perfectly well, saw him so clearly it drafted him within four days of the mobilization order — but Odile knew the difference between being seen and being known. Tomas had wanted to be a church bell-caster. He had drawings. He had that look boys got in Vessany in autumn, half terror and half relief, like men boarding a ferry they'd already paid for. He wrote her one letter from the training depot, full of jokes about the food, and then the letters stopped, and then a telegram arrived in the careful gray language governments use to avoid saying anything true, and her mother began setting a fourth place at the table anyway, and neither of them ever mentioned it, and that silence — that maintained, tidy, well-kept silence — had gone on now for six years.

So Odile understood something about hiding things so thoroughly you forgot where you'd put them.

The question that kept her awake was not *is Oyelaran hiding something* — that was settled; she had enough for Form 11-C and both of them knew it. The question was the one she'd caught herself asking at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling while the radiator knocked: *what does a person have to be carrying, to carry it this carefully for nine years?*

She knew what the state would say. The state would say it didn't matter what a person carried. Only what category they fit.

Odile had spent six years discovering she disagreed with the state about almost everything worth disagreeing about, and had done precisely nothing about it, because disagreement, unregistered, weighed nothing at all.

Well. Perhaps it was time to find out what it weighed.

* * *

She chose the counting house's public hours, mid-afternoon, when the clerks' pens rattled like rain on a tin roof and no one looks twice at a woman with an official satchel. She asked at the desk for the manifests clerk, and a boy with ink to his elbows pointed her down a corridor smelling of lamp oil and cold stone, and there, at a slanted desk beneath a high salt-fogged window, sat Paloma Oyelaran — smaller than Odile expected, neater, with those dark eyes that went still the moment they landed on the satchel.

"Master Oyelaran. Odile Brac, census." She kept her voice level, unhurried. "Not an audit. May I borrow five minutes?"

Something crossed the clerk's face — not fear exactly, but the shadow fear casts when it's trying to be polite. "There's a records room upstairs."

"Not upstairs," Odile said quietly. "Here is fine. Or the quay, if you'd rather. I find people talk better near water."

They ended up on the quay, because of course they did. Oyelaran pulled their coat close and walked beside her to the rail where the river moved under its skin of ice, dark and patient, and the gulls screamed their winter grievances overhead, and Odile understood immediately why the clerk had chosen this: here, the wind tore words apart before they could travel.

"You've been thorough," Oyelaran said at last, watching the water. "I wondered when you'd come back."

"I have a question first, and then I'll tell you what I know, and you can decide how much of my knowing matters." Odile gripped the rail. The iron bit cold through her gloves. "Why? You've kept yourself safe for nine years. Perfectly safe. Invisible as smoke. One letter — one piece of paper with the wrong name on it — and all of it burns. Why risk it?"

The silence stretched long enough that a barge went by, its stack trailing smoke, its deckhands stamping and not looking at them.

"You already know why," Oyelaran said finally. "You wouldn't ask otherwise. You'd have filed your form."

Odile exhaled, and it came out as fog, and she watched it shred in the wind. "My brother conscripted himself to death because the state saw him clearly and knew him not at all," she said. "I take the census. I make people into categories. It's honest work, mostly, and I'm good at it, and every year I'm a little better at not thinking about what it is. And then there's you. Nine years of a life folded up so small it fits in a margin. And I sit at my desk with a regulation that says turning you in is the same as doing right, and I find—" Her voice caught; she smoothed it flat. "I find I can't remember what the categories are for."

"They're for making it simple," Oyelaran said softly. "Simple isn't the same as safe. I learned that young. My mother said the river doesn't care what your papers say; it only cares whether you can swim." A pause. The wind dropped, briefly, and in the hush the ice ticked and groaned below them like old floorboards. "You're telling me you haven't reported me."

"I'm telling you I don't know yet what I am," Odile said. "That's the truth of it. I came to ask my question, and you answered it, so now I'll tell you what I know: I know about a regatta. I know about a name that isn't yours to say out loud anymore. I know about a fishmonger. And I know the ice will break in six weeks, maybe five, and after it breaks, the patrols double, because everyone knows the river is the only door left open in this whole country."

Oyelaran had gone very still — the stillness of a diver, Odile realized, of someone measuring depth.

"So here is my dilemma, Master Oyelaran, laid out plainly, because you strike me as someone who respects plainness." She turned to face them fully. "If I write my report tomorrow, I am a good servant and you are gone. If I sit on it, I risk my position, my pension, possibly worse — for a stranger." She hesitated. "For a person I met in a doorway with a satchel, who has lied to me beautifully for two years running. That is the arithmetic. And I find the numbers won't stay in their columns."

"And if I told you," Oyelaran said, very quietly, "that whatever you decide, I'd understand it — would that change anything?"

"No," Odile said. "Because I didn't come for permission. I came because I wanted to see the face of someone who chose danger over safety, and find out if they regretted it."

"And do I look like I regret it?"

Odile studied the thin, wind-burned face, the eyes that held hers without flinching, and thought of Tomas's drawings of bells, and of her mother's fourth place setting, and of every morning she had pinned on the census sash and become a woman made entirely of columns.

"You look," she said slowly, "like someone who finally gets to breathe."

Something moved behind the clerk's expression — gratitude, maybe, or grief, or both wearing the same coat. Then Oyelaran glanced past her shoulder, toward the harbor mouth, and Odile saw the stillness return, deeper this time, the diver's stillness.

"Then breathe quickly," Paloma said. "Because I signed papers yesterday that make me very easy to find — and there's a gala tonight at the Halvard house, and Duret announces new security measures at midnight, and after midnight, Mrs. Brac—"

The gulls scattered off the pilings all at once, screaming, as somewhere downriver a patrol launch sounded its horn.

"—after midnight, none of us are invisible anymore."
