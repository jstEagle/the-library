## 7. Ice Thinning

The lawyer's office smelled of ink and old radiators, and Paloma sat across from him with the inheritance papers squared on the desk between them like a hand of cards neither of them wanted to play first.

"Take your time," said Mr. Anselm. He was a small man with a large mustache and the patient air of someone who had waited out better hesitations than this one.

Paloma took their time. They read every line twice, because that was how they had been trained—nine years in the Halvard ledger room taught you that the dangerous clause was never the loud one. It was always the quiet one, tucked mid-page, wearing plain words.

There it was, halfway down the second sheet: *The claimant hereby attests, under registration law, to full and truthful declaration of parentage, birthplace, and allegiance.*

Truthful declaration. The whole thing balanced on it. The diving rights were real—the river archives confirmed what Aunt Bisi's letters had insisted for months, that Mama had held hereditary rights to the shallows at Kettle Reach, rights that passed by blood and could not lapse while an heir lived and claimed them. But a claim required a claimant, and a claimant required a name entered honestly into the census rolls of Vessany. No more Oyelaran, clerk, b. unspecified, guardian: Halvard Trading Company. No more nine years of practiced blandness. Just: Paloma, child of Sefu Oyelaran of the diving boats and Adaeze his wife, born on the river, allegiance their own.

"You understand," Anselm said gently, "that once you sign, you are visible. To the census office, to the conscription board, to anyone who cares to look. A person of standing can hold property. A person of standing can also be *found*."

"I understand," Paloma said.

And they did, which was the strange part. For nine years invisibility had been the whole architecture of their life—a life built like the ledger room itself, everything filed, nothing displayed, every exit mapped. It had kept them safe the way a locked strongbox keeps a letter safe: intact, preserved, and never read by anyone, including yourself.

But Ilse was on the other side of the river, and the ice was talking.

It had started three nights ago—that low, groaning language the river speaks when the cold deepens and the ice begins to think about letting go. Old boatmen called it the river clearing its throat. Paloma had grown up hearing it from the deck of their mother's boat, and had always understood it as a countdown. When the ice began to speak, spring was coming, and spring meant patrols on open water, checkpoints on both banks, a border that would harden from a line on a map into a wall of gunboats.

If Ilse was going to cross, it had to be now, in the thinning dark, on the thickening ice—or not at all.

* * *

That evening Paloma spread the map of the river on the floor of their rented room, weighting the corners with a teapot, a boot, a tin of biscuits, and the complete works of a poet they had never much liked, and considered the water as their mother would have wanted them to consider it.

Not as a border. As a living thing with habits.

The ferry bridges were hopeless; papers there, lanterns, a sergeant who looked at faces the way Odile Brac looked at ledgers. The commercial crossing at Stone Quay was worse since the gala announcement—Duret's new security measures had gone up like scaffolding, all stamped manifests and doubled watchmen. Which left the north reach, and within it, Kettle Reach: the old diving shallows where the river ran shallow over gravel bars and the ice grew treacherously thin even in the cruelest February.

Everyone knew the shallows were dangerous. That was why no one patrolled them. A patrolman assigned to Kettle Reach was a patrolman standing on ice he didn't trust, in the coldest hour of the night, for a post nobody expected anything to happen at. Paloma counted on exactly that arithmetic. Fear makes men punctual about avoiding places.

Mama had dived those shallows for pearls until her hands went blue at the knuckles, and she had taught Paloma the first law of thin ice before she'd taught them letters. *The ice tells you before it breaks,* she'd say, kneeling on the gray surface with her ear cocked like a doctor at a chest. *A click is conversation. A crack is an argument. A long tearing sound is the end of the discussion.* She had died ashore, of all undignified things, a fever in a boarding house, but Paloma had inherited her ears.

So: Kettle Reach, the night of the winter gala.

The logic was almost funny. Duret's own party, the Halvard winter gala, the one night of the year when every officer, watchman, and important clerk would be indoors drinking the company's excellent sherry and congratulating themselves on their patriotism. The security measures announced for midnight would not even be fully staffed until the following dawn. And Paloma would be there—at least at first—in company livery, pouring wine, invisible in the most conspicuous way possible: as furniture. Then, somewhere past ten, a quiet slip through the service corridor, a change of clothes, and the long walk north along the towpath where the lamplight gave out.

The plan had one flaw, and the flaw wore Paloma's own face. If anything went wrong—if they were stopped, questioned, caught anywhere near that river—then the person the authorities found would need to be somebody with a reason, somebody real, somebody whose presence on the north road had an innocent explanation. A ghost could not walk a towpath. Ghosts get arrested.

Which was why, the next morning, Paloma walked back to Mr. Anselm's office and signed.

* * *

The pen scratched. That was all. Nine years of hiding, and the whole edifice came down to the sound of a nib on rag paper, a small dry scratch like a mouse in a wall.

"There," Anselm said, sanding the signature with the tenderness of a man closing a coffin or a cradle—he seemed unsure himself which. "You are now, in the eyes of Vessany, a person of standing. Holder of the Kettle Reach diving rights. Congratulations are perhaps insufficient."

"They'll do," Paloma said.

They filed the claim at the registry house before noon, because if the exposure was going to happen it should happen early, with days of legitimate paper-trail between the signature and the gala. Let the clerks stamp things. Let the notice go up on the public board, where anyone might read it—including a meticulous census taker with a memory like a filing cabinet. Especially her. Paloma had thought about Odile Brac a great deal that week, more than was comfortable. Signing truthfully was, among other things, a message: *You cannot unmask me. I have already come out of the box, and closed the lid behind me.*

What the notice could not explain—and what no form in Vessany had a field for—was why a person would do such a thing. Why anyone would spend nine years building a wall of silence and then, in a single winter, take a hammer to it on purpose.

Because the wall had become the cell, Paloma supposed. Because Ilse Marchetti was on the far side of a frozen river holding a promise made at a regatta a decade ago, when they were both young enough to think promises were free. *If it ever turns bad, I'll get you across.* Well. It had turned bad. Badness was now official policy, published in the gazette, enforced by watchmen.

Paloma bought rope, a coil of waxed cord, two flares of the kind fishermen used, and a flask of brandy from an apothecary who asked no questions because apothecaries in Vessany had learned the fiscal wisdom of asking none. They bribed the ferryman Gaspard with more money than the errand strictly required—not for the crossing, which Gaspard would not attempt, but for his silence and, more valuably, for his knowledge of the patrol rotations on the north bank, which he recited with the relish of a man gossiping about neighbors.

"And the ice at the Reach?" Paloma asked.

Gaspard spat thoughtfully over the side of his moored boat. "Singing every night. Cracked through at the near bar Tuesday—I heard it from here. You're not thinking of walking it?"

"I'm thinking of a great many things," Paloma said. "Mostly of my mother."

Gaspard looked at them a moment longer, and something in his weathered face shifted—an old man recognizing an old woman's child. "Then you know what you're doing," he said, which was either a blessing or an accusation, and took the money.

* * *

Two evenings before the gala, there came a knock at the door of the rented room, three knocks, measured, official in rhythm, and Paloma's heart performed a small cold maneuver before they opened it to find Odile Brac on the landing, hatless, carrying no ledger—only herself, and the wind off the river in her coat.

"I saw the registry notice," she said. "Congratulations on your sudden existence."

"It happened gradually," Paloma said. "The paperwork happened suddenly."

She did not smile. She looked past them into the small room, at the map still rolled on the table, at the coil of waxed cord, and Paloma watched her catalog each item without touching any of them, the way she had once catalogued discrepancies in a registration book.

"I'm off duty," she said at last. "I want you to know that whatever I see or don't see this week, I have decided—" she paused, choosing the words with visible care, "—that the census is a blunt instrument, and I am tired of blunt instruments. My brother was one. The army used him accordingly." She pulled her collar up against the wind. "I only came to say: whatever you're doing, do it cleanly. The ice at Kettle Reach sang all last winter too, the year I was posted north. It lies about its thickness near the gravel bars. Trust your ears, not your eyes."

And she was gone down the stairs before Paloma could find a single safe word, leaving the room colder and the future somehow warmer.

* * *

The last night, Paloma packed. Rope, cord, flares, brandy, dry clothes wrapped in oilcloth for Ilse, a photograph of the two of them at the regatta that had survived every burning and nearly hadn't survived the burning. They ate the biscuits off the map. They rolled the map away.

Then they sat by the window a long while, listening to the river.

It was singing tonight, low and continuous, a sound like a giant turning in its sleep. Somewhere under that gray shell, four hundred meters of water moved toward the sea, indifferent to borders, to firms, to censuses, to promises. Tomorrow night Paloma would stand on it and ask it to hold—for one hour, for one crossing, for one promise made by a girl who no longer existed to a girl who very much still did.

The cost was counted. Visibility, standing, a name in the rolls, a trial's worth of exposure waiting like a bill in the mail. Paloma accepted all of it the way you accept a fare: paid gladly, to go where you were going.

They blew out the lamp and lay down in their clothes, and slept better than they had in nine years, which frightened them a little, and did not stop them.

In the morning, the gala invitation sat on the table where it had been all week, gold-lettered, absurd: *The Halvard Trading Company requests the pleasure…*

Paloma dressed in livery, checked the oilcloth bundle one final time, and went out into the white morning to pour wine for the empire, until ten o'clock.
