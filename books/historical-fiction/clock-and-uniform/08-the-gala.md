## 8. The Gala

The Halvard Trading Company's winter gala was held, as always, in the counting-house's great hall, where the ledgers were usually kept under lock and the chandeliers only came down from the attic once a year to be dusted and hung like captured weather. Paloma had attended for nine consecutive winters, first as the child who fetched coats, then as the junior clerk who refilled glasses, and now as the senior ledger-keeper who was expected to stand near the punch bowl looking respectable and available for questions about freight tonnage.

They had dressed carefully tonight, which meant dressing forgettably. A grey coat. A collar that buttoned to the throat. The kind of person a room agrees not to see.

The plan had been simple, and Paloma distrusted how simple it had been. Duret would be occupied until at least two in the morning — he gave the same toast every year, three times, because half the guests arrived late — and patrols along the river doubled their intervals on gala nights, the constables preferring their own firesides to the ice. Midnight. Ilse would come down to the shallows at midnight. Paloma would leave here by eleven-thirty with the forged pass sewn into their glove lining and be waiting at the old diving bank by one.

Nine years of hiding, and it would all be over by dawn. One way or another.

"Paloma." Duret appeared at their elbow holding two glasses of the good sherry, which meant he wanted something. "You look like a man about to give evidence."

"I look like someone standing next to a punch bowl," Paloma said.

"That too." He handed over a glass anyway. His face was flushed with warmth and brandy but his eyes were doing their usual quiet arithmetic, taking inventory of the room. Of Paloma. "You've been distracted this week. Ledgers don't lie, whatever people think they do. Your columns have been perfect, which is itself suspicious. Perfect columns mean a mind somewhere else."

"My aunt has been unwell," Paloma said, and hated how smoothly it came. Bisi was, as far as Paloma knew, in excellent health and currently winning an argument with a lawyer by post.

"Ah." Duret nodded, satisfied, and Paloma felt the small shame of it settle somewhere behind the ribs. Nine years of small shames. They stacked up inside a person like unpaid invoices.

Across the hall, the string quartet sawed through a waltz nobody danced to. Company men stood in clusters talking about freight rates with the desperate cheerfulness of people who had read the morning's dispatches. Someone had already broken a glass. The chandelier light caught the pearl pin at the collar of old Mistress Anselm — river pearls, Vessany pearls, probably from the very beds Paloma's mother had once dived, and Paloma looked away before the looking could become a thing visible on their face.

Duret raised his voice to the room. "Friends! Before the toasts — a brief word on business."

* * *

Duret had not wanted to give this speech.

He had drafted it four times, and each draft had sounded less like him and more like the memos coming out of the capital lately, all of which seemed to have been written by men who had never met a single person whose life they were rearranging. But the orders were the orders, and the gala — every principal employee gathered in one warm room — was the efficient place to deliver them.

"Friends," he said, and the quartet mercifully stopped. "I'll keep this short, since young Ferro there looks ready to drink the fountain dry." Polite laughter. Good. Start soft. "As of tomorrow, the company operates under new security directives. You'll see the notices posted Monday. In summary: all manifests touching enemy territory now require countersignature by myself or Mistress Anselm. Dock access after dark is restricted to badge-holders. And—" he paused, because this was the part he'd argued about by wire, and lost— "as of midnight tonight, movement across the river without a stamped transit pass is a chargeable offense. Not a fine. A charge."

The word moved through the room like cold air through a cracked window. He watched faces absorb it: the young ones confused, the older ones calculating, everyone quietly thinking of someone on the other side of that river, because in a port city everybody was from somewhere else two generations back.

Midnight, he said again, for clarity. The new passes could be obtained from the harbor office beginning Monday morning.

He found Paloma Oyelaran's face in the crowd while he spoke. He hadn't meant to look for it; the face simply drew his eye the way it had drawn his attention for months now, ever since the census business, ever since Odile Brac's careful questions had set a small alarm ringing in him that he couldn't locate or silence. Paloma stood perfectly still with the untouched sherry in hand, and their stillness was wrong. People reacted to bad news with flinches, with murmurs, with a glance at a neighbor. Paloma went still the way animals go still.

He told himself it proved nothing. He told himself a great many things lately.

"—to the company," he finished, raising his glass, "and to better seasons ahead."

"To better seasons," the room echoed, and the quartet started up again, and the machinery of the evening resumed as though nothing had shifted underneath it.

Everything had shifted underneath it. Duret knew it the moment he saw Paloma set down the full glass and begin, with terrible patience, to work the room toward the door.

* * *

Paloma counted minutes now instead of money.

Midnight. Not two o'clock. The announcement sat in their chest like swallowed ice. Every exit plan had been built around patrol rotations that doubled their gaps after midnight; the harbor office wouldn't issue a single legitimate pass before Monday, which meant the forgery in their glove had just gone from backup plan to only plan, and the forgery was good but it wasn't Monday-good. It was gamble-good. It was everything-depends-on-a-tired-constable-good.

Eleven-ten. They needed the second item — the packet of roubles and the letter of passage in Bisi's name that lent the forgery its spine — from the coat room, and they needed to be gone by quarter past.

The coat room was a converted office off the main hall, hung with furs, lit by one lamp, tended by a boy named Piet who had been paid two days' wages to develop an urgent and conveniently timed headache. Paloma slipped in, retrieved their own coat, and knelt to work loose the floorboard beneath the third hook from the door — a hiding place dating from the letter years, chosen precisely because no one hid things in coat rooms, everyone knowing coat rooms were where people hid things.

The board came up. The oilcloth packet was there. Paloma's fingers closed on it—

"Don't let me interrupt the cleaning."

Duret stood in the doorway with his coat over his arm, watching.

There is a particular quality to the silence that follows being caught kneeling on the floor of a coat room at eleven-fifteen at night with contraband in your fist and war in the air. Paloma lived an entire biography in it: the arrest, the trial, the sentence, the promise dying on the river unkept, Ilse waiting at the shallows forever like a figure in a story mothers tell.

Then they stood up, and heard themselves say, lightly: "A button. Off my coat. I thought I heard it drop."

Duret's eyes went to the raised floorboard. To the packet. Back to Paloma's face.

"A button," he repeated.

"Mother-of-pearl." Paloma turned their cuff into the light, and there — God forgive them, and God thank the pearl beds — was the gap where a button should be, because Paloma had unsnapped it in the washroom an hour earlier on a possibility so faint they hadn't let themselves name it. "I sew my own buttons. My mother insisted. She said a clerk who can't hold a needle will end up paying a tailor's prices for opinions."

It was a joke. It was a mother. It was exactly the sort of small true thing that lies are built from, and Paloma delivered it while their heart performed feats of engineering.

Duret didn't smile. He came into the room slowly, laid his own coat over a chair, and looked at the open floor gap a moment longer.

"You know," he said, conversationally, "in thirty years I have never once known a floorboard to eat a button. And yet here we are." He straightened his cuffs. "Paloma. I'm going to ask you something once, and I want you to understand that I am asking it as the man who signed your indenture papers when you were sixteen and couldn't stop crying, though you denied it ever after."

"I wasn't crying."

"You were absolutely crying." He almost smiled. Then it went away, and what remained was tired and honest. "Is there something in that packet that will cost me my position if I pretend I didn't see it?"

The question hung there. Paloma understood, distantly, that this was mercy wearing a practical coat — that Duret was offering them a last exit, a chance to put the packet back, to go upstairs and drink sherry until two and keep being invisible. All they had to do was lie completely. Not a button-lie. A whole lie, face to face, to the closest thing to a friend the company had ever given them.

"No," Paloma said. "There's nothing in that packet that concerns the Halvard Trading Company."

It was even true. That was the filthiest part. The packet concerned a promise, a ferryman, a girl on the far bank — none of it company business. None of it anyone's business but the river's.

Duret studied them for a long moment. Whatever he was reading in Paloma's face, he read it to the bottom.

"The floorboard," he said finally, turning away to collect his coat, "is a fire hazard. Have maintenance look at it Monday." He shrugged into the fur collar. "Enjoy the rest of the gala, Sandoval. The sherry really is very good this year."

He left. Paloma did not breathe until his footsteps reached the hall. Then they pressed the board down, pulled on their coat, and stood a moment with their hand against the door, feeling the shape of what had just happened — the loyalty stretched between them and Duret like wet rope, fraying audibly, both of them pretending not to hear it snap.

* * *

Outside, the cold hit like a hand.

Snow was falling in fat, indifferent flakes. Down the hill, the river lay white and silent under the lamps, and beyond it, somewhere past the far shore, Ilse Marchetti was presumably looking at the same sky, checking the same clock, trusting a promise made at a regatta nine years and one lifetime ago.

Midnight, the new law said. Paloma checked their watch: 11:22. The window had shrunk, but it existed. It was theirs. They turned up their collar, sewed shut in every seam, and walked fast toward the water, leaving the light and music of the gala behind them like a country they were emigrating from.

At the corner by the fish market, a lantern swung.

A patrol, early. Moving toward the embankment.

Paloma stepped into the shadow of the awning, and began to run the numbers all over again.
