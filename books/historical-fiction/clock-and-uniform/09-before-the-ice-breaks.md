## 9. Before the Ice Breaks

The river at night was not the river I knew.

I had known it as a child knows a mother's back—warm, brown, humming with fish and rope and my mother's voice counting breaths beneath the surface. Tonight it lay under a skin of grey-white ice, moonless, exhaling cold like something sleeping too deeply to be woken gently. The old diving shallows, where Mama had taught me that water is only dangerous to people who fight it, were frozen over for the first winter in my memory. Nine years in Vessany had taught me many kinds of fear, but none of them sounded like ice.

*If it ever turns bad, I'll get you across.* I had said it on a bright afternoon four years ago, both of us sticky with regatta wine, laughing, because it was unthinkable that anything would turn bad. Ilse had laughed too, then gone quiet in that way of hers—tucking the sentence away somewhere behind her ribs like a coin she didn't want to drop.

Tonight I was going to find out whether she still carried it.

* * *

I left the gala through the kitchens, coat collar up, the forged transit pass folded against my heart where it had lived so long I'd begun to think of it as a second heartbeat. The inheritance papers were signed—that was the strange arithmetic of the whole plan: to do one illegal thing safely, I had first done a legal thing that terrified me more. My name, my mother's name, her rights to a stretch of river I could not currently walk across without committing treason. Registered. Visible. A person the state could now see clearly enough to arrest.

Mama would have found this funny. She spent her whole life being seen exactly as she wished—as a diver, a boat, a pair of lungs—and considered paperwork a superstition of the shore-dwellers.

The fishmonger's boy met me two streets from the water and said nothing, which is the most expensive service in Vessany during wartime, and I paid him anyway. Then the lanes ran out and there was only the embankment, the frost-slicked steps down to the ice, and the far bank—a smudge darker than the sky, where a single shuttered lantern swung once, twice, three times.

She came out of the dark the way she used to come out of her father's warehouse: fast, certain, already apologizing with her shoulders for the danger she was causing.

"You're late," Ilse whispered, when we stood close enough to share breath.

"I was at a party," I whispered back. "There were canapés."

"There are *always* canapés with you." Her laugh cracked in the middle, and I understood suddenly how frightened she'd been—not tonight, but all these months, alone on the wrong side of a border drawn by men who had never once been cold. Her hand found mine through two layers of glove. "The promise holds?"

"The promise holds. Old shallows, ten minutes' walk. The ice is thin there but the patrols don't bother—the charts say it's deep water. Nobody expects anyone to cross where you'd have to swim."

"Can you swim? In this?"

"My mother," I said, "was a pearl diver. I have been underwater since before I could walk. It's the walking I've never trusted."

* * *

The shallows were a pale scar in the river's skin, ice grown patchy over sandbars Mama had named like relatives—this one Auntie Sandbar, that one the Old Fool, because it shifted every spring just to spite her. We went out onto it slowly, boots testing each step, our breath rising around us like small ghosts who had opinions about our choices.

Ilse talked to keep the fear moving. "When we're across, I'll write. Not letters—" she caught herself; letters had gotten people burned— "just. Words. Somehow. You'll know they're from me."

"I'll know."

"And you'll come too. After. When it's safe."

I did not answer, because the honest answer was *perhaps*, and because at that moment the Old Fool chose to speak. The ice under Ilse's next step made a sound I had heard once before in a collapsing warehouse—a long, singing groan, the sound of a structure announcing its resignation.

We froze. Literally; the cold had been working toward it all night.

"It's fine," Ilse said, which is what everyone says.

Then the lantern came.

It bloomed at the near bank—white, swinging, deliberate—and behind it, voices. Two men, maybe three, boots ringing on the frost-stiff planks of the embankment stairs. Not the lazy patrol I'd bribed past in my planning. Someone had moved the schedule. Duret's midnight security measures, arriving early, or simple bad luck, which has always been Vessany's most reliable export.

"Run," I breathed.

Running on ice is a lie the body tells itself. We went fast and low toward the far bank, and the river began to talk underneath us—cracks traveling outward like rumors, sharp reports that I felt in my teeth. Ten meters from the far shore, where the shallows deepen toward the channel, the ice simply declined to continue the discussion.

Ilse went down without a cry. That was the worst of it—no scream, just a gasp swallowed by black water, and her hands slapping white fragments, finding nothing.

I did not think. Thinking is for shore-dwellers. My mother's voice rose in me the way it had risen a thousand times off the diving ladder of our boat: *Don't fight the water. Greet it. Let it hold you until you're ready to hold it.*

I dove.

The cold was not temperature anymore; it was a fact, total and immediate, a fist closing on my heart. Down in that blackness I heard her—felt her thrashing, fighting, drowning the way landlocked people drown, vertical and furious. I caught her wrist and pulled her toward me instead of up, wrapped myself around her back the way Mama had shown me with drowning nets, and held her still until her panic ran one beat ahead of her air and she went limp enough to steer.

*Greet it,* said the voice in my blood. *Now lift.*

We broke the surface into air like knives. Ilse coughed water and prayer in equal measure. The hole we'd made was already shrinking, edges groaning inward, and the patrol lantern was closer—I could hear individual footsteps now, and one voice shouting something official about stopping.

"Hold the ice edge," I gasped, hauling us along it toward the far bank, breaking weak ice ahead of us with my elbow, one arm-length at a time. My fingers had stopped reporting pain, which meant they had stopped reporting. "Ilse. When I throw you, crawl. Don't stand until it's thick. Crawl like a scandal spreads."

"That's not—" she coughed, "—how scandals—"

"*Crawl.*"

I got a foot on the sandbar below—the Old Fool, faithful even now, holding me an arm's length higher than the river intended—and put everything my mother had given me into one push. Ilse went up over the lip of the bank like a landed fish, sprawling onto solid snow, and turned immediately, hair freezing in ropes across her face, to reach for me.

Behind me, on the near bank, the lantern light swept the ice and found us. Found *me*. Boots thundered onto the frost, and a voice—Duret's voice, God help me, he must have come down to check the patrol himself—called my name. Not "halt." Not "in the name of the law." My name, spoken the way he said it in the ledger room, and somehow that was worse.

I looked at Ilse's hand, reaching. At the far bank, black and free and only an arm's length from her fingers.

I thought of nine years of practiced blandness. Of burned letters. Of being nobody, from nowhere, owing nothing—safe as a locked empty room. And I thought of Mama, whose whole philosophy of water was that the river cannot drown what refuses to hide from it.

I took Ilse's hand. Held it one full second, warm against my ruined cold ones, long enough for the lantern to see, long enough for history to take notes.

"Go," I said. "Live loudly. It turns out I'm terrible at living quietly."

"Don't you dare—" she started, and then the boots were close, and she pressed my knuckles once, hard, against her mouth, and was gone into the dark, crawling then running, alive.

I turned around.

Duret stood at the edge of the shallows with the lantern raised, its light falling on me dripping and half-frozen and—for the first time in my adult life—completely legible. Behind him, two constables panted steam like workhorses. He looked at me for a long moment, this man who had ground personal histories into contraband with genuine regret, and shook his head slowly.

"Paloma Oyelaran," he said, almost gently. "Of the Halvard Trading Company. And formerly of nowhere at all."

"That last part," I said, teeth chattering, "is hereby amended."

The constables took my arms. The ice sang underneath us, patient, keeping everything—the sandbars, the secrets, the names of drowned boats—for another spring. As they walked me up the embankment steps, I did not look back at the river. I looked forward, at the town lit yellow against the night, at the hearing room and the charges and whatever came next, and I realized I was not afraid.

I was registered. I was visible. I was, by any measure the state could devise, thoroughly caught.

Somewhere across the water, Ilse Marchetti was alive.

They could take everything else. They'd have to form a committee first.

But even as the constables' grip tightened, even as the courthouse loomed ahead with its lamps burning late for me specifically, one question followed me up the steps like a stray dog: what, exactly, does one wear to one's own trial—and would Odile Brac, wherever she was, finally tell the truth?
