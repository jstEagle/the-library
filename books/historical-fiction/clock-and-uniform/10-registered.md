## 10. Registered

The hearing room had once been a counting house, and it had not entirely renounced its old profession. The judge's bench was a raised clerk's desk; the seal of the Republic where the Halvard Company's scales had hung; even the way the light fell through the high windows, in long ruled stripes like columns in a ledger, suggested that whatever happened here would be entered, totaled, and carried forward.

Palomo Oyelaran sat with their hands folded and counted too. It was a habit of nine years' indenture: count what is in front of you, because you cannot count on what is not.

One window that opened only a hand's width. Two guards who had stopped glowering at them by the third day and now mostly looked bored. Three charges read aloud each morning in a voice like a man reciting a shipping list: aiding an enemy national, falsifying a transit document, and — this was the one the prosecutor said with real feeling, as though it were the hinge of civilization itself — registering falsely at census.

Four months since the river.

Five degrees of cold that seeped up through the floorboards no matter how many braziers they lit.

And one chair against the far wall, occupied every session by Odile Brac, who was not called as a witness for the prosecution and had not yet been called for anyone at all. She sat with her census satchel on her knees like a churchgoer with a hymnal, and she watched Paloma with the same terrible patience she had brought to their paperwork two winters running.

Paloma had decided, somewhere in the fourth week, that they were not afraid of her anymore. This decision required renewing daily, like a permit.

"State your name for the record," the prosecutor said that morning, though he had done so eleven times before. He was a thin man named Corvel who wore his patriotism the way some men wore borrowed coats — correctly, but without warmth.

"Paloma Oyelaran."

"Occupation."

"That has been under discussion," Paloma said. "Clerk, formerly. Diver, by inheritance. Currently I believe the term is *prisoner awaiting disposition*, which I'm told pays nothing."

Someone in the gallery — there was a small gallery, mostly company men who came for the entertainment — made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Judge Anser did not look up from his papers, but he let the sound live a half-second longer than he needed to.

Corvel pressed on. "You do not dispute crossing the frozen river at the Vessany shallows on the night of February the ninth?"

"No."

"You do not dispute that the woman you assisted, Ilse Marchetti, was a national of the state with which we are at war?"

"I dispute the usefulness of the word," Paloma said. "But no. She was born there. She was stranded here when the borders closed, then stranded there again after I moved her. Geography has been very busy in both our lives."

"The word is not decorative, Oyelaran. It carries law."

"So does *drowning*," Paloma said, "and I have some experience of both. She went through the ice. I pulled her out. If the law requires me to regret the pulling, then the law and I will simply have to disagree, politely, for as long as it likes."

Anser's pen paused. That was all. A pause, noted by everyone in the room, acknowledged by none.

* * *

The inheritance claim was heard on a grey Thursday, when the ice on the river had begun its long argument with the spring and lost a little more ground each night.

Advocate Serrado stood for the estate of Maren Oyelaran, née Faleye, late of the diving boats, deceased these nineteen years. She was a broad, unhurried woman with ink-stained cuffs, and she handled the documents the way Paloma's mother had handled wet rope — firmly, without ceremony, certain they would hold.

"The rights in question," she told the court, "are hereditary water-rights to the eastern shallows, held continuously by the Faleye line under the old port charters, predating the Company's lease by sixty years. They were never sold. They lapsed into administrative silence because the holder was a woman the port preferred not to see, and because her child was signed into indenture at eleven years old, which is an age at which one cannot contest a lease."

"They were never registered," Corvel objected.

"Neither was the river," said Serrado, "and yet it continues to arrive."

The judge asked his questions quietly. Had the claimant's parentage been established? It had — sworn statements from Bisi Oyelaran, fisherwoman of the lower bank, three pages of testimony that made Paloma cry in a corridor afterward and would deny crying in any corridor forever. Had the claimant registered truthfully? Here Paloma straightened.

"On March the second of this year," they said, "under my own name, my mother's name, and my birthplace, all correctly entered. Registered by census taker Brac personally." A nod toward the far wall, which Odile returned with the gravity of a notary. "I am, for the first time in my life, exactly where the record says I am."

"And where," Corvel said softly, "is that? You are a person of enemy blood on your mother's side—"

"My mother was a diver," Paloma said. "She pulled drowned men out of that river for thirty years, including, I am given to understand, the father of at least one gentleman now seated behind you, sir, who has not met my eye since this began. If the state wishes to call her foreign, it may. But it should know that it is calling its own river foreign, and the river has never once cared what we call it."

Silence, of the kind that is either very good or very bad, and Paloma could not tell which, and had learned over four months that this was simply what waiting felt like now: not terror, but arithmetic.

* * *

Odile Brac took the stand on the last day, called by Serrado over Corvel's objection, and the room rearranged its attention around her like furniture.

She gave her name, her office, her years of service. Then Serrado asked her one question.

"You conducted the respondent's registration in March. Did you find it truthful?"

Odile looked at Paloma. It was not a long look, but it contained, compressed, everything of that night in the stairwell of the census annex — *why would anyone choose danger over safety* — and everything since, which was her own answer, delivered not in words but in the fact that she had come back with her satchel and her forms and registered them properly, knowing everything, reporting nothing until summoned.

"It was," she said, "the most truthful registration I conducted that year. I checked."

"Against your earlier files?"

"Against my earlier files," Odile agreed, "which contained discrepancies. Those discrepancies are now resolved. My office concerns itself with accuracy, Advocate, not with ancestry. When the record is corrected, my work is done."

Corvel rose. "You are aware the respondent confessed to forging a transit pass using skills acquired in the Halvard ledger room?"

"I am aware," said Odile, "that the forgery was detected by me within four days, reported by me to no one, and rendered moot by events. I am also aware," and here her voice did something Paloma had never heard it do, which was to become slightly less level than a ledger line, "that my own brother was conscripted two years ago and last wrote to us from a place the army will not name. I have spent those two years asking people questions they are afraid of. I know what fear looks like in a signature. Hers"—a small correction—"theirs was fear. What they did at the river was something else. I registered it accurately. That is all I can say, and I will not say less."

She stepped down. She did not look at Paloma again, which Paloma understood to be a courtesy between people who had already seen entirely too much of each other.

* * *

The sentence came down on a morning so bright it seemed commissioned for the occasion.

Judge Anser read the verdicts in order. Guilty of falsifying a transit document — unavoidable, he observed, since Paloma admitted it with what he called "an enthusiasm unbecoming a defendant." Guilty of aiding an enemy national, with the qualification, entered into the record in Anser's dry voice, that the court found the act was "humanitarian in character, executed at grave personal risk, and directed at a single stranded individual rather than the enemy state," a distinction Corvel clearly wished to strangle with his own cravat.

Then the disposition.

"The claim of hereditary water-rights is upheld. The respondent is a person of standing, a registered holder of property, and not subject to deportation. The court notes that the respondent's lawful status was achieved before the offense, not after, and that this weighs in mitigation." He set down his pen and looked at Paloma directly, which no official in Vessany had done in nine years. "The sentence of confinement is commuted to two years' supervised labor at the eastern docks, being the location of the respondent's registered water-rights and, I am informed, their childhood. The Republic apparently believes," and here the judge permitted himself something that was nearly, almost, in adequate light, a smile, "in returning things to the water they came from."

Paloma stood. Their legs shook, and they let them.

"I thank the court," they said, and meant the strangest collection of things: the judge, the ink-stained advocate, the woman with the satchel, a mother nineteen years gone, a river that kept what it kept and sometimes, grudgingly, gave it back.

* * *

The docks in April smelled of tar and thaw, and Paloma loved them with a ferocity that surprised them. Supervised labor turned out to mean mending nets, logging salvage, and arguing daily with a foreman named Gaspard about whether the third winch was haunted. (It was. Paloma knew its ghost personally.) The work left marks on their hands that no ledger had ever left, and they found, to their mild scandal, that they preferred these.

They registered everything now. Catches, hours, repairs. There was a pleasure in it — the small vanity of a person whose name finally matched the paper.

The letter came on a Tuesday, carried across on the ferry like any other piece of the world, franked and ordinary and addressed in handwriting Paloma would have known upside down, underwater, in the dark.

They waited until evening to open it, sitting on the mooring bollard where their mother used to coil rope, gulls shouting their opinions overhead. Inside, one page.

*Paloma —*

*I am told you pulled me through the ice and then stayed behind to be caught, which is the single most inefficient rescue plan I have ever been party to, and I was party to it, so I know.*

*The border commission reviewed my case in March. Your advocate sent someone to speak for me — a meticulous, terrifying woman with a satchel. I have never been so frightened by a person taking notes. My registration is approved. I am coming back in May, legally, by the bridge, in daylight, wearing an absurd hat.*

*You once promised to get me across. Consider the debt discharged. All remaining obligations are to be renegotiated in person.*

*— I.*

P.S. *Your aunt says the winch is not haunted. Your aunt is wrong.*

Paloma read it twice, then a third time, then laughed out loud at the river, alone, visible from every direction, entirely unconcerned.

Below the surface, past the shallows where the light broke into coins, past the old diving grounds where their mother's rights ran green and quiet, the water held its usual counsel. But Paloma no longer needed it to keep anything. Everything worth keeping, they had finally put down in plain ink, under their own name, where anyone could find it.

They folded the letter, tucked it inside their shirt, and walked up the dock toward the lamps, counting — out of habit, out of joy — every step.
