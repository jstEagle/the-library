## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

There are three rules to carrying other people's secrets, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

Rule one: be nobody. Not poor-nobody — poverty draws the eye like a wound — but gray nobody. The woman whose papers are always in order and therefore never checked twice. The one who waits in queues without fidgeting and answers questions with exactly as many words as the question requires. In Veldenmark's capital that winter, being nobody was a profession, and I was very good at it.

Rule two: never carry anything you cannot destroy. A courier who loves her cargo is a courier who hesitates, and hesitation is how names get read aloud in rooms with bright lamps. Everything I carried could burn. I practiced burning things until it stopped meaning anything to me. This turned out to be useful training for other parts of my life, though I didn't know it yet.

Rule three: do not be cared for. Cared-for people are missed when they don't come home, and missed people get looked for, and looked-for people get found. I had spent six years making sure no one in the world would notice my absence. It is a strange thing, then, that the story I have to tell begins on the day someone noticed me.

But I'm ahead of myself, and a courier should know better than to arrive early.

* * *

That December morning the capital wore its occupation like a borrowed coat that didn't fit — buttons straining at the checkpoints, sleeves flapping with new flags. Snow had fallen overnight and been trampled into gray slush by boots that were not Veldenmark's boots. At the Karoliner Bridge checkpoint, two soldiers of the Provisional Authority waved through a fishwife, a priest, and a man selling roasted chestnuts, and then stopped me.

Of course they stopped me. That's the joke of invisibility: it only works until someone decides to look.

"Papers."

I gave him the courier's pass — third fold, right hand, slightly damp from where I'd held it, because a document that looks too crisp looks important. He read my name, Casper Iversen, which was true, and my occupation, clerical assistant, Ministry of Protocol, which was true in the way that a skeleton is true of a person.

"Purpose in the Alsergrund district?"

"Delivering minutes." I let my shoulders slump the small amount that communicates tedium. "The ministry wants everything copied before the archives close. Nobody tells us why."

He almost smiled. There is a fellowship among people who have been made pointless by larger powers, and even an occupying soldier feels it some mornings. He waved me through, and I walked on across the bridge with eleven pages of cipher sewn into the lining of my coat, thinking, as I did every time I passed a checkpoint, that rule two existed because of days like this one.

The dispatches were nothing dramatic. Grain allotments. A list of magistrates still loyal to the Republic of Veldenmark, which was to say, a list of men who would be arrested by spring if anyone read it carelessly. The last page concerned a name I did not know yet, and would come to know better than my own: Brandt.

Ambassador Henrik Brandt had been the Republic's envoy to three capitals and none of them wanted him anymore, so he had been recalled to a homeland that no longer properly existed, and he had refused to leave. The Provisional Authority tolerated him the way you tolerate an old dog that growls at guests — barely, and with an eye toward quieter solutions. My orders were to deliver his correspondence, observe nothing, report nothing, and under no circumstances engage him in conversation beyond weather.

I intend to be honest in this account, since honesty is the only currency I have left, so I will admit here at the outset: I broke that last rule within four hours.

* * *

The Brandt residence stood on a street that had once been fashionable and now was merely stubborn — four stories of yellow stone with frost ferning up the windows, chimneys smoking thin because coal was rationed and the ambassador refused to burn what his neighbors couldn't afford. The door opened before I knocked. That should have told me something about the household. Houses with secrets learn to hear footsteps.

"You're late," said the woman at the door. She was perhaps my age, dark-haired, with the kind of stare that conducted audits. "The post comes at nine."

"The post comes at nine," I agreed, "when the bridges are open. The bridges were not open."

She considered this, decided I was insolent rather than incompetent, and stepped aside. I would learn later that this was Ilse Brandt's method of meeting the world: assume everyone is failing until proven dangerous, and revise accordingly.

The hall smelled of dust and yesterday's coffee and, faintly, of candle wax, because the electricity had been cut in November and the household had gone over to candles with the practicality of people who had stopped expecting things to improve. Marta, the cook, appeared from belowstairs — a wide, calm woman who took my wet coat without asking and put a cup in my hands without asking either, and both acts were performed with such authority that refusal seemed legally impossible. I drank the coffee. It was mostly chicory. It was the best thing I had tasted in a year, and I hated myself for thinking so.

Rule three, you understand. I want it on the record that I knew exactly what was happening to me and permitted it anyway.

Ambassador Brandt received me in a study lined with books that had been catalogued for evacuation twice and never evacuated, because there was nowhere left to evacuate them to. He was older than I expected — seventy, maybe, with a face like a coastline, all erosion and persistence — and he was standing at the window watching the soldiers on the corner with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he disliked.

"Minutes from the ministry," I said, and held out the packet.

"So they claim." He weighed it in his hand. "And you walked them across the city yourself? They don't trust the mails anymore."

"No one trusts the mails anymore."

"Hm." He looked at me then, properly, the way I imagine he had once looked at ministers and liars, and I felt the peculiar discomfort of being *seen* by a professional. "You're Iversen. They said you'd be the one."

"They say very little about me, sir. That's the arrangement."

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point of you." And then, instead of dismissing me, which was the script, he said: "Have you eaten?"

I want to explain why that question did what it did. Six years a courier. I had slept in waiting rooms and eaten standing up in markets and once spent a whole night on a chapel floor in the mountains sharing body heat with a stranger's mule, and in all that time no handler, no official, no fellow traveler of any rank had ever asked whether I had eaten. Couriers are instruments. Instruments don't eat. You wind them, you use them, you wipe them down and case them.

"I couldn't say, sir," I said, which was a lie, because I could say, and the honest answer was *not since yesterday*.

"Marta," he called, mildly, as if summoning weather. "Another plate."

"That's not necessary—"

"It's entirely necessary. Sit down, Iversen. If the Republic has nothing else left, it has soup, and I will not have it said we let its servants starve in the doorway." He was already turning back to the window, dismissing me the way you dismiss family, which is to say without ceremony and with every expectation of seeing you at dinner.

I sat down. Marta put bread in front of me, dark and dense and real, and Ilse Brandt watched me from the doorway with her audit-stare, and I ate the soup of a country that had eleven weeks left to live, and I made a mistake. I let my shoulders come down half an inch. I let the coffee warm my hands longer than efficiency required.

Somewhere in me, behind a locked door, a voice said: *rule three.* And another voice, quieter, said: *they have candles.*

* * *

I delivered to the Brandt house twice more that month. This required no falsification of my route; the ministry, in its death throes, had developed a sudden passion for protocol, sending ceremonial documents back and forth across the occupied capital like a patient pacing a sickroom. On the second visit, Tomas — the clerk, nineteen, ears like handles, a boy who blushed when spoken to and took notes when spoken about — showed me the household ledger, unprompted, because he was proud of it. Coal: declining. Potatoes: adequate. Candles: sufficient for eight weeks. He had drawn a little graph. I looked at the graph of their dying light and said, "Very tidy," and he glowed like a stove.

On the third visit, Ilse caught me in the hall.

"You walk differently coming in than going out," she said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Coming up the street you're watching the rooftops. Leaving, you watch the gutters. Which is it you're afraid of — snipers or sewage?"

"Neither. Habit."

"Habits tell the truth when people won't." She leaned against the newel post, arms crossed, wearing a sweater that had been mended with more devotion than skill. "My father thinks you're intelligence. Proper intelligence, not ministry errand-girls. He says you have the hands for cipher work."

"Your father," I said, "has an old man's romance about strangers."

"Probably. He also invited you to dinner Thursday, so whatever you are, you'll be it at our table."

I should have declined. I want that entered into the record too. Every rule I had, every scar the rules had grown from, said: eat alone, sleep alone, be nobody somewhere else. But the house was cold and lit with candles, and Marta had begun leaving bread for me wrapped in cloth as though I were expected, which no one had ever done, and Ilse was looking at me with the particular challenge she used in place of warmth.

"Thursday," I said.

* * *

Here is what I carried out of the city that day in my coat lining, and what I carried in the place where the dispatches should have been, which no checkpoint has ever had the means to search:

Outwardly: routine. Confirmation of a neutral vessel expected at Havnvik in February, manifest details pending. A request from the foreign ministry-in-exile that Ambassador Brandt prepare to depart, phrased with the delicacy of people who have learned that old patriots can be insulted by rescue.

Inwardly: the smell of candle wax. The graph of the coal. The sound of Ilse Brandt saying *Thursday* like a verdict I was glad to have handed down.

I passed three checkpoints going out and was searched at none of them, because I was nobody, and nobodies cross cities unremarked. From the far bank of the river I looked back once — I never allowed myself that, usually — and saw the yellow house with its frost-ferned windows and its thin smoke, one lit window on the third floor where someone moved behind a candle flame.

Eleven weeks. That's what the arithmetic said, though I didn't know the figures yet. Eleven weeks until the purge lists, the ship, the quay. Eleven weeks until I would learn precisely what a sealed letter weighs, and what it costs to carry one, and what it costs to open one.

But that evening I knew none of it. That evening I knew only that I had broken rule three, cleanly and without struggle, and that the strangest part — the part I would spend a winter refusing to examine — was that being folded into a stranger's dwindling warmth felt less like failure and less like danger than anything I had ever done in service of the Republic.

It felt like coming off a long road and finding the lamp lit.

I told myself it was operational. Access to the ambassador, cover identity, all of it defensible in any debriefing. I wrote it up that way in my own head, filed it under duty, sealed it under my rules.

The candle in the third-floor window burned until nearly midnight. I know because I watched it from the bridge, in the snow, much longer than a courier watching a window can justify.
