## 2. Household of Ghosts

My second rule of couriership, after *never speak first*, is this: learn the house, then forget it. Know where the exits are, which floorboards announce you, who sleeps light and who drinks. Map it all, carry the map in your head, and never once let the map know it lives there too.

By the third week of December I had mapped the Brandt residence so thoroughly I could walk it blindfolded, which was fortunate, because the electricity had become a matter of rumor rather than fact, and I did most of my walking in the dark.

The house had been built for a different century and a larger country. It stood on Vester Square like an old officer at attention long after his regiment stopped caring, its windows shuttered now against both the cold and the eyes across the square. The faction's men had taken the customs house opposite, and their lantern burned all night with the patient confidence of something that expects to outlast you.

I knew the house the way I knew cipher keys: structurally.

Ambassador Aldric Brandt rose at five, without an alarm, without complaint, as though punctuality were the last ministry still functioning under him. He took his coffee black and thin — Marta stretched everything thin those weeks, coffee included — and he read until seven by a candle he rationed with an accountant's precision. He had been ambassador for nineteen years, back through three governments, and he treated the collapse of the Republic of Veldenmark roughly the way he treated bad weather: regrettable, forecastable, and no excuse for arriving late.

I watched him one morning re-fold a dispatch from a government that no longer existed, address a reply to ministers who were already in the ground or in the cells beneath the Hall of Records, and seal it anyway. When he caught me watching, he said only:

"Paper keeps, Miss Iversen. Governments don't. We file accordingly."

I have repeated that sentence to myself many times since, usually at moments when it was least comforting.

Ilse Brandt was harder to map, because she moved. Her father occupied rooms; Ilse occupied the whole house, appearing in the kitchen, the library, the drafty upstairs corridor, always mid-thought, always as though she'd been there for hours and would be there forever. She was twenty-six and spoke the way her father wrote — precisely, and with more edge than strictly necessary.

"You're limping," she told me my fourth morning, before I'd said good morning to anyone.

"I'm walking."

"Left foot. You've done something to your ankle and you've decided that if nobody notices it will stop being true." She pushed a chair at me. "Sit. Marta has a poultice that smells like sin but works."

It worked. That was the trouble with the household. Everything worked. The poultices, the rationed candles, Tomas's talent for coaxing the stove into cooperation with fuel that should not, by any law of combustion, burn. The house functioned at a quarter strength and gave full warmth anyway, and I sat in that warmth every evening telling myself it was tactical. A courier embedded in a safe house observes. Observing is not the same as belonging. Warmth is a resource, like bread, and one may consume resources without owing anyone gratitude.

That is what I told myself. I am, by profession, very good at telling myself things.

* * *

Marta ran the kitchen the way border guards run borders: absolutely, and with a private conviction that the rules existed for everyone else. She had cooked for the Brandts for thirty years and had apparently decided, sometime around my second day, that I was underweight, and that this was a security problem she intended to solve personally.

She did not ask questions. This was the great mercy of her. In a winter when every conversation in the capital was a small negotiation over how much truth could safely change hands, Marta asked nothing and fed everyone, as though soup could settle accounts that words only complicated.

"Eat," she would say, setting down a bowl. Not *please*. Not *how are you*. Just: eat. And I ate, because refusing Marta would have required a kind of courage I did not have and was not being paid for.

Tomas was twenty, the junior clerk, all elbows and earnestness, with ink permanently on his right hand and a look of permanent apology. He copied documents in a hand so beautiful it seemed like a rebuke to the age. He idolized the ambassador with a purity I found, frankly, alarming — devotion was a liability, in my trade. Devotion got people followed.

But I caught myself teaching him anyway. There was a night in the library, candlelight and frost ferns on the glass, when he was struggling with a routing slip and I leaned over his shoulder and corrected it, and then stayed an hour longer than I needed to, explaining how to read a postal district for signs of interception. He listened the way people listen to scripture.

"You should write this down," he said.

"No," I said. "You should remember it. Things written down get read."

He laughed, and Ilse looked up from across the room, and I realized I had made a joke — voluntarily, aloud, in front of witnesses — for the first time in eleven months. My rules filed a formal objection. The rules were losing.

* * *

And Ilse.

Here is what I will say about mapping a person: houses hold still. Ilse did not. She argued politics with her father over dinner the way other families pass the salt — routinely, warmly, and with occasional breakage. She read the foreign papers faster than the ambassador did. She had her mother's eyes, I learned from Marta, and her mother's habit of asking exactly one question too many.

She noticed things. That was the danger of her, and later the salvation of her, though I didn't know yet that those could be the same quality. She noticed that I checked the street before lighting a lamp. She noticed that I slept with my boots near the bed. She noticed, one evening, that I flinched at the sound of the door knocker, and instead of pretending not to see — the standard courtesy, the decent thing — she looked straight at me across the table and said:

"You count the seconds between the knock and the second knock."

"Do I?"

"Three, four. If there's no second knock you relax. Why?"

"Because two knocks close together means official business," I heard myself say, "and official business in this city is rarely good."

Which was true, and also not the answer, and she knew it was not the answer, and she let it go. Letting it go was its own form of speech. I was beginning to learn her grammar.

* * *

The warning came on the twenty-second of December, folded inside a bill for coal.

It was good work — a commercial cover, mundane arithmetic, a delivery note in a merchant's cramped hand. Anyone intercepting it would have seen a coal merchant overcharging a diplomat, which was merely the ordinary corruption of the season. But the figures were in a column system I recognized, old service training, barely used since the armistice, and I sat in the pantry with the paper flat on the shelf between jars of preserved plums and read it twice.

*Inventory lists circulating. Names attached. Collection phase begun in capital. Surnames A–M first. Advise principals review holdings.*

*Review holdings.* In the old service idiom, that meant: decide what you can afford to lose.

I stood in the cold pantry and did arithmetic. *Brandt* began with B.

Through the wall I could hear the kitchen: Marta scolding the stove, Tomas laughing at whatever Marta had said to the stove, Ilse's voice rising and falling above them both like weather. The ambassador's tread overhead, unhurried, punctual, five o'clock even in the dark of the world.

They were not, strictly speaking, my problem. I carried dispatches. I delivered codes. What happened to the people sheltering me fell outside my commission, and my commission was the only thing standing between me and being nobody again — a woman with no family name worth saying, no house, no country currently issuing passports. Rules exist for reasons. Rule three: the courier carries the message, not the consequences.

But rule three had been written by a version of me who had never eaten Marta's bread, never been poulticed by Ilse, never watched an old man seal letters to dead ministers because paper keeps and someone must.

I read the warning a third time. Then I folded it along its creases, held a corner to the stove until it caught, and dropped it into the ash bucket, and stirred.

"Casper." Ilse, in the doorway, arms crossed against the cold, watching me stir ashes with more attention than ashes deserved. "Bad news?"

"The coal merchant," I said. "He's raised his rates."

"Mm," said Ilse, who noticed everything, and whose eyes stayed on mine a moment too long. "Well. At least he's honest about wanting something from us. Most of the city isn't anymore."

She turned back toward the kitchen. I stood in the pantry with the smell of burnt paper and plums, and understood — with the clarity that comes only when it's too late for clarity to be useful — that I had crossed a line I could not find again in the dark.

The names were being collected. And somewhere in my chest, unfiled, uncoded, unprofessional, a list of my own had started keeping itself.
