## 3. What the Letter Said

There is a particular skill to intercepting post in your own father's house, and I regret to report that I have mastered it.

It isn't theft. Let me be clear about that, at least to myself, since I appear to be the only jury convening these days. Theft implies the letter was ever going to reach its destination intact. In Veldenmark this winter, letters do not travel; they are processed. They pass through the checkpoint on Vaskergasse where a man named — I am told — Herr Kroll opens them with a blade he keeps sharp for the purpose and a face kept deliberately blank for the same reason. Anything interesting goes into one drawer. Everything else goes onward, minus whatever amused him.

So when the courier came at dusk — a boy of maybe sixteen with frost in his eyebrows and the particular walk of someone carrying something worth more than his boots — and asked not for Father but for *the household*, and handed over a packet addressed in a hand my father would not recognize because it had been designed by people whose entire profession was being unrecognized — well. The boy waited at the door while Marta fetched him bread, and the packet sat on the hall table under my hand like a sleeping animal, and I confess that my conscience did not so much object as clear its throat and then think better of it.

The seal was wax, good wax, the kind that cracks cleanly if you warm a knife over a candle and go slowly. My father taught me that trick when I was nine, ostensibly so I could reseal my grandmother's jam parcels. He never said, *one day you will use this to read treason at your own kitchen table*, but fathers rarely annotate their lessons that far ahead.

I want to say I hesitated. What I actually did was glance at the door, listen to Marta telling the courier about her knees as though her knees were the last living things in Veldenmark, and slide the knife along the fold in one motion, the way you pull off a bandage or admit something true.

* * *

The letter itself was short, which surprised me. I had expected pages — conspiracies always seemed like they ought to require pages, all that machinery of betrayal wanting documentation. Instead there were four paragraphs in a clerk's hand, the kind of handwriting that has been trained out of all personality, and I will transcribe what I remember of them here because I have found that writing it down is the only way to make it stay in one place instead of swimming through my skull at three in the morning:

> *The list has been ratified at the second level. Names within the inner circle are confirmed for inclusion. The residence on Brandtstrasse is designated for action, not arrest. Action, in this context, the reader will understand.*

I understood. I understood the way you understand a fall from a height: entirely, and too late to matter.

> *The daughter's name appears independently. This is noted as unusual and possibly an error of compilation, but errors of compilation are not corrected at this stage.*

My name. Mine, separately from my father's, which meant someone had thought about me specifically, had sat in some warm office somewhere with my file open and decided I was worth ink. There is a vanity in even being frightened of that, I know. I felt it anyway — the horrible little flare of being *seen*, followed immediately by the understanding of what being seen means now.

> *A vessel under neutral registry will call at Havnvik within the month. Berths will be limited. The faction permits certain departures, as it is cheaper than certain alternatives. The bearer of this communication can arrange manifest access at a price consistent with previous arrangements.*

> *Burn this.*

I did not burn it. That is the confession at the center of everything that follows, so I may as well place it here, early, where it can be properly weighed: I did not burn it, because burning it meant it had only ever existed in my memory, and my memory has lately become an unreliable country — borders shifting, provinces declaring independence at inconvenient hours. Paper at least stays where you put it.

Also — and this is the part I like least about myself — I did not burn it because I wanted proof. Some cold practical creature I did not know I harboring stood up inside me and said: if you are going to betray your father's trust, his privacy, his entire philosophy of bearing things quietly, then get value for it. Keep the letter. Know the price of manifests. Be, for once in your sheltered, useless life, useful.

The cold creature folded the letter back along its creases, resealed the wax so neatly that even Father might not have known, and put it in the false bottom of my sewing box, which I had previously used for love letters and once for a flask, and which now held the single most dangerous object in the house.

Then Marta came in and asked why I was standing in the dark, and I said I was watching the snow, and she gave me the look Marta gives when she knows perfectly well I am lying but has decided, on some private ledger of her own, that my lies are none of her business yet.

* * *

Here is what you must know about my father, Ambassador Henrik Brandt, so that you understand the size of what I could not say to him.

He is not a brave man in the way the broadsheets mean it — no sabre, no speeches from balconies. His courage is made entirely of patience. For thirty years he has practiced a diplomacy consisting of sitting very still while louder men ruin things, and then quietly salvaging what can be salvaged after. He signs documents he despises and files memoranda that will outlive everyone who laughed at them. When the faction entered the capital, he did not flee. He had the shutters repaired, told Marta to stretch the flour, and continued answering the republic's mail as though the republic still existed, which in his accounting it does, technically, the way a drowned man is technically wet.

He knows things. Of course he knows things; he is the ambassador, the whole embassy now, ambassador, clerical staff, and boiler repair. He knows the names being collected — Tomas heard a rumor weeks ago and passed it up the chain, and Father went grey for a day and then resumed being grey permanently. But knowing in general is survivable. It is the specific that kills, and I was holding his specific in my sewing box: *action, not arrest*. A neutral ship. Limited berths. A price.

And here was the arithmetic that kept me awake: if I told him, I would be telling him two things at once. First, that we were marked. Second, that his daughter had opened his correspondence, which meant that every sealed thing he had ever protected — and he has spent his whole life protecting sealed things, it is practically his religion — was now compromised at the source, by blood, by me. He would forgive me. That is precisely the problem. He would forgive me instantly and completely, and then he would begin making calculations with his face carefully arranged into calm, and those calculations would involve trading himself away to buy my passage, because I have watched him long enough to know that my father's love expresses itself almost exclusively in logistics.

He would send me away and stay. He was already composing that letter in his head; I could see it forming behind his eyes across the dinner table, in the way he looked at Casper's official transit papers and then at me and then at the candle, doing sums.

Oh — Casper. Yes. Her, too. Because of course the letter changed her as well, though she didn't know it yet, and I will come to that, because coming to it is the reason I am writing any of this down at all.

* * *

Casper Iversen is the most transparent person I have ever met, which is a strange thing to say about a professional secret-carrier. She thinks her invisibility works. She arranges herself into this careful grayness — plain coat, plain voice, sits near the wall, eats exactly what she is given, thanks Marta for everything twice — and believes we don't see her. Meanwhile Marta has stopped asking whether she takes sugar because she noticed Casper always drinks it bitter on bad days and sweet on good ones, which is more information than most people reveal in a decade of conversation. Tomas worships her. My father trusts her, which for him is nearly a sacrament.

And I —

I watch how she holds her cup. I know the sound of her boots on the stairs versus anyone else's, and I know which stair creaks, and I know she skips it, which means she has counted the stairs, which means she is paying attention to this house the way you pay attention to something you intend to keep. I have spent three years constructing elaborate reasons to be in the hallway when she passes through it. I have opinions about her hands. I am aware of how humiliating this would be to explain aloud, and so, naturally, I explain nothing aloud, and neither does she, and we conduct our entire acquaintance in the grammar of the unspoken, which suits me fine right up until roughly three in the morning, when it suits me considerably less.

That night — the night of the letter — she came down past midnight for water and found me sitting in the kitchen with the lamp unlit. I didn't hear her boots; that's the point of her. One moment I was alone with my guilt and the snow ticking against the glass, and the next there was a person in the doorway saying my name softly, twice, the way you'd speak to something skittish.

"You're sitting in the dark," she observed.

"I'm conserving candles."

"There's a full box in the pantry."

"I'm conserving them *principally*."

She got water anyway, moving around the kitchen without light, and I watched her silhouette find everything by memory — the pump handle, the chipped cup she favors — and I thought: she would carry this letter for me. Whatever it said, whoever it condemned, she would take it into her teeth and run, because that is what she is *for*, and she has never once in this house let herself be anything else for.

"Ilse." She set the cup down. "What's happened?"

And here is where I should tell you that I lied. I said *nothing*, said it lightly, said it with the practiced ease of a woman who has been rehearsing lightness her whole life. She looked at me for a long moment in the dark, and I felt her not believing me the way you feel weather change, and she let it go, because she is nobody's interrogator, and she went back upstairs skipping the creaking stair.

I sat in the kitchen until the window turned the color of old pewter and thought about what I had just done. I had lied to the one person whose lie-detection I respect. I had protected my father's ignorance at the cost of my own honesty, and protected my own secret at the cost of her help, and I had done it all so smoothly that I scared myself a little. Guilt, it turns out, is excellent training. Every day you practice concealment on the people you love, you get better at it, and worse.

By dawn I had arrived somewhere. Not peace — nothing so dignified — but a decision wearing peace's coat: I would tell her. Not Father, not yet, not until I knew how the land lay and what the manifests cost and whether the cold creature in my chest was right that there was a move to make. But Casper would know the roads. Casper would know what a berth weighs. And Casper, God help me, already looks at me as though I am a letter she is afraid to open.

She was in the study before breakfast, sorting dispatch cases, her collar crooked, utterly unaware of me in the doorway in the way that only she can manage.

"Casper," I said, and closed the door behind me, and turned the key.
