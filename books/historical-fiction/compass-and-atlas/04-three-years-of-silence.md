## 4. Three Years of Silence

I have three rules, and I had kept them for three years. Carry nothing you cannot destroy. Trust no one who offers trust freely. Never sleep in a house where you have been given a name.

The third rule I had broken in November, when Ambassador Brandt started calling me *our courier* at dinner instead of *the courier*, and Marta began setting my place without asking whether I'd come. The second rule was bleeding out by degrees — you cannot share a larder with people for a season and remain a stranger to them; hunger is a kind of intimacy. But the first rule still held. Whatever I carried lived in my head or burned within the hour, and no one in that frostbitten residence knew what I was except the man who paid me and the codes I memorized like psalms.

Then Ilse read a letter she shouldn't have, and I discovered that rules are like ice on the canal: solid right up until they aren't.

* * *

I knew before she confessed. That's the thing about learning someone's silences — once you've mapped them, new ones announce themselves like missing teeth.

Ilse Brandt had always been loud in the way that clever people in occupied cities are loud: too many opinions delivered at volume, aimed at safe targets — the curfew, the coal shortage, the regime's fondness for purple ink on official notices ("as if tyranny weren't embarrassing enough"). She filled rooms so no one would look too closely at what was being carried through them. I recognized the technique. I used it myself, professionally.

But for four days after the letter came, she went quiet. Not melancholy-quiet, which suits her the way mourning lace suits a knife — decoratively, briefly — but *operational*-quiet. She watched her father when he wasn't looking. She counted the household at meals, her eyes moving from Marta to Tomas to me like a woman tallying souls against some ledger only she could see. And twice I caught her watching me with an expression I could not file under any known heading: guilt wearing curiosity's coat.

The letter itself I never saw. It arrived sealed, addressed in a hand I didn't recognize, and it went straight into the ambassador's study, and whatever it said stayed there with him. But I carry dispatches between a dying republic and its last friends abroad; I know what paper weighs when it's bad news. That envelope had weighed like a stone in a river, pulling everything around it down.

On the fourth night I found her in the pantry, sitting on a flour barrel in the dark, and I understood two things at once. First: she was hiding there because hiding is where you go when you know something that will break other people. Second: I was in love with her, and had been for long enough that the discovery felt less like news than like finally reading my own handwriting.

"Tell me," I said, and closed the door behind us.

* * *

She looked at me for a long moment in the dark, flour dust drifting down like patient snow. "You first," she said. "What did you burn last Tuesday?"

Ah. So we were doing this.

"I don't burn anything," I said. "I'm a translator. Freelance."

"Casper." My name in her mouth had always done something structurally unsound to my chest, and now she deployed it like a crowbar. "You come and go through checkpoints that stop everyone else. You eat like a person keeping accounts. You watch doors. My father talks to you alone after dinner and comes out older every time. And Tuesday night I saw you feed something to the stove and stand over it until it was ash, and then you thanked the stove." A pause. "You *thanked* it. Who thanks a stove, Casper, unless the stove has just eaten their secrets?"

"The stove and I have an arrangement," I said. "It doesn't tell mine, and I don't tell its."

She didn't laugh. That was how I knew how frightened she was.

So I sat down on the opposite barrel, and I made a decision that violated rule two in its entirety, and I told her. Not everything — the codes, the routes, the names of the dead men who had trained me went unspoken — but the shape of it. That I carried the republic's voice out through the occupation's teeth, one swallowed sentence at a time. That the residence was a way station for more than dispatches. That the ambassador was dying by inches of the same knowledge that kept him alive, because a man who knows where all the bodies are buried makes inconveniently good company for the gravediggers.

"And us?" she asked. "Why does he keep us here? Why keep *you* here?"

"Because a courier needs somewhere warm to remember," I said. "And because nobody suspects a household of ghosts."

She took this in. Then, quietly, in the voice of someone paying a debt: "There's a list."

I did not move. Every instinct I owned stood up at attention.

"A purge list. Names are being collected in the capital — real names, family names, addresses." Her hands found each other in her lap and gripped. "Ours is on it. Father's. Mine. The letter said—" She stopped, started again. "The letter named us. It said a neutral ship calls at Havnvik. Limited berths."

"How limited?"

"That's not the part that matters." She looked up, and in the dark her eyes were wet and furious at being wet. "The part that matters is that my father read it, folded it, put it in his desk, and kissed me goodnight as though the world were ordinary. He's known for four days. Four days, and he says nothing, and he winds the clocks, and he asks Marta about the barley, and I have to sit across from him at breakfast knowing—"

"Ilse."

"—knowing that he's already decided something, because that's what he does, he decides things in silence like a man settling accounts, and none of us get a vote—"

"*Ilse.*"

She stopped. The house ticked around us, pipes contracting in the cold like old bones.

"You're going to tell me to be calm," she said.

"I'm going to tell you that now there are two of us who know, and that changes the arithmetic." I heard my own voice go flat and professional and hated it, and did it anyway, because flatness is load-bearing. "It also means we're both carrying secrets through the same checkpoint, and couriers who don't declare their cargo to each other get each other killed."

Silence. Somewhere above us, Tomas dropped a boot.

"You knew something was wrong," she said at last. "You've been circling me for days like a cat around a broken vase."

"You've been circling *me*."

"Because I thought you might be the danger." She laughed — one short, cracked note. "Isn't that absurd? I thought maybe you'd been sent to *watch* us. To confirm the list. And then I decided that was impossible, because—"

"Because?"

"Because nobody who fakes loving our dinners that much can be working for them."

I should have let that pass. Instead I heard myself say, "I don't fake it."

The pantry got very small and very bright, the way rooms do when something true has just been said in them. Flour hung in the air between us. Ilse looked at me, and I watched three years of my careful invisibility come apart like wet paper.

"No," she agreed softly. "You don't."

* * *

We took the candles upstairs eventually — to her room, ostensibly, because I'd claimed I needed to look at maps of the Havnvik road, which was even true. But maps were the excuse, and we both knew it, and neither of us pretended otherwise, and that mutual refusal to lie may have been the most intimate thing that happened that night. Everything else was quieter.

She lit two candles because one felt insufficient and three felt theatrical. She sat cross-legged on the bed with the atlas open and her hair coming loose from its braid, and I knelt on the floor tracing a road I mostly knew by heart, and we conducted the rest of the conversation in the grammar of conspiracy: everything important said sideways, everything dangerous said plainly, everything unbearable said barely at all.

"If the ship comes," she said, "how many berths do you think?"

"For a listed ambassador?" I turned a page I hadn't read. "Two. Perhaps three if his name buys mercy. It won't buy five."

"Marta. Tomas."

"I know."

"They'll stay anyway. Marta would die in that kitchen rather than leave it. Tomas would follow us to the dock and give us his boots." Her finger came down on the map, over my hand, over the road to Havnvik, and stayed there. "Casper. Look at me."

I looked at her. Rule three, broken past repair.

"When I thought you might be sent to kill us, I spent two nights deciding I didn't care," she said. "That if you came for us, I'd let you close enough to talk first. Do you understand what that means? I trusted you *before* I had reasons. I built reasons afterward, like a coward, like furniture around a fact I couldn't face." Her thumb moved once across my knuckles. "The fact hasn't changed. Only the furniture."

"Ilse." Her name came out wrecked. I tried again, courier's discipline, steady hands: "Whatever happens at Havnvik — however the berths fall — this can't go anywhere. Not now. Not here. There's no version of the next six months where we get to be—"

"I'm not asking for six months." Fierce, low, leaning toward me so the candle flames bent. "I'm asking for honesty between conspirators. I'm asking that if one of us dies, the other knows what she was to the dead. I'm asking you to stop pretending your rules still exist."

"They exist."

"Name one."

Silence. The candles considered this testimony and found it wanting.

"We tell no one," I said finally. "Not Marta, not Tomas. Not your father."

"Not even my father."

"*Especially* not your father. He's carrying enough weight to sink him. We don't add ours to the manifest." I held her gaze. "Swear it."

"Sworn." No hesitation, and I loved her for the hesitation's absence, and hated myself for needing it. Then, softer, with the ghost of her usual sharpness returning like circulation to a frozen limb: "You realize we've just declared something, in the middle of a war, in a pantry and a bedroom, by candlelight, like the world's most doomed novel."

"I've read worse endings."

"So have I." She blew out one candle, then the other, and in the sudden dark her hand found mine and held on. "We'll write a better one. I refuse on principle."

I stayed another hour. We spoke of roads, and berths, and lies we'd need, and only once — just before I left, at the door, with the corridor cold beyond it — did either of us say anything without armor. She said, "Come back in spring." As if it were a plan. As if spring were a place you could navigate to with a decent map.

I said, "Count on it," which is a courier's phrase, meaning *I will try*, meaning *the road may eat me*, meaning, tonight, *yes*.

* * *

In my room I lay awake cataloguing what I'd just done. Three years of rules, and in one evening I'd traded them for a shared secret with a marked woman in a condemned house. If the regime ever took me, they wouldn't need instruments. They'd need only to mention her name and watch me fold.

Below me, the ambassador's clock struck two, patient as ever, counting down hours it didn't understand. And beneath the ticking — fainter, from the direction of the study — the scratch of a pen. The ambassador was awake, writing something in the smallest hours, sealing what he wrote.

Four days since the letter. Four days of his silences stacking up like sandbags against a flood.

By morning, I decided, I would find out what he was writing. By morning, I'd know exactly how many berths we were counting — and whose name would have to fill the one that didn't exist.

The scratch of the pen went on. I did not sleep.
