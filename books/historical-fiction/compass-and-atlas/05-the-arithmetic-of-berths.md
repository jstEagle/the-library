## 5. The Arithmetic of Berths

I have spent forty years teaching young men that diplomacy is the art of counting. Counting votes, counting divisions, counting the seconds of silence after an unacceptable proposal. I said it so often that my daughter can recite it in her sleep, with an unflattering impression of my voice attached. But I never taught anyone the arithmetic I performed tonight, alone at my desk with a single candle and a ledger no one will ever audit.

The count was simple. That is what made it obscene.

* * *

The confirmation came at four in the afternoon, delivered by a fish merchant who does not sell fish. Henrik Voss — I will allow myself the luxury of his real name only here, in the privacy of my own skull, since every other document in this house must lie about him — sat in my study drinking the chicory substitute Marta serves us now and told me, in the flat cadence men use for weather and funerals, that the *Vestkyst* would make her call at Havnvik in nineteen days. Neutral flag. Swedish master. Two berths secured by my own government before its government ceased meaning anything.

Two.

One for Casper Iversen, whose passage is written into her credentials as courier, inviolable, the last piece of legal fiction the Republic of Veldenmark still commands on foreign soil. And one more, purchased at ruinous cost through channels Voss declined to describe, for whoever else we could smuggle aboard under whatever name we could forge well enough to survive a harbor inspection.

"One berth beyond the courier," Voss said, setting down his cup with the care of a man who had broken things in other people's houses before. "That is what the money bought. I'm sorry, Ambassador. I asked for three."

"You asked for three," I repeated, because repetition buys time, and time is the only currency left to me that isn't counterfeit.

"I asked for three." He looked at the window, where the frost had grown its own gray parliament across the glass. "They gave one. They said — and I will spare you nothing, because you've never wanted to be spared anything — they said the Republic's credit extends exactly as far as one old man's signature is still worth. After you, nothing. The account closes."

There was a compliment buried somewhere in that, and like everything else this winter, it arrived wrapped in a threat.

He left at half past four. I sat in my study until the light failed, doing sums.

* * *

Here is the ledger, then. Let me be exact, because exactness is all I have left to be.

My daughter Ilse requires one berth. This is not arithmetic; this is axiom. A man who would hesitate over that line deserves neither the title of father nor ambassador, and I have clung to both titles the way drowning men cling to each other, each believing the other floats.

Casper Iversen has her own berth, purchased not with money but with her particular talent for being nobody, which this country has made more valuable than any name. She does not know about the second berth. I have watched her refuse the sugar ration twice without appearing to notice it was offered, and I understand her kind: she would surrender the extra place the way she surrenders everything, silently, as though it were simply the correct procedure. Which is precisely why I cannot tell her. People who give things away that easily must sometimes be protected from their own bookkeeping.

Tomas requires a berth. Nineteen years old, my last functioning clerk, the keeper of files that will matter someday if there is ever a someday worth keeping files for. His face when he copies dispatches has the same concentration Ilse had at nine, building ships out of matchsticks that never quite floated. I find I cannot do the sum that ends with Tomas staying.

Marta requires — no. Marta refuses. She informed me of this in October, standing in my doorway with a rolling pin held like a scepter, when I first raised the possibility of evacuation. "I'm not a document, Herr Ambassador," she said. "Documents get carried. I stay with my kitchen." I could argue with a minister. I have never once won an argument with Marta, and the Republic's collapse has not improved my position.

And then there is the line item I have been circling for six hours, the entry written in my own hand that I keep trying to erase and keep finding again beneath the ink like a watermark:

Ambassador Edvard Brandt. One seat. Unallocated.

* * *

The mathematics of it are almost funny, if you have the sort of mind that finds humor in gallows ledgers. The regime wants me dead — I read their letter, or rather, I am told it exists, which in occupied countries amounts to the same certainty — but they want me dead *legally*. There is a distinction that matters to them, these new men. Shot while escaping is untidy; tried and condemned is legitimate. My death, properly conducted, would close the Republic's account permanently. No signature, no claim, no restoration. I am not a man to them. I am a clause.

Which means my survival is worth more than my life. An old diplomat's conceit, perhaps. But consider: as long as I breathe outside their jurisdiction, every treaty, every appointment, every debt the Republic incurred remains technically extant. Dead, I am a martyr, and martyrs are useful to everyone except themselves. Alive and inconvenient, in some neutral port with my seals and my cipher books and my stubborn signature, I am a problem the new regime cannot close. A living republic in one body, walking around, being counted.

Ilse alive is worth everything to Ilse, and to me, and to whatever future she will build out of the wreckage we are leaving her.

Brandt alive is worth something to the future too. Something abstract. Something written in the grammar of treaties rather than the grammar of daughters.

I sat with that comparison until the candle guttered, and I confess the abstractions did not win. Fathers are poor arithmeticians. We always round up the child and round down ourselves.

But I was not permitted the comfort of a clean answer, because I am also still, God help me, the last ambassador of a republic that trusted me with its name.

* * *

So I did what diplomats do when the truth is unbearable: I wrote two versions of it.

The first draft began: *My dear Ilse — By the time you read this, you will be at sea, and I will still be ashore, and you will be angry with me in a way that may take decades to forgive, which is proper; anger is a better inheritance than grief.*

I wrote eleven pages. I explained. God knows I explained — the clause, the signature, the living republic, the arithmetic of futures weighed against futures. It was eloquent. I made myself cry twice, which at my age is either proof of sincerity or of vanity, and I honestly could not tell you which. In that version I stayed, and died — soon, probably, but *legally*, on paper, in a courtroom performing the last useful act of my career: refusing, on the record, to recognize them. Every tyrant needs an old man to condemn. It is the one role left that I can play without rehearsal.

The second draft began: *My dear Ilse — Forgive the deception, but you inherited your mother's talent for argument and I cannot afford to lose this one.*

In that version I went with her. I let the Republic's clause die unwitnessed, let the signature go dark, chose one girl over one idea. It was shorter. Six pages. It contained the sentence *"You are not an abstraction"* and I had to stop writing for some minutes after it, because my hand had betrayed me into honesty.

Between the drafts I heard the house settle. This residence used to hold a staff of fourteen and echo with consequence; now it holds five of us and echoes with weather. Somewhere below, Ilse was arguing with Tomas about inventory lists — I could hear the rise and fall of it through the floorboards, her voice sharp as a tack, his patient as rope. Somewhere below, Casper was presumably not sleeping, because that woman treats sleep like an unauthorized expenditure. Somewhere below, Marta was baking bread at midnight because tomorrow's flour would keep worse than today's, and because, I suspect, the smell of it is the last weapon against despair available to this household.

Five people. Two berths. One signature.

I looked at the two drafts lying side by side, and I understood that whichever I kept, I was choosing the shape of my daughter's grief, and there is no version of that choice a man can live inside comfortably. Leave her fatherless with a cause. Or carry her fatherless father to safety and let the cause starve. Grief in one flavor or the other; grief guaranteed; the only variable remaining is whether she gets to hate the regime or hate me.

Then I noticed the third possibility, the one that had been sitting in my ledger all evening wearing someone else's name.

The berth did not have to carry *me* in either direction. Names are fungible. Papers are forgeable. Casper Iversen spends her life making nobody out of somebody, and I have watched her do it with a precision that borders on artistry. A manifest is just a list, and I have spent forty years negotiating the contents of lists. There might be an answer that is not the first draft and not the second — a third document, drafted not by a father and not by an ambassador but by both, badly, at midnight, gambling.

I am too old for gambles. That is exactly why I am the one who can afford them. What can their prisons mean to a man whose remaining assets are a fountain pen and a death sentence already issued?

* * *

I burned both drafts in the hearth.

It took longer than I expected. Eloquent paper resists fire the way eloquent men resist retirement — page by page, curling, flaring up in protest, insisting on its own importance right to the ash. Eleven pages of leaving. Six pages of staying. I fed them in alternately so the flames couldn't tell which argument was winning, and for a moment the heat made shadows on the wall like two old men arguing, and then there was only one shadow, mine, doing what shadows do.

The ash smelled like my father's study. Everything smells like my father's study lately. I choose to believe this is memory rather than decay.

What remains is the third path, unwritten, requiring a courier's hands and a daughter's ignorance and a clerk's silence and Marta's bread, and possibly a miracle, though in my experience miracles are merely coincidences that arrive pre-negotiated. Nineteen days until Havnvik. Five souls. Two berths, unless I spend the last coin of my credibility making a third out of paperwork and nerve.

Tomorrow I will begin moving the pieces. Tomorrow there will be papers to alter and conversations to conduct with people I love, deceptions to construct out of tenderness, the whole familiar masonry of protecting people from the truth.

Tonight there is only one more thing to record, and it is this: as I ground the last of the ash into the grate, I heard Casper's step on the stair — she walks like snowfall, but I have learned her weight — pause outside my door. A hesitation. Three seconds, perhaps four. Then she continued on to her room, and I heard no more from anyone until morning.

A courier, trained all her life to notice sealed things, does not hover outside a closed door without reason.

I wonder, now, what she was carrying past it.
