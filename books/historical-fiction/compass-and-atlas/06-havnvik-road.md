## 6. Havnvik Road

I had always assumed that if I died in service to the Republic of Veldenmark, it would be behind a desk — papercut sepsis, perhaps, or crushed under an avalanche of misfiled treaties. I had not imagined it would be in the back of a fish wagon, wearing the identity of a dead man named Oskar Vell, whose papers described him as a herring merchant of "middling height and unremarkable features."

The forger had been generous. I am, at best, a man of unremarkable features.

We left the capital before dawn on the fourth of Deepmonth, packed into Marta's cousin's wagon among barrels of salted cod that smelled like they had died twice. The ambassador rode up front as "Uncle Brandt," a retired schoolmaster visiting family in Havnvik before his health finished what the Republic had started. Ilse was his niece. Casper and I were the hired help. Marta was everyone's aunt, because Marta has the kind of face that makes "everyone's aunt" seem not just plausible but inevitable.

The false papers were good work. That should have comforted me. Instead I spent the first thirty kilometers cataloguing everything that could go wrong with them, which is what I do instead of praying.

"The ink on your transit seal is wrong," I told Casper somewhere past the second river crossing, keeping my voice under the creak of the wheels. "It's too fresh. Nobody stamps a travel pass that neatly unless they want it examined."

"Then we'll hope the checkpoint men are paid by the stamp, not by the examination." She said it lightly, but she'd already turned the papers once in her fingers, checking the grain of the light on the seal, and I saw her do a thing I had never seen her do in two years of watching her: she hesitated.

Casper Iversen does not hesitate. She delivers. She vanishes. She stands at the edge of rooms like a coat hung on a hook — present, useful, unnoticed. Hesitation was not in her repertoire. I filed it away with all the other things I was choosing not to think about, in the drawer where I kept the sound of Ilse crying softly in the night and the way the ambassador had stopped writing letters he used to write every week.

The road to Havnvik follows the coast when it's honest and cuts inland through pine country when it isn't. We took the dishonest route. It cost us a day and gave us fog, and I have never been so grateful for weather in my life.

* * *

The checkpoint came on the third afternoon, where the coast road narrows between a granite bluff and a frozen marsh. You could see it from half a mile off: two wagons angled across the lane, a braziers' smoke standing straight up in the dead air, four soldiers in the grey-and-black of the Provisional Authority. New uniforms. Old faces, mostly — the same border men who had checked papers under the Republic, now wearing different colors and checking them harder.

"Everyone breathe," Marta said, in the tone she used for telling us dinner was ready. "You are boring people. Practice being boring."

We practiced. The ambassador rehearsed his schoolmaster story with the quiet thoroughness of a man who had spent forty years making weaker arguments survive. Ilse sat with her hands folded and looked out at the marsh as if it personally interested her, which was very good acting, because Ilse has never found anything outside of a book or an argument interesting in her life. Casper became furniture. It is her gift. I honestly believe you could seat dinner guests on her without anyone remarking on it.

Then it was our turn, and the whole performance fell apart in ninety seconds.

Not because of us. Because of a boy.

He couldn't have been more than nineteen, this soldier, with the particular eagerness of a young man who has recently been given authority and a list of things to be suspicious of. He took our papers one at a time, reading each with his finger moving under the lines, and I watched him arrive at my page and stop.

"Oskar Vell," he said.

"That's me," I said, in what I hoped was the voice of a middling herring merchant. It came out as the voice of a clerk doing an impression of one. Somewhere behind me, Marta exhaled through her nose.

"You're listed as registered in Kolvern district."

"I was. Family moved."

"Moved when?"

"Before the winter."

"Before which winter?"

And here is the thing about fear: it does arithmetic badly. I opened my mouth to answer a question that had no good answer, and the soldier's eyes changed — I saw it happen, the little brightening of a man who smells something — and he said, "Step down from the wagon. All of you. Papers stay with me."

The ambassador descended with the slow dignity of age, which helped, because old men are hard to hate and harder to suspect. Marta followed, indignant in the manner of aunts everywhere. Ilse came down last, chin high, and I wanted to tell her to lower it, because pride reads as guilt at a checkpoint, but her voice was gone from my throat.

The older sergeant — he had come over when the boy raised the papers, and there was frost in his mustache and boredom in his eyes — held my identity up to the grey light.

"This seal's been restamped," he said.

"No, sir, it—"

"It's been done over. Look at the bleed." He wasn't angry. That was the worst part. He was simply correct, the way the world is sometimes correct at the least convenient moment. "Where did you get these papers?"

Silence. The specific silence that comes before everything ends. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the cod barrels, and how we would never explain the cod, and how the history of the Republic of Veldenmark might therefore end in a ditch beside a fish wagon, and how I had not even finished transcribing the trade memoranda—

"May I?" Casper said.

She stepped forward — out of furniture-hood, out of invisibility, into the light — and she spoke a sentence. Not to the sergeant. To the boy. A sentence in a language that wasn't a language: four syllables, flat and unhurried, arranged in a rhythm I didn't understand but the boy clearly did, because his whole body changed, the eagerness draining out of him like water from a pierced barrel.

The sergeant turned. "What did she say?"

"Nothing," the boy said, too quickly. "It's nothing. Road clearance protocol. For courier traffic."

"Courier traffic," the sergeant repeated. He looked at Casper. She looked back at him with the flat patience of someone who has stood in many queues in many weather conditions and outlasted worse men than him. He looked at our papers. At the ambassador's bent shoulders. At Ilse, who was staring at Casper with an expression I could not read then and have not stopped trying to read since.

"Go on, then," the sergeant said finally. "Clear the road. And get those papers redone in Havnvik; they're rubbish."

We rolled through. Neither wagon behind us so much as jingled.

* * *

Nobody spoke for six miles. The fog came back and folded around us like it was embarrassed on our behalf.

It was Marta who broke it, passing bread back without ceremony. "Well. That went smoothly."

"That did not go smoothly," I said. "That went catastrophically, and then it went inexplicably."

"Inexplicably," Casper agreed, quietly.

I looked at her. She was sitting with her back against the cod barrels, hands loose in her lap, and she had the look of a person who has set fire to a room and is now calmly watching it burn. I had spent enough nights in the residence's study to know what her cipher books meant to her — not the books themselves but the permissions inside them, the phrases and countersigns that let her pass through a hostile country like a rumor. Some of those phrases were single-use. Some of them, spent, closed doors permanently. Some of them, spent, told the people on the other side of the door exactly who she was.

"Which one was that?" I asked.

She didn't pretend not to understand. That's another thing about Casper: she wastes no effort on lying to people inside the circle, possibly because lying is expensive and she budgets. "A clearance phrase. Harbor-courier class."

"And spending it means?"

"It means when the harbor office reviews its logs — and they review their logs — they'll find a phrase burned at an inland checkpoint, three days before any scheduled courier run. They'll know someone used it who shouldn't have. They'll start looking for the someone."

"How many people know what that phrase sounds like?"

"In Veldenmark? Perhaps forty. In the new regime's service—" she paused, doing sums she had clearly already done, "—most of the ones worth worrying about."

"So they'll be looking."

"They'll be listening." She took the bread from Marta and ate a piece with perfect calm, and that was the moment I understood the thing I have carried ever since. She hadn't hesitated at the checkpoint to weigh whether our lives were worth her safety. She had hesitated because she was counting — counting how much of herself the road to Havnvik would cost, item by item, and deciding to pay it anyway, the way you pay for something you cannot afford when the alternative is worse.

She would never mention it again. I want that recorded. In all the months after, through everything that came at the quay and after the quay, she never once invoked it, never let it purchase her so much as a softer word. Sacrifice performed without applause, and without receipt.

Ilse moved down the wagon — the wagon swayed, the cod complained — and sat beside her, and said nothing, and put her hand over Casper's where the soldiers couldn't see. Casper let her. That was all. It was the loudest thing I have ever witnessed.

The ambassador, up front, did not turn around, but I saw his shoulders come down from around his ears, half an inch. He knew too. Ambassadors always know the exchange rate.

* * *

Havnvik announced itself the next morning as a smell — fish, tar, woodsmoke, the sea underneath it all like a bass note — and then as a skyline of cranes against a sky the color of dishwater. A neutral port is a strange thing: a city pretending the world hasn't ended, selling rope and news to both sides and judging neither. Flags of four nations hung over the harbor basin. None of them were ours.

We came down the hill road into the tangle of streets above the quays, and I felt the peculiar lightness of a man arriving somewhere safe, and the immediate, professional suspicion of that feeling. Safe is a story checkpoints tell you before they check you.

We took rooms at an inn called the Anchor's Daughter, chosen by Marta from intelligence I have never fully learned, and the ambassador paid in coin counted out with schoolmasterly precision, and for one evening we almost managed to be boring people. Marta cooked. Ilse argued politics with a Swalese shipping clerk until he surrendered. Even the ambassador laughed twice, which I logged as a historical event.

Casper went out alone "to make contact with the ship's agent," and came back after dark, and she was composed, and she was polite, and she poured herself tea with a steady hand.

But she left the outer door barred and then stood looking at it for a long moment, as if expecting it to open.

And the next morning, while Ilse and I argued about luggage in the entryway, a man came up the inn's steps whom I recognized instantly and completely, because I had seen his portrait pinned above my own desk for a year: Aldric Venn, formerly of the Foreign Office, currently of whatever the Provisional Authority called its smiling department.

He looked straight past me, past Ilse, directly at the stairs where Casper had just appeared.

"Sister Iversen," he said pleasantly. "What a small world the coast is."
