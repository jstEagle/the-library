## 7. The Faction's Offer

Havnvik in winter smells of fish, tar, and the particular desperation of people waiting for ships that may never come. I had been in the port four days, lodged above a chandlery whose owner asked no questions because I paid in coin and silence, both of which I had in short supply. The Brandts were hidden two streets over, in the back rooms of a widow who owed Marta a debt from some earlier catastrophe neither of them would describe to me. I spent my days walking the quays, counting cranes and gun barrels, learning which cafés the harbormaster's clerks frequented and which ones the men with new armbands preferred.

I tell myself it was tradecraft. Walking the same routes, wearing the same coat, being nobody — my oldest discipline. But there is a version of the story where I was simply afraid to sit still, because sitting still meant thinking about manifests, and manifests meant arithmetic, and arithmetic meant that whatever I calculated, someone I loved would come out on the wrong side of the equals sign.

So I walked. And on the fourth day, walking, I made the mistake of stopping.

There is a coffeehouse on the Sildergade called the Anker, distinguished from its neighbors by nothing except a brass anchor over the door gone green with salt air. It was warm, and I had been cold for a week in ways that had nothing to do with the weather. I went in. I ordered coffee that tasted of roasted acorns and war economics. I sat by the window where I could watch the door, because even when I permit myself a small cowardice like sitting down, I do not abandon all my principles.

Rainer Voss walked in twenty minutes later.

I knew him before I saw his face — by the walk, actually, that rolling seaman's gait he'd never earned and never shed, acquired at some prewar posting and worn ever since like a stolen coat he'd grown into. We had shared a cipher room in the Foreign Ministry for three years. He'd taught me the Veldenmark double-transposition when I was nineteen and still believed that systems held because they were well-made. He'd brought sweet buns on Fridays and remembered everyone's mother's name. Everyone had liked Rainer. That was precisely what made him good at his work, and everyone had always known, distantly, that his work was knowing things.

Now the lapel of his excellent wool coat bore a small enameled pin I did not recognize, which told me everything the pin itself did not.

"Casper Iversen," he said, sliding into the chair across from me uninvited, as if we were resuming a conversation from Tuesday. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"Times change." He signaled the waitress without looking at her — two more coffees, the gesture said, the ease of a man who had already bought this establishment in some spiritual sense. "I've missed you at the Ministry. The new people are very enthusiastic. Enthusiasm is exhausting."

"I hear you've joined the enthusiasts."

"I've joined the winners." He said it without apology, and somehow that was worse than shame would have been. Shame I could have used. "There's a difference, these days, and it's the only difference anyone checks."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about temptation: it does not arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like a colleague. It brings you coffee you didn't have to pay for. It remembers your mother's name.

"The ship," Rainer said, stirring three sugars into his cup as though sweetening could improve acorns. "The *Meridian*. Calls at Havnvik Thursday, berths for twelve, priority list already drawn. Your name isn't on it."

"No one's name is on anything until the harbormaster signs it."

"Don't be coy, it doesn't suit you. You were always the straightest arrow in the building." He leaned back, and the chair creaked its approval of him. "Here's the situation. The Provisional Authority wants Brandt. Not dead — dead Brandts make martyrs, and martyrs make speeches, and we have enough speeches. They want him tried. Properly. Legal paper, legal rope, foreign observers invited so no one can say we're butchers." He smiled, and it was the old Friday-morning smile, the bun-sharing smile, and I wanted to put my fork in it. "For a trial we need his ciphers. His books, his keys, his correspondence ledgers. Proof he ran intelligence operations against the lawful government of Veldenmark while wearing its flag. Without the books, he's just an old man with opinions. With them, he's a traitor with a filing system."

"And I'm supposed to hand them to you."

"I'm supposed to offer," Rainer said mildly. "You're supposed to decide. Same division of labor as always."

He laid it out then, and I will grant him this: he laid it out beautifully. Immunity papers, full amnesty, retroactive to the first day of the provisional government. A berth home on the *Meridian* or any ship after — passage out of this frozen purgatory whenever I liked, under whatever name I liked. Money, though he mentioned it last and lightly, as if money were an embarrassing afterthought rather than the point. In exchange, the cipher books. Nothing else. No names of household staff, no testimony against Ilse, not even my presence at a trial.

"We don't need you," he said. "That's the mercy of it. We need paper."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then eventually someone less friendly finds you, and they won't offer coffee, and Brandt loses his books anyway plus whatever else falls out of your pockets when they search you." He spread his hands. "I'm not threatening you, Casper. I'm reading you the weather."

I looked out the window at the harbor. Grey water, grey sky, the masts of ships standing against it like a forest of thin black trees, every one of them a promise somebody might break. Somewhere behind me, two streets over, an old man was probably doing sums at a kitchen table, and a young woman with sharp eyes was watching him do them, and neither of them knew that I was at this moment being priced.

"How did you find me?" I asked.

"You crossed two checkpoints between the capital and here using a phrase from the consular rotation." He shrugged. "Someone wrote it down. Someone always writes it down. Then a woman matching Ilse Brandt's description bought winter coats for three people in a market town that sells coats to maybe ten travelers a month. You may be nobody, Iversen, but you're nobody traveling with somebodies."

I filed that away — *they know the household travels together; they know roughly how many* — and hated that even now, across a table from him, part of my mind kept working. Old habits. Rainer had given me those too.

* * *

"Tell me the truth," I said. "If I give you the books. What happens to him?"

"A trial."

"Rainer."

"A trial," he repeated, holding my eyes, and I will say this for the traitors of the world: the good ones can hold your eyes. "A real one, with defense counsel we'll allow and evidence we won't forge. He's sixty-eight years old, he committed his crimes in writing, and he'll hang by law instead of by lamppost. In the capital right now, that's a generous sentence. Men who look like me are shooting men who look like him in cellars, and no one writes it down. This way, there's paper. There's procedure. There's a record someone can appeal to someday, when the weather changes again." He turned his cup a slow quarter-circle on its saucer. "You think I came here to hang an old man. I came here because the alternative to me is men who think paperwork is for cowards."

And there it was — the whole rotten, seductive architecture of the thing. Because he was nearly right. Because I had seen the cellar-men too, or their aftermath, in the capital. Because *legally* is a word that has comforted more grieving families than *quickly* ever has, and I could not entirely explain to myself why.

Because, most of all, the books were not mine to trade, and the price of keeping them was not mine either, and yet every road out of that coffeehouse seemed to run through someone's neck.

"You taught me the double-transposition," I said. "Do you remember?"

"Autumn of '31. You learned it in nine days."

"It took me six. I let you think nine because you were proud of teaching it and I didn't want to take that from you." I set a coin on the table for my coffee, precise, unnecessary, a small ceremony of remaining myself. "You were the best of them, Rainer. That's why this hurts, and I want you to know it hurts, because I suspect you came here partly hoping it wouldn't."

His face did something complicated and quickly put away. "The offer stands until the *Meridian* sails. Thursday, tide at noon."

"I know when it sails."

"Of course you do." He stood, buttoning the expensive coat. At the door he paused, half-turned, and for a moment he was only a man from a cipher room who used to bring sweet buns. "Give up the books, Casper. Whatever else you're carrying — and you're carrying something, you always were — paper survives. People don't. I've watched a lot of people not survive lately. Save yourself. Let the old republic live in a book somewhere instead of in a grave."

Then he was gone into the grey, and I sat a long while with the cooling acorn coffee, doing the arithmetic he'd left me.

Because here is the thing he never understood about me, in three years of sharing a room: I am a courier. I carry things. It has never once been my job to open them, weigh them, or decide whether their contents justify the cost of their delivery. My whole moral life fits in one sentence: *what is entrusted to me arrives where it is meant to go.* The ambassador gave me the books before we left the residence — pressed them into my hands in the frostbitten study with a look I had taken, until this week, for simple trust.

Sitting in the Anker, I understood at last that it had been something heavier than trust. It had been confidence. He had known someone would come to buy them, and he had chosen me as the person who would not sell.

I left the coffeehouse and walked back through the fish-smell and the tar-smell, past the harbor bar where the *Meridian*'s advance agent was drinking with the harbormaster's clerks, and heard, in passing, what I was perhaps meant not to hear and certainly could not afford to ignore:

the *Meridian* had moved up her sailing. She would signal departure tomorrow morning, not Thursday noon.

Two streets over, a lamp burned in the widow's back window. Behind it, an old man and his daughter were spending what remained of our time together, and I had less of it than any of us knew, and one name to choose, and a former friend's offer folded in my pocket like a knife I had promised myself I would not open.

I climbed the stairs. Ilse opened the door before I knocked — she always did — and read my face in the space of a breath, because she always did that too.

"They found you," she said.

"Yes," I said. "And we sail tomorrow."
