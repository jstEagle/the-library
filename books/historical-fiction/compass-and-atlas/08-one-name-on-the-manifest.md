## 8. One Name on the Manifest

The ship announced itself the way death does in Veldenmark — not with drama but with paperwork. A boy in a fish-oil-stained coat came up the lodging house stairs at dawn, knocked once, and handed me a slip of paper: *SS Meridian, berth confirmed, departure at six bells. Manifest closes at noon.*

Noon. I sat on the edge of the cot and did the arithmetic that mothers do at funerals and generals do before offensives, which is to say I counted hours as if they were coins and I were deciding which beggar to pay.

Six hours until the manifest closed. One berth beyond Casper's official passage — that had been settled weeks ago, in the careful non-conversations my father and I had been having since Havnvik. And one of us was going to be on it.

I knew which one he intended. Fathers are not subtle; they only think they are. My father had been saying his goodbyes all week in the grammar of men who believe tidiness is a form of courage: he'd reorganized his papers twice, given Marta his mother's silver thimble, and last night he'd asked me to recite the names of my grandmother's sisters because, he said, "a person should keep those somewhere." Somewhere other than a sinking country, apparently.

So I went down to find him before he could arrange his own martyrdom without so much as consulting the committee of people who loved him.

* * *

He was in the little back room the landlady called the study, which meant it held a desk missing one leg, propped on a stack of hymnals. He looked up when I came in, and I watched him do the thing he'd done my whole life: assess, adjust, prepare to be reasonable. It is very difficult to fight with a man who is always preparing to be reasonable.

"You've seen the signal," he said.

"I've seen the signal."

"Then we should discuss—"

"No," I said. "You'll give some speech about how the republic needs its ambassador, or its ambassador's testimony, or whatever we're calling it this week, and I will interrupt you, and you'll say Ilse, let me finish, and I won't, and then we'll have wasted forty minutes we don't have." I pulled the hymnal-stack chair closer and sat, so that our knees nearly touched. "Papa. I know what you're going to do. I found out before we ever left the capital. I've known since the letter."

Something crossed his face — not surprise. Grief arriving on schedule, like a train he'd been expecting.

"You read it," he said quietly.

"I read it."

"And you said nothing."

"And you said nothing," I returned. "We are apparently a family of archivists. We file things and never open them."

He almost smiled at that. He had a particular smile for when I'd scored a point and he was proud and disappointed in equal measure; I'd been collecting them since I was seven. This one was fainter than most. The fuel for those smiles was running low.

"Listen—" he began.

"If you say *let me explain the political necessity*, I swear on Marta's cooking I will scream loud enough to bring the harbor patrol."

"Ilse." He took my hand. His hands had always seemed enormous to me, hands that could hold an entire diplomatic crisis, and now they were just old hands, cold at the fingertips, trembling very slightly in a way he would have been mortified to know I noticed. "I am seventy-one years old. I have eaten at the tables of nine heads of state, and I cannot remember a single meal. I have done the work I was put here to do, and most of it failed, and the parts that didn't fail are being burned in barrels in the capital square right now. What remains of use in me is testimony. Memory. If they kill me, they kill a signature on old documents. If they kill you—"

"Don't."

"—they kill everything you haven't done yet. That is not sentiment, Ilse. It is accounting."

"It is cowardice wearing accounting's coat," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because his face didn't harden the way I wanted it to. It softened. That was the terrible thing about my father; he could absorb any cruelty you dealt him by simply agreeing that your pain was legitimate.

"Perhaps," he said. "I've considered that. At three in the morning I consider it quite thoroughly." He squeezed my hand. "But here is what I've decided about decisions like this one. There is no version where I get to be right. There is only the version where I get to be honest. So I will tell you honestly: I want you on that ship. I want it selfishly, the way I wanted you to come home safe from every foolish errand of your youth. And I will not argue you onto it, because an argument can be won, and this mustn't be won by anyone. It has to be chosen."

"Papa—"

"There's someone who has to choose too," he said. "And she's standing outside the door trying very hard to look like she isn't."

* * *

Casper came in with the studied casualness of a woman who had absolutely been listening at the door, and had decided honesty about it was cheaper than being caught. She carried the manifest copy — she always carried papers now, tucked against her ribs like a second heartbeat — and she wouldn't quite meet my eyes.

"The harbor master's clerk wants confirmation by noon," she said to the room in general. "One name. In ink."

"One name," my father said, "for one berth." And then he did the thing that undid me more than any speech could have: he stood, kissed the top of my head the way he did when I was small enough to be lifted, and walked toward the door. "I'm going to see if Marta will feed me something before noon. A man shouldn't make other people's hardest choices on an empty stomach." At the threshold he paused. "A courier knows how to carry things. But choosing what to leave behind — that's the whole profession, in the end. Both of you know that better than anyone." And he was gone, taking his reasonableness with him, leaving us alone with the worst piece of paper in Veldenmark's short history.

The silence between Casper and me had a texture I'd learned by heart over three years — the silence of two people circling a fire neither will admit to warming their hands at. Now it was full of the ship's horns in the harbor, the gulls screaming their idiot commentary.

"You heard him," I said finally. "He won't take it. He'll stand on this dock until they sail if he has to."

"I know."

"So one of us writes his name and one of us writes mine, and whichever way it goes, one of us is wrong forever."

"Yes." She set the manifest on the desk. Her hands were steady. Casper's hands were always steady; it was one of the first things I'd loved about her, the stillness of her, as if the whole country shaking apart couldn't reach past her wrists. "Ilse."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do your courier voice. Don't tell me it's simple arithmetic, that he's worth more to the republic alive, that I'm — that I'm just—"

"You were going to say *just his daughter*." She looked at me then, straight on, and her voice wasn't the courier voice at all. It was the one from the candlelight night, the one I'd kept folded up inside me through checkpoints and false papers and the whole long gray road to this port. "There is no *just*. Not with you. There never was." Her jaw tightened. "That's why I hate this. Every professional instinct I have says write his name. Every other part of me — which I swore I'd never let near this work — says write yours and burn the rest of the world."

"Then don't ask me to decide," I whispered. "Please. You're the courier. Choosing is your whole profession. He said so himself."

"That's exactly why I can't," she said. "Because I could choose. God help me, I could write that name in ten seconds and live with it the way I've lived with everything else I've carried. But you'd spend your life knowing I chose for you. And I'd spend mine knowing I took your father with my own hand." She picked up the pen. Held it out to me across the desk, nib first, like a duelist offering the choice of weapons. "So here's what I propose. Neither of us chooses alone. We both hold the pen. Whatever name goes down — we wrote it together. Both of us. All three of us, really, because he's made his choice too, even if he's pretending it's Marta's cooking he's interested in."

My hand closed over hers around the pen. Ink hung at the tip, dark as the water in the harbor.

And that was when Tomas burst through the door without knocking, white as the foam on the breakwater, and the words came out of him in a single breath:

"The Meridian's manifest — there's a problem — Casper, they've posted it — your name isn't on it. Someone struck your passage. Your berth doesn't exist anymore."
