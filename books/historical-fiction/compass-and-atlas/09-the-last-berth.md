## 9. The Last Berth

The manifest clerk at Pier Four had a cold and a stamp, and of the two, the stamp was the more dangerous. He sat behind a wind-scoured window in a booth the size of a confessional, breathing his illness onto every document that crossed his desk, and I stood in line with three other passengers watching him work and thinking, with a calm that surprised me: *I have forged documents for a dying republic for six years, and now I must do it faster than a man can sneeze.*

The ship was called the *Meridian Star*, which was optimistic naming for a neutral freighter with two cabins and a hold full of tinned herring. She sailed on the morning tide. The harbor clock said we had ninety minutes.

My berth was secured — passage under the name Vinter, Casper Vinter, courier, authorized by an office that no longer existed to represent a state that had eleven days left to breathe. That paper would get me aboard. It would not get Ilse aboard. It would not get her father aboard. For that, I needed arithmetic, forgery, and luck, in roughly that order.

Berths were listed by name, one per soul. The ambassador's papers identified him as Ambassador Brandt, and the new regime had learned to read. Ilse's papers identified her as his daughter, which was worse than reading her name aloud; it was a confession of relation. Neither could travel as themselves. Both could not travel as one person.

Unless, of course, they could.

My cousin Anders Vinter had died of consumption in the spring of the war's second year, in a town whose records had burned the following autumn, in one of those administrative fires that happen when men are fleeing and take only what burns well. Anders had been twenty-three, unmarried, unphotographed, and mine to remember or misremember as circumstance required. Circumstance now required that Anders Vinter be alive, thirty-one years old, and boarding the *Meridian Star* with his traveling companion — a cousin from the coast, unwell, given to keeping her veil down.

One berth. One dead man. Two travelers who would spend four days at sea being, alternately, the same person.

It was not a plan that survived daylight. It only had to survive the gangway.

* * *

I found them in the shadow of the fish sheds, where Marta had established what she called a picnic and what anyone else would have recognized as a wake with bread. Tomas stood at the corner of the building doing his impression of a man loitering, which he had practiced so often this winter that he had actually become good at it. Life teaches you the strangest skills.

Ambassador Brandt held the forged papers I'd made the night before — Anders's life, written in my steadiest hand — and studied them for a long moment.

"Anders," he said. "You've given me a young man's name."

"You'll wear it better than he did."

Ilse laughed — one sharp breath through her nose, the laugh she used when laughing properly would cost too much. She stood close to her father with her hand on his sleeve, and I saw what she had seen in that room with the manifest list, what she'd told me in the dark of the Havnvik safehouse two nights ago with her voice flat as slate: *He means to stay. He's already decided. He gave me the choice like it was a lesson.*

A courier should know how to choose. So he'd said. And then he'd sat back and waited to see if his daughter loved him enough to let him die, or himself enough to leave him. A cruel gift, delivered gently. Old men are capable of astonishing cruelty when they're being brave.

Well. Two could play at handing people impossible choices.

"Ambassador," I said, "when you board, you are Anders. You do not answer to anything else, you do not correct the clerk, you do not make any observation about herring. Ilse is your cousin. She is ill. She does not speak."

"I resent that," Ilse said.

"You speak beautifully. That's the problem. You argue like other people breathe." I looked at her father. "Sir. This works only if you both go."

"Both," he repeated, mildly, as though testing the word for cracks.

"Both." I took a breath and committed myself to the lie in front of witnesses, because witnesses make lies heavier and harder to drop. "There is one berth beyond mine. One. I struck a bargain yesterday with the purser — a shared cabin, two souls under one name, paid in cipher books he can sell to three governments. It's arranged. It is done. Argue with me on the quay and I will forge your signature out of spite."

None of that was true except the last part, which I intended to make true within the hour by methods the purser would learn about posthumously to his career. The ambassador looked at me for a long time. He was not a fool; he had been a diplomat for forty years, which meant he had spent forty years recognizing exactly this tone, the tone of a junior official announcing that everything is arranged precisely because nothing is. His eyes went soft and terrible, and I understood that he knew, that he perhaps had known since Havnvik Road, and that he was going to let me have my lie because refusing it would break something none of us could afford to break this morning.

"Then I am Anders," he said quietly. "Until the tide."

Marta pressed a bundle of bread into Ilse's hands and did not trust herself to speak, which for Marta was a eulogy. Tomas hugged the ambassador awkwardly, the way boys hug fathers who are not theirs but might as well be.

And then there was nothing left between us and the quay but the walking.

* * *

I will not describe the pier in detail, because I have spent months trying not to dream about it and describing it feeds the dream. Wind off the water with iron in it. Gulls arguing over the herring crates. The *Meridian Star* low in the water, her rail lined with silhouettes of people who had also spent their savings on arithmetic. The regime had posted two men at the foot of the gangway with a list and a lantern, though it was noon — the lantern was theater, and effective theater, because a man checking names by lamplight in daylight believes his own authority completely, and belief is contagious.

I went first. Vinter, Casper — courier, official passage, papers in order. The man with the lantern barely looked up. My name meant nothing to him. Being nobody is a profession, and I have always been excellent at my profession.

Then I walked past the booth with the sneezing clerk, and while he bent to blow his nose I turned the manifest page toward me, dipped his pen, and wrote.

Anders Vinter, one berth, paid. Below it, in smaller script, the annotation clerks use and never check: *with traveling companion, invalid.* I struck through my own name — Casper Vinter, cancelled, passage forfeit — in a firm hand, the hand of a woman who had chosen this, because the worst forgeries are the hesitant ones and I could not afford to hesitate even in ink.

My berth. Six years of checkpoints and silence and frostbite earned in service of a republic that was about to stop existing, purchased finally into a single square meter of deck on a northbound ship — and I crossed it out the way you'd cancel a subscription.

It took eleven seconds. Nobody applauded. That is how sacrifice usually goes; I recommend getting used to it early.

The purser would find no bargain waiting, only my forged authorization and the ambassador's remaining funds sewn into the lining of a coat I would never need again. Let him rage. Rage keeps a man warm at sea.

* * *

They came down the pier together, father and daughter, and I watched the transformation with something like pride. The ambassador had found, somewhere between the fish sheds and the gangway, the slightly apologetic stoop of a man who has been ill for years and bored of it. Ilse walked half a step behind him with her veil down and her hands folded, mute, demure — Ilse, who once argued a checkpoint sergeant into apologizing to *her*. The performance was flawless. I hated it. I loved it. Both things at once seemed to be the day's special.

At the gangway, the lantern man looked up.

"Name."

"Anders Vinter." The ambassador's voice was perfect — hoarse, unhurried, a man reciting his own name for the ten thousandth time.

The lantern man consulted his list. Somewhere behind me, on the far side of the fish sheds, Tomas knocked over a stack of barrels.

I want to say, in my own defense, that I had planned nothing of the kind. I want to say it, but Tomas caught my eye across the crowd as the barrels went down with a crash like artillery, and Marta's voice rose above the sudden commotion — shrieking about her spilled catch, demanding compensation from God and the harbor authority respectively — and I understood that they had planned it without me, the cook and the clerk, my strange brave family, because someone had to make sure the lantern man's attention had somewhere else to go, and they had decided, in the democracy of people who love without saying so, that it would be them.

Every head on the pier turned toward the fish, including the lantern man's, including his partner's. In the three seconds of that turning, the ambassador and his daughter went up the gangway like ghosts going home.

I heard the clerk call "Vinter, party of two!" and heard the lantern man wave them through without looking, because there were barrels rolling and a fishwife in high dudgeon and a boy apologizing in seven directions, and a dead cousin and his sick companion were simply not interesting.

The whistle blew. Twice, long, the sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

* * *

There is a moment on every quay when the rope comes off the bollard and the space between ship and shore becomes uncrossable. It happens slowly and all at once, the way the important things do.

Ilse was at the rail. The veil was up now — she was aboard, she was Ilse again, and she had found me in the crowd the way she has always found me, in any room, in any weather, as though I were lit.

We did not wave. Waving is for people who expect to be waved back at, and we were both professionals.

She put her bare hand flat against the rail, palm out. I put mine against the piling nearest the water, palm out, twelve feet of cold air and widening sea between our hands, aligned like bookends around the gap where a promise lived. Her mouth moved — three words, or two, or one, I have decided not to choose among them, I keep all three versions like coins from different mints — and then the current took the hull and the gap grew and grew, and the *Meridian Star* went north with everything I had ever done that mattered standing at her rail.

The ambassador did not look back. Good. Looking back is for people who regret their arithmetic.

I stood on the pier until the ship was a shape, then a smudge, then a rumor. The gulls resumed their arguments. Marta found my arm without being asked and anchored it, and Tomas appeared on my other side, barrel dust still on his coat, and none of us spoke, because there is a kind of grief that needs quiet the way a wound needs pressure.

"Well," Marta said finally, to the water. "Spring comes early here, they say."

"It doesn't," I said. "But it comes."

We turned inland, three nobodies with one dead cousin between us and nothing in our pockets but a stolen cipher habit and a republic's worth of memory. Behind us the harbor clock struck one, indifferent and exact, keeping time for whoever would rule the country next.

I had a letter to write. Not yet — not for months, maybe. But I knew already the shape of it, the route it would take, the hands it would pass through, the port where it would land. Some dispatches you carry because you're paid. Others you carry because the alternative is drowning.

I started composing it before we cleared the fish sheds. The first word was easy. It was her name.
