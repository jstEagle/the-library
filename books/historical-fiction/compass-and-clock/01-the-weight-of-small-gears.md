## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The workshop measured nine feet by eleven, and Wendell Eriksen knew it the way a blind man knows a room—by touch, by echo, by the exact distance between every fixed point. He could stand at his bench with his eyes closed and reach for the burnisher without looking. He could find the tin of rouge by its smell alone, sharp as struck flint beneath the oil and brass. Nine feet by eleven, and in it he had spent six years learning to make time small enough to hold.

He was measuring it now, though not in feet. He was measuring it in portable things.

It was a habit that had come to him late, or perhaps early—he could no longer remember which—and it had arrived the way most unwelcome truths arrive: unbidden, on an ordinary evening, while doing something ordinary. He had been sorting his tools into their roll of green baize after a day's work on a widow's mantel clock, and he had noticed, quite suddenly, that everything he owned fit into two cases and one coat pocket. The tool roll. The loupe and its leather case. A photograph folded to the size of a playing card. Eleven crowns and forty öre in a tin that had once held throat lozenges. The debts did not weigh anything at all, but they took up more room than everything else combined.

He laid them out on the bench now, in the light of the single lamp, and looked at them the way a man looks at his own hand—as something familiar made briefly strange.

The tools first. His master's tools, mostly, inherited along with the shop and the name above the door when old Petersen had died three winters past. Wendell had added only four pieces of his own: a pair of pliers with jaws fine enough to lift a hair spring, bought secondhand from a drowned man's estate; a staking set missing two punches; a graver he had ground himself from an old file and never told anyone about, because a clockmaker who ground his own gravers was either very poor or very proud, and he was unwilling to be examined on which. The baize roll smelled of camphor and iron. When he closed his eyes and pressed his thumb against it, he could feel each tool through the cloth like bones under skin.

Then the photograph. It was the only photograph in the world, so far as he knew, that showed all three of them together: his father, tall and squinting against harbor light; his mother, whose smile in pictures always looked like she was holding back a better one; and himself, aged seven, standing slightly apart in the way of children who have already learned they are the afterthought of a family rather than its center. His father's hand rested on his shoulder in the picture. Wendell had spent years trying to remember whether that weight was real or only photographic—a gray smudge where a hand should be—and he had finally concluded that memory, like a movement, once tampered with could never be trusted to run true again.

His father had gone to sea the following spring and had not come back. *Lost off the Skagen shoals*, the letter said, in the careful language people use for drownings, as though the sea were a thief who might be shamed into returning what it took. Wendell had been eight. Astrid had been twelve, and had cried for exactly one afternoon before deciding, apparently permanently, that grief was a poor investment. Their mother had cried for eleven years, quietly, in the way of women who cry while working, and then she had died too, and the crying had stopped, and the shop had passed to him the way water passes down a hillside—without effort, without ceremony, without anyone asking if it wanted to go there.

And then the debts. He did not take these out. They stayed where they lived, in the ledger drawer beneath the bench, written in his own careful hand because no one else would write them down for him. Herr Kolvig at the chandlery. The coal merchant. Six months' rent owed to Fru Dahl upstairs, who pretended not to count the weeks and counted them anyway, he could see it in her face on the stairs. The total came to something just under a hundred crowns, which was not ruinous, which was in fact the precise amount of money that keeps a man alive without ever letting him begin.

Nine feet by eleven. Two cases and a pocket. That was the whole inventory of Wendell Eriksen, clockmaker, aged twenty-three.

He was still looking at it when the knock came.

* * *

Knocks at the workshop door after dark meant one of three things: a customer with an emergency, a creditor with a grievance, or Astrid with a scheme. He had learned to tell them apart by rhythm. Customers knocked apologetically, twice, soft. Creditors knocked with the flat of the hand, impatient, as though the door had personally wronged them. Astrid knocked exactly three times, crisp as a signature.

This knock was none of these. It was formal. Measured. Four knocks, evenly spaced, delivered by someone who had rehearsed the gesture on the walk over and considered it part of the message.

Wendell wiped his hands on the rag that hung from the bench vise—it was more grease than rag by now, a fossil of rags—and opened the door.

A boy stood on the step, perhaps fourteen, wearing a post office cap two sizes too large and an expression of tremendous self-importance barely containing its own terror. Behind him the street ran wet and orange under the gas lamp, rain beginning again in that fine, indecisive coastal drizzle that never quite committed to weather. The boy thrust out an envelope. Not a common envelope. Heavy paper, cream-colored, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and addressed in a hand so upright and disciplined it looked typeset:

**HERR WENDELL ERIKSEN, CLOCKMAKER — BY HAND ONLY**

"Sign here," the boy said, producing a board and pencil with the flourish of a customs officer.

Wendell signed. The boy examined the signature, found it acceptable, touched his cap, and departed at a pace calculated to suggest urgency without requiring him to actually run in the rain.

Wendell stood in the doorway a moment, the envelope cold and heavier than paper had any right to be, then shut the door and carried it to the bench like something that might go off.

He broke the seal with his thumbnail. Inside were two sheets. The first was brief, and read:

*To Herr Eriksen, Clockmaker.*

*The Observatory at Vesterhavn requires the services of a journeyman skilled in the repair of precision regulators. Our master clock has developed faults which local craftsmen have proven unable to correct. You are recommended to us by correspondence with the late Herr Petersen's trade references.*

*Terms: thirty crowns per month, lodging provided, duration estimated at one month, possibly longer. Travel paid. Reply by return post to the undersigned.*

*Halvard Eriksen, Keeper of the Observatory, Vesterhavn*

Wendell read the name twice. Then a third time, holding the page closer to the lamp, as though the letters might rearrange themselves under scrutiny and become someone else's.

Eriksen. Halvard Eriksen.

He knew the name the way you know a word in a language you don't speak—recognized its shape without possessing its meaning. His mother had spoken it exactly once in his hearing, and only once, and never afterward, in that tone she reserved for things too heavy to lift twice. It had been a winter evening; he remembered the stove ticking; he remembered her hands going still over her mending. *Your father's brother is keeper of the observatory at Vesterhavn,* she had said, to the mending rather than to him. And then, after a silence long enough for the kettle to sing: *We do not write to him.* And that had been the whole of it—the entire history of half his blood, delivered in two sentences between stitches, and never mentioned again in eleven years of Sundays.

Thirty crowns a month. He did the arithmetic before he could stop himself, the way a hungry man counts bread. Thirty crowns a month, plus lodging, plus travel, for a month or longer. His debts were just under a hundred. He could clear them by spring. He could clear them by February. He could pay Fru Dahl and stand in front of Herr Kolvig and watch the man's face change from pity to respect, and buy new punches for the staking set, and—

He caught himself and put the letter down, and stood with both hands flat on the bench, breathing.

Because there was a second sheet. He had almost missed it, folded small inside the first. This one was different—thinner paper, a different hand, hurried where the other was disciplined, and unsigned. Only five lines:

*Come quickly. Come before the year turns. Do not write ahead that you are coming, and do not tell your sister. There are things here that concern your father, and less time than you think.*

Wendell read it four times. The lamp hissed. Outside, the drizzle had thickened into honest rain, running down the window glass in long silver threads, and somewhere below in the street a cart went by, its wheels hissing on the wet cobbles, and the sound seemed to pass through the whole building and out the other side, leaving the silence behind it ringing.

There are things here that concern your father.

His father. Who had been dead for fifteen years, officially, in the way the sea makes men official. Whose grave was a date on a stone in the churchyard with no body under it, only the town's agreement to believe. Whose face Wendell could no longer hold steady in his mind—it drifted now, blended with the photograph, blended with the faces of strangers on the street who happened to be tall and squinting—and this drift frightened him more than he ever admitted, this slow erosion, this tide going out of him grain by grain until one day there would be nothing left to lose.

And yet. And yet some stubborn sediment at the bottom of him had never accepted the stone. Fifteen years, and he had never once been able to look at the harbor without scanning it—not for ships, he told himself, only out of habit, only because clockmakers notice motion—but scanning it, all the same, the way a dog scans a road for someone who isn't coming home.

Restless ambition, Astrid called it, when she called it anything. *You've got Father's itch,* she'd said once, not kindly, meaning the wanting-to-be-somewhere-else that lived in their father like a second heart. She said it as diagnosis. Wendell had never decided whether it was also warning.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud, not to Astrid, not to anyone, hardly to himself except in the last hour of sleepless nights: he was afraid that leaving meant disappearing. That the Eriksen men were a kind of tide themselves—that they went out and did not come back, that the sea or the world or whatever waited beyond the harbor mouth simply kept them. His father had left a wife, two children, a trade, a town, and had been erased by one letter. What did Wendell have that would resist erasure? Two cases and a pocket. A name on a door that wasn't even fully his. If he walked onto a steamer tomorrow, how long before the shop let rooms, before Fru Dahl rented his bench space to a cooper, before Astrid mentioned her brother the way one mentions a cousin in America—fondly, vaguely, in the past tense?

The fear sat in him now, cold and specific, sitting across the bench from the ambition like two brothers who did not speak to each other. Go, said one. Thirty crowns. Your debts. Your father's name in a stranger's handwriting, alive on good cream paper. Stay, said the other. Stay where leaving cannot happen to you twice.

*Do not tell your sister.*

That instruction alone should have sent the letter into the stove. Secrecy was Astrid's native element; she swam in it; and anyone who tried to keep a secret from Astrid Eriksen had already lost it. She would find out—she always found out—and she would want to know why he hadn't told her, and the not-telling would cost him more than any telling could. But the hand that wrote those five lines had known that. Had weighed it. Had decided the sister was a risk worth instructing against, which meant the sister was a factor at all, which meant—

Which meant whoever wrote it understood his family better than he did. That thought was not comfortable company.

Wendell picked up the first letter again and read the signature once more. *Halvard Eriksen, Keeper of the Observatory.* An uncle he had never met, writing to him in a hand like engraved plate, offering wages. And beneath it, unsigned, urgent, a stranger's whisper about his father and about time running out—*before the year turns,* the century itself turning with it, 1899 rolling toward 1900 like a great wheel coming around, and everyone in the papers saying the new century would change everything, as though centuries were clocks that could be wound forward past a fault.

He looked at his inventory on the bench. The tools in their baize. The photograph, face-down now, showing only its blank back with a corner worn soft from handling. The tin of coins. All of it fitting, all of it portable, all of it ready—which was either preparation or prophecy, depending on which brother at the bench you believed.

The rain worked at the windows. The stove ticked, cooling, marking time the cheap way, without a escapement worth the name. On the wall above the bench hung the shop regulator, Petersen's finest piece, which Wendell wound every Sunday evening and corrected every Sunday night, and which was, at this moment, losing four seconds a day no matter what he did to it—a small private failure he had never confessed to anyone, the one mechanism in his keeping that refused to be mastered.

He blew out the lamp, because the oil was nearly gone and oil was money, and sat in the dark with the two letters in his lap, feeling the weight of them. Paper weighed nothing. He had weighed everything else in his life; he knew precisely what nothing weighed.

In the morning he would write his acceptance. He knew it already, the way one knows the strike of midnight is coming from the pause before it—that gathering silence, that held breath in the works. He would write to Vesterhavn, and pack the baize roll, and lie to Astrid, badly, and take the steamer north into the fog.

But tonight he sat a while longer in the dark, listening to the rain, and let himself say the true thing once, quietly, to no one:

*I'm coming to find out what happened to you, Father. Whatever it was. Even if it's worse than drowning.*

Outside, far down the harbor, a ship's bell rang the hour—eight strokes, unhurried, indifferent—and the sound crossed the black water and climbed the wet streets and came through the workshop window like a summons from a larger clock, one he had been winding toward all his life without knowing it.
