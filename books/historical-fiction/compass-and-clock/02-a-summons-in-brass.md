## 2. A Summons in Brass

The steamer *Petrel* left on the morning tide, and Wendell Eriksen was aboard her with forty minutes to spare, which he regarded as a kind of moral victory. He had spent the night before in the manner of all his departures: packing, unpacking, repacking, weighing each object in his hands as though the scale of his life could be settled by arithmetic.

He traveled light. He always traveled light. A canvas roll of tools wrapped in oilcloth—pliers, pin vises, the good loupe with the cracked casing he had never replaced because his father's thumb had worn the crack smooth. A spare shirt. The photograph of a woman he could not entirely remember and a man he remembered entirely too well, folded into the lining of his valise where the cardboard had gone soft from years of being taken out and put back. And at the bottom, wrapped in a page of last Tuesday's shipping news, the letter from Vesterhavn Observatory, which he had read so many times the creases were beginning to fail.

*The master clock is failing. We require a journeyman clockmaker immediately. Wages generous. Apply to the Keeper.*

No signature. Only the observatory's seal pressed hard into the wax—a tower above three waves—and beneath it, in a different hand, smaller and more hurried, as if added after the ink had dried: *Come quickly.*

He had told himself, all through the sleepless dark, that the wages were the whole of it. Eleven months behind on the workshop rent. The supplier in Christiania who no longer extended credit. His landlady's face when she knocked, that particular gentleness people use on the drowning. The summons had arrived like a rope thrown to a man already underwater, and a man does not ask the rope where it has been.

But there was the other thing, the thing he did not say even to himself in the daylight: Vesterhavn was two hundred miles north along a coast his father had sailed, and the letter had come from an observatory whose keeper's name he knew, had always known, the way one knows a word in a language one pretends not to speak.

*Halvard Eriksen.*

His uncle. If uncle was the word for a man who had never once written, never once appeared at a funeral or a wedding or a christening, who existed in the family only as a closed door—the way his mother had spoken of him, briefly, bitterly, and then not at all. Wendell had been seven when he'd first worked out that the name in the old parish record, *Halvard, son of Eriksen*, belonged to someone still living, and he had asked his mother about it exactly once. She had been kneading bread. She did not stop kneading bread. "We don't go north," she said, and that was the entire geography of the matter, delivered into the dough with both fists.

She was eleven years dead now. His father, drowned—or not drowned—thirty years this spring. And Wendell was twenty-six years old, standing at the rail of the *Petrel* as she cleared the breakwater, watching his own town diminish into its smoke, carrying in his valise a letter signed by a man whose face he had never seen but whose handwriting, some animal instinct told him, he would recognize.

The gulls followed the ship out past the light, crying their flat grievances, and then they turned back, and the coast unrolled northward, gray rock and black pine and the occasional red of a farmstead burning alone against all that gray like a coal in ash.

* * *

He rehearsed deference the way he rehearsed everything: silently, repeatedly, with attention to the mechanism of it.

"Keeper Eriksen. I've come about the clock." No—too abrupt; a man who wanted information should not sound like a man delivering an invoice. "Keeper, sir—I'm Wendell Eriksen, of—" and here was the difficulty, the hinge on which every rehearsal caught. *Of what? Of the southern branch. Of your brother's line. Of the family you do not go north from.* He could not very well introduce himself as a stranger; the man would learn his surname within a minute regardless, would see it signed in the observatory ledger, would hear it spoken by the harbor officials arranging payment. And yet to announce himself as kin felt like laying a card face-up on the table before understanding the game.

Perhaps the name meant nothing anymore. Thirty years was long enough for a name to wear smooth, the way water wears stone—not away, merely featureless. Perhaps Halvard Eriksen would take the word *Eriksen* the way one takes any common patronymic, with the mild indifference of a man who has heard ten thousand names and kept none of them.

And perhaps not.

"You'll want to mind your hands on that rail," said a voice behind him. "She ices over by afternoon, even in September."

Wendell turned. The speaker was an old man wedged into the lee of the deckhouse, wrapped in a coat that had been fine thirty years ago and was now merely faithful—fur collar gone bald at the peaks, brass buttons polished by habit rather than vanity. He had the weathered, collapsible look of men who had spent their lives being folded small by wind. A leather satchel rested on his knees, held there by both hands, and Wendell's eye—trained by trade—went to it at once and noted the shape of instruments inside. Barometer case. Thermometer box. A meteorologist, then, or something near it, riding the coastal boats between stations the way parsons rode their parishes.

"Thank you," Wendell said.

"You're the clockmaker." It was not a question. The old man nodded at the valise, at the roll of tools whose outline showed through the oilcloth the way bones show through skin. "Word went round the quay this morning. *Petrel's* carrying a clockmaker north." He said it with a faint relish, as though the ship might otherwise have been carrying a bishop or a bomb. "To Vesterhavn."

"That's right."

"Hm." The old man looked out at the water for a while, and Wendell, having exhausted the social capacity granted him at birth, looked too. The coast went on performing itself: rock, pine, rock, the white smudge of a waterfall falling straight into the sea.

"They tell you anything," the old man said at last, "when they hired you?"

"The letter said the master clock was failing."

"Failing." The old man tried the word out and found it wanting. He patted his satchel. "I've kept stations on this coast for forty-one years. Forty-one years I've taken readings at Vesterhavn light, because the time ball up there sets the chronometers for every master from here to the Skaw. Forty-one years, and in all that time that clock has been the most honest object on the coast of Norway. Failing." He shook his head slowly, like a man declining a dish. "Clocks like that don't fail. They're not rivers. They're not hearts. Everything in them is made to be understood." He turned and looked at Wendell properly for the first time, and his eyes were pale gray and unhurried. "So when a thing like that begins to lose time, a thoughtful man asks himself: what changed?"

"Metal fatigues," Wendell said. "Pivots wear. Oil thickens—"

"Yes, yes." The old man waved this off with the air of a man swatting a competent fly. "And sometimes a file finds its way into a place a file ought not to be. I say nothing. I only say what I have seen, which is nothing, because I see weather, not clocks." He smiled then, suddenly, and the smile took twenty years off him and gave them back as mischief. "You'll do well up there, I expect. Young man, good tools, polite manners. Just remember one thing about Vesterhavn."

"And what's that?"

The old man leaned toward him, and lowered his voice, and the wind chose that moment to ease, so that the words came across clean as a coin passed palm to palm:

"Vesterhavn keeps its own time. Not Norway's time. Its own." He sat back, satisfied. "Everything up there runs to that clock—the ships, the tides, the money, the marriages. When that clock speaks, the harbor moves. So you'll forgive an old instrument-man for asking: what sort of place does it become, when the thing it trusts most starts lying to it?" He lifted his chin toward the bow. "Keep its own time, and keep its own secrets. That's Vesterhavn. The two are the same discipline, I expect. Keeping anything requires lying a little about how it's done."

Wendell laughed—one short breath of it, surprised out of him—but the old man did not laugh, and so the laugh died awkwardly somewhere around Wendell's second rib.

"You talk as if the clock were alive," Wendell said.

"I talk as if the town were," the old man said. "The clock is just the heart of it. And hearts, young man—" he tapped his own chest, twice, gently, "—hearts keep secrets from their owners all the time. Ask any doctor." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and touched his hat brim. "My station's next. You've another four hours to Vesterhavn, if the fog holds off. If it doesn't hold off—" he paused at the deckhouse door, "—well. Then you'll have longer to think about files."

At the little landing stage he disembarked, was received by a fisherman's wife with two children and a handcart, and was folded away into the life of the coast as though he had never existed. But his satchel stayed in Wendell's memory, and so did his questions, sitting there like grit in a bearing.

*What changed?*

Wendell stood at the rail a long time, and rehearsed again, under his breath, to the rhythm of the paddles.

*Keeper. I've come about the clock. My name is Wendell Eriksen.*

And if the old man at the desk goes white, or still, or stands up so fast his chair falls—

*Then my name is worth something to him,* Wendell told himself. *And whatever it's worth, I can spend.*

It sounded better in rehearsal than it felt.

* * *

The fog came for them an hour past noon, the way fog comes on that coast—not arriving but assembling, drawn up out of the sea in ropes and banners until the world was reduced to the width of the deck and the color of unwashed wool. The paddles slowed. The whistle began to cry at intervals, hoarse and formal, a voice calling names into an empty room. Sound grew strange: the creak of the rigging came from everywhere, the sea became a vast breathing thing just beyond sight, and once—Wendell was nearly sure—a bell struck somewhere off the port side, four slow strokes, though no chart showed a bell there, and the mate who was supposed to have heard it swore he hadn't.

Wendell went below, ate a heel of bread and a hard egg, and could not finish either. He came back up. The fog had not improved with his absence.

He found work for his hands instead, because his hands were wiser than the rest of him and knew what hands were for. He took out the tool roll and inspected each piece by feel in the dimness—jaws true, pivots clean, the tiny screwdrivers seated in their channels like organ pipes—and then he took out the letter and read it again in the gray light, and the words had begun to rearrange themselves the way words do when read too often.

*Come quickly.*

Quickly, after thirty years of silence. Quickly, for a clock that had kept perfect time since before Wendell was born.

Unless the clock had not kept perfect time. Unless the old man's forty-one years of readings contained, somewhere in their careful columns, a drift too small for any ship's master to notice but large enough for a keeper to see and choose not to correct. Wendell turned this over and could not make it mean anything, and set it down, and picked it up again. That was the trouble with a mind trained on mechanisms: it refused to tolerate an unexplained motion. Every effect wanted its cause, every gear its tooth, and somewhere in the dark of the fog ahead was a machine with a flaw in it, and the flaw was either in the brass or in the man, and he would know which within a day.

The afternoon died without visibly ending. The light simply thinned, and the fog took on a luminous quality, sourceless, as if the whole sky were a lit pane of frosted glass. Wendell paced the deck. The other passengers—two merchants, a family going to a funeral—had all retreated below or into the smoking room, and he had the wet world to himself, and was glad of it, and was lonely in it.

He thought about his father.

Not the drowning—that story was worn smooth as a beach pebble, told at funerals and firesides until it had no edges left—but the man himself, which was harder. Kristoffer Eriksen, who had wound the shop regulator every Sunday night of Wendell's early childhood, lifting Wendell up to see the pendulum through the case glass, saying—Wendell could still hear it, could still feel the man's beard against his ear—*Listen. That's the earth turning. You're holding the earth turning in your two hands.* Who had then, one April, boarded a northbound coaster with a leather case strapped under his coat, and never come back, and left behind a widow, a daughter of nine, a son of not-quite-four, and a shop full of clocks that all, eventually, stopped.

Astrid remembered him. Wendell had only the winding of the clock, the beard, the smell of pipe smoke and metal polish, and one other thing he had told no one, ever, because it sounded like a child's invention and because some part of him had always suspected it was the truest memory he owned: the last night, his father kneeling in the doorway in his coat, holding him by both shoulders, saying, *Whatever they tell you, Wendell—you listen, and you nod, and you remember whose you are.* And then he was gone into the dark, and the dark had kept him.

Whose he was. Wendell had spent twenty-two years assuming it meant *a clockmaker's son*. Lately, reading the word *Eriksen* in a stranger's urgent hand, he wondered.

"Vesterhavn!" The call came down from the bridge, muffled by fog to almost tenderness. "Light's reported! Quarter mile!"

Wendell went to the rail.

For a long moment there was nothing—only the fog, pressing close, and the ship's slowed heartbeat beneath his boots. Then the fog moved. It did not part so much as fail, thinning all at once like breath fading from glass, and out of it, impossibly high, impossibly near, rose the tower.

It stood on its black headland the way a candle flame stands in a dark room: not large, exactly, but commanding every other fact of the landscape. He saw first the light itself, a steady white eye above the fog line; then the gallery beneath it, dripping condensation; then the long shaft of the tower descending into rock; and clinging to the leeward side, half-hidden, the low stone buildings of the observatory—dome, offices, keeper's house—like a barnacle colony grown on a lighthouse's flank. And below the light, mounted on the seaward face where every ship in the harbor could read it, hung the time ball, a great black sphere on its mast, waiting, patient, ready at noon tomorrow to drop and tell a hundred captains what o'clock God's earth considered it to be.

The fog closed again. The tower vanished. It had been visible, Wendell calculated, for perhaps eight seconds.

Eight seconds, and already his heart was behaving badly, quick and shallow, the way it had behaved at seven years old outside the parish records room. Because he had seen, in those eight seconds, that the tower was exactly the shape of the tower he had drawn in the margins of his schoolbooks without knowing why—drawn over and over, a tall thin rectangle crowned with a light, until a schoolmaster rapped his knuckles for idleness. He had never been to Vesterhavn. He had never seen a picture of it. And he had been drawing it all his childhood like a prayer he didn't know the words to.

The bell rang forward. Fenders went over. Somewhere ahead, muffled to a murmur, the landing stage bells of Vesterhavn answered, and the *Petrel*'s whistle replied, and the two sounds argued politely in the fog about the exact position of things, which is all that sound is ever arguing about.

The ship slid in. Shapes assembled themselves out of the gray: stone quay, bollards, a craned arm, a knot of figures in oilskins. One figure stood apart from the others, at the head of the steps that climbed from the quay toward the observatory, and did not come down to meet the boat. Even at that distance, even through fog, Wendell could see that this figure was old and upright and still, and that it was watching the gangway with the fixed attention of a man counting something—passengers, perhaps, or seconds, or sins.

Wendell gathered his valise and his tools. His hands, he noticed, were perfectly steady. It was everything else that was shaking.

He stepped onto the gangway, and the fog received him, and the figure at the top of the steps raised one hand—not in greeting, he thought, but in the gesture of a man calling for silence before speaking—and Wendell began, out of thirty years of habit, to rehearse once more, silently, climbing: *Keeper, I've come about the clock—*

The old man spoke first. And the first words out of him, before welcome, before business, before even Wendell's name—which he could not yet have known, which no one on that quay had yet had time to tell him—were these, pitched low through the fog so that they reached Wendell alone:

"So. You have his eyes. Come inside, boy, before anyone sees whose they are."
