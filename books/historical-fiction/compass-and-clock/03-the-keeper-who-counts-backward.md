## 3. The Keeper Who Counts Backward

The road up to Vesterhavn Observatory was cut into the cliff like a file drawn across stone, and Wendell climbed it with his tool case knocking at his knee and the fog pressing close enough that he could taste its salt on his teeth. Below him the harbor lay dissolved in white, masts reduced to pencil strokes, the steamer that had carried him north already a rumor of smoke. Above him, where the tower should have been, there was nothing at all—only a darkness in the fog that might have been masonry or might have been weather.

He had rehearsed the whole of the climb. *Good morning, sir. Wendell Eriksen, journeyman clockmaker, of Copenhagen. You wrote concerning your master clock.* He had practiced the bow—not too deep, not too shallow—and the arrangement of his hands, one on the case handle, one loose at his side, a posture he hoped read as competence rather than desperation. He had rehearsed it because the letter had offered three months' wages in advance, and because men who accept such sums learn to make themselves small and smooth, like stones in a current.

What he had not rehearsed was the door opening before he knocked.

The keeper stood framed in the doorway with a lamp raised, and the light fell full upon his face, and Wendell's rehearsed greeting died somewhere between his lungs and his lips.

He knew that face. Not from photographs—he had never been allowed photographs—but from the mirror, distantly, the way one knows a melody heard once in childhood. The same long jaw. The same pale eyes set deep under a shelf of brow, though Halvard Eriksen's were rimmed now with the red of broken capillaries and ringed with skin gone gray as solder. He was older than Wendell's father would have been, or looked it; illness or guilt had done the work of extra decades. His hands, hanging at his sides, were enormous, spotted, and utterly still—the hands of a man who had learned never to gesture.

"You're the boy from Copenhagen," the keeper said.

"Wendell Eriksen, sir. Journeyman clockmaker. I came about—"

"I know what you came about." Halvard stepped back from the doorway without inviting him in, which was itself a kind of invitation, or a test. "Come in, then. Mind the threshold. The stone's worn hollow."

Wendell crossed it. Behind him the door closed with the softness of a thing well maintained, and he understood immediately that everything inside this tower would be maintained in exactly that fashion—oiled, adjusted, attended—except whatever it was that had gone wrong, which would be wrong in some manner the keeper did not wish to name over a threshold in open air.

*He knows,* Wendell thought, hanging his coat on a peg that was already polished smooth by decades of coats. *He looked at me and he knew.* And then, because there was no honest answer to that knowledge that did not open every door in his life at once, he thought: *And so do I.*

Neither of them said it. The silence between them took on the particular density of two people agreeing, wordlessly, to pretend.

* * *

The tower's interior climbed around the clock the way a hand closes around a pocket watch. Stairs spiraled up along the inner wall, and through openings in each landing Wendell could see the great machine itself—or its bones—a forest of rods and counterweights descending into the dark below like roots. The air smelled of oil and cold stone and, beneath both, something faintly medicinal. Camphor, he decided. Or digitalis. The keeper's breath had a labored quality, a hitch at the top of each inhalation, as though his lungs were negotiating terms.

"The clock stopped Tuesday last," Halvard said, climbing ahead of him with one hand flat against the wall. "Four minutes past six in the evening."

"Stopped dead?"

"Stopped *correctly.*" The old man paused on a landing and let the lamplight find his face again. "That's what troubles me. A failing escapement stumbles. A worn pivot drags. It loses time by degrees, ugly, unevenly. This—" he made a small, contemptuous motion with two fingers, "—this simply declined to continue. As if it had considered the matter and found the hour inconvenient."

Wendell said nothing. He was listening to the tower. Even stopped, the observatory was not silent; water moved somewhere below, and wind worked at the lantern room overhead, and beneath all of it ran a faint vibration, the building's own pulse, transmitted down through forty meters of masonry from the sea.

They came up into the clock room, and Wendell forgot, for a long moment, everything else.

The master clock of Vesterhavn occupied the entire circular chamber. Its movement stood taller than a man, brass and iron and steel arranged with the austere beauty of a cathedral's ribbing, and every surface of it had been kept clean enough to eat from. The pendulum hung motionless, its bob a great lens-shaped mass of iron, and above it the seconds hand of the standard regulator stood frozen at the position of four minutes past six, its tip trembling faintly whenever the wind leaned on the tower.

He had repaired hundreds of clocks. He had never stood inside one before.

"Your letter said the striking train seized," he managed.

"My letter said what letters say." Halvard lowered himself into a chair near the south window—a captain's chair, worn to the color of driftwood—and Wendell noticed how carefully the old man lowered himself, how he braced his left arm against the chair's armrest before committing any weight. "Look at it with your own eyes. That's why you're here. Eyes are cheaper than opinions."

So Wendell opened his case and laid out his tools on the velvet roll with the ritual care his master had beaten into him—files left, pliers right, screwdrivers ranked by blade width—and began the work of understanding.

The seizure, when he found it, explained nothing. The striking train had indeed jammed, a lifting pin caught against a bent gathering pallet, but the bend was trivial, the work of ten minutes, and it could not have stopped the going train. He corrected it anyway, because a clockmaker corrects what he finds, and moved deeper into the machine. He removed the escapement bridge. He lifted free the escape wheel, the heart-wheel, the component that permitted the entire mechanism to live one tick at a time—and held it under the lamp.

For a while he did not move at all.

The escape wheel's teeth are cut to precise angles; they must be, for each tooth catches the pallet and is released, catch and release, catch and release, the heartbeat of every clock ever built. On this wheel, four teeth—four, evenly spaced, which was why the fault had hidden so long—had been altered. Filed. Not crudely. Someone had taken a fine needle file to those four teeth and changed their faces by perhaps half a millimeter each, an alteration invisible to any eye but one trained to look, sufficient to let the pallet slip past without catching, sufficient to stop the train only at certain intervals, only sometimes, unpredictably.

It was beautiful work. It was wicked work. It was, Wendell realized with a sensation like stepping into cold water, the work of someone who understood escapements very well indeed.

"This wheel has been filed," he said.

Behind him, the chair creaked. When he turned, Halvard Eriksen was watching him with an expression that gave away nothing except, perhaps, exhaustion of a very old vintage.

"Yes," the keeper said.

"Then your letter was false, sir. Broken clocks don't require lies. Sabotage does." Wendell set the wheel down on the cloth with exaggerated gentleness, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not permit them the satisfaction. "Someone in this tower did this. Or someone who comes to it."

"Someone," Halvard agreed, in the tone of a man conceding a point in chess.

"May I ask why you didn't write *that* in the letter?"

The old man rose—slowly, one hand on the windowsill, breathing through what Wendell recognized as genuine pain—and walked to the great wheel housing. Standing beside the frozen machine, he seemed diminished, a man dwarfed by his own life's monument.

"Because," he said, "if I had written the truth, the firm would have sent an inspector, and inspectors ask questions about more than gears." He turned his head, and for one unguarded instant his pale eyes held Wendell's, and the recognition in them was naked, unmistakable, a flare of something between grief and terror. "They ask about the keeper. About his health. His fitness. Whether an observatory whose time governs every ship's departure from this coast ought to be trusted to a dying man alone."

*Alone.* The word fell strangely, and Wendell heard what it carried: *alone, with his secrets, with his filed gears, with whatever conscience had driven him to slow the world's business.*

"You understand escapements," Halvard went on, his voice recovering its dryness. "You'll stay in the east room. The housekeeper, Maren, will see you fed. You'll begin tomorrow at first light and you'll report to me and to no one else—to no magistrate, no harbormaster, no member of my staff—until I tell you otherwise. Those are my terms."

"And if I refuse them?"

"Then you may take the next steamer south and I will pay you a month's wages for your trouble, and the clock will remain as it is, and ships will sail from Vesterhavn on guesswork until someone drowns." He said it without drama, a ledger entry, and somehow that was worse. "I don't imagine you'll refuse. Your letter of introduction says you support a mother in reduced circumstances. Debt makes honest men flexible."

The cruelty of it was so casual that Wendell almost missed the other thing buried inside it—that Halvard Eriksen knew about his mother, knew about the debts, had known enough to write the summons in the first place. A stranger did not know these things. An observatory's clerk did not know these things.

An uncle knew them.

"Yes," Wendell said. "I'll stay."

"Good." Halvard crossed to the door, moving with the careful economy of a man rationing his strength. At the threshold he paused, his back to the room, his hand resting on the frame as though the tower were something he was holding up.

"The east room was aired this morning," he said. "Maren puts lavender in the linens. If the smell offends you, open the window and let the sea in instead. Most people prefer the sea."

Then he was gone, and his footsteps descended the spiral stair at the pace of a countdown, slower at each landing, until Wendell could no longer distinguish them from the sound of the water below.

* * *

He did not sleep, not really. He lay in the narrow bed in the east room with the lavender sharp in his nose and the sea working at the cliff below, and he assembled and disassembled the evening the way he would assemble a difficult movement, testing each piece for fit.

His father had had a brother. This was among the facts of Wendell's childhood, spoken rarely, usually by their mother after the second glass of aquavit on winter nights, delivered to the ceiling rather than to her children: *Halvard got the observatory. We got the sea. That was the division of the Eriksens.* And later—after the *Solveig* went down with all hands in '86, after the mourning cards and the closed shutters—his mother had written one letter to Vesterhavn. Wendell had carried it to the post office himself, nine years old, proud of the errand. No answer had ever come. He remembered standing at the post office window for weeks afterward, waiting to be called forward, learning early the specific humiliation of hoping for a letter that did not intend to exist.

Tonight, in the lamplit clock room, his uncle had looked at him and flinched behind his own face. Had known him. Had counted backward through thirty years to place that jaw, those eyes, and had elected—as one elects a gear ratio—to pretend otherwise.

Why summon him, then? Why send for the son and not merely the skill?

Unless the summons was not about the clock at all. Unless the filed teeth were not the only mechanism in this tower that had been deliberately altered to slow something down.

Wendell rose before dawn, dressed in the dark, and let himself up the spiral stairs with an unshaded candle. In the clock room the great machine waited, silvered with the first gray light coming through the lantern slats above. He sat down before the escape wheel he had removed the night before, still lying on its velvet, and examined it again in the clean honesty of morning.

Yes. Four teeth, evenly spaced, filed by a practiced hand.

But now, looking longer, he found what fatigue had hidden from him: a fifth tooth, near the wheel's far edge, scored differently. Deeper. Recent. The file marks shone bright against the older patina, bright as a fresh scratch on old furniture.

Someone had touched the wheel since the original sabotage. Someone had added to it—perhaps within days. Perhaps within hours of his own arrival.

He was still crouched there, candle in hand, when the tower bell above him rang seven, and somewhere below, faintly, he heard the front door of the observatory open and close, and a woman's voice call out that breakfast was on the table.

And on the stone step just outside the door, though he could not yet see it and would not understand it for another hour, something had been left in the night that would change the shape of his year entirely:

a wicker basket, rocking gently in the wind, wrapped against the cold.
