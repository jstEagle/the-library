## 5. The Housekeeper's Silence

Maren Kold had kept the observatory's kitchen for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned that the sea gave back nothing it took without first changing its shape. Driftwood came home as splinters. Men came home as rumors. And now, on a raw October morning with gulls arguing over the tide line, the sea had sent back a child.

She stood at the kitchen doorway with a kettle in her hand and watched Wendell Eriksen carry the wicker basket in from the step, his elbows held out stiffly from his sides as though the thing inside might be a live coal. The wool blanket had slipped as he lifted it, and she saw the crest before he did—the stag's head and the three stars, worked in blue thread gone soft with age—and something turned over in her chest like a key in a lock that had not been opened since she was young enough to believe locked things stayed shut.

"Fetch the doctor," she said, because saying anything else was impossible.

"It's breathing," Wendell said. "I checked. It's—she's breathing."

"I know she's breathing. I've ears." She set down the kettle hard enough to rattle the stove plate. "Doctor's house is below the churchyard. Go. Run, if your legs remember how. I'll have warm milk and warm linen when you're back, and we'll see what the night left us."

He went. She heard him take the shingle path at a half-run, boots slipping, and she stood alone in the kitchen with the basket on the table and the fog pressing at the windows like a face looking in.

The child was perhaps two months old, red-faced and furious with hunger, fists bunched against the cold. Maren unwound the blanket with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect, and there was the crest again, close up: the stag, the stars, the particular way the embroiderer had crossed her stitches at the antler points—left over right, always left over right, because Fru Eline Eriksen of the Søborg branch had insisted upon it and had boxed the ears of more than one maidservant for sloppiness.

Maren knew that stitching. She had been fourteen years old when she learned it, kneeling on a footstool in a drawing-room that no longer existed, in a house that had been sold twice over since.

The note was tucked beneath the child's body. *Forget the face.* Three words in a slanting hand, written fast, pressed hard enough to tear the paper at one stroke's end. She read it once, folded it along its existing creases, and put it in her apron pocket, and by the time Wendell came back down the shingle path with Doctor Bråten puffing behind him, Maren had milk warming, linen heating by the stove, and a face arranged into the plain blankness that nineteen years of service had taught her to wear like an apron.

* * *

The doctor pronounced the child underfed but sound—"whoever left her wanted her found somewhere warm," he said, peering over his spectacles at the quality of the wool—and prescribed patience, goat's milk thickened with gruel, and a woman's arms. He looked at Maren when he said the last part, and Maren looked at the stove.

"She'll need nursing or near enough," Bråten said. "There's the widow Sandvik in the lower town, if—"

"There's me," Maren said. "I've raised other people's children before. I'll manage."

Wendell started to say something—she saw the words form—but she cut him off with a look, and mercifully the boy had sense enough to swallow them. He was Halvard's nephew; that much had been obvious from the moment he stepped through the door two days past. The same long bones, the same pale lashes, the same habit of standing too straight as if apologizing in advance for taking up room. Halvard had gone grey around the mouth when the summons letter had been answered. Maren had noticed. Maren noticed everything in that house, and said nothing about any of it, which was the whole of her employment in a single sentence.

After the doctor left, Wendell stood turning his cap in his hands. "You recognized the crest," he said. "On the blanket. You went white as the crockery."

"I went pale because it's cold and I've been up since four."

"The crest, Maren. Eriksen. My family's crest, worked on a child's blanket, left on my uncle's doorstep with a note telling me to forget her face. You know what it means. You burned—" He stopped. "Where's the note?"

"What note?"

"The note that came with her."

Maren crossed to the stove, lifted the lid of the firebox, and stirred the coals with the poker, unhurried, letting him watch her do it. "Paper comes into this kitchen and paper goes into this fire," she said. "That's been the rule since before you were born, likely. Your uncle writes letters he wants nobody reading after him. It's a kindness to the dead and the living both."

"That wasn't yours to burn."

"No," she agreed. "It was nobody's. That's rather the point of burning."

He stared at her, and she saw the anger in him fighting with something younger and more frightened underneath it, and she felt for him, she truly did—but feeling was not telling, and she had promised, and promises made to dying women in drawing-rooms with the curtains drawn did not dissolve because a kind-eyed boy asked nicely twenty-six years later.

"The child stays," she said. "I'll feed her, keep her warm, mind her through the nights. You'll want to send word to a magistrate or a minister or some such, and you may do as your conscience directs. But the blanket, the note, and however she came here—those are not subjects for this kitchen. Are we understood?"

"You can't just decide that."

"Somebody has to decide things in this house. The men are busy with clocks." She picked the baby up, and the small weight settled against her shoulder with the terrible familiar rightness of bread dough rising under a cloth, and she hated herself a little for how easily her body remembered. "Her name will keep until someone's entitled to give her one."

* * *

Nursing was harder than remembering. The child would not take the goat's milk from the spouted cup, screamed herself purple at the spoon, and finally, exhausted, accepted it soaked into bread tied in clean muslin, sucking with a desperation that wrung Maren's heart sideways. She sat by the stove through the small hours with the bundle against her breastbone, feeling each pull of the tiny mouth like a tug on a line running back through the years to a house in Søborg where another infant had sucked bread-and-milk the same way, while a woman in a shawl paced the floor and a girl of fourteen held her breath in the corner and swore, later, with her hand on the woman's Bible, that she would carry certain things to her grave.

Fru Eline had made her swear twice, actually. Once when the child was born. Once, three years afterward, when the letters started coming and it became clear that the grave might arrive sooner than expected. *Whatever comes to this door, Maren, whatever name walks in wearing my family's crest—you hold your tongue and you hold your nerve. There are people who would use what you know. Kindness is not the same as courage, but sometimes they wear the same coat.*

Eline Eriksen had been eleven years in the churchyard at Søborg. Her promise had outlived her by nearly three decades, and here it was, standing in the kitchen at Vesterhavn at two o'clock in the morning, rocking a hungry child while the master clock ticked somewhere overhead like a slow enormous heart.

Because that was the other thing. The clock. Maren had lived under that clock for nineteen years and knew its voice the way a wife knows a husband's cough—and for months now it had been speaking wrong. A hesitation in the strike, a thinness in the tick. Halvard climbed to the tower room less and less; his heart was failing him by visible degrees, and the clock ran down like everything else in him. And then the young man had arrived, and within a day of his arrival Maren had found metal filings on the tower-room floor, bright curls of them, silver against the stone, and she had swept them up before Halvard could see and hidden them in a jam jar at the back of the pantry because she could not have told anyone, least of all herself, why.

She was a woman who kept jars of things she couldn't explain. Filings. Promises. A folded scrap of a memory of Eline's voice saying *forget*, though the note had said it plainer than Eline ever had.

The child slept at last near dawn. Maren laid her in the lined laundry basket by the stove and went to the window. Fog again, the harbor invisible, the masts of the herring fleet reduced to smudges. Somewhere out there ships waited to sail, and none of them could move until the observatory gave them the hour, and the observatory could give nothing until the boy upstairs coaxed the great machine back to honesty. Whole livelihoods hanging on gears and escapements, on the difference between one minute and the next. And here was a whole other life hanging on nothing so mechanical—a child set on a step like driftwood, timed to the exact days when a clockmaker's nephew would come calling.

Nobody could have known he'd come. Except somebody had arranged the timing anyway, or fortune had, or—

Or the letters. Maren went still at the window. There had been letters, years ago, that passed through her hands because Halvard's eyes were bad and he trusted her with the post. Letters from abroad with foreign stamps, and Halvard reading them alone in the tower room and burning most of them after, and never, ever speaking of them. She had delivered one such letter unopened to a different address once, twenty-five years past, on instructions from a dying woman, and she had never let herself wonder what it said.

She wondered now. She wondered with the force of nineteen quiet years breaking their banks.

* * *

Halvard came down to the kitchen at noon, moving along the wall with one hand on the plaster, breathing in careful installments. He looked at the basket. He looked for a long time.

"Well," he said finally. "So it's come to this house too."

"You knew?" Maren kept her voice flat.

"I knew someone would come eventually. Not in a basket." He lowered himself into the chair she'd placed by the stove years ago for exactly these descents, his joints cracking like green wood. "Whose is it, Maren?"

"You're asking me?"

"I'm asking what you know."

"And I'm telling you," she said, taking the basket and moving it to the far side of the table, out of his reach—not consciously, but her arms did it anyway—"that I know how to nurse a child and keep a kitchen. The rest is above my station."

Something moved across the old man's face that might have been respect, or grief, or both wearing each other's coats. "She has the crest," he said.

"She has a blanket."

"Maren." His voice cracked on her name, and for a moment she saw the boy he must have been, before the sea took his brother and the lie took everything else. "That child is kin to me or she isn't. Either way, she's under my roof, and I've precious little roof-time left."

"Then rest easy," Maren said, more gently than she intended. "She's fed. She's warm. Whatever she is, she's being cared for. Isn't that all any keeper can say of what's in his charge?"

He didn't answer. He sat looking at the basket with his hands open on his knees, palms up, like a man waiting for weights to be set in them, and Maren turned back to her sink and her dishes and let the silence do its work.

* * *

It was three days before she burned the note—three days because, in truth, she had already burned it, the first morning, thrust deep into the firebox before Wendell returned with the doctor, and what happened on the third day was only the finishing of it.

Wendell had begun pulling the master clock apart, and the kitchen filled with his coming and going: hands black with oil, eyes bright with the peculiar fever the work put in him. He was good—better than good, the clock had already steadied under his fingers—and he was kind to the child in the fumbling way of men who want to be kind and don't know the grammar of it. He held her wrong and let Maren correct him. He hummed to her, tunelessly. On the second night Maren had come down to find him pacing the kitchen with the baby against his shoulder, walking her through a colic hour, and the sight had done something to her resolve that she did not examine closely.

On the third evening he came down searching his pockets. "The note," he said. "I want to compare the handwriting. There are papers in the tower—my uncle's correspondence—I need to know if—"

"There is no note, Master Eriksen."

"There *was* a note."

"Yes." She dried her hands on her apron. "And there was a fire lit that same morning, and papers go into fires in this house, as I believe I mentioned."

"You destroyed evidence."

"Evidence of what? Three words? Forget the face?" Something rose in her then, twenty-six years of held breath coming up all at once, and she mastered it barely, and what escaped was only her voice dropping low. "Boy. Whoever wrote that note wrote it to be forgotten. That's the entire meaning of a note like that. Somebody loved that child enough to carry her through fog to a stranger's door, and hurt enough—or frightened enough—to leave no name. You dig at that ink and you'll raise ghosts that eat families. I've seen it done."

"You've seen it," he repeated softly. "Not read about it. Seen it."

Maren turned back to the sink. Behind her, the kitchen clock ticked. Above them both, faint through floors and beams, the master clock answered, still half a shade wrong despite the boy's repairs, like a heart with a murmur.

"I'll say this much," she said to the dishwater, "and then it's closed. That crest on the blanket wasn't worked by a machine. It was worked by hand, left-over-right stitch, by somebody taught old-fashioned, in a household that cared about doing things properly. Which means whoever gave up that child didn't come from nothing. They came from somewhere, and something went wrong there, and it isn't mine to tell and it isn't yours to dig."

"Søborg," Wendell said quietly.

Her hands went still in the water.

"Halvard mentioned the name last night," he went on, and she could hear him watching her back. "When I asked why he wouldn't speak of my father. He said one word—*Søborg*—and then his heart pained him and he sent me from the room. You've gone white again, Maren. Second time this week. Cold kitchens, is it?"

"The stove's hot enough to bake bread," she said. "Mind the clock, Master Eriksen. Clocks forgive. Families don't." She lifted the last dish from the water, set it in the rack, and walked past him toward the stairs with the candle held steady and her spine held steadier, and only when she reached the landing did she allow her hand to press flat against the wall.

Below, faintly, she heard him cross to the stove and crouch before the firebox, stirring the ash with the poker, hunting for scraps of paper among the cinders. Let him hunt. What he'd find was a curl of char with half a word on it—*for-*—and nothing more, because she had fed the note to the flames in pieces across three mornings, a careful housekeeper to the end.

What he could not find, what no poker would ever turn up, was the other thing she carried: that she had seen the crest's stitching before, on a christening blanket, in a drawing-room with the curtains drawn, wrapped around a child whose father was said to be drowned and whose mother had made a girl of fourteen swear on a Bible.

And that the child in that blanket had been named for a dead sister.

*Ilsa.*

Down in the kitchen, the baby began to cry, and Wendell began to pace, and above it all the master clock counted on toward midnight, half a shade wrong, holding its secret the way she held hers—one tooth of the escapement at a time.
