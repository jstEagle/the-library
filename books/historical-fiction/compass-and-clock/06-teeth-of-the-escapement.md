## 6. Teeth of the Escapement

The master clock of Vesterhavn Observatory had a voice, and for three days Wendell Eriksen had been learning to listen to it.

It was not like the parlor clocks of his apprenticeship, nor even the tower clock in his hometown whose bells he had once oiled from a ladder swaying over the market square. This machine was a cathedral of brass and iron, its great trains rising through the tower's spine like the vertebrae of some ancient animal, weights hanging on cables thick as his thumb, pendulum suspended in its own sealed shaft where the sea air could not gnaw it. When everything ran true, it spoke in a single syllable—*tick*—so deep and so regular that he felt it in his back teeth rather than heard it with his ears. When something ran false, it spoke in stutters, in hesitations, in the small apologetic coughs of metal meeting metal at the wrong angle.

For three days it had been coughing.

He worked by lamplight because the tower's narrow windows gave him only an hour of decent gray daylight before the fog rolled back off the water and swallowed them whole. The observatory's other rooms—the meridian hall with its transit telescope, Halvard's cluttered office, the kitchen where Maren left bread and salt fish and no conversation—all existed around him like rooms in a house he was not yet invited into. The tower was his province now. He climbed its ladder each morning before the gulls woke and descended each night with iron filings in his hair and oil under his fingernails that no amount of lye soap would fully lift.

The filed gear was still the heart of the matter. He had removed it on the second day, held it to the lamp, and confirmed what he had known at first touch: this was not wear. Wear is honest. Wear happens where friction lives, along the pitch faces of teeth, in the pivot holes, in the slow grinding concession of metal to metal over decades. What he saw on that escape wheel was different—a tooth shortened deliberately, filed flat and then dressed down with emery so the cut would look old, look natural, look like nothing at all unless you put your thumbnail against it the way he had.

Someone had wanted the clock to fail slowly. Not to stop—that would be too loud, too obvious, a thing that would summon help within the week. No: someone had wanted it to drift. To lose seconds, then minutes, then enough time that every ship's departure governed by the Vesterhavn signal would slide out of truth like a name sliding off a gravestone.

Wendell did not have the imagination to invent such malice, but he had the craft to recognize it, and recognizing it had changed the shape of his days. He no longer merely repaired. He interrogated.

* * *

On the fourth morning the fog came in so thick that when he stood at the tower's base and looked straight up, the lantern room vanished entirely, and the tower seemed to be a column holding up nothing. He carried his tools up in the canvas roll he'd brought from home—his father's roll, though he permitted himself to think of it as simply *the* roll—and laid them out on the bench with the ceremony he always observed: pliers left, files right, screwdrivers ranked by size like choristers.

The escape wheel he had already replaced with a spare from the storeroom, a wheel cast forty years ago and never fitted, still bright from its box as if it had been waiting all that time for exactly this work. He had dressed its pivots himself. Now he turned to the rest of the train, working downward through the going mechanism, checking each wheel's teeth against his lamp and loupe, hunting for a second sabotage the way a man who has found one weevil searches the whole sack of flour.

He found none. But he found something else.

The third wheel down—the one driving the strike train, the mechanism that threw the hammer against the great bell above—had been running rough in a way he couldn't attribute to dirt alone. Its arbor turned with a faint hitch, a catch at the same point in every revolution, so slight that only a man who had spent four days listening to this clock's voice would have noticed. He drew the wheel from its plates, expecting a bent tooth, a burr, a grain of grit fused in old congealed oil.

The hub was wrong. That was his first thought, absurdly: *the hub is the wrong color.* Where the rest of the wheel showed the dull pewter of aged brass, the hub's cap gleamed, freshly soldered, the seam hidden so well that only the difference in tone betrayed it. Someone had opened this gear the way a cook opens a pie.

His hands went very steady. They always did at moments like this; it was the steadiness afterward, the shaking, that he distrusted. He set the wheel on felt, took up the smallest knife in his roll, and worked the point into the solder seam. The blade slid. The cap turned with a soft grating sigh, half a turn, and lifted away.

Inside the hub, packed into the hollow with a wad of wool gone yellow with age, lay a folded packet of paper, thin as a wafer, wrapped in oilcloth.

Wendell sat back on his stool. Above him the clock's remaining trains ticked on, indifferent, and below him the fog pressed against the tower like a sea standing on end. His first instinct—one he was ashamed of even as he felt it—was to put the cap back and say nothing. Some things are sealed for reasons. Some hollows are made precisely so that what they hold will be forgotten.

But his fingers were already untying the oilcloth.

* * *

There were two documents inside.

The first was a shipping manifest, folded in eighths, the paper soft with handling. It listed cargo out of Copenhagen aboard a brig called *Vestvind*—timber, iron stock, barrels marked in an abbreviation he didn't at first understand, and then, entered in the manifest's plain commercial hand as casually as any crate of goods: *one sealed document case, per instruction of Herr T. Eriksen, to be delivered intact.*

*T. Eriksen.* Tobias Eriksen. His father.

Wendell read the line four times. The letters did not rearrange themselves. The ink had been dry for thirty years and it still said what it said: his father's name, attached to a sealed case, riding a ship's manifest like contraband or treasure, dated the spring before Tobias Eriksen's boat went missing and the sea was allowed to take the blame.

He had been nine years old when the word came. He remembered the minister's hand on his mother's shoulder, the smell of the church, Astrid at eleven standing beside him with her chin up and her eyes furious and wet. *Lost at sea,* the town said, and grief made it true, and grief also made it simple. A man goes out in a small boat; a storm comes; the sea keeps him. There was no room in that story for manifests. There was no room in that story for a sealed case being shipped ahead of a man who was supposedly sailing to his death.

The second document was thicker. He unfolded it on the felt with the care owed to something flammable.

An indenture of inheritance. Drawn up in a lawyer's cramped, confident script, witnessed by two names he did not know, and concerning property he knew only from his mother's rare, bitter mentions: the Eriksen mercantile holdings in the southern ports, warehouses and wharf shares and the interest in three vessels, all of which—according to everything Wendell had ever been told—had passed decades ago to the line of Halvard Eriksen upon the death of the elder branch's last direct holder. That was the story. The elder line died out; the estate went to its nearest kin; the sea, in its generosity, simplified matters.

But the deed in his hands named Tobias Eriksen as principal heir. Not Halvard. And the date of execution fell two years *before* the elder line's last holder died—which meant the succession everyone accepted was not a succession at all. It was an occupation. It was a house occupied while its true deed lay hidden in the belly of a clock, and the man who had hidden it there—

Wendell closed his eyes. He could see the tower room above, the narrow stair, the old man's face the day they met: the flicker of recognition buried so fast under courtesy that anyone less practiced at reading faces would have missed it entirely. *Your father's boy. You have his hands.*

Halvard had recognized him instantly and pretended otherwise. Halvard had let the sea be blamed. And somewhere in the years between, Halvard had taken the proof of his brother's—or cousin's, the relationship blurred across generations of Eriksens who stopped counting—of his kinsman's inheritance and sealed it inside the one place he trusted more than any vault: a machine he tended every day of his life, wound every evening, listened to every night. The keeper had kept more than time.

Unless—Wendell turned the thought over carefully, testing it the way he tested a suspect wheel—unless Halvard was not the hider but the keeper of another man's hiding. Unless Tobias himself had placed the documents, or an accomplice had, and Halvard's guilt was only the guilt of silence, which is a lighter metal but weighs on a man just as long.

He could not know. That was the dread of it, coiling cold around the thrill. The thrill had come first, hot and immediate, the pure joy of discovery that every craftsman knows—the finding of the fault, the answer under the cap—and for one breath in that lamplit tower Wendell had felt like a boy again, feeling clever, feeling chosen. Then the paper had given up its names, and the thrill curdled into something with weight and edges. Because either his father had been a thief who ran, or his father had been an honest man destroyed by thieves, and both possibilities ended the same way: with Wendell's childhood built on a lie, and the liar still alive, still winding this clock, still eating Maren's bread downstairs with his soft dying heart.

A man lost at sea cannot write a manifest. A man lost at sea cannot inherit. Which meant that either his father had never been lost—or someone had needed very much for everyone to believe he was.

* * *

Wendell did the thing his father had taught him without ever meaning to teach it: he worked. When his hands did not know what to do with a truth, they cleaned something. He reassembled the strike train with the hollowed hub empty, the cap resealed with fresh solder that he darkened with a rag and a drop of acid until it matched the old metal. Let the gear keep its secret's shape even if it had lost its secret's substance. He fitted the wheel back between its plates, pinned the arbor, oiled the pivots with exactly two drops from the glass needle bottle, and swung the train into motion.

The hitch was gone. The strike train ran silent and smooth, gathering itself for hours yet before it would need to throw its hammer, and Wendell watched it go around with the strange grief of a man watching a mechanism that had been carrying a message to him for thirty years, ticking it over and over in brass, waiting for someone to open it up.

*You were in there the whole time,* he thought. *All those years I was a boy without a father, you were sitting in a gear in a tower, saying his name.*

He folded the documents back into their oilcloth. He considered the hiding places available to him: under the floorboard of his lodging, inside the lining of his tool roll, sewn into the waistband of his trousers. All of them places a magistrate might search if it ever came to magistrates, and the deed had already taught him that word should frighten him. An inheritance claim invites scrutiny of how the deed left the family—he had no reason yet to know how true that warning would prove—but he felt its shape already, the way one feels weather coming in an old scar.

In the end he hid the packet where it had lived all along, or nearly. He cut a new hollow—not in the strike wheel, which now ran honestly, but in the wooden core of the bench itself, beneath the felt where he laid his finest work. He bored it with a spoon bit, seated the oilcloth packet in the cavity, and capped it with a disc of oak cut to fit, glued and dusted with shavings until the join disappeared. A clockmaker's bench holds a hundred scars; one more would tell nothing.

Only when it was done did he let himself think about Astrid, three hundred miles south, writing her sharp ambitious letters, certain that the family had been robbed and hungry to prove it. She would want this paper the way starving people want bread. And she was not wrong—that was the terrible part. The deed proved their father was robbed, or proved their father was a robber; it could be read either way depending on which side of it you stood, and Astrid would stand wherever the money was.

He wiped down the bench. He banked the lamp low. Through the tower's slit window the fog had begun to lift off the harbor, and the masts of the moored ships stood out of the white like pins in pincushion, and somewhere among those ships men were waking to a tide table governed by the clock above his head—a clock that told the harbor a lie of minutes, drifting slow, delaying departures, keeping ships in port for reasons no one had explained.

*Who benefits from delayed ships?* he asked the question of the fog, and the fog declined to answer.

He descended the ladder on legs stiff from hours of crouching. In the passage below, Maren was scrubbing the flagstones, sleeves rolled, and she glanced up at him with those flat gray eyes that gave away nothing and had, he suspected, been giving away nothing for a very long time.

"You've been at the strike train," she said. Not a question.

"The wheel was damaged." He kept his voice at the level pitch he used for customers. "Repaired now."

"Master Halvard doesn't like the strike train touched."

"Master Halvard hired me to repair the clock."

She wrung her cloth into the bucket and said nothing further, but as he passed her she spoke once more, quietly, to the floor rather than to him.

"Some things run better when they're late, Mr. Eriksen. You'll forgive me saying so. Some things oughtn't arrive when they're expected."

Then she gathered her bucket and went down toward the kitchen, and Wendell stood in the passage with the cold stone at his back and the words settling over him like the fog outside.

When he pushed open the door of Halvard's office an hour later—to report progress, to watch the old man's face as he did so—the keeper was not there. His chair stood empty, his cold pipe in the ash tray, and on the desk, weighted beneath a brass paperknife, lay a sheet of paper bearing three lines in a shaking hand:

*Whatever you found, leave it where you found it. Some doors open outward into the sea. Ask me nothing until I am strong enough to answer.*

It was signed with a single initial: *H.*

Wendell read it twice, then folded it into his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like a second, smaller deed. Below the window, out on the gravel path, he heard footsteps going down toward the shore road—uneven footsteps, an old man's steps, hurrying badly—and when he reached the glass he saw Halvard Eriksen bent into the wind, making for the harbor as fast as his failing body would carry him.

And behind him, at the gate, letting the old man get well ahead before following at a distance with her shawl drawn up against the fog, went Maren.
