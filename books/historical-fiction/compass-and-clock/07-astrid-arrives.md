## 7. Astrid Arrives

The letter came to Astrid Eriksen on a Tuesday, slipped beneath the door of the dressmaker's shop in Kolding where she had spent three years hemming other women's futures and learning, stitch by stitch, how fabric could conceal a body's truth as easily as a word could conceal a heart's. The envelope held no return address. The hand that had written it was careful and cramped, the ink of someone accustomed to small spaces and small measures of it—a workshop hand, a ledger hand. Inside, a single sheet.

*Your father did not drown.*

She read it four times before her hands stopped shaking. Then she folded it into the smallest possible square, tucked it into the lining of her stays, where she kept the money she did not declare to anyone, and asked Mrs. Brandt for two days' leave on account of her aunt's failing health.

Her aunt was not failing. Her aunt had died in '93, and Astrid had wept at the funeral with genuine feeling and an eye, even then, for who was watching her grieve. Grief well-performed opened purses; this too was a lesson of the needle trades, where every widow's veil was also an advertisement.

*Your father did not drown.*

There were only two people alive who might know such a thing and care whether she knew it: her brother Wendell, whose letters came twice a year and said nothing, and Uncle Halvard, who had never once written, not since the funeral, not since the sea took—no. Not since they *said* the sea had taken Anders Eriksen, and Astrid, eleven years old and already fluent in the arithmetic of absence, had watched her mother stop eating in the same slow way a clock stops, gear by gear, until one morning there was simply no more ticking in her.

Astrid had been left with a name that opened no doors and a face that opened most of the others. She had used both. She had used everything. And now, on a coastal steamer north through the grey teeth of November, she stood at the rail with salt drying on her lips and rehearsed what she would say to whichever of them had sent the letter, and she found that beneath the calculation—the cold, patient arithmetic she trusted more than any prayer—her hands would not stay still.

* * *

Vesterhavn announced itself the way a debt collector announces himself: by standing where you cannot avoid him. The observatory rose out of the fog above the harbor like something the town had grown rather than built, its lantern gallery crowned with the last pale breath of daylight, and below it the houses crowded down to the water in ranks, their windows lit against the early dark. Bells were ringing somewhere—harbor bells, marking the quarter-hour—and Astrid noticed, with the particular irritation of a woman who has been counting her own time in other people's shops for years, that the bells did not quite agree with the little watch pinned inside her coat. Vesterhavn ran on its own clock. Everyone in the street seemed to move to it without question, fishermen and clerks and children all bending their lives to a rhythm issued from the tower above them.

*Well,* she thought, buttoning her collar against the wind. *We shall see about that.*

She asked a boy with a barrow for the way to the observatory and he pointed up the cliff road without asking why, which told her something useful: strangers arrived here often enough, drawn by the clock or the keeper or the sea's rumor mill, and were absorbed without remark. A town that did not notice arrivals would also be slow to notice departures. She filed that away.

The housekeeper who answered the observatory door was broad-shouldered and weathered, with eyes that went from Astrid's face to the road behind her and back again, measuring, before she spoke.

"I'm looking for Wendell Eriksen," Astrid said. "He's my brother."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes—not surprise exactly. Recognition arriving late, like a letter delayed in the post. "You'd best come in, then," she said, and stepped aside, and Astrid understood immediately that Maren—she would learn the name within the hour—had known who Astrid was before Astrid had said it. Which meant someone had spoken of her. Which meant her brother had been here long enough to speak.

The hall smelled of oil and brass and, faintly, of milk. That last detail Astrid registered and set aside, though it would come back to her later, as details do, wearing different clothes.

Wendell came down the stairs with a wrench in his hand and grease to his elbows, and when he saw her he stopped so abruptly on the third step that he swayed, a man whose footing had been pulled out from under him by kindness rather than malice. He looked older than twenty-two. He looked like their mother around the eyes, and Astrid felt that observation land somewhere beneath her ribs where she kept the things she could not afford to carry openly.

"Astrid." Her name came out of him half-strangled. "What—how did you—who told you I was here?"

"Nobody told me." She let him hold her gaze while she removed one glove, finger by finger, unhurried. "A letter came. Unsigned. It said my father didn't drown." She watched his face do something complicated and unsuccessful—watched him glance toward the kitchen doorway, where the housekeeper had gone very still—and Astrid smiled, because the smile cost less than the scream building underneath it. "So. You've been here five weeks, brother, writing me your usual two paragraphs about the weather and the work, and in all that time you never once thought to mention that you'd found proof Papa is alive."

"It isn't—that isn't—" Wendell came down the last steps slowly, setting the wrench on the newel post like a man laying down a weapon he wasn't sure he was done with. "It's not proof. It's a document. It's complicated."

"I have spent nine years being told things are complicated by men who benefited from the complication." She heard her own voice sharpen and did not soften it. Sharpening was honest. Sharpening was the only inheritance their father had left her besides the name. "Where is Uncle Halvard?"

"Upstairs. Sleeping. He's ill, Astrid. His heart."

"Then he can hear me from his bed, if it comes to that. But first—" she unbuttoned her coat, deliberately, settling into the room as though she had already decided to stay, which she had, on the steamer, somewhere past the second headland—"you are going to show me this paper. And then, Wendell, you are going to explain to me why you sat on it like a hen on a bad egg instead of sending for me the same hour."

* * *

The deed, when he finally laid it on the kitchen table between them, unfolded like a confession.

Astrid read it three times. She made herself go slowly, the way she had taught herself to read contracts in the shop—every clause a possible hook, every name a door. The paper was old enough to crack along its folds. The language was formal, notarized, witnessed twice. And there, in the middle of the third clause, in the plain brown ink of a solicitor's office in a city she had never seen: *Anders Eriksen, of the elder line, lawfully entitled to the sum of twelve thousand kroner and the properties thereto appertaining, viz. the warehouse at Søndre Quay and the dwelling-house upon Kirkegade.*

Twelve thousand kroner. A warehouse. A house. Astrid did the arithmetic the way other people breathed, and the total arrived in her chest like a fist.

"We were told he had nothing," she said quietly. "We grew up with nothing. Mama took in sewing until her eyes went, and you were apprenticed out at eleven because they couldn't feed you, and all that time—" Her voice caught on something and she despised it, despised the catch, mastered it. "All that time this existed. This *entitlement*. And Halvard knew. Halvard kept it hidden in a *clock*, of all the sanctimonious—"

"You don't know that he kept it hidden," Wendell said. "Papa took it. That's what it looks like. He took the deed and he ran, and whatever happened after—" His hands moved helplessly, a boy's gesture still, and she loved him and hated the gesture in equal measure. "Whatever happened, Astrid, he *left*. He left us."

"He left us because he was running with twelve thousand kroner and a man carrying that much doesn't get to write home." She stood and walked the length of the kitchen, working it through, feeling the shape of the thing assemble itself under her hands the way a dress takes form on a form—first the outline, then the seams. "Think, Wendell. Use that mechanical head of yours. If Papa took the deed intending to claim it, he'd have claimed it within months, wherever he landed. It hasn't been claimed. So either he's dead after all—" she raised a hand to stop Wendell's protest—"or he couldn't claim it, because claiming it meant explaining where he'd been, and what he'd done to get it out of Halvard's keeping. Or—" and here she stopped, both hands flat on the table, looking down at the cracked old paper with something close to tenderness, which frightened her a little, "—or he's been waiting. All these years. Waiting until it was safe. Until the man who wronged him was old and dying and the property had appreciated and nobody remembered the quarrel."

"And you believe that."

"I believe it's *possible*," Astrid said, "and possibility, Wendell, is worth more than certainty in a courtroom. Certainty you must prove. Possibility you need only raise." She straightened. "This is ours. Ours and yours—our father's entitlement, descended to his lawful children, since he is neither dead nor divested. Halvard's branch has enjoyed the name and standing of this family for thirty years on the strength of a drowned man who didn't drown. Well. He's dying now anyway, poor lamb. Nature is settling accounts ahead of us. What remains is the property."

Wendell was quiet a long moment. When he spoke his voice was low and careful, and she recognized the tone—it was the one he used with recalcitrant mechanisms, the gentle coercive voice of a man talking a spring back into place. "Astrid. There's more here than paper. There's a child."

"Yes. The baby. The housekeeper's, presumably, or some parish foundling—" She had heard the story on the walk up from the harbor, extracted from a fishwife in exchange for two øre and a compliment. An abandoned infant, a note, a crest. She had assembled the facts the way she assembled everything, quickly, and discarded them nearly as quickly. "What has the child to do with—"

"The wool was embroidered with the Eriksen crest." Wendell's eyes didn't leave hers, and Astrid felt, with the sensation of a floorboard giving slightly underfoot, that he was watching her the way she watched other people. When had he learned that? Who had taught her careful, pliable brother to look for the lie beneath? "Our crest, Astrid. Someone in our family wrapped that child and left her at this door. And the note said—"

"I don't care what the note said." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, and part of her stood back and observed with clinical interest: *there it is, the seam showing.* She smoothed it over. "I care about a warehouse and a house and twelve thousand kroner, which is to say I care about the difference between the life we actually got and the life we were owed. A foundling in embroidered wool is either a message or a mistake, and either way it is not *leverage we need*. Don't let sentiment complicate the claim. Sentiment is what ruined Mama."

"That's a cruel thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say. Cruelty and truth are cousins, Wendell; our family has always confused them for strangers."

* * *

She found the magistrate the next morning, because finding people was the other thing she did.

His name was Magistrate Holm, a widower of perhaps fifty with a good coat going slightly shiny at the cuffs and the particular loneliness of a man whose authority everyone respected and whose conversation nobody sought. He took his morning coffee at the harborside establishment called, with the humorless literalism of provincial Denmark, the Coffee House, at a corner table facing the water, and he was alone, and Astrid wore her blue wool dress because blue flattered her against grey skies and because it was the nearest thing to respectable mourning she owned, and mourning, subtly deployed, invited questions.

She did not sit at his table. She sat near it, ordered, waited, let the silence do its work. Men like Holm noticed a woman alone who did not seek their notice; the noticing accumulated; and after four minutes—he was punctual, she noted, checking her watch against the harbor bell and finding them once again at odds, a whole minute adrift, the whole town a minute adrift, and what did that say about the man who kept this town's time—after four minutes he cleared his throat and said that the weather was turning, and the conversation began.

She gave him nothing for two days. She let him see her at the Coffee House, at the chandlery, walking the quay with her chin lifted and her eyes busy. She asked a shipping clerk, audibly, about the laws governing claims on unclaimed property. She let the town's machinery do what small-town machinery does, grinding rumor into anticipation. By the third day Magistrate Holm was inviting her to take coffee at his own table, and by the fourth she had mentioned—casually, folding the information into talk of her journey, her family, the sad illness of a relative—that she was the daughter of Anders Eriksen, and watched the name land.

"Eriksen," Holm repeated. His cup paused halfway to his mouth. "The keeper at the observatory is an Eriksen."

"My father's brother," Astrid said, and smiled, and let the smile carry everything it needed to: the orphan, the hardship, the long patience. "Though I confess, Magistrate, the relationship has brought us very little but the name. My brother repairs clocks. I sew hems. The Eriksens of Vesterhavn keep a lighthouse of time over this whole coast, and my family has stood outside it in the cold for thirty years."

Holm set down his cup. He was not a stupid man—she had worried briefly that he might be, stupidity being so much harder to work with—and she saw the caution assemble itself behind his eyes like weather coming in. "Family quarrels," he said, "are not generally matters for a magistrate. Unless a law has been broken."

"Oh, I should think several have," Astrid said lightly, stirring her coffee. "Documents mislaid. Entitlements suppressed. Whether anything *provable* occurred is another question entirely." She looked up at him through her lashes, a trick she had learned from watching the dressmaker's wealthiest client, a woman who had buried two husbands and flourished. "But you mustn't think I've come to make trouble, Magistrate. I've come to make things *right*. There's a difference. Trouble makes noise. Rightness makes records."

He laughed—surprised into it, charmed despite himself—and Astrid counted the laugh the way she counted everything: as capital, banked.

Walking back up the cliff road in the late afternoon, the observatory's lantern kindling above her and the harbor bells striking their private, incorrect hour, she permitted herself, briefly, honestly, the feeling underneath all the rest. It was not greed. Greed was what people called a woman's ambition when it inconvenienced them. What she felt was closer to justice, or wore justice's coat, which in practical terms amounted to the same thing: her mother had sewn until her eyes failed; her brother had been handed to strangers at eleven; Astrid herself had hemmed ten thousand hems for other women's bodies while her inheritance slept in a clock. If reclaiming it required managing a dying uncle, a soft-hearted brother, a lonely magistrate, and one inconvenient foundling, then she would manage them. Love, her mother had always said, was the thing you made room for after the work was done. Astrid had simply reversed the order. It was, she told herself, a mercy to Wendell that she loved him enough to do the work for both of them.

Inside, the hall smelled of milk again, stronger now, and of lamp oil, and beneath both, faintly, of the sea. Wendell was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with a strange stillness she hadn't seen in him before, and Maren stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed tight as a ship's rigging, and between them, in the space they had left, Astrid felt the shape of a thing withheld.

"Well?" she said, unpinning her hat. "You both look as though the tide's come in indoors. What's happened?"

Wendell glanced at the housekeeper, and the housekeeper looked back at him, and Astrid understood, with the sudden sick clarity of a woman who has just realized the contract she's reading has a clause she missed, that they had agreed on something in her absence—and that whatever it was, it concerned the child, and that her brother's next words were going to change the price of everything.
