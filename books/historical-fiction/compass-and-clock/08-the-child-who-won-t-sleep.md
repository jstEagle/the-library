## 8. The Child Who Won't Sleep

The child arrived with the tide and, like the tide, refused to be sent away.

Wendell had told himself it would be a matter of days. Maren would find a family in the village; the parish minister would make inquiries; some woman with a full heart and an empty cradle would come up the cliff road and carry the whole problem off wrapped in Eriksen wool. But three nights had passed and no one had come, and Vesterhavn was not a village that asked questions of the observatory so much as a village that watched it, and the storm-light in the tower windows had begun to look, to Wendell's exhausted eye, less like duty than like siege.

He worked by lamplight in the clock room because the master clock did not care whether he slept. Its broken escapement lay open on the bench like a patient mid-surgery, its wheels spread out in ordered ranks, each one cleaned and numbered and waiting. He had promised the harbor board the time signal would hold through December. Promises were the only currency he had brought north, and he meant to spend them well.

The crying started at half past ten.

It came up the stairwell from the keeper's quarters, thin at first, then gathering force the way wind gathers off open water—not the furious squall of a hungry infant, which he could have understood, but a long, level, desolate keening, as though the child were sounding a depth inside herself that had no bottom. He gave it a quarter hour. He gave it another. At eleven, when his eyes had begun to swim over the gear teeth and the keening had not changed pitch or purpose, he set down his tweezers, wiped his hands, and went down to see what a clockmaker could possibly know about the repair of a human being.

Maren sat in the rocking chair with Ilsa—*the child*, he corrected himself, she had no name yet—walked upright against her shoulder, patting the small back in a rhythm as old as houses. The fire had burned low. The room smelled of coal smoke, lanolin, and the particular warm-milk sweetness that seemed to have colonized the entire ground floor in three days.

"She won't settle," Maren said, without turning. "Fed, dry, warm. She just cries. Like she's calling someone."

"Like she's counting," Wendell said, before he thought better of it.

Maren glanced back at him then, and in the firelight her face was unreadable in a way that made him feel he'd said something either very foolish or very true. "Take her a turn," she said. "My arms need their rest more than my ears do."

She transferred the child into his hands with the brisk efficiency of someone handing over a parcel, and Wendell found himself holding—for only the third time in his twenty-three years—a living person smaller than a loaf of bread. She was heavier than she looked. That was the first thing. She had a real weight to her, a gravity all her own, and she leaned into his chest with her whole furious body, fists balled against his collarbone, face contorted, and cried directly into his ear as though he had personally wronged her.

"Well," he said. "Yes. All right."

He walked. There was nothing else to do. He paced the length of the keeper's sitting room, four steps to the window, four steps back, past the sea chest and the closed shutters and the framed text above the mantel that Halvard had hung there decades ago and never once been seen to read: *I have considered the days of old.* Four steps, four steps. The crying did not falter. Out beyond the shutter seams the wind worked at the tower, and below, muffled by stone, came the sound he had lived inside for three days now—the great pendulum's slow tick, tick, tick, the master clock running on its temporary auxiliary train, accurate enough for the harbor, not accurate enough for him.

"Your uncle sleeps like the dead," Maren observed from her chair. "Or like the dying."

"Don't say that."

"It's a house. Things get said in it."

He walked. The child cried. His shoulder began to ache in a deep, grinding way that he suspected would become permanent, and somewhere around the fortieth circuit he abandoned dignity and tried the things he imagined other men did: he bounced slightly, which made it worse; he hummed, which startled them both; he held her out at arm's length and looked at her, and she opened her eyes—and this stopped him cold—she opened her eyes, two dark wet gleams in the low light, and looked at him with an attention so complete and so unimpressed that he felt he was being examined for a position he was not qualified to hold.

"I don't know what you want," he told her quietly. "I'm being honest with you. Nobody wants that more than I do."

She resumed crying. But there was something changed in it now, he thought—or he decided there was, which amounted afterward to the same thing—a note less of summons than of grievance, as though the two of them had at least established terms.

He carried her upstairs.

* * *

The clock room was cold at midnight, cold and enormous after the small rooms below, and lit only by his work lamp, whose circle of yellow fell on the bench, the gears, the brass spine of the master clock rising into the dark like something between a column and a mast. He had gone up without a plan. He went up because pacing needed a longer corridor, and because the alternative was admitting that a creature eight weeks old had beaten him.

The moment they crossed the threshold, the crying thinned.

Wendell stopped in the middle of the floor, one hand cupped under the wool-wrapped bundle, and listened. The change was subtle—the keening dropping to a whimper, the whimper to a hitching, gulping quiet, the small body still rigid against him but no longer driving at him. And underneath it, filling the room the way water fills a hull, the tick.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The auxiliary train ran slower than the great wheel would, a half-beat adrift from the tempo the clock was built to keep, and the sound of it was deep and even and utterly without feeling, and the child had gone silent against his chest with her cheek flattened to his shirt, listening.

"You cannot be serious," Wendell said.

But she was asleep. Not settled—there had been no gradual surrender, no drifting down the long slope toward rest—but simply *asleep*, abruptly, the way a lamp goes out when the oil runs low, as if the ticking were something she had been searching the world for since the basket appeared on the observatory door and here it was at last, vast and steady, keeping time on her behalf.

He stood in the middle of the cold room holding a sleeping baby and felt something shift in his chest with a click he would later, professionally, compare to a detent engaging.

He should take her back down. He knew he should take her back down; Maren would have opinions about drafts and damp and the lungs of infants. Instead he walked to the bench, cleared a space with his elbow among the disarmed wheels and the oiled rags, and sat on the stool with the bundle in the crook of his arm, and let the clock tick, and did not move for a long time.

Her face, in sleep, was a different country from her face awake. The fury drained out of it and left something startlingly composed—small brows already suggesting opinion, a mouth that pursed and unpursed around some dream-argument, a chin tucked into the wool. And at the collar of the wrapping, where the fold gaped, the embroidery showed again: the Eriksen crest, the compass rose over the crossed keys, worked in thread gone soft and gray with handling. Someone had stitched that crest with care a long time ago. Someone had held this wool against a child of their own and then put it in a basket and carried it through the dark to a door in the fog.

"Forget the face," Wendell said aloud, to the room. "Whose face? Hers? Mine?"

The clock ticked. It was, as always, indifferent.

He looked at the crest until the lines blurred, and thought of his father's hands—he could barely summon them anymore, just a memory of knuckles and a smell of linseed—and of the word *drowned*, which had hung over his boyhood like weather, explaining everything and illuminating nothing. Drowned men did not embroider. Drowned men did not leave crests on babies. Somebody living had done this, and somebody living knew.

Against his arm the child sighed, a tiny seismic event, and her fist unclenched in her sleep, and Wendell Eriksen, who measured his life in portable things, who had rehearsed for years the art of leaving before leaving became impossible, sat in the cold tower room and discovered that he had already begun, without any decision on his part, to keep something that could not be packed.

* * *

The nights organized themselves after that, the way mechanisms do.

Repairs until ten. The handoff from Maren, who pretended reluctance and arranged her day around it. Then the stairs, the cold room, the bench, the tick. She would fuss on the climb, working herself toward the evening's first outrage, and the moment the threshold passed under his boots she would go quiet, head turning—eight weeks old and turning already, Maren said she'd never seen it—toward the sound. As if checking that it was still there. As if she had been promised.

He learned her in the dark the way he learned a movement: by feel. The cry that meant wind and wanted walking. The cry that meant hunger and could not be argued with, which sent him down the stairs sheepish and returned him up again with a bottle Maren had left warming. The cry—rare, terrifying—that spiked and would not be soothed at all, and on those nights he simply walked the long floor with her pressed to his shoulder and the clock ticking and told her about the works.

"The escape wheel," he murmured, on the fourth night, her breathing finally ragged-easy against his neck, "releases the power one tooth at a time. That's all time is, do you see? Power, let go slowly. Everything else—the chime, the hands, the signal flags down at the harbor—all of it is just the wheel agreeing to give up its teeth in order." He turned, four steps, turned. "You'll want to remember that. Most people think time passes. It doesn't. It's spent."

She slept. He talked anyway. He found, to his private astonishment, that he was telling her things he had never told anyone—not the facts of the deed hidden in his trunk, not yet, but the shape of them, the fear of them, circling the secret the way he circled the room. His father. The name on documents he had no right to read twice. Uncle Halvard in his room below, counting backward through his own remaining days. Astrid, three hundred miles south, who would tear this coast apart for what that deed proved, and call it justice.

"You and I are in it now," he told the top of her head, the fine dark down of her hair catching the lamplight. "Whatever it is. I haven't decided what to do about that."

She was, of course, no help whatsoever.

On the sixth night, the naming happened, and afterward he could never locate the moment of it, only discover it done.

Maren had asked—twice, carefully—if he'd thought about what to call her, and both times he had answered that naming was a legal act, a claiming, and that the child's claim was precisely the question the whole coast was going to want answered soon enough, and better to wait. Sensible words. True words. He believed them right up until the moment, somewhere past two in the morning, when she woke early from a dream, whimpering, and he heard himself say, in the low automatic voice he used only for her, "Hush, Ilsa. Ilsa, hush. I'm here."

The room ticked on. She settled. And Wendell sat perfectly still on the stool with his heart going oddly, understanding that the name had been living in his mouth for days, that he had been saying it silently every time he counted her fingers in the lamplight, and that he had not chosen it any more than one chooses the hour at which grief arrives or the moment a spring, wound too long, finally lets itself down. Ilsa. It was his grandmother's name—the Eriksen grandmother, dead before he was born, whose portrait had hung in the stairwell of his childhood beside the crest. A name from before the drowning. A name from before the lie.

"There," he said, uselessly, softly. "Now you're someone."

And then, because the honesty of two in the morning permits nothing less: "God help us both, because I don't know whose you are."

* * *

He did not hear the step on the stair until it was nearly at the top.

Halvard stood in the doorway of the clock room, wrapped in a dressing gown that had been good wool thirty years ago, one hand braced against the frame. The lamplight caught him from below and carved his face into ridges—hollow cheeks, the gray lips, the eyes that had pretended for six days not to know his own brother's son. In the silence the clock ticked and ticked, and the old man looked at the clockmaker's bench, and at the child asleep in the crook of Wendell's arm, and at the wool folded back from her face with the crest showing plain in the light.

For a moment—one breath, two—something moved behind Halvard's eyes that was not coldness at all. It looked, unbearably, like recognition; like a man seeing two ghosts seated together at one bench.

"You've made her a nest in your machinery," Halvard said at last. His voice was dry as worn felt.

"She sleeps to the clock. Nothing else quiets her."

"Nothing else," the old man repeated, and there was a weight on the phrase that Wendell could not parse—regret, or dread, or some arithmetic being run and rerun behind the failing heart. Halvard's gaze stayed on the child a moment longer. On the wool. "Mind how loudly she learns to sleep, boy. This tower has kept secrets by staying quiet. A baby's silence is a different instrument entirely."

Then he turned and went down the stairs, slowly, one hand to the wall, and Wendell was left with the tick and the sleeping child and the sudden, certain knowledge that whatever his uncle knew about the crest and the basket and the note in his trunk, he had known it for thirty years—and that the mask of cold courtesy was beginning, night by night, to slip.

Against his arm, Ilsa sighed and gripped his shirt in her sleep, and held on.
