## 10. The Magistrate's Interest

Astrid Eriksen had dressed for the occasion the way other women dressed for war: carefully, and with an inventory of weapons.

The gray wool was new—not new enough to suggest desperation, not so fine as to suggest delusions above her station—and beneath it she wore the small garnet brooch their mother had carried from Denmark in a pocket sewn into a child's coat. She had polished her boots herself at dawn, by candlelight, because the landlady's girl had cold hands and Astrid could not abide the idea of scuffed leather when she sat down across from a man whose signature could unmake thirty years of theft.

She carried the deed against her breastbone, folded into eight panels inside a leather folio that had once belonged to their father. That was deliberate too. If the thing had been good enough for him to carry across an ocean and half a lifetime of lies, it would do for a magistrate's desk in Vesterhavn.

The harbor office stood at the elbow of the quay, a squat brick building with salt-scoured windows and a weathervane bent permanently east by forty years of storms. Astrid climbed its three steps slowly, letting the sea wind push at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades. *Go on,* the wind said. *Go on.*

Inside smelled of lamp oil, wet rope, and the particular staleness of paper that had been argued over. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs looked up, took in her coat, her posture, the folio, and asked if she had an appointment.

"I have something better than an appointment," Astrid said. "I have patience, but I'd rather not use it."

The clerk disappeared through a door and returned almost immediately, wearing the expression of a man who has just learned that his morning will be more interesting than planned.

* * *

Magistrate Jorunn Brandt received her in a room with one tall window and a view of nothing but sky and masts—the view of a man who liked to know what was coming into his harbor before it came in. He was younger than she expected, perhaps forty, with a beard trimmed close as a hedgerow and eyes the color of slate water: patient, cold, capable of sudden depth. He did not rise when she entered. He gestured instead to the chair opposite his desk, and Astrid understood the gesture for what it was—a first move in a game he already considered himself to be playing.

She sat anyway, and arranged her folio on her knee.

"Frøken Eriksen." Her name sounded different in his mouth—weighed, tested for cracks. "You wrote that the matter concerned property."

"Property, legitimacy, and restitution." She let each word land separately, like coins on a counter. "Magistrate, I won't waste your morning with grievance. Grievance is for people without documents."

Something shifted at the corner of his mouth. Not quite approval; more the interest of a man watching a card player show discipline. "And you have documents."

Astrid opened the folio with hands that did not tremble. She had practiced this part on the steamer north, rehearsed the unfolding, the angle at which the lamplight would fall across the vellum. She laid the deed flat on his blotter and turned it to face him.

"This is a conveyance of inheritance executed in eighteen sixty-nine, naming my grandfather, Anders Eriksen of Vesterhavn, as grantee of the shipping concern and warehouse properties on the east quay—properties which passed, upon his death without lawful transfer, into the possession of his brother's line." She watched his face while she spoke, the way she had once watched customers' faces across a haberdasher's counter in Ålborg, reading the flicker before the refusal. "My father was the lawful heir after Anders. My father was lost at sea before any transfer could be made. The estate passed sideways—illegally—to Halvard Eriksen, keeper of the observatory, who has held it these thirty years under cover of grief and fog."

Brandt read. He read slowly, and Astrid made herself be still. Outside the window a gull screamed and was answered. Somewhere below, a winch groaned under cargo. The magistrate's forefinger moved along the margin of the vellum, pausing at the seal, pausing at the signature.

"Where," he said at last, without looking up, "did this come from?"

"It has been in my family."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the truest answer available to a daughter whose family history was conducted out of her hearing." Astrid kept her voice level, pleasant even. "The document surfaced recently. In connection with the observatory."

Now he did look up. And Astrid felt, in that moment, the ground tilt slightly beneath the chair legs—the first sensation all morning that she had not rehearsed for. His slate eyes were not impressed and they were not skeptical. They were *calculating*, and the arithmetic was not hers.

"Frøken Eriksen," Brandt said, leaning back. "Let me tell you how inheritance claims work in a harbor town, since you've come so far to make one. A claim is never only a claim. It is a question, and questions have a way of multiplying. You assert this deed makes your line the rightful holder of the east quay properties. Very well. Then the court must ask: if your father was the lawful heir, why did he never record this deed? Why did he never contest his uncle's possession? Where was he between eighteen sixty-nine and his reported loss? And—" the finger tapped the vellum once, gently, like a man knocking on a door he suspected no one wanted opened, "—how exactly did a document last seen leaving this country in the hands of a man presumed drowned find its way back into the hands of his children?"

"The presumption of drowning," Astrid said carefully, "is precisely what I intend to correct."

"Is it." He drew the deed toward himself an inch, then another, until it lay squarely in front of him, and Astrid realized with a cold slide in her stomach that she had given it to him—that he now possessed the thing, or at least its position, and she possessed only her voice. "Then you intend to establish, before a tribunal, that Wendell Eriksen's father absconded with papers belonging to another branch of his own family. You intend to argue that a man who took this deed abroad was robbed by its return. You intend to build a claim on the reputation of a dead man who cannot defend it—or a living man who can."

"He is neither dead nor alive to anyone here," Astrid said. "He is a story. Stories don't testify."

"Stories," said Brandt, "are the only thing this town does testify. Ask any fisherman's widow." He folded his hands on the desk, atop her deed, and studied her the way he might study a vessel riding too low in the water. "You have ambition, Frøken Eriksen. I respect it; this coast runs on ambition. But hear me plainly, because I'll say it once. An inheritance claim invites scrutiny of how the deed left the family. Scrutiny of your father. Scrutiny of your uncle. Scrutiny of whoever brought this paper back across the water—and of everyone currently keeping house at that observatory. Once I open a file, I do not get to choose which truths walk through the door behind it. Neither do you."

Astrid heard the warning perfectly. What startled her was the flicker underneath it, quick as a match struck behind cupped hands: the way his gaze had dropped to the folio's old leather, the way his finger had paused at the seal. He knew something. There was a name or a date in this town that touched this paper, and Jorunn Brandt knew it, and he was telling her—in the language men used when they wanted credit for honesty without paying its price—that the road she meant to walk ran past doors he controlled.

Good. Doors that opened for some were doors worth knowing.

"I understand the risk," she said. "I'm prepared for it."

"Preparedness is a comfort to the prepared." He rose at last, taking her measure one final time, and slid the deed back across the blotter toward her. "I'll keep this under review. Unofficially. Bring me provenance—witnesses, correspondence, anything showing lawful passage of this document through the years. Bring me something better than a fold mark and a story about surfacing." At the door, as the clerk showed her out, he added, mild as weather: "Give my regards to your brother. Clockwork is honest work. It's a pity when family business interrupts it."

* * *

Outside, the wind had swung north and gone raw. Astrid walked the quay with her chin up and the deed pressed hard against her sternum, and only when she reached the shelter of the fish market's stone arcade did she allow herself to stop and take stock, the way she took stock after every negotiation: gains, losses, exposed positions.

Gains: the magistrate had taken the deed seriously. He had not laughed, had not called it forgery, had not shown her the door. He had asked for provenance—which was work, yes, but work meant process, and process meant outcome.

Losses: he had kept her longer than courtesy required. He had asked about her father with the ease of a man consulting memory rather than curiosity. And that last remark—*a pity when family business interrupts it*—had been aimed, she was nearly certain, at information she didn't yet know he had.

She stood among the empty stalls, breathing fish-and-ice air, and did what she always did when a plan developed a hairline crack: she thought of Wendell.

Her brother had been summoned north by a letter, and Astrid had been summoned north by one too—an anonymous note, cheap paper, blocky print, telling her only that *what was hidden at Vesterhavn observatory concerns the Eriksen inheritance.* Someone wanted them both here. Someone had set these pieces on the board deliberately, and Astrid had been so hungry for the game that she had never stopped to ask whether she was playing it or being played.

The deed named her father. The deed proved robbery. But provenance—the magistrate was right about that, damn him—provenance was the whole architecture. Where had the paper been? Who had carried it, who had kept it, who had put it where Wendell would find it, folded inside a hollowed gear like a heart inside a chest?

Halvard. Dying Halvard, hiding in his tower with his clock and his guilt. But Halvard had no reason to help her claim; everything she won came out of his line's hands. Which left—

Maren, the housekeeper, with her closed face and her careful silences. Maren, who had been at the observatory long before Halvard's health failed. Maren, who had taken one look at the crest on that foundling's wrapping and gone white as sail canvas.

Astrid turned that over, walking on. The child. Everyone at that drafty tower orbited the child now—Wendell most of all, moon-eyed, carrying the infant through the clock room at midnight as though it were made of the same glass as his escapements. She had allowed herself to dismiss the baby as sentiment, as weakness, as the softness in Wendell that had always frustrated her. But the wool had borne the Eriksen crest. Somebody had wrapped a nameless infant in thirty-year-old family cloth and left it on that doorstep days after the deed came to light.

Coincidence was a luxury Astrid had never been able to afford. Two relics of the same buried history, arriving within a week of each other, at the same isolated building, in the same dying man's keeping—it was not coincidence. It was a message. Perhaps several messages, to several recipients.

She bought a cup of coffee from a cart at the arcade's end and drank it standing, scalding, thinking.

If the child belonged to the story—if the baby was somehow tied to her father, to the years he had vanished into, to whatever life he had built beyond the reach of the deed—then the infant was not leverage merely. The infant was evidence. Living, squalling, unwitnessable evidence of where her father had been and whom he had known, and therefore of how the paper had traveled, and therefore of everything the magistrate demanded and everything Astrid needed.

And if the inquiry Brandt threatened ever truly began, it would begin with the child too. Parish officers asked simple questions with sharp bottoms: Whose is it? Who left it? Why here? Questions Halvard would answer badly from his deathbed, Wendell worse from his conscience, and Maren—Maren, who burned things and said nothing—perhaps not at all.

Astrid tipped the last of the coffee back and looked out over the harbor, at the ships riding their moorings in the gray light, at the observatory tower rising above the town like a raised finger. She had come north certain of one thing: that the world owed the Eriksens of her line a fortune, and that she intended to collect. She still believed it. But for the first time since the anonymous letter, she felt the shape of a second truth pressing against the first, cold and patient as deep water:

She was not the only person in Vesterhavn who wanted something opened. And the magistrate's slate eyes, when he had spoken of scrutiny, had not been warning her off.

They had been measuring how much weight the door would bear.

At the harbor office behind her, through the salt-scoured window, she could see Jorunn Brandt at his desk, and on his blotter, unfolded where he thought no one could see it—he had kept a copy of the deed's first page, taken while her attention was on his words—his pen moving steadily, making notes in a hand she could not read.

Astrid pulled her collar up against the wind and started toward the observatory road. If Maren wouldn't tell her where the child came from, Maren would tell her something else. Everyone told Astrid something else, eventually.

It had simply never occurred to her until now to wonder what the telling would cost.
