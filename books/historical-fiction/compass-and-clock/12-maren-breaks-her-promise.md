## 12. Maren Breaks Her Promise

Maren Sørensen had kept other people's secrets for thirty-one years, and she had developed, over that span, a private taxonomy of them. There were secrets that sat light as sea-foam—someone's fondness for gin in their tea, someone's illegible shame over a card debt. There were secrets that sat heavy but still, like furniture: you learned to walk around them until they became part of the room. And then there were the third kind, the ones that moved. That breathed. That woke at night and cried and had to be fed from your own body's aching, so that the secret was no longer something you carried but something you *were*, and there was no setting it down in any corner of the house because it followed you from room to room with its small fists opening and closing.

Ilsa was the third kind.

The child had been with them six weeks now, and Maren had learned her the way one learns a difficult tide table: the fretful hour before dawn, the way she rooted and grumbled when the wind came around north, the astonishing, disarming gravity of her dark eyes when she was fed and warm and willing, briefly, to consider the world. Maren had told herself she would not love her. She had watched enough children pass through other women's arms to know that loving was a door once opened did not close again quietly. But Ilsa had a habit, when falling asleep against Maren's collarbone, of sighing—a whole-body sigh, ancient and resigned, like an old woman setting down a heavy basket—and Maren had felt the door swing wide on its rusted hinge and knew there was no shutting it now.

Which was why, on the morning Astrid Eriksen came to the observatory kitchen with snow in her fur collar and a lawyer's patience in her eyes, Maren was already so tired, and so full of love, that her promise had very little left to stand on.

* * *

The kitchen occupied the base of the observatory tower, round-walled and dim, warmed by a stove that Halvard had never thought worth replacing and which Maren coaxed along like a sulky horse. She was scalding feeding vessels when the knock came—not the double rap of the fish boy or the flat palm of the postman, but three measured strikes, evenly spaced, the knock of a woman who had decided in advance exactly how much authority each strike should carry.

Astrid came in on the cold air without waiting to be asked. She unwound her scarf in slow spirals, hung her coat on the peg as though she had hung coats here all her life, and surveyed the kitchen with the particular expression she wore in every room she entered: that of a person taking inventory.

"You'll be Maren," she said. It was not a question. "My brother writes of you. He says you burn things deliberately and feed people whether they want it or not."

"He'd not be wrong." Maren kept her hands in the dishwater. Warm water, she had found, kept a face steady better than any resolve. "You'll want tea."

"I'll want ten minutes and the truth." Astrid pulled out a chair, turned it, and sat astride it with her forearms folded across the back—an unladylike posture assumed deliberately, Maren suspected, the way a duelist chooses a weapon in front of witnesses. "But tea first. My hands are frozen through."

Maren set the kettle down and made the tea properly, because whatever else might be said of Astrid Eriksen—and much could be, and most of it Maren already believed—she was Wendell's sister, and there was a grief in that fact Maren was not yet ready to look at directly. She set the cup down. Astrid wrapped both hands around it and did not drink.

"Six weeks ago," Astrid said, "a baby arrived at this door in a basket. Wrapped in wool worked with the Eriksen crest. A note that my brother has not shown me, though he has shown me everything else—the deed he dug out of that clock, the letters, all of it. He shows me everything except the one thing that arrived wrapped in our own name." She turned the cup a quarter turn on the table, precise as a gear engaging. "So I am asking you plainly, housekeeper to stranger: who left that child?"

"The tide leaves many things," Maren said. "Driftwood. Rope. I don't question where it comes from."

"Don't." Astrid's voice dropped, lost its performance, gained something colder underneath. "Don't give me the servant's answer. I've been watching you since I walked in. You looked at that child's basket before you looked at me. You knew what I'd come about before I said it, and your hands have been shaking in that water since." She leaned forward. "I am not my uncle. I am not going to pretend not to see what's in front of me. Who brought that baby here, Maren?"

And there it was, laid out between them like a knife on a cloth: the question Maren had spent six weeks refusing to answer, in the voice of the one person who had a right—by blood, if by nothing else—to ask it.

Because that was the rot at the center of it, the thing Maren had carried since the night in November when a girl too young to be anyone's mother had stood shivering at her sister-in-law's door in Kristiansand, and Maren—then in service to that household, then trusted, then *used*—had taken the bundle into her own arms and promised, God forgive her, promised on her mother's Bible, to carry it somewhere it would never be found.

She had not known, then, whose instruction she was truly following. She had been told it was mercy. A girl in trouble; a family saved from scandal; a child given to a woman who could not keep her own. It was only afterward—only when the name Eriksen began appearing in conversations it should not have appeared in, only when she understood whose money had paid for her silence and whose inheritance had been unpicked stitch by stitch to make the wool—that Maren had understood she had not been hired for mercy at all. She had been hired to make a person disappear.

"I can't tell you," Maren said. Her voice came out low and even, and she was grateful for it. "There are things I promised, and promises are the only property I've got."

"Promises." Astrid laughed once, without warmth. "To whom? To my uncle, who let his own brother drown on paper? To a dead woman? Whoever it was—" she rose, crossed to the window that looked out on the gray harbor, and stood with her back straight as a mast, "—they've no claim on you now. The people who made those promises are dying or dead or, in my brother's case, so tangled in guilt he can't see past his own hands. The world is about to change, Maren. There's a magistrate in this town who owes me his consideration, and a deed with two names on it, and before this year turns there will be an accounting of everything my family buried. Everything." She turned. "Including that child."

Something went cold in Maren's chest, colder than the draft under the door.

"Including," she repeated slowly, "the child."

"She's an Eriksen. Look at the wool. Someone worked that crest by hand, stitch by stitch, and there are perhaps four people alive who know those stitches. That child is either evidence or she is leverage, and I have not yet decided which." Astrid said it lightly, testingly, the way a woman tests ice—but Maren saw her swallow after she said it, saw the flicker behind her eyes, and understood that some part of Astrid was horrified by her own mouth. That was somehow worse. A monster you could fight. A clever woman talking herself into something, hearing her own cruelty come out sounding like strategy—that was a thing that could go on for years.

"You'll not decide anything of the kind," Maren said.

"Excuse me?"

"You heard me." Maren took her hands from the water at last and dried them on her apron, slowly, giving herself time, giving the words time to arrange themselves into their final order. Thirty-one years of holding her tongue, and here it all was, rising like floodwater against a door she had braced with her own body. "You'll not decide what that child is. Not you. Not your brother. Not the magistrate nor the parish nor the Lord Almighty Himself, though He'll have His say soon enough about all of us."

Astrid stared at her. "You forget yourself."

"No." And now it came, the flood breaking through, thirty years of it, and Maren heard her own voice shake and did not care. "No, I remember myself, Miss Astrid. That's just the trouble. I remember a kitchen in Kristiansand, eleven years ago, where I scrubbed floors for your husband's family—the *other* line, the branch your deed says was robbed, and mayhap it was, I'm no judge of old money and older grievances. I remember a girl of sixteen brought to that kitchen at midnight, blue with cold, and a child born before sunrise with the whole household pretending not to hear. And I remember being handed that child wrapped in wool—your family's wool, worked with your family's crest—and being told where to take her, and being paid to forget her face."

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Astrid had gone very still, and very pale beneath her winter color.

"That was before my time," she said, but her voice had lost its footing.

"Aye, it was before your time." Maren stepped closer. "It was before mine too, in a sense. I didn't know whose child she was. I told myself I didn't need to know—a poor woman learns not to ask. It was only later I pieced it together. The crest. The money. The way your husband's family spoke of Vesterhavn—of *Halvard*—like men speaking of a debt owed them by God." She drew breath. "And I remember, Miss Astrid, a letter. Come to that house last spring, unsigned, asking after the welfare of a certain old man at Vesterhavn, and asking—asking delicate-like, the way one asks about a wound—whether the matter of eleven years ago still slept quiet. And I remember who read that letter aloud at the breakfast table, laughing, saying old scandals made fine entertainment."

"I don't know what you're—"

"You know *everything*," Maren said, and the words came out with the weight of a bell striking. "You knew before you ever set foot in this town. That anonymous letter that fetched you here—who do you think carried the knowledge that put it in your hand? You came to Vesterhavn knowing there was a child, knowing there was a deed, knowing precisely which wounds to press to open this family like a locked box. Don't stand in my kitchen and ask who left that baby as though you didn't already have her measured and priced. You knew of Ilsa all along."

The name fell into the room—Ilsa, spoken aloud for the first time to anyone but Wendell and the sleeping child herself—and Maren watched it land. Watched Astrid's composure crack, just at the corner of one eye, just for a heartbeat, before the steel came back up.

"Ilsa," Astrid repeated. Almost gently. As though tasting it.

"That's her name," Maren said. "Wendell gave it to her without deciding to, the way good things happen. And she is not evidence and she is not leverage, and if you try to make her either—if you so much as speak her name in the magistrate's office—I will tell him everything I've just told you. Every name, every date, every payment. Do you understand me? Your claim dies in that room the moment they start asking how the robbed branch of this family treated its own blood. Inheritance claims invite scrutiny, Miss Astrid. I'd wager someone's told you that already."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The kettle sighed itself cool on the stove. Out in the entryway the wind worried at the door, patient as always, wanting in.

Then Astrid did a thing Maren had not expected. She sat down. Slowly, all the dueling posture gone out of her, and she pressed both hands flat against the table as if steadying it, or herself.

"Eleven years ago," she said quietly. "You held her when she was hours old."

"I did."

"And now you hold her again." Astrid looked up, and there was something naked in her face that no magistrate would ever be permitted to see—something that looked, astonishingly, like envy. "Do you know what I was doing eleven years ago? Learning to run a household on a husband's salary that never stretched, writing letters to relatives who'd stopped answering, telling myself the Eriksen name would amount to something someday if I just—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "Someone in this family has spent thirty years letting other people pay for what happened. My uncle. My father's ghost. Now my brother wants to spend himself the same way, standing in front of that tower signing his name like it costs nothing. And you—" she gestured at Maren, at the apron, at the whole kitchen, the whole architecture of service and silence, "—you've been paying longest of all."

"Aye," Maren said simply. "I have."

"And you'd pay more. For a child that isn't yours."

"She's everyone's and no one's, that one. Same as the rest of us." Maren picked up the cooling tea and poured it out the window for the cats, a small ceremony of ending. "But somebody has to keep her, and it seems it's fallen to people who keep things. Clocks. Secrets. Children." She turned back. "Go home, Miss Astrid. Think on what you mean to do before the year turns, because I'm done keeping your family's secrets out of fear. What I keep now, I keep out of choice—and I'll choose differently than you expect."

Astrid stood. She wound her scarf with slow hands, and at the door she paused, her back to the kitchen.

"The letter," she said. "The one that came to Kristiansand last spring, unsigned. Asking after Halvard's conscience." Her voice was careful again, but underneath it ran something else—a wire of dread, taut and singing. "Did it say who sent it? Did it sign itself in any fashion? Was there a postmark?"

"Abroad," Maren said. "That's all I'll say. And that's more than I should."

Astrid nodded once, as though a sum had finally balanced, and went out into the snow.

* * *

Maren climbed the tower stairs slowly, one hand on the cold stone wall, the way she did when her knees complained and her conscience complained louder. At the landing outside the clock room she paused, because Wendell was in there—she could hear him, the small brass music of his tools, the murmured half-sentences he spoke to the mechanism as though it were a horse he was gentling—and she found she could not yet face him. What she had just done would reach him soon enough; secrets, once broken, travel fast through a house with thin walls. Let him come to her.

Instead she went on up, to the little room off the keeper's quarters where Ilsa slept in a drawer lined with flannel, and she lifted the child out of her blankets and held her against the window where the gray afternoon light could find her face.

"Well," Maren said to her softly. "It's done. I've gone and broken it, the promise. Thirty years I kept my mouth shut for other people, and six weeks of you undid it." Ilsa regarded her with that grave dark gaze, one fist working free of the blanket, and gripped a strand of Maren's hair with the sudden fierce strength of the very young. Maren laughed—a wet, surprised sound—and pressed her lips to the small forehead. "Aye. That's about the size of it. You hold tight, little one. Seems that's the whole trade now, holding tight to things that ought to have been let go long ago."

Below her, through the floorboards, she felt rather than heard the master clock shudder and begin to strike the hour—four slow bronze notes rolling down through the tower, out over the roofs toward the frozen harbor.

And out on the white road below the observatory wall, small and sharp against the snow, Astrid Eriksen was not walking home. She was walking, Maren saw with a slow settling of the heart, toward the harbor office—toward the magistrate—her head bent against the wind and her steps quick and deliberate, like a woman who has just discovered that the truth is a currency, and means to spend every coin of it before anyone can stop her.
