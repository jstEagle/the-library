## 13. Two Names on One Deed

The storm had spent itself by morning, leaving the harbor strewn with its leavings—kelp in dark ropes along the quay, a fish crate split open on the stones, the masts of the stranded fleet swaying like a forest deciding whether to stand. The air smelled of salt and coal smoke and something colder beneath both, the mineral breath of a winter that had only just begun to mean what it said.

Wendell found Astrid in the observatory's lower parlor, standing at the long window with her hands folded at her waist, watching the ships she had learned to price in a single glance. She had dressed for the occasion, as she dressed for every occasion—the gray traveling wool, the brooch shaped like a thistle, her hair pinned so precisely it seemed drawn on with ink. She did not turn when he entered. She never needed to. He had known her twenty-six years; he could feel her attention the way one feels a change in barometric pressure.

"You've been up all night again," she said. "I can hear it in your feet. You walk like a man apologizing to the floor."

"The child slept. I didn't."

"Pity." She turned then, and her face was composed into the expression he dreaded most—not cruelty, which he could answer, but reasonableness, which he could not. "Sit down, Wendell. We should speak plainly while the house is empty. Maren has taken Ilsa down to see the gulls, and Halvard is sleeping, or pretending to, which amounts to the same charity."

He did not sit. He stood by the cold hearth with his hands in his pockets, feeling there the folded edge of the deed he had carried against his hip for three days now, as though it might warm him. Two names on one page: his father's, in ink gone brown as old blood, and beneath it, added in a different hand entirely, the name of the other branch of the family—the line Astrid had decided, somewhere between the harbor office and the magistrate's drawing room, was owed them everything.

"I spoke with the harbormaster this morning," Wendell said.

"Of course you did. You speak with everyone. It's your one talent I never managed to envy."

"The *Meridian* sails on the thirty-first. With the tide, before the year turns. There is one berth left. One passenger berth, Wendell—one, on the last ship out before the new century, and after her, nothing until the ice clears in March."

Astrid's eyes were the same gray-green as their mother's had been, but where their mother's eyes had softened when she looked at you, Astrid's performed an arithmetic. He watched the numbers move now.

"And?" she said.

"And you went to the magistrate yesterday. Maren told me. You showed him the deed."

"Maren," Astrid repeated, softly, tasting the name. "Maren who won't say where she comes from. Maren who burns notes and hides babies and calls it loyalty. Yes, I showed the magistrate the deed, because a deed hidden in a gear tooth is worth nothing, and a deed shown to the right man is worth a shipyard." She crossed to the small table where the decanter stood and poured herself sherry she would not drink—she never drank it; she simply liked holding something while she thought. "You've read it, of course. You've had it in your pocket so long you're wearing a hole in your coat. Father's name, Wendell. Our father's name, on papers granting title to land and shares and a house in Christiania that could keep ten generations in comfort. Papers that should have come to us. Papers our uncle let the sea swallow—or claimed he did—and kept instead in a clock, like a spider keeping flies."

"He kept them for a reason."

"He kept them because he is a coward, and cowards are excellent archivists." She set the glass down without touching it to her lips. "Halvard is dying, Wendell. We both know it. The confession he's dribbling out to you at midnight is worth exactly as much as the paper it isn't written on. But the deed is written. The deed exists. And the magistrate has confirmed for me that a claim properly filed—properly witnessed, properly pursued—would restore to our family everything that was stolen from it. The house. The shares. The name, unbesmirched. Do you understand what that means? We would be *somebody* again. Not a clockmaker's orphan and a seamstress's charity case. Eriksens. With property. With standing."

"We were always somebody."

"Speak for yourself." Her voice did not rise. It never rose; it sharpened, like wire drawn through a plate. "I have spent eleven years hemming other women's skirts and smiling at women who pretended they couldn't remember my surname. I have eaten dinners of bread and resentment and thanked God for the bread. You hid in gears and springs where nobody could ask you who your people were. Don't tell me we were somebody. I did the arithmetic you refused to do."

Outside, a gull cried over the stranded fleet, and the wind worried at the window frame with thin fingers. Wendell took his hands from his pockets. They felt strange out in the open air, empty, unaccustomed.

"The magistrate asked you how the deed left the family," he said. "I know he did. It's the first question any honest man would ask. A document that vanished thirty years ago reappearing in the hands of a dressmaker's daughter from Sandvik—"

"*Eriksen*."

"—from nowhere," he continued, "and the second question, the one he hasn't asked yet because he's waiting, the patient ones always wait—is *what else* came back with it."

The room went very quiet. The master clock above them was not yet running—its silence filled the tower like held breath—but Wendell had lived so long among clocks that he heard time anyway, in the tick of the cooling stove, in the creak of the house adjusting itself to the tide.

"What else came back with it," Astrid said slowly, "is a baby."

"Yes."

"A baby left in a basket with a note telling us to forget her face. A baby wrapped in wool bearing a crest that may or may not mean anything. A baby who appears, by the strangest of coincidences, at the precise moment a deed surfaces—don't look at me like that, Wendell, I can add. Someone sent her here. Someone connected to those papers. Which makes her not a tragedy, whatever you've decided to make her in your sleepless nights. It makes her evidence."

"She's six weeks old."

"Evidence doesn't have an age." Astrid picked up the glass again, and this time she did drink, a small precise swallow, and he understood she needed it, and that frightened him more than anything else she'd said. "Listen to me. I love you. I want you to remember, afterward, whatever happens, that I loved you first and reasoned second, because you will want to forget it. But hear the reasoning. That child is the thread that leads back to whoever sent her, and whoever sent her knows how our father died, or didn't die, and where those papers traveled, and why. She is leverage. She is the one thing we hold that the other side of this transaction wants badly enough to pay for. And there is a ship leaving on the thirty-first with one berth left—"

"No."

"—with one berth left," she repeated, over him, "which is worth more than money, because money can be counted and a berth cannot. There are people in this town right now, Wendell, respectable people, desperate people, who would pay anything to be out of Denmark before the inquiry the magistrate is contemplating, and we hold the key to the last door. Sell the berth. Sell the information the child represents. Take the proceeds, file the claim, and by spring we will have the house in Christiania and a life in which neither of us ever hems another stranger's skirt."

"You want to sell her."

"I want to *price* her," Astrid said, and her voice cracked on the word, just slightly, just enough that he saw beneath the wire to the girl underneath, the girl who had once sat up all night with him when he had fever, reading him stories from a torn newspaper because they owned no books. "There is a difference, whatever you'd like to believe. I am not suggesting we hand her to a slaver. I'm suggesting we discover who wants her found and negotiate. That is not monstrosity, Wendell. That is the world. The world prices everything—labor, grief, honor, names. I learned it across a sewing table. You learned it bent over a workbench, charging by the wheel. The only people who pretend otherwise are the people who can afford to."

He looked at his sister for a long moment. The light through the window had gone silver and hard, storm-washed, and it lay across her face like the frost it would become by evening. She was beautiful in the way of things built to withstand weather, and he loved her with a love that had no convenient shape, that could not be argued with or filed smooth, and he understood, standing there, that this was the true sabotage—not the filed gear, not the stopped clock, but two people who shared a childhood looking at one infant and seeing two entirely different objects: a sister, and a sum.

"When Father left," Wendell said quietly, "you were nine."

"Eight. You were two weeks past your first birthday. You wouldn't remember, which I suppose is your privilege."

"I remember being told he drowned. I remember Mother going gray in a single winter. I remember you deciding, somewhere in there, that the world was a ledger and we were on the wrong side of it." He stepped away from the hearth. "And I understand it, Astrid. God help me, I understand it better every day I spend in this house. But if we sell that child—if we make her a line item—Ilsa becomes exactly what Halvard made of Father: a person erased so that a story could balance. And I will not carry that into a new century. I'd rather carry the debt."

"The debt," Astrid said, "is a fantasy. The debt is a feeling. The claim is *real*, Wendell. It has paper behind it. Feelings don't keep you warm in March."

"They keep you human in December."

She laughed then—a short, sharp sound with nothing in it of laughter—and turned back to the window. Below, on the quay, he could see Maren's dark figure, the bundled white shape in her arms lifted briefly toward the sky. Showing her the gulls. Even now, even in the middle of everything, some part of the household still insisted on tenderness, and the sight of it hurt him like pressure on a bruise.

"The widow Rask has offered to take her," Wendell said. "You know this. She sails on the thirty-first, to her cousins abroad, and she has nursed six children of her own and buried four, and she has a softness about her that Ilsa needs, and cousins with farms, with milk, with distance. One berth, Astrid. I'm asking you to stand with me in giving it to her."

"And what happens to the inquiry? What happens when the magistrate decides that a motherless infant abandoned at an observatory is parish business? You've seen how towns treat foundlings, Wendell. The workhouse at Helsingør has a mortality list longer than the shipping register. You would keep her here, visible, claimable, while you perform your little redemption at the ledger with your name signed in public—and she pays the interest."

"At least she'd be paid in kind, not sold."

"At least *sold* she'd be safe."

They faced each other across the cold hearth, and the words hung between them like smoke neither could fan away. Somewhere above them, in the tower, the great dead clock waited with its secret emptied out, its hollow gear holding nothing now but the memory of paper. Wendell thought of his father writing letters from abroad, letters Halvard had answered, and wondered which of them, in the end, had been the one who priced his family correctly.

"There's something else," Astrid said at last, and her voice changed—the arithmetic pausing, something almost gentle moving underneath. "You should hear it before you decide I'm a monster. If you give the berth to the child, I'll file the claim alone. I'll pursue it alone, sign alone, stand in whatever rooms alone. I'll do it because someone in this family must stop being poor, and it clearly isn't going to be you. But understand what it costs, brother. To stand alone in those rooms, I will need the magistrate's goodwill. And his goodwill will need the answers to questions you don't want asked. About the baby. About where she came from. About whether a clockmaker who can't account for a foundling ought to be trusted with an observatory's time." She paused. "Your redemption and my ambition, Wendell, are sailing on the same ship. And there is one berth."

The stove ticked. Outside, the tide was turning; he could feel it in the floorboards, the whole coast exhaling.

"And Halvard?" Wendell asked. "Does he get a vote?"

"Halvard," said Astrid, lifting her chin, "has until Christmas. Ask him quickly."

* * *

He climbed the tower stairs because his legs wanted motion and the tower was the only direction left. Ninety-one steps he had counted on his first night, and he counted them again now, letting the numbers steady him the way numbers always had, and came out into the round stone room where the master clock stood silent with its pendulum hanging like a held tongue.

The deed was in his coat. He drew it out and unfolded it on the lead-topped workbench, smoothing the creases with the flat of his hand the way he smoothed a warped brass plate—gently, patiently, as though patience could make a thing true. His father's name. The other name beneath. Thirty years folded into a gear tooth, and now unfolded under a gray window while the tide turned below and a child who was either his niece or his leverage laughed somewhere in the wind at gulls.

Two names on one deed. He had assumed it meant the ink.

But standing there, with Astrid's voice still ringing in the stairwell—*your redemption and my ambition are sailing on the same ship*—he understood, with the cold clarity of a level finding its bubble, that the two names were theirs. His and hers. Brother and sister, written on the same claim, pulling the same paper in opposite directions, and the paper would tear before the year was out unless one of them let go.

From below, faintly, came the sound of the front door, and footsteps on the bottom stair—light, quick, deliberate, climbing.

Astrid was coming up.
