## 14. The Clockmaker's Ledger

The tower room above the clock chamber had a smell all its own—not dust exactly, though there was dust enough, but the smell of paper that has been shut away from weather for thirty years: dry, faintly sweet, like the ghost of smoke in an unlit chimney. Wendell had passed its door a hundred times since his arrival at Vesterhavn, had felt the draft of it against his knuckles each time he climbed to service the escapement, and had never once tried the handle. It was Halvard's room. Whatever lived up there belonged to the keeper as surely as the tide belonged to the moon.

But Halvard was sleeping now—Maren's laudanum, measured out in drops smaller than hope—and the house below him had gone quiet in the way houses only go quiet in deep winter, when the sea itself seems to hold its breath between gales. Ilsa slept in Maren's room, her small breath rising and falling like something on a calm sea. And Wendell stood in the corridor with a candle in one hand and his own heartbeat in his ears, trying the handle of the tower room door.

It was not locked. Of course it was not locked. A man who wanted his secrets kept would have locked them away; a man who left the door open was either too proud or too tired—or, Wendell thought later, had been waiting all these years for someone with a reason to come in.

The candle found shelves first. Rows of them, built into the curve of the stone wall, sagging under the weight of ledgers bound in gray sailcloth, observation books, almanacs going back decades, boxes of correspondence tied with tarred string. Then it found the desk—a ship's table bolted to the floor, scarred with ring-marks from a hundred mugs of coffee—and on the desk, square in the center as if placed there yesterday, a ledger newer than the rest, its cloth cover rubbed pale at the corners by handling.

Wendell set the candle down and did not touch anything for a long moment. He was a clockmaker; he understood the ethics of opening cases. Some movements you could disturb without harm. Others would run backward, or stop entirely, the moment you interfered.

He opened the ledger.

* * *

It was not, strictly, a logbook, though it wore the clothes of one. The columns were ruled for wind and barometer and the times of transit observations, and Halvard had filled them faithfully, his small precise hand marching down the years—*transit observed 2h 14m 06s; glass falling; wind backing NNW*—but threaded through the meteorology, in the margins, in the blank pages at the back of each volume, ran another record entirely.

Wendell turned page after page, and the tower room grew warmer around him, or seemed to, though the candle burned no brighter.

Because there, in the margin beside an entry from March 1871, in handwriting Wendell knew better than his own—handwriting he had watched form itself across shopping lists and letters and the flyleaves of his childhood books—was a line:

*Letter received. He says the oranges grow wild along the walls.*

His father's handwriting. His father's words. In his uncle's ledger, filed between a barometric reading and a note about the fog signal, like contraband hidden in plain sight.

Wendell sat down on the desk's stool because his legs declined to do otherwise. He turned the page with fingers that had disassembled movements finer than spider silk and now trembled like a novice's.

April 1871: *Letter received. Says the boy will be seven soon. Asks after Astrid by name.* In the column where the wind should be recorded, Halvard had written nothing at all.

June 1871: *Reply sent. Told him the boy is apprenticing. Did not tell him where.*

Wendell read on, and the years peeled away like leaves off a branch. His father—the man who had walked into the sea off Skagen harbor in November of 1869, or so the family had been told, so the insurance papers said, so the stone in the churchyard claimed with its patient carved dates—wrote from Marseille. From Genoa. From a port called Oran that Wendell had to find on the atlas in the corner, tracing the African coast with a fingertip gone cold. Twice a year, then three times, then once a year, regular as an equinox, the letters arrived and were recorded in this ledger and answered, and every answer was noted here too, in Halvard's cramped hand, a correspondence conducted across two continents and thirty years of deliberate silence.

Alive. Not drowned. Alive in 1875, alive in 1882, alive in 1890—

Wendell's eye went down the column of years the way a man walks toward a cliff edge knowing what is at the bottom of it.

The last entry was dated the fourteenth of September, 1897. *Letter received. Says his health holds. Says he thinks of the clock. Asks again if the boy knows.* And beneath it, in Halvard's hand, the reply recorded: *Answered. Still no.*

Still no. Two words, and beneath their weight Wendell felt the whole architecture of his life shift and settle into a new configuration, the way a building settles when some buried timber finally rots through. All those years. All the winters he and Astrid had gone hungry in the flat above the shop while neighbors whispered about the Eriksen curse, the Eriksen luck, the father who had taken the boat out and not come back. All the nights he had lain awake composing speeches to a dead man—explanations, accusations, forgivements rehearsed to a headstone. His father had been reading letters under orange trees on the Mediterranean while his children ate bread and pretended it was enough.

And Halvard had known. Had stood in this very tower, keeping perfect time for the ships of Vesterhavn, winding the master clock with hands that held the truth of it, and had written *still no* year after year after year.

* * *

The candle had burned down two fingers' width before Wendell moved again. Grief came first—he was surprised by that; he had expected fury to arrive alone—but grief came first, sharp and stupid and tender, grief for a man who was not dead, which is a different animal entirely from the other kind. The dead are finished with you. The living have simply chosen elsewhere. Somewhere south of here, in a city where oranges grew wild along the walls, a man with Wendell's own crooked knuckles and his own habit of humming when he worked was growing old under a name that was not his, and might, if the sea and his health held, still be reachable by post.

Reachable. The word sat in Wendell's chest like a swallowed coal. Reachable, and unanswered for thirty years, because Halvard had decided the silence served everyone. Served the father running from whatever he had done with the deed. Served the family spared the scandal. Served Halvard himself, Wendell thought, and here the coal flared—served Halvard best of all, Halvard who got to keep his clean reputation and his lighthouse-clean conscience while his brother's children grew up half-orphaned on a lie he maintained with the same meticulous care he gave the escapement.

He pulled the ledger toward him and began at the beginning, properly this time, reading every entry, and the shape of it assembled itself in his mind the way a movement assembled on the bench: wheel by wheel, until the whole mechanism stood revealed.

The first letter had come eight months after the disappearance. Wendell read the marginal note twice to be certain. *Received, care of the harbor office, no return address. He confesses everything. Says the deed was his only leverage and he had to use it. Says he will send for the children when he is settled.* And then, in a hand pressed harder than the others, the nib nearly cutting the paper: *I wrote back that they are better off thinking him dead. God forgive me.*

God forgive me. Written once, in 1870, and never written again. Thirty subsequent replies, and not one repetition of the plea—as if forgiveness, once requested, could be assumed forever after, like a debt paid in installments and forgotten.

There were other things in the ledger too, things that made Wendell's skin prickle. Small sums entered in the accounts pages, marked only with dates and initials: *J.M., 4 kr.; J.M., 4 kr.; J.M., 6 kr.* Paid quarterly, beginning in the spring of 1870 and continuing—Wendell checked—to within the last two years. He did not know who J.M. was. But he knew what money paid quietly and quarterly usually bought, and he thought of Maren's face, watchful and kind and closed, and felt something turn over in him that he was not ready to examine.

There was a letter folded into the back cover of the ledger, and his hands were on it, were actually unfolding it, when he heard the stair creak.

* * *

He had time only to close the ledger over the letter and turn. Maren came up the last steps with the candle she carried held low, and its light climbed her slowly—apron, shawl, chin, eyes—and stopped at her eyes, which went from Wendell to the open ledger and back again with a look he had seen on her face only once before, the morning the basket had appeared at the door.

"You should be sleeping," she said.

"So should you."

"I'm too old for much sleep and too honest for much of it anyway." She came to stand beside the desk, and she did not look at the ledger again, which was itself a kind of statement. "Halvard wakes at four. He'll want his drops."

"Maren." Wendell's voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw. "My father."

It was not a question, but she heard everything inside it. Her hands found each other in her apron and held on. For a long moment the only sound was the great clock below them, turning over its seconds like coins it could not spend, and the wind worrying the stonework at the north face.

"He wrote," Maren said at last. "Yes."

"Thirty years of letters, and he let us bury an empty grave."

"He let—" She stopped. Corrected herself with visible effort. "*Halvard* let. Don't fold them together in your mind, boy. They aren't the same man, whatever blood they share."

"Halvard answered every one of them." Wendell stood, and the stool scraped, and somewhere below a rafter shifted with the wind. "Every single one. He knew my father was alive in 1870. Knew it when he sat at our table at Christmas. Knew it when Astrid cried herself sick the second winter. He sat across from two children and let them grieve a living man because it was tidier."

"Was it tidy?" Maren's voice cracked suddenly, unexpectedly, like ice giving way. "Do you think it was *tidy*, keeping a secret that size? Watching a man carry it up these stairs every day of his life?" She shook her head. "You don't know what you've read. You've read dates and margins. You haven't read the letters themselves."

"Then show me the letters."

"They're not mine to show."

"They're not his to hide."

For a moment they faced each other over the desk like opposing counsel, and Wendell saw something move behind Maren's eyes—calculation, or fear, or the exhaustion of a woman who had been carrying her portion of other people's secrets for longer than he had been alive. When she spoke again her voice had gentled, and somehow that was worse.

"Wendell. There are letters and there are letters. There are the ones your father wrote, yes. But there's one more—one that came to this house before any of them, one that started all of it." She looked toward the dark window, where the sea was invisible but audible, a vast slow breathing beyond the stone. "One that asks a person to keep watch over a conscience. And a person can keep watch for so long before she starts to wonder whether she's guarding the prisoner or the jail."

The words landed strangely—too heavy, too old, weighted with a history he couldn't yet parse—and Wendell gripped the desk's edge. "Whose conscience?"

Maren picked up his guttering candle as though it were hers, as though the exchange were the most ordinary thing in the world, and set it burning steady.

"The letters are in the sea chest under Halvard's bed," she said. "All of them, in order, wrapped in oilcloth, the way a man keeps things he means someday to give over. Take them. Read them. You've earned the truth more than anyone living." At the stairhead she paused, her back to him, her hand on the rail worn smooth by thirty years of her own passing. "But when you've read them, don't decide tonight what your father was. Decide tomorrow what you are. There's a difference, and it matters more than you know."

Her footsteps descended and faded, and the house closed around her silence again.

* * *

The sea chest yielded its treasure without resistance, as if it had been waiting too. Wendell carried the oilcloth packet to the desk, laid it open, and there they were: letters in his father's hand, dozens of them, sorted by year, the oldest browned and soft at the folds, the newest crisp enough that he knew without checking which envelope held the last.

He did not read them in order. He reached instead for the most recent, the September letter, the one whose marginal summary had already broken him open—*says his health holds; says he thinks of the clock*—and unfolded it under the candle with hands that refused steadiness.

*My dear Halvard,* it began, and the voice rose off the page so completely that Wendell looked up, absurdly, half expecting the room to hold the man himself—*My dear Halvard, another autumn here, and I am told my heart is stubborn as the rest of me. The doctor prescribes rest, which I take to mean he has run out of ideas. I think of the clock often. Do the escapement teeth still wear unevenly on the third wheel? I have dreamed twice of repairing it, and in the dreams my hands are young. Tell me about the boy. You never tell me about the boy. Is he tall? Does he have the patience? I was not patient at his age, and look where it landed me. If he ever learns the truth—if it ever stops being kinder to keep it—tell him the deed was never about the money. Tell him a man can only run so far from his own name before he starts writing it out just to see it. Yours, in exile that stopped being romantic twenty years ago, Kristoffer.*

Kristoffer. His father's name, spoken by his father's own pen, after thirty years of hearing it only in the past tense, spoken over a grave. Wendell read the signature four times. Then he laid the letter flat on the desk, very carefully, the way one lays down something breakable, and put his face in his hands, and the tower room held him while he wept—for the tallness his father would never know, for the patience he had learned too late to matter, for the name written out across the water by a man running from it, still running, perhaps, or perhaps long since standing still and waiting.

When he raised his head the candle had burned almost to nothing. Outside the arrow-slit window the sky had begun, imperceptibly, toward gray. Below him the master clock ticked on, faithful, ignorant, and Wendell understood suddenly why Halvard had wound it every day of his life with such ferocious devotion: it was the only thing in Vesterhavn that had never been lied to.

He gathered the letters—all of them—and squared their edges on the desk. His fury was still there, banked and real, a furnace that would need feeding in the days to come. Halvard would wake at four and want his drops, and Wendell would sit beside him and place the last letter on the blanket, and ask him, finally, face to face, why *still no*—why thirty years of answers when a single visit, a single ticket south, a single sentence spoken over Sunday dinner would have cost so little and returned so much.

But that reckoning waited for morning. What would not wait—what had already put its hand on the door of him and would not be argued back—was simpler and larger: his father was alive. Might, with a stubborn heart, be alive still. There existed in the world a direction one could walk, a port one could reach, a door one could knock on, and behind it a man named Kristoffer Eriksen who dreamed of young hands on the third wheel.

Wendell snuffed the candle as the first true light came thin and cold through the slit. And it was then, folding the last of the oilcloth, that his thumb caught on something sewn into its lining—a stiffness, a paper thickness, older and yellower than any letter. He worked it free with his fingernails.

A single sheet, folded twice, unsigned, in a woman's hand. He read it once by the graying window light, and read it again, and the floor of everything he thought he knew tilted beneath him like a deck in weather.

*Watch him,* it said. *Keep his conscience awake if you can. And when the child comes—and she will come—remember whose she is, and whose she is not.*
