## 15. Midnight Rehearsal

Maren had kept house at Vesterhavn for twenty-six years, and in all that time she had learned that a household announced its future in small sounds: the particular creak of a stair before bad news arrived, the way a kettle sang flat when grief was in the room, the rhythm of a man's boots on the tower steps—hurried meant fear, measured meant resolve, and dragging, dragging meant he had been sitting with the dying keeper again, learning something he did not wish to carry.

On the last morning of the year she woke before dawn to the sound of Wendell's boots on those stairs, hurried and then slowing, hurried and then slowing, as though he could not decide which man he wanted to be when he reached the bottom.

She rose and lit the stove and set the kettle on, and while it heated she stood at the kitchen window and watched the harbor assemble itself out of the dark. The storm of the week past had spent itself; the water lay flat and pewter-colored, and riding at anchor in the roadstead was the *Solveig*, her masts bare against the gray sky, coaled and provisioned and waiting for the tide that would come with the turning of the year. The last ship out before the new one began. There were people in town who said she would carry the first mail of the century to ports Maren had only seen on charts, and there were other people who said nothing at all about the *Solveig* because they could not bear to.

Maren was of the second kind this morning.

She had been of the second kind for eleven days now, ever since the evening she had sat across from Astrid Eriksen in the parlor with the lamp turned low and told her everything—the crest, the delivery, the summer night nineteen years ago when a frightened girl from the other branch of the family had been brought to her back door, and what Maren had promised, and whom she had promised it to. She had broken a silence she had kept longer than most marriages last, and she had done it because Astrid had a way of asking questions like a customs officer has a way of opening trunks, and because Maren had understood, finally, that the silence was no longer protecting anyone. It was only growing heavier, the way ice grows heavier on a roof until the beams groan.

The beams were groaning now.

She heard Ilsa wake—not crying, thank God, but making her small talking sounds, the syllables without meaning that the child strung together like beads—and Maren went up to the little room under the eaves where the girl slept in a cot that Halvard had once used for storing almanacs.

"There you are," Maren said, lifting her. "There you are, my lamb."

Ilsa weighed almost nothing and also everything. Nineteen months old now, or near enough—Maren counted her age from the night at the back door, though the note pinned to the wool blanket had said otherwise, and the note had been a lie, and Maren had known it was a lie even as she read it, because she had held this child before, smaller, screaming, brand-new, in a rented room above the chandler's shop with rain coming down like judgment. Forget the face, the note had said. As if a face could be forgotten. As if Maren had not watched that face being made, red and furious and astonishing, in the light of a single candle.

She dressed the child by the window while the sky went from charcoal to slate to a thin, reluctant silver. Downstairs the kettle was complaining. In the tower above them all, behind three feet of granite, the master clock ticked on—she felt it more than heard it, the way you feel your own pulse, a great patient heart in the bones of the building, keeping time for every ship and every soul in Vesterhavn.

Wendell had repaired it twice now. Twice sabotaged, twice mended. Maren knew things about those filed gears that Wendell did not know she knew, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed coin.

But that was not today's work. Today's work was wool.

* * *

She had bought the cloth in November from the draper on Harbor Street, two yards of good navy melton with a lining of soft gray flannel, and she had told herself then what she had been telling herself every day since: that children need warm coats, that coastal winters are cruel, that a growing girl cannot go wrapped in shawls forever. These were all true things. They were true the way the tide is high—factually, relentlessly, and without any reference whatsoever to the thing she was actually doing, which was sewing a coat for a farewell.

The *Solveig* sailed with the midnight tide. There was one berth left. There had been one berth left since the harbormaster's list was posted, and everyone in the household had walked past the knowledge of it for days as carefully as one walks past thin ice, testing, not speaking.

Wendell had not decided. Maren could see the indecision in him the way she could see weather coming in his uncle's knuckles. He loved the child—God help him, he had named her without deciding to, Ilsa, spoken into the dark of the clock room one exhausted night, and Maren had heard it through the floor and wept into her dishwater—but loving a child and keeping a child were different countries, and between them lay the magistrate's inquiry, spreading like spilled ink. Astrid had sold the deed's story to the authorities, or parts of it, and questions had begun arriving on official paper, and official paper in Vesterhavn ended in parish custody, in foundling registers, in children raised by strangers who fed them adequately and never once called them lamb.

So Maren sewed.

She set up at the kitchen table in the best light, with Ilsa on a quilt at her feet chewing the wooden horse Maren had carved for her, and she cut the melton along chalk lines she had drawn and redrawn until they were true. The scissors made their small decisive sounds. Outside, the harbor glinted. Somewhere below the floorboards the clock ticked, and she worked in time with it, stitch and tick, stitch and tick, as if the great machine beneath the house were sewing alongside her, keeping her seams even.

A traveling coat needed to be generous, she told herself. Room to grow. A child sailing to warmer climates—or colder, no one would say exactly where the widow's relatives lived, only that it was far and safe and that Mrs. Brandt was a godly woman with steady hands—would need a coat with years in it. Maren cut the sleeves long and let down the hem allowance twice. She sewed a double row of stitching at every stress point, because a coat that failed in a foreign wind was a failure that could not be mended from across the sea, and she put her whole attention into the collar, turning and pressing it until it stood soft and round as a cupped hand.

"You'll be smart as a captain," she told Ilsa, who banged the wooden horse against the floor and informed her, at length and unintelligibly, of something urgent. "Yes, I know. Yes. You always know."

The truth was in the coat whether she stitched it in or not. She knew that. Wool holds intention the way it holds warmth. Every seam she ran was a sentence in an argument she was having with Wendell without words: *this child must go where the inquiry cannot follow; this child must be given away so that she may be kept.* And underneath that sentence, in thread finer than any she owned, another one: *forgive me. I have done this before. I gave you away once already, in a manner of speaking, when I carried you to that door and wrote that lie about your age, and I have been paying for it in silence ever since.*

The morning wore on. She basted the lining. She felled the inner seams flat so nothing rough would touch the child's skin. Her mother had taught her that, forty years ago and four hundred miles south, in a kitchen not so different from this one except that it had smelled of peat instead of salt. *A garment is a promise you make to someone else's winter,* her mother used to say. Maren had thought it was just a saying. Everything her mother said had turned out, in the end, to be instructions.

* * *

At noon Halvard asked to be carried down to the kitchen.

This was unprecedented. The keeper had not left the tower room in nine days; Dr. Kvist came up to him like a tide table, regular and grim. But today Halvard wanted the kitchen, wanted the smell of bread and the ordinary noise of living, and so Wendell bore him down the winding stairs on his back like a man carrying sacks of flour, and Maren cleared the big chair by the stove and packed it round with blankets, and the dying man sat in her kitchen looking smaller than the child beside him.

He was a skeleton upholstered in wool. His hands, those hands that had adjusted the great escapement ten thousand times, lay on the blanket like dropped tools. But his eyes were still quick—quicker than they had any right to be, quick with guilt mostly, and lately with a terrible urgency, as if he were trying to finish an accounting before the ledger was taken from him.

"Smells like my mother's kitchen," he said.

"It does not," Maren said, stirring the pot. "Your mother burned water."

That surprised a laugh out of him, and the laugh cost him a coughing fit, and while Wendell held the glass of water to his lips Maren watched the two of them—the nephew and the uncle, thirty years of silence standing in the room between them like a third person—and felt the old ache of watching people circle the one thing they needed to say.

Halvard had confessed to Wendell in fragments, Maren knew. The father not drowned. The deed taken. The sea blamed. He had begged for documents to be burned and Wendell had refused, and now Halvard was writing instead—a confession in his cramped keeper's hand, pages of it accumulating in the drawer of the tower desk, and Maren had seen Wendell reading them at night with a face like a man reading his own autopsy.

"The clock will ring the century in properly?" Halvard asked. "You're certain?"

"Certain," Wendell said. "The escapement is true. I've rehearsed the strike twelve times."

"Rehearsed." Halvard smiled at the ceiling. "A rehearsal for midnight. Thirty years I've kept this clock so that nothing in this harbor would be uncertain, and I couldn't keep one honest hour in my own family." His eyes found Maren then, and there it was again, the look that was part apology and part accusation and entirely unbearable. "Maren keeps things too. Better than I do. Worse than I do."

"Hush," Maren said, too sharply, and turned back to her pot so neither of them would see what her face was doing.

Because he was right, and because he was wrong, and because both at once was more than she could hold while the gravy needed skimming. She *had* kept things. She had kept the secret of Ilsa's birth for nineteen months and the secret of the family's shame for thirty years, and she had filed the gears of the master clock with her own hands on two black October nights, kneeling in the clock room with a file borrowed from Wendell's own case, her heart going like a trapped bird, delaying ships, delaying departures, buying hours for the truth the way a miser buys candles—one at a time, each one precious, each one a small sin committed against the whole town's trust in time.

She had done it because of a letter. Because seven years ago a letter had reached her postmarked from a port she'd had to look up, in handwriting she recognized from old invoices in Halvard's files—the father's hand, alive somewhere, asking her only this: *watch over my brother's conscience, and when the moment comes, don't let the sea keep the blame.* She had read it a hundred times. She had not yet shown it to Wendell. There had not seemed to be a right day, and now there were only hours left, and the letter was folded in the lining pocket of her apron, and she could feel its corners against her hip like a held breath.

Tonight, she told herself. After midnight, perhaps. When the clock had struck and the ship had gone and there was no more use for delay. Tonight she would put the letter in Wendell's hands and stand whatever judgment followed, and the gears she had filed and the promise she had kept and the promise she had broken would all be one story at last, told plainly, in daylight, like an honest woman.

"Coat," Ilsa announced suddenly, pointing a stubby finger at the half-finished garment on the table.

Everyone stopped. Even the pot stopped, almost.

"Coat," the child said again, with the tremendous authority of the very young, and toddled two steps and grabbed the hem of navy melton in her fist.

Halvard laughed until he wept, quietly, without explanation, and Wendell looked at the coat, and then at the child holding it, and then out the window toward the harbor where the *Solveig* rode at anchor, and Maren saw him do the arithmetic he had been refusing to do for days: one berth, one child, one coat already sewn. Saw him understand what her needles had been telling him all morning.

"Maren," he said, very low.

"Bread's nearly done," she said, and took the loaf from the oven because her hands required employment or they were going to shake.

* * *

In the afternoon the household fell into its strange rehearsal, and Maren moved among the preparations like a ghost invited to its own haunting.

Wendell climbed the tower and ran the striking train yet again—twelve strokes, they could hear them roll down through the floors, bronze and unhurried, the sound that every ship in the roadstead would set its final departure by. Halvard dozed in the chair and woke at each stroke and counted them with his lips. Ilsa napped on the quilt with the wooden horse in her fist. The sky outside cleared to a hard pale blue, and a northwesterly freshened, good sailing wind, cruel timing, and the flagstaff at the harbor office ran up signals Maren chose not to decode.

At four she finished the coat.

She held it up to the window light and turned it slowly, and it was good work, the best she had done in years, every seam honest, every stress point doubled, the collar soft as forgiveness. Then she sat down and did the thing she had been avoiding for six days: threaded a needle with white silk, and inside the coat, low on the lining where only a seamstress or a mother would ever find it, she embroidered three small letters. Not a name—names could be traced, names could summon magistrates. Just initials, hers and the father's intertwined, a thread of proof stitched against forgetting, against the day when a grown woman somewhere across the water might wonder where she came from and take a coat apart to find out.

"Forget the face," Maren murmured to the needle. "Never again."

Then she heard the front gate, and looked up, and saw Astrid coming up the path from the harbor road in her fine borrowed coat and her fine cold smile, and behind Astrid, at a careful distance, a figure Maren recognized with a lurch of the stomach: the magistrate's clerk, carrying his leather document case, hurrying to catch her up.

They were coming here. An hour before midnight, with the tide making and the *Solveig*'s boats already hoisted, Astrid Eriksen was climbing the observatory path with the law at her heels, and whatever she had traded to get her claim secured, Maren understood in her bones that it had cost more than she knew—and that the bill was about to be presented at this door.

"Take her up the tower," Maren said, rising, her voice steadier than her hands. "Wendell—take the child and the coat, and don't come down till I call you."

The knock came before she finished the sentence.
