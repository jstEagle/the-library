## 16. Betrayal at the Harbor Office

The harbor office smelled of tar and old decisions. Astrid Eriksen noticed the smell first—the way she always noticed things first, cataloguing them the way other women catalogued lace patterns or recipes—because noticing was a kind of ownership, and ownership was what she had come for.

Magistrate Holm's office sat above the customs shed, its windows fogged with salt spray, its single stove working hard against the December damp that crept in through every seam of the building. A ship's lantern hung from a hook by the door, unlit, its glass clouded white with age. Papers were stacked on his desk with a precision that surprised her; she had expected a man who ruled a harbor town to be sloppy, comfortable, corruptible in a lazy way. Instead the stacks sat square as masonry, each one squared to the desk's edge, and the magistrate himself rose when she entered, which she had not expected either.

"Miss Eriksen." He gestured to the chair opposite his desk. "You wrote that this concerns an inheritance."

"It concerns more than an inheritance." She untied her cloak but did not remove it—a calculated hesitation, keeping herself half-ready to leave—and sat with her spine straight, her hands folded over the satchel in her lap. Under her gloves, her fingers had gone cold on the walk up from the observatory road. The wind came off the water in long gray pushes, carrying the smell of stranded kelp and coal smoke, and she had thought, twice, about turning back. She had not turned back. She never turned back. "It concerns the name of this town, Magistrate. It concerns whether Vesterhavn is governed by records or by whatever an old dying man decides to keep hidden in a clock."

Holm settled into his chair. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the particular stillness of a man who had spent decades watching ships come in and deciding which of them to look at closely. His eyes went briefly to the satchel.

"You've been at the observatory," he said. Not a question.

"Since October. My brother is repairing the master clock." She let a small silence sit there. "And our uncle, Halvard Eriksen, is the keeper. Dying, I'm told. Slowly enough to be inconvenient."

"And the inheritance?"

Astrid opened the satchel and drew out the deed—but only partway, only enough that the wax seal showed, red as a wound against the yellowed paper. She had practiced this. On the steamer north from Aalborg she had rehearsed it in the mirror of her cabin: how far to draw the paper out, how long to hold it there, how to watch a man's face without seeming to. Their father's documents, smuggled across the sea thirty years ago and swallowed by a clock like a coin into a drain. Proof that the Eriksens had been robbed—not by pirates, not by the sea, but by their own blood.

"This names my father," she said. "It names property held in trust—land in Aarhus, shares in a shipping concern, monies that should have passed to his line and did not. My uncle knows where it has been all these years. He knew before my father ever took ship. He let the whole family believe my father drowned so that no one would ever come asking." She slid the deed back into the satchel, slowly, and buckled the strap. "I am not here to ask you to take my word for any of it, Magistrate. I am here because a claim like mine will need to be made properly. Through channels. Through you."

Holm leaned back. The stove ticked and shifted, and somewhere below them a cart rolled across the cobbles, its driver singing something tuneless against the wind.

"You understand," he said, "that inheritance claims invite scrutiny."

"I'm counting on scrutiny."

"Scrutiny does not aim itself, Miss Eriksen. It aims at everything in range." He folded his hands on the square stacks of paper. "If a deed left Denmark illegally—if it was carried out by a man fleeing obligations, then carried back in by his children—it becomes not simply a claim on property but a question of how it traveled. Questions of legitimacy. Questions of *conduct*." He said the last word gently, the way a man lays down a card he doesn't especially want to play yet. "And questions of persons attached to the household where such documents are found."

The wind pressed against the window glass and retreated. Astrid heard herself say, "What persons?"

"Your uncle keeps a household," Holm said. "A housekeeper. Your brother. And I understand there is a child."

For a moment the harbor office seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts. "A foundling," she said. "Left at the door in November. She's nothing to do with—"

"A foundling left at the door of the man named in a contested inheritance, wearing wool embroidered with the family crest?" Holm's voice stayed level, courteous, terrible. "You see how a magistrate must notice. Parish custody exists precisely for children whose origins cannot be established. Normally it takes months of correspondence. But when an inquiry into fraudulent conveyance opens a household's papers—" he spread one hand, an open palm offering nothing, "—the child becomes part of the record. Names are traced. Letters are read. And a parish overseer with a cold January ahead of him does not leave an infant in an unheated tower while he waits for the post."

"She has a name," Astrid said, and heard how quickly it came out of her, how defensive, and hated it.

"Then someone gave her one. That will be looked at too."

Outside, the fog horn sounded from the breakwater, one long mournful note that seemed to go through the walls rather than around them. Astrid looked at the deed-shaped weight in her satchel and understood, with the slow cold clarity of standing in seawater, that the conversation had already happened—that while she had been arranging her posture and her pauses, Holm had been doing arithmetic, and she had walked into his office carrying the variable that completed his equation.

She could still stand up. She could still buckle the satchel and walk out and let Halvard die with his secret and let Wendell keep his little foundling and let the Eriksen name stay buried under thirty years of salt. She could be safe.

She had crossed a sea to be in this chair.

"The deed," she said, "is genuine. I'll swear to its provenance. My uncle concealed it inside the observatory clock—my brother found it during repairs, which makes the discovery lawful, witnessed, and documented. I want a formal inquiry opened into the concealment of estate assets by the keeper of Vesterhavn Observatory." She paused. "And I want it understood, when the property is restored, that the restoration was accomplished by the daughter of the wronged party. By me. Publicly."

"Of course." Holm drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, and a pen, and dipped it. "You'll make your statement now. I'll have the clerk copy it clean tomorrow."

She dictated. Her father's name, the year of the supposed drowning, the finding of the deed in the hollowed gear, the date, the witnesses—or witness; Wendell's name she gave reluctantly, feeling it leave her like a coin dropped into deep water. Holm wrote in a quick, even hand, stopping her twice to confirm spellings, once to ask whether Halvard Eriksen was presently of sound mind, and she answered that he was dying and lucid by turns, which was true and served her purpose either way.

When it was done he blotted the pages and set them on the corner of the desk, apart from the others, and rang a small bell on his sideboard. A clerk appeared—a young man with ink on his cuffs and sleep still in his face, summoned from some room behind the shelves—and took the pages away without a word.

"That's it, then," Astrid said.

"That's the statement." Holm stood, and after a moment she stood too. At the door he stopped her with nothing but his voice. "Miss Eriksen. A kindness, freely given: the inquiry will open within the week. The parish overseer moves faster than the crown. If the child matters to anyone in your family, now is the time to think about what 'part of the record' means for a baby in winter."

"She isn't mine," Astrid said.

"No," Holm agreed. "That is exactly the problem."

* * *

She walked down to the water because she didn't want to go back up the hill yet.

The fog had thickened since morning; the harbor was a suggestion of itself, masts dissolving overhead into gray, the moored ships reduced to dark hulls and the sound of halyards knocking. Lamps burned along the quay in soft halos, and the stones were slick with spray. Astrid pulled her collar up and walked without direction, past the fish sheds with their smell of brine and iron, past the chandlery shuttered for the night, until she reached the end of the pier where the ferry to the steamer anchorage used to land, back when there had been a ferry, back when she was a girl and their father would lift her onto the bollard and tell her she had salt in her blood, their whole line had salt in it, sailors and builders and men who owned things.

Owed things, she corrected herself. Had owned. Would own again.

She had done it. That was the thing she kept returning to as she stood at the pier's end with the fog beading on her wool. She had done the thing. Three months ago she had been a bookkeeper's sister in a rented room in Aalborg, adding columns for a shipping agent who called her *girl*, and now there was a sworn statement on a magistrate's desk with her name at the bottom of it, and by spring there would be land in Aarhus and shares in a shipping concern and a house with a stove that drew properly, and everyone in two counties would know that Astrid Eriksen had taken back what was taken from them.

So why did the victory taste like the air in here—brine and tar and old decisions?

Because of the way Holm had said *the child becomes part of the record*. Because of the clerk's sleepy face disappearing behind the shelves with pages that now existed forever, in duplicate, in a file that some other clerk would carry down the hill to the parish overseer's warm kitchen, where a comfortable man with a ledger would run his finger down to a line reading *infant female, approx. three months, origins unknown*, and tap it twice.

She tried to assemble the argument she'd used on Wendell, the good argument, the true-sounding one: the child was leverage, the child was a stranger, the child was a knot someone else had tied around their family's throat and they owed her nothing but the cutting. Ilsa had been left at the door by persons unknown wearing their crest by design or accident, and Astrid had not asked for her, had not held her longer than courtesy required, had told herself from the first week that the baby was a scheme of Maren's or a burden Wendell had adopted out of that soft streak in him that was their mother all over again.

But she had known. That was the thing sitting in her chest now, heavy as wet rope. Maren had broken finally, two weeks ago, in the kitchen with flour on her hands, and told her—told her plainly, with that flat steady courage the woman had, that Astrid herself had arranged for the child's birth, years of letters and money sent south and instructions followed to the letter, and that Astrid had paid to forget it. Astrid had called her a liar. Astrid had stood in that kitchen and felt the floor of her own history shift under her feet and had called the housekeeper a liar and walked out, and had not walked back in, and had spent every day since building the lie higher, plank by plank, until it was tall enough to stand a claim on.

And now she had sold the roof off the top of it.

*I'm not sending her away,* Wendell had said to her on the gallery outside the clock room, that last bad night, his voice low and final in a way she'd rarely heard from him. *Whatever else happens.* And she had said, *Then you're choosing a stranger over your own blood,* and she had meant it as a weapon and it had landed as one, and she had gone to bed satisfied with the cut of it.

Now the words came back to her rearranged, the way words do at a pier's end in fog: *your own blood*. Whose? Hers, yes. Wendell's, if he chose. But the baby wore the crest. The baby had been bought with Eriksen money, born of Eriksen arrangements, delivered into the world by Astrid's own instructions before she'd chosen to burn the correspondence and pretend the whole account had been settled. The child was not a stranger. The child was a receipt for something Astrid had done and disowned, and tomorrow morning an overseer would read her sworn statement—the one naming the household, the one inviting scrutiny of every person under Halvard's roof—and open a page with Ilsa's name on it, or rather with the blank where her name should be.

Astrid gripped the rail. Below her the black water moved without sound, and somewhere out in the fog a ship's bell rang once, twice, marking the hour to nobody.

There was still time. That was the arithmetic she could do, the only arithmetic that mattered tonight. Inquiries took days to organize; overseers took days to rouse; the roads were frozen and the post was slow and Christmas came between now and anything. There was time to go back to Holm and amend the statement, narrow it, strike the language that swept the whole household into the record. There was time—perhaps—to fix what she had done before it finished becoming what she had done.

Or there was time to go home to the observatory and sit by the fire and say nothing, and let the machinery she had started grind forward on its own, because grinding forward was what it would do now; statements did not unsworn themselves; magistrates did not forget; and if she intervened, if she so much as hinted that the child should be shielded, she would be conceding in writing that the child mattered, that the child was hers to shield, that there was a connection worth hiding—

The fog horn sounded again, closer, and Astrid realized she was crying, briefly and furiously, the wind taking the tears off her face before they could properly form, and she wiped her cheek with the heel of her glove and laughed once, a short ugly sound swallowed by the fog, because she had come down this hill to sell a secret and buy back her name, and instead she had set in motion the one thing in the world that could take from her the small sleeping person she had spent three months insisting meant nothing to her at all.

She turned up her collar and started up the hill toward the observatory, fast, the fog closing behind her, already composing the sentence she would say to Wendell—*we have a problem, and it's mine*—and knowing, with the whole dark harbor at her back, that whatever she said at that door, the clerk's copies were already drying somewhere in the lamplight, and the tide of paper was moving, and the new year was coming for them all.
