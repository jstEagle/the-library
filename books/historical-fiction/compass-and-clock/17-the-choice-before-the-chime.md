## 17. The Choice Before the Chime

The storm had spent itself by morning, but the harbor it left behind was a held breath.

Wendell stood at the observatory's east window with Ilsa against his shoulder, watching the masts of the trapped ships lean and drip in the thinning fog. Beyond them, gray water heaved itself against the mole in long exhausted swells. Somewhere under all that movement lay the channel, and somewhere beyond the channel lay the open sea, and riding at anchor in the roads—impatient, coal-smoking, bound for ports with warmer names—was the *Vestria*, the last ship that would clear Vesterhavn before the new year closed the ice against her hull.

One berth remained. He had counted it three times that morning, as though arithmetic might change between counts. One berth, one ship, and the tide she needed would turn at half past eleven—half an hour before midnight, half an hour before the century ended, if the master clock in the tower above him did what master clocks were supposed to do and told the truth about time.

Ilsa stirred against his collar. She had learned to sleep in the crook of his arm the way other children slept in cradles, and her breathing had the small reliable rhythm of a well-made escapement, tick and tock, in and out. Four months old now. She could fix him with those grave gray eyes and grip his thumb with a determination that shamed him.

"There she is," he said quietly, because he had taken to narrating the harbor to her in the small hours, as though she might store the words against some future need. "The little green ketch. And the big black one that thinks it owns the bay."

She made a sound against his neck that was not quite a word but believed itself to be one.

Below, in the town, the inquiry would be convening again—the magistrate's clerks arriving with their ledgers, questions sharpened overnight. *How did the deed leave the family? Who handled the documents? Where, precisely, was this child found, and by whom, and why does her wrapping bear an Eriksen crest?* Astrid had set the machinery moving when she sold her information, and machinery, once started, ran on its own weight. Wendell knew that better than most men. He had spent his life among mechanisms, and the one thing he had learned without wanting to learn it was that you could not un-ring a bell, could not unwind a spring by wishing, could not put a filed gear back the way it was before someone took a file to it.

The child was warm against him. The world below was cold and procedural. Between the two hung the question he had been circling for two days like a moth around a lamp: *send her away to safety, or keep her beside me and face what comes together?*

He did not know yet. He climbed the tower stairs to ask a dying man.

* * *

Halvard Eriksen occupied the tower room the way a candle occupies its last inch of wax—guttering, translucent, still stubbornly alight. He lay propped against pillows Maren had arranged and rearranged a dozen times, and his hands, which had once adjusted the great clock's regulating nut to a tolerance finer than a hair, now trembled so badly they could barely hold the blanket's edge. His face had gone the color of the paper in his ledgers. But his eyes were clear. They had been terribly clear since the confession began coming out of him in pieces, like cargo jettisoned from a sinking ship.

"You look," Halvard said, "like your father deciding whether to jump a gap between trains."

"I don't remember my father deciding anything."

"No. You wouldn't." A breath that caught and released. "That was rather the entire point."

Wendell dragged the chair to the bedside and sat. Through the tower room's round window, the harbor lay framed like a painting of everything at stake: the *Vestria*, the mole, the town with its slate roofs steaming in the pale sun. The tide table was chalked on the slate by the door in his own handwriting. Half past eleven. Hours remaining.

"The magistrate sent word," he said. "There'll be a preliminary hearing the day after New Year. If the deed is entered as evidence—and Astrid has already given them enough to want it entered—they'll want to know who had custody of the documents. And who had custody of the child found wearing the family crest. They'll ask whether Ilsa is a claimant or a pawn or worse. They may ask for her to be taken into parish care until matters are settled."

"Parish care." Halvard said the words the way other men said *reef*. "I've seen what parish care is. The orphanage at Rønde takes in forty children a winter and buries six."

"Then you understand my problem."

"I understand it better than you do, boy, because I solved it wrong thirty years ago." Halvard's hand came out from under the blanket and closed—shaking, but closing—around Wendell's wrist. His grip had surprising life in it, the way a fire can surprise you even as it dies. "Send her away."

"Uncle—"

"Listen to me. There is a widow aboard the *Vestria*—Maren pointed her out to me at church last Sunday, before either of us knew how much would depend upon it. Fru Katrine Brandt. Going to her sister's family abroad, a woman with kind hands and no patience for gossip. One berth left on that ship, and it can be hers by tonight if you pay the purser, and the purser will take your money because pursers always do." The old man's voice gathered force, spending strength it plainly could not afford, and Wendell thought: *he is rationing himself, choosing which truths to buy with his last coins.* "Send the child to safety. Then stay. Stay and stand up in front of the magistrate and sign your name where everyone can see it. Claim the deed. Claim the inheritance. Save the name."

"The name," Wendell repeated.

"Your name." Halvard's eyes burned. "Do you know what I have watched happen to our family's name for thirty years? It became a thing people lowered their voices to say. *The Eriksens.* Lost father. Broken branch. An observatory full of secrets and a keeper who let a good man be called drowned rather than admit what he'd done. I have carried that rot until it reached my heart, and now—" he coughed, a dry terrible sound, and waited it out against the pillow— "now the only thing left I can do is watch you not make my mistake."

"And what mistake was that?"

"Hiding." The word came out cracked. "I hid behind the sea. Your father hid behind a foreign name on foreign manifests. And the name we both claimed to protect—" his grip tightened— "it just went hollow. A name is not kept by running from the light, Wendell. It is kept by standing in it."

Wendell looked down at the trembling hand on his wrist. He thought of the hollowed gear, the folded deed, the letters he had found in the ledger—his father's handwriting, alive across all that distance and silence, answered year after year by a man too ashamed to tell anyone else the correspondence existed.

"If I keep her," Wendell said slowly, "the inquiry may take her anyway. Into parish care, into a courtroom, into whatever the magistrate decides is proper while gentlemen argue over a piece of paper."

"Yes."

"But if I send her away, I don't know where she is. I don't know if the widow is what she seems. I don't know if I'll ever—" His voice failed on the edge of it, and he hated that, hated the crack in the mechanism of himself. "Thirty years ago my father chose distance over difficulty. He took the papers and ran, and the family fell apart in his wake, and I grew up measuring my life in portable things because I learned from him that staying means disappearing. And now you're asking me to do it again. Put a child on a ship. Let the horizon solve what courage should."

"I'm asking you to save her," Halvard said. "Whatever it costs you."

"Are you?" Wendell stood, and the hand fell away. He crossed to the round window, where the *Vestria* smoked patiently at anchor, and heard behind him the wet labor of the old man's breathing. "Or are you asking me to protect the name the way you protected it—by sending the inconvenient person out of reach? You let my father go. You let the sea take the blame. Now there's one berth and one child and one convenient ocean, and you're telling me the honorable thing is to use it."

The silence behind him had weight. When Halvard finally spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"That was cruelly said."

"It was truly said." Wendell turned. "I'm sorry for the cruelty. But you should hear it, because I have been hearing it all morning, in my own voice, and it sounds exactly like yours."

He expected anger. What he got was worse—a slow closing of the eyes, a settling of the body deeper into the pillows, the surrender of a man conceding a point in the last argument of his life.

"Perhaps," Halvard said. "Perhaps the rot goes deeper than I knew. Perhaps I cannot tell anymore which of my counsels are wisdom and which are only the same cowardice wearing better clothes." He opened his eyes. "So don't take my counsel, Wendell. Take the arithmetic instead, since you're a clockmaker and clockmakers trust arithmetic. One berth. Two people who need it—you and her. If you give it to yourself and flee the inquiry, you lose the name and possibly the child regardless, because papers travel slower than ships and magistrates write ahead. If you give it to her, you keep the name and lose her, maybe forever. If you keep her—"

"—I gamble both of us on the mercy of a magistrate and the testimony of a household."

"On the truth," Halvard corrected softly. "Which is heavier than mercy but holds more."

Wendell looked at his uncle a long moment. The light through the round window had shifted; noon was approaching, and with it the time signal, the great ball on the tower's mast preparing to drop for the harbor's benefit. Below them, faint through stone, he could feel the clock working—the heartbeat of the building, tick after patient tick.

"Ilsa quiets to the clock," he said, apropos of nothing. "Did you know that? Nothing else in the world settles her. Not Maren's singing, not warm milk, not walking the floors. Only the escapement. She lies there listening to it like it's telling her something."

"Perhaps it is."

"Perhaps." He rubbed his eyes. "When did I start calling her Ilsa? I never decided. I just looked down at her one night at three o'clock and the name was already true."

Halvard smiled, and for a moment the dying man's face was young—or at least less old. "That's how the important things arrive. Undecided."

* * *

He went down to the kitchen because he could not stand the tower room's smell of camphor and endings, and because Ilsa was hungry, and because there is a kind of thinking that only happens while your hands are busy with bottles and boiled water and the small engineering of feeding an infant in a cold farmhouse kitchen.

Maren was there, of course. Maren was always there—broad-shouldered, silent, stirring something, her face carrying that closed expression he had come to think of as her natural weather. She took Ilsa from him without being asked and fed her with the practiced economy of a woman who had nursed many children, and Wendell stood at the window with his back to the room and watched the fog finish lifting off the water.

"You've been upstairs," Maren said. It was not a question.

"He wants me to put her on the *Vestria*."

The spoon stopped against the pot's rim. Resumed. "And you?"

"I don't know." He turned around. "Maren. You've known her four months. You found her wrapped in that wool. You know something about the crest—I've seen you not say it a hundred times." He crossed the kitchen and stood close, close enough to see the weariness in her. "If I send her across an ocean on the strength of a stranger's kind face, I need to know that everything knowable about her has been told to me first. Whatever promise you made, whoever you made it to—is it worth more than this child's whole life?"

Something moved behind Maren's eyes then, quick and painful, like a fish turning under ice. She looked down at Ilsa, who had finished and was lying against her shoulder in milk-drunk gravity, one fist curled beneath her chin.

"She deserves better than promises made to frightened people," Maren said at last, and her voice was strange, flattened, as though she were reading words off an interior page. "You're right about that. But Wendell—" and here she looked up, and he saw that she was afraid, truly afraid, in a way that had nothing to do with the magistrate or the inquiry— "before you decide, before you put her on any ship, you have to understand that everything you think happened in this house these past months—the broken clock, the filed gears, the timing of it all—"

She stopped. Outside, far down the hill, the town bell rang the quarter hour, and the sound seemed to pass through the kitchen like a wind through a fence.

"What?" Wendell said quietly. "What about the gears?"

Maren pressed her lips together. Ilsa stirred. On the slate by the door the tide table read *11:30, high water* in his own hand, and through the window the *Vestria* sat at anchor in the brightening bay, one empty berth, hours remaining, the whole gray century waiting to find out what he was made of.

Maren set the spoon down with great care, as if laying down a weapon.

"Sit down," she said. "There's a letter you haven't read, and a thing I've done that you'll want to forgive me for, and no time left to say either gently."
