## 19. The Last Berth

The tide would turn at 3:40 by the observatory's reckoning, and the observatory's reckoning was the only one that mattered in Vesterhavn, so the whole harbor arranged itself around that number like iron filings around a magnet. By two o'clock the *Mette Kirstine* had her steam up, her decks stacked with crates of cured fish and sealed letters bound for Copenhagen and beyond, and a knot of passengers huddled at the gangway with their breath rising in white flags against the gray afternoon. The wind had dropped after the storm but the cold remained, the kind of cold that lived in the bones of the town itself, in the wet stone of the quay, in the tarred rope, in the bell buoy out past the mole that clanked at long intervals like something patient counting.

Wendell Eriksen walked down to the water with Ilsa against his chest inside his coat, her head tucked beneath his chin, and he counted his own footsteps because it was easier than thinking. Two hundred and six steps from the observatory door to the harbor office. He knew because he had measured everything in Vesterhavn the way he measured everything: precisely, compulsively, as if accuracy could protect him from what the measurements described.

The berth was real now. It existed as a paper ticket folded inside his waistcoat, third position on the passenger manifest of the *Mette Kirstine*, purchased under a name that was not quite his name—*W. Erikssen, joiner*—because the inquiry into the sabotaged clock had not yet concluded and a man under suspicion travels better as someone else. The ticket had cost him nearly everything the observatory had paid him. It bought passage south along the coast, then across to Hamburg, then onward wherever onward went. It bought disappearance. His father had proven it could be done; the sea made an excellent fiction of a man.

And for three days, since Halvard had gripped his wrist with failing fingers and rasped *send the child, save the name*, Wendell had been carrying two futures in his chest the way he carried Ilsa—one in each lung, neither letting him breathe fully.

* * *

The widow Fru Sigrid Balle was waiting near the foot of the gangway beneath a traveling umbrella she did not need, since no rain fell, only the sky's gray sifting of light. She was perhaps sixty, broad through the shoulders, with a face like a good loaf—plain, risen honestly, made to sustain people—and she wore mourning black that had faded toward charcoal, as if grief itself had been washed too many times. Her late husband had been a ship's chandler in Aalborg; her sister had married well in Antwerp years ago and written every Christmas without ever receiving an answer, until this winter, when Fru Balle had finally answered the oldest letter and been told, *come, there is room here for you, come before you are too old to travel.* Maren had known her from church. Maren had vouched for her with the whole force of her quiet, stubborn character, which was worth more than any affidavit.

"Clockmaker," she said when Wendell approached, and her eyes went immediately to the bundle inside his coat, and softened, and hardened again, both at once—the softening for the child, the hardening for whatever promise she had made him. "You'll want to do this quickly, I think. Long farewells sink ships."

"Not yet." His voice came out strange, flattened, as though his ears were packed with wool. "Not quite yet."

She looked at him a moment longer, then nodded and turned to watch the harbor with him, standing beside him in companionable silence, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in weeks.

The quay seethed gently. A porter wheeled trunks. A customs man in a cap too large for him stamped papers with the boredom of the immortal. Somewhere behind them, up the hill, the observatory tower stood blind against the low cloud, its time ball hanging at the mast-top waiting for its hour, and Wendell felt the master clock in his body even from here—he had wound it that morning, checked the escapement tooth by tooth with a jeweler's loupe, run his thumb along the teeth Maren had once filed and he had restored, feeling for any flaw, any excuse for the town's suspicion to sharpen further. It ran true. Whatever happened tonight, the century would be greeted honestly. That much he could guarantee, and guaranteeing it was the only thing holding him upright.

Ilsa stirred against his collarbone. She had begun, in these last weeks, to make small sounds that were almost words—not words, but the scaffolding of them, the intention of speech arriving ahead of speech itself. When she was held against the ticking of the master clock's case she went silent and attentive, her whole small body listening, as if she understood that the sound was the world's spine and she wished to learn it. Maren said the child had clockwork in her blood. Wendell had stopped arguing.

"You've fed her?" Fru Balle asked quietly.

"An hour ago. There's goat's milk warmed in the flask in the basket, and instructions written out—she takes it best at blood-warmth, you test it on your wrist—" He heard himself begin the litany and forced himself to stop, because the litany was a door and behind the door was everything. "It's all written."

"I know how to raise a child, clockmaker. I raised four and buried one." She said it without self-pity, a fact laid among facts. "What you haven't written is anything about yourself. Your name. Where she came from. When she's old enough to ask, and she will ask, children always ask—what do I tell her?"

"That she was loved," Wendell said. "By people who couldn't keep her safe. That's true, even if it isn't the whole truth."

"And the whole truth?"

He looked out at the water. The harbor was the color of old pewter, streaked where the pale sun broke through and failed to warm anything. Out beyond the mole the North Sea stood in its ancient indifference, the sea that had swallowed his father in name only while truly swallowing his childhood, his family, thirty years of a boy's belief that grief was honest because everyone around him grieved so convincingly. Halvard lying upstairs now with his heart stumbling toward its final hour, letting the sea take the blame all those years, and his father alive somewhere with a pen in his hand writing letters that Halvard answered and burned and answered and burned—

"The whole truth is still being written," Wendell said. "Tell her that. Tell her the rest is coming to find her."

Fru Balle studied him. Then she reached over and adjusted the shawl at Ilsa's back with a practiced hand, tucking it the way women tuck things, decisively, against weather.

"All right," she said. "Then we'd better board."

* * *

But he didn't move yet, and neither did she, and in the interval the harbor gave him a series of small sights he knew he would carry the rest of his life, each one ordinary and therefore unbearable.

A sailor coiling line with quick economical hands. A girl of maybe eight hauling a doll in a wool coat up the gangway, the doll's painted face tilted backward at the sky. The customs man yawning into his fist. Steam curling from the *Mette Kirstine*'s stack and bending south with the wind, pointing the way she would go, as if the ship were already gone and only her body lingered.

He thought about the ticket in his waistcoat. He thought about it the way a drowning man thinks about air, in whole-body flashes. Because the arithmetic was simple and Astrid had made him look at it plainly, two nights ago in the parlor with her jaw set and her eyes bright with something between fury and tears: *One berth, Wendell. One. You can put the child on that ship and stay here to face a magistrate who already thinks you broke the clock twice—or you can take her with you and vanish together, or you can send her to the parish and save yourself, but don't stand there pretending there's a version where nobody pays.* And she was right, that was the terrible thing, Astrid had always been rightest when she was being most ruthless. The inquiry into the sabotage had not named him, but it circled him; the magistrate's clerks had been to the workshop twice; his tools had been examined and handled by men who did not know what they were looking at and suspected it anyway. If the inquiry turned, it would turn on him. A man with a forged-sounding name on a ticket, a dead keeper's secrets in his pockets, a stolen deed in the case, and no witness but a housekeeper who had confessed to filing the gears herself and thus made the whole town believe the household was rotten to its rafters.

If he stayed, he might lose everything—his liberty, certainly; his reputation, likely; the chance to follow Ilsa later, possibly forever. If he fled, he would confirm every suspicion, forfeit his father's name publicly forever, and teach his daughter from her first year that the Eriksens solved problems by disappearing.

His father had chosen the ship. His father had chosen the sea and the false story and the letters sent home like coins thrown down a well. And Wendell had spent his whole life in the shape that choice left behind—the son of a drowned man, the nephew of a liar, the apprentice measuring his life in portable things so that he too could always leave.

He looked down at Ilsa. She had fallen asleep against him, her mouth working slightly, her breath warm and damp at his throat. The wool of her blanket carried the faint embroidery at its hem—the Eriksen crest, the compass rose and the hour-star, stitched by hands he would probably never identify. *Forget the face,* the note had said. He had spent a season learning that face instead: the gray-blue of her eyes settling toward something darker, the cowlick at her crown, the way she gripped a finger with the desperate competence of a sailor gripping rigging.

There was no version where nobody paid. Fine. Then let him pay in the coin he could count.

"Fru Balle," he said. "Her name is Ilsa. I chose it without deciding to, which I'm told is how the important choices get made. Ilsa. Write it down somewhere she'll find it."

"Ilsa," the widow repeated, tasting it, approving. "A good strong name. Short names survive crossings."

* * *

The gangway plank was narrow, salt-white, flexing very slightly with the harbor's breathing. Wendell walked it with Ilsa in his arms and the widow's steady tread behind him, and at the top a steward took Fru Balle's trunk ticket and gestured them toward the second-class companionway, and everything proceeded with the dreadful ordinariness of departures, the stewards' politeness, the smell of fresh paint over old salt, the lamps already lit below because the December day was giving out.

They found a stretch of rail on the leeward side, sheltered, where the funnel's warmth drifted down and made a pocket of almost-comfort. Below them the coalheavers worked the last sacks into the bunkers. The tide was within the hour.

Wendell unwrapped Ilsa enough to see her face. She woke partway, blinked at the strange lights, the rigging, the gray ribbon of harbor beyond the rail, and made her almost-word sound—a rising syllable, a question mark of noise.

"Yes," he told her. "That's about right. That's exactly the question."

His hands were shaking now. He noticed them the way he noticed a clock running fast—with professional disappointment. He was a craftsman. His whole life he had trusted his hands more than his heart, and now the hands knew what the heart had ordered and were grieving it early.

Fru Balle opened her arms.

For a long moment he could not do it. It seemed physically impossible, the transfer of weight from his arms to hers, as though Ilsa had become load-bearing, part of his structure, and removing her would bring the whole edifice down. He thought of Halvard upstairs dying with his confession half-told. He thought of the magistrate's men who would come to the observatory tomorrow, and the day after, patient as tide. He thought of the parish custody officer whose existence Astrid's betrayal had summoned into motion—a decent man, probably, following rules written by people who had never held a child—but rules nonetheless, and institutions, and ledgers where Ilsa would become a case number in a column.

Over his dead body. Well. Over his living, visible, accountable body. That was the difference between him and his father, and he chose it now, finally, wholly, the way you set a balance wheel spinning—no, the way you *stop* it, pin it, fix the beat.

"Her blanket smells of the workshop," he said, his voice wrecking on the word *workshop*. "Oil and pine. Don't wash it unless you must."

"I won't."

"There's a carved gear in the basket. Poplar, badly finished—I made it badly, I was crying at the time. It's not a toy for chewing, it's—" He stopped. Started again. "It's hers. From me. When she's older she'll know what it is."

"What is it?"

"A gear from a clock that keeps perfect time," Wendell said. "Or will, if I have anything to say about it."

Fru Balle's eyes were bright. She did not offer comfort, which he was grateful for; she simply held out her arms again, patiently, the way dawn holds out light.

He lifted Ilsa from his chest. She weighed nothing and everything. For one more second he pressed his lips to the cowlick at her crown, breathed her in—milk and wool and warmth—and then, with the same controlled motion he used to lower a movement into its case, seat it true, and release the winding click, he placed his daughter into the widow's arms.

Isla's face crumpled. She cried—one sharp, offended note of it, the cry of a person interrupted—and Fru Balle gathered her in against the black wool of her coat, rocking, murmuring something in the low singsong dialect of Jutland that had soothed four children and one burial, and Ilsa's cry wound down into hiccups, and then into that listening silence, and then she was watching Wendell over the widow's shoulder with her grave darkening eyes, and he smiled at her because it was the only inheritance he could give her that no inquiry could touch.

"That's it," he said softly. "Good girl. Sleep now. Grow tall."

He stepped back. The deck moved under him and he let it.

* * *

He was halfway down the gangway when she called his name—not Ilsa, she couldn't, but Fru Balle, quietly, from the rail above: "Clockmaker. She'll write to my sister's address, and my sister writes to Maren's cousin in Hjørring. There's a road for letters. Leave one open at your end."

He stood on the plank between ship and shore, suspended over dark green water, and nodded, unable to speak. A road for letters. His father had known about roads for letters and used them to haunt a family for thirty years. But this road would carry news of a living child growing tall in Antwerp, and that was a different order of ghost entirely—the holy kind.

Then the bosun's whistle shrilled, the plank came away under the steward's hands before Wendell's feet had fully left it, and the gap opened between him and the ship: first inches, then feet, then the slow black yard of harbor water that was also an ocean. Lines splashed aboard. The *Mette Kirstine* shuddered, gathered herself, began to slide from the quay with the immense unhurried confidence of things that are punctual because other things depend on them.

Wendell stood on the quay and watched. He did not hide his face, though half the town's eyes were on the departing ship and some fraction of those would swing to him, the clockmaker, the suspect, the keeper's mysterious nephew, and let them look. Let them see a man standing openly at the water's edge with empty arms and red-rimmed eyes, staying. Visibility was the price. He had paid worse prices for worse things.

The ship cleared the mole as the light failed. Out past the bell buoy her navigation lamp appeared, a single ember on the pewter sea, moving steadily south. He watched until the ember and the dark became indistinguishable, and the bell buoy clanked once, twice, keeping its patient count, and the cold came up through his boots and claimed him for the shore.

Then he put his hands in his pockets, found the folded ticket he no longer needed—*W. Erikssen, joiner*—and tore it slowly into pieces small enough for the tide to take. They scattered from his fingers like white moths and were gone.

Up the hill, the observatory tower waited, and in it a dying man, a furious sister, a housekeeper with a conscience like a filed gear, and a master clock running true toward midnight on the last night of everything. The magistrate's clerk would come in the morning. Let him come.

Wendell turned and began the two hundred and six steps home, and behind him, far out on the black water, the *Mette Kirstine* sounded her whistle once—long, low, fading—like a voice answering across a great distance, or a promise being kept somewhere out of sight.
