## 20. When the Century Turns

The tower room smelled of oil and cold brass and the last hour of a dying century.

Wendell stood before the master clock with his coat still on, his fingers stiff from the walk up the spiral stair, and for a long moment he did nothing at all but listen. Silence. The great machine that had governed Vesterhavn's harbor for sixty years—the pendulum that had sent a thousand ships out on the tide, whose time-ball had dropped at one o'clock every afternoon so that captains could set their chronometers and trust their lives to it—hung dead above him, its weights sunk to the floor, its escapement still.

He had cleaned it. He had re-cut the teeth of the sabotaged gear with his own files, working through the afternoon while Maren sat below with Halvard's door closed and the doctor's bag unpacked in the hall. He had oiled every pivot, tested every locking face, run the train by hand through three full revolutions until the click of the escapement came as regular as a heartbeat. The clock was ready. It only needed to be told to begin.

That was the thing no one understood about clocks who had never built one. They did not start themselves. Somewhere in the dark of the past someone had lifted these weights for the first time, and everything since—the schedules, the departures, the marriages timed to arriving steamers, the funerals held until the tide was right—had been falling. Gravity spent, order made of it. And when the falling stopped, when someone filed a tooth or let the weights run down, it fell to a man with warm hands to stand in the cold and begin it again.

Wendell thought of Ilsa asleep in Maren's arms down in the keeper's house, wrapped in the traveling coat Maren had sewn and pretended was only for warmth. He thought of the widow Andersen, who would carry her aboard the *Selma Maas* tonight, who had promised letters, who had held the baby once already and looked at Wendell over her small head with an expression that said *I know what this costs you*. He thought of the berth he might have taken himself—paid for, witnessed, entered into the ship's book in his own hand—and how it sat now empty beside him in the shape of a child gone safe across dark water.

He did not regret it. That was the strange part. He had expected regret like a stone in the chest, and instead there was something lighter and harder to name, grief with no weight to it, loss without the usual gravity. He had put the most precious thing he owned on a ship because keeping her meant gambling her safety on his own ability to fight a magistrate's inquiry, and he was done gambling with other people's lives to protect his own comfort. His father had done enough of that for the whole family.

His father. Alive, perhaps, somewhere beyond the postmarks of the world, writing letters that Halvard had answered for thirty years and burned or hidden or kept—Wendell did not yet know which. That accounting waited for morning too.

He took out his watch. Eleven minutes to eleven.

No. He looked again, and his breath caught before pride caught up with him. Eleven minutes to twelve. He had lost an hour somewhere in the day—not in the work, which had been exact, but in himself, in the standing and the thinking and the long slow understanding that a century does not end at any particular moment except the one men agree upon. The clock did not care. The stars did not care. Only the harbor cared, and the people in it, and the little iron bell in the tower that would speak for all of them at once.

Below, through the frost-furred windows, Vesterhavn lay scattered with light. Every lamp in town seemed to be burning; every window along the harbor front held a candle or a lantern or a face lit by firelight, waiting. Out on the water the *Selma Maas* rode at anchor, her riding lamps doubled for the occasion, steam already breathing from her funnel in pale ghosts. She would sail on the tide whether the clock spoke or not—but she would sail *marked* by it, and everyone aboard would carry the sound of the old century ending in their ears for the rest of their crossing, maybe the rest of their lives.

Wendell laid both hands on the winding crank.

The weights rose heavy under his arms, forty years of habit in the mechanism even after his repairs, and he wound them slowly, listening to the ratchet take each notch. Click. Click. Click. It sounded like footsteps climbing stairs. It sounded like a heart being convinced to beat. When the great drum stood full he moved to the second weight, then the third that drove the chime train, and the tower filled with the groan of coiled energy settling into itself, patient again, ready again.

Then he set the hands.

This was the part he had rehearsed. The minute hand must be eased forward, never backward, past the eleven, past the quarter, until it stood at the last minute of the last hour of the eighteen hundreds. He worked it gently, feeling through his fingertips for the warning click of the chime rack assembling itself—one stroke for each quarter-hour stored, the whole year's worth of quarters gathered and waiting to be spent in a single peal. Twelve hours of strikes banked against midnight. He could feel them the way a man feels coins counted into his palm.

He stepped back. The pendulum hung motionless, its bob polished by six decades of lamplight and air, a disc of dull gold in the dark.

"Right," he said aloud, to no one, to the machine, to his father wherever the letters had found him and wherever they hadn't. "Keep the time."

He put his shoulder to the cradle of the pendulum and drew it back, and let go.

Tick. A pause so long his stomach dropped. Tock. Another pause, and then tick again, and the pause shortened with every swing as the escapement woke and remembered itself, the pallets taking their teeth, the train uncoiling notch by measured notch down through the gears he had filed and fitted and bled over for two months. The sound grew until it filled the tower, until it seemed to come out of the walls themselves, and Wendell stood in the middle of it with his hands open at his sides and felt something in his chest unlock that had been stiff since he was nine years old and watched his mother close the door on the sea.

The clock was running. The century could end now.

* * *

He went down the stairs two at a time and crossed the frozen yard to the keeper's house, where Maren stood at the window holding Ilsa, dressed for travel, absurdly awake in the small hours, her dark eyes fixed on the lamp as if she were already counting what she saw.

"She'll sleep on the ship," Maren said, before Wendell could ask. "The engine shakes them down like a rattle. I told the widow."

"You've done this before," Wendell said.

Maren's mouth tightened—not quite a smile. "I've put people on ships before." She shifted the baby against her shoulder. "It's not too late to keep her and fight."

"It is, though. That's what time is." He reached out and touched Ilsa's wool cap, the Eriksen crest stitched at its hem in thread gone grey with age. "The inquiry comes Thursday. Astrid's sold whatever she sold to buy it. If Ilsa's here when the parish men arrive, she becomes evidence, and evidence doesn't get held—it gets taken. She goes where they put her." He swallowed. "She goes to relatives abroad, in a first-class cabin, with a widow who dotes on her, and in twenty years she can be angry at me by letter. That's the best inheritance anyone in this family has managed yet."

Maren looked at him a long moment. Whatever promise had bent her loyalty for thirty years seemed, tonight, to have been paid off or broken past mending; she looked lighter and sadder at once, a woman setting down a load and missing its weight.

"Your father wrote that you'd be stubborn," she said. "In the letter. The one that asked me to watch Halvard's conscience. He said, *the boy will do the right thing and hate every minute of it, see that he doesn't hate himself besides.*"

Wendell's throat closed. Down in the harbor, faintly, came the clang of the *Selma Maas*'s bell—first call, half an hour out.

"I'll carry her down," he said.

* * *

The pier was black with people.

Word had gone round, the way word does in harbor towns, that the master clock would ring in the new century properly for the first time in months, and half of Vesterhavn had come out to hear it. Lanterns swung along the quay. Someone had brought a barrel organ and given it up against the wind. Breath rose from the crowd in a low fog of its own, and through it Wendell carried Ilsa high against his chest, wrapped to her nose, with Maren behind him carrying the traveling basket and the papers and the widow Andersen's glove that had been left as a token of trust.

At the gangway the widow waited, straight-backed in her good coat, her face weathered into kindness by whatever losses had earned her the right to carry another woman's—another man's—child across the sea. She did not hold out her arms at once. She looked at Ilsa, then at Wendell, and said, quietly, under the crowd-noise, "Her name for the ship's book?"

"Ilsa," he said. It still startled him, saying it—that he had named her without deciding to, sometime in those long nights of pacing the tower with her crying against his collarbone until the great clock's tick pulled her under. "Ilsa Eriksen."

"Eriksen." The widow nodded slowly. "Then she has a name to grow into. Good. Names are ballast." She opened her arms.

Wendell transferred the warm weight of his daughter—Ilsa was not legally anything of his, and would never appear on any paper as such, and the word rose in him anyway, unbidden and permanent—into the stranger's arms. Ilsa blinked at the lanterns. She did not cry. She made one small sound, a syllable with no meaning yet, and gripped the widow's finger with her whole fist, and Wendell understood with a lurch that she would not remember him at all, that memory begins later than love, and that he would simply have to be content to exist on the far side of that gap, in letters, in the future tense, in the story the widow would tell her until she was old enough to ask questions the widow had promised not to answer fully.

"The tide won't wait," the mate called down. "Nor the year."

Maren pressed the basket into the widow's hands and turned away quickly, her shoulders shaking once, twice, and going still by force of will.

"Write," Wendell said. "To the observatory. Vesterhavn. It will find me."

The widow smiled. "I'll write when she cuts her first tooth. I'll write when she walks. I'll write so often your uncle's housekeeper will curse my name." She kissed Ilsa's head, and turned, and went up the gangway into the lit doorway of the ship, and the mate took the child's basket with a gentleness Wendell had not expected, and the doorway emptied, and the plank came in.

The crowd began to count. Not the minutes—the year. Somewhere near the front someone had produced a watch and was calling out, and voices took up each number as it fell: *four—three—two—*

Wendell stood at the very end of the pier with the harbor wind pushing at his back and watched the mooring lines fall away, watched the tug take the *Selma Maas*'s bow, watched her running lights begin to slide out across the black water, slow as the minute hand of a clock.

*"—one—"*

The master clock spoke.

The first stroke rolled down out of the tower over the whole harbor at once, vast and brazen and older than anyone living, and the crowd's shout broke under it and rose around it. Stroke upon stroke the old century died ringing—twelve strokes, thirteen, no, twelve, Wendell counted automatically and found his count correct and laughed at himself for checking—and out on the water the ship's whistle answered, one long note held against the bells, and the *Selma Maas* passed the harbor light with her deck crowded with passengers waving hats and handkerchiefs at a new year none of them could see yet.

*1900.* The number felt invented. The number felt like a door.

The chime faded by degrees into the ordinary ticking rush of the wind, and the ship grew small, and then became only lights, and then one light, and then the dark took her the way the sea takes everything, completely and temporarily and with no malice at all. Wendell stayed until the last spark went out, because leaving sooner felt like abandoning her twice, and because staying was the whole point of him now. He had spent his life measuring things he could carry. Tonight he had measured the exact weight of letting go, and found his arms still knew how to hold themselves afterward.

Maren touched his sleeve. "The magistrate's come up to the tower," she said. "He's asking for the keeper. There's no keeper fit to receive him." Her eyes met his, steady. "There's a clockmaker, though."

* * *

The tower room was warm now—a stove had been carried up, and a knot of the town's elders stood about it stamping the cold from their boots, and Magistrate Holm waited beside the observatory ledger with his hat in his hands and his official patience arranged carefully over whatever Astrid had paid him to think.

The ledger lay open on its stand beneath the lamp: the great book in which every keeper for sixty years had signed the daily rating of the clock, the errors, the corrections, the weather. Halvard's handwriting marched down the pages in columns, growing shakier by the year, and stopped ten days ago.

Holm cleared his throat. "The clock is certified for service, then? The harbor will want it entered."

"It is certified." Wendell took the pen. "By the man who repaired it."

He wrote slowly, in his best workshop hand, each letter formed the way he formed the teeth of a wheel—no flourish, no hiding, nothing that could be filed away or denied. The nib scratched loud in the quiet room. Around him the elders leaned in, and Maren stood just inside the doorway with her hands folded, watching, and the master clock ticked behind them all, keeping the new century's first minutes honestly for the first time in months.

**December 31st, concluded January 1st, 1900. Clock rated true. Error nil.**

And beneath, where the space asked only for the keeper's mark:

***Wendell Eriksen, clockmaker.***

Holm read it. His eyebrows rose a fraction—the name was known to him now, tied up as it was in deeds and inquiries and a sister who had spent a week cultivating his favor—and Wendell met his eyes evenly and did not look away and did not explain.

"My sister will tell you," Wendell said, capping the pen, "that this name is disputed. My uncle will confirm, if he lasts the week, that it is not. The documents are in the safe downstairs, and I am done pretending otherwise. Whatever inquiry you intend, sir, you may direct it at me in daylight, in my own name, at my own address." He nodded upward, at the ticking dark above the ceiling. "I live here now."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then old Captain Voss, who had set his chronometer by this clock for forty years, spat comprehensively into the stove and said, "About time somebody in this town signed their own name," and the tension broke into laughter and stamping and coats being fetched, and the room emptied of everyone but Wendell and Maren and the magistrate, who lingered, folding his gloves.

"Master Eriksen." Holm's voice was different now—lower, unhurried, the voice of a man no longer performing. "The inquiry convenes Thursday. You should know that among the papers sold to my office this week was a claim regarding the deed to the Sundvik properties—and an allegation concerning a child's parentage that, if substantiated, places the matter outside my jurisdiction entirely and into the parish's hands." He settled his hat on. "You'll want counsel. And you'll want to know precisely who sold what, and when, because whoever drew up that allegation knew things about your household that only a member of it could know."

The stove ticked. Above them, faint and faithful, the master clock counted out the quarter-hour—the first quarter-hour of the twentieth century, struck and recorded and kept.

"Astrid," Wendell said. Not a question.

"I make no allegations myself," said the magistrate. "But the tower steps are narrow, Mr. Eriksen, and I'd climb them carefully if I were you. Family matters descend faster than they climb."
