## 21. Reckoning in the Tower Room

Astrid Eriksen had dressed for victory.

She had chosen the grey wool with the black braid at the collar—the one she'd worn to their mother's funeral, because grief and triumph demanded the same severe architecture—and she had pinned her hair so tightly it pulled at her temples, a small constant pain that kept her sharp. She climbed the observatory road in the blue hour after dawn, her boots cracking the ice-skin of puddles, and she rehearsed her opening sentence all the way up: *I have come to discuss the disposition of the estate.* Six words, each one a nail.

The magistrate's inquiry was two days old. She had fed him the deed's particulars in the harbor office, watching his eyes widen at the sums, feeling the whole machinery of law begin to turn on the axle she had built for it. Once the claim was filed, no one—not Wendell, not that silent housekeeper, not the dying keeper, not the foundling—could stand between Astrid Eriksen and what had been stolen from her line thirty years ago. She had told herself this on the walk up, and told herself again at the door, and the telling had almost worked.

Maren opened the door before she could knock.

The housekeeper's face was the same flat northern calm it always was, but her hands were red from washing, and behind her the hall smelled of lye and lamp oil, of a house scoured through in a single night as if something needed burning out of its very grain.

"Mr. Halvard is asking for you," Maren said. Not *wishes* to see you. Asking. As though the request had been issued by someone with no authority left to be refused.

"Is my brother here?"

"Everyone is here." Maren stepped back from the doorway. "That's rather the point."

---

The tower room had changed since Astrid had last stood in it. Then it had been a museum of Halvard's precision—instruments aligned, charts squared to the desk edges, the great clock's case gleaming like a shrine. Now the room looked raided by weather. Charts lay unrolled across the floor, weighted at the corners with brass fittings. The clock's face hung open on its hinges, pendulum stilled, and Wendell sat beneath it on a low stool with his sleeves rolled and his forearms dusted grey, looking less like an apprentice than a man who had been taken apart himself and reassembled hastily.

Halvard lay in the daybed they had moved into the tower's warm corner, propped against pillows, and Astrid felt the shock of him before she allowed herself to feel anything else. He had been dying since autumn—that much had been plain from the first letter—but dying had been a process then, a slope. Now he had arrived somewhere. His skin had the translucence of paper held to a window, and his hands, those hands she remembered from childhood as enormous, quick, capable of mending anything, lay folded on the blanket like borrowed gloves.

"Astrid," he said. His voice was dry gravel poured slowly. "Come in. Shut the door. The wind has opinions today."

She shut the door. She did not sit when he gestured to the chair; she remained standing, because standing kept the sentence ready in her mouth.

"I've come to discuss—"

"The disposition of the estate." A ghost of a smile crossed him. "Your brother said you would open with exactly that. He knows you better than you think."

"Then he knows I'm right to open with it."

"No one is disputing your arithmetic, girl." Halvard coughed, a small terrible sound, and waited it out with the patience of a man who had been counting things his whole life. "Sit down. What I have to say cannot be said twice, and I will not spend my last breaths saying it to a standing woman who isn't listening."

She sat. On the edge of the chair. Her spine did not touch the back of it.

Maren brought tea and set it near Astrid's hand without meeting her eyes, and there was something in the angle of that avoidance—a careful, deliberate not-looking—that made Astrid's stomach tighten before anyone spoke. She had spent three days cultivating Maren, pressing coins and flattery and finally threats into the housekeeper's silence, and she had believed the silence bought. She saw now that she had mistaken a promise for a price.

"Maren has told us about your conversations," Halvard said mildly, following the same track Astrid's mind had just run. "About the letters. About what you asked her to say, and what you offered her to say nothing else. Don't glare at her. She kept faith longer than you gave her credit for, and she broke it only when keeping it meant doing harm." He drew a breath that whistled faintly. "Which brings me to the documents."

He nodded toward the desk, and Wendell rose, took up a leather folio Astrid recognized—he had carried it past her twice in the corridor at the harbor inn, guarded it like a relic—and laid it open on the blanket across Halvard's knees.

"Read it," Halvard said. "Aloud, if you can. Your brother has heard it. Maren has heard it. It should be you."

Astrid took the topmost papers. Her hands were steady—she made them be steady—and she read.

It was a confession, dated in October, witnessed by Maren's blunt signature at the foot. In Halvard's precise draftsman's hand, the story unspooled that she had spent a lifetime reconstructing wrong: her father, Kristian Eriksen, had not drowned in the foundering of the *Selene* off the Skaw. He had boarded another vessel entirely, carrying the deed to the Vesterhavn holdings and the shipping manifests that proved the family's second fortune—the branch fortune, the elder line's inheritance that by rights should have passed to Halvard's father and thence to Halvard. Her father had taken them under agreement with a party abroad, payment arranged, passage arranged. A defection, planned and paid for. And Halvard—who had discovered the scheme too late to stop it, or perhaps, he wrote, *too late to want to*—had let the sea take the blame. Had buried the truth in the wreck report. Had answered, in secret, every letter his brother sent from abroad over thirty years, letters still tied in a bundle in the tower cabinet, and had told no living soul, because the truth served no one and the lie at least kept a family name clean enough to inherit.

Astrid read to the end. The signature at the bottom was shaky but unmistakable, and beneath it, in fresh ink, a second line added that morning: *I confirm all herein and add only this—I have wronged both branches equally, and ask pardon of neither, but offer it to all.*

The room was very quiet. Underneath everything, faint as a pulse through a wall, came the tick of some smaller clock in some other room, keeping on.

"You see the difficulty," Halvard said gently, when she lowered the pages. "Your claim rested on the deed proving our branch was robbed. Instead it proves our father was a thief. The property returns to the elder line—to me, such as I am, and after me to whoever holds my written wishes. The magistrate will see this document within the week; I have already instructed the solicitor. And the inquiry—" he paused, choosing words the way a man chooses footing on ice, "—the inquiry into the child's custody proceeds regardless. That is the part, Astrid, that I would have you hear plainly. Your visit to the harbor office set wheels moving that do not care whose ambition loaded them. There are men in this town now who look at that baby and see a ward of the parish, a nameless thing to be processed. You did that."

"She's not nameless," Astrid said, and heard, too late, how the words leapt out of her, defensive and fast. "You named her. Ilsa."

"Yes," Wendell said quietly, from his stool beneath the dead clock. "We did."

And that—that small plural pronoun, *we*, enclosing the child as naturally as arms—struck her somewhere she had not armored.

---

They dismissed themselves by degrees, the way families do when the wound is too open for company. Maren went below to the kitchen with a look at Astrid that was neither forgiveness nor its opposite, only assessment, the look of a woman deciding what a person is worth. Halvard sank into sleep mid-sentence, his breath going shallow and tidal, and Wendell touched Astrid's elbow and tipped his head toward the narrow stair to the gallery above the clock, and she followed because refusing would have required a reason and she had none left that would hold weight.

The gallery ran around the tower's interior, iron-railed, and below them the great clock stood open like a patient on a table, its gears exposed, its heart accessible. Through the salt-hazed glass the harbor spread out grey and glittering, the masts of the winter fleet leaning together, and among them, at the long pier, the ship—the ship that had sailed two nights ago with the turning tide, carrying a child named Ilsa away into dark water. Astrid had not gone down to the dock. She had watched from her window at the inn, arms crossed against the cold, telling herself she felt relief, and had known even as she told herself that the tightness in her chest was not relief and never would be.

Wendell leaned on the rail beside her. He had their father's build, she realized—or the build their father must have had; she had only photographs and one memory of a man lifting her onto a cart, smelling of tar. Wendell had the same slope of shoulder, the same way of standing slightly angled toward whatever he was working on.

"You're going to tell me I've ruined everything," she said, to fill the space before he could.

"I'm going to tell you what happens next, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to want to slap me, and I'd ask you to wait until I finish anyway."

Despite herself—a muscle-memory despite, from a thousand childhood negotiations over sweets and chores—her mouth twitched. "Finish, then."

"The solicitor comes Thursday. The estate settles as Halvard's confession directs: the deed restored, the property vested in the elder line, and Halvard's written wish—which I signed as witness yesterday—is that upon his death the whole passes to equal division between his blood heirs. That's you and me, Astrid. Half and half. The claim you wanted, arriving by the ugliest possible road, but arriving."

She stared at him. The harbor light moved on the water below, indifferent, silvering. "Half."

"Half. Less expenses, less the costs of the inquiry, which are not small, but half." He turned to face her fully, and she saw the exhaustion in him, days of it stacked up, and under the exhaustion something harder, something he was holding in place by will alone. "Now the condition."

Of course. She braced.

"When the widow writes—and she'll write by spring, she promised, she's a woman who keeps promises—we go to her. Or she sends Ilsa back, if she will, once the parish matter is settled; I've retained counsel, and Halvard's name opens doors that were shut to a nameless infant. Either way, that child comes home to this family. And when she does, she needs more than a brother who works sixteen hours in a tower. She needs an aunt who is clever, and ruthless when ruthlessness serves her, and knows exactly how to make magistrates and solicitors and everyone else in this town do what she wants." He almost smiled. "I'm offering you half of everything, Astrid, and full use of the only talents you've ever had, aimed at raising my daughter instead of selling her."

*My daughter.* He said it as though it had always been true, as though the naming had been a formality appended to a fact already fixed in the world's grain. Something in Astrid's chest shifted like ballast.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you take your half and your pride and a town that remembers what you tried to do in the harbor office, and you spend the rest of your life rich and alone, which I know—" he said it gently, and gentleness from Wendell had always wounded deeper than any blade, "—is the one future you actually fear."

She looked away, down at the clock. Its gears lay in ordered disarray, waiting for his hands. Below it, on the floor, she could see the corner of a wicker basket, lined with wool, empty now, kept anyway. Kept the way sailors keep a compass from a lost ship.

"I did it to survive," she said, and was surprised to hear her own voice crack on the word. "Do you understand that? You left. You always leave—Father left, you left for your workshop and your gears, and I stayed with Mother while she shrank, and I learned that nobody was coming to save us, so I saved us, I saved *myself*, the only way anyone ever taught me, which is to take. That's all the taking ever was. Taking what should have been ours because I could not bear one more year of watching other people have things." Her hands had closed on the rail until the cold iron ached. "And then there was a baby in a basket, and I looked at her and saw leverage. I looked at her and saw a bargaining chip." She swallowed. "She was wearing our crest, Wendell. She had our wool on her little body, and I stood in the harbor office and handed her to the parish like a parcel."

The tower ticked around them, all its small clocks agreeing.

"I know," Wendell said.

"You don't—"

"I do. Because I nearly did worse. I stood on that dock with a berth in my hand and a chance to sail away from the whole mess, and I put the baby in it instead of myself, and I want you to understand that it cost me. I cried like a child myself, on the pier, in front of God and the widow and the fish carts. Choosing rightly doesn't feel like virtue, Astrid. It feels like amputation." He looked out at the harbor, where the water lay flat and bright and merciless. "So I'm not asking you to be good. I'm asking you to practice. One act at a time. Starting with a coat."

"A coat?"

"Maren's sewing one. For spring, for the journey, in case Ilsa comes back to us colder than she left. She pretends it's just for warmth. We all pretend things." He straightened off the rail. "Take the afternoon. Think. But know that the offer doesn't expire, and neither does the door. Halvard taught me that about clocks—half their accuracy is in letting people reset them without shame."

He went down the stair, his footsteps ringing on the iron treads, leaving her alone in the high gallery with the wind pressing at the glass and the harbor glittering below like spilled coin.

Astrid stood a long time. She thought of the funeral-grey dress she had put on that morning, armor for a victory that now lay in pieces on Halvard's blanket, and how strange it was that losing felt lighter than winning had. She thought of a small face she had seen exactly once, in lamplight, furious and red and utterly new, and of how she had calculated against it, and of how the calculation now sat in her memory like a stone she would carry the rest of her life—or like a stone she might, with effort, begin to build something out of.

Below, Halvard slept. Above, the empty clock waited for its hands.

Astrid unpinned her hat, set it on the rail, and went down the stairs toward the smell of bread and the sound of Maren sewing, and at the bottom of the tower she stopped, and cleared her throat, and said, in a voice she hardly recognized as her own:

"What size does she take now? Children grow. By spring, she'll need it bigger."

Maren's needle paused. The housekeeper looked up, and something moved behind her eyes—careful, cautious, the beginning of an thaw measured out drop by drop—and outside, far across the grey water, a gull cried once and was answered.
