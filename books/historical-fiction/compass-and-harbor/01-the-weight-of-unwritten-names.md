## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Names

The boy from the transport had a face like wet paper and a name he kept repeating as though someone might take it from him. *Corporal James Whitlow, Fourth Battalion, Second Company.* He said it every time Silas changed his dressing, the way other men said their prayers.

"I know," she told him, winding fresh linen around what remained of his left arm. "James Whitlow. I've got it written down."

He stared at her with eyes too old for twenty. "It's not written down. They said—the muster book went down with the barge. They said there's no record."

"Then now there is."

She kept the ledger in the pocket of her apron, against her hip, where its corners had worn a soft groove into the fabric over three years. It was an ordinary account book once, bought from Chandler's for sixpence, blue cloth boards gone grey at the edges, the spine held together now with surgical tape and stubbornness. Inside, in her small even hand, were four hundred and eleven names. She knew the number the way other women knew their age. Some nights it was the last thing she thought before sleep, like counting sheep, if sheep were the dead.

The field station had been the fish auction house before the war took Harrow Bay by the collar and shook it. The smell never quite left—brine under the carbolic, salt under the blood. Forty cots in rows where the scales used to hang. Silas had started as a volunteer the first autumn, when the wounded came up from the beach landings on carts that rattled, and she had never entirely stopped, because someone had to hold the basin while they vomited, someone had to write the letters home for men whose hands were ruined, someone had to notice which ones stopped breathing in the night so that somebody, somewhere, might say the name out loud afterward.

That last part was hers alone.

The official record was a fiction with good posture. She'd learned that early, when Private Ansel Rooke died of his wounds on a Tuesday and appeared in no report at all, having been transferred between units on paper in a way that made him nobody's man and therefore nobody's loss. The adjutant hadn't lied exactly. He'd simply let the ink skip. There were others like Rooke—a dozen the first year, then more. Men who'd been marked absent without leave hours before a shell found them. Men with the wrong regiment number, wrong spelling, wrong everything. Men whose deaths would have complicated somebody's ledger upstairs, and so were quietly folded away like linens no one wanted to look at.

Silas looked at them. That was all she did, really. She looked, and she wrote, and she kept the book dry.

*Corporal James Whitlow,* she wrote that evening, sitting in the narrow wedge of light between the coal shed and the station wall, *Fourth Battalion, Second Company. Died 14th March 1917, eleven minutes past seven in the morning, Harrow Bay field station. His mother's name is Agnes. He spoke of her apple trees.*

She had gotten the apple trees out of him two days ago, when he could still form words that weren't his own rank and number. Now she pressed the ledger closed and sat a moment longer, listening to the gulls argue over the harbor, and felt the familiar ache settle into place like a joint that always dislocates and always goes back.

Four hundred and twelve.

* * *

Her mother was waiting up, of course. Maren Eastwick did not sleep so much as doze upright like a ship at anchor, surfacing whenever Silas came through the door, no matter the hour.

"You smell of carbolic," Maren said from her chair by the stove, which was banked but not out. She was small in the chair these days, smaller every month, her hands knotted in her lap like driftwood. But her eyes were still sharp enough to fillet you. "Sit. Eat. There's broth."

"There's always broth."

"And you never eat it. One day you'll fall over and I won't be strong enough to pick you up." She nudged the bowl across the table. "Tobias is asleep?"

"For once."

"He worries in his sleep. Tosses like a boat at anchor. He gets that from Edmund."

Silas didn't answer. She ate the broth, because refusing meant a conversation, and conversations about Edmund were doors she kept locked even inside her own house. But Maren watched her with that terrible patience of the long-sick, who have nothing left to spend their time on except noticing.

"There's talk," Maren said at last. "Down the lane. Mrs. Cade says the requisition order is signed. Not rumored—signed. Naval hospital, whole harbor, and the town with it. Soldiers at the customs house by Friday, she says. Papers seized. Every paper."

"Mrs. Cade also says the Germans shelled Whitby from a balloon. There are no papers with my name on them."

"There are papers with *other* names on them." Maren's voice stayed level, which was how Silas knew this had been building in her for weeks. "Silas. Girl. If they come through that door with their inventory lists, what will they find?"

"A ledger."

"A ledger full of dead men the army decided didn't exist. Do you understand what that means? It means you know something they paid to forget. People have disappeared for less, and that was before—" she waved a hand at the window, at the war in general, "—all this."

"The book stays with me or it doesn't exist. No one knows."

"I know."

"You're not the army."

"No," Maren said softly. "I'm your mother. Which means I've spent nineteen years watching you carry things you shouldn't, and I'm tired of watching." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the hollows of her face. "There'll be transport ships when they evacuate the south side. Berths for civilians. Take one. Take two. Take Tobias and go to your aunt in Falmouth, and let this town keep its own secrets for once."

"Harrow Bay is not its secrets."

"No. It's its harbor. And harbors get requisitioned." Maren coughed then, a thin dry sound that went on too long, and Silas was beside her before she'd finished, hand flat against the birdcage of her ribs until the spasm passed. When Maren could speak again she did it against Silas's shoulder, quiet and merciless. "You think staying here honors him. It doesn't. Edmund is dead whether you sleep in his town or not."

Silas stood, gathered the bowl, kept her hands busy. "I'm not leaving the harbor."

"It isn't the harbor you're guarding and you know it."

"Goodnight, Mother."

From the doorway she looked back, because she always did, and Maren had already turned to the window, small and straight-backed, watching the dark shape of the breakwater against the lesser darkness of the sea. Nineteen years since Edmund had walked down that same breakwater at seventeen with his kit bag and his grin, and never come back—not dead, which would have been simple, but *dismissed*, struck from the rolls for walking away from a trench he'd already been buried in twice. Desertion. The word had done what the shell failed to do; it had killed him thoroughly, in every register that mattered, so that when the letter came a month later saying he'd been found drowned off the mole at Boulogne, there was nothing to grieve officially. No pension. No plaque. No name among the fallen.

Only a sister with a sixpenny ledger and a habit of collecting other people's forgotten dead, as if enough names in one book might weigh the scale back toward balance. As if one of them, someday, might be his.

* * *

She couldn't sleep, so she did what she always did. She went to the harbor.

Midnight owned the place honestly. No naval searchlights yet—that was coming, everyone said it—and so the water lay black and patient beyond the mole, and the boats knocked gently against their moorings like old men muttering in a tavern. Silas walked the length of the quay with her collar up, past the winches and the tarred rope, past the customs house with its dark windows, and stopped where the stones were worn smooth by a century of feet. Her feet. Edmund's feet. Everyone's.

This was why she couldn't go. Not duty, not the ledger even—or not only. It was this: that the town had swallowed her grief the way the sea swallowed ships, without a ripple, and if she left, there would be nothing left anywhere that remembered the exact sound Edmund's boots made on these particular stones. Falmouth had her aunt and clean air and no danger in it. Falmouth also had no ghost worth keeping.

She took the ledger out and held it in both hands, feeling its weight. Four hundred and twelve names, and every one of them a small act of insubordination. Someday, she told herself—as she told herself most nights—someone would read them. An inquiry, a widow's petition, a historian after it was all over. The book would surface and do its work, and the men the ink skipped would stand up out of the blank pages and be counted.

She just had to keep it out of other hands until then.

Behind her, a door banged. Lamplight bloomed in the customs house windows—one lamp, then another, moving room to room at an hour when no clerk worked. Figures crossed the glass. Not fishermen. Not navy. Greatcoats, and the particular unhurried confidence of men carrying documents.

Silas slid the ledger into her apron pocket and stood very still on the worn stones, watching the lights move through the customs house like something searching the town in its sleep.

They were two days early.
