## 2. Requisition

They came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the fishing boats would have gone out, if there were still fishing boats to go out.

Silas heard them first—the engines, low and wrong, too heavy for the coast road. She was at the field station again, wrapping a boy's forearm where the shrapnel had opened it like fruit, and she paused with the bandage half-tensioned and listened. The boy flinched under her hands.

"Keep still," she said. "It's not for you."

By seven the whole town knew. By eight they were everywhere: soldiers in khaki moving through Harrow Bay with the unhurried competence of men who had done this before, in other towns, to other people who had also thought themselves unremarkable. They chalked numbers on doors. They roped off the east pier. A lieutenant stood on the harbor steps reading from a paper in a voice pitched to carry, and the words came down the street like weather: requisition, emergency provisions, naval hospital, authority vested.

Silas stood at the edge of the crowd with her hands in her coat pockets and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for weeks. A naval hospital meant records. Records meant clerks. Clerks meant that everything written down in Harrow Bay would be read by someone whose job was reading.

The harbor office held forty years of ledgers, manifests, crew registries, casualty reports. It held, in a locked drawer that was supposed to hold tea coupons, hers.

She went home the long way, past the chandlery and the shuttered pilot house, and found two soldiers already at her door—not knocking, just standing, the way weather stands against a window.

"Ma'am. Residence is being inventoried."

"It's my mother's house," Silas said. "And I haven't invited you in."

"Doesn't require invitation, ma'am." The younger one wouldn't meet her eyes. The older one did, and there was an apology in his face that his posture refused. "Papers only. Personal effects stay."

*Papers.* The word landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

Inside, Maren was propped in her chair by the cold hearth, awake, watching the door with the fierce clarity she sometimes had before noon. Her eyes went from Silas to the soldiers and back.

"So," Maren said. "It's today."

"It's today," Silas said, and heard how much of her own fear her mother had already carried alone.

The inventory took two hours. The soldiers were thorough without being cruel; they lifted the oilcloth from the table and looked beneath it, ran fingers along book spines, counted blankets. One of them copied down the titles of every volume on the shelf, including Maren's Bible, including Silas's worn copy of *Audubon*, and she understood that they were not looking for anything in particular. They were looking for *everything*. That was worse. Indiscriminate attention was the one thing her life had been built to avoid.

In her room, the drawer came open at the first touch because she had never once, in six years, imagined needing to defend it.

The ledger was plain—marbled boards gone soft at the corners, a chemist's account book in its first life. The soldier flipped it open, frowned at the dense columns of names in her small upright hand, dates, vessels, causes of death written in language the official reports had never used. *Drowned, presumed. Fever, untreated. Shell shock, self-inflicted.*

"What is this, ma'am?"

"Accounts," Silas said.

He looked at her. He was maybe twenty, with a farmboy's sunburn long since faded to pale, and he could plainly read well enough to know these weren't accounts of money. She watched him decide—watched the decision move across his face like cloud shadow—and then he tucked the ledger under his arm with the rest of the stack and said, "All documents are remanded to the harbor office for cataloguing. You can file a claim for return through the civil liaison."

"A claim," Silas repeated.

"Yes, ma'am."

The door closed behind them. Maren reached out and caught Silas's wrist, her grip still surprisingly strong.

"Which papers?" Maren asked. "Tell me which ones."

"The ones that matter," Silas said, and sat down heavily, and put her face in her hands, and let herself feel it for exactly one minute: the violation of it, cold and total. Six years of names kept safe by being kept private, and now they sat in a government building under a colonel's eye, waiting to be made into whatever someone else needed them to mean.

* * *

Colonel Ashworth arrived at the harbor office on Thursday and did not like it, which surprised him.

Not the town—the town was irrelevant, a grey appendage to a serviceable deepwater harbor. What he did not like was the office itself: the smell of tar and old paper, the disorder, the sense that here was an institution that had been run for decades on memory and goodwill rather than system. He had been given the requisition as a matter of course—a hospital required a port, a port required administration—and he intended, before anything else, to make the records legible. Wars were lost in filing errors. He had seen it.

His clerks worked in rows. He walked behind them.

The material divided itself, as material always did, into the useful and the sentimental. Manifests: useful. Crew registries: useful, once cross-referenced against conscription age. Casualty reports: badly incomplete, which interested him. He pulled three years' worth and laid them side by side and found the gaps immediately—the same pattern of omission, civilian deaths entered nowhere or entered under euphemism, as though the harbor had agreed among itself not to see what happened to its own.

Then his senior clerk, a meticulous corporal named Brandt, brought him a box of items flagged as anomalous.

"Private papers mixed into the civic collections, sir. This one appears to be a personal record. Civilian deaths, mostly. Some military." Brandt hesitated. "Some entries reference serving personnel, sir. Unofficially."

Ashworth took the ledger and opened it at random and understood within four lines what he was holding. Someone in this town had spent years doing, by hand and in secret, the accounting his own bureaucracy declined to do. Names the reports lacked. Dates. Circumstances stated plainly enough to be actionable—or embarrassing. He turned pages with the flat attention he brought to any document, no more, no less, until he reached an entry dated March 1916, and stopped.

*Eastwick, Edmund R. Private, 4th Btn. Left post night of 14th. Body recovered from the cut below the mill, 16th. Reported as killed in action. His sister knows. I know. The sea keeps what it's told.*

He read it twice.

There was a file, Ashworth knew, because there was always a file. Eastwick, E.R.—desertion under fire, posthumous question of clemency, referred upward and quietly closed. The family would have been told nothing. Families were told nothing; that was the entire architecture of the thing. And yet here it was in a chemist's account book, in a woman's hand, dated days after the event—proof that the truth had lived in Harrow Bay all along, kept by someone who had chosen silence over testimony.

He did not think of it as leverage. That word belonged to lesser officers. He thought of it as *unaccounted material*, and unaccounted material was dangerous precisely because it existed outside the system, and his task—his genuine, professional, unromantic task—was to bring everything inside the system, where it could be weighed and used or destroyed properly.

"Log it," he said. "Seized document, item—" he checked the chalked number on the box "—forty-one. Full catalogue entry. Cross-reference the military names against central records."

"And the owner, sir?"

"The owner will come to us," Ashworth said. "They always do, when it's their name in the book."

He set the ledger on his desk rather than in the crates, squared it against the desk's edge, and looked at it for a moment longer than efficiency required. There was a discipline to this kind of record-keeping that he almost respected. Almost. Respect, in his experience, was simply the honest form of threat assessment.

Outside his window, the harbor lay flat and silver under a low sky, and somewhere in the town a woman who had spent six years being no one in particular was learning that she had become, as of Thursday, an entry in his inventory.

Ashworth found he was curious about her. He noted the feeling, filed it, and disapproved of it equally.

* * *

Silas heard it from the harbormaster's boy, who came running up Cutlass Lane with his cap in his fist and no breath to spare.

"They've got a list," he gasped. "Miss Eastwick—they're making a list. Everything in the office, they're writing it all down, and Mr. Prynne says they're sorting families by it. Who goes, who stays, who's—" He stopped, swallowed. "Who's *reliable*."

Silas stood very still in the lane. Below her, the harbor lay under army grey, cranes standing idle like hanged men, and in the harbor office—her harbor office, everyone's harbor office—some clerk was at this moment transcribing a dead man's name out of her hand and into theirs.

Edmund had been gone sixteen months. She had buried him twice: once in the ground below the mill, once in ink. She had believed, all this time, that the second burial was the safe one—that paper kept what flesh could not, that a secret written in a private hand was a secret kept.

She had been wrong. Paper answered to whoever held it.

"Tobias," she said quietly, when the boy had gone. Her nephew was in the kitchen, pretending not to have heard, fifteen years old and listening the way boys his age listened—with his whole body. "Tobias, come here."

He came to the doorway. He had Edmund's jaw and Maren's dark eyes and none of anyone's patience.

"They took the ledger," he said.

"Yes."

"Then they know. About Da."

"Yes," Silas said again, and watched the fury rise in him like water finding its level, and knew that the next chapter of all their lives had just begun to be written—by someone else's pen, in someone else's book.

She picked up her coat. Whatever the ledger meant now, whoever it belonged to now, she was going to walk down to that office and stand in front of it, and see with her own eyes what her silence had become.

Behind her, Maren said nothing at all.
