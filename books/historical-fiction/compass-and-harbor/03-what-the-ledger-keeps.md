## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

Three days after the requisition, Harrow Bay smelled of boot polish and tar, and the two smells had nothing to do with each other anymore.

Silas walked the high road from the field station with her hands in her pockets, counting what had changed. The harbor office flew a flag now. Soldiers stood at the fish gate in pairs, breathing steam into the morning. A sign had been nailed to the customs house door—*ALL RECORDS SEALED BY ORDER OF COL. R. ASHWORTH*—and beneath it someone had scratched, in a child's hand, *whose records?* Someone had already tried to scrub it off. The ghost of the question remained.

She had gone to the office twice since they took it. Both times a corporal younger than Tobias had turned her away with the same flat courtesy: *Civilian inquiries to the Provost, Tuesdays.* As though the town's whole memory could be filed into an afternoon.

The third thing she counted was this: people were looking at her differently.

Not with suspicion. Not yet. With the particular attention a village pays to a house whose windows have gone dark before anyone knows why. Mrs. Pellowe crossed the street to walk beside her outside the bakery and said, too casually, "They were asking at my Tom's shop which families had men away. Asking which ones came back." Then she peeled off again, having delivered the parcel, not waiting for a reply.

Silas stood in the cold with a loaf she hadn't meant to buy.

*Which ones came back.* Edmund had come back. That was the trouble.

* * *

She found out properly on Thursday, in the queue outside the requisitioned mission hall, where a lieutenant with a clipboard was sorting the town into futures.

Evacuation priority. That was the phrase going round like a fever: the transport *Persephone*, berths limited, families assessed. Assessed by what, no one would say. By need, some said. By loyalty, said others, and lowered their voices as if loyalty were a sickness you could catch through a wall.

Silas joined the queue behind the Coles and ahead of old Tam Brice, and when her turn came she gave her name to the lieutenant and watched him find it.

He didn't search. He knew where it was.

"Eastwick," he said. "Maren Eastwick, head of household. Tobias Eastwick, dependent." His pencil hovered. "And you are?"

"Silas Eastwick. The daughter."

His pencil moved, stopped, moved again. He turned his clipboard slightly toward a sergeant standing at his shoulder, and the sergeant leaned in, and whatever was written in the margin beside her family's name made both their faces do the same small thing—not quite a wince, not quite satisfaction.

"Category B," the lieutenant said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means your application is flagged for review."

"Flagged by whom? On what grounds?"

"Review means review, madam." He stamped something. "Next."

She did not move. She had spent forty-one years learning how to be unremarkable in a room, and she spent all of that training now, standing very still, letting the silence do her arguing. The lieutenant looked up despite himself.

"The review panel sits Monday," he added, quieter, because quiet invited confession. "If there's anything you'd like to have clarified before then—anything at all—you can put it in writing to Colonel Ashworth's office."

*Anything at all.* She heard it the way you hear a floorboard shift under weight: an offer shaped like procedure.

"I'll put it in writing," she said, and took the stamped paper, and left.

Outside, she read the form under the mission hall's guttering lamp. Category B. And in the corner, a reference number beginning with the letters **HB-R**, followed by a string of digits she recognized.

She had written those digits herself. Eleven years ago, in her own hand, in her own ledger. Entry 114. The army had indexed her dead.

* * *

Tobias was waiting for her at the top of Cutters' Row, and she knew before he spoke that he knew.

He had his father's shoulders and his mother's temper and neither parent alive to moderate either, and right now all of it was aimed down the street at the mission hall. "Category B," he said. "That's what they told the Widow Marsh, and her son's in a asylum. That's what they say they give to—" He stopped, jaw working.

"Families under suspicion," Silas finished.

"Of *what?*"

She took his arm and steered him walking, because a boy standing still in the street grows roots that other people water with gossip. "Of nothing they'll name."

"They've got our file. I went past the office window last night—the lights were on, they're cataloguing everything—and I saw the boxes, Aunt Silas. They've got a box with a red tag on it and Corporal Whitcombe was carrying it like it was dirty. I asked him what was in it and he said *records*, and I said whose records, and he looked at me—" Tobias's breath came short. "He looked at me like I should know."

"He shouldn't have spoken to you at all."

"I want to go in. I want to ask Ashworth straight, face to face, what a fifteen-year-old is suspected of. I want to see him write it down." Tobias planted himself, pulling against her grip the way he'd been pulling against the whole world since his father's ship went out and didn't come back whole. "You taught me not to swallow lies. You did."

"I taught you not to eat them fast," Silas said. "There's a difference between swallowing and chewing."

He almost laughed. It saved them both, that almost.

They walked down toward the water because Tobias could never stay angry inland; the sea pulled the fury out of him like a splinter drawn. The tide was low and the harbor wall showed its old bones, the stones the fishermen had cut before the war, before the last war, names and initials chiseled into the granite by men who wanted to be known where they stood. Silas looked at those carved names a long moment and thought about ink instead of stone, and about how much easier it is to erase a man who was only ever written down.

"Aunt Silas." Tobias had gone quiet, which was worse. "It's Dad, isn't it. Whatever's in that box. It's about Dad."

The gulls screamed over the mudflats. Somewhere behind them a soldier laughed at nothing.

"Your father," Silas said carefully, "is listed in the harbor records as lost with his ship."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that keeps you out of that building until Monday."

He turned on her then, and he was Edmund's height, Edmund's stance, Edmund's stubborn chin, and it cost her something physical to stand in front of that resemblance and refuse it. "You always know things," he said. "Everyone says it. *Silas knows, ask Silas, Silas keeps track.* Well—keep track of this. If they've made us into something less than other people, I'm not going to stand on the sand and chew on it."

"No," she agreed. "You're not."

Because she saw it then, plainly, the way she could read weather off the water: if Tobias walked into that office with his fists and his grief, he would walk out conscripted. Fifteen and furious was exactly the shape the army liked its fuel. One raised voice in front of the wrong sergeant, one insolent answer to the wrong question, and her sister's boy would be in a uniform by spring—if he was lucky—or in a cell first, and then a uniform anyway. Category B wasn't just a flag on a berth list. It was a hand hovering over a lever.

So she did the hardest thing she had done since the telegram came about Edmund. She used the truth the way the officers used it—as a tool with an edge—and she handed Tobias only the blunt end.

"There is a record of your father in that building," she said. "I don't know what it says. Your grandmother may know more than me. But I know this: everything we are to them right now lives on paper, and paper is argued with, not punched. You hold still until Monday. You keep your voice level and your hands in your pockets and you let me find out what entry they think they've found. Can you chew until Monday?"

The tide sucked at the stones. Tobias stared at the water as if it owed him an answer, and slowly, grudgingly, the sea's patience got into him.

"Monday," he said. "But Aunt Silas—if they take Grandma off that list, or if they try to send me without you two—I'm done chewing. Swear you'll tell me the truth before Monday. All of it. Whatever it is."

*All of it* was a ledger with 200-odd names in it and a brother marked *deserted, circumstances unknown,* and eleven years of silence balanced on top like a loaded crate. She looked at the boy who deserved the load and couldn't carry it yet.

"I'll tell you what you need," she said. "Before Monday. That I swear."

He accepted it the way the young accept half-measures—filed, not forgiven—and walked ahead of her up the slipway with his shoulders squared against the wind.

* * *

That night she sat at Maren's bedside with the lamp turned low and the stamped paper on her knee, and she thought about entry 114.

She had written it herself, eleven years ago, in the harbor office after hours: *Eastwick, Edmund. Rank, Able Seaman. Status: returned to district, discharged under cloud. Disappeared, circumstances unknown.* She had written it because the parish register wouldn't hold him and the naval record was sealed and someone had to say, somewhere, in some permanent place, *this man existed.* She had thought of the ledger as mercy. A private mercy, kept in private ink.

Now the mercy had been confiscated, catalogued, cross-referenced, and converted into a category. Her compassion, weaponized by clerks who had never seen Edmund laugh. Every unnamed sailor she'd ever sheltered in that book was now evidence, and every family she'd tried to honor was being weighed against a list they'd never agreed to be on.

*Maren slept, breath whistling. Or seemed to sleep.*

"Monday," Silas said softly to the dark. Two days. Two days to learn what exactly the army believed it had, what exactly they wanted in exchange for the word *cleared.*

Because that was what *anything at all* meant. She had run the arithmetic all the way home. Officers did not flag widows and boys for review out of diligence. They flagged them to make the flag itself negotiable. Someone wanted something from her—testimony, silence, surrender—and her family's future was the price tag hung facing outward.

Well. They had seized the ledger. They had not seized her. Everything in that book lived in her head as well, every name, every date, every circumstance, and unlike paper, she could choose what came out of her mouth.

She rose, banked the fire, and stood a moment at the window. Down the hill, the harbor office burned its late light, cataloguing the town's dead into boxes. Somewhere in one of those boxes was a page with her handwriting on it, and a colonel she'd never met was deciding what her brother had been.

Silas put her coat back on.

There were two days until Monday, and she intended to spend the first one finding out precisely what Colonel Ashworth thought he knew—starting with the one man in Harrow Bay who still had keys to everything and owed her a debt from before the war.

The harbormaster's window, she noted, was lit too.
