## 4. The Berth List

The notice went up on a Tuesday, nailed to the customs house door with four brass tacks that gleamed like coins someone had refused to spend.

Silas read it twice. The paper was army stock, the typeface official, but the words were simple enough for anyone: *HMT Persephone will call at Harrow Bay on or about the fourteenth instant to embark wounded personnel and priority civilian evacuees. Berths are limited. Inquiries to the harbormaster's office.*

*On or about.* As if a ship were weather.

A knot of townsfolk had gathered beneath the notice, and none of them spoke. Mrs. Callum had her reading glasses pushed up onto her forehead and was pretending she hadn't needed them. The Prewitt brothers stood shoulder to shoulder, hats in hand, the way men stood at gravesides. Someone's breath fogged the morning air and hung there, unwilling to disperse.

"Fourteen berths," said old Nan Trelawney, who counted everything. "I heard it from the clerk himself. Fourteen, and there's better than two hundred of us left."

"That's for the sick first," Mrs. Callum said quickly, too quickly. "The sick and the young."

"The sick and the young," Nan repeated, and looked at Silas.

Silas did not look back at her. She was already doing the arithmetic she knew would keep her awake: Maren, whose lungs had begun to sound like paper being crumpled; Tobias, fifteen years and seven months old, tall enough now that recruiting sergeants squinted at him and asked his birth date twice. Two berths needed out of fourteen. And between her family and those berths stood a ledger in an army strongbox, and a word in it—*deserter*—written in her own careful hand.

She walked home by the harbor road because it was faster, and because she could not bear the cliff path anymore. The sea had turned the color of gun metal overnight, and the Persephone's name on that notice seemed less like a promise than a deadline nailed shut.

* * *

Maren was worse by afternoon.

Silas heard it before she saw it—the change in her mother's breathing, audible through the bedroom door, a wet whistle where there had been only a dry rattle a week ago. Dr. Penhale had come Monday and Tuesday both, which itself was an answer. He never came twice in one week unless he'd been asked to.

"She has fluid," he told Silas in the kitchen, keeping his voice low though there was no one left in the house to hear it but Tobias, and Tobias heard everything now. "It will need drawing off, perhaps more than once. She should not be here for the winter, Silas. Damp houses, coal smoke, no proper medicines since they requisitioned the dispensary stocks." He rubbed his eyes under his spectacles. "She should be on that ship."

"I know."

"There's a form." He produced it from his bag with the reluctance of a man handing over a summons. "Certificate of medical priority. I can sign it today. It won't guarantee her a berth, but it puts her name near the top of whatever list they're keeping."

Silas took the form. It was thin paper, carbon-copied in triplicate, and somewhere in the machinery of the occupation it would pass across a desk where a man named Ashworth sat. Her signature at the bottom would tell him exactly where the Eastwicks stood: desperate, exposed, asking.

"Sign it," she said. "Thank you, Thomas."

He paused at the door, hat in hand, a small gray man made smaller by the uniform requirements he ignored entirely. "There's talk," he said, not turning around, "that the screening officers are weighting the lists. That some names go up and some names go down, and it's got nothing to do with lungs."

Then he was gone, and Silas sat alone with the certificate and the sound of her mother breathing two rooms away.

Tobias came in from the yard at dusk with an armful of kindling and the particular brightness in his eyes that meant trouble. He dropped the wood by the stove too hard.

"They posted the numbers," he said. "At the harbor office. Conscription ages. Sixteen as of the first of March."

"You're fifteen."

"For now." He crouched by the fire, feeding sticks in with a violence that had nothing to do with kindling. "Seven months, Auntie. They've put it in writing so nobody can pretend otherwise. Seven months and I'm legal."

Silas watched the firelight move on his face—the same long jaw as Edmund, the same stubborn brow—and felt something in her chest fold closed like a fist.

"Then we make sure you're gone before March."

"And if there's no berth?" He didn't look at her. "If they give it to the Prewitts' girl instead? If my name's downgraded because of—" He stopped. He had been stopping himself from saying it for weeks, and the effort showed on him like a bruise.

"Because of what your father did," Silas finished, quietly. "Say it, Tobias. It doesn't get heavier for saying."

"It isn't fair." His voice cracked on the last word, fifteen years old again for just a moment. "He wasn't a coward. You know he wasn't. And they've got it all written down somewhere in a file like he was a thief, and now it follows us onto a *list*, and nobody will even say it to our faces."

"No. They won't." She crossed the room and put her hand on his shoulder, feeling the new breadth of it. "Which is why you don't go shouting at sentries. You want to fight them someday, fight them with something sharper than fury. Fury is what they're armed with."

He shrugged her hand off—not cruelly, just the way a boy does when comfort threatens to become tears—but he stayed by the fire, and he stayed quiet, and that was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

The harbormaster's office still bore its old painted sign, *J. CROWE, HARBOURMASTER*, though a stencil had been stamped above it now in black: *MILITARY PORT AUTHORITY — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL*. Jonah Crowe sat behind his own desk like a man hosting burglars, and the two clerks at the filing cabinets along the wall were army men, blue-uniformed, sorting the harbor's century of paper into piles that meant nothing to anyone who loved it.

Jonah saw her come in and something moved behind his eyes—warning, apology, love, hard to say. Forty years he'd run this harbor. Silas had been eleven when he'd fished her out of the basin after Edmund dared her to walk the mooring lines. He owed her nothing and had never once acted like it.

"Miss Eastwick," he said, loud enough for the clerks. "Business?"

"Inquiry. Regarding civilian berths."

He pulled a ledger toward himself—a real one, harbor-issue, and she hated how her stomach tightened at the sight of any ledger now. "Persephone calls on the fourteenth. Civilian accommodations are limited to the aft compartments. Priority is determined by the screening board." A slight pause. "Medical certificates carry weight. So do other considerations."

"What other considerations?"

"Character of record," he said, and the phrase was so clean, so bureaucratic, that she understood it had been drilled into him. "Service history of the household. Conduct."

One of the clerks looked up. Young, freckled, bored. Jonah held his gaze with the flat patience of a man who had stared down gales.

"Walk me out, Jonah," Silas said.

On the quay, with the wind pulling at their coats and the water slapping the pilings below, he finally spoke like himself. "I'm sorry, Silas. There's a list inside the list. The board meets Friday week. Names go up, names go down, and I'm not shown the arithmetic."

"Who does it?"

"Ashworth's staff. Major Fell, mostly. Though—" he glanced along the empty quay, and lowered his voice to almost nothing, "—the Colonel initials the final sheet himself. Every name. Nothing moves without his pen."

"The fourteenth."

"The fourteenth. The list closes Friday." He took her elbow, steadying her against a gust, or against the news; she couldn't have said which. "Silas. Listen to me. Your mother's certificate will be filed—I'll see to it, I'll carry it up the stairs myself. But the boy." He hesitated. "The boy's age cuts both ways. Fifteen is safe. Nearly sixteen is a question, and questions go to the board, and the board has his father's file in front of it."

"Then I need the question answered before Friday."

"How?"

She looked out at the harbor—at the gray water, the seized cranes, the transport lights already rigged along the eastern mole like a stage being set for a play only the army had a script for. Somewhere in the harbor office above them, in a strongbox or a filing drawer, lay every name she had ever kept. Every dead man the reports had swallowed. Every entry that could shame a command—if shame was a thing that still reached men in that building.

"I'll think of something," she said.

Jonah's grip tightened once on her arm, then let go. "Friday," he said again, like a bell tolling, and went back inside to sit among soldiers in the office that had been his life.

* * *

That night Silas laid the medical certificate flat on the kitchen table and signed it below Dr. Penhale's tidy script: *Silas E. Eastwick, daughter and next of kin.* Her handwriting looked strange to her, thinner than usual, as if the pen knew what it was signing away.

Maren slept propped on pillows, breath whistling. Tobias had gone to bed without supper, which was his way of protesting things too large for protest. The house ticked and settled around Silas like a ship taking on water slowly enough that drowning could be called peace.

Friday. Nine days. One berth that mattered most, one berth that mattered equally, and a colonel's pen over both.

And she knew—sitting there in the lamplight with the certificate drying, knowing it the way one knows a storm is coming by the smell of the air—that sooner or later the summons would come. Ashworth would send for her. Men like him always arranged the board the way they wanted it, and she was the only piece on his table that moved.

When the knock sounded at the door, close to midnight, three measured raps in military rhythm, she was somehow not surprised at all.
