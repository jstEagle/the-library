## 5. Ashworth's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a private so young his cap sat on him like a borrowed thing. He wouldn't meet her eyes. *Colonel Ashworth requests the presence of Mrs. Eastwick at the harbor office, ten o'clock.* Requests. As if she could decline. As if Harrow Bay still contained a single door that opened by her own hand.

Silas dressed with care, because care was the last currency they hadn't confiscated. Her good grey dress, the one without a mended hem. She pinned her hair so there was nothing soft to grab. In the glass she saw a woman she recognized and disliked: someone preparing to negotiate.

Maren watched from the chair by the window, her hands folded on the blanket like two birds that had forgotten how to fly. "Don't sign anything," she said.

"I wasn't planning to."

"Your father said the same thing before he signed the lease on the fish house." A pause. "Sign anything they put in front of you, but read it twice."

It was as close to a blessing as Maren had given anyone in three years.

The harbor office still smelled of tar and old paper, but the paper was different now — stamped, filed, catalogued by men who had arrived six weeks ago and already treated the town's memory as inventory. The ledgers of forty years of landings, tide tables, crew lists, letters never claimed: all of it boxed and numbered in a language of forms that had nothing to do with salt.

Ashworth received her at the long table where the harbormaster had once settled disputes over damaged nets. He had cleared everything from its surface except a single folder, squared to the table's edge with a precision that felt like a message.

"Mrs. Eastwick. Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me anywhere else to be."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite amusement, more the ghost of respect. He gestured to a chair. She stayed standing. He accepted this, which told her he'd been briefed on her, and she hated being briefed about.

"You've been busy," he said. "Petitioning Mr. Callum regarding berths on the Persephone."

"I have a family."

"You have an entry." He opened the folder. Inside, she knew without looking, was Edmund — his service number, his regiment, the date he walked away from a trench outside Ypres and the date they caught him, the sentence commuted, the word *deserter* following him through every line like a dog that wouldn't stop barking even after his death at Passchendaele wearing another man's uniform and somebody else's courage.

She made herself look at it. Four years since his death and the paper still had the power to make her ears ring.

"The record is inaccurate," Ashworth continued, "in one particular. It lists no dependents. An administrative oversight. Corrected, it would complicate matters — for you, for the boy, for the allocation of civilian passage." He closed the folder. "I can correct it differently."

There it was. She kept her hands still at her sides.

"A berth on the Persephone," he said. "Guaranteed. Your mother as well, if her health permits transport. And Private Eastwick's file amended — the desertion struck, the record cleaned. In exchange—"

"In exchange I say what you want said."

He didn't insult her by pretending otherwise. That was almost worse.

"The ledger you kept," Ashworth said. "Forty-one names, by my count. Men recorded nowhere else. Men whose deaths were noted in your hand while official channels recorded them as absent, transferred, or unaccounted." He laid a typed page beside the folder — her own ledger summarized, itemized, reduced to evidence. "You will testify that these were deserters, thieves, and men dead of their own misconduct. You will confirm that the omissions in the record were justified omissions. And you will do so publicly, under signature, so that when the inquiry comes — and it will come — the army's accounting stands on the word of the woman who kept the count."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the file remains as written. Tobias turns sixteen in March. Conscription boards are not known for their archival generosity."

The room was very quiet. Through the window behind him she could see the harbor — her harbor, gray and patient under low cloud, the Persephone riding at anchor like a promise someone else intended to keep.

Her silence had kept her safe for years. That was the whole architecture of her life: tend the wounded, write the names, tell no one. Silence as shield, silence as prayer, silence as the only monument she could afford to build. And now he stood there proposing to pick up her silence and use it as a club.

That was the trap, she understood then, and it was finer than she'd credited. He wasn't asking her to lie, exactly. He was asking her to make her truth serve his lie. *They were deserters* — some of them were. Tam Rennie stole boots off corpses; she'd written it down herself. But Joss Merriam had died carrying a wounded sergeant two miles through mud, unnamed because his officer couldn't be bothered with paperwork during a retreat. And Peter Lyle, nineteen, who simply stopped, mid-sentence, on the fire step, and never started again — no wound, no reason, just a boy whose heart the war had quietly turned off. To call them criminals was to bury them a second time, and this time she would be holding the shovel.

And the price of refusing was Tobias.

She looked at Ashworth — the pressed uniform, the ink-stained middle finger, the small tired pouches under eyes that might once have been kind — and thought: *you know exactly what you're asking.*

"No," she said.

"Mrs. Eastwick—"

"No." Steadier now. "You can have my signature or my silence, Colonel. You've had the second for four years and it bought you nothing but a cleaner conscience. You want the first, and the first costs more than you're offering. A berth and an expunged file against forty-one men's names? You're not buying testimony. You're buying absolution, and I don't sell that."

His face didn't change. But his hands, resting flat on the table, went very slightly white at the knuckles.

"Consider the consequences," he said.

"I've considered nothing else since the army drove its lorries through my town." She turned to go, then stopped at the door, because fairness was a habit she couldn't seem to break. "Joss Merriam carried a dying man out of the mud until his own heart gave. If you must have a list of criminals, Colonel, at least have the decency to know it isn't true."

She left the folder where it lay.

* * *

After she'd gone, Ashworth sat alone in the requisitioned office for a long time.

He was not a cruel man. He needed Major Hartley to understand that, though Hartley was in London and there was no one left to explain it to. Cruelty was inefficient. What Ashworth practiced was administration, and administration, done honestly, was a kind of mercy: order instead of panic, procedure instead of whim, the machine grinding forward predictably so that at least the grinding could be anticipated.

But the file lay open in front of him and the machine did not feel merciful this morning.

He had read the ledger himself, three times now, after his clerks seized it. Not skimmed — read. The woman kept better records than his own adjutants. Date, name, regiment where known, cause of death where ascertainable, and — this was the part that had kept him awake — a line of witness for each. *Saw him fall near the Pilckem road. Brought word to his mother myself.* She had built, alone, in a fishing town the war had decided to forget, the exact apparatus of accountability that his own command had failed to maintain across three years and two fronts.

And she was right about Merriam. He checked. He'd pulled the battalion diary, and there it was between the lines of a chaotic withdrawal: a sergeant mentioned, unnamed, *lost during evacuation of wounded, conduct exemplary.* The kind of sentence officers wrote when they intended to follow up and never did. Merriam deserved a medal and had received, instead, an omission, preserved by accident and now defended by policy.

Ashworth had come to Harrow Bay to close accounts. Requisition the station, consolidate the hospital, ship the civilians out, deliver a tidy theater of operations to his superiors before the spring offensives made tidy things laughable again. It was supposed to be simple. Towns were simple. Paper was simple.

The woman was not simple.

He thought about the way she'd said *absolution,* like a diagnosis. He thought about his own ledger — the one that lived in him, unlined — of men he'd processed, categorized, sent onward, whose names he had never once spoken aloud because speaking them implied they required speaking, and the machine did not require anything of the sort.

Hartley wanted the inquiry neutralized. Hartley wanted witnesses. *Get the woman's mark on it,* the telegram had said, *and the matter closes itself.* Clean, efficient, cruel in the way Ashworth had promised himself he would not be.

He took out a fresh sheet of paper and began drafting the amendment to Eastwick's file anyway. The pen moved. The words assembled themselves into the shape of mercy: *record corrected, passage guaranteed.* All of it contingent, all of it revocable, all of it bait — and he sat there knowing it was bait, knowing she knew, writing it regardless, because the alternative was explaining to London why a colonel had chosen forty-one dead strangers over a functioning evacuation schedule.

*One berth,* he wrote, then crossed it out and wrote *two.*

Outside, gulls wheeled over the mole in the flat light, and the Persephone's deck crew were coiling rope, and somewhere in the lanes above the harbor a fifteen-year-old boy was learning what his uncle's file contained — Ashworth was counting on that, actually; grief made people pliable — and none of it, he noticed, made him feel any lighter than the fog coming in off the water.

He signed the draft. Then he sat with his hands flat on the table, in the quiet, listening to the harbor he had stolen, waiting for a feeling of accomplishment that did not arrive.

* * *

Silas walked home along the sea wall, the wind pushing at her back like a hand that wanted her to hurry. She had refused. She held the refusal in front of her like a lantern: she had stood in the room where her father settled net disputes and said *no* to a colonel of the British Army, and the world had not ended.

But the world had also not changed. Tobias was still fifteen going on conscriptable. Maren was still failing by increments, her breath shorter each week, her mornings later. The berth list was still posted in Callum's window with too few names on it and a final date beneath — five days now, four, the numbers falling away under her feet like the cliff road in a storm.

She climbed the hill to the house. The door stood open, which it never did.

Inside, Tobias sat at the kitchen table, white-faced, and in front of him lay a sheet of paper he had no business possessing — army stock, stamped header, the copied extract of a file that had been sealed inside the requisitioned office an hour ago. Someone had let him see it. Someone had *wanted* him to see it.

"Aunt Silas," he said, and his voice cracked on her name, "what did you do?"

Before she could answer, from the bedroom doorway came a sound she hadn't heard in weeks: her mother, standing upright without the chair, eyes clear as winter water.

"Maren—" Silas started toward her.

"Sit down, both of you." Maren's voice was thin but iron underneath. "It's time someone in this family told the truth out loud."
