## 6. Maren's Truth

The lucidity came back to Maren Eastwick on a Tuesday, without warning and without mercy.

Silas knew it before she reached the bedroom door. For weeks the house had sounded one way—the wet rattle of her mother's breathing, the mumbled conversations with people forty years dead—but this morning there was a stillness in it, an attention, and when Silas pushed open the door with the tray of barley tea, Maren's eyes were open and fixed on her with the terrible clarity of someone who has been away and has come back knowing more, not less.

"You've been crying," Maren said. Her voice was thin but level. "Not today. Recently. It's in your face. It settles into the bones of it."

"Mother." Silas set the tray on the washstand, buying herself a moment with the small ceremony of it—pouring, stirring, the spoon ringing against the cup. "You should drink this while it's warm."

"I should do a great many things." Maren pushed herself up against the headboard, and Silas moved to help and was waved off, firmly, the way she'd been waved off at seven years old when she'd tried to carry something too heavy for her. "Sit down, Silas. I've used up three weeks of fog to get here and I don't intend to spend them talking about tea."

Silas sat. The chair creaked under her. Outside, a patrol truck ground past on the coast road, and they both listened to it fade, the way you listen to weather.

"There's a ship," Maren said. "The Persephone. Mrs. Callow told me before I went under, and I expect the whole town's been chewing it since. Berths. A handful of them." She watched Silas's face. "And my daughter has spent every waking hour since trying to give hers away."

"The list isn't final."

"No. But you've decided, haven't you. You've done that thing you do—you've gone into that back room of yourself and started arranging us like cargo. Tobias sails because he's young and conscriptable. I sail because I'm ill, or I stay because I'm ill, whichever suits your arithmetic. And you—" Maren's hand came out from under the blanket, pale as paper, and gripped Silas's wrist with a strength that startled them both. "You don't appear anywhere on the page. You never do."

"Someone has to stay to be reasonable about all this."

"Don't be clever. I raised you; I know exactly how expensive your cleverness is." Maren released her wrist and lay back, breathing carefully, rationing her air the way she now rationed everything. Then, quietly: "Tell me about the ledger."

The room went very still.

"What ledger?"

"Silas." One word, and the full weight of her mother's attention on it. "Edmund's boat went out in November of 1915 and didn't come back, and within a month my daughter—who had never kept a diary in her life—was carrying a book everywhere like a second heartbeat. You think I was too far gone to notice? I noticed before the first name was in it. I've known since the beginning."

Silas found she could not speak. Three years of concealment, folded so carefully into the ordinary motions of her days—and her mother had simply been waiting, patient as tide, for her to stop hiding or to stop pretending the hiding mattered.

"How much do you know?" Silas managed.

"Everything worth knowing, I suspect. That you write down the men the navy won't. The drowned ones nobody claims. The ones who walked into the water at Miller's Point. And Edmund."

"Edmund is in the official record. Lost at sea, vessel unreturned."

"Edmund is in *your* record," Maren said. "Which is the true one. Which means you wrote down what actually happened to my son, which means you know, which means you have been carrying it alone for two years while visiting me twice a day and holding this family together with your two hands." Her breath caught; she rode through it. "Tell me. I'm his mother. I'm owed the truth by someone in this world."

So Silas told her. Not all of it—not the pages and pages, not the sixty-one names that had come before him—but the entry. Edmund Eastwick, aged twenty-four, last seen on the shingle below the east jetty, his coat folded on a bollard with his identification disc on top of it, neat as a man setting a table. Not lost at sea. Not blown up in some strait. He had walked into the water of his own accord, and Silas had found the coat two days later, and had understood, standing there with the cold coming off the harbor, that no report would ever say so—and that if she wanted her brother to exist at all, she would have to be the record herself.

When she finished, the light had shifted across the floor. Maren was quiet for a long time.

"I knew," she said at last. "Not the details. But I knew my boy, and I knew what the war was making of him. He came home on leave that October and he sat in that kitchen and didn't eat, and when I touched his hand he flinched like I'd struck a wound." Her eyes were dry, and somehow that was worse than weeping. "He wasn't taken from us, Silas. He chose. And I want you to hear me say this properly, because I suspect no one ever has: what he did was not shame. It was mercy. He was drowning long before the harbor got him. He put his coat somewhere safe because even at the end, my son thought of whoever would find it. That wasn't a coward's act. It was a tired man's act, and God has wider shoulders than any army."

"He could have waited," Silas said, and heard the child's grievance in it, two years old and still hot.

"Yes. And then he'd have died badly somewhere else, in uniform, and they'd have given us a printed card." Maren reached for her hand. "Listen to me now, because I won't be this clear again—I can feel the fog queuing up at the edges of me. You have spent your whole life since you were nineteen being the strong one, the invisible one, the one who carries. First Father's debts, then Edmund's silence, now mine. You've built yourself into a hiding place and called it duty."

"Mother—"

"Take the berth."

Silas stood up. It was reflexive, the way you stand when a room gets too small. "Tobias is fifteen. If he stays, they conscript him. You can't cross a gangway in your condition. There's no version where—"

"There is one version where my daughter lives," Maren said, "and you are going to stand there and tell me it's impossible, and I am going to lie here and watch you do it, and it will be the same speech you've given yourself every night for a week. I know, because I taught you the habit." She closed her eyes, opened them. "I'm dying, Silas. We can say it plainly between us; we've earned plainness. I will die in this town or I won't, but I will not have my last work be watching you erase yourself to keep the rest of us afloat. Stop carrying us. Put it down. Just once, put it down."

* * *

Silas walked out of the house without her coat and didn't notice until the wind found her.

She went down through the lanes the long way, past the shuttered chandlery, past the naval wire that had gone up along the north quay like a scar. The town was quieter than it had ever been in her life—occupied towns make a particular kind of quiet, orderly and wrong—and the harbor itself lay flat and gray under a sky the color of old zinc.

She climbed to the sea wall below the east jetty and sat where the fishermen used to sit, and she took the ledger out of her satchel because her hands demanded something to hold.

It looked so small in the failing light. A cloth-bound book, oil-stained, its spine cracked white. Sixty-two names. Sixty-two men the empire had misplaced and moved on from, and at the end of the list, in her own careful hand: *E. Eastwick.*

Her brother's coat had been folded. She kept coming back to that. Folded, squared, deliberate—a man finishing a task. And she had written it down because no one else would, and the writing had become the keeping, and the keeping had become the whole architecture of her life, beam and joist and roof. Take it down and what stood?

Maren's voice followed her down the hill: *Stop carrying us.*

There was driftwood on the shingle, dry and pale. There was the oil lamp in her satchel, meant for the walk home through the cut. It would take nothing—a match, a minute, and the ledger would be smoke over Harrow Bay, and Ashworth's bargain would be moot, and the names would belong to no one, which is perhaps what the dead had wanted all along. Rest. Unwitnessed. Let the official record have its tidy lies; let her brother be *lost at sea* forever; let her be a woman with empty hands boarding a ship.

She gathered the driftwood without deciding to. Her body made the pile while her mind stood apart, observing, the way she observed soldiers' wounds. She struck the match against the box and it flared, and she held it to the corner of the ledger's cover, and the cloth began to brown and curl—

—and she saw, in the matchlight, the first page. Not the names. The line above them, written the first night three years ago, in handwriting younger and more frightened than hers:

*So that no one is no one.*

The match burned down to her fingers. She dropped it, crushed it out against the stone, and pressed the smoldering corner of the cover dark with her palm, and sat there shaking in the wind with a smell of scorched cloth and salt.

Not property. She had been thinking of it as property—hers to guard, hers to trade, hers to burn. But it had never been hers. It was theirs. Sixty-two men lent it to her, one entry at a time, and Edmund had lent it to her most of all, and she could no more destroy it than she could dig up his grave and scatter him.

She could only decide who carried it next, and at what price.

The tide was turning. Out past the mole, gray water moved like something breathing, and somewhere beyond it, in three days or four, the Persephone would come in through the mist to take away everyone whose papers said they belonged elsewhere.

Silas put the ledger back in her satchel, picked a splinter of char from under her fingernail, and began to understand—with the cold certainty of a door closing—that whatever she chose, she would have to choose it soon, and in front of everyone.

She rose and turned toward the lane, and stopped.

A figure stood at the top of the shingle, small and furious and fifteen, holding a lantern that lit his face from below. Tobias had clearly run the whole way from town, and he was looking at her scorched ledger-hand, at the burnt patch on the bag, at her face, reading it all with the speed of the young.

"Aunt Silas," he said. "They've posted the berth list. It went up an hour ago."
