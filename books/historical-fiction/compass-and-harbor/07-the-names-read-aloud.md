## 7. The Names Read Aloud

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and Silas knew before she opened the door that something had already gone wrong. You learn the sound of trouble in a town like Harrow Bay—the particular weight of a fist on wood, the way it doesn't wait.

It was Mrs. Callum from the chandler's, her shawl thrown over her nightdress, breath steaming in the dark.

"It's Tobias," she said. "He's gone to the harbor office."

Silas did not remember dressing. She was on the street with her coat unbuttoned and one boot half-laced, and the town was a smear of grey around her, gulls screaming over the requisitioned rooftops, and she was running—past the shuttered bakery, past the wounded soldiers' barracks with their barred windows, down toward the water where the harbor office stood with its new flag and its new sentries and its new lock on a door that had never needed one before.

He had promised. That was the thing that kept striking her as she ran—not that he'd gone, but that he had sat across from her at supper two nights ago, jaw set, and said *I could get it back*, and she had said *you will not*, and he had looked down at his plate and said nothing, and she had believed that silence was agreement. She had spent three years learning which silences held. She had forgotten that fifteen-year-olds break like ice in spring—all at once, without warning, taking whatever stands on them under.

The sentry saw her before she saw him. He raised a hand and she went past him anyway—not fast, not slow, just a woman of forty walking with terrible purpose toward a door—and something in her face made him hesitate a half-second too long.

Inside, the office she had known since childhood had been gutted. The harbormaster's desk was pushed against the wall. Filing cabinets stood in ranks like soldiers. And in the center of the floor, under the hanging lamp, two military policemen held Tobias by the arms while Colonel Ashworth stood over the harbor master's log table, where a single object lay: the ledger, its leather cover scuffed, its string binding cut.

Tobias's lip was split. His sleeve was torn at the shoulder. He was not crying. He was looking at the ledger the way you look at a body.

"Miss Eastwick." Ashworth did not turn. "Your nephew picked a lock he had no business knowing how to pick. Interesting skill in a boy his age. It suggests a household with things worth hiding."

"He's a fisherman's nephew. We all mend nets."

"With wire picks?"

"He mends what he's given."

Ashworth turned then. He was in full uniform, though it was barely dawn, and Silas understood that he had been here all night—that he had been waiting, perhaps, for exactly this. He picked up the ledger and weighed it in one hand like a man judging a purchase.

"You know what this is now," he said. "Military property. Seized document. Your nephew has committed theft of government records during wartime, entry into a restricted installation, and resistance to arrest." He said it flatly, itemizing, the way he catalogued everything. "He is sixteen next month, I believe. Old enough to be conscripted. Old enough, in fact, to be *useful*." He let the word sit. "A boy with his record—who would question where he was posted? Who would ask after a deserter's son?"

"My brother was not—"

"A boy," Ashworth continued, as if she hadn't spoken, "who could be on a transport to the front by June. Or"—and here his voice changed, softened into something almost gentle, which was worse—"he could be on the Persephone when she sails Thursday. With a clean file. With your mother beside him, even; I can find berths for the infirm when I'm asked properly." He set the ledger down between them. "You know my terms, Miss Eastwick. They haven't changed. Testify. One statement, witnessed, confirming what the omissions implied—that the unnamed were deserters, thieves, men who chose their fates. Sign it, and this incident tonight becomes a misunderstanding. Refuse, and it becomes evidence."

Silas looked at her nephew. Tobias was staring at her, and there was something in his face she had never seen there before: fear for someone other than himself. He shook his head, small and desperate. *Don't*, the shake said. *Don't give them anything.*

She looked at the ledger instead. Three years of names in her own hand. Edmund's name among them—not the lie the army had written, but the truth she had recorded: *Eastwick, Edmund. Left station 3rd October, 1914, carrying Private Weller, shot through both legs. Returned alone 6th October. Weller alive in Boulogne per letter received. No officer will enter this.*

Her silence had been a shield once. Her silence had kept her family fed and left alone and invisible. And now it was a knife in Ashworth's hand, and he wanted only for her to grip it herself.

Outside the windows, the street was filling. Word traveled fast in Harrow Bay at dawn—Mrs. Callum had done more than knock—and Silas could see faces at the glass: the chandler, the netmenders, old Pearce who ran the ferry, women with shawls over their heads. The whole occupied town, watching through its own windows.

They were waiting to see what she would do.

And Silas Eastwick, who had built a life on being unseen, understood suddenly and completely that she was done.

* * *

Later, Tobias would try to describe what happened next and fail. He would say *she just walked forward*, as if that explained anything, as if walking had ever been so heavy or so simple.

She crossed the room and took the ledger out of Ashworth's reach—not snatching, just taking, the way she took wounded men's temperatures, the way she took names for the record. Then she climbed onto the harbormaster's raised log bench, so that she stood above the table, above the officers, facing the windows and the faces pressed against them.

"Miss Eastwick." Ashworth's voice had an edge now. "Step down."

"I'm going to read," she said, and opened the ledger. "You'll want to write this down. Since you're fond of records."

"*Step down.*"

"'March 1915,'" Silas read, clear enough to carry through glass. "'Corporal Denholm, Aloysius. Age thirty-one. Shell shock, second occurrence. Ordered returned to front despite medical note, copy enclosed. Found drowned at Cutter's Point. Ruled accident. Note attached stating no further inquiry desired.'"

The room went very still.

"'April 1915. Private Okafor, Samuel. Gangrene, left foot. Waited eleven hours for transport. Died on the dock. Entered nowhere because the transport log showed departure at six hours, and correcting it would embarrass—'"

A captain near the door moved toward her. Ashworth raised one finger and the captain stopped. Silas never understood why—whether it was curiosity, or calculation, or some crack opening in the man—but he let her read.

"'June 1915. Stoker Reyes, Tomás. Not a soldier at all—a trawlerman taken for labor, lost off the lightship when they refused to recall the crew in the storm. Four children. The navy paid nothing, entered nothing, because he was never officially aboard.'"

Her voice did not waver. That was the strangest part, Tobias thought afterward. She read the way she read the weather—plainly, completely, without drama—as though these words had been waiting three years in her hand and she was simply setting them down at last where they belonged, which was everywhere, in every ear in Harrow Bay.

Through the window, the crowd had grown. She could see mouths moving, repeating the names to those behind them. Someone had begun writing them on a scrap of paper passed hand to hand. Old Pearce had his cap off.

"'September 1915. Private Weller, Jonah.'" Her voice caught, once, and she let it. "'Shot through both legs at the retreat. Carried from the field by Cpl. E. Eastwick against direct orders. Survived. Repatriated. His rescue entered nowhere, because the man who carried him was listed as a deserter, and a deserter cannot be credited with saving a life.'"

She looked up from the page, directly at Ashworth.

"That is what your omissions imply, Colonel. That is what you asked me to call desertion and crime. This book holds forty-one people the army decided not to happen. I decided otherwise. Every name in it is true, and I have copies, and now so does everyone standing outside your window."

Ashworth's face did not change. But his hand, resting on the table, had closed slowly into a fist, and Silas saw in it—not triumph, not fury—something closer to a man watching a tide come in over ground he had been told was solid.

"Take the ledger," he said quietly to the military policemen. "Take all of them downstairs. Charge the boy with—"

"With what?" said Pearce, from the doorway. He had come in without permission, cap in hand, and behind him came Mrs. Callum, and the chandler, and the netmenders, filling the office the way water fills a breach. "Charge the boy with reading? Half the town heard every word. You going to conscript us all, Colonel?"

There were too many of them. Everyone in the room knew it. Ashworth looked at the crowd, at the ledger, at Silas still standing on the bench with the book open in her hands—and for a long moment the only sound was the harbor itself, slapping the pilings, indifferent as ever to whose names were written down.

"Everyone out," Ashworth said at last. "Except the Eastwicks."

The room emptied slowly, reluctantly, the way crowds do when they are afraid to stop witnessing. At the door, Tobias heard Mrs. Callum whisper to Pearce: "Forty-one. She kept forty-one of them alive in a book."

Then they were alone—the three of them and the colonel—in the gutted office, with the dawn coming up grey over the water.

Ashworth sat down heavily behind the seized desk. He looked older than he had yesterday, Silas thought. He looked like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Do you understand," he said, "what you've just started?"

"No," Silas said, closing the ledger. "But I imagine you do."

Tobias stood very still, his split lip swelling, watching his aunt hold the book against her chest—and understanding, for the first time in his life, exactly what his family had been carrying, and how much it was about to cost.
