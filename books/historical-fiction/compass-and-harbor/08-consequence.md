## 8. Consequence

The cell smelled of salt and wet stone, which was to say it smelled of Harrow Bay, and Silas found that almost funny. They had locked her inside the town she refused to leave.

They had taken her at the harbor office door—not violently. Two corporals, one of them a boy no older than Tobias, unable to meet her eyes. "Preventive detention pending inquiry," the officer in charge had said, as if the words explained anything. She had gone without protest because Tobias was watching from across the square, and because struggle would give them a reason to look at him.

That had been four days ago. Or five. The window was a slit of light that moved across the floor like a slow hand.

She passed the time the only way she knew how: she reconstructed the ledger from memory. Names written in her mind on air, entry by entry, in order. *Tomas Wren, deckhand, lost off the Gull, March 1915. Private Edwin Carrick, drowned attempting landing, unnamed in report.* Forty-one names. She could not afford to lose a single one. Whatever they did to her, whatever became of the paper ledger seized from her house, the names would survive as long as she did—and if they took her out to sea or stood her against a wall, then they would live in Tobias, who had heard her read every one aloud while officers shouted for silence and townsfolk began, unbelievably, to shout with her.

She had not known Harrow Bay could be loud.

* * *

Colonel Ashworth stood at the window of the requisitioned harbor office and watched a queue form outside the town hall that stretched past the fish market, past the chapel steps, down to the water. Petitions. Hundreds of signatures, ink still drying on some pages. A woman named Mrs. Penhale had organized it—Silas Eastwick's neighbor, he recalled from the files—with the efficiency of a harbormaster and the fury of someone who had buried a son twice: once in fact, once in paperwork.

His adjutant laid the morning's tally on his desk. "Two hundred and six signatures so far, sir. The vicar has signed. The schoolmaster. Half the crews of the *Dart* and the *Mercy*."

"The ships are ours."

"The men are not, sir."

Ashworth dismissed him and sat for a long time with the file open in front of him. Eastwick, Edmund. Desertion. And beneath it, in the seized private ledger's careful hand—or its copy, now transcribed by his clerks under duress of public opinion—a list of forty-one men recorded dead or disappeared whose names appeared nowhere in official record. Not lost. Not fallen. *Omitted.*

He had told himself, three years ago, when the first omissions were made, that it was triage. That grief unrecorded was still grief; that ink changed nothing. He had believed that a clean report was a kind of mercy for families who would only torture themselves with ambiguous paperwork. He had been wrong about the mercy part. He understood that now, watching the queue. The omissions had not spared anyone. They had simply transferred the burden of memory to whoever was strong enough to carry it, and Silas Eastwick had carried it alone for years, in secret, unpaid, at risk of exactly this.

He thought of his own brother's name, carved in stone at Aldershot with full honors, and felt the unfairness of it like a stone in his boot. Some men got stone. Others got nothing, unless a woman in a coastal town broke the law to write their names in a notebook.

The telegram from Command arrived at noon. It used the word *containment*. It asked whether the situation required *his personal attention*, which meant it required a scapegoat, and he knew whose name was being prepared for that role.

* * *

Maren was failing. They let Silas see her on the sixth day—an escort of one bored corporal walking her through streets that felt rearranged, as if the army had moved the whole town two inches left of where she'd left it.

Mrs. Penhale opened Maren's door before she knocked. "She's been asking. I've been reading her the petition signatures, all two hundred, to keep her spirits up." Her voice cracked on the number. "It's two hundred thirty now."

Her mother lay small against the pillows, but her eyes were clear—clearer than Silas had seen them since winter. "There she is," Maren said. "My jailbird."

"Mama."

"Sit. I can't sit up and argue properly, so you'll have to come down to my level." Silas sat on the bed's edge and took the thin hand. "I'm proud of you," Maren said. "I want to say it while I still say everything correctly. I'm proud of you, and I'm furious at you, and those are the same thing in this family, always have been."

"You shouldn't be proud. I'm in a cell. Tobias is—" She stopped. She didn't know what Tobias was; she hadn't seen him since the harbor office. Mrs. Penhale answered the question she couldn't ask.

"He's organizing. Him and the other boys—he's collecting testimony from the fishermen's wives. Your nephew has a gift for stubbornness that I won't speculate about the source of."

Silas laughed, and it turned into something else, and Maren held her hand through it.

"I saw Edmund," Maren said, after a while. "Before he went. You think I didn't know. You've carried it like contraband for six years, and I let you, because carrying things is how you love people. But I knew." Her thumb moved over Silas's knuckles. "He came to say goodbye and I gave him bread and I told him to run fast and never look back, and I have never once regretted it. Write that in your book if there's room."

"There's room," Silas said. "There's always room."

"Good girl." Her mother closed her eyes. "Now go home, even if home is a cell. And eat something. Prisoners who starve themselves out of guilt are just doing their captors' work for them."

Walking back with the corporal, Silas realized her mother had not once mentioned the berth. Not the Persephone, not passage, not escape. As if survival were a detail, and the names were the substance, and Silas had finally gotten the order right.

* * *

Ashworth came to the cell himself, in the evening, without adjutant or escort. The guard admitted him and withdrew, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other through the kind of silence that precedes either an execution or absolution.

"You're being released tomorrow," he said. "Formally, it will read 'released without charge pending the outcome of the inquiry.' The inquiry is happening. Command fought it; the petition and the vicar's letters made it unavoidable. Your testimony will be wanted. All forty-one entries."

"And my brother's file?"

"Will be part of the inquiry's scope. Which means, for the duration, it cannot be used against your family. Afterward—" he paused, and chose honesty, which seemed to cost him—"afterward depends on findings I don't control."

Silas studied him. Up close he looked like a man who had not slept well in a week and had decided to stop pretending otherwise. "Why are you here? You could have sent the adjutant."

"Because what I have to say shouldn't arrive secondhand." He took a folded paper from his tunic and held it out through the bars. "One civilian berth aboard the Persephone. Confirmed. Priority allocation, medical grounds—it can be assigned to your mother or your nephew, per your designation. It is not contingent on anything. Not testimony, not silence, not cooperation. I am telling you that plainly so you don't have to wonder."

She took the paper. Her hands were steady but her voice wasn't quite. "In exchange for?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point of the exercise." He looked at the wall above her head, at the salt-stained stone. "When I offered you terms, I believed the ledger was a threat to good order. I've since concluded that good order which requires forty-one men to be nobody is not order at all. It's arithmetic." A pause. "You were right to refuse me. I want you to know that I know that."

"You could say so at the inquiry," Silas said. "Out loud. Where it counts."

Something crossed his face—the flicker of a man measuring the distance between what is decent and what is survivable, and finding it wider than expected. "Perhaps," he said. "I haven't decided yet what I'm willing to lose."

"No," Silas agreed. "But you're deciding. That's more than most."

He inclined his head, almost a bow, and turned to go. At the door he stopped, back still to her.

"Your nephew," he said. "He'll make trouble someday. Good trouble, if he's lucky." Then he was gone, boots fading down the corridor, and Silas sat alone with a single berth in her hands and two people she loved and one choice, arriving now whether she was ready or not.

Outside the slit of window, the tide was coming in. Somewhere out in the mist, the Persephone waited at anchor, and tomorrow she would walk free onto streets that had signed their names for hers—and she would have to choose which of them the ship would carry, and which it would leave behind in Harrow Bay, in the town that had finally learned to speak.
