## 9. The Last Berth

The notice came on a Tuesday, pinned to the customs house door with four brass tacks and the army's characteristic indifference to punctuation. ONE BERTH. EASTWICK. PERSEPHONE. DEPARTS FRIDAY TIDE.

One berth. Not two.

Silas read it three times while the gulls argued overhead and the harbor fog thinned against the morning, and she found that her hands were steady. She had expected to feel torn open. Instead she felt something closer to clarity—the clean, cold kind that comes after a fever breaks, when you know exactly how much of you is left.

Ashworth had not written her name. He had written *Eastwick*, as if leaving the space blank were a courtesy, as if the choice were still hers to make in any direction she liked. Perhaps it was. But Silas had spent three nights in a cell learning the difference between a choice and an arithmetic, and this was arithmetic. Tobias was fifteen and conscription-age in eleven days. Maren's lungs were filling like a tide coming in. And Silas—Silas had made her bargain on the dock in front of God and the assembled officers of His Majesty's Army, and the price of that bargain had never been her own passage.

She walked home through streets that still smelled of pitch and disinfectant. The field station had emptied by half; the wounded who could travel had gone on the earlier transports, and those who couldn't lay in the converted net-loft under canvas ceilings, waiting their turn. Harrow Bay felt like a house after a funeral—everyone speaking quietly, everything already half-packed.

Maren was awake when Silas came in, propped against pillows that Tobias fluffed with the excessive care of a boy who has learned his grandmother is fragile and cannot bear it. Her eyes were clear. They had been clearer every day since the arrest—a cruelty of dying, Silas thought, that lucidity should arrive only at the end, so that nothing could be softened or unsaid.

"You've seen it," Maren said. Not a question.

"I've seen it."

"And?"

"One berth."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were dry. "Then we pack the boy's things tonight, and we don't waste Friday morning on tears."

"Mother—"

"I have carried you for fifty-one years, Silas Eastwick, and I will not be carried onto a transport ship to die at sea with strangers." She said it plainly, the way she'd once announced the price of mackerel. "I will die in my own bed, in my own town, with my daughter holding my hand, and I will consider it a good death. There are worse fates than being buried where you're known."

Tobias turned away sharply, toward the window, his shoulders rigid. Silas watched him watch the harbor and understood that he was doing what she had done at fifteen: memorizing something he was about to lose.

* * *

They packed by lamplight. Two shirts, a sweater Maren had knitted before her hands failed, the photograph of Edmund taken the summer before he enlisted—he was laughing in it, caught mid-word, forever about to say something that would have made them all forgive him. Silas held the photograph a long time before wrapping it in oilcloth.

Then she went to the chest at the foot of her bed and took out the ledger.

It had come back to her ten days ago, returned without comment in a government envelope, all its seized authority dissolved. Ashworth's inquiry had done its work; the names were in the official record now, transcribed, stamped, witnessed. The ledger was only paper again. But paper keeps what institutions forget, and she had kept it anyway, the way you keep a key to a house you no longer live in.

Tobias looked up from his kit bag. "What's that?"

"Sit down."

He sat.

"This is your uncle," Silas said. "Not the file they wrote about him. Not the word they used. Him. Every name in here is someone this town owed something to and didn't pay. Your father is the first entry." She opened it to the page she knew by touch in the dark: *Eastwick, Edmund. Lost off the Point, March 1915. He was afraid, and he went anyway.* "I wrote that last part myself. It's the truest sentence anyone ever wrote about him."

Tobias's jaw worked. He was so like Edmund it hurt—same dark hair, same stubborn set of the mouth, same fury at a world that dealt in categories instead of people.

"Where I'm going," he said, "there might be nobody who knows any of this."

"That's exactly why you're taking it." She pressed the ledger into his hands. It looked small there, absurdly small, all those years and all those dead fitting between a boy's palms. "There will be harbors where nobody knows these names, Tobias. Churches, registries, kitchen tables. You find one. You read them aloud until someone else can say them back to you. That's all remembrance is—someone willing to say it out loud after you're gone. Can you do that?"

He looked down at the ledger, and then at her, and his eyes were wet but his voice held. "I promise."

"Say it properly."

"I promise I'll carry the names somewhere they'll be read." He swallowed. "All of them."

"All of them," Silas agreed, and for the first time since the officers had marched into her town, she wept—not from grief, or not only, but from the strange lightness of setting something down after carrying it so long she'd forgotten it had weight.

* * *

Friday came in fog so thick the Persephone existed only as a sound—a low, patient hooting somewhere out in the gray, like a large animal calling through weather.

The dock was crowded anyway. Half of Harrow Bay had turned out, because that was what Harrow Bay did now; whatever else the army had requisitioned, it had not managed to requisition the habit of showing up for one another. Mrs. Calloway from the bakery pressed a paper sack of rolls into Tobias's hands. Old Fenwick, who hadn't spoken above a murmur since his sons went down with the *Larkspur*, gripped the boy's shoulder and said, clearly enough for the whole pier to hear, "You're a Harbor boy. Wherever you land."

An army corporal checked papers with the bored efficiency of a man counting sacks of grain. Tobias had his kit, his ticket, the photograph wrapped in oilcloth against his chest—and the ledger in a satchel Silas had sewn a strap for, so it could be carried close and carried always.

Maren had insisted on coming. It had taken an hour to walk the four hundred yards to the dock, Tobias on one arm, Dr. Prewitt's wheeled chair abandoned halfway as useless on the cobbles, and she stood now leaning on the bollard with her breath whistling faintly, refusing, absolutely refusing, to sit.

"You'll write," she told Tobias.

"Every week."

"You'll write even when there's nothing to say. Especially then. That's what family is—correspondence with no news in it." She reached up and touched his face. "You have your father's heart. Don't let anyone tell you it's a weakness. It was never a weakness. The world simply wasn't built gentle enough for it."

Tobias hugged her with a desperate gentleness, as though she might break, and Silas saw Maren hold on longer than her lungs allowed, paying for every second gladly.

Then it was Silas's turn, and there was too much to say and she had never been good at saying things twice, so she said only what mattered.

"You are not what happened to your father," she said. "You are what you carry forward. Carry it well."

"I will."

The whistle sounded. The fog shifted, and the Persephone's black hull materialized forty yards out, sudden and enormous, its deck rails already lined with faces. A gangway went down. The line began to move.

Tobias walked to it. Stopped. Came back once more, and kissed his grandmother's cheek, and then Silas's, and then he was climbing the gangway with his satchel tight against his ribs, and at the top he turned and raised one hand—not waving, Silas thought, saluting, in the way only a boy of Harrow Bay could salute a departing ship—and then the rail took him and the crowd and the mist folded over everything.

They stayed until the engines swallowed the whistle, until the fog closed like water over a stone. Maren's hand found Silas's and gripped it, hard, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that needed saying.

The walk home took two hours. Maren stopped twice to rest on walls, and Silas waited without hurrying her, watching the fog lift off the harbor in ragged sheets.

Halfway up the lane, Maren spoke. "You did right, girl."

"I know."

"No—I mean you did right *all along*. The ledger. The reading. All of it." She paused for breath. "Your brother would have—"

"I know," Silas said again, softer, and Maren let it go, and they climbed the rest of the way home through the thinning mist.

That night, alone at her kitchen table while Maren slept, Silas took out a fresh notebook she had bought from Prewitt's shop weeks ago and had not yet dared to open. She dipped her pen.

At the top of the first page, in a hand steadier than she expected, she wrote:

*Ledger of the Remembered. Begun this day, Harrow Bay. First entry: Eastwick, Tobias. Sailed on the Persephone, carried well.*

And beneath it, because the fog outside was lifting and the tide was turning and somewhere across the water a boy was keeping a promise:

*Second entry: Hartwell, Maren — still here, still mine.*

She had eleven more names to write before bed, given to her that afternoon by neighbors who had begun, without asking permission, to bring her their dead.
