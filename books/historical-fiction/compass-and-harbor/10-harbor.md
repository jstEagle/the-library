## 10. Harbor

The inquiry came in on the morning tide like everything else in Harrow Bay—uninvited, salt-stained, and impossible to ignore.

Three men in civilian wool sat at a table carried up from the harbor office and set down on the fish-market floor, because the requisitioned rooms were still being inventoried and the market was the only hall large enough to hold the town. Silas watched from the back as the clerks unboxed the seized ledgers, hers among them, its cover swollen from the damp of the harbor office where Tobias had hidden it under a floor of tarred planks.

She had not touched it since the day she'd read the names aloud. She found she didn't need to. She knew every line by heart—the drowned pilot whose body the patrol boat reported as flotsam; the boy who'd walked into the winter sea rather than sail back to the front; her brother Edmund, entered in her own hand with nothing but his name, his date, and the single word *gone*.

The senior clerk read findings in a voice pitched for filing cabinets, but the market had gone quiet enough that every word carried. The names would be restored to official record. The omissions of 1915 through 1917 were acknowledged as irregular. Compensation for the families was to be determined by a subsequent board.

*Irregular.* The word landed soft as fish scales and meant exactly what it meant.

Afterward, the crowd dispersed slowly, the way crowds do when they are reluctant to admit a thing has ended. Widow Cray stopped beside Silas and pressed her hand once, hard, saying nothing. Old Pell, who had ferried wounded men up from the pier for two years without once asking what the ledger cost her to keep, nodded across the room like a man closing out a season's accounts.

Silas went home. Maren was awake, propped by pillows at the window, watching the gulls argue over the tide line.

"Well?" her mother said.

"The names go back in the record."

Maren considered this the way she considered weather. "And Edmund?"

"Edmund too. All of it. In ink they can't take back."

Her mother closed her eyes, and Silas couldn't tell if it was peace or exhaustion, and understood that at this point the distinction no longer mattered much to either of them.

* * *

Spring came to Harrow Bay the way it always did—not arriving so much as accumulating, fog lifting by degrees until one morning the harbor wall showed its full gray length and the water beyond it lay flat and bright as hammered tin.

The army left in April. It happened without ceremony: lorries loaded before dawn, the field station's canvas struck, and by evening the hospital wards stood empty, their floors still bearing the ghost-rectangles where cots had stood. Someone had chalked a tally on the ward wall—a count of men treated, or lost, or simply passed through—and Silas asked that it not be scrubbed away. Let the next use of the building read it and wonder.

The town began putting itself back together the way bodies do, unevenly, scar tissue over the worst of it. The chandlery reopened. The ferry resumed its stuttering schedule. Children who had spent two years hauling water for orderlies went back to school and forgot most of what they'd seen, which Silas counted as the war's only mercy.

It was Pell who started the carving. No one planned it; that wasn't how Harrow Bay did things. He simply turned up at the harbor wall one grey Saturday with a mason's chisel and a borrowed mallet and began cutting the first name into the stone above the tide mark—*T. Voss, 1915*—and by afternoon a dozen people had gathered, and by the following week there was a rota, because the wall belonged to everyone and therefore the work did too.

Silas came down to watch on the fourth day. They were working alphabetically, which meant Voss first, then the Walcott boy, and then—

"Eastwick," Pell said, seeing her. "You'll want to do this one yourself."

He handed her the chisel. Her hands knew paper and pen and the weight of a wounded man's arm; they did not know stone. The first strokes went crooked. She corrected them. The letters grew under her hand slow and permanent, each one taking twenty times the effort of ink and lasting a hundred times longer.

E-D-M-U-N-D E-A-S-T-W-I-C-K.

Not *deserter*. Not *gone*. Just his name, cut into the wall he'd grown up diving from, at the height where a man standing on the quay could put his hand against it.

She worked until her wrist burned. When she finished, she stepped back, and the crowd behind her—she hadn't noticed it forming—did something she hadn't expected. No applause. No words. They simply waited while she laid her palm flat against the cold stone, and then, one by one as they drifted off toward their suppers, each of them touched the name too, lightly, the way you greet something you intend to keep.

* * *

Maren died in May, between the apple blossom and the fruit set, which Silas thought was exactly the sort of timing her mother would have chosen—after the spectacle of winter, before the ease of summer, requiring attention but never pity.

She had been lucid again for most of the last week, sitting up in the chair by the window that the neighbors had carried in without being asked, wrapped in a shawl that Mrs. Cray had knitted during the occupation out of unravelled army blankets—"so the wool would learn better habits," she said.

On the last evening, Maren watched the light go gold over the water and said, "You know I never thanked you properly. For staying."

"You told me to leave. You ordered me onto that ship."

"I did. And you disobeyed me, which is the Eastwick inheritance, God help us." Her voice was thin but steady. "I used to think your father's stubbornness skipped you. You were always so quiet. So good at disappearing into rooms." She turned her head, and her eyes were clear and sharp and entirely her own. "You never disappeared, girl. You just wrote things down while everyone else was talking. There's a difference."

Silas laughed despite herself, and the laugh hurt, and she held her mother's hand through the long dusk while the harbor lights came on one by one along the quay.

Maren died three days later in her sleep, and the town buried her on the hill above the water, beside Edmund's empty grave—which was no longer empty in any way that mattered, because the stone now bore the truth of him, restored by board order, and half the town had walked up behind the coffin to see it done right.

Silas did not weep at the graveside. She wept afterward, alone in the kitchen, briefly and violently, over the washing-up—because grief in Harrow Bay kept practical hours, and there were cups to dry.

* * *

June brought Tobias's first letter, postmarked a port town in the north where the Persephone's passengers had scattered into new lives. His handwriting had changed—slower, more deliberate, the letters of someone who had learned what writing costs.

*Aunt Silas. I have the ledger with me. I've copied it out twice already, once for keeping and once for sending. Mr. Aldous at the free library here has agreed to house the original in a case where anyone can read it. He says records belong to the people they describe. I didn't tell him you taught me that, because you never said it outright—you just did it for eleven years while everyone pretended not to notice. The names get read here. A man came in last month looking for his uncle and found him on page four. He shook my hand and couldn't speak. I thought you should know what your handwriting is doing in the world.*

*I am apprenticing at a print works. They let me set type. Every letter goes in backwards and comes out right, which feels like the whole of everything I've learned this year.*

*Come visit when you can. Or don't, and I'll come home. Either way, write me more than one page. I know you can.*

Silas read the letter twice at the kitchen table, then folded it into the drawer where she kept the things that mattered, and took out a new notebook.

It was plain, cheap, bought from the chandlery's restocked shelves—nothing like the old ledger, which had been her father's before it was hers, bound in leather gone soft as skin. She had thought for a long time about what the new one should be. Not a record of omission; that work was finished, carved in stone where the tide could never reach it. Something else.

She dipped her pen and opened to the first page and wrote:

*Harbor Book of Harrow Bay. Begun June 1918 by Silas Eastwick. Containing the names, deeds, dates, and small histories of this town, entered here so that they may be found.*

Then, beneath it, in her careful hand:

*Maren Eastwick, d. May 1918. Kept a clean house, a sharp tongue, and both her children's secrets. Said mercy was not shame. Was right.*

She wrote all evening. Widow Cray, and the shawl, and why. Old Pell, and the chisel, and the rota. The boy from the fish market who had carried water for two years and now wanted to learn navigation. The chalk tally on the ward wall, preserved under varnish by unanimous vote of a town that had never voted on anything unanimously before. Names and deeds and small histories, page after page, the ordinary chronicle of a place refusing to forget itself.

When the light failed she lit the lamp and kept writing, and somewhere past ten there came a knock at the door—three raps, unhurried, familiar. She opened it to find Pell on the step, cap in hand, squinting past her at the lit table and the open book.

"Saw your lamp," he said. "Thought you'd want to know. The board's sent word—the inquiry's final report goes public next month, and there's talk of a proper memorial at the wall. A reading, maybe. Of the names." He paused, working up to whatever he'd actually come to say. "They'll want someone to do the reading. Someone who knows them all by heart."

Silas looked back at the lamp, at the new ledger lying open, its pages filling steadily with everything remembered instead of everything lost.

"Tell them," she said, "I'm available."
