## 2. Census Morning

The fog came off the harbor before dawn and settled into the streets of Verrance like a guest who intended to stay the season. By the time Hazel Crane had lit the shop's lamps and opened the shutters of Crane & Sons Print Shop, the fog had worked its way into everything—beading on the iron frame of the press, softening the edges of the type cases, turning the window glass into a gray mirror in which she could see only her own shape, blurred and indistinct.

She liked herself better that way.

The morning ritual was always the same, and she performed it now with the devotion other women gave to prayer. First the stove, fed with shavings from yesterday's cutting waste so the room warmed before her fingers needed to be nimble. Then the water for the widow's tea, carried up the narrow stair to where Aunt Verity sat wrapped in two shawls despite the mildness of the spring, her hands folded on the table as though they had been set there years ago and never picked up again. Then the ledger.

The ledger was the heart of it. Every shop kept one; every shop in Verrance was required to keep one, open at all times to inspection by the Bureau of Trade and Records. *Crane & Sons*, it said at the top, in her father's careful hand, and beneath that the columns: commissions received, work delivered, wages paid. Her father had been dead eleven months. Her brother Aldous had been dead three years. And yet the ledger went on recording the labor of dead men, because the alternative was to record hers.

Hazel dipped her pen and entered the morning's work under *Aldous Crane*: two hundred wedding invitations for the Harrow family, cut and composed, delivery by Friday. Under *Aldous Crane*: repair of the chandler's shipping stamps. Under *Aldous Crane*: a new set of numerals for the customs house, though the customs house believed those numerals had come from the workshop of a man named Pell, who had died in the fever year and could not contradict them.

Her handwriting when she wrote as Aldous was different from her own—a little larger, a little more careless at the descenders. She had practiced it for three years until the deception lived in her wrist rather than her mind. That was the trick of it, she had learned. A lie you had to remember would fail you eventually. A lie you had trained into your body would hold even when your mind wandered.

She blotted the page, closed the book, and set it on its shelf beside the door where any inspector might find it, immaculate and damning in equal measure.

* * *

The census taker came at half past nine, which meant he had already been walking the ward since eight, which meant he was either diligent or ambitious, and Hazel suspected both.

She saw him through the window first: a lean man in a good but unremarkable coat, carrying the flat leather satchel that all census men carried, pausing at the door of the rope-walk to consult a list. The fog had thinned to a pale gauze, and his outline was sharp against it—the kind of outline a punch cutter appreciated, clean edges, no wasted line. Dark hair going gray at the temples too early. A face that looked like it was accustomed to being listened to and had not yet learned to distrust the habit.

*Wren,* she thought, remembering the notice posted at the fish market. *Elias Wren, Assistant Enumerator, Third Ward.* She had read the notice twice, the way she read everything official—once for the words and once for what the words were built to hide.

The bell above the door rang when he entered, a small bright sound entirely wrong for the moment. He stepped inside and stood a moment in the fog-dim light, letting his eyes adjust, and Hazel watched him take in the shop the way customers did: the great press with its iron screw gleaming dully, the sloped cases of type along the wall, the smell of ink and oil and lampblack that lived in the plaster itself. People always breathed deeper in a print shop. There was something in the air that felt like importance.

"Good morning," he said. "I'm looking for the head of household."

"The head of household is my aunt," Hazel said. "The widow Crane. But she keeps to her rooms—her health is poor. I manage the accounts. Hazel." She let the pause sit just long enough. "Hazel Fernsby. Her niece."

Every word of it true, if you held each one up to the light alone. It was only the shape they made together that was false.

He produced the census form and laid it flat on the counter, weighting the corners with small brass discs from his satchel. Up close he smelled of soap and cold air and, faintly, of the cheap tallow candles the Bureau burned in its offices. His pen was a good one—Hazel noticed pens the way other people noticed faces—and his hand moved down the form with the unhurried precision of a man who had done this four hundred times and expected to do it four hundred more.

"Name of household," he said, writing as he spoke. "Crane. Trade?"

"Printing."

"Number of persons residing?"

"Two. My aunt and myself."

"Apprentices? Journeymen?"

"None at present." This was the dangerous country, and she crossed it without slowing. "We do piecework mostly. Commissions. My late cousin's old clients still send us their smaller jobs."

Something flickered across his face—not suspicion, she decided, only the ordinary courtesy of a man hearing about the dead. "My condolences," he said, and wrote something in the margin. She could not read it upside down and hated herself for trying.

He asked about the building next—rooms occupied, hearths, whether they kept livestock (a question that made her almost smile; the only living thing in Crane & Sons besides themselves was a spider in the type case who had survived every attempt at eviction). He asked the value of stock. He asked how long the family had held the premises.

"My grandfather took the lease," Hazel said. "Forty-one years ago."

"And the business passes through...?" He glanced up, pen poised.

"The family," she said. "It stays in the family."

He nodded and bent again over the form, and Hazel allowed herself one slow breath. Outside, a cart rattled past loaded with herring barrels, and the driver cursed the fog in the colorful grammar of the quay. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary morning. The census taker's pen scratched, and the world remained arranged exactly as she had built it.

Then his elbow caught the edge of the counter.

It was nothing—a small knock, the kind of thing that happened a hundred times a day in a working shop. But the counter by the register was where she kept the loose sheets awaiting sorting, and the knock sent a drift of them sliding toward the floor, and among them, spinning once in the air like a leaf deciding which way to fall, was a pamphlet.

Hazel's hand shot out and caught it.

Or nearly caught it. Her fingers brushed paper, closed on air, and the pamphlet landed face-up on the boards between them, and for one suspended second neither of them moved.

She knew what he was seeing. She had printed forty of them two nights past, after midnight, for a client whose name she had not asked and whose coin she had taken anyway because coin was coin. A crude woodcut of the harbor. A list of mooring fees, old rates beside new ones, the new ones circled in red. And at the foot of the last column, small enough to be missed by anyone not looking, the printer's mark.

Not the Crane mark—that was a simple anchor-and-C, public knowledge, harmless. This was the other one. Hers. The compass rose nested inside a lantern, cut so fine that the sixteen points of the rose seemed to glow within the lantern's frame. She had designed it at nineteen, alone at the bench after everyone slept, and she had sworn she would use it only on work that mattered, and then she had spent three years using it on almost nothing because almost nothing was worth the risk.

And now it lay on the floor between her and a servant of the Governor's Bureau, glowing like a confession.

"That's mine," Hazel said.

The words came out steady. She was almost proud of them later, remembering. Steady, mildly bored, the voice of a woman pointing at spilled flour.

"I mean—it's shop stock. A job we ran. The client hasn't collected." She crouched, gathered the pamphlet with the rest of the scattered sheets, squared them against her knee with a brisk professional tap-tap-tap, and slid the whole stack into the drawer beneath the register. Her hands did not shake. She had trained them not to, the same way she had trained them to write as Aldous. "Fog makes everything slippery. Paper worst of all."

Elias Wren had not moved. He stood with his pen still poised over the form, and he was looking at the drawer, not at her—at the place where the pamphlet had gone, as though he could see through oak. Then he looked up, and his eyes were pale gray, the color of the fog outside, and they rested on her face for a moment longer than the census required.

"You said the client hasn't collected," he said.

"That's right."

"What is the client's name?"

"I don't take names for uncollected work." She smiled, and the smile felt like a mask fitted over a mask. "Half of Verrance drops off jobs and forgets them. If I recorded every intention, the ledger would be twice as thick and half as honest."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, perhaps, of a point scored—or of a wall encountered. He returned to the form.

"Occupation of residents," he said quietly. "You're listed as... niece. No trade given."

"I help with the accounts."

"The accounts." He wrote it. *Accounts.* The word looked absurdly small on the form, a single row of letters standing in for everything her hands knew how to do. "And the printing itself? Who manages the press?"

"We hire by the day when a job requires it." Another lie, smooth as fresh-milled type metal. "There are journeymen who walk the wards looking for day work. It's cheaper than keeping a man fed all winter."

"Mm." He finished the form, signed it—*E. Wren, Asst. Enum.*—and began gathering his brass weights, dropping them one by one into the satchel with small precise clicks. "That concludes the household schedule. Thank you for your patience, Miss Fernsby."

"Not at all." She held the door for him. Fog-light fell across the threshold. "Will there be another visit? I know the Bureau sometimes sends men back to verify."

"Only if a schedule raises questions." He paused on the step, half in the shop's dimness and half in the white morning, and turned back. For a moment he seemed about to say something else—she watched the sentence form behind his eyes and fail to reach his mouth. What came out instead was: "Your aunt. The widow Crane. She was born in Verrance?"

"In Verrance, yes. Why?"

"No particular reason. The Bureau likes completeness." He touched his hat brim—a gesture more habit than courtesy—and stepped down into the street. "Good day, Miss Fernsby."

The bell rang behind him, small and bright and wrong.

* * *

She waited until his figure had dissolved into the fog toward the rope-walk before she let herself feel anything, and then she made herself wait a while longer, because feeling things in a print shop was a luxury with a price. She checked the drawer. She checked the pamphlet—forty printed, thirty-nine accounted for, the fortieth now buried at the bottom of the scrap bin beneath a layer of misprinted bills, where it would go into the stove that evening and become smoke. She checked the street twice through the gap in the shutters.

Then she climbed the stairs to her aunt's room, where Verity sat by the window with her tea gone cold, watching the fog eat the harbor masts one by one.

"A census man," Hazel said. "He's gone now."

Verity's hands were quiet in her lap. They were beautiful hands, or had been once—Hazel could see the architecture of it still, the long fingers, the fine wrists. Hands that had been taught things. "Did he ask about Aldous?"

"He asked about the business. I told him it passed through the family."

Verity nodded slowly, the way she nodded at most things, as though the world confirmed what she had already privately concluded. "Your father used to say the census was the gentlest knife the government owns. It cuts nothing. It only draws the outline, so the other knives know where to fall." She lifted her cold tea, considered it, set it down. "You were calm?"

"I was calm."

"You were always calm. Even as a girl, when the press screw slipped and crushed your father's thumb—you didn't cry, you fetched the splints." Verity looked out at the fog. "I used to wonder if it was courage. Now I think it was practice. You'd been rehearsing invisibility since before you needed it."

Hazel did not answer. Downstairs, the shop ticked and settled around its secrets—the press, the cases, the drawer, the spider. Somewhere in the fog a ship's bell counted the hour, muffled to almost nothing, a sound you would miss entirely unless you were listening for it.

She went back down and worked through the afternoon with the steadiness Verity had named, setting type for the Harrow invitations, cutting a replacement ligature for the chandler's stamps, eating bread and cheese standing at the bench because sitting felt like surrender. The pamphlet burned at dusk. She watched the corners curl and blacken and told herself the matter was finished.

But sleep did not come easily, and when it came it brought the census taker's pen scratching, scratching, writing *accounts* in a hand too neat, and the compass rose glowing inside its lantern on the floorboards, sixteen points, and every one of them aimed at her.

And she did not know—could not know, lying awake in the dark above the silent press—that Elias Wren had stopped three doors down, in the lee of the rope-walk wall, and opened his satchel, and taken out not the census forms but an older paper, creased soft with handling, bearing a typeface commission signed six years ago by a dead man.

Bearing, at its foot, a mark he had seen only once before today.

A compass rose, nested in a lantern.
