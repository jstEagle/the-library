## 3. One Question Too Many

Elias Wren had a theory about doors.

Eleven years of counting households had taught him that a door told you everything before you ever knocked on it. A freshly painted door with a polished knocker belonged to people who wanted to be seen. A door patched with tarred canvas and a boot-heel print at knee height belonged to people too busy surviving to care. And a door that was clean, and quiet, and closed with the particular precision of a thing that had been closed the same way ten thousand times—that belonged to people who wanted very much not to be noticed.

The door of Crane & Sons Print Shop was that last kind. He had stood before it that morning with his ledger under his arm and felt the same small cold recognition he always felt, the census taker's instinct, the sense of a household holding its breath behind the wood.

He had written: *Widow Crane. Niece, resident, assisting in the trade.* Two lines. He had written two lines for a household that smelled of ink and lamp oil and hot metal, and something in him had known—had known the way a clerk knows when a column will not sum—that two lines were not enough.

Now he stood three doors down, in the lee of the rope-walk wall, out of the wind that came off the harbor carrying salt and the low moan of the chain-gongs at the breakwater, and he unfolded the old paper again.

It was creased soft as cloth from six years of handling. He told himself it was a working document, part of his records, kept because the commission bore a signature relevant to a licensing dispute long since settled. He told himself this the way he told himself many things, in the flat administrative voice that had gotten him through eleven years of counting other people's lives without ever once having to account for his own.

*Commission for the cutting of a title face,* it read, *in the manner called Old Verrance, fourteen sorts to the fount, to be delivered to Crane & Sons Print Shop, Lantern Row.* Dated the spring of the year the fever came. Signed at the foot in a careful, upright hand:

*Aldous Crane.*

Below the signature, pressed into the paper with an iron stamp rather than inked—the way a cutter signs work meant to last—a small device no bigger than a thumbnail.

A compass rose, nested in a lantern.

Elias held the paper close to his chest and looked at nothing. The rope-walk stretched away above him, its long spindles creaking as the hemp twisted, and somewhere a gull screamed once and gave up.

The mark on the pamphlet this morning had been inked, not stamped. It had been smaller, cruder, cut into something soft—potato, perhaps, or soft pine—and printed hurriedly, the lantern's little window slightly flooded where the knife had slipped. But the geometry was identical. Not similar. Identical. The compass rose with eight points, the north point elongated just past the others by a hair's width; the lantern whose crown curved in a particular asymmetric swoop, like a wave deciding to break. He had seen that swoop exactly once before, and he had been looking at it again now, in Aldous Crane's careful hand, on a commission signed six years ago by a man who had been in Cold Harbour churchyard these five years past.

Dead men did not cut pamphlets.

* * *

He walked home the long way, which was to say he walked past the customs house and along the quay, because the long way gave him time, and time was what he needed and could not name.

Verrance at dusk was a city pretending to be smaller than it was. Fishing boats came in stacked nose to tail, their hulls green with weed below the waterline. The fishwives were packing up, their scales catching the last light like scattered coins. On the customs house steps, a pair of the Governor's men leaned against the balustrade with the loose-limbed ease of men who knew they would not be moved on, and Elias pulled his satchel tighter against his hip and did not look at them, and was aware, even as he did it, that not looking was itself a kind of looking, a kind of confession written in the angle of a neck.

His rooms were above a chandler's store on Bell Rope Lane. He climbed the outside stair, let himself in, lit the lamp, and set the satchel on the table without opening it.

He made tea he did not drink. He sat down. He opened the satchel and took out the commission, and laid it flat, and looked at it until the candle had burned a ring of wax around its own wick.

Here was what troubled him, and he made himself look at it squarely, because Elias Wren was a man who summed his columns to the last digit or lay awake over the remainder: the mark should not have frightened him. A printer's device was a printer's device; every shop in the city had one, and half of them were compasses or anchors or suns or hands. If a dead man's mark had turned up on a pamphlet, the explanation was simple enough—the son had inherited the punch, the niece had found the old stamps in a drawer, some apprentice had copied what hung framed above the bench. Shops were continuous things. Marks outlived their makers the way names outlived their owners. There was nothing in any of it worth a second line in anyone's ledger.

Except.

Except that the pamphlet had not been dropped. He had replayed it all afternoon, the way he replayed every discrepancy, and the more he played it the more certain he became. The girl—Hazel, the niece, the one with ink worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that no lye would ever shift it—the girl had not dropped it the way one drops a thing by accident. She had dropped it the way one drops a thing when one has decided, in half a heartbeat, that dropping is safer than being seen holding it. Her fingers had opened. That was all. A controlled release, quick as a card trick, and then she had bent for it with the right amount of flustered apology and taken it back, and her face had done something interesting, which is to say it had done nothing at all, and faces that do nothing at dusk on census morning are faces doing a great deal of work.

And there was the matter of the widow's answers. Elias had counted four hundred and twelve households in the Lantern Quarter alone and he could conduct the interview in his sleep, and so he knew precisely how strange it was that a woman running a print shop—a licensed trade, a registered premises—had answered every question about the business in the first person plural. *We keep the shop. We take commissions. We have no apprentices at present.* We, we, we, a word deployed like a smoke screen, and when he had asked, gently, who cut the type—for the forms required it, *number of skilled persons engaged in the letter trades*—the pause had lasted only a moment but had been deep as a well, and the answer had come: *My brother did. Before.* Past tense. A dead brother, folded neatly into a dead father's shop, and neither of them alive to be asked anything further.

Dead men made convenient answers. Dead men could hold licenses and sign commissions and absorb questions the way sand absorbed rain.

Elias got up and paced the length of his room, seven steps and back, the floorboards complaining under him. Below, the chandler was drawing his shutters; he could hear the bars going home, one, two, three.

Here was the other thing. Here was the thing he had been circling since morning like a moth around a flame it did not trust.

The commission was signed by Aldous Crane, but the mark beneath the signature was not Aldous Crane's mark.

He was sure of it. Six years ago, when the licensing dispute had been live and he had handled this very paper with his clerk's gloves on, he had noted the device idly, the way he noted everything, filed it in the cabinet of small facts that never left him. And he remembered—not thought, *remembered*, with the terrible fidelity of a memory that has waited years to become useful—that Aldous Crane's own stamp, on the shop license still nailed inside the doorframe of the premises on Lantern Row, was a plain crane bird, wings raised, standing in water. Everyone in the printing quarter knew it. The crane was the man; the man was the shop.

But the commission bore the compass-and-lantern instead. Which meant that six years ago, someone in that shop had already been signing work with a device that was not the master's, and Aldous Crane—dead these five years—had let that device stand beneath his own name. Had signed *above* it, in fact, as if in witness. As if in permission.

Or as if, Elias thought, stopping mid-stride, the signature were the courtesy and the mark were the truth. As if the master had put his name to work he had not made, made by hands he could not acknowledge, and had done it in the only honest way available to him: by signing underneath the real maker's sign, subordinate to it, a witness rather than an author.

Who cuts type in a shop and cannot put their name to it?

The question sat in the middle of the room like a stone dropped in still water, and the ripples went out and out, and Elias watched them reach places he did not want them to reach.

* * *

He had reported a printer once before.

He did not think of it often, and when he thought of it he thought of it in the language of his reports, which was to say in the passive voice, which was to say in the language of a man standing to one side of his own life. Eleven years ago. A compositor in Saltmarket named Joren Vale, arrested for printing sedition—broadsheets against the harbor levies, true ones, though truth had not been the relevant category. Elias had not accused him. Elias had merely filled in a form, correctly, completely, noting an unregistered press at a registered address, because the form required it and Elias Wren did not leave fields blank. The form had gone where forms go. Men had come in the night. And in the spring, on a gallows erected on the customs house quay in front of several hundred spectators who had been ordered to attend, Joren Vale had hanged, and his press had been broken publicly on the stones, and his wife and child had been shipped east on a convict hull, and Elias had stood in the crowd because clerks were required to stand in crowds, and he had watched, and he had gone home, and he had eaten supper.

He had not left a field blank since. That was the joke he did not tell anyone. That was the penance: completeness. If he filled every form perfectly, if his columns summed and his counts matched and his registers were beyond reproach, then perhaps the arithmetic would balance someday, some ledger somewhere would absorb the weight of Joren Vale's neck, and the equation would come out even.

It had not come out even. It was not coming out even tonight.

Because tonight there was a girl with ink in her knuckles and a face like a locked drawer, and a pamphlet she had dropped with a dead man's living mark on it, and a form on his table—he took it out now, the census return, the fair copy, the one that would go to the district office and from there, he happened to know, to a new desk that had appeared in the customs house this winter staffed by men who did not wear uniforms but had very good boots—tonight there was a form on his table with a blank space where the mark's cutter ought to be named.

The new desk was Inspector Hale's. Everyone in the census office knew what it was for, though no one said so aloud. The Governor had grown tired of pamphlets. The Lantern Quarter had grown loud. And the census—the great patient census, eleven years of Elias's careful counting—had quietly acquired a second purpose, the way a lantern acquires a second purpose when you carry it through a door you mean to close behind you. Count the households. Note the presses. Name the hands.

*Number of skilled persons engaged in the letter trades.*

Elias dipped his pen. Held it over the blank.

He thought: *I am a servant of the record. The record does not lie. If I write down what I suspect, I have lied. If I write down what I know, I have killed.*

He thought: *I don't know anything. I have a creased paper and an instinct. Instincts are not evidence. A compass rose is not a crime.*

He thought, and this thought arrived unbidden, in a voice that sounded like the girl's, level and quiet across the counter of the shop: *We keep the shop.* We. Who was we? Who hid inside that word like a stowaway?

Outside, the fog was coming in off the harbor, the thick white Verrance fog that ate sound and direction, and the harbor bell rang its slow blind count, and somewhere in the white a patrol's boots went by, four men, unhurried, the heels striking in time like a press pulling proofs.

Elias set down the pen without writing.

He picked it up. Set it down again.

In the end he wrote: *Crane & Sons Print Shop. Proprietor, Widow Crane. Trade continued per original license. Skilled persons: as recorded, deceased.*

It was true. Every word of it was true, or had been true, or was arranged in such a way that truth shaded off its edges like fog shading a lamppost. *As recorded, deceased.* Aldous Crane was recorded. Aldous Crane was deceased. The sentence committed him to nothing and revealed nothing, and it was the finest piece of composition Elias Wren had produced in eleven years of writing plain things, and he despised it, and he was proud of it, and he sat looking at it while the candle guttered and understood that something in him had cracked, cleanly, like ice under a boot—a hairline, a whisper of give, the whole frozen surface of his dutiful life suddenly announcing that beneath it there was moving water.

He had not reported the pamphlet. That was the fact he kept returning to, turning it over like a coin of uncertain metal. He had stood in that shop for twenty minutes with the pamphlet lying face-up on the counter between them, its little compass rose staring up at him like an eye, and he had finished his questions, and thanked her, and left, and the pamphlet had stayed where it was, unmentioned, unreported, a secret sitting in broad daylight between two strangers who had agreed, without a word passing between them, to pretend it wasn't there.

Why?

Because he suspected, said the clerk in him. Because suspicion unconfirmed does not belong in a record.

Because she had looked at him, said something else in him, some older thing, some thing with Joren Vale's face on it—looked at him with those steady grey eyes when he'd asked about the mark, and said, *an old family device*, and waited, absolutely still, to see what he would do with it. And in that waiting he had felt the whole weight of his office balance on a pinhead: report, or don't. And he had chosen don't. He had already chosen, standing at that counter, and everything since—the long walk, the tea, the pen lifted and set down—was only the choosing explained after the fact, the way a man explains a wound after he has already stopped the bleeding.

There was one more thing he could do, said the clerk, who was, Elias was beginning to understand, a coward with excellent handwriting.

He could ask her.

Not as a census taker. Off the books. After dark, when the shop was closed, when no boots were listening. He could knock on that clean quiet door and ask the question the form did not contain: *who cut the compass?*

And what, said the older thing, do you imagine you'll do with the answer?

Elias looked at the commission on his table, at the little lantern with its wave-curved crown, at the signature of a dead man standing witness over work he had not made. He looked at the fair-copy form, with its fog-shaded truth drying in his neat clerk's hand. He looked at the blank space in his own chest where certainty used to live.

Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper, and did not write a report on it. He wrote an address instead—Lantern Row—and folded it away, and banked his fire, and lay down in the dark above the chandler's shop while the fog pressed at the windows like a hand.

Tomorrow, the returns went to the district office. Tomorrow, Inspector Hale's new desk would receive eleven years of Elias Wren's careful counting and begin, patiently, to read it backwards.

Tomorrow, he would finish his rounds.

But tomorrow evening—Elias stared into the dark and admitted it at last, the way one admits a sum that will not balance—tomorrow evening he would walk back down Lantern Row, off the record, out of uniform, and knock twice on the door of Crane & Sons, and ask one question too many.

And God help him, he already knew he would believe whatever answer she gave him.
