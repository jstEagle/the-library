## 4. The Lantern Uprising Stirs

The rain came to Verrance the way debt came—slowly at first, then all at once, and always with someone standing behind it.

Hazel heard it before she saw it: a soft percussion on the shop's south-facing windows, so faint it might have been mistaken for the settling of the type cases overhead. She set down her graver mid-stroke and listened the way her father had taught her to listen—not with her ears but with the whole of her attention, the way a cutter reads a line of steel before ever touching it. Rain on glass sounded different from rain on slate, different from rain on the harbor water that lay three streets east. This was window-rain. Window-rain meant customers stayed home, meant the day would be quiet, meant she could work without interruption until the light failed.

She bent back over the punch clamped in her bench vise—a small order from the chandlery on Rope Walk, forty labels for tallow candles, nothing in it worth remembering—and let the graver find its angle again. The steel whispered under the tool. Somewhere in that whisper was the only conversation Hazel had ever trusted completely: the one between hand and metal, where every word was true or the work showed it.

"Labels again." Marta's voice came from the composing room behind her, dry as old paper. "You'll cut your way into the grave making labels for candles."

"Better than cutting my way out of one."

Marta snorted. Hazel heard the shuffle of her slippers across the floorboards, the particular creak of the third board from the doorway that no amount of planing had ever cured, and then the older woman was beside her, close enough that Hazel could smell the camphor she rubbed into her arthritic hands and, beneath it, the ghost of ink that never washed out of anyone who had spent fifty years at the case. Marta Voss had been setting type since before Hazel's father had bought the shop, and she treated the compositor's trade with the proprietary tenderness of a woman who had outlived two husbands and every apprentice ever assigned to her.

"Move your elbow," Marta said. "You're crowding the light."

Hazel moved her elbow. It was easier than arguing. In eleven years of working side by side they had built an entire language out of such small corrections—*move your elbow, mind your leading, you've set the 'qu' backwards again*—and neither of them had ever once said aloud what both of them knew: that the hands guiding the graver belonged to the finest cutter in Verrance, and that those hands were attached to a woman whose name appeared nowhere in the shop's records except as *niece, resident.*

That was the arrangement. That was the architecture of Hazel's whole life, load-bearing and invisible, like the timbers under a house.

"The widow Crane wants the shutters latched before dark," Marta said, straightening with a wince. "And she wants to know if you'll take the mending basket round to Mrs. Pell tomorrow."

"I'll take it."

"And she wants—" Marta paused, and something in the pause made Hazel look up. The old woman was watching her with an expression Hazel couldn't read, which was unusual; Marta's face was normally as legible as a broadsheet headline. "There's talk," Marta said. "Down at the Anchorfish last night. Talk I'd sooner you heard from me than from a stranger."

Hazel set the graver down carefully, aligning it parallel with the bench edge as her father had drilled into her—*a tool left crooked is a tool left angry*—and gave Marta her full attention.

"Talk about what?"

Marta glanced toward the front windows, where the rain was thickening against the glass, and lowered her voice though there was no one in the shop to hear them. The press stood silent under its canvas cover, the galleys racked and empty, the whole room holding its breath the way rooms do when they are full of other people's secrets.

"You know the land decrees," Marta said. "The ones the Governor published in the spring. The ones that redrew the boundaries out past the salt marshes—gave the tidal flats and the commons to the Crown's favorites, turned half the fishing families off ground their grandfathers drained with their own hands."

"I know them." Everyone in Verrance knew them. The decrees had gone up on the customs house door in fine engraved script, sealed with the Governor's arms, witnessed by his council, unimpeachable. There had been grumbling, petitions, a fistfight outside the Anchorfish that had cost Old Bram two teeth. But paper signed and sealed is paper signed and sealed, and the fishing families had learned what everyone in a port city eventually learns: that the law flows downhill like water, and pools wherever the powerful decide to dig a basin for it.

"Well." Marta pulled a stool from beneath the bench and sat down heavily, which she only did when a story was going to take some telling. "There's a man come round the taverns lately. Quiet fellow, dockside accent, keeps to himself. Last night he bought a round for the whole room—which should have been the first warning, because nobody buys a round in Verrance without wanting something—and when the cups were full he asked a question. Just one question. He asked whether anyone present could read a decree closely enough to tell true engraving from false."

Hazel felt the words settle over her like cold air through a cracked pane. "False how?"

"That's the thing." Marta leaned forward. "He didn't say. He just asked. And then he put a coin on the table—a gold piece, Hazel, a full sovereign, more than most men see in a month—and said that anyone who brought him a *copy* of the spring decrees, a printed copy, not the engraved original, would earn another. And then he left before the cups were empty."

"A copy of a public document. Anyone can walk to the customs house and read it off the door."

"Anyone can *read* it." Marta's eyes were sharp. "But who has a printed copy lying about? Who even knows if there *is* a printed copy?" She let the silence sit for a moment, and Hazel understood, with a slow drop of the stomach, exactly why Marta had chosen this shop, this morning, this conversation.

Because there was a printed copy. Of course there was. Crane & Sons had printed the decrees in March—three hundred broadsheets, commissioned through the Governor's own stationer, run off on their press in a single long night while Hazel had stood at the stone checking every sheet. She remembered the job vividly. She remembered because the commission had come with an odd instruction: print the text exactly as provided, make no corrections, query nothing. Her father—her father had still been alive then, still able to sit up in his bed and sign the delivery docket with a shaking hand—and Hazel had queried nothing, because querying nothing was how you survived in a city where the Governor's name opened doors and closed others.

Three hundred copies. Most had been distributed to the parish offices, the harbor master, the guild halls. Some had surely been thrown away, used to wrap fish, lining drawers in houses all along the waterfront. And somewhere among those three hundred sheets was whatever the quiet man in the Anchorfish was looking for—or rather, whatever someone behind the quiet man was looking for.

"Marta," Hazel said slowly, "what are people saying? About the decrees?"

"They're saying," Marta said, "that the seal is right and the signature is right and the wording is right, and that none of that matters, because the *authority* isn't right. They're saying the Governor never had power to grant those lands—that the grant needed assent from the colonial council, and the council never gave it, and that somewhere between the council chamber and the printing office, somebody wrote the assent in anyway." She spread her camphor-scented hands. "They're saying the document we printed was already a forgery before our press ever touched it. Or worse—they're saying it wasn't, and the forgery came after, and nobody can prove which, because nobody kept the printed sheets to compare."

Hazel looked down at her hands. Ink had worked itself into the creases around her knuckles, as it always did, a stain no pumice ever fully lifted. Her hands knew that job. Her hands remembered the weight of the decree forms, the particular stiffness of the paper stock the stationer had supplied, the way the Governor's arms had sat slightly crooked in the forme because the woodcut block had a hairline check in the oak-leaf border that Hazel herself had noticed and been ordered to ignore.

*I noticed,* she thought. *I noticed everything, and I said nothing, and three hundred lies went out into the world wearing our mark.*

Not her mark. The shop's mark. But the distinction had grown thinner over the years, worn smooth like a coin passed too many times.

"This is dangerous talk," she said aloud. "Tavern talk. Tavern talk is cheap."

"It is." Marta rose from the stool, joints crackling. "But the man asking questions wasn't talking. He was listening. And there's a difference, girl, between a storm you hear coming and a storm that's already over your roof." She shuffled toward the composing room, then stopped at the threshold. "I'm too old to be brave," she said, not turning around. "I've buried enough friends to know what the Governor does with printers who ask questions. You remember Joss Amberley."

Hazel did. Everyone did, though no one spoke of him. Joss Amberley, printer, hanged four years ago from the customs house gantry with a pamphlet stuffed in his mouth—the pamphlet unreadable now, ink run black down his chin like a second beard. His press had been confiscated and broken publicly in the market square, the type scattered into the harbor, and after that the printers of Verrance had learned their lesson so thoroughly that for four years the city's underground had produced nothing but rumor and resentment, which were, as her father used to say, the only commodities that need no press to circulate.

"I remember," Hazel said quietly.

"Then keep your head down, and I'll keep mine, and we'll let the fishermen fight their own battles." Marta said it firmly, but her eyes, when she finally turned, held something else—something apologetic, almost sorrowful, as if she were sorry to be handing Hazel such sensible advice. "The mending basket," she added. "Don't forget."

Then she was gone into the dimness of the composing room, and Hazel was alone with the rain and the graver and the half-cut candle label, none of which required anything of her but the next stroke.

She worked for another hour. The label took shape under her tools: a candle, a flame, the chandler's initials in a cartouche. Competent, clean, forgettable work. Work that would feed the widow Crane and keep the rent paid and leave no trace of Hazel Crane anywhere in the world except in the muscle memory of her own hands.

*Keep your head down.* She had been keeping her head down for eleven years. It was practically a craft in itself, and she was a master of it, the same way she was a master of the graver. She knew exactly how much space a person could occupy without being seen. She had learned it the hard way, at fourteen, when the grief was fresh and the shop's creditors were circling and the only way to save her father's life's work had been to become, on paper, nobody at all.

And yet.

The itch started low, somewhere beneath the sternum, the way it always did. It was an uncomfortable sensation, this itch—inconvenient, unwelcome, and utterly immune to reason. It had visited her in March, when she'd checked the decree formes and felt the wrongness humming in the spacing like a badly cast sort. It had visited her at Joss Amberley's hanging, when she'd stood in the crowd with her hood up and felt the rope's shadow fall across the whole trade. It visited her now, sitting in her chest alongside the memory of Marta's voice: *somebody wrote the assent in anyway.*

If it was true—if the decrees were forged—then every family pushed off the marshlands had been robbed twice: once of their land, and once of the truth of it. And the instrument of the second theft, the thing that had dressed the lie in respectable clothing and nailed it to the customs house door, had been a printing press. Had been, in the smallest, most deniable sense, *hers.*

Hazel put down the graver and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars.

*It's not yours to fix. You cut punches. You stay invisible. Invisible people don't hang.*

But the counter-voice was quieter these days, and getting quieter, and she suspected she knew why. Eleven years of erasing herself had not, it turned out, erased the self. It had only taught the self to wait.

* * *

The rain settled in for the evening, and the shop grew dim, and Hazel lit the bench lamp and the lantern in the front window—the signal, per custom, that Crane & Sons was open for orders, though no orders would come on a night like this. The flame threw her shadow huge against the back wall, a giantess bent over a giantess's work, and Hazel watched it while she ate bread and cheese standing up, the way she always ate when the widow Crane was at her sister's across town.

Marta had gone home at dusk, pausing at the door to say, "Think on what I told you. And think on what I *didn't* tell you," which was so precisely Marta that Hazel had almost smiled.

Now the shop ticked and dripped around her. She ought to lock up. Instead she found herself walking to the flat files where the job dockets were stored, eleven years of commissions in their labeled folders, and pulling the one marked in her father's hand: *Govt. Stationer — Land Decrees — March.*

The docket was thin. Commission letter, delivery note, payment receipt. No proof copy—proofs of government work went back to the stationer, standard practice, no exceptions. Hazel unfolded the commission letter and read it again by lamplight, though she knew it by heart. *Print 300, text as supplied, no alterations, no queries.* The stationer's seal. A clerk's initials. Nothing unusual, nothing damning, nothing at all—except the instruction itself, which had seemed merely officious in March and now seemed like a man gagging a witness in advance.

No alterations. No queries. As if whoever drafted the letter had known there would be things to alter, things to query.

Hazel stood in the lamplight holding the paper, and the itch became something sharper, closer to hunger. Because here was the thing she had never said to anyone, the thing that lived in the deepest drawer of her: she was good enough to have caught it. If she had been allowed to proof the text against the council's record—if she had been anyone at all instead of a niece, a ghost, a pair of hands—she might have caught the forgery in March. Three hundred sheets. One careful reader. That was all it would have taken, maybe, to stop the theft before it wore its crown.

Instead she had done what she was told, because doing what she was told was the price of existing, and she had paid it so long it hardly felt like paying anymore.

She slid the docket back into its folder. She squared the corners. She told herself the feeling would pass by morning, the way it always passed, drowned under work and caution and the thousand small labors of being no one.

She was blowing out the lamp when she heard footsteps in the street.

Not the widow Crane—the step was too long, too deliberate. Not a drunk; drunks wove. These steps came straight up the lane and stopped directly before the shop, and through the rain-blurred glass Hazel saw a figure pause at the window, silhouetted against the lamplight she had not yet extinguished. A man. Slim. Hat brim dripping. He stood there a moment, reading the shopfront as if it were a notice posted for his benefit—*Crane & Sons, Printers*—and then, to her astonishment, he raised his hand and knocked. Twice. Politely. At nearly nine o'clock, in the rain, on a night when every honest shop in Verrance was shuttered.

Hazel's hand went instinctively to the bench drawer where her father's knife lay wrapped in oilcloth.

Through the glass, the man waited, patient as a summons. And as her eyes adjusted to the gloom, she caught the detail that turned her blood to seawater: pinned inside the brim of his hat, catching the lantern light, was the small tin badge of the census office.

The count had come to Crane & Sons. And the man standing in the rain, waiting to be let in, was the last person in Verrance Hazel wanted anywhere near her father's ledgers—or her father's daughter.

She reached for the lamp and blew it out.
