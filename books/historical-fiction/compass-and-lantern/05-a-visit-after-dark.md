## 5. A Visit After Dark

Elias Wren had counted four hundred and eleven households in the parish of Lower Verrance, and he could not stop counting the fifth.

It was not the widow Crane's answers that troubled him—those had been unremarkable, delivered in a voice like dry paper: two persons resident, herself and a niece, no lodgers, no apprentices, no trade but printing, and printing hardly a trade anymore, was it, sir, not since the Governor's tariffs. It was not even the girl herself, though she had stood in the doorway with the stillness of a heron at the water's edge, answering only when addressed and then in exactly as many words as the question required. No. What troubled him was the pamphlet.

He had carried it home in his coat pocket the way a man carries a splinter he cannot reach. Three miles through the drizzle, up two flights to his rooms above the rope walk, where he set it on his desk beneath the window light and told himself he was only examining an artifact of his office. Census men collected things. Papers, names, small facts. It was a collector's instinct, nothing more.

The pamphlet was eight pages, badly printed on thin stock, the kind of thing that came off a jobbing press in a hurry. *On the Lands of Verrance Province*, the title ran, and beneath it a screed against the new land decrees—the same decrees every tavern muttered about, the ones that turned three generations of harbor families into tenants on their own ground overnight. Sedition, probably. Certainly the sort of paper Inspector Hale would pay silver for. But none of that was what kept Elias standing over his desk while the candle guttered.

It was the mark on the last page, tucked into the colophon like a signature too honest to be legible: a compass rose enclosed in a lantern, cut small and clean, the rays of the compass rendered as fine as spider silk inside the lantern's glazing bars. A printer's device. An owner's pride.

Elias knew it. That was the trouble. He knew it the way he knew the shapes of letters themselves, because seven years ago, before the fever took old Aldous Crane and his son Wren-named-for-no-one, Elias had sat across a desk from Aldous Crane and recorded the commission of a shop sign—a carved wooden lantern with a compass in its belly—for the census rolls' register of tradesmen's marks. He remembered the old man's hands, black with ink to the second knuckle, drawing the device in the ledger with a swan-quill. *My design*, Aldous had said. *Cut it myself, and my boy will cut the next one.* And he had signed the entry: **A. Crane, Cutter**.

The pamphlet's mark was not Aldous Crane's cutting. Elias had spent eleven years training his eye to distinguish hands—the census required it, required him to know a forged signature from a genuine one, a copied device from an original—and this was a different hand entirely. Finer. More certain. Where the elder Crane's lines had been workmanlike, this was artistry: the lantern's glazing bars tapered to hairlines, the compass needle given the faintest suggestion of trembling motion, as if the whole device might swing true at any moment. This was a punch cut by someone who loved the metal. Someone young, perhaps. Someone whose hand had steadied over years of secret practice.

Someone who was not the dead son, and not the dead father, and therefore did not exist.

Elias sat down slowly at his desk. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere down the rope walk a watchman called the half hour in a voice worn smooth as a stair rail.

He thought: *I am a census taker. It is my duty to report irregularities.* The words arranged themselves in his mind with the tidy rectitude of a well-set column, and he examined them the way he examined signatures, and found them wanting. Because he knew—had known since his second year in the office, had felt it growing in him like damp in a wall—what the census of Verrance had become. It had begun as counting. It had become hunting.

He had seen the memoranda circulated from the Governor's secretary to the census office six months past. *Note all households engaged in the printing trade. Note presses, type stocks, apprentices bound to the trade. Note any irregularity of residence or inheritance.* On its face, administration. Beneath, a net. The Governor wanted no record of his forged decrees leaving the province, and the only hands capable of printing such proof belonged to printers, so the printers would be found, and counted, and—when the moment came—closed upon. The census Elias walked door to door performing was the first draft of a list of arrests.

And now he held a pamphlet that could put the noose around one particular throat, a fine young throat attached to a girl who answered questions in exactly as many words as required, and he found that he could not make himself write the report.

He blew out the candle and sat in the dark, and was ashamed—not of what he might do, but of how long it had taken him to feel this. Eleven years of carrying lists. Somewhere behind those lists were faces he had never looked at properly.

* * *

He went back on Thursday night, which was cowardly, because Thursday night the shops along Tallow Row shuttered early and the streets emptied, and he told himself it was discretion when he knew it was the desire not to be seen. He wore no badge. He carried no ledger. In his inner pocket, wrapped like something stolen, was the pamphlet.

Crane & Sons Print Shop showed a single light above the shutters, on the floor where a workroom would be. Elias stood across the lane in the mouth of the cooper's alley for a long moment, rain finding the gap between his collar and his neck, and asked himself, with a bureaucrat's precision, what exactly he intended to say. *Good evening. I have returned outside all procedure to ask whether you are secretly alive.* There was no form for this. There had never been a form for this. All his life he had trusted forms, and here he stood at forty-one years of age with none to shield him.

The light upstairs moved. Then, before he had resolved anything, the shop door opened a hand's width, and the girl's voice came through the gap—low, level, without welcome.

"The shop is closed. Business hours are posted."

"I'm not here on business," Elias said. "Or—I am, but not the office's." He heard how it sounded. He took off his hat, rain and all, so she could see his face bare and his brim empty of any tin. "My name is Elias Wren. You'll remember me. I counted your household on Tuesday morning."

"I remember."

"May I come in for ten minutes? You may keep the knife in your hand the whole time. I'd think better of you if you did."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked in the gutters. Somewhere a dog shook itself and fell silent again. Then the door widened just enough, and Hazel Crane—he was nearly sure that was not her name, and the certainty of it sat in his stomach like a swallowed coin—stepped back into the dark of the shop and let him pass.

Inside smelled of oil, of metal filings, of old paper and the sweet rot of paste, and underneath it all something mineral and cold like the inside of a bell. She lit a single lamp on the cutting bench and set herself behind it, and he saw that she had indeed taken up a knife—a burnishing knife, broad-bladed, meant for pressing type into paper, but heavy enough in the right grip. Her knuckles were pale around it.

"Ten minutes," she said.

Elias did not sit, though a stool waited by the composing stone. He stood where he was, hat in both hands, water pooling off its brim onto the flagstones, and he said the thing he had rehearsed for two days and two nights and which still came out wrong-footed and plain.

"On Tuesday you dropped a pamphlet. Or it dropped near you—it doesn't matter. I picked it up. I've read it. And I've spent two days failing to file the report that goes with it."

Her face did not move. But her thumb shifted on the knife's handle, a fraction, the way ice shifts before it cracks.

"The report," she said.

"There's a memorandum out of the Governor's office. Six months old. I've seen it." He made himself hold her eyes, though looking at her was like looking into the lantern mark itself—all that fine work hidden behind dark glass. "This year's count isn't a count. Every printer, every press, every apprentice and journeyman in Verrance is being entered into a separate register. Names, addresses, stocks of type. When the register is complete, it goes to Inspector Hale, and Hale has warrants drawn in advance. They mean to close the trade down in a single week, all at once, so no shop can warn another."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft and straightened. When she spoke, her voice was still level, but slower, each word placed like a sort into its case. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I take the register." The confession tasted of iron. "I have been building the list of arrests door by door, street by street, smiling at widows and writing down their sons. I counted your household and wrote *two persons, trade of printing* in a book that will be read by a man who burns shops. And then you dropped that pamphlet, and I saw the mark on it, and—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I recognized the mark. I registered its commission seven years ago. Aldous Crane drew it in my ledger himself. But the cutting on your pamphlet isn't his hand, and it isn't the son's, because I took the son's specimen the same day, and his lines were uncertain. Whoever cut that mark learned after both of them were dead. Whoever cut it—" he gestured, helplessly, at the bench, the lamplight, the whole dim kingdom of the shop— "*is in this room.*"

For a long moment Hazel Crane was absolutely still, and Elias understood that he was watching a woman decide, with the totality of a judge, whether he would leave this room alive. He found he did not blame her for the arithmetic. He had spent eleven years teaching this city that a man with a list was a man with a hook.

"You should go," she said at last.

"Probably."

"You come here, at night, no witness, and accuse me of—" she didn't finish it. Accusing her of existing. Of mastery. Of being, in her own shop, her own self. He watched her choose the sentence carefully, the way a compositor chooses, and he realized she was doing what he did: setting words in order to control what they revealed.

"I accuse you of nothing," Elias said. "I report nothing. That's what I came to say. I can't—unmake the register. God knows I've tried to find a way; there isn't one; the pages are countersigned weekly. But I can tell you what's coming, and I can tell you when. And I can ask you one question, which is the real reason I'm standing in your shop like a fool with his hat dripping on your floor."

Hazel's eyes narrowed. Rain drummed the shutters. Down the lane, the cooper's dog began to bark at nothing and gave up.

"Ask it," she said.

Elias turned his hat over in his hands. Inside the crown, where he had once pinned the tin badge, there was a pale rectangle in the felt, sun-faded around a shape he had removed before walking out his own door tonight—the first time in eleven years he had left the house without it, and it had felt like stepping out without skin.

"When the warrants come," he said, "they will come for whoever cut that mark. Hale doesn't want the pamphlet—he wants the *hand*. A pamphlet is paper; paper burns. But a cutter can cut again, and Hale knows it, and so do the men who wrote the memorandum. So my question is not *who are you*, because you've built a life on that question having no answer, and I respect the masonry of it more than you'd believe." His voice caught on something and he smoothed it flat. "My question is: when they come for the hand, will you still be here to hide it? Or is there somewhere—some cellar, some ship, some name—that already knows you exist?"

He saw the question strike her. Not the words—their weight. He saw it in the way her gaze went, for half a heartbeat, toward the floor, as if she could see through stone and timber and packed earth to something beneath, some other room he did not know about. Then it was gone, shuttered, and she was the heron again, still water, nothing to catch.

"There is no one," she said. "There is no mark. My uncle cut that device before I was born, and he is dead, and I am my aunt's niece from inland, and I set type badly and sweep floors. That's what your register says. That's what the truth is."

"That's what the register says," Elias agreed softly.

"And you'll change nothing in it?"

"I'll change nothing in it." He put his hat back on, and the rain-darkened brim threw a shadow across his eyes, and for a moment the census taker of Lower Verrance looked older than his years, and tireder than his sins. "But hear me, because I'll only grow a conscience once: the register finishes in nine days. Hale's warrants follow within the fortnight. Whatever you are hiding in this room or under this city—you have nine days to decide what to do with it. After that, decisions get made for you."

He crossed to the door. His hand was on the latch when her voice stopped him—not loud, but pitched with a precision that made it seem louder than it was.

"Why?"

He turned. She had not moved from behind the lamp, but the knife lay flat on the bench now, abandoned, and her arms were folded, and her eyes had a fierce wet brightness that she would deny to her grave.

"Why warn me? Why risk yourself? Men like you don't—" She stopped, recalibrated, chose honesty as a weapon for once instead of armor. "I watched you Tuesday. You love the forms. You love the little boxes and the columns and the correct entries. Why burn it?"

And because it was night, and raining, and because he had carried the memory eleven years like a stone sewn into his coat, Elias Wren told her a piece of the truth.

"Because I have taken a man to the gallows with a form before," he said. "Years ago. A printer, in the eastern parishes. I wrote down that he had a press he hadn't licensed, and six weeks later I stood in a crowd and watched them hang him, and everyone around me cheered, and I remember thinking that the worst part wasn't the hanging. It was that his name had gone through my handwriting to get there. My hand. My little correct columns." He worked the latch open. Cold air and the smell of wet stone came in. "I swore I'd never again be the instrument that turns a name into a body. I haven't kept the oath. I've broken it eleven years running, one household at a time. But tonight—" he stepped halfway over the threshold, into the rain, and looked back at her once— "tonight I keep it. Nine days, niece of the widow Crane. Use them."

He pulled the door shut behind him and stood a moment in the dark lane with the rain coming down through the gaps between roofs, heart hammering like a fist on a locked door, unable to say whether he had just saved a woman or condemned them both. Behind the shuttered window, the lamp still burned. Ahead of him, three streets away, the census office windows showed lights where the clerks worked late over the register, entering names in fair round hands.

Elias turned up his collar and walked toward those lights, because a man who missed his evening signing would be noticed, and noticed men were questioned, and he had nine days of lying to do.

Behind him, in Crane & Sons Print Shop, Hazel Crane stood a long time without moving. Then she lifted the lamp from the bench and carried it, slowly, down the stairs that led below—stairs Elias had not seen, that did not appear on any plan of the building—to a door he had never guessed at, behind which something waited that had been waiting for exactly nine days' worth of permission.

She knocked twice, paused, and knocked once more.

From below, in the dark, a voice answered.
