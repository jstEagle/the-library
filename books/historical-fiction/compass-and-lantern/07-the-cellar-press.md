## 7. The Cellar Press

Marta led me through the chandler's district at the hour when the fog came off the harbor and ate the streetlamps whole.

I had said no. I wanted that recorded somewhere, in some ledger of my own conduct: *Hazel Crane said no.* I had said it twice, once in the shop with my hand flat on the forme to keep it from trembling, and once again on the stairs outside the widow Odile's boarding house, where Marta had stopped and looked at me the way a compositor looks at a line of type set wrong—not angry, only certain it must be corrected.

"You cut for the dead," Marta had said. "It's time you cut for the living."

Now she walked ahead of me with her shawl pulled up and her cane tapping a rhythm on the cobbles that I suspected was not a rhythm at all but a signal, three taps and a pause, three taps and a pause, and every lamplighter we passed nodded to her as though she were his grandmother. Perhaps she was. In Verrance, everyone over fifty seemed to be somebody's grandmother, and grandmothers were the one class of citizen the Governor's men had never thought to fear. That, I would learn, was their great error.

The chandler's shop sat at the crook of Tallow Row, where the street bent around an old customs post like water around a stone. Its windows glowed amber with rendered fat and beeswax candles stacked in pyramids; the smell reached halfway down the block, sweet and animal, tallow and honey and something burnt underneath. A boy sat on the step turning a knife over his knuckles, and I thought, absurdly, *that child will lose a finger,* before I understood he was juggling it the way other children juggled stones—bored, practiced, half asleep.

"Auntie," the boy said, without looking up.

"Tam." Marta's voice changed, shed its market-day warmth and became something clipped and efficient. "Is she down?"

"She's been down since the tide turned." He stood then, and looked at me—looked at me properly, the way people rarely did, head tipped, eyes going from my ink-stained fingers to my face and back again. "You're the cutter."

"I'm nobody," I said. It came out smooth as oiled paper. I had been saying it for eleven years.

Tam grinned. "That's what they all say down there. Come on, then. Mind your head on the third stair. It's a liar, that stair—it looks taller than it is."

He went through the shop, past a counter where a sleepy apprentice weighed candle-ends for a fishwife, past racks of dipped wicks hanging like pale kelp, to a door marked *STORAGE — KEEP OUT* in letters so faded they were nearly an apology. Behind the door, stairs went down into the dark, and the dark breathed out cold air that smelled of wax and oil and, beneath it, unmistakably, ink.

I stopped on the top step.

Eleven years is a long time to practice being invisible. You learn the shape of it the way you learn a letterform—the exact pressure of the graver, the angle of the shoulder, how much of yourself to shave away each morning so that by noon you are nothing but the niece, the girl who sweeps, the poor relation who happens to have steady hands. I had built my invisibility punch by punch, and I knew its weight better than any matrix in the shop. And standing on those stairs, smelling ink in a cellar under a chandler's shop, I felt that whole carefully cut form begin to shift in its bed, loose, ready to spill.

"Hazel," Marta said behind me, gently, using my name like a hand on my back. "Nobody's asking you to stay. Only to look."

*Only to look.* My father used to say that about the cutting bench. *Only look first, Hazel-girl. The steel tells you everything if you only look.*

I went down.

* * *

The cellar was larger than the shop above it, which meant it had been dug out secretly, or had belonged to something older—a smuggler's vault, a warren, one of the old salt-cellars from before the Harbor Act. Candles burned in niches along the walls, dozens of them, the chandler's own stock put to work lighting what it was never meant to light. And in the middle of the room, under a low arch of blackened brick, stood a press.

Not a jobbing press, not one of the little hand presses that turned out playbills and funeral notices. This was an iron common press, full-sized, its platen scarred and re-surfaced, its screw freshly greased, standing on a platform of ship's timbers that had clearly come from more than one vessel. Around it: a composing stone with type already locked in chase. A case of sorts with the compartments labeled in a careful hand. A stack of dampened paper under oilcloth. A drying line strung with broadsheets hung like laundry, their ink still shining faintly in the candlelight.

I crossed to the drying line before I could stop myself. Old habit. Proofreaders' hands move toward fresh print the way sailors' hands move toward rigging.

*THE GOVERNOR'S DECREE IS A FORGERY*, said the topmost sheet, in a bold condensed face I didn't recognize. Below it, smaller: *Compare the seal. Compare the hand. Demand the original record.* And below that, at the foot, a small woodcut device: a lantern, and inside the lantern's glass—

A compass rose. Eight points, the north point elongated, the whole rose tilted two degrees off true, exactly as a cutter tilts it when she wants the mark to sit lively rather than stiff.

My mark. Or near enough to make my stomach drop through the floorboards.

"That's not mine," I said, and heard my own voice go thin. "I never cut that."

"No," said a woman's voice from the shadows beyond the press. "You didn't. But whoever did learned from the same hand you did—or you both learned from the same mistake."

She stepped into the candlelight, and I understood at once why Tam had said *she's been down since the tide turned*, as though this were weather. She was tall, broad through the shoulders, with sun-cracked skin and hair bound back in tarred cord, and she wore a coat that had been a naval officer's coat once, before someone had picked off the buttons and replaced them with bone. Her left hand was missing two fingers. She held a proof pull in the remaining ones, and she offered it to me the way you offer a knife handle-first.

"Odile Vasseur," she said. "Master of the *Morning Tide*. You're Crane's girl."

"I'm—" I began.

"Don't," she said, not unkindly. "We haven't got time for the version you tell the census man. Marta says you're the best cutter south of the capital. Marta doesn't flatter. Marta thinks flattery is a form of theft." She nodded at the pull in my hands. "Look at the mark. Tell me what's wrong with it."

I looked. It was hard to look at anything else; the thing was a wound dressed up as a signature. Someone had taken the compass-lantern device—the one my brother Joss had cut, the one I had finished after he died, the one I had pressed into the margin of perhaps forty pamphlets in the early years because grief makes you careless—and copied it. Copied it badly. The lantern's panes were uneven. The compass points were symmetrical, which ours never were; Joss had always cut the east point a hair short, a private joke about the east wind that had wrecked his first apprenticeship voyage. And the tilt was wrong—two degrees the other way.

"The east point," I said. "It should be shorter. And the rose leans west, not east. Whoever cut this worked from a printed impression and reversed it in their mind." I looked up. "Where did you get an impression of our mark?"

"From a pamphlet dropped in a gutter on Census Morning," Odile said, "by a girl who answered to the name of Crane's niece." She let that sit between us like a heavy sort on a stone. "A census clerk found it. A census clerk who, we're told, has not reported it. Do you understand yet why you're standing in my cellar?"

I did not answer. My hands were doing something without my permission—turning the proof pull over, reading the presswork itself, the impression depth, the evenness of the packing. Cheap paper, hasty make-ready, but the press was sound. The press was very sound.

"Who cut this forgery?" I asked.

"Ferro. Our mechanic. He's good with iron and hopeless with steel." Odile shrugged. "It doesn't matter. What matters is this: papers bearing that mark are moving through three checkpoints tomorrow night, and if Inspector Hale's men know anything—and Hale knows everything eventually—they'll know the true mark has a short east point and a western lean. They'll seize the documents, trace them here, and hang everyone whose name appears in this room, starting with the grandmother and ending with the boy." She took the pull back from me. "Unless the documents bear the *true* mark. Cut fresh. By the hand that made it."

"And why would I do that?"

"Because you've spent eleven years cutting beautiful things for a dead man's name," Odile said, "and I think it's eating you alive. I've seen that look before. On men who lost ships. On women who lost countries." She turned the proof pull toward the candlelight, and the forged compass rose threw a small warped shadow on the brick. "Belonging isn't a thing you find, Crane's girl. It's a thing you print. Enough copies, and it becomes true."

* * *

They gave me tea, of all things—black, bitter, brewed in a candle-melting pot—and a crate to sit on beside the composing stone, and for a while they simply let me be near the press, which was, I suspect, the whole strategy. You do not argue a printer into loving a press. You put her next to one and wait.

Tam sat cross-legged on the floor sorting damaged type into a salvage box, chattering the entire time in a low, ceaseless stream, the way water talks to itself in a culvert. He was the runner, it emerged. He carried messages in hollowed candle-ends, in fish, once—in his words, delivered with enormous pride—*inside a loaf of bread what was also a hat.* He knew every alley in the chandler's quarter, every constable's drinking habits, every door that stuck and every door that didn't. He was maybe twelve. He had been running since he was nine, since the year the land decrees came down and his family's plot on the marsh side was reassigned to a sugar factor with a signature no one could read.

"That's the thing nobody says," Tam told me, holding up a battered lowercase *e* to the light, checking the face the way I checked faces, and something in my chest turned over at the sight. "Everybody thinks it's about the Governor being wicked. He is, don't mistake me, he's wicked as a wet cat. But it's about the *paper*. Paper decides who owns what. Paper decided my mum's house wasn't hers anymore. So whoever cuts the paper—" he pointed the *e* at me like a tiny accusatory finger— "is the actual government. That's what Marta says. Marta says the pen's mightier than the sword but the *punch* beats them both."

"Marta," I said, "talks too much."

"She does," Tam agreed happily. "But she's usually right. Like about you. She said you'd come down if we just let you smell the ink." He grinned, gap-toothed. "Did it work?"

I looked at the press. The press looked back at me, patient as a confessor.

"It worked," I said.

Across the cellar, Odile and Marta were bent over the composing stone, arguing in the shorthand of people who have argued for years—about routes, about tides, about whether the *Morning Tide* could take the sealed plates out with the wool shipment or whether they'd need to wait for the grain convoy and its heavier escort of bribes. I caught fragments. *Three checkpoints.* *The customs house queue.* *The bridge of chains, if the drawtide's late.* *Hale watches the queues himself now, the bastard, he likes watching faces.* Each fragment landed on me like a type falling from a stick—one, then another, spelling out a sentence I did not want to read.

They needed me not just for the mark. They needed me for what the mark would travel inside. Documents authenticated by the true compass-lantern would pass from hand to hand across the city, through the customs house, across the bridge, down to the quay where the *Morning Tide* waited, and every hand they passed through would belong to someone in this cellar's orbit—this boy, this smuggler, this old woman with her tapping cane. If I cut the punch, I was not lending them a symbol. I was tying myself to all of them, knot by knot, the way a forme is tied into its bed with printer's twine. You can untie it. But the cord remembers.

"Hazel." Marta had come over. Up close, in the candlelight, I could see how old she truly was—older than the shop, older than my father's stories about her, a woman who had been setting type since before the Harbor Act and who had buried, I happened to know, a husband and a press in the same decade. "You're doing your father's face. The one he made when a customer asked for something foolish."

"He made that face?" I said. "I thought I invented it."

"He lent it to you. Along with the gravers and the eye trouble." She sat down on the crate beside me, slowly, joints cracking like small type falling. "I'm going to say a thing now, and you'll want to throw the tea at me. Your father knew."

The cellar noise—Tam's sorting, Odile's muttering, the drip of the tide somewhere below the floor—seemed to recede.

"Knew what," I said.

"That the bench was yours. From the time you were fourteen. He showed me a hinge plate you'd cut, a replacement for the press upstairs, and I asked him which foundry had done the work, and he laughed until he had to sit down. Then he said, 'If anyone asks, it came from Brakel & Sons, and if my daughter ever asks, it came from nowhere.'" Marta's eyes were steady and kind and terrible. "He wasn't hiding you to shame you, girl. He was hiding you the way you bank a fire. So it would last. So it would be there when the cold came." She nodded at the press, at the broadsheets, at the whole warm dangerous cellar. "The cold's come."

I looked down at my hands. Ink in the knuckle creases, graver callus on the right thumb, the fine permanent stain along the index finger where the steel rests. Eleven years of erasing myself from every ledger, every commission, every memory of every customer who had praised *Crane & Sons'* work to my face while I swept the floor. I had thought the hiding was my father's cage around me. It had never once occurred to me that it might be his arms.

"The mark," I said at last, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The true one. It's not a copy of Joss's. Joss started it, but I finished it after—" I stopped. Started again. "After. There are things in it he never saw. If I cut a new punch, it'll be mine, fully mine, and every document it touches will carry it. You're not asking to borrow a dead man's signature."

"No," Odile said from across the room. She had gone quiet sometime during Marta's speech, and now she came over and stood above us both, arms folded, bone buttons catching the light. "We're asking you to sign yours."

The candles flickered in a draft from the stairwell. Somewhere overhead, the chandler's shop closed for the night, bolts sliding home, the apprentice whistling off into the fog. Down here, the press waited with its platen raised like a held breath.

I thought of the ledger in the shop's back room, the one I balanced every month, in which every piece of my work appeared under a dead man's name. I thought of Elias Wren's careful census questions, and the pamphlet I had dropped, and the strange mercy of a stranger's silence. I thought of Tam's salvaged lowercase *e*, held up to the light.

"Terms," I said.

Odile's mouth twitched. "Go on."

"I cut the punch. It stays mine—I keep the punch, or it stays in my keeping, wherever that is. Nobody else cuts from it, nobody strikes copies, and when this is over, whatever 'over' means, the mark comes back to me." I stood up. My legs had gone stiff on the crate and I didn't care. "And I want it known, in this room, tonight, that the cutter's name is Hazel Crane. Not Crane's niece. Not Crane's sons. Hazel."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Tam, from the floor, held up his battered *e* again and said, solemn as a magistrate:

"Witnessed."

"Witnessed," Marta said.

Odile studied me for a long moment, and whatever she was weighing—my usefulness, my nerve, the odds I'd crack at the first checkpoint—she apparently found the sum acceptable. She spat in her palm and held it out, an old sailor's contract older than any paper.

"Witnessed," she said. "Welcome to the crew, Hazel Crane."

I took her hand. Her grip was rope and iron, and it did not let go quickly.

"Now," she said, releasing me and turning to the shelf where the gravers lay wrapped in oilcloth—good gravers, lozenge-section, better than mine, and I hated how much that pleased me—"the tide turns at four, and Ferro's forged mark goes on the decoy run at dawn whether we like it or not. Which means the true punch needs to exist by tomorrow night." She unwrapped the steel and laid the tools out on the composing stone with the reverence of a surgeon laying out instruments. "Can you do it?"

I picked up the smallest graver. Turned it. Tested its edge against my thumbnail the way my father taught me before I was tall enough to see over the bench.

The steel was honest. The steel was always honest. It was everything else in my life that had been set in counterfeit type.

"For a mark this size," I said, "in tool steel, cut clean and hardened proper? Give me a blank, a lamp, and nobody talking to me until dawn."

Tam immediately opened his mouth—to ask something, obviously, probably several things—and Marta's hand descended onto his head like a benevolent stamp.

"I'll fetch the blank," Odile said. "There's a broken die from the mint seizure in the strongbox. Best steel in Verrance, and it owes the Governor a grudge." She paused at the strongbox, key already in the lock, and looked back at me across the candlelight. "One more thing, Cutter. Whatever you're leaving upstairs—the name you answer to, the life you sweep around—understand that once your mark is on these documents, there's no striking it from the edition. Ink doesn't apologize."

"I know," I said. "I've spent eleven years learning exactly what ink won't do."

She smiled then, quick and wolfish, and unlocked the box.

And that was how, in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, with a boy sorting wounded letters at my feet and an old woman humming a tune older than the Harbor Act and a smuggler counting out stolen mint steel like a dowry, I sat down at last to cut my own name into the world—beginning, as all my names began, with a lantern, and a compass inside it, pointing west.
