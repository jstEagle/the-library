## 8. Cutting the Compass

The cellar press smelled of tallow and iron and the particular sweetness of lamp-black worked into wood over years—Marta called it the smell of truth being made, which Hazel had thought was sentiment until she'd spent three nights beneath the chandler's shop and learned that everything down there was made of something else: the press of old ship's timber, the type cast from melted candle-stubs, the paper pulped from sailcloth too rotten to fly. Nothing in Verrance was what it claimed to be. She fit there better than she wanted to admit.

But tonight the cellar was empty except for her, a single oil lamp turned low, and the graver.

She had asked Odile for one night alone with the steel. *A cutter doesn't work with an audience*, she'd said, and Odile had looked at her for a long moment with those harbor-gray eyes that seemed to weigh everything against its price, and then she'd shrugged and taken Tam up the stairs by the collar, saying something about fish markets and the hour before the boats came in. Marta had pressed Hazel's shoulder once on the way out—not a word, just the pressure of an old woman's hand, warm through the linen—and left her the lamp and a jug of chicory gone cold.

So it was only Hazel and the blank face of the punch, a bar of good steel no longer than her thumb, and the work she had promised them.

She set the punch in the vise and did not touch it yet. This was the part her father had taught her first, before letters, before knives: *look at the steel until you can see the letter already living inside it.* Every punch was a burial, he used to say. You put the shape into the metal and it stayed there longer than any man who cut it. Longer than the shop. Longer than the name on the door.

*Longer than the name on the door.*

Her father had been dead eleven months and she had not yet mourned him properly. There had been no time—the funeral was a half-day affair between press runs, because the widow Crane could not be seen letting the business slip even for grief, and Hazel had stood at the graveside in her brother's coat with her hands folded like a woman with nothing in them, while inside her hands were full of everything: the bench, the gravers, the forty-one punches her father had cut in his last year when his eyes were going soft at the edges and he'd begun, without ever saying it aloud, to hand the fine work to his daughter and sign it with his own name. She had buried him under a lie of convenience and called it filial duty, and then she had gone back to the shop and cut three commissions that week because grief does not pay the rent.

And Wren—her brother, her beautiful careless brilliant brother, who could pull a perfect page but couldn't cut a straight stem if you held his elbow—Wren had been dead four years, drowned off the breakwater in a squall with two other boys and a skiff none of them should have taken out, and Hazel had grieved him so loudly and so publicly that no one had ever thought to ask why the shop's finest work kept coming from the bench after he was gone. She had spent her mourning like coin, buying herself invisibility with every tear. It was the cruelest economy she knew. She had wept for the wrong reasons at the right grave, and the true grief—the small, private, unspeakable grief of a sister who had loved her brother and also, in some corner of her heart she still would not light a lamp in, resented him for the ease of his name—had never been given its hour.

Tonight it would have its hour. Tonight the steel would take it.

She picked up the graver.

* * *

The design had come to her whole, the way designs sometimes did, in the gap between sleeping and waking three nights ago in the cot Odile kept behind the paper stores. A lantern. Not the street-lanterns of Verrance with their smoked glass and their tax seals, but a working lantern, the kind sailors carried, with a loop handle and a door that opened. And inside the lantern, where the flame would be, a compass rose—eight points, the cardinal four long and sharp as needles, the intercardinal four short, all of it radiating from a center no wider than a pinhead.

Light that tells you where you are. Direction that burns. She had woken with her fingers already moving on the blanket, tracing stems.

Now she roughed the outline onto the steel face with a needle point, breathing slow, the way her father had taught her—*the breath is the third hand, Hazel; hold it wrong and the line goes wrong*—and made the first cut.

The sound filled the cellar. That was the thing no one who didn't cut could understand: the sound. The thin bright whisper of hardened steel parting steel, a note so high it was almost silence, almost the ringing after a bell. Her father's shop had been full of it all her childhood, that whisper underneath the clatter of the press and the murmur of customers, and she had thought for years it was just noise, the way children think rain is just weather. Then one evening when she was nine, standing on a crate to watch him work, she had understood that the whisper was the letter being born—that every sound she heard was a small permanent thing coming into the world—and she had been unable to speak for the rest of the evening, and her father had noticed, because he noticed everything about her hands and nothing about anything else, and he had put the smallest graver in his kit into her palm and said, *Well then. If it moves you, learn to make it.*

She made the first stem of the compass rose. North. Long, clean, tapering to nothing.

*You always start north,* her father said in her memory, in his workshop voice, the voice that was half instruction and half prayer. *North is the cut you make when your hand is freshest and your doubt is smallest. Everything after north is negotiation.*

She cut east. South. West. Her shoulders ached in the old familiar architecture, the hunch of the craft, and she let them ache. Around her the cellar held its breath. Somewhere overhead a cart rattled across the chandler's floorboards, and the jars of tallow on their shelves caught the lamplight and threw it back amber, and the shadows of the hanging type-cases swayed like a congregation.

East was for her father. She hadn't planned it that way, but the cuts arranged themselves into meanings the way water finds channels, and she let them. East, where the sun rose over the harbor he'd walked to every morning of his life with his collar up against the fog, whistling off-key, carrying his lunch wrapped in waxed cloth like a schoolboy though he was fifty-one. East, where the customs house stood whose clerks he'd charmed and cheated in equal measure, because a printer who paid full duties on imported steel was a printer who didn't eat. East, where he'd taken her once, aged twelve, to see the tall ships, and bought her a candied orange, and said nothing the whole walk home, and it had been the best day of her life precisely because of the saying-nothing, because with him silence was never absence, it was company.

The east point went in true. She wiped the swarf away with her thumb and felt the edge of it, sharp enough to draw blood from a careless finger, and thought: *there you are, Papa. There's your morning.*

* * *

South took longer.

South was Wren.

She sat back from the vise and drank the cold chicory and let herself look at the problem the way she'd look at a difficult counter—a letter with too many curves crowded into too little space, requiring either invention or surrender. Four years of surrender. Perhaps it was time for invention.

Because here was the thing she had never said to anyone, not to Marta, not to the dark, not to God if God was taking minutes: she had loved her brother and she had hated him a little, and both loves had been enormous, and neither had ever been allowed out of the shop. He was the son. Crane & *Sons*. Two words that had built the whole architecture of her life: the shop signed with a plural that was a lie even when he lived, since their father had never had another child and had needed the *Sons* the way some men need a beard, for respectability. When Wren died, the lie became load-bearing. The shop was Crane & Sons; the census rolls said Crane & Sons; the guild registry, the tax assessments, the commission ledgers—all of it said sons, plural, present tense. And so Hazel, who had been cutting since she was nine and masterful since she was sixteen, had stepped into her dead brother's name the way you step into a river, and the river had closed over her head, and she had breathed its water for four years and called it survival.

He would have laughed. That was the worst of it. Wren would have found it hilarious—his shy, serious, ferociously talented little sister signing his name on work ten times finer than anything he'd ever cut, and getting away with it, getting away with it for *years*. He'd have toasted her. He'd have told everyone. That was the difference between them: he spent himself loudly, she hoarded herself, and if he had lived she would have gone on hoarding forever, cutting masterpieces into the dark and letting his name drink the credit, and she would have called it love.

Maybe it was love. Maybe it was fear wearing love's coat. She was no longer sure there was a difference worth the ink.

She set the graver to the south point and cut it short. Deliberately short—the intercardinal length, not the cardinal. Let south be smaller than the others. Let the rose acknowledge that some directions are harder to face than others, and go on pointing anyway.

*There, Wren. There's your share. Small, and true, and mine to give.*

Her eyes were wet and she let them be, because tears and cutting oil were both just liquids and the steel didn't care, and because this—this was the mourning, she realized. Not the graveside. Not the black dress or the neighbors' casseroles or the priest's Latin. This. The work done slowly and honestly with the dead person's lessons in your hands. Her father had known it; he'd cut his finest punch, the double-struck ampersand the guild still talked about, six weeks after their mother died, and when Hazel as a girl had asked him how he could work at such a time, he'd looked at her with eyes red-rimmed and steady and said, *Where else would I put her?*

Where else would she put them? Here. In steel. In a mark that would outlast the Governor and the Governor's decrees and probably the Governor's empire. Grief, hammered into permanence. Mourning as manufacture.

She cut the four remaining points, short and quick now, her hand warmed into certainty, and then the circle that bound them, and then she began the lantern itself.

* * *

The lantern was the hard part and she knew it going in, and she was glad of it, because the hard part was where the defiance lived.

Anyone could cut a compass. Compasses were everywhere—on charts, on coins, on the tailpieces of almanacs. Anyone could cut a lantern; the Lantern Uprising had not yet risen, but lanterns hung on every corner of Verrance already, taxed and numbered and dull. But a compass *inside* a lantern—direction held in a vessel of light, carried, portable, private—that was a sentence, and sentences were dangerous, and cutting a dangerous sentence into steel small enough to hide in a palm was the closest thing to shouting that Hazel Crane had ever done.

She cut the handle first, a single sweeping curve that had to be perfect or the whole mark would look like a gallows, and she took three passes at it and scraped two of them out, and on the third she stopped trying to make it elegant and let it be strong instead, thick at the base where a real handle would bear weight, and it came right. Then the body of the lantern, the roof with its little chimney cap, the base with its drip-pan, and then the door—the open door, swung wide, because a closed lantern was a hidden light and she was finished, as of tonight, with hidden lights.

The open door was the smallest cut on the punch and the one that mattered most. Two lines, each shorter than a grain of barley. She held her breath through both.

When it was done she sat back, and her neck cracked when she rolled it, and the lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil, and she had no idea how many hours had passed. The punch lay in the vise with its face still rough, unpolished, the cuts holding shadow—but the shape was in it. Living in it. She could see it the way her father had taught her to see: not the steel but the impression it would leave, ten thousand impressions, a thousand broadsheets, a hundred thousand pages, each one stamped with a compass burning inside a lantern with its door thrown open, and on every single one of them, invisible, uncredited, undeniable, her hand.

*Longer than the name on the door,* her father had said. Well. The name on the door was ash waiting to happen—she'd known it since the night Elias Wren had stood in the shop doorway with her dropped pamphlet in his glove and she'd seen her own future in his careful, troubled face. Names burned. Steel kept.

She polished the punch face with progressively finer stones, then the crocus powder, then leather charged with rouge, working by feel more than sight in the guttering lamplight, until the cuts shone black and clean and the steel around them gleamed like harbor water at dawn. Then she struck a proof: a scrap of the cellar's rag paper, the punch tapped twice with the mallet, soft, the way you greet rather than command.

She lifted the paper to the lamp.

The compass-lantern looked back at her, crisp and entire, eight points and an open door, and beneath its ghost she saw the other ghosts it was made of: her father's east, her brother's south, her own stubborn north, the west she hadn't assigned to anyone because west was where the ships went and west, she suspected, was where hers was bound.

Hazel Crane, who had erased herself from every ledger in Verrance, sat in a smuggler's cellar at three in the morning and wept over a piece of steel, properly, thoroughly, for the first time in four years—for her father, for her brother, and a little, at the very end, for the girl she had been before she started disappearing, the girl on the crate watching the graver whisper, who had wanted nothing in the world except to be allowed to make the sound.

When the crying was done she washed her face in the rain barrel by the stairs, struck a second proof, and hid the first inside her bodice, against her skin, where the resistance's secrets apparently lived now along with everything else.

Then she heard the footsteps on the stairs above—heavy, unhurried, more than one pair, and the particular deliberate tread of men who had a warrant and wanted you to hear them coming—and the lamp went out under Marta's quick hand as the old woman materialized out of the dark of the paper aisle, gripping her wrist, whispering one word:

*Hale.*
