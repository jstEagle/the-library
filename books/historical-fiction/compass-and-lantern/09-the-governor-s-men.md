## 9. The Governor's Men

The Census Office of Verrance occupied the second floor of the Harbor Customs Building, and its windows looked out on exactly the thing Elias Wren was paid to pretend did not exist: the whole breathing city, stacked and jumbled and alive, every rooftop a household, every household a number in his ledgers. On clear mornings he could see all the way to the terraced gardens above Saltrow, where the poor hung their washing like signal flags of surrender. He had counted those rooftops himself, four years running. Eleven thousand two hundred and six dwellings. Somewhere under one of them, right now, a woman was sharpening a graver against an oilstone, and he was about to be asked to find her.

He had not slept. The previous night he had sat at his own table with the pamphlet's memory spread out before him like a corpse on a slab—the compass rose nested in the lantern, cut with a confidence no dead apprentice should have possessed—and he had tried, method by method, to construct the arithmetic by which his duty and his conscience might both be satisfied. He had failed. Subtraction kept eating the wrong number. Every calculation ended with Hazel Crane's hands in irons, or Elias Wren's own name entered into a ledger of another kind entirely.

Now he stood before Inspector Hale's desk with his hat in his hands like a schoolboy, and the morning fog had come inside with him and settled somewhere behind his ribs.

Hale did not look up immediately. That was a technique; Elias knew it was a technique because he had watched Hale use it on clerks, on witnesses, on the wives of men awaiting transport. The Inspector let you stand in the silence until your small guilt began to speak on your behalf. His office smelled of lamp oil and cold tea, and behind him the wall was papered—not with wallpaper but with broadsheets, warrants, execution notices, forty years of Verrance's printed disobedience arranged like trophies. Elias had always found the wall obscene. A man should not decorate himself with other men's deaths.

"Wren." Hale set down his pen with exaggerated care, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge. "Sit."

Elias sat.

"Do you know what this is?" Hale held up a sheet of cheap paper. Even upside down, even across the desk, Elias recognized the impression. The compass in the lantern. His stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution, like a ship taking water.

"It appears to be a pamphlet," he said. His voice came out level. Four years of counting households had taught him something after all: how to make a number sound like nothing.

"It appears," Hale agreed, "to be seditious libel. Printed on a hand press, stock from the Millhaven rag works, ink cut with tallow—which tells us the press is small, underfunded, and close to the harbor." He turned the sheet around and slid it across. "And this mark, Wren. Tell me what you see."

Elias looked. He made himself look the way a census man looks—at categories, not people. "A device. A maker's mark. Unlicensed, or I'd have it registered."

"Unregistered, then. Cut by hand. Do you know how many men in this city can cut a punch like that?" Hale leaned back. "I'll save you the arithmetic. Seven. Two are dead, three are accounted for, one is ninety years old and hasn't held a graver since the last King's birthday. Which leaves—" he opened his hands, empty, patient, "—one. Or someone the trade doesn't know about. Someone who never signed the guild rolls. Someone invisible."

The word landed in the room like a dropped type sort, small and hard and precisely aimed.

"The Governor," Hale went on, "is of the opinion that this pamphlet is connected to the land decree disputes in the western parishes. He is of the further opinion that whoever cut this mark has connections to the smuggling houses along Rope Walk. He has instructed me"—and here Hale's voice took on the flat ceremonial quality of a man reading terms of surrender—"to identify and apprehend the cutter of this device before the end of the week. That is five days, Wren. Five days, and I am to deliver to the Governor's secretary a name."

"A name," Elias repeated.

"A name and a body. They go together in this business." Hale smiled, and it was worse than not smiling. "You're wondering why this lands on your desk. Let me relieve you of the suspense. You count every hearth in this city. You know who lives where, who's died, who's been born, who's lying on their census forms. There is no man in Verrance better positioned to find a person who does not wish to be found. It is, if you think about it, the very same skill. I count sedition. You count souls. We both work by enumeration."

*We both work by enumeration.* Elias felt the sentence go into him like a splinter and lodge. Because it was true, and truth spoken by the wrong mouth still cut.

"I'll require access to the parish registers," he said, buying time the only way he knew, in procedure. "The guild rolls. Shipping manifests for—"

"You'll require nothing." Hale picked up his pen again, dismissed him already, and that was when Elias understood that the trap had closed some minutes ago and he had simply not yet felt the teeth. "Because you've already done the work. Four years of it. Five days, Wren. I'd have started tonight, if I were you."

Elias rose. At the door, Hale spoke again without looking up.

"One more thing. The Crane woman's niece. You counted her household in the spring." The pen scratched. "Odd household, that. A widow, an old compositor, a grown nephew nobody in the parish remembers ever seeing. You noted the discrepancy yourself, I believe. In your own hand."

The fog behind Elias's ribs turned to ice.

"I note everything, Inspector. It's my trade."

"Mm," said Hale. "Mine too."

* * *

He walked home the long way, through the fish market, because the long way passed the print shop.

It was raining again—that fine, persistent drizzle that Verrance wore half the year like a mourning veil—and the streets were the color of old pewter. Gulls argued over the gutting tables. A boy ran past him with a basket of eels, shouting the tide times as though they were news, and for one absurd moment Elias thought of Tam, of the resistance's runners, of the whole hidden circulation system of the city, blood moving through vessels he had spent four years mapping without once admitting what the body was for.

The print shop's windows were dark. Of course they were dark; it was midmorning and no one had been arrested in the night—he would have known, wouldn't he, he told himself, he would certainly have known—but the dark windows said nothing either way. Dark windows were the most honest architecture in the world. They told you only that there were things inside you could not see.

He stood across the street in the rain like a fool, hat dripping, and let himself think it plainly for the first time: *she will hang.* Not *she may be caught*, not *the risk is real*. She will hang, on a scaffold outside the customs house, and the notice announcing it will be printed by one of the seven remaining cutters, and her mark will be struck from every press in the city, and eleven thousand two hundred and six dwellings will go on counting their hearths and none of them will ever know that the finest punch-cutter in Verrance died for a lantern and a compass rose.

Unless.

Unless the week produced a different answer. Unless the enumeration went differently this once. Unless a man who had spent his whole career making people into numbers decided, at last, to unmake one.

He knew what that cost. He had paid it before.

* * *

That night, in his rooms above the chandler's on Bell Lane, Elias took down the box from the top of his wardrobe.

It was a census box, government issue, tin, with a lock whose key he kept on his ring like everyone else in the office. But this box held no census. It held the year 631, and a man named Aldous Ferrin, printer, of Wheelwright Street, and a file two fingers thick with Elias Wren's neat handwriting on every page: places frequented, associates observed, publications traced, testimony given. Testimony given, and a verdict returned, and on a gray Tuesday eleven years ago a rope and a cart and a crowd that mostly came for the pickpockets, and Aldous Ferrin's last pamphlet—a defense of harbor wages, four pages, badly printed—selling on the black market within a month for more than its weight in silver, because the city knew a martyr's mark when it saw one.

Ferin had been guilty of printing. He had not been guilty of anything worth dying for. Elias had known it at the time in the way a man knows weather coming—in his joints, ahead of the evidence—and he had testified anyway, because the file was thorough, because thoroughness was his virtue, because a young man with a steady hand and a talent for being noticed by important men does not refuse to notice what he has been asked to notice.

Afterward he had requested the census posting. Everyone thought it was ambition. Enumeration of the entire city: it was the largest ledger a clerk of his grade could hope to touch. Only Elias knew it was penance with a bureaucratic title—that he had taken the whole city into his keeping so that never again would one name slip through his fingers without his knowing it, without his feeling the exact weight of it. Every household he counted was a promise. *This one I see. This one exists. I will not lose you.*

And now the skill itself had been requisitioned. Penance turned instrument. Hale wanted the census to do what it had always secretly been built to do—not to see the city, but to hunt through it.

Elias sat with the tin box open on his knees for a long time. Rain worked at the window. Downstairs, the chandler's dog turned twice on its mat and sighed.

Then he did the only thing his nature permitted: he began to plan, in his careful clerical hand, on the backs of old tide tables, an alternate enumeration. If the cutter were to be found by Friday, the finding would need witnesses, dates, a documented chain. If the finding were to point anywhere but at a certain shop on Candlemaker's Row, the misdirection would need to be flawless, because Hale was not a fool—Hale was the opposite of a fool, Hale was a fool's nightmare, a patient man with resources and a memory. Which meant Elias needed something better than a lie. He needed a true thing arranged in a false order. He needed, God help him, to falsify a census.

There were seven cutters in Verrance, Hale had said. Two dead, three accounted for, one ancient, and one unknown.

But Hale had said *men*. And Hale had said the guild rolls, and the guild rolls recorded apprenticeships, and apprenticeships recorded masters, and masters recorded commissions, and somewhere in the commission books of the last fifteen years there was, Elias now remembered with a sensation like stepping off a stair that wasn't there, an entry for a funeral monument of all things—a memorial typeface, commissioned by a grieving father, cut for a headstone rubbishing trade by a master whose signature Elias had once copied out as practice because it was so beautiful. A master named Crane. A son named Crane. A commission signed—

Signed by whom? Signed by the person who actually held the graver? Or signed by the person whose name was allowed to hold it?

He closed the tin box. He put it back on the wardrobe. Then he took it down again, wrapped it in oilcloth, and hid it in the false bottom of his rain barrel, because a man who had decided to commit treason ought at least to hide his evidence properly, and because the barrel was where the city's secrets apparently lived now, along with everything else.

Five days. He blew out his lamp and lay in the dark listening to the rain, doing what he always did when he could not sleep: counting. Not rooftops tonight. Days. Hours, after a while. The noose does not tighten all at once; it tightens by increments, each one survivable, each one measured, and a man can stand in the middle of the rope and tell himself he is only standing until suddenly he cannot.

Somewhere across the sleeping city, under a dark window on Candlemaker's Row, she was asleep too, if he knew anything about exhausted printers, with ink still in the creases of her knuckles and a secret proof pressed against her skin like a second heartbeat. By Friday, Hale would have a name. By Friday, someone would hang for the compass and the lantern.

Elias Wren stared at his ceiling and made the census keeper's oldest vow, the vow that lived underneath every column and every cross-check, the vow that had brought him to the trade and betrayed him in it and now, at last, demanded payment:

*I know who you are. All of you. Every soul in this city, I know where you sleep.*

*And this week, God forgive me, so does Hale.*

At dawn the drums began in Customs Square—the Governor's men mustering—and beneath them, faint and rhythmic and utterly indifferent, the turning of the tide.
