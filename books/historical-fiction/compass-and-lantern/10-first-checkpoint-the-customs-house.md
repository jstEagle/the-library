## 10. First Checkpoint: the Customs House

The coffin arrived at Crane & Sons two hours before dawn, carried by men who did not speak and did not look at one another, and it was the strangest thing I had ever helped to dress.

It was a real coffin—elm, well-joined, smelling of fresh sawdust and the tallow the chandler used to seal his crates—but what went into it was not a body. Odile's crew packed it in the cellar of the chandler's shop by lamplight while I supervised, because no one else understood what they were handling. The copper plates, each one wrapped in oilcloth and then in felt, and then in a second skin of waxed canvas, went into trays that Marta had built from old composing frames, lined with wool. Around them, to give the weight its honest excuse, we poured loose lead type. Sacks of it. Sorts and sorts of it—the worn-out letters of decades, the e's and t's and a's my father had bought by the hundredweight because they died first, every page eating them like bread.

"Lead is heavy," Tam had said, watching us pour the last sack, his eyes enormous in the lamplight. "Dead heavy."

"That's rather the point," Odile said dryly, and latched the lid.

So we made a coffin of lead type, and by seven o'clock that morning it sat on a handcart outside the customs house on Cordwainer Quay, and I stood beside it dressed as a woman who had never cut anything sharper than bread.

* * *

Invisibility, I have learned, is not the absence of being seen. Anyone can be unseen—a beggar is unseen, a gull is unseen. Invisibility is a craft, like cutting a punch, and it has rules.

The first rule: never hide. Hiding is a flag. Walk through the door as if you own a key to it, as if you have walked through it four hundred times, bored, thinking of your dinner. The second rule: be smaller than the eye wants to travel. Not stooped—that draws attention—but *ordinary*, ordinary in every particular, a woman whose face a witness could not describe an hour later if you paid him. The third rule, the hardest: keep your hands quiet. Hands confess everything. My hands wanted to shake.

I had chosen my costume with a cutter's precision. A widow's grey, ten years out of fashion, mended at the cuffs where a workingwoman would mend it. A shawl that smelled faintly of tallow, because I had slept with it folded in the chandler's storeroom for two nights so it would tell the truth about me. Spectacles with plain glass, which fogged at the edges when I breathed too fast, and which I had learned, in front of Marta's cracked mirror, to push up my nose with exactly the fidgeting impatience of a woman who found them tiresome. My hair pinned under a cap. No rings. Ink under the nails—real ink, ground in that morning, because a customs officer might not notice ink but a printer would, and there were printers on the other side of those doors too.

"You'll do," Marta had said, looking me over in the mirror, and then she had done something she almost never did. She took my face in her two rough hands and kissed my forehead. "Walk like grief, girl. Grief is the best disguise in Verrance. Nobody stops a grieving woman; they're afraid she'll cry on them."

I was not pretending the grief. That part was easy. My father had been carried through those same customs house doors twice a year for thirty years, carting type he'd ordered from the capital, and every time he'd paid the tariff and complained about it and come home to complain about it again over supper. He should have been the one wheeling this coffin. He should have been alive to do it. And Aldous—my brother, my shadow-self, the name I had been living inside like a coat that never fit—

Stop. Rule of rules: keep your hands quiet. Keep your thoughts quieter.

I took the cart's handles and joined the queue.

* * *

The customs house on Cordwainer Quay had been built by the same generation that built the breakwater, back when Verrance believed in its own future, and it wore its granite like a boast. Inside, the hall rose three storeys to a ceiling of ribbed iron, and the light came down in grey shafts through clerestory windows furred with salt-spray, so that everything below—the queues, the desks, the officers in their bottle-green coats—moved through a kind of permanent overcast. The sound was the sound of the empire doing arithmetic: stamps, ledgers, the scratch of nibs, the flat voices reading manifests aloud. Underneath it all, the sea, hitting the quay foundations with a patience that felt personal.

There were three queues. One for fish and salt, one for dry goods, one for *miscellaneous*—and miscellaneous was mine, because a coffin of type was nothing if not miscellaneous. The miscellany line moved slowly. Ahead of me stood a man importing a church organ in eleven crates; behind me, a woman with forty live chickens that kept up a low, reproachful muttering, like the queue itself made flesh.

And at the head of the hall, on a raised dais that had held the tariff schedules for sixty years and now held something else, stood Inspector Hale.

He had not been there yesterday. Tam's runners had confirmed it before dawn: Hale had taken personal command of the customs house for the day, standing where everyone entering must pass beneath his gaze, watching the queues the way a heron watches water—not hunting, not moving, simply attending, with that terrible stillness some men are born to. He was a lean man, silver at the temples, in a green coat better cut than regulation allowed, and he did not read papers at his post the way other inspectors did. He read faces.

I looked at him once—once only, the length of a breath, the way you check a blade's edge—and then I gave him to the category of furniture. Wall. Ceiling. Weather. Rule one: never hide. Rule two: be ordinary. I became a tired woman with sore shoulders calculating whether the tariff on lead would ruin her month.

The coffin rode the cart behind me. I did not look back at it. Looking back at cargo is the oldest mistake in smuggling; even Tam knew that, and Tam was twelve.

The church organ ahead of me took twenty minutes. An officer with ink-stained cuffs made him open three of the eleven crates, ran his fingers along pipes that gleamed like saints' bones, and stamped everything with the weary satisfaction of a man who had found nothing and known he would find nothing. The chickens behind me expressed opinions. Somewhere high in the iron ribs of the roof, a pigeon shifted and sent down a fine rain of dust that caught the grey light.

Then it was my turn.

* * *

The officer at the miscellany desk was young—young enough that his coat still fit him, which meant he'd been issued it recently—and he had a cold, and he wanted, more than anything in the world, to be somewhere warm. I registered all of this in the first second, the way I register the grain of boxwood before setting a graver: this is the material I must cut.

"Manifest," he said, sniffing.

I handed it up. It was beautiful work; Elias had written it himself, and I had not appreciated until that morning how dangerous a man can be who understands paper. Death certificate, properly filed, for one *Crane, A., printer*. Shipping manifest for the return of effects to family in the northern parishes: *one case printing type, lead, approx. nine hundred weight, sold or returned at family's discretion.* Harbor duty paid receipt, genuine, bought legitimately two days ago through a broker who owed Marta a favor. Every stamp real. Every signature real. The lie lived only in the space between the documents, the way a forgery lives in nothing you can point to.

"Coffin," the young officer said.

"My brother's." I let my voice go flat and slightly hoarse, the voice of a woman who has said the word too many times this week. "He was a printer. Died in March." Which was true. Everything true except the man himself. "The type's worth more than the passage home for it, but it was his. You understand."

He did not understand; he was nineteen. But he performed understanding, which amounted to the same thing, and pulled the manifest closer to check the weights.

"Nine hundred weight of lead," he said. "You'll want to open it."

"Officer—" and here I deployed the thing Marta had drilled into me over two nights of rehearsal until I hated it and loved it, the crack that comes just before tears but never quite arrives, "—it's sealed per regulation. Section twelve. Sealed coffins of dead stock aren't opened without a supervisor's counter-sign, and Supervisor Brell won't be in till noon, and the tide—" I stopped. Swallowed. Let my hands, quiet hands, tighten once on the strap of my shawl and go still again.

Rule four, which Marta taught me and which I had resisted believing: know the regulations better than they do. Bureaucracy is a lock, and locks are made to be opened by the right key, and the right key is always made of paper.

The young officer frowned at the manifest. He sniffed. He looked past me at the coffin—at the plain elm, the tarred seams, the stencil of the Crane & Sons mark that my father had cut himself twenty years ago, a small honest mark, a maker's mark, sitting in plain sight like the best lies—and I watched him weigh the mathematics of his morning: a supervisor's signature versus a queue of chickens and a wet quay and a warm stove somewhere off the hall.

"It's really nine hundred weight?" he asked. "Heavy?"

"Heavier than my heart, and that's saying something," I said, and heard myself say it, and thought: *who taught you that sentence? You did. Ten minutes ago. You are a person who invents sentences like that now.*

He almost smiled. Then he stamped the manifest—once, twice, the second stamp harder, as if to settle the matter with himself—and waved me through, and called, "Next," past me to the chickens.

I wheeled the coffin through the arch, under the great carved arms of the harbor commission, out into the raw wind of the quay side, and I did not breathe properly until the customs house wall was a full street behind me and the Morning Tide's masts stood up over the rooflines ahead like three needles waiting for thread.

That was when I saw the boy.

Tam came out of the fish-market crowd the way he always did, sideways, as if the crowd had exhaled him, and fell into step beside the cart without seeming to walk at all. He didn't look at me. He looked at the customs house doors we'd just left.

"He's come out," Tam said, very low. "Hale. He's coming down the steps. He's looking at the carts."

* * *

Later—I told myself later I would take this apart and examine how it had happened, the way I examine a failed punch to learn where the steel gave—but there was no later just then. There was only the present tense, which had narrowed to the width of the quay.

I did not run. Running is a flag. I kept the cart rolling at the pace of a tired woman with a heavy load, and I listened, and behind us I heard the footsteps: unhurried, hard-heeled on stone, and with them a voice, Hale's voice, dry as a filing system, saying to someone—his clerk? a harbor watchman?—"the miscellany queue. Start with anything heavier than it looks."

Anything heavier than it looks. Nine hundred weight of lead is exactly as heavy as it looks; that was the whole architecture of the plan. But a coffin, sanded smooth, is also nine hundred weight of something else entirely, and Hale was a man who had spent his life noticing that two things can share one shape.

"Ma'am." His voice reached me before he did. "A moment."

I turned the cart. Stopped. Faced him.

Up close, Hale was worse than the dais had made him, because up close his attention had mass, like weather. His eyes went to the coffin first—he was good, he was very good, he looked at the cargo before the carrier, because carriers perform and cargo only sits—and then, after a full three seconds of the coffin, they came up to my face. Plain glass spectacles between us. Grey shawl. Tallow smell. Ink under the nails, thank God, thank God for Marta and her insistence on truth in the details.

"This came through the house just now?" he asked.

"Ten minutes gone," I said. "Stamped." I produced the manifest from my basket with hands that were quiet because I had ordered them to be quiet on pain of hanging, and held it up, and did not extend it, because a grieving woman does not hand things briskly; she holds them and waits to be helped.

He did not take it. He read it upside-down across the space between us, which told me he had done this ten thousand times, and then he said, "Crane. Printer." His eyes came back to mine and stayed there with the mild, bottomless patience of a man fishing in deep water. "There was a print shop by that name on Tanner's Row."

"My father's shop," I said. "Mine now." And the sentence was true and false at once, braided so tightly I could not have found the seam with a graver, and something in me flared—briefly, horribly—with pride. *Listen to yourself. Listen to what you can do.*

"And the brother on the certificate. Aldous Crane."

"Died in March." Flat. Hoarse. Twice-said.

"Mm." Hale looked at the coffin again. He stepped around it—one slow circuit, half of it only, the half a courteous man permits himself—and laid two fingers on the lid, on the tarred seam, and I stood in the wind and watched a man decide whether to open my brother's coffin, and inside my brother's coffin lay everything: the plates, the decrees, the forged seal-work, the compass-and-lantern in copper that would hang me and Marta and Odile and Tam and a census taker who had chosen treason with a steady hand. All of it sleeping under nine hundred weight of my father's worn-out letters, under a thousand little lead a's and e's that had printed christenings and bills of sale and, once, I remembered suddenly, a broadsheet mourning the hanging of a printer whose name I will not put in this sentence.

Hale took his fingers off the lid.

"The seal's per regulation," he said, half to himself, half to his clerk, who had drifted up trailing the smell of the queue. "Section twelve. Brell countersigns if anyone wants it opened before noon." He paused, and the pause was the longest object on that quay, longer than the breakwater, longer than the Morning Tide lying at anchor with her hold empty and waiting. "Where's the shipment going?"

"Northern parishes. Family plot," I said. "The type goes to a buyer in Larchmont, if anyone will take it. Lead's lead."

"Lead's lead," Hale agreed softly.

And then he did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, which was nothing. He straightened his coat, inclined his head with a courtesy so precise it was almost an insult, and said, "My condolences on your loss, madam. Good morning"—and walked past me, down the quay, toward the fish market, unhurried, hard-heeled, his head already turning toward the next cart, the next queue, the next shape that might be two shapes.

I stood there until he was ten steps gone. Twenty. Then I took the cart's handles, and I rolled on toward the Tide's berth with my heart going like a drum in a procession, and Tam materialized at my elbow, white-faced under his freckles.

"He touched it," Tam whispered. "He touched the lid."

"He touched the lid," I agreed.

"Why'd he stop?"

Ahead of us, the harbor light swung against the grey sky; the gulls wheeled and screamed over the breakwater; the tide, turning, muttered under the quay stones like a crowd deciding something. And I realized I knew the answer, and the answer was worse than any alternative, and it went into me like cold water.

Because Hale had seen the stencil. Because he had seen the Crane & Sons mark, my father's honest mark, and he had not been satisfied—and a man like Hale files his dissatisfactions; he does not spend them. He had let us through because the paperwork was perfect and the regulations bound him and Brell wasn't due till noon. But I had seen his face when his fingers rested on that tarred seam, and it had not been the face of a man concluding. It had been the face of a man beginning.

"He stopped," I said to Tam, "because he intends to come back."

And the Morning Tide lifted gently at her moorings ahead of us, ready, patient, hers to sail the moment we reached her—but between us and her gangway lay a quarter-mile of open quay, and at the harbor gate beyond it, the second checkpoint, and I had just learned the only lesson the customs house had to teach:

Invisibility is armor, and armor wears thin, and somewhere behind us, unhurried, hard-heeled, a man was writing *Crane* in a small notebook, and underlining it.
