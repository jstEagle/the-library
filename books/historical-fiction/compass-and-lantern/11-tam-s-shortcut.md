## 11. Tam's Shortcut

The fish market woke before the sun did, and so did Tam.

He'd been running messages since he was nine, which meant four years of learning the city the way a rat learns a ship: by feel, in the dark, along routes no grown person would ever think to take. Verrance at dawn smelled of brine and tar and the sweet rot of yesterday's catch, and Tam loved it, though he'd have bitten his own tongue off before admitting it. Love was a thing you kept quiet. Like everything else worth having in this city.

Hazel Crane walked behind him, and she was doing it wrong.

"You're walking like a lady," he said over his shoulder, not slowing. "Ladies get remembered."

"I'm walking like a woman who doesn't want to be here," she said.

"That's worse." He ducked under a line strung with drying herring, their silver bodies catching the first grey light like coins nailed to a board. "Remembered is bad. Noticed is bad. You want to be furniture, missus. Furniture don't get stopped at checkpoints."

"I'm not a missus."

"Everyone's a missus to a runner. It's cheaper than remembering names." He said it lightly, but he watched her face when he said it, because that was another thing four years of running taught you: watch faces. Faces told you what mouths wouldn't. Hers did something complicated — a little flinch, like he'd stepped on a bruise she'd forgotten she had. He filed it away. Tam filed everything away. His head was a warehouse with no windows and very good locks.

The rope walks came next, long open corridors where the ropewalkers paced backward all day, paying out cordage thicker than a man's arm. In the early hour they were empty except for the smell — hemp and pitch, sharp enough to taste — and the great spools standing silent as sleeping cattle. Tam slipped between two of them and held the canvas flap aside.

"Through here. Mind the tar. It don't come out."

She minded the tar. She was careful about everything, this printer woman. Careful the way Marta was careful, hands always half-curled like they were holding something invisible and precious. Tam decided he liked her, in the way he liked things he hadn't yet been paid to like, which was to say provisionally, and with reservations.

"Odile says you cut the mark," he said, because silence made him itch and because watching her flinch twice in one morning seemed like good business.

"Odile talks too much."

"Odile pays on time. That buys a lot of talking." He squeezed through a gap between a stack of cable coils and the wall, then reached back to guide her through — not because she needed it, but because it was faster than waiting for her to find it herself. Her hand was cool and ink-stained. Ink in the creases, deep in, the kind that never washed out. A lifer's hands. "Marta says you cut it better than your father ever did."

That stopped her. Just for a step, just a hitch in her stride, but Tam caught it the way he caught everything.

"Marta says a lot of things," Hazel said finally.

"She does. That's the problem." He grinned back at her, quick and crooked. "You want to know a secret? The whole cellar's held together by people saying too much. Odile says too much about money. Marta says too much about burning things down. Ferro doesn't say enough, which is its own kind of saying-too-much, if you ask me. Nobody asked me. Nobody ever asks me. But I hear everything, because I'm small and fast and everyone assumes a boy carrying a parcel has no ears."

"Do you repeat what you hear?"

"To who?" He laughed. "I'm the only one who knows everything. That's my job. Knowing everything and telling nobody. It's a terrible job. The pay's awful."

"What *is* the pay?"

"Bread, mostly. Sometimes cheese, if the news is good." He said it cheerfully, and it was even mostly true, and if there was an ache underneath it he'd built walls around that ache years ago and posted sentries and they were very well trained.

* * *

The fish market opened onto the harbor road, and the harbor road was where the trouble lived, so Tam took her off it before the light got strong enough for faces to matter. Down through the gutting sheds, where the floor ran slick with scales and blood-water and the whole world stank of the sea's insides. Women worked the tables with knives that moved too fast to follow, and they looked up as Tam passed and nodded, because a runner who'd carried their letters home from sons on distant ships for four years earned nods everywhere.

"This way feels wrong," Hazel murmured.

"It feels wrong because it smells wrong. Wrong-smelling is safe. Nobody checks the places that make them gag." He hopped a puddle that was less water than opinion. "Checkpoints are for roads. Roads are for people with something to lose. We've got nothing to lose, us gutter-folk, so we go where the blood drains."

"That's grim."

"That's Verrance." He glanced up at her. She was taller than most women he ran for, and she kept looking at the shed roofs like she was reading them, which was odd, but printers were odd, everyone knew that. "You nervous?"

"No."

"You're lying."

"Yes," she admitted, and something in her voice surprised him — dry, almost amused. Most grown-ups got angry when he caught them lying. She just agreed with him, like lying was weather they were both standing in.

"Good. Nervous means smart. The ones who aren't nervous are the ones who get taken." He threaded between two sheds into a passage barely wider than his shoulders, walled with salt-crusted planking. "Squeeze through here. Don't touch the left side, there's nails."

She followed. Of course she followed. But halfway through the passage she spoke, low, in the dark:

"Tam. What do you know about Odile's price?"

Ah. There it was. He'd wondered how long it would take. Marta wanted the mark cut; Odile wanted collateral; and Tam sat in the middle with both their words in his pockets like stones.

"I know she wants the punch," he said. "The original. The one you cut."

"And what do you know about why?"

He considered lying to her. Then he considered that she had ink in her skin and fear in her face and had still come out here before dawn to walk his filthy shortcuts, and he decided against it.

"I know Odile had family once. Before Verrance. Somewhere across the water." He kept moving as he talked, voice pitched under the slap of water against pilings. "I know she doesn't talk about them and I know she keeps a letter in her coat pocket that's so old the paper's gone soft as cloth. I know she reads it sometimes when she thinks nobody's looking, and I know she looks like someone reading a letter from the dead." He paused at the passage's mouth, checking the lane beyond — clear, just gulls and a drunk asleep in a doorway. "I'm not saying that makes taking your punch right. I'm saying nothing Odile does is about money, even when it's about money. Money's just the shape her scaredness takes."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment behind him.

"How old are you?" she asked.

"Thirteen. Nearly fourteen."

"You talk like forty."

"I listen like eighty. It evens out." He stepped into the lane and beckoned. "Come on. We've got the bridge queue to beat and I want breakfast after, and if you're nice I'll show you the drain under the chandlery that saves ten minutes and costs you only your dignity."

* * *

They ate standing up, at a stall near the rope walks that sold fried dough and strong tea in cups you suspected had never been fully clean. Tam ate like a seagull, fast and acquisitive, one eye always on the street. Hazel ate slowly, watching him.

"You didn't have to tell me about Odile's letter," she said.

"You asked."

"I asked what you knew. Not what you felt."

"Same thing, with me. Everything I know, I felt first. That's how it gets in." He tore his last piece of dough in half and handed her the bigger piece without thinking about it, and then thought about it, and was annoyed at himself. "Anyway. You looked like you needed telling. People carry things better when they know other people are carrying things too. That's just physics."

"That's not physics."

"It's gutter physics. Different rules." He drank his tea and studied her sideways. Here was the thing he'd noticed since the cellar, the thing he hadn't said to anyone because saying it might make it not true: Hazel Crane stood like a person standing at the edge of a room she'd been invited into but didn't believe the invitation was real. Waiting for someone to take it back. Tam knew that stance. He'd worn it for years after his mother went into the fever wards and never came out. He knew every seam of it.

"You've got brothers or sisters?" he asked, casual as anything.

"No."

"Me neither. Not anymore." He let that sit exactly as long as it needed to, then hopped off the crate. "Right. Bridge next. Stay close, do what I do, and if anyone asks, you're my aunt and you're simple and I'm taking you home. Being simple explains any face you make. It's the best cover in Verrance. Half the city uses it and half the city isn't pretending."

Something happened to her expression then — something quick and complicated, gone before he could file it properly. Almost like being called *aunt* had landed somewhere tender.

"Tam," she said. "If this goes wrong today—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, you run. You don't wait for me. You don't come back for me. You run to Marta and you tell her the route's burned. Promise me."

He looked up at her, this stranger with dead men's secrets in her hands, and found — to his considerable irritation — that he didn't want to promise. Which was stupid. Runners promised nothing to nobody; that was rule one, carved somewhere inside him next to his mother's name. Runners were wind, not rope. Wind didn't tie itself to things.

"Promise," she said again.

"Fine. I promise." He scowled at his tea. "But I'll be bad at keeping it. Fair warning."

"That's the most honest answer I've had all week."

They dumped the cups and went. And as they came out onto the harbor road proper, into the noise and the press of carts and the far-off rattle of the chain bridge being lowered for the morning, Tam did the thing he never did, the thing that broke rule one clean in half: he reached up without thinking and took hold of the corner of her sleeve, just two fingers, just to keep her in the crowd. The way you'd hold a sister's sleeve. The way someone had once held his.

She looked down at his hand. She didn't shake it off.

And that was when he saw them — three men in the Governor's grey, standing at the mouth of the bridge queue, turning travelers' papers over slow, slow, slow, while a fourth man with a ledger walked the line and looked at faces.

Not faces generally. One face. Looking for one face.

Tam's fingers tightened on the sleeve.
