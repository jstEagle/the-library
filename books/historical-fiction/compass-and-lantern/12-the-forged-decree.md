## 12. The Forged Decree

The Census Office of Verrance kept its dead paperwork in the basement, and Elias Wren had learned, over eleven years of service, that the basement kept better secrets than any archive above it.

He descended the stairs at half past six in the evening, when the clerks had gone home to their suppers and the lamps in the upper rooms were being shuttered one by one. The stairwell smelled of tallow and damp stone, of paper left too long in the dark, of the particular mildew that grew only in buildings that stood near the sea and never quite dried. His boots rang on each step. He counted them out of habit — thirty-one steps from the ground floor to the landings of records — because counting was what he did instead of praying.

In his coat pocket, folded into quarters and wrapped in oilcloth, lay a copy of the Governor's Land Settlement Decree as it had been published three months past, distributed to every parish house and posted on every customs wall from the harbor gate to the northern farms. Elias had carried it for two weeks now. He had read it so many times he could recite its clauses in his sleep — *all lands held under provisional grant shall be confirmed to present holders upon registration* — words that sounded like mercy and were in fact theft, dressed in the long formal sentences that law wore when it wanted to be believed.

But tonight he was going down to compare it with something older.

* * *

The basement of the census office was not, strictly speaking, open to him. It belonged to the Registrar of Deeds, a separate office that shared the building the way two families share a thin-walled house, politely and with resentment. The deed vaults required a key Elias did not hold. The reading room beside them, however — the anteroom where petitioners waited while clerks fetched their documents — was public accommodation, and its lamp was lit until eight, and its outer door was locked by no one, because no one imagined a census man would want to sit among deeds after dark.

No one had ever watched Elias Wren closely enough to learn otherwise. That was the whole architecture of his life: a man so deliberately dull that no door was barred against him.

He let himself into the reading room, lit the second lamp, and sat down at the long scarred table. Then he took out his penknife, his blotting sand, a fresh nib, and twelve sheets of good rag paper he had bought with his own coin at a stationer's on Cooper's Lane — bought with cash, from a shopkeeper who would remember nothing, because Elias had chosen the shopkeeper carefully the way he chose everything now.

Then he went to the duty clerk's bell-pull and rang it, twice, the signal for records retrieval.

Old Sorrel came down eventually, a stooped man with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent grievance of someone whose supper had been interrupted. He looked at Elias without surprise and with minimal interest.

"Census? This hour?"

"Deed verification," Elias said. "Cross-referencing household claims against original grants. I have a list." He produced the list. It was genuine in form; he had written it that afternoon at his desk, in plain view, doing the work he was paid to do. That was the beauty of it. Treason, done properly, looked identical to diligence.

Sorrel squinted at the list. "Land grants. Which parcels?"

"The Crane holdings first," Elias said, and heard his own voice stay level, steady as a plumb line. "And the others on the sheet."

It had taken him four days to understand what he was looking for, and another two days to admit it. The published decree confirmed land titles across the whole Verrance district. But confirmation required that the titles exist. And Elias, whose entire profession was the counting and naming of households, had begun cross-checking the decree's schedule of confirmed grants against the deed registry — the way he checked anything, mechanically, at first, the way a man straightens a crooked picture frame without thinking.

The decree named forty-one parcels granted to "present holders upon registration."

Only nine of those parcels had ever been registered to anyone at all.

The other thirty-two were smallholdings — fishing plots, orchard terraces, salt meadows — held by families for generations under old use-rights that had never needed paper. Under the published decree, those lands would pass by "confirmation" to whatever men presented themselves with the Governor's stamp and the right fees. Elias had traced three of the names already. Two were shipping magnates. One was the Governor's own brother-in-law.

A decree that confirmed existing title was administration. A decree that invented title out of fog was forgery wearing administration's clothes. And it had been printed, beautifully printed, ten thousand copies, and posted on every wall in the province, and every farmer who could read it had felt the cold hand close around his fields and had told himself the law must know what it was doing.

Elias intended to prove that the law knew exactly what it was doing.

"Crane," Sorrel muttered, shuffling off between the shelves. "There's an old grant. 1741, I'd wager. Wait there."

* * *

Elias waited. He listened to the sounds of the vault: the dry whisper of drawers, the cough of stone settling, Sorrel talking to himself in the half-voice of men who work alone among documents. Somewhere above, a carriage passed on the street, and the lamplight trembled minutely in its brackets.

He thought about Hazel Crane's hands.

He had been turning over that image for days now, the way one turns a coin found in the street — surprised to possess it, unsure whether keeping it is luck or theft. Her hands on the pamphlet she had tried to hide, quick and sure, and the small tell of her breath when she realized what she was holding. He had gone back to his rooms that night and pulled the file he should never have kept: the typeface commission from nine years ago, the specimen sheet signed *Tobias Crane*, master cutter, showing the compass rose cut into a lantern — and beneath the signature, in the corner of the plate itself, the mark, tiny as a fingernail, the same mark that had been stamped into the margin of a seditious pamphlet in a shop where Tobias Crane had been seven years in his grave.

Dead men cut nothing. Therefore someone else held his tools and his name and his silence. Therefore the niece who answered his census questions with her eyes fixed politely on the middle distance was either a forger of identities or the keeper of one — and Elias, who had spent eleven years putting names into ledgers and believing that a name in a ledger was the same thing as truth, found that he did not want to report her. Found that the wanting-not-to had grown roots.

He had reported a printer once before. Once was enough for a lifetime. He had been twenty-four and certain, and the man's name had been Ansel Doye, and Elias had watched him hang on a market morning with a crowd that had come for the spectacle and stayed for the shame.

He did not think of that often. He thought of it constantly.

"Here we are." Sorrel returned with a document box and laid a folder on the table with the exaggerated care clerks reserve for things they resent having to carry. "Grant of 1741, parcel surveys appended. Sign the register, will you? And mind the seal — the last fellow bent the corner and I heard about it for a week."

Elias signed. Sorrel withdrew to his stool by the vault door and took up his pipe unlit — smoking forbidden below stairs — and began to doze by increments, as old men do, in honest installments.

* * *

The genuine decree was a modest thing.

That struck Elias first, before he even read it. It was two pages, closely written, sealed in wax that had gone amber with age, signed by a lieutenant-governor three administrations dead. It granted to *Josiah Crane, printer, and his heirs* certain premises and a salt meadow east of the rope walks — granted, confirmed, witnessed, surveyed. It named its boundaries by landmarks: a leaning elm, a drainage ditch, the high-water line. It was the work of people who had walked the ground. You could feel the mud of the salt meadow in its phrasing.

Elias laid the Governor's published decree beside it and began, line by line, clause by clause, the comparison.

The language diverged in the fourth paragraph. In the genuine instrument, grants ran *to the holder of record and his lawful heirs*. In the published version, the phrase had become *to the registered holder or such person as shall establish possession to the satisfaction of the Commissioner of Lands*. Innocuous to a farmer. To a clerk, it was a trapdoor: *satisfaction of the Commissioner* meant satisfaction of whoever paid the Commissioner, and the Commissioner of Lands was appointed by the Governor and had, Elias happened to know from the civil lists he copied every year, three new country houses in two years.

Further down, the forgery grew bolder. The genuine decree reserved common rights — gleaning, grazing, driftwood gathering — *in perpetuity to the inhabitants of the parish*. The published version reserved them *until final survey*. A phrase of five words, and behind those five words, hunger: the power to dissolve commons whenever convenient, which is to say whenever valuable.

But it was the schedule that damned everything. Elias worked through it parcel by parcel, cross-checking against the registry volumes Sorrel fetched him grumbling from the shelves, and the pattern emerged with the terrible cleanliness of arithmetic. Wherever the published decree confirmed a title that existed in the registry, its language matched the record faithfully — because those holders were rich enough to check. Wherever it confirmed title that existed nowhere but air, its language turned vague, boundaryless, dependent on the Commissioner's satisfaction.

Thirty-two parcels. Thirty-two families — the Cranes' neighbors, the fisher houses on the low strand, the orchard men up the valley — whose land existed in the Governor's decree and nowhere else, and would therefore be taken by anyone the Governor wished to favor, legally, irrevocably, with the full weight of printed law behind the taking.

Elias sat back. The lamps hissed. Sorrel snored faintly by the vault door.

He had suspected. Everyone in the lower offices suspected; suspicion was the currency of the civil service, spent in whispers over claret-free dinners. But suspicion was smoke. What lay on the table before him, in two neat columns of comparison, was fire.

* * *

He began to copy.

Not the genuine decree entire — that would take hours he did not have, and a stolen original missing from the vault would be noticed by morning. He copied what mattered. He copied the granting clause verbatim, with its date and signatories. He copied the reservation-of-commons clause verbatim. He copied the boundary descriptions of six representative parcels, including the Crane salt meadow, word for word. Beside each, in a column ruled with a steadiness that surprised him, he set down the corresponding passage from the published decree, and beneath each pair he wrote in his small precise clerk's hand: *Registry volume IX, folio 214: no record.* *Registry volume VII, folio 88: no record.* On and on. Evidence arranged like typeset lines, justified at both margins.

His hand did not shake.

He noticed this, somewhere in the second hour, with a kind of detached wonder. Eleven years of entries, of counts and names and tidy columns, had built into him a hand that did not shake — and now all that discipline, all that patient gray obedience, was flowing into the service of its opposite. Every letter he set down tonight was treason. He knew the statute numbers; he had filed men under them. Misprision. Forgery of state instruments, if he accused the Governor falsely, and sedition if he accused truly. The gallows at the end of both roads unless the road ended differently.

He wrote on. Steady as water finding its level.

Because here was the thing he had finally understood, sitting in this basement among the dead paper of the living city: he had spent eleven years believing that his work was neutral. Counting was counting. A name entered was a name entered. But the census rolls he compiled fed the tax assessments that choked the smallholders, and the household registers he certified gave the grey-coated men their lists of who lived where — who to summon, who to search, who to hang. Neutrality had been the most comfortable lie he owned, and he had worn it like a well-cut coat for a decade, and tonight he took it off.

Ansel Doye had gone to the scaffold on the testimony of a man who was only doing his job. Elias could not give Doye back his life. He could put this paper — his careful, damning, beautiful paper — into hands that would use it.

Hazel Crane's hands, perhaps. Or the smuggler woman's. Or Marta's, or whoever moved beneath the pamphlets and the lantern marks. It hardly mattered which. What mattered was that the proof be true, and legible, and out of his custody before Inspector Hale finished closing the circle he was so patiently drawing around the print shops of Verrance.

Because Hale was drawing it. Elias had seen the requisitions cross his desk: extra patrols at the bridges, authority to inspect cargo manifests, a quiet purchase of informers funded through an account labeled *harbor improvements*. Hale was not hunting pamphlets. Hale was hunting printers, and printers were made of people, and one of them answered census questions with her eyes on the middle distance and had hands like that.

He finished the sixth parcel comparison, sanded the page, blotted it, and folded the twelve sheets into the oilcloth wrapper. Then, deliberately, he copied the whole thing a second time — a fairer copy, compressed onto four sheets, the essential clauses and the registry citations alone. One copy to go out into the world tomorrow. One to hide badly, perhaps, if hiding was even possible for a man whose rooms were searched on a signature.

He signed neither copy. Treason, done properly, was anonymous.

* * *

"You'll want these reshelved," Sorrel said, waking, and Elias started guiltily — though he had done nothing, this hour, but sit in a public reading room with public documents, and the guilt was therefore either madness or conscience, and he no longer distinguished between them.

"Yes. Thank you, Sorrel." He helped return the folders to their boxes, careful of the seals, careful of the corners. His fingers lingered one moment on the amber wax of the 1741 grant — Josiah Crane's mark pressed into it, some old printer's device, half worn away. A compass, he thought. Or nearly. The dead leaving their signs everywhere, waiting for someone to read them.

On the stairs going up, thirty-one steps, he counted again, and on the last step he understood what he had been assembling in the basement besides evidence.

The published decree had been *printed*. Beautifully, professionally printed — he had examined his folded copy under a lens days ago, admiring despite himself the crisp serifs, the even impression, the workmanlike setting. State printing was done in the capital, but this had local hands in it; he was nearly sure; the ink told stories to anyone who spoke ink. Which meant the Governor had used a press. And a press meant a cutter, and a cutter meant matrices, and somewhere in this city the man who had cut the Governor's forgery might also be — or fear, or hate — the woman who cut compasses into lanterns.

Two presses. Two marks. One city running out of room for both.

He pushed out into the night air, where the fog was coming off the harbor in slow grey ropes, and buttoned the oilcloth packet inside his coat, flat against his chest, over his heart, the way a man carries the one document that can hang him.

He would need to get it to Crane & Sons tomorrow. And he would need to get it there past the grey coats at the bridge, past Hale's ledger-men walking the queues and looking at faces —

— and as he turned toward the waterfront, he saw the lanterns of the checkpoint still burning at the far end of Chain Street, and beneath them, unhurried, unmistakable even at this distance by the set of his shoulders, Inspector Hale himself, standing in the fog, watching the road to the print shop.
