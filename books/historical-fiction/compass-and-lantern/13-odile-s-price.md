## 13. Odile's Price

The chandler's cellar smelled of tallow and brine, and by the time Hazel descended the last of the stairs that night, the smell had worked itself into her clothes, her hair, the creases of her palms. She suspected she would carry it forever now—the smell of the thing she was becoming.

Odile was waiting at the long table beneath the hanging lantern, and she was not alone in spirit even if she was alone in body; the smuggler captain filled a room the way weather fills a harbor. She sat with one boot up on the bench, a chart weighted at its corners with candle stubs spread before her, and she did not look up when Hazel's foot found the floor. She let Hazel stand there a moment. That, too, was a kind of navigation.

"Sit," Odile said finally, as though granting a tide its turn.

Hazel sat. Between them on the table lay the sealed case—oilcloth over cedar, cedar over iron—and beside it, wrapped in a square of Marta's old apron, the punch.

She had brought it because they had asked her to bring it. That should have warned her.

"You've seen the manifest I drew up," Odile said, tapping the chart without looking at it. "The *Morning Tide* clears on the morning tide—there's a joke in that somewhere and Tam will find it eventually. Two days. The decrees go in the false bottom of my strongbox, under the sugar manifests where no customs man has ever bothered to look, because customs men are honest about exactly one thing: their boredom." Her finger moved along the coastline drawn in faded ink, out past the breakwater, past Verrance's reach, to a small confident capital letter far to the north. "Six days at sea if the weather holds. Nine if it doesn't. Then your Governor's forgeries are read into the record, and everything changes."

"Everything," Hazel said, and heard how much weight she put on the word, and how much doubt.

Odile looked up then. Her eyes were the color of deep water over sand, and they did not blink often enough. "You doubt me."

"I don't know you."

"No," Odile agreed, pleasantly. "You don't."

* * *

They had met nine days ago, and Hazel had been counting the way a miser counts, in small hoarded increments: nine days since Elias Wren had stood in the doorway of the print shop with a pamphlet in his hand and her secret mark face-up between them like a card turned too soon. Nine days since the ground of her life had tilted and refused to settle. Seven days since Marta had walked her down the chandler's stairs and said, *this is Odile Vasseur, she runs the Tide, and she is the only reason any of this leaves the harbor.*

Seven days of watching Odile ask questions that were not questions. Seven days of watching her count things—crates, men, hours, risks—with the flat unhurried competence of someone who had never once been surprised by a price.

Tonight the price had arrived.

"The cargo is settled, then," Hazel said. "The case goes aboard."

"The cargo is settled." Odile leaned back. The lantern swung slightly above them, and the shadows of her shoulders moved on the stone wall like something larger waking. "The question is what I carry it *for*."

"Coin. You said coin."

"I said coin for freight. Freight is boxes. Boxes don't hang people." Odile's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "What's in that box isn't freight, Miss Crane. It's a verdict. If Hale boards my ship and finds those decrees, he doesn't fine me. He hangs me from the customs house yardarm—or whatever the harbor equivalent is; the man has an instinct for ceremony. So we're not talking about freight anymore. We're talking about risk. And risk has a different arithmetic."

Hazel kept her hands still on the table. This was a discipline she had learned young, at the cutting bench, when her father would set a graver in her fingers and say, *the metal knows when you're afraid.* Everything knew when you were afraid. Odile especially.

"Name it," Hazel said.

"The punch."

The word sat between them for a long moment. Hazel made herself look at the little bundle of Marta's apron cloth, as if seeing it fresh could buy her a second of neutrality before the refusal came.

"The compass punch," Odile went on, watching her the way a pilot watches a shoeline. "Your mark. The original steel, cut by the hand that cuts everything else. While I hold it, nothing forged under your mark can pass without my knowing—because anything true will match what I hold, and anything false won't. It makes me the only honest mint in Verrance." She smiled, thin as a blade's edge. "And it means that when this is done—if it's done—you can't cut another mark without coming through me first. Collateral, Miss Crane. Assurance. Call it what you like. But the *Morning Tide* sails with it, or the decrees stay ashore."

* * *

Later—much later, standing in the fog outside the chandler's with the taste of the argument still in her mouth—Hazel would try to trace exactly when she had known she would refuse. Not when Odile named the price. Earlier. Perhaps in the first breath after the lantern swung. Some part of her had known from the moment Marta had first said *they need your mark*, that the mark would be asked for, and that some line inside her had already hardened around it like steel around a stamping die.

Because the punch was not steel. That was the thing Odile could not see, or would not.

The punch was her father's hands over hers on the graver, the first winter after her mother died, teaching her how to read the grain of metal the way sailors read cloud. It was the night her brother Corin had come home drunk on cheap wine and ambition and announced he would be the shop's master cutter someday, and their father had laughed—not unkindly—and looked straight at Hazel over Corin's head. It was the drawer she kept it in, lined with felt, next to the paper pattern of the very first compass rose she had ever cut, at eleven years old, crooked as a drunk fence and treasured anyway. It was every hour of her life spent becoming invisible so that the work could be visible, all of it annealed into four ounces of tempered steel shaped like a promise she had never been permitted to speak aloud.

Give it to Odile Vasseur. Give it to the sea.

"I can't," Hazel said.

"You can," Odile said. "You simply won't. There's a difference, and I respect the second more than the first, but it doesn't change my terms."

"It's mine."

"It's leverage. Which is worth more than mine, these days." Odile gathered the chart, folded it with quick precise movements, and set a candle stub on it to keep it closed. "Understand me, girl. I'm not your enemy and I'm not your patron. I'm a woman who keeps a ship alive in waters where ships sink. Every man on my crew eats because I choose correctly. If I carry your box and Hale takes my ship, my crew hangs beside me. So I want a hold on the one thing in this city that everyone—from the resistance to the Governor himself—apparently wants untouched." She shrugged, vast and easy. "It's good business."

"It's ransom."

"It's trust," Odile said, "bargained for. Which is the only kind there is. The kind given freely is called luck, and luck sinks ships."

* * *

Hazel walked. She didn't decide to walk; her legs simply declined the offer of the stairs back up to the street door and carried her instead out through the chandler's yard, into the lanes behind the fish market, where the fog came off the harbor in slow grey ropes and every lamp burned with a halo like a saint's portrait gone wrong.

Tam fell into step with her somewhere near the rope walks, materializing out of the murk the way he did, all elbows and questions.

"You talked to the captain," he said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone in this conspiracy spoke in statements wearing question's clothing.

"We talked."

"She asked for the punch, didn't she." When Hazel didn't answer, he nodded, satisfied, dodging a fish cart with the ease of a body that had never once worried about being struck. "She asks everyone for the thing they won't give. Old Ferro says it's how she counts you. Says the ones who hand it over, she uses. The ones who refuse—" he grinned sideways at her, gap-toothed and gleaming in the lamplight, "—those are the ones she crews."

"Ferro says a lot of things."

"He does. He talks to the press like it's his wife. Marta says that's what comes of living under a shop." He hopped a puddle. "Are you going to give it to her?"

"No."

"Good." He said it with such simple certainty that Hazel almost laughed. "Corin wouldn't have, neither."

Hazel stopped walking.

Tam got three steps on before he noticed, and turned, and she saw the mistake land in his face the way a dropped sort lands on a compositor's stone—all at once, irretrievably. His chatter had a hundred doors in it and he'd just opened the wrong one.

"I didn't mean—" he started.

"How did you know that name?" Hazel kept her voice level. Down here, in the dark, in the fog, with only a boy to hear it, and still the old reflex: level, always level, the surface smooth so no one thinks to look beneath.

Tam scuffed his boot against the cobbles. "Marta. One night. She was talking about the shop, about before. About the son who died and the daughter nobody counted." He glanced up, quick and apologetic and utterly unafraid of her, which somehow was the worst of it. "I forget sometimes what's secret. There's so many secrets now they get mixed up in my head like cases after a fire drill."

Hazel started walking again. After a moment he caught up.

"I'm sorry," he said, quieter.

"It's all right."

"It's not, but thank you." A pause. "He sounds like he was loud."

"He was loud," Hazel said. The fog took the words and softened them. "He was loud and fast and certain, and he died in a way none of us could believe, because people like that aren't supposed to stop." She hadn't meant to say it aloud. She had not said it aloud to anyone. But the boy walked beside her and expected nothing, and the words came out the way type falls into a stick—one at a time, in order, until the line is full. "He drowned. Off the breakwater, four years ago. They said he'd been drinking. I've never once believed it, and I've never once said so, because saying so would have required existing."

Tam considered this with the gravity of the young. "Now you exist," he offered.

"Now I exist."

"And you're going to refuse the captain, and she's going to take the box anyway, because she needs the decrees on that ship worse than she needs your steel. Ferro says—"

"Ferro," Hazel said, "should write a book."

Tam grinned. The fog swallowed them both.

* * *

The stalemate held for two days, and it held the way a dam holds—silently, with pressure accumulating where no one could see.

On the morning of the sailing, Hazel came down into the cellar before dawn, because sleep had become a country she could visit but not reside in, and found Odile alone at the table, mending a net with fingers that moved like they'd been doing it since before language.

The punch sat on the table between them. Untouched. Still wrapped in Marta's apron.

"You didn't take it," Hazel said.

Odile didn't look up from the net. "I gave you until the tide."

"You said the decrees stay ashore without it."

"Mm." A knot pulled tight. "Here's what I learned in nineteen years of running cargo, Miss Crane, most of it illegal and all of it instructive. There are two kinds of collateral. The kind a man gives you because he doesn't care about losing it—that's worthless, and you weigh anchor and pray. And the kind he bleeds to keep." Now she looked up, and in the low light her eyes were unreadable as ballast water. "You bled. All night, I'd wager. So I know the mark matters to you more than your own neck, and that tells me the documents under your mark are worth carrying. The punch stays with its maker. Consider the price paid in a different currency."

"What currency?"

"Doubt," Odile said. "I doubted you. Now I don't. That's rarer than steel." She rose, and the room rearranged itself around her height. "The case goes aboard within the hour. Your boy runs it to the quay—he's faster than any of my men and lighter than any law. And Miss Crane—" she paused at the stair, one hand on the rough wall, "—when this is done, I'll want to talk to you about cutting a ship's papers. Proper ones. There's a market, and you have the hand for it, and I pay better than revolution."

She climbed the stairs. The net lay finished on the table, its knots small and perfect and impossible to unpick.

Hazel stood alone in the lantern light with the punch and the case and the strange hollow feeling of having won something without being sure what. Trust bargained for, Odile had said. The only kind there is. And yet some stubborn ember in her chest insisted there was a third kind, the kind that couldn't be held as collateral by anyone—the kind that lived in a boy's careless apology and an old woman's apron cloth and a cell of conspirators who kept choosing each other.

She picked up the case. It was heavier than four ounces of steel and lighter than a life, and outside, far off toward the harbor, the fog bell began to toll the turn of the tide.

She had just reached the stairs when Tam came down them two at a time, white-faced, taking them like a man descending into deep water.

"Hazel." He was breathing hard. "There's smoke over the east quarter. Over your street."
