## 14. Fire in the Type Rack

The cellar had a smell Hazel had come to think of as her own second skin—tallow and lamp oil, the green bite of fresh-cast type metal, the dust of paper shavings that settled on everything like gray snow that never melted. For three weeks she had worked below the chandler's shop from dusk until the bells rang two, and in that time she had learned the room the way she had once learned her father's bench: by ear, by smell, by the small grammar of its noises. The drip of brine through the foundation stones. Tam's boots on the ladder, always too fast, always announcing him three steps before he arrived. The particular creak Odile made in a chair, like a ship taking a slow swell.

She knew this room. Which was why, when the fire came, she knew before anyone said a word that it had not come from nowhere.

* * *

It began as an ordinary evening, if anything in Verrance could be called ordinary now. The city above them had grown a new texture of fear since the burned rack of matrices at Crane & Sons' old competitor on Tanner's Row—the Governor's men had stepped up their searches, and Hale's inspectors had taken to walking the quays in pairs with a list in hand. Down here, though, the cellar kept its own weather. Marta sat at the composing stone with her spectacles pushed up into her white hair, setting the first pages of what they had begun to call, only half-jokingly, *the truth*. Odile counted coin at the corner table because Odile counted coin the way other people breathed. Tam lay on his stomach under the press, narrating its parts to himself in a whisper, having decided sometime last week that he would be a mechanic when he grew up, or a captain, or a spy, whichever paid best.

Hazel worked at the casting bench with the door of the melting pot open, ladling metal into the mouth of the caster in a thin silver thread. She was cutting matrices for the smaller body size—a long task, delicate, the kind of work that emptied her mind the way prayer was supposed to empty other people's minds. Each letter she cut was a small act of faith that there would be a press to use it on, a city to read it, hands to hold it.

"Your lantern," Marta said without looking up.

Hazel glanced over her shoulder. The compass-in-lantern proof hung on the drying line where she'd pinned it that morning, and the candle behind it guttered in a draft, throwing the little mark huge and trembling across the stone wall. A compass rose inside a lantern. Her father's compasses. Her brother's lanterns—or rather, the lanterns she'd invented for her brother so that no one would ever look twice at her. It occurred to her, not for the first time and with no more gentleness than usual, that she had spent eleven years building a ghost out of type metal, and that the ghost was now better known in Verrance than she was.

"It wants trimming," she said. "The counters are too tight at the small size."

"Everything you make wants trimming, and everything you make is perfect." Marta slid a line of type together with the economy of forty years in the trade. "That's the whole trick of you, girl."

Tam emerged from beneath the press with a smudge of grease across his nose. "Odile says the *Morning Tide* docks Thursday."

"Odile says a great many things," Odile murmured, not looking up from her coins.

"But Thursday's true?"

"Thursday is true." Odile set the last coin in its column and closed the box with a snap. "Whether I keep my promise by then depends on whether any of us live that long, little runner, so don't gloat yet."

It was almost fond, the way she said it. Almost family. Hazel turned back to the caster and told herself not to get used to the sound of it.

* * *

The fire announced itself as a smell before it announced itself as light.

She was deep in the cutting—her third matrix of the night, the *R*, its counter nearly finished—when the odor reached her: not tallow, not oil, but the sharp sweet char of sizing and paper, the smell of a book burning. She lifted her head like an animal scenting weather.

The type rack stood against the east wall. It was Marta's rack, old and scarred, holding four wooden drawers of matrices and the boxed punches Hazel had cut over the past weeks—the compass punch among them, wrapped in wool, waiting for Thursday. Above the rack hung the drying proofs. Below it sat a basket of oiled rags they used for wiping down the press.

A thread of smoke rose from the basket, thin as a hair.

For one heartbeat Hazel's mind performed the terrible arithmetic of craft: *the rags were wiped down at nine; it is past midnight; linseed oil heats itself in folded cloth, everyone knows this, it happens, it has happened in every print shop since printing began.* And in the same heartbeat, another voice, colder: *the rags were folded flat last night, because I folded them.*

"Fire," she said, and her voice came out flat and strange, and then she was moving.

Marta saw it a breath later and did exactly the right thing, which was nothing dramatic—no scream, no running—just a low word, "*Odile*," and the three of them converged on the rack while Tam scrambled up the ladder stairs. They could not use water near the metal and the melting pot still glowed at Hazel's back, so they smothered: the leather apron off the press hook, the sand bucket, Odile swearing in harbor French as she beat the flames down into the basket. The apron caught at its hem and she tore it away and stamped it. Smoke rolled along the ceiling in a fat gray river, found the air shaft that vented up through the chandler's yard, and began, horribly, to leave—to announce them to whoever might be watching the yard.

"The shaft," Hazel said. "It's drawing."

"I know what a chimney does," Odile snapped, but she was already dragging the sand pail to the base of the shaft to choke the draft.

It took perhaps four minutes. In that time they lost the bottom drawer of matrices entirely—the old ones, Marta's, the letters she'd been setting for thirty years—and scorched the corner of the second, and the heat had already begun to curl the proofs on the line so that Hazel's lantern mark warped and blackened as she watched, the flame eating outward from the center of the compass rose until there was nothing left of it but a ragged ring of char.

Then it was over, and they stood in the reek of it, five people in a stone cellar, listening to the ordinary sounds of the house above—Ferren the chandler moving around in his bedroom, a floorboard complaining—and the less ordinary sound of Tam crying without noise, the way children cry when they have learned that noise is dangerous.

Nobody's hands were burned. That was the miracle Hazel would remember later, the thing she returned to: nobody's hands were burned. Printers' hands were their fortunes. She looked down at her own fingers, black to the second knuckle, steady on the sand shovel, and thought: *I watched those proofs burn and I felt nothing about the proofs. Only about who lit them.*

That was the moment the poison went in. She felt it go, cool and precise, somewhere behind her breastbone.

* * *

They salvaged in silence. Marta knelt at the ruined drawer and lifted out matrix after matrix, laying them on the stone in rows like casualties, sorting salvageable from dead with a touch so gentle it hurt to watch. Odile doused the basket a final time and carried it out to the yard. Tam fetched water without being asked, making trip after trip up the ladder, and each time he came back down his eyes went to Hazel first, then to the burned rack, then to Hazel again, as though checking whether the world were still standing.

And Hazel stood apart, at her bench, doing sums.

The rags. She had wiped the press herself at nine o'clock and laid the rags unfolded over the rail of the press frame, because folded rags with linseed and lamp oil will heat, and her father had drummed that into her before she could write her own name: *unfold your wiping cloths, Hazel, unfold them every night, fires sleep in folds.* At some point between nine and midnight, someone had gathered them, folded them—folded them tightly, she could see the creases in the half-burned cloth Odile had brought back—and packed them into the basket beneath the rack. Beneath the rack. Not beside it, not by the door where they belonged, but directly beneath four drawers of matrices and a box containing the compass punch.

If the apron had come down thirty seconds later, the punch would have gone with the drawer. The mark itself. The one irreplaceable thing in the room, the thing the whole plan hung on.

An accident would have been careless anywhere. This carelessness had chosen its ground.

"Hazel." Marta's voice, very quiet. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm thinking."

"Think out loud, then, because you've got a face on you like a verdict."

Hazel looked around the cellar—at Tam frozen halfway down the ladder with the water pail, at Odile paused in the doorway with the basket on her hip, at Marta kneeling amid her dead letters—and heard herself say, in a voice she did not entirely recognize: "Someone folded those rags."

Silence. The brine dripped. Somewhere above, Ferren's floorboard complained again.

"They were on the press rail," Hazel said. "Unfolded. I left them myself. Someone folded them and packed them under the rack, where the oil would do its work slow, where we'd find the fire late. That's not accident. That's aim."

"You're tired," Marta said, but gently, the way one speaks to a person standing on a ledge.

"I'm right."

Odile set the basket down. "The girl's arithmetic is sound," she said slowly. "Linseed in a closed fold will cook. But it cooks in hours, not minutes, and it doesn't choose its bed. If someone wanted fire under that rack, they'd want it under that rack." Her eyes moved to the charred line where the proofs had hung, and Hazel watched the same conclusion arrive behind them. "The punch survived because the apron came fast. Next time it might not."

"Next time?" Tam's voice cracked on it. He had gone white. "Who would—who'd *do* that? Ferro wouldn't. Ferro fixed the press when—" He stopped, because he heard himself, and everyone heard him stop.

"Ferro has the keys," Hazel said. "Ferro is here alone most afternoons while we sleep."

"And Ferro's brother sits in the Governor's workhouse on Hale's say-so," Marta added, very quietly, "waiting on the Governor's mercy."

The words dropped into the room like type falling on stone. Hazel hadn't known about the brother—Tam had let that slip days ago, chatter in a fish market, and she had pocketed it the way she pocketed everything—but she heard it now spoken plainly and understood how long it had been sitting in all their minds unspoken, a splinter none of them would touch.

"That's not evidence," Tam said loudly. "That's just—that's a story. Ferro's been here since before any of you. He taught me the screws on the platen. He calls me *poussin*." His chin was trembling and he held it high. "You can't just decide."

"No," Hazel said. "We can't." And she hated herself for how much of her wanted to decide anyway.

Because that was the poison working, wasn't it? She stood in the first room that had ever made room for her—these people who knew her mark and her name and her crime and her grief, who had offered her belonging like a struck match—and she could feel the suspicion spreading through it like water through paper, finding every seam. If Ferro had done this, then the cellar had a door open to Hale that Hazel couldn't see, and every page they set was being read over their shoulders, and Thursday's shipment was already a story someone else had written. And if Ferro hadn't—if it truly was an accident, oil and fold and chance—then she had just stood in front of the people who trusted her and put a noose around an innocent man's neck with nothing but her own certainty for rope.

Either way, something in the room was broken. She understood that with a sick clarity. Trust was like type: cast once, it held its shape forever, but crack it and you could melt it down and never quite drive the memory of the flaw out of the metal.

"We do three things," Odile said at last, and her captain's voice flattened the room. "One: the punch and the good matrices sleep elsewhere from tonight—I'll say no more where. Two: nobody speaks of this above stairs, to anyone, including Ferro, including especially Ferro. Three:"—she looked at Hazel, and her eyes were hard and not unkind—"*you* look at that basket in daylight, with your cutter's eyes, and you tell me plain whether a hand folded those cloths or whether oil lied to us. Because if you're wrong, girl, you'll have hanged a man in your head for nothing, and if you're right, we need to know it clean, not in the dark with smoke in our lungs."

"I'll tell you plain," Hazel said.

But when Odile had gone up the ladder with the salvaged box hidden under her coat, and Marta had finally consented to sleep, and Tam had curled himself in the old sailcloth under the press facing the door the way he always faced the door, Hazel knelt alone at the burned rack and picked through the ashes with her bare fingers, and found, wedged in the basket's charred rim, a thing that stopped her breath.

A twist of wire. Copper, fine-gauge, the kind used to bale candle wicks—a chandler's wire, from a chandler's shop, the stock of which Ferro alone kept in the cellar. It had been looped twice and pinched flat, deliberately, in the manner of a man sealing a fold shut so the oil would cook trapped. She turned it in the lamplight. The pinch marks were clean, recent, the copper bright where the fire had licked it.

Proof. It should have felt like proof. It felt like ash in her palm.

She thought of Ferro's broad hands teaching Tam the platen screws. She thought of a brother in the workhouse. She thought of Hale's list, and the inspectors on the quay, and how easily a man breaks when the lever against him is family. She thought, unwilled and unforgivable, of Elias Wren saying, weeks ago in this very room, *every cell has someone inside it who opened the door.* She had pitied the printer he'd betrayed then. Now she sat in her own cellar counting her own household for traitors, and understood that he had not been describing wickedness. He had been describing arithmetic.

Above her, faintly, the floorboards shifted—someone turning in sleep, someone rising, someone going to the privy, someone carrying a message into the fog. She could no longer tell the sounds apart, and that was the true ruin of the night, more than the matrices, more even than the scorch where her lantern mark had hung. The room had taught her its grammar, and now the grammar lied.

Hazel closed her fist around the wire and looked up the dark throat of the ladder, toward the rooms where Ferro slept, and made herself say the next sentence in her own head, because it had to be said by someone:

Tomorrow, before Thursday, before the tide, before she could talk herself out of it—she would watch Ferren's mechanic the way she had once watched the whole city watch her brother. She would learn his folds.

From the top of the ladder, soft as a draft, came the sound of the cellar door closing.
