## 15. Hale Closes In

The census office smelled of lamp oil and cold tea, and by the third hour of Inspector Hale's monologue Elias Wren had learned the precise rhythm of his own dread. It kept time like a metronome. Tick: the inspector's boot heel on the floorboards. Tock: the scratch of Hale's pen as he underlined something in a manifest that had no business being underlined.

"The coffin," Hale said again, for the fourth time, as though repetition could wring truth from the word the way a press wrings ink from a plate. "Four hundred pounds declared as lead type. Lead type, Wren. For a funeral."

"Sentimental families sometimes bury a printer with his letters," Elias said. He was pleased with how steady it came out. He had spent eleven years learning to make his voice into a drawer that slid shut without sound.

Hale looked up. He had the kind of face that weather had given up on—pale, faintly pitted, the eyes a gray so light they seemed to have been bleached of opinion. Men underestimated him for it. Elias did not. He had watched Hale take a smuggler's confession apart once, patiently, the way a boy takes the legs off a fly, and he had never since mistaken the man's mildness for slowness.

"With his letters." Hale smiled without warmth. "Well. The coffin went to Crane & Sons Print Shop on Tanner's Lane. The undertaker who signed the waybill has never heard of the deceased. The lead, when my men weighed the return shipment—" he paused, savoring it, "—was four hundred pounds exactly. Nothing had been removed. Which means either the coffin carried type in and type out, like some devotional object being passed among the faithful, or it carried something else entirely and the something else is still sitting in that shop on Tanner's Lane."

*Or the something else has already been moved through three checkpoints and is sleeping in a smuggler's hold,* Elias thought, and slid the drawer shut.

He said: "It would be simpler to ask the Cranes."

"I intend to." Hale set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the manifest's edge. That care was the worst thing about him. Violent men were legible; careful men were not. "I've walked past that shop twice now. There's a widow who answers the door as though she's been standing behind it all day waiting for me. And there's a smell." He breathed in through his nose, theatrical, remembering. "Ink and acid and hot metal. My father was a farrier, Wren. I know the smell of workshops. That shop isn't dying. Dying shops don't order that much copper."

Elias turned a page of the household ledger in front of him—a page he had already read three times and would read three more, because the motion of his hands gave his face permission to be blank. Crane & Sons. Household of two. Widow Agnes Crane, fifty-one. Niece, Hazel Marsh, twenty-six. His own handwriting on the enumeration form. *Niece.* The lie he had swallowed whole on a bright morning while a pamphlet lay face-down on the counter behind her, its corner printed with a mark he could not stop seeing when he closed his eyes: a compass rose folded inside a lantern.

He knew where that mark came from. He had catalogued it himself, six years ago, in the commission files—*Crane, J., master cutter, compass rose within lantern border, punch and matrices, private commission, paid in full.* Signed by a dead man. Cut, if the pamphlet was anything to go by, by a living one.

"You're quiet," Hale observed.

"I'm adding figures."

"You're always adding figures." Hale stood, buttoned his coat with the same terrible patience. "Raid is set for Friday. Dawn. I want the premises sealed before the market carts block the lane. You'll be there, Wren—you counted the household; your presence makes the warrant tidy. Two days." He gathered his papers, then stopped, one hand flat on them, and looked at Elias with those bleached eyes. "You saw the shop. You saw the niece. Tell me about her."

"There was nothing to tell. A young woman keeping books for her aunt."

"Hm." Hale held the look a moment longer, weighing him on some scale only he could read, and then took his papers and his cold tea and left.

Elias sat alone in the office for a long while after, listening to the metronome of his own pulse, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just been measured for a rope along with everyone else on Tanner's Lane.

* * *

He did not go straight to the print shop. That was the first discipline: never straight. He walked west instead, bought a roll of cough drops he did not need from an apothecary, crossed through the fish market where the gulls screamed over the gutting tables, and let two streets and forty minutes accumulate between himself and the office before he turned down Tanner's Lane at dusk.

The shop windows were dark except for one lamp burning low at the back, and through the glass he could see her silhouette bent over the bench, hands moving in the small precise economy of someone doing work that mattered to no one watching. Hazel Marsh. Or whoever she was. He knocked the pattern he had used before—two, pause, one—and the silhouette went still.

A long moment. Then the bolt drew back and she stood in the doorway with the lamp behind her, so that he saw her mostly as outline: the sharp line of her jaw, the smudge of graphite on her cheekbone she hadn't noticed, the eyes that were already reading him the way she probably read proofs—hunting errors.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," he agreed. "May I come in anyway?"

She hesitated, then stepped aside, rebolting the door behind him with a sound like a knuckle cracking. In the lamplight the shop assembled itself around him: the wall of type cases, the iron press with its black arms folded like a sleeping insect, the sweet resinous smell of ink and the sharper bite of acid from the cutting room. He had been in a hundred printers' shops in his career. This was the first one that felt inhabited rather than merely occupied.

"Hale traced the coffin," he said, because she deserved the wound clean, without bandaging. "The waybill, the undertaker, the weight of the return shipment. All of it. He's scheduled a raid on this shop for Friday at dawn."

Her face did not collapse. That was the thing about her that continually wrong-footed him—he had seen hardened smugglers come apart faster. What happened instead was a kind of rapid recalculation, visible only in her eyes, as though somewhere behind them a compositor were re-setting a entire line of type at speed, pulling out sorts, inserting new ones.

"Friday," she repeated. "Today is Wednesday."

"Yes."

"Then we have—" she glanced toward the back of the shop, where a cheap calendar hung beside the cutting-room door, "—thirty-six hours. Maybe less, if he lies awake as well as he underlines."

Elias blinked. "You know him?"

"I know the type of him." She moved past him to the counter and began, without appearing to decide to, tidying it—a tray of brass rules here, a burnisher there. Hands that needed to be doing something. He recognized the habit because he shared it. "My father said the dangerous ones are never the brawlers. Brawlers want you to know they're coming. The dangerous ones count." She looked up. "How much does he have? Truly. Not what he showed you. What he knows."

And there it was, the question underneath all his questions, the one he had been carrying up Tanner's Lane like a stone in his shoe: how much do I tell her, and how much of what I tell her becomes a confession of my own?

"He has the shipment," Elias said. "He doesn't yet have the mark. But the mark is the only thread that leads anywhere worse than this shop, and if his men are thorough they'll find plates, matrices, correspondence—Hazel, they will pull the floorboards up and sift the ash in the stove. Whatever is still here that carries that compass, it cannot be here Thursday night."

"It isn't here now." Something in her voice made him look at her properly. "Most of it. There's the original commission file in your census office—"

"—which I will misfile tonight," he said, "between the ferry licenses and the tallow inspectors' reports, where no one has looked since the last administration."

For the first time since he'd known her, she almost smiled. It transformed her face and frightened him, how much it transformed it.

"And the raid itself?" she asked. "What does he expect to find?"

"Evidence of sedition. Proof of the underground press. Names." Elias heard his own voice drop, and hated the reason for it. "He'll want the widow's account first—she's the legal head of the household. He'll separate everyone. He'll be gentle with her and hard with you, because he'll assume the niece is the soft point of entry." He paused. "He may be wrong about that. But you should not be in this building when they come."

"And go where?" she asked, too lightly. "Disappear? I've spent six years being invisible, Elias. It's a skill, not a refuge." She set down the burnisher. "There's the cellar press, the chandler's. Marta can move what needs moving. But listen—" and now she came around the counter and stood close enough that he could see the graphite smudge, the fine tired lines at the corners of her eyes, "—you can't be linked to any warning. If Hale even suspects—"

"He already suspects." He said it plainly because there was no gentler version. "He watched me today the way a man watches a ledger he thinks has been cooked. I saw him deciding whether to trust the arithmetic. So yes—I'm likely already half-caught, which means the margin for caution is thinner than it was yesterday, which means every hour I stand in this shop increases both our dangers." He took out his watch, an inheritance from a father who had died believing his son honest, and looked at the time the way one looks at a diagnosis. "I'll leave by the alley. I won't come again before Friday. If the raid goes as planned, Marta will know by noon—the office gossip travels faster than any runner Tam has. If you hear nothing by noon, assume the worst and move everything."

"Assume the worst," she repeated. "That's your professional recommendation."

"That's my—" He stopped. What was it? His penance? His trade? He had reported a printer once, years ago, and watched from the gallery as the sentence was read out, and everything he had done since—the meticulousness, the counting, the whole gray architecture of his life—had been built on top of that one act like a city built on a filled-in well. "My amends," he finished quietly.

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a cart rattled past and the lamplight shivered across the type cases, making the little lead letters seem to tremble in their compartments, ten thousand small voices waiting to be arranged into sentences.

"Elias." Her voice was different now, stripped of its armor. "If Friday goes badly—if they take someone—don't try to be clever. Don't stand in any doors. Promise me."

"I promise to stand in exactly as many doors as the law requires."

"That is not a promise."

"It's the only kind I'm equipped to keep." He moved toward the alley door, then stopped with his hand on the latch, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in his chest since the office like a swallowed coal. "The commission file. Your father's. When I misfile it tonight, I'll copy the signature page first."

"Why?"

"Because signatures are evidence, and I have decided—" the coal burned as he said it, and he found, to his surprise, that the burning felt less like guilt than like purpose— "to spend the rest of this week committing treason with a steady hand. Goodnight, Hazel."

He let himself out into the fog. Behind him the shop lamp glowed in the window, small and stubborn, and ahead of him the lanes of Verrance unspooled dark and dripping, thirty-six hours wide, every hour now a coin he could feel spending itself out of his pocket one by one whether he walked or stood still.

Somewhere down by the harbor a ship's bell rang the second watch. Thirty-four hours left. He began to walk faster.
