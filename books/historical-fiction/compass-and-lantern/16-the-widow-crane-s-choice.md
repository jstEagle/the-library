## 16. The Widow Crane's Choice

My aunt Agatha Crane had a way of moving through the print shop as though it were a church she did not believe in—respectful, distant, slightly resentful of the incense. She dusted the ledger shelf on Tuesdays. She watered the plant in the front window that had been dead since the spring and never admitted it. She wore black because the town expected it of her, seven years past my uncle's funeral, and she kept the shop's accounts in a copperplate hand she had learned at a finishing school in Aldermoor that no longer existed.

What she did not do was come down to the cutting bench. In all the years I had worked there—first as a child sweeping galleys, then as the niece who "helped with the type," then, after Tam's death, as the ghost who had become the shop's whole beating heart—she had never once stood behind me while I worked the graver. I had told myself this was indifference. Aunts of her generation did not descend; they presided, elsewhere.

I was wrong about that, the way I had been wrong about so many things.

It was the fourth morning after Odile's price, and I had not slept properly since. The compass punch sat wrapped in oilcloth in the false bottom of my father's toolbox, and every hour of the day I performed a small arithmetic of danger: how many steps from the bench to the coal cellar door, how long it would take Marta to turn the sign, whether Inspector Hale's men had walked past the window twice or three times since breakfast. The arithmetic had become a kind of music. I could not stop composing it.

Agatha came down at half past nine, carrying the tray with the coffee she made for me every morning without ever once acknowledging that she made it, and she stopped at the foot of the stairs.

She was looking at the bench. Not at me—at the bench. At the scatter of steel punches in their grooved wooden rack, at the magnifier hanging from its hook, at the little tin of jeweler's rouge, at the half-finished matrix clamped in the vise with its reversed letters staring up like a face in a mirror.

"Hazel," she said.

I put down the burnisher. My hands, I noticed distantly, had gone very still, the way they went still when I was cutting the fine serifs of a lowercase e—the moment where one slip ruined everything.

"You cut those." It was not a question. Her voice had none of the tremor I would have expected. It was flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading an account book and finding a column that did not sum.

"Cut what," I said, which was foolish, and we both knew it was foolish, and she let the silence sit between us until it had said everything either of us needed to say about my talent for lying badly to family.

"The punches." She came closer, and I watched her eyes move over the rack the way a customs man's eyes move over a manifest—item by item, weighing each against something carried only in her head. "Your father bought his first six from Vantry in the capital. He always said he'd teach Corin to cut his own someday. Corin died before—" She stopped. She started again, quieter. "These aren't your father's. And they aren't Corin's."

"They're mine," I said.

The words came out smaller than I intended. I had rehearsed this confession a hundred times over seven years—in the bath, in the dark, in the middle of cutting a punch when the work emptied me enough for honesty to leak through—and in every rehearsal I said it grandly, defiantly, flinging it down like a gauntlet. In practice it came out the way secrets actually leave the body: thin, frightened, relieved.

"I've been cutting them since I was fourteen. Father taught me on the sly because Mother thought the fumes would ruin my lungs and the gravers would ruin my hands and the ambition would ruin everything else. When Corin took the fever, Father couldn't hold a graver anymore—his hands shook, you remember—and someone had to finish the Halvard commission, the hymnal faces, or we'd have had to return the advance and we owed the paper supplier through the quarter. So I finished it. Signed with his initial. And then he died too, and the shop would have died with him, because nobody buys type from a girl and a widow, and I just—" I ran out of breath. "I just kept being him. On paper. Every commission, every invoice. *C. Crane, cutter.* And you signed what I asked you to sign, and you never asked, and I thought—I thought you didn't want to know."

Agatha set the coffee tray down on the stone floor with a click that sounded absurdly loud. Then she did something she had not done since I was nine years old and had fallen off the sea wall: she took my chin in her hand and turned my face up to hers.

Her eyes were pale gray, the color of the harbor in February, and behind the glass of them I saw something rearrange itself—not shock exactly, but the settling of a long weight into a truer position, the way a ship's ballast shifts when the tide turns.

"Child," she said, "I have known how to read a balance sheet for fifty years. Do you imagine I never noticed that the shop's output tripled after Corin died? That the invoices were written in a hand I watched you practice at this very table when you were eleven?" She let go of my chin. "I signed what you asked me to sign because asking questions is a luxury for people whose questions cost nothing. Mine would have cost us the shop. So I dusted the ledgers and I watered a dead plant and I let you lie to me, because the lie was keeping bread on this table and you alive and un-looked-at, and I understood perfectly well what you were buying with it."

"You knew," I said. "All this time, you knew."

"Knowing and saying are different trades," said Agatha Crane. "I never crossed the line between them. Neither should you have had to." And then, before I could answer, before I could even decide which of ten answers I wanted, she picked up the coffee tray again and said, "There are two men standing across the street by the rope walk, and they have been there since eight o'clock. One of them is Inspector Hale."

* * *

The knock came at noon precisely, which I learned later was a habit of Hale's—he believed punctuality unsettled people who lived by irregular hours, and printers lived by very irregular hours indeed. By then Agatha had moved through the shop like weather: the toolbox with its false bottom relocated to the flour bin upstairs; the oilcloth bundle gone wherever she had put it, somewhere I genuinely could not guess, which frightened me almost more than if I'd known; the front room straightened, the fire poked down, the dead plant turned so its bare stems faced away from the door, as if even a dead thing could be arranged to look less guilty.

Marta had been sent to the cellar press under the chandler's shop with word for Odile, over my objection and against every instinct I owned. "If they take me," Marta said at the door, gripping my wrist with her compositor's fingers, ink still in the creases of her knuckles, "they will take an old woman who delivers candles, which is nothing. If they take you, they take the mark, and the mark is the whole voyage. Go upstairs. Be a niece. You have been practicing being invisible for seven years—today is the day it pays."

And so I sat upstairs at the kitchen table with a ledger open in front of me, doing sums that meant nothing, while Agatha answered the door.

Inspector Hale filled the doorway the way cold air fills a room. He was not a large man, but he had the trick of seeming larger than the frame that held him, and his eyes went around the kitchen—over the ledger, over me, over Agatha—with the unhurried thoroughness of a man taking inventory for a seizure.

"Mistress Crane," he said. "Forgive the intrusion. I'm conducting inquiries regarding certain printed matter of a seditious nature. You'll have seen the broadsheets—the ones bearing a device of a compass inside a lantern."

"I don't read sedition," said Agatha. "It's bad for the digestion." She stepped back from the door, which was either an invitation or an insult, and Hale chose to treat it as an invitation, because men like Hale always did.

He came into the kitchen. His gaze crossed mine for one full second, and I felt that second the way you feel a blade pass near your cheekbone—a pressure of displaced air, a coldness, no cut. I looked back down at my ledger and turned a page and wrote a figure in the margin, some nonsense number, my hand steady because seven years of hiding had trained my hands better than any soldier's drill.

"This is my niece," said Agatha, before he could ask. "Hazel. She keeps the accounts. Hazel, fetch the inspector a chair."

I fetched the inspector a chair. My hands wanted to shake and I did not permit it; I had discovered long ago that terror and craft use the same muscles, and if I held my body the way I held a graver—elbows in, breath shallow and even—it could not betray me.

"Your household employs no men?" Hale asked, settling onto the chair like a magistrate assuming a bench.

"My husband is seven years dead. My brother-in-law with him, God rest them both. The shop has a compositor, old Marta Ostrow, who comes days. There is no cutter at Crane & Sons, inspector, except what remains of my husband's work. We sell from stock. We haven't cut new faces since before the fever year."

"That's interesting," said Hale, "because the Vantry foundry in the capital holds a register of type commissions, and theirs shows a series of original punches supplied by Crane & Sons within the last three years. Recent work. Fine work, I'm told. The sort of work only a handful of cutters in Verrance could produce." He drew from his coat a folded broadsheet—I knew it before he opened it, the resistance's declaration, the lantern-and-compass stamped crisp and black at its head, a thousand copies loose in the city by now—and laid it on my clean kitchen table, on top of my honest ledger, like a stain. "Someone cut that device. Recently. Skillfully. Someone with access to a cutting bench and the knowledge to use it."

"Then someone is using my husband's name," said Agatha.

The room went very quiet. I kept my eyes on the ledger. Through the window I could see the rope walk, the two shapes of Hale's men pretending to loiter, a gull hanging motionless over the harbor wind.

"Explain," said Hale.

Agatha folded her hands. She had put on the gray shawl she wore to funerals, I realized; she had dressed for this the way other people dress for court. "After Josiah died, I found among his effects a box of unfinished punches and a notebook of designs in his hand. Among them a sketch of a compass rose inside a lantern—he drew it years ago for a festival emblem, the Harbor Illumination of '41, the committee rejected it as too busy. I sold work from his stock under his name because a widow selling her dead husband's remaining inventory is charity, inspector, and a widow admitting she has no income at all is a debtor. If that device appears on seditious print, then someone has stolen my husband's design and used his dies, or copied them, and I would be grateful to know who, since it is evidently costing me visits from the census office at my dinner hour."

Every word of it was a lie, and every word of it was delivered in the voice of a woman who had spent fifty years reading balance sheets aloud to men who thought arithmetic was above her understanding. Hale turned to look at her fully, and I watched him do the thing I had watched him do in queues and doorways all my life: search for the crack, the tremor, the seam. Agatha gave him nothing. She gave him less than nothing. She gave him the mild, faintly impatient face of a woman who had buried a husband and a brother-in-law and a brother and intended to be buried herself in due course and found the whole business tedious.

"The notebook," Hale said. "This design. I'll need to see it."

"You'll need a magistrate's warrant," said Agatha pleasantly, "and my solicitor, Mr. Pell, will want to be present, and he charges by the hour, which I will deduct from the city's compensation for the harassment of a war widow. Shall I send for him?"

I had never loved anyone so much in my life.

Hale stood. He took the broadsheet back, folded it along its existing creases with two precise fingers. His eyes came to me one more time, and this time they stayed.

"You keep the accounts," he said. "For a print shop. You'd know the commissions. You'd know what work passed through, whose hand cut it."

"I know the figures," I said. My voice came out level, low, bored. Seven years of practice, spent in a single sentence. "The names I wasn't asked about. Mistress Crane handles the names."

"She does," agreed Agatha, opening the kitchen door. "Good afternoon, inspector. Mind the third stair; it talks more than I do."

He paused in the doorway—one heartbeat, two—and I understood with terrible clarity that he did not believe a word of it, that he was filing us away in whatever cabinet he kept instead of a soul, that the third stair's creak under his boot was the sound of our name being entered in a ledger of his own. Then he was gone down the stairs, and out the shop door, and across to the rope walk where his men fell in beside him like punctuation.

* * *

We did not speak until his footsteps had faded entirely and another quarter hour besides, in case of listeners, in case of habits, in case the walls of the house had grown ears along with everything else. Agatha sank onto the kitchen chair Hale had vacated and, for the first time in my living memory, her hands were not folded. They were shaking. Just slightly. Just at the fingertips, the way a struck bell goes on trembling after the sound has stopped.

"You knew," I said again, helplessly. "You knew, and you let me think—"

"I let you think what kept you safe," she said. "That was the arrangement, though neither of us ever spoke it. You hid; I dusted. Now the hiding's worn through." She looked up at me, and beneath the funeral-gray composure something else moved—something fierce and tired and ancient. "Do you know what I did all those Tuesdays, child, dusting ledgers I knew were fiction? I read them. Your father's first punch, the Halvard commission, the hymnal faces—every one of them yours, every one of them beautiful, and no one in this city will ever see your name at the bottom of any of it unless you put it there yourself. I decided a long time ago that if the day ever came when the lie failed, I would stand in front of you and lie louder. Today it came."

"He'll be back," I said. "With the warrant. With soldiers."

"Then we won't be here with the things he wants." She rose, brisk now, the tremor folded away somewhere with the shawl's true purpose. "Get the oilcloth bundle from the flour bin. Not the toolbox—the bundle, the new punch, the one you cut for them. It goes to the cellar press tonight, or to Odile's ship, or into the harbor, anywhere that isn't here. And you—" she caught my sleeve as I turned, and her grip was stronger than her hands had any right to be. "Hazel. Whatever you're carrying for those people, whatever debt you've taken on, hear me: I claimed your work as mine today and I will claim it again tomorrow and the day after, in front of magistrates if it comes to that, and I am an old woman whom nobody looks at, and that has always been the most useful thing about me. But you must promise me the only thing I've ever actually asked you for."

"Name it," I said.

"When it's done—when the ship sails, when it's finished—sign something. Anything. One commission, one proof sheet, your own name in your own hand, and let me witness it before I die." Her voice did not waver, but her eyes had gone bright and terrible. "Seven years I've watched you erase yourself. I'm done permitting it."

Down in the street, faint through the fogged glass, I heard boots again—two pairs, passing the shop, slowing, passing on. Hale's men walking their rounds. Or the beginning of the round that would end at our door with a warrant and a fire.

I promised her. Then I went for the flour bin, and the bundle, and the dark.
