## 18. What Elias Buried

The census office smelled of tallow and wet wool the morning after the bridge, and Elias Wren found that he could no longer sit at his desk without seeing Hazel Crane's hands.

They had crossed the Bridge of Chains together in the grey hour before dawn, a census ledger under her arm full of nothing but folded decree copies, and he had spoken to the guardsmen in the flat bureaucratic music of his office—*harbor survey, section nine, seal of the Assistant Registrar*—and they had believed him, because men believe paper. And afterward, standing on the far bank while gulls argued over the fish gutting tables below, she had not thanked him. She had simply looked at him for a long moment, the fog beading in her dark hair, and said, "You've done this before."

Not *you're good at this*. Not *that was quick thinking*. She had said: *you've done this before.* The way one says it of an old wound. Of a scar that aches before rain.

He had not answered her then. He had touched his hat brim, and walked up Rope Street with his ledger case swinging at his hip like a pendulum counting out something other than hours, and he had gone to work and misfiled four documents in a row, which for Elias Wren was the equivalent of screaming.

Now it was evening, and the office was empty except for the stove's low mutter and the rain beginning against the shutters, and he was walking through Verrance toward the print shop with a bottle of plum brandy under his arm—the kind of small, honest bribe old sailors brought to card games—and rehearsing words he had never said aloud to anyone.

Twelve years. He had carried it twelve years, the way a man carries a stone in his boot on a long march: first agonizing, then merely painful, then so familiar he had forgotten he could put it down.

* * *

The print shop was lit from within like a hearth seen across a winter field. Lamplight spilled gold through the diamond panes onto the cobbles of Bindery Lane, and beneath the smell of rain Elias caught the shop's own weather—ink and oil and hot metal, the particular sharp sweetness of molten type alloy that had been the background scent of his whole childhood in this quarter. He stood outside longer than he meant to, listening.

Inside, voices. Hazel's, low and precise. Marta's, cracked and laughing. A boy's voice—Tam, the runner, who talked the way water runs downhill—and beneath them all the rhythmic breathing of the hand press being worked, *thump-shhh, thump-shhh*, like a great animal sleeping.

Elias raised his hand to knock, and could not.

*You have no right,* said the voice in him that sounded like his father. *You are the man who counted households for a tyrant. You are the ink-stained finger that pointed.*

But another voice answered—the one that had been growing louder these three weeks, since the morning he'd held that pamphlet with its compass rose cut into its lantern and felt the floor of his certainty give way—and it said: *Then you owe her the truth at minimum. Truth is the only coin you carry that isn't stolen.*

He knocked.

* * *

Marta let him in, wiping compositional ink on her apron, sizing him up with eyes like awls. "The census man," she said, without hostility and without welcome. "Come to count our spoons?"

"Come to count nothing," Elias said. "I've come to tell Hazel something. If she'll hear it."

Hazel was at the correcting table, a galley proof before her, a bodkin in her hand with which she made corrections more delicate than any surgeon. She looked up. In the lamplight he could see the fine metal dust that never entirely left her fingers, silvering the creases of her knuckles like frost. She set down the bodkin.

"Marta," she said, "would you take Tam through the sort cases? He still can't tell a ligature from a swash."

"He cannot," Marta agreed, and shepherded the protesting boy toward the back racks, and though Elias suspected she missed nothing of what would follow, she gave them the fiction of privacy the way she gave everything else—efficiently, and without commentary.

Hazel did not invite him to sit, exactly, but she moved a stool back from the correcting table with her foot, which amounted to the same thing. He sat. He set the brandy on the table between them like evidence entered into record.

"That's a bribe," she said.

"Yes."

"For what?"

"For what I'm about to ask you to do," Elias said. "Which is listen."

She waited. That was a thing he had learned about her in these weeks of conspiracy—that she could wait with her whole body, that silence was a tool she kept as sharp as any graver. Around them the shop ticked and settled, the stove ticking in the corner, the press cooling, the rain working at the shutters.

"Twelve years ago," Elias began, and his voice came out wrong, too formal, the registrar's voice, and he stopped and started again. "I was nineteen. I had just taken the census examination. I was second in my cohort." He heard himself say it and nearly laughed at the absurdity—*second in my cohort*—the pathetic pride of it, still living in him like a splinter. "My father had been a clerk before me, and his father before him. We were a family of ledgers. I believed—I need you to understand this, not to forgive it but to weigh it properly—I believed with my entire being that records were sacred. That a thing written correctly became true, and a thing true became good. I thought I was serving order. I thought order was the same thing as justice."

"They usually arrive together," Hazel said quietly. "For people who've never needed to doubt either."

"Yes." He looked at his hands. They were steady; they were always steady; that steadiness had once been the death of a man. "There was a printer in Salt Row. His name was Anselm Ferrow—not related to—" He waved a hand, dismissing the coincidence of names that had haunted him since learning of the mechanic Ferro, as if the world were playing variations on his guilt. "No relation. Anselm Ferrow printed ballads. Satirical ones, mostly, about the harbor fees. Bad poetry, honestly. Terrible rhymes. I knew him slightly; he sold sheets to our office boy. And one day a directive came down—a new ordinance requiring all print shops to register their presses, their typefaces, their output, quarterly. It was framed as tax administration. I believed that. I *wanted* to believe it, which is worse than believing it."

Hazel's face did not change, but her stillness changed quality. He pressed on, because if he stopped now he would never begin again.

"I conducted the registration myself. Salt Row was in my district. I counted Anselm Ferrow's press, and I measured his type cases, and I recorded the marks on his forms, and I noticed—he had a mark, you see, a little device he stamped in the colophon of every sheet, a thistle inside a gearwheel—and I wrote it down. Because it was there, and recording things was what I did. Three months later the ordinance was amended. Retroactively. Registration data was transferred to the Inspectorate of Public Order." He swallowed. "You know what happened next, or you can imagine it. They came for printers whose marks matched seditious sheets. There was a list. Anselm Ferrow's thistle was on it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the back of the shop, Tam's voice rose in protest—*"but why does the f-f-flourish go there"*—and Marta's answer rumbled too low to catch.

"He was tried in the spring," Elias said. "It was a public hanging. They were public in those days; the Governor wanted the quarter to see. And I went, because I thought—I told myself—that not going would be cowardice. That a man should witness the consequences of accurate work." His voice failed on the last words, and he let it fail. "He saw me in the crowd. Salt Row is not large. He saw me, and he did not curse me, which I think would have been easier to survive. He looked at me the way one looks at weather. At something that had simply happened to him."

He had not meant to say that part. It came out of the place where he kept it, twelve years deep, still bleeding.

"And afterward I requested transfer to the archives. Then back to the field. Then the archives again. I volunteered for every recount, every redistricting survey, every tedious audit no one else wanted. People thought I was ambitious. My superiors praised my thoroughness." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound even to his own ears. "I learned every street in Verrance household by household, and I told myself I was doing penance by knowing the city truly. By knowing where the widows lived who couldn't pay the hearth tax. Where the children slept six to a bed. I memorized the miseries my paperwork served, and I called it conscience."

Hazel picked up the brandy bottle, poured two small measures into cups that had recently held tea, and pushed one toward him. Her hand did not shake. His, he noticed distantly, did.

"Why are you telling me this now?" she asked.

"Because on the bridge," Elias said, "you saw that I had done this before. Lied with authority. And you were right, and I could not bear for you to think—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "I could not bear to take your trust while wearing a clean shirt. The shirt is not clean, Hazel. It has never been clean. When I use my office to shield your smuggled decrees, I am not a good man finally doing good. I am a man returning stolen goods."

* * *

She drank, slowly. He watched her turn his confession over the way she must turn a proof sheet to the light, checking for flaws, for forgery, for the tell-tale double-struck letter that betrayed a counterfeit.

"You said he didn't curse you," she said at last. "Anselm Ferrow. What did the crowd do?"

"Nothing. Crowds do nothing. That's the horror of them. Two hundred people watching a bad poet die for rhyme and thistles, and the silence was total, and in the silence a bell rang somewhere—the harbor bell, eight o'clock—and I have never since heard a bell without—" He gestured vaguely at his own chest, unable to finish.

"My brother," Hazel said.

He looked up.

"Corin. You've heard the name—Crane & Sons. Corin Crane was the master cutter. Everyone in this quarter believes it still, including, until very recently, the census." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only the terrible economy of a person deciding to pay a debt in kind. "Corin drowned when I was seventeen. Before that, for six years, every punch and matrix that left this shop bearing the Crane mark was cut by me, and signed by him, because my father believed—and the whole world believed—that a girl's hand couldn't hold a graver. So when you say you wore a false shirt, Elias Wren, know that you're drinking brandy with a forged name. We are both counterfeits. The difference is that mine kept people alive and yours killed a man."

The words landed like hammers on cold type—precise, unsoftened, meant to bite. He took them as they were meant. But then she set down her cup, and leaned forward, and her voice changed.

"But here is what I think you actually came to ask. Not forgiveness—you don't believe you deserve it, I can see that from the moon. You came to ask whether knowing what you are changes what we're doing. Whether you should take your guilty shirt and go home, and leave the smuggling to people whose hands are cleaner."

"Isn't that a fair question?"

"It's the wrong question," Hazel said. "The question isn't whether your hands are clean. Nobody's are. Marta spent twenty years setting pious broadsheets for the Governor's church while hating every line. Odile would smuggle anything for coin and says so. Tam lies professionally and enjoys it. You want to know if your guilt disqualifies you." She shook her head slowly. "Guilt doesn't disqualify. It qualifies. It's the only credential some of us have. The dead man you watched hang—he wanted the truth printed. That's all. Ballads, satire, the small insolent truth of bad rhymes about harbor fees. And now we have the chance to move a much larger truth onto a ship, and you're telling me the man carrying it once served the lie." She turned her cup in her fingers. "Good. Then you know exactly what we're fighting. You've shaken its hand. You've been its pen."

Elias found that his eyes were burning, and that he was too tired and too hollowed-out to be ashamed of it.

"Anselm Ferrow," he said. "When they asked him at the scaffold whether he had anything to say, he said—" and here twelve years of rehearsal finally discharged themselves, the sentence he had carried so long it had worn a groove in him— "he said, *'Tell the truth anyway.'* That was all. Tell the truth anyway. And everyone thought it was defiance of the Governor, and it was, but I have always known—I have known for twelve years—it was addressed to me."

The room held still around the words. Rain, stove, the settling of metal type in its cases like a slow tide.

"Then that's your job," Hazel said.

"What?"

"On the Morning Tide. There will be testimony—mine, Marta's, yours. Your name goes in the deposition, Elias. Your account of how the census was weaponized, how registration became a hunt list. Signed." She held his gaze. "Tell the truth anyway. To the capital, in writing, under your own name. That's the only forgiveness on offer, and it isn't mine to give. It's his."

Elias Wren sat in the lamplight of Crane & Sons and felt the stone shift in his boot—not gone, nothing would ever make it gone, but loosened, movable, transformed from a secret into a purpose. He thought of the ledger he would write. He had already, God help him, begun composing it years ago in sleepless nights, never dreaming it would ever leave his head.

"All right," he said. His voice was rough. "Signed. Under my own name."

"Good." Hazel stood, and the conversation was clearly over, folded and pigeonholed like a finished proof. But at the correcting table she paused, and without looking at him, added: "For what it's worth—my father used to say a misprint caught early costs almost nothing. It's the one you let stand and reprint a thousand times that ruins the edition." A beat. "You caught yours. Late. But you caught it."

From the back of the shop came a crash, a squawk from Tam, and Marta's voice raised in exasperation—*"those are fourteen-point bodoni caps, you small catastrophe, gather them in ORDER"*—and the ordinary life of the shop resumed around them like water closing over a dropped keel.

Elias stood to go, and had his hand on the door latch when Marta emerged from the sort racks, wiping her hands, and fixed him with those awl-point eyes.

"Census man," she said. "Before you go. There's word from the cellar. Odile's crew says the Inspectorate bought itself a new pair of eyes this week—an informant inside the trade. Someone who knows presses." Her voice was mild as milk. "Someone who knows *marks*."

The cold moved through Elias like a draft under a door. He thought of Ferro the mechanic, of his careful hands, of the coincidence of names that had pricked at him for weeks.

"Hazel," he said quietly, turning back into the lamplight. "We have a problem."
