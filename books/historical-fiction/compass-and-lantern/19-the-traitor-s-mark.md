## 19. The Traitor's Mark

Tam Voss had three rules for running messages through Verrance, and he recited them to himself the way other boys recited prayers. *Never take the same street twice. Never look at what you carry. Never trust a door that opens too fast.*

He was breaking none of them on the morning after the fire, which was why it stung so badly when the truth found him anyway — not through cunning or courage, but because a boy who delivered everything eventually delivered himself into the one room he should never have entered.

The chandler's shop above the cellar press smelled of tallow and scorched iron that morning. The smell had crept up through the floorboards overnight, sweetish and wrong, like meat left too long near a lamp. Tam noticed it first when he came down Rope Walk with a basket of rolls balanced on his hip, whistling the fish-gutters' tune, feeling important in the way he always felt important when the morning's bread was his to deliver. Bread meant the press was running. Bread meant Hazel had slept in the cellar again, curled under her coat beside the stone steps with her hands still ink-stained even in sleep.

The chandler's front room was shuttered. Old Bess the chandler had gone to her sister's in Saltmarsh for the week, leaving the cellar to Odile's arrangement, and Tam had the key on a string around his neck where it lay against his chest like a second heartbeat. He let himself in through the alley door, as instructed, and stood a moment in the dark letting his eyes adjust, listening.

Listening was rule four, the unwritten one. Listen before you knock. Listen before you speak. A runner who listened lived longer than a runner who talked, and Tam intended to live until he was at least fourteen.

Below, someone was moving. Not the quick, purposeful sounds of Marta setting type — Marta moved like water finding its level, all patience and no wasted motion. This was heavier. Dragging. Something being shifted across stone with a low grinding scrape, then silence, then the scrape again.

Tam set down the bread.

He told himself afterward that he'd only gone down to see if help was needed. He told himself that for a long time, actually, right up until the night he stopped telling himself anything and simply knew: he had gone down because the sound was wrong, and wrong sounds were his business now. He was the ears of the cellar press. Nobody had said so out loud, but he'd made himself believe it, the way you make yourself believe you're brave by walking the dark streets until the dark gets bored of you.

The stairs were narrow and smelled of smoke that shouldn't be there. The cellar press occupied the old brine-storage vault beneath the chandler's shop, a low brick room where the sea used to come seeping through the walls before somebody, decades back, had laid a floor of ship's ballast stone over the mud. The press itself sat in the center like a fat black beetle — Odile's smuggled Albion, its iron frame sweating salt-damp, its screw gleaming where Hazel oiled it every third day without fail. Racks of type lined the walls. Paper hung from lines strung overhead like strange grey laundry, sheets of the resistance's handbills drying in the cellar chill.

And in the corner, where the matrix rack had stood — the rack Hazel had built herself, mortised from an old ship's locker, holding the punches and matrices she guarded more carefully than her own name — there was a black wound in the wall.

The fire had eaten the rack to charcoal. It had climbed the bricks above it in a fan of soot, licked along a ceiling beam, and died there, starved, leaving the rest of the cellar untouched. Someone had dragged the wreckage into a corner and thrown sacking over it, but the smell rose through the cloth like a confession.

Ferro knelt at the base of the wall with a bucket and a brush, scrubbing soot off the stones.

He didn't hear Tam on the stairs. He was a big man, Ferro, with forearms like mooring posts and a beard going grey at the chin, and he worked the way he did everything — slowly, thoroughly, with his whole weight behind it. He'd been the press mechanic since before Tam joined the runners; he'd rebuilt the Albion's screw thread when it stripped in the first month, and he'd taught Tam how to pack a bearing with grease without getting it in his cuffs. He hummed while he worked, an old rope-walk song about a girl from the Salt Isles, and he was humming it now, low and steady, scrubbing soot off a wall as if cleanliness could bring back what had burned.

Tam stood on the bottom step and looked at the covered wreck and the scrubbed wall and the humming man, and something cold walked up his spine on small feet.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he would turn over and over in his mind for days afterward, in the fish market and the rope walks and the dark of his cot in Odile's loft: the fire had happened two nights ago. Two nights, and nobody had touched that corner except to smother it. Hazel had stood over the ashes with her face white as fresh paper and said, quietly, in the voice she used when she was most angry, *"This wasn't chance."* And everyone had nodded, and everyone had looked at each other, and nobody had looked at Ferro.

But Ferro was scrubbing the wall *now*. At dawn. Alone. Before anyone else came down. Scrubbing soot off stones that no amount of scrubbing could make clean, in a cellar where the fire had already been pronounced murder.

"Morning, Tam."

Tam nearly fell down the last three steps. Ferro hadn't turned around. He'd heard him — of course he'd heard him, the stairs announced everyone — and he kept brushing, steady as tide.

"You're up early," Tam said. His voice came out higher than he wanted. He cleared his throat and tried again, aiming for the easy chirp he used with customs men and harbor guards. "Brought the rolls. Marta likes hers burnt on one side, I put them on top."

"Kind of you." The brush kept moving. Slop, slop, slop, against the stone. "Set them on the press bed. Mind the ink."

Tam set the basket down. He should have gone back up. Rule five, the one he invented that morning and never wrote down because writing it down would mean admitting it existed: *when a grown man is cleaning up a thing that was already cleaned, leave.*

Instead he heard himself say, "Shame about the rack."

The brush stopped.

For the space of three heartbeats the cellar was silent except for the drip of the tide-channels somewhere behind the far wall, the old brine runs that still filled and emptied with the harbour, a sound like slow breathing. Tam watched Ferro's broad back. He watched the man's shoulders rise once, deliberately, the way a man breathes when he is putting something away inside himself.

"Aye," Ferro said. "A shame." He turned around then, and he smiled, and it was a good smile, a warm smile, the smile of a man who had taught a boy to grease bearings. "Hazel took it hard. Those matrices were half her life."

"She cut some of 'em herself." Tam nodded at the covered wreck. "The compass ones. Took her a week, Marta said. Week of sitting up past midnight with her gravers, not talking to nobody."

"That's craft for you." Ferro wrung out the brush. Water ran grey down his wrist. "You spend a week making a thing and one bad hour unmakes it. That's why I stick to iron. Iron forgives."

Tam laughed, because it was expected, because Ferro was Ferro, because the alternative was standing in a smoke-smelling cellar with the cold thing on his spine growing teeth. But his eyes went, without his permission, to the floor beside the bucket.

There was a smell in this cellar that didn't belong to tallow or smoke or salt. It was sharp and resinous, sweet at the edges, and Tam knew it the way every child of the Verrance docks knew it: pitch-oil. Lamp oil cut with pine pitch, the cheap kind, the kind the harbor lamps burned, the kind that spluttered and clung and *stuck* to whatever you poured it on. It wasn't sold in shops this deep in the warren districts. You bought it from the lamplighters' yard off Chain Lane, or you stole it, and either way your boots carried the smell of it for days.

There was a dark ring on the ballast stone near the burned corner. Old, mostly scrubbed — scrubbed with the same diligence as the wall — but oil doesn't scrub out of porous stone, not entirely, not ever. And the ring was wide. Wider than a spill. Wide as the arc a man's arm makes when he swings a can back and forth, laying a trail.

Tam looked at the ring. He looked at Ferro's boots, standing square and patient on the stones, leather cracked and salt-stained. He thought, very clearly and very calmly, the way he thought when a dragoon's shadow fell across an alley mouth: *his boots don't smell of pitch-oil.*

They'd been washed.

* * *

He got out of the cellar the way he'd learned to get out of everywhere — sideways, chattering, harmless. He made a joke about Marta's burnt rolls. He said Odile wanted a count of dry paper stock by noon. He climbed the stairs two at a time and let himself out into the grey morning light of Rope Walk and stood there in the fish-and-tar smell of the city breathing like a man who'd been underwater.

Then he did what Tam Voss did best. He ran. But he ran *thinking*, which was new, and thinking while running turned out to be like carrying two baskets at once — possible, but it changed your balance.

Here was what he knew, laid out in his head like type in a composing stick:

One. The fire had started in the matrix rack, dead center, in the middle of the night when nobody was below. Cellar fires didn't start themselves in the middle of iron racks. There was nothing in that rack to burn but wood and paper and a drop of oil, and Hazel kept the oil tin stoppered and shelved across the room. Somebody had brought fire to it.

Two. Pitch-oil. The ring on the stone. Somebody had *laid* the fire, laid it careful, enough to destroy the rack and not enough to burn the press — because burning the press would bring the watch, and inspectors, and questions, and the whole cellar pulled apart by lantern-light. Whoever set it had wanted exactly one thing destroyed and nothing else disturbed.

Three. Whoever set it had known the cellar. Known where Hazel's rack stood, known the oil tin was across the room, known the hours the cellar slept, known that the chandler was away and the stairs creaked on the fourth step only if you took them fast. A stranger would have burned the whole place by accident. Only family burns things this precisely.

Four. Ferro had been alone in the cellar at dawn, erasing a fire that had already been erased, humming a rope-walk song.

Tam stopped at the mouth of Fish Gut Alley, where the gutting tables ran with silver scales and the gulls screamed their endless greedy argument, and he leaned against a post and felt something crack in his chest that he didn't have a name for yet.

It wasn't fear. Fear he knew; fear was an old friend, fear was the taste in his mouth at every checkpoint, fear was the reason he was alive at twelve years old in a city that ate messenger boys for breakfast. This was worse. This was the feeling of a floor giving way — not the street, not the city, but the particular small floor he'd built for himself out of the people in the cellar. Marta who saved him rolls. Odile who paid him honest and taught him knots. Hazel who'd looked at him the first week and said, *you've good eyes, boy, use them,* and he had walked taller for a month. And Ferro. Big slow Ferro, who fixed things. Who had shown him how the screw thread caught, turning the iron wheel with Tam's small hands under his big ones, saying *feel that? That's the whole press in one motion. Everything turns on what catches.*

Everything turns on what catches. And something in Tam had caught that morning on a scrubbed wall and a washed boot-heel, and now the whole wheel was turning wrong.

* * *

He spent the day doing his rounds, because rounds were the shape his panic took. He carried Marta's letter to the sailmaker on Quay Street. He carried Odile's tally-book to the tide-watcher on the breakwater. He carried a folded handbill sewn into the lining of his cap past a checkpoint guard who patted his shoulder and called him *sonny* and never knew he'd patted a printing house. And all day, underneath the errands, he watched.

He watched Ferro at midday, when the mechanic came up into the chandler's shop to eat, sitting on the loading step with bread and cheese, looking out at Rope Walk with an expression Tam had never bothered to read before because he'd never needed to. Now he read it. It was the look of a man watching a road for a carriage. Not nervous. *Waiting.* Patient as a mooring post.

He watched Ferro talk to a carter from the coal yards, laughing, slapping the man's shoulder — ordinary, all ordinary — except that the carter's route ran past the Customs House, and the Customs House was Inspector Hale's parish, and Hale's men drank at the Anchor and Chain where carters drank, and information in Verrance traveled the way water traveled: downhill, silently, filling every low place.

None of it proved anything. Tam knew that. It was a rope of might-bes, and he could have unraveled it with one good tug of doubt — *maybe he scrubs because he's ashamed the fire happened on his watch, maybe the oil ring is old, maybe waiting is just waiting.* He held that rope in his mind all afternoon and tried to make himself let go of it.

Then, at dusk, he followed the wrong instinct home.

Ferro left the chandler's shop as the fog lamps were lit, walking heavy and unhurried toward the Anchor and Chain, and Tam — rule six, invented on the spot, broken forever after — *followed a man instead of a message.*

He kept to the gutters and the shadow-lines, the skills Odile had drilled into him until they lived in his knees. He hung back forty feet, then thirty, using the evening crowd as cover, a small boy among hundreds of small boys all streaming homeward through the blue fog. Ferro went past the Anchor and Chain without going in. Past the ropewalk gate. Down toward the harbor stairs, where the fog came off the water thick as wool.

At the foot of the stairs, under the last working lamp, a man waited. Tam knew the silhouette before he knew the face — the long coat, the neat hat, the way the man stood with his heels together like the ground was a parade ground. A customs man. Not just any: Tam had memorized the faces that mattered, and this one belonged to Inspector Hale's own corridor, a clerk-officer named Dree who carried papers and never smiled.

Ferro and Dree spoke for less time than it takes to drink a glass. Tam couldn't hear the words — the fog ate them — but he saw Ferro's hand move, saw something small pass between them, saw Dree tuck it away inside his coat with the flat efficiency of a man pocketing a debt. Then Dree turned and went up the harbor stairs toward the Customs House, and Ferro stood alone under the lamp a moment, rolling his shoulders as if setting down a weight, and Tam saw his face full in the lamplight.

It wasn't a traitor's face. That was the terrible thing, the thing Tam would carry longest. It was tired. It was the face of a man coming out from under a load, and it looked — Tam searched for the word and hated himself for finding it — it looked *relieved*, the way a man looks relieved when a fever breaks, even if the fever cost him something he can't get back.

Ferro went home. Tam stayed crouched behind a bollard until the fog closed over the big man's back, and then he sat down on the wet stones of the harbor stair and hugged his knees and shook, quietly, the way he'd taught himself to shake when he was smaller — silently, completely, so no one would ever know.

Because he understood it now, or the beginning of it, the way you understand drowning by swallowing. Ferro had a brother. Everyone in the cellar knew that much — the brother in the Marsh Gaol, sentenced on a smuggling charge two winters back, the brother Ferro sent money to whenever a job paid extra, the brother whose letters came smelling of gaol-mildew and hope. Odile had tried to buy his release twice through lawyers and once through bribery and failed all three times, because the Marsh Gaol answered to the Governor's courts, and the Governor's courts answered to nothing at all.

And Inspector Hale held the keys to the Marsh Gaol's paperwork.

Tam pressed his forehead against his knees. He could see it whole now, the way you see a whole picture when you finally step back far enough: a big man with a brother rotting cell by cell, and a polite inspector who never threatened, never forced, only *asked* — asked about shipments, asked about faces, asked, lately, about a print mark, a compass inside a lantern — and offered, in exchange, a transfer order, a pardon, a brother walking free into the fog. And what was a rack of matrices against a man's blood? What was any of it? Hazel's week of midnight gravers, Marta's patient type, the whole cellar's dangerous hope — weighed against one flesh-and-blood man in a stone room, and what would Tam himself have chosen, if it were *his* brother, if it were *his* hand on the oil can?

That was the crack in his chest, opening wider. Not that Ferro was wicked. That Ferro was *breakable*, the way everything was breakable, the way Tam was breakable, and that the breaking hadn't even required cruelty — just love, bent slightly, aimed at a lock.

Adults broke in complicated ways. That was the lesson, and it was a bad lesson, and he wanted to give it back.

* * *

The fog had thickened to soup by the time Tam climbed out of the harbor district, and he made himself walk the long way, past the lantern-maker's row where a hundred glass flames swung in the windows, because he needed to see light that belonged to no conspiracy. He thought about who to tell. Odile would want Ferro strung up by his own crane-hook — Odile trusted like a knife cuts, all edge, once. Marta would weep and then forgive and then be fooled again. Hazel —

Hazel would understand.

That was the strangest thought of the whole long day, arriving last and settling deepest. Hazel, who hid her own name in ledgers, who had spent her whole life learning what it cost to be unseen — Hazel would look at Ferro's washed boots and tired face and she would not see a monster. She would see the arithmetic. She would hate the answer and still do the sum.

But telling her was a weapon too, and Tam had learned today that weapons pointed both ways. If he told her wrong, in the wrong words, Ferro would bolt to Hale and the cellar would burn for true, press and all, maybe with people in it. If he told her right — if he brought her proof, clean and undeniable, the way she brought proofs off her press, sharp-edged and exact —

Proof. That was the word that lifted him off the lantern-maker's step and set him running again, cold air burning in his lungs, fog streaming past his ears.

Dree had taken something small from Ferro's hand. Papers, most likely. Ferro couldn't write more than his name; Tam had seen him labor over a parts list like a man swimming upstream. So the something was written by another hand — a report, a list, a confession in ink. And papers, once passed, had to go somewhere. They went to the Customs House, into the evening intake, into the tray system Tam had watched a hundred times from the coal carts: the night box on the second-floor landing, emptied at dawn by the duty clerk, and before dawn —

Before dawn, the box sat full and unguarded for one hour, while the clerks took their breakfast in the canteen below, and the landing window on the harbor side had a latch Tam's fingers had already met once, last spring, delivering a note for Odile.

Tam stood in the fog outside the Customs House, small and breathless and twelve years old, looking up at the lit window of the second-floor landing where the night box waited like a lantern with something trapped inside it.

Tomorrow, before the clerks finished their bread, he would read what Ferro had given Hale. He would carry it to Hazel the way he carried everything — fast, whole, and burning his hands.

Above him, behind the frosted glass, a shadow crossed the landing and paused. Long coat. Neat hat. Heels together, like the ground was a parade ground.

Inspector Hale, it seemed, was working late.
