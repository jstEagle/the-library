## 20. Ferrocement

The chandler's cellar smelled of tallow and wet stone, and tonight, beneath those familiar comforts, of something ranker — the smell of men arguing about what to do with a traitor.

Hazel felt it before she understood it, the way she could feel a bad casting in a mold before she ever lifted the lid: a wrongness in the air pressure of the room. She came down the ladder with her satchel of tools against her hip and found them all gathered around the press like mourners around a bed. Odile perched on the stone lip of the oil cistern, boots swinging, a knife lying on the stone beside her not as threat but as punctuation. Marta stood with her back against the composing frame, arms crossed over her apron, jaw set like someone chewing a word she refused to spit out. Tam sat cross-legged near the stairs, uncharacteristically silent, his runner's cap crushed in his fists.

And at the center, bound to a chair with the same cord they used to tie bundle paper, sat Ferro.

He was smaller than Hazel remembered. That was the first thing that struck her, absurdly — that treachery should come in such an ordinary shape. A broad-shouldered mechanic with ink-stained knuckles and grease worked so deep into the creases of his hands it would outlive him. He had rebuilt the press's flywheel twice since Hazel had known him. He had once spent four hours on his back beneath the platen, swearing softly in harbor dialect, coaxing a stripped gear back to life with wire and stubbornness. His hands were beautiful, for a mechanic's hands. They lay now in his lap, tied at the wrist, and they did not move at all.

"So," Hazel said, and set her satchel down by the stairs with a click of brass against stone. Every head turned. "I missed something."

"Read your morning delivery?" Odile asked. Her voice was light, which meant it was dangerous.

Tam flinched. Hazel looked at him — really looked — and saw it there, the burning-hands thing he carried, the thing he'd promised to bring her fast and whole. He hadn't brought it at all. He'd brought everyone else to it instead.

"Show me," Hazel said.

* * *

The night box copy was passed around the room twice before it reached her, as if handling it too long might scald. When it finally came to her hands she read it standing under the single whale-oil lamp, and the words arranged themselves into a shape she knew intimately, because she had cut the original of it herself.

Ferro had given Hale the shipping schedule of the *Morning Tide*. Not the cargo manifest — that much, at least, Odile had kept close — but the berth assignments, the tide tables, the window of three days in which the ship would sit in Verrance harbor taking on legitimate freight while its illegal freight waited somewhere between here and the quay. He had given Hale the location of the second checkpoint drop. He had given Hale, in his careful mechanic's blockhand, a description of *the cutter*: a woman, young, works at Crane & Sons, cuts a compass rose inside a lantern.

He hadn't known her name. Small mercies. Hale would know it soon enough anyway; the man could read a customs ledger as well as anyone.

"He did it for his brother," Tam said suddenly, from the floor, his voice cracking in the middle like a boy's voice does when it has been holding still too long. "His brother's in the Marsh Gate. They told Ferro they'd — they said—"

"Nobody said anything to Ferro that Ferro didn't agree to," Odile said. "A man who sells you agrees to be sold."

"They have his brother, Odile."

"They have *leverage*. There's a difference, and the difference is that leverage only works on men who've already decided what they're worth." Odile picked up her knife, turned it once, set it down again. The gesture was unhurried, almost bored, and entirely for show. Hazel had learned to read the smuggler's hands the way she read type: everything deliberate, every motion a sentence in a language of negotiation. "So. We settle it tonight. Three ways this goes." She held up one finger. "We turn him loose and hope he lies for us when they ask questions — I don't recommend it, but it's on the table." A second finger. "We put him on a boat north with nothing but his coat, and he takes his chances with whatever Hale thinks happened to him." A third finger, and this one rose slowly, and the lamplight caught the blade beside her hand as if the two were in conversation. "Or we give him to the harbor. Men who work the water have their own way with informers, and it doesn't leave a body for the inspectors to find."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, the chandler's shop creaked as it settled into the fog-heavy evening, a normal sound in a room where nothing normal remained.

Marta spoke without uncrossing her arms. "I've buried printers before," she said. "In '09, after the bread riots. I stood in a square with two hundred other people and watched a boy of nineteen hang because somebody in his cell of six had talked, and nobody ever learned which one. I swore then I'd never again be in a room with a traitor and a choice." Her eyes went to Ferro, and there was no softness in them, none at all, but there was something older than hatred — weariness, maybe, or memory wearing weariness's face. "I should want the third thing. Everyone assumes I want the third thing." She paused. "I don't know what I want. That frightens me more than he does."

All eyes went, then, though some of them tried not to, to Hazel.

She understood why, and resented it a little. She was the newest among them, if you counted the way Elias counted things — by weeks, by small meetings, by the slow accumulation of shared risk. But she was also the one whose name was on the papers Hale wanted, whose mark was stamped in wax on every sealed plate in the strongbox, whose brother was already dead of this same disease of secrets. If anyone in the cellar had a right to vengeance, the ledger said it was her. She could feel the room leaning toward her the way type leans toward the magnet on a proofing press — click, and done, and whatever she said would be the setting in which all the other words arranged themselves.

And the terrible thing, the thing she would examine later in the dark of her narrow room above the ruined quiet of the print shop, was that her first feeling was not rage.

It was recognition.

She looked at Ferro, tied in the chair, and she saw a man who had done arithmetic. That was all treachery ever was, in her experience of watching people break — not wickedness, but arithmetic. My brother's life against your schedule. My blood against your cause. She had run the same sums herself for years, alone at the cutting bench, and she had always chosen zero: my safety against everyone else's need, my silence against the truth, my invisibility against the whole bleeding world. She had been, in her own quiet way, the most selfish creature in Verrance, and she knew it now, knew it the way you know a watermark — invisible until you hold the page up to the light.

Ferro had simply made the opposite choice on the same cold ledger. It did not excuse him. Nothing excused him; the plates he'd described to Hale would get people hanged, might yet get *her* hanged, and Tam — God, Tam, twelve years old, carrying names in his head like lit coals. Excuse was not the question. The question was what a person did with broken material, and that, at least, she knew something about.

"You don't punish bad metal," Hazel said.

Odile's eyebrows went up. Marta frowned. Tam looked from face to face like a boy watching a ball he couldn't follow.

"In the shop," Hazel said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected, "when a matrix cracks — and they crack, they do, especially in cold weather, the metal remembers being poured and it wants to go back — you don't throw it away and you don't pretend it's whole. Both are lies. You ask what the flaw is. You find the line of the fracture, and then—" she stopped, surprised by her own next words even as she said them, "—and then you cast around it. You build so the flaw carries weight it can bear and none it can't. A cracked matrix can still strike ten thousand clean impressions if you know where the crack runs. We know where Ferro's crack runs. We'd be fools to melt him down for it."

"Say it plain," Odile said, but she was smiling faintly now, the knife-flicker of a smile, because she had seen where this was going before anyone. Smugglers thought in cargo. Hazel was proposing to load a traitor like a hold.

"Feed him," Hazel said. "Feed Hale. Through Ferro. Everything Ferro learns from now on, we choose for him to learn. Let him keep selling us — sell him the version of us we want Hale to buy."

"That's a long game," Marta said slowly. "That's a game with our necks as the stakes."

"Every game in this city is played with our necks. At least this way we choose the table." Hazel crossed the room and stood in front of Ferro's chair, close enough to see the exhaustion in him, the gray under the stubble, the way his eyes wouldn't quite settle on hers. Close enough to smell the machine oil on him and remember, unwillingly, his hands inside their press, gentle with its gears. "Look at me."

He looked. His eyes were wet. She had not expected that, and it undid something in her chest that she had to knot back down before she could speak.

"I'm not doing this for mercy," she said. "Understand that. If I thought hanging you would keep Tam alive, I'd walk you to the docks myself and hold the lantern. But it won't. Hale doesn't return brothers. The moment your usefulness ends, your Renna rots in the Marsh Gate regardless, and we've lost a mechanic and gained a corpse. Whereas if the leak keeps flowing—" she let the silence carry it, let the mechanic's mind do what mechanic's minds do, follow the mechanism to its end.

"You want me to lie to them," Ferro said. His voice was rough, disused, like a press after a long idling.

"No," Hazel said. "I want you to tell them the truth. Just my truth. The truth I cut for you." She glanced around the room. "We'll need a new manifest. Something worth finding. Something that leads Hale where we want him — away from the quay, away from the tide tables, away from the *Morning Tide* and everyone who ever touched her. And Ferro is going to discover it, in his own good time, in a place where discovery looks natural. Because that's what he's good at now. Finding things."

"And if I refuse?" Ferro asked quietly. "If I'd rather take the harbor's way out than play double?"

It was Odile who answered, rising off the cistern lip, but it was Hazel whose eyes he held. "Then we'll grieve you," Hazel said, "and mean it, and manage without you. Nobody here is going to make you a corpse to feel safer. We're printers and smugglers, not the Governor." She heard her own words and marveled at them — *we*, spoken as easily as breathing, when eight months ago the only we in her life had been a dead man's name on a shop sign. "But I think you'll say yes. Because you did the arithmetic once with your heart in the balance, and I'm offering you the same sum with better numbers. Your brother lives as long as Hale believes you. Hale believes you as long as we're worth believing. It turns out, Ferro, that your brother's life is now kept in our vault. Guard it well."

The room was very quiet. Tam was staring at her with his mouth slightly open, the look of a boy revising his map of an adult. Marta had uncrossed her arms at some point without noticing. And Odile laughed — one short bark, genuine, startled out of her.

"*Mon Dieu,*" the smuggler said. "The mouse roars."

"I was never the mouse," Hazel said, and heard the truth of it land strangely even in her own ears. "I was just very good at hiding."

They worked until the small hours. It was decided, in the end, with Marta's grudging blessing and Odile's professional enthusiasm, that the decoy would be a shipment — because Hale's whole apparatus was built to intercept shipments, and a hunter fed is a hunter tame. Details tomorrow. Tonight they needed only the shape of the lie, and Ferro needed to learn it well enough to be caught by it convincingly.

Near the end, cutting Ferro's cords and watching him rub feeling back into his wrists, Hazel felt the strangest sensation — not satisfaction, exactly, and certainly not warmth, but something adjacent to both. She had stood in front of a man who might have killed her and chosen the future over the flame, and the choosing had not torn her apart. It had assembled her, click by click, like type in a chase.

She was walking up the ladder when Tam caught her sleeve at the top.

"You surprised me," he said. "That's all. You surprised everybody."

"People do that," Hazel said. "When they stop hiding."

* * *

The fog had thickened over the harbor streets by the time she let herself out the chandler's side door, and she walked the long way home along the rope walk because she did not want to think yet and walking let her defer it. Lanterns hung at the corners in their iron brackets, each one a small captive sun, and she counted them as she passed — seven, eight — the way her father had taught her to count sorts, one by one, so that nothing in the case was ever lost.

Her father. Her brother. She realized she had thought of them tonight without pain, almost without noticing — had reasoned from her father's craft as if quoting a living teacher — and that grief itself had become a tool she could pick up and set down. Perhaps that was what mourning became when you finally did it properly: not a wound but an inheritance.

At the corner of Foundry Lane she stopped. A notice had gone up on the customs post since morning, paste still shining wetly at its edges, and by lantern light she read it twice.

By order of Inspector Hale, a reward of two hundred crowns was offered for information identifying the cutter of seditious marks. Below the official seal, reproduced in bold relief by some government engraver who had never held a graver in his life, squatted a crude imitation of her compass rose inside its lantern — wrong proportions, clumsy rays, the glass panes drawn square instead of arched. An ugly counterfeit. A forgery of her forged mark. She almost laughed.

Then she saw the last line, newly added, ink still darker than the rest:

*All vessels' manifests subject to inspection without notice, effective immediately.*

Effective immediately. Before the *Morning Tide*'s window opened. Before their real manifest could move a single crate toward the quay.

Someone had moved faster than they planned. Someone in the customs house had been awake all night behind frosted glass, working late, heels together like a man on parade ground.

Two hundred crowns for her mark. Total inspection for her ship. Hale wasn't hunting anymore, Hazel understood, standing in the fog with the wet notice shining at the corner of her eye.

He was building a net — and unless they fed him something better by week's end, the *Morning Tide* would sail into it empty-handed, and everything they'd saved would hang with everything they loved.
