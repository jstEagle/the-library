## 21. The False Manifest

The corrupted compass took me two days to cut, and it was the hardest thing I had ever made—harder than honest work, because it had to be wrong in exactly the right ways.

A counterfeit mark is not simply a bad copy. Anyone can cut a clumsy rose; a clumsy rose reads as forgery at a glance and tells the finder nothing except that someone clumsy was here. What I needed was something subtler: a mark that *believed itself*. A compass rose inside a lantern, as mine was, but with errors that only I would know were errors—and even then, only because I had put them there on purpose. The north point of the rose tilted three degrees past true, the way my father's hand had drifted in his last winter when his eyes began to fail. The lantern's crown carried seven rays instead of my eight, because Tam had once told Ferro, in the careless way boys tell mechanics things, that the mark had seven. The crosshatching beneath the lantern's base ran left to right instead of right to left, because I am left-handed and Ferro, who had watched me sweep shavings from the bench more than once, might have noticed the direction my wrist traveled.

Every flaw was a confession planted where a hunter would look. Every truth was a small lie built on top of it.

I worked by tallow light in the cellar beneath the chandler's shop, with Marta reading aloud beside me—not pamphlets, tonight, but an old book of sermons she claimed to find soothing, though I suspect she chose it for its power to put a person to sleep, which was the point. We could not risk conversation. The walls of that cellar were no longer ours alone. Somewhere above us, or below us, or threaded through the cracks between us like damp, was Ferro.

* * *

Ferro. Even now, weeks after Tam had come running down the cellar stairs white-faced and whispering, I could hardly hold the shape of it in my mind. Ferro, who kept the press alive with oil and patience and profanity. Ferro, who had taught Tam to set type by feel with his great scarred hands folded over the boy's small ones. Ferro, whose brother sat in the Governor's stone jail on a charge of seditious printing—true, as it happened—and whose brother's life had been weighed against ours and found, by whatever scales turn inside a frightened man, to be heavier.

He fed Hale our plans the way a man feeds a fire he cannot put out: one stick at a time, hating himself, hoping each stick would be the last. Odile wanted him drowned in the harbor. Marta wanted him forgiven and watched forever, which is its own kind of drowning. It was I who had said the word that stopped them all mid-argument, I who heard myself say it and barely recognized the voice: *use him.*

I had never thought of myself as a person who could say such a thing. But the shop was ash by then. My father's punches had melted in their case. There was a version of Hazel Crane who would have wept over Ferro's betrayal and another version who would have sharpened her gravers for revenge, and both of them had burned with the type rack. What remained was a cutter, and a cutter looks at metal and asks what it will do.

Metal does not betray you. Metal only does what it is told.

* * *

"Show me again," Odile said, and I slid the proof across the table to her, face up, in the yellow circle of the lamp.

She studied it the way she studied charts before a crossing—with total attention and total silence, her thumb resting on the paper's edge as if the manifest might try to escape. Odile Vasseau had run cargo through more ports than I could name, and half of those runs had been under false papers, so a forged manifest was, to her, a familiar animal. She knew its gaits. She knew where it limped.

"The ink," she said at last.

"Fresh," I said. "But aged."

"That's the trick of it, isn't it." She leaned closer. To the eye, the manifest declared a shipment of lantern oil bound for the Saltworks wharf, consigned to a factor named Harlan Grieve—a man who existed, who owed the resistance a favor, and whose name would survive an hour's inquiry without cracking. The seal at the bottom was a customs stamp Elias had reproduced from memory, and it was very nearly perfect, which frightened me more than any of the deliberate flaws, because it meant how far Elias Wren had already traveled from the man who counted households.

Odile picked up the lamp and held the flame low behind the page, letting light bleed through the fibers. "You've thinned the ink where a handler's thumb would rub it."

"A clerk checks a manifest forty times a night. He doesn't read it after the third time—he touches it. So the wear has to be where hands go, not where eyes go."

"And the mark?"

"The mark is the whole sermon." I turned the page toward myself and tapped the little lantern with its tilting rose. "Ferro has seen my true mark twice—once on the coffin plates, once on the second batch of decrees. If we send anything out bearing the true mark now, he reports it within the hour and Hale knows everything moves. But if Ferro finds a mark that's *almost* mine—" I shrugged. "A man who informs for coin needs his information to be worth coin. He won't report doubt. Doubt earns nothing. He'll report certainty, and certainty is what I've built for him out of seven rayed stars and backwards hatching."

Marta looked up from her sermon. She had that expression she got sometimes, the one that made her seem like a well with something moving far down at the bottom. "You're giving him a lie to sell," she said. "And Hale will pay for it."

"Hale has been buying from him for weeks," Odile said. "Let the goods be false for once."

* * *

We chose the warehouse on Coil Street the way you choose a stage for a play you intend to be interrupted in: for its sightlines, its doors, and its history. It belonged to a grain merchant who wintered in the capital, rented through a name Marta had used before, and stood four streets from the harbor gate—close enough that a shipment staged there would make sense to anyone watching, far enough that no watchman's routine patrol would stumble on it by accident. If Ferro was going to discover our false shipment, he would have to be *led* to it, gently, by the hand, the way you lead a man who believes himself the leader.

That was the part I found hardest to sit with in the nights that followed. Not the forging—I understood forging, I had been raised on the grammar of it, the way a lie is built from true materials honestly worked. What troubled me was the shape of the thing itself. We were building a trap out of trust. Ferro would see the manifest and believe, for some span of hours, that he finally held something valuable enough to buy his brother's freedom, and that belief—that small, desperate flare of hope in a breaking man—was the bait. We were not deceiving his cunning. We were deceiving his love.

Tam caught me thinking about it. He was good at catching me; he had a runner's instinct for the moment a person's feet went still.

"You're doing the face," he said. We were sorting the false shipment by lamplight—crates of actual lantern oil, because Odile insisted every detail be load-bearing truth, and the manifest lay on top of the lead crate like a letter on a grave.

"What face?"

"The one where your mouth goes flat and your thumbs argue with each other." He mimed it, unkindly well. "It's the Ferro face. Everyone gets it these days. Marta prays at it. Odile sharpens things at it."

"And what do you do at it?"

"I run," he said, simply, tying off a cord. Then, quieter, in the voice he saved for observations he wasn't sure adults could bear: "My da used to say there's two kinds of sorry. The kind that fixes nothing and the kind that carries a crate." He patted the oil barrel beside him. "This is a carrying-a-crate sorry. That's the best kind there is. Better than crying at it, anyway."

From the mouths of children, as Marta would say, and the mouths of runners most of all. I tied my own cord and let my thumbs rest.

* * *

The planting was done at the hour when the tide turns and the city's blood runs thinnest—between the last of the harbor bells and the first gray suggestion of morning, when even the cats are ashamed to be awake. Odile brought the cart. I rode in the back among the crates, wrapped in a fishmonger's canvas that smelled of brine and old money lost, and Tam walked the shafts beside the mule, whistling a tuneless whistle that told the world a boy was abroad with a mule and nothing else, nothing to see here, move along.

Coil Street received us without comment. The warehouse lock yielded to Odile's pick in less time than it takes to tell it—she had made a wax impression a week prior, back when the plan was still being argued into existence, and she handled her picks the way I handle gravers, with that economy of motion that comes from ten thousand repetitions of the same small violence against the same stubborn material.

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and rat and spilled grain gone sweet with rot. We worked by shuttered lantern, slivers of light only. The crates went onto the pallets near the loading door where they'd be seen first by any eye that came looking. The manifest went on top, weighted at one corner with a coil of rope so it wouldn't drift, angled just so—facing the door. A document lying perfectly square says *filed, forgotten.* A document lying slightly askew says *dropped in haste,* and haste is a scent hunters follow.

Then the last piece, the piece we had argued over longest. Evidence of ourselves.

It was Marta's idea, and it was elegant, and I hated it. Beneath the manifest, tucked into the seam of the second crate, we placed a single sheet of proofs—misprints, pulled from the cellar press in an earlier hour, bearing fragments of seditious text and, in the corner, the corrupted mark stamped twice. Real paper, real ink, real wrongness. If Ferro lifted the manifest, the proofs waited underneath like a second and deeper vein of ore. He would not need to report suspicion. He would need only carry that sheet to Inspector Hale and lay it on the man's desk, and Hale—who had stared at my true mark until it lived behind his eyelids, who had burned my shop partly out of policy and partly out of the peculiar rage of a man unable to catch a signature—would see it and believe he had finally, finally closed his fist around the compass.

"He must find it himself," Marta had said, when we debated whether to simply leave word for Ferro of the shipment's existence. "If we tell him, it's a message, and messages can be traps, and he's begun to think in traps lately, poor soul. But if he *discovers* it—if the discovery belongs to him, if he can feel the weight of his own cleverness in his hand—then he won't question it. No man questions his own cleverness. It's the one miracle he believes in."

So we left the discovery to him, and the leading of him to it we accomplished by other means: a rumor, seeded through the fish market by Tam's network of runners' gossip, that Grieve the factor was holding oil at Coil Street for a buyer who paid in silver and asked no questions. Rumor is water; it finds every crack. And Ferro, we knew, drank wherever Hale's thirst led him.

* * *

We were three streets away when the bell at the harbor rang the half hour, and I made Odile stop the cart.

"Why?" she said. "There's nothing to see."

"There's everything to see. I cut that mark. I want to know what it does."

What it did, in the event, was nothing—at least nothing we could witness. Coil Street stayed dark. The warehouse kept its secret the way the sea keeps a hull: patiently, with the whole weight of the water pressing in and not one drop admitted. Tam, perched on the crates beside me in the cold, fell asleep against my shoulder somewhere in the second hour, and I let him, and the weight of him was warm and ordinary and the only clean thing in the whole arrangement.

It was near dawn when the door across the street opened—not the warehouse door, but the door of a lodging house opposite, and a man came out into the blue-gray light with his collar up and moved toward the warehouse at the pace of a man taking a shortcut, not a man on a mission. Ferro. I knew him by the roll of his shoulders and the way he paused at the warehouse corner to look up and down the street with a mechanic's habitual assessment of load and strain. Then he went to the loading door, tried it—locked, as we'd left it—and went round the side, where the window with the broken latch waited, because we had broken the latch ourselves two nights before and left the break to weather.

The window took him a minute. Then he poured himself through it the way big men do, all at once and somehow silently, and he was gone into the dark, and I sat in the fish-smelling canvas with my heart going like a treadle and Tam asleep against my arm, and I understood that I had just watched a man walk into the machine we had built him to be.

"Feel it?" Odile murmured from the shafts, not turning around.

"Yes."

"What's it feel like?"

I thought about it honestly, because Odile deserved honesty and because I didn't yet know the answer myself. "Like cutting," I said at last. "Like the moment the punch goes through and you don't know yet if it held its edge or crumbled. You've done everything right. And you don't find out until the impression comes off the press."

Below the street, below the silence, Ferro was finding our manifest. Below the silence, he was lifting it toward the window's gray light, and the corrupted compass was telling him its careful lies, and somewhere in his chest hope was rising—the terrible currency of the thing, the part we had spent and could not take back. He would go to Hale today. He would lay the sheet of misprints on the desk like a man laying down a debt. And Hale—

Hale would come for Coil Street. Hale would raid the warehouse with all the ceremony of a man who believed his long hunt was over, and he would seize thirty crates of honest lantern oil and a manifest signed by a factor who would swear, plausibly, truthfully, that he had never seen the papers in his life. And the Governor, who had already been humiliated once by empty plates, would stand in front of a second haul of nothing, and this time there would be a trail leading back from the false manifest, and the trail would not lead to the cellar press or the chandler's shop or the ash that had been Crane & Sons.

It would lead to Elias Wren's office. Because Hale, curdled and suspicious, patient as rust, had already begun to wonder why his census man asked so many careful questions and reported so few answers, and a predator who has been cheated once looks twice at everyone who was standing nearby.

I saw it then, sitting in the cart with the child asleep on my shoulder and the dawn coming up the color of tin. I saw the shape of the next cruelty before it arrived, the way a cutter sees the fracture line in bad metal before the hammer falls. Our trap would close beautifully on Hale. And in closing, it would throw its shadow straight across Elias.

"Drive," I said to Odile. "We need to warn him before Ferro finishes his breakfast."
