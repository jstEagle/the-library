## 22. Hale Takes the Bait

The fog came off the harbor at dusk the way it always did in Verrance—thick as wool, smelling of tar and cold iron—and Inspector Ambrose Hale stood at the window of his office above the customs house and watched it swallow the masts of the warships one by one, like a hand closing over candles.

He had been patient for eleven weeks.

Eleven weeks of informants' notes written in grease pencil on scraps of paper that smelled of fish and fear. Eleven weeks of watching queues at the customs house with a spyglass while clerks counted bales of salt cod and nobody, nobody, carried anything worth arresting. Eleven weeks of Ferro's reports arriving folded inside invoices for press oil—the mechanic's handwriting growing shakier each month, the way a man's hand does when he is selling his brother's life by the ounce and cannot bear to count what remains.

And now, at last, the ledger had balanced. The manifest Ferro had copied sat on Hale's desk, its wax seal unbroken because Ferro had been clever enough to lift it whole with a heated knife. *One crate, printing plates, assorted matrices, consigned to the Morning Tide, care of the Saltworks Warehouse, Rope Walk.* A compass rose stamped into the lead seal—a lantern set inside it, if you looked long enough, and Hale had looked long enough. He had looked at that mark until it burned itself into the back of his eyelids. It was the same mark that had appeared on three pamphlets, two broadsheets, and one forged land decree that had made the Governor wake his household at midnight with shouting.

Tonight he would take it off the board.

Hale dressed without hurry. He had learned long ago that haste was a confession—that men who ran to their victories were men who expected few of them. He buttoned his coat to the throat, settled his hat low against the fog, and took the stairs down past the customs clerks, who bent over their ledgers like monks at prayer and did not look up. They never looked up. That was the trouble with this city. Everyone had learned not to look up.

* * *

The Saltworks Warehouse crouched at the end of Rope Walk like something that had drowned and been propped back upright. Salt rot had eaten its timbers gray; the windows were boarded; the tide slapped at its pilings with a sound like slow applause. Twelve dragoons moved through the fog behind Hale, their boots quiet on the wet cobbles, their carbines slung—not because Hale feared resistance, but because he wanted the printers alive. Dead men could not name their accomplices. Dead men could not be made to write out, in their own careful hands, every name they had ever trusted.

Ferro was waiting at the side door, a shuttered lantern held at hip height, his face a smear of shadow and sweat.

"It's there," the mechanic whispered. "Crate's marked. I checked an hour ago."

"Who has access?"

"Odile's people run the floor. But they don't go near the north bay after dark. Superstition." His laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "They say it's haunted."

Everything in this city was haunted, Hale thought. Haunted harbors, haunted presses, haunted dead brothers and dead fathers whose names got printed on things they'd never touched. He put his shoulder to the door.

Inside, the warehouse breathed. That was the only word for it—the great dark volume of the place inhaling and exhaling with the tide beneath the floorboards, and somewhere overhead the creak of rope under load, though no cargo moved. The dragoons spread out with practiced silence, lanterns hooded, and Hale walked the center aisle with Ferro's light bobbing ahead of him, counting bays the way other men counted rosaries.

North bay. There.

The crate sat alone on a pallet, squared and honest-looking, stenciled in fresh black paint: *PLATES — CRANE & SONS — HANDLE WITH CARE.*

Crane & Sons. Even the name was a kind of insolence. Hale had pulled the registry months ago—one widowed aunt, one niece of no recorded trade, one son dead these six years. A shop of ghosts, printing pamphlets that moved through Verrance like weather. He had wanted the cutter of that compass-lantern mark more than he had wanted any single man in eleven years of service, because marks were signatures, and signatures were confessions, and confessions were how an inspector climbed.

"Open it."

The crowbar went in with a sound like a rib cracking. Two dragoons hauled the lid back, and Hale leaned over the crate with his lantern raised, and for one long moment he did not understand what he was looking at.

Then he understood.

Lead. Not type—lead. Great ugly pigs of it, casting-house scrap, the dross skimmed off the melting pots and poured into crude molds to give it weight. Dozens of them, packed in straw, each one stamped with a compass rose so crude it might have been cut by a child with a nail. No matrices. No plates. No punches. Nothing but weight dressed up as treasure, and the mark repeated across every ingot like laughter.

The warehouse exhaled around him. Somewhere in the rafters, rope groaned.

"Empty it," Hale said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Every crate. Every barrel. Tear up the floorboards if you have to."

They worked for two hours. They found salt. They found forty years of salt, crusted into the grain of the wood, white as bone, and they found rats, and they found a child's shoe gone stiff with brine, and they found nothing else. The Morning Tide's consignment existed nowhere but on a piece of paper that had been allowed, deliberately, patiently, to fall into Ferro's hands like a coin dropped for a beggar.

Hale stood in the ruined north bay with straw clinging to his boots and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like a blade laid flat against the back of the neck, that he had not caught the resistance.

He had been *used* by it.

They had known about Ferro. Of course they had known—you did not run a press out of a cellar beneath a chandler's shop for years without learning which man in your crew flinched when the word *brother* was spoken. They had fed the traitor a false manifest the way you'd feed a poisoned rat to a dog, and they had let him carry it home in his mouth, and they had let Hale spend twelve dragoons, one night, and eleven weeks of patience prying open a box of scrap metal.

Worse. Far worse. Because now Ferro was burned. Whatever thread of information still ran from the cellar press to the customs house had been cut tonight, cleanly, deliberately, and the enemy knew exactly where Hale's ear had been pressed against the wall. Every name Ferro had ever given him was now suspect. Every report. Eleven weeks of intelligence, rendered worthless in a single evening—and worse than worthless, because a wise enemy seeded false reports among true ones, and Hale would now have to reread all of it wondering which crumbs were bread and which were stone.

"Inspector?" One of the dragoons, young, holding up an ingot. "Shall we load them as evidence?"

Hale looked at the crude compass rose stamped in the lead. Evidence. Yes. Let the record show that the celebrated Inspector Hale of the Governor's own commission had seized four hundred pounds of melted rubbish at considerable public expense.

"Load them," he said.

* * *

The Governor received him at nine the next morning, which meant the Governor had already heard.

Verrance's Government House sat on the high ground above the town, all pale stone and tall windows, built by men who had wanted the sea visible but never close. Hale had walked its corridors a hundred times, and he knew their acoustics the way a sailor knows rigging: he could hear, before he reached the audience chamber, the particular quality of silence that meant someone inside was angry and being polite about it.

The Governor stood at the window with his back turned. Secretary Voss hovered at the writing desk, quill poised, wearing the expression of a man who hoped very sincerely to be elsewhere. On the desk lay a copy of Ferro's manifest, and beside it, arranged with cruel precision, a single lead ingot—the one some clerk with a sense of humor had carried up from the warehouse.

"Hale." The Governor did not turn. "Tell me about the compass."

"The mark, Your Excellency, is the signature of the underground press operating—"

"I know what the mark is. I have seen it on a forgery of my own seal, Mr. Hale, displayed in my own city, read aloud in taverns by dockworkers who can barely spell their names." Now he turned, and his face was composed in the way that expensive tailors compose faces—every line correct, nothing wasted. "What I do not know is why four hundred pounds of scrap lead is sitting in my evidence room stamped with it."

"The shipment was a decoy, Excellency. The resistance became aware of our informant and—"

"They became aware of your informant." The Governor crossed to the desk and picked up the ingot, turning it in his soft, ringed hands as though weighing a loaf. "Which means either your informant was careless, or your informant was never yours at all, or—" he set the lead down with a small, precise click, "—you were careless. Choose whichever explanation costs you least sleep, Inspector. The result is identical. I gave you eleven weeks and a free hand, and you have delivered me a box of ballast."

"Excellency, the mark itself proves the connection to Crane & Sons. The shop on Bellows Lane—"

"A print shop. In a city full of print shops." The Governor's voice did not rise, which was somehow worse than rising. "Do you know what happens next, Hale? Next, the capital asks me for my quarterly report, and my quarterly report must contain something better than *a box of ballast*. The decrees are being read in coffeehouses from here to the border passes. My own seal, counterfeited. My own words, put in my own mouth, saying things I never said—and every week that passes, the lie grows roots." He came around the desk slowly. He was not a large man, but he had spent thirty years learning to occupy space. "I do not require cleverness from you, Inspector. I require results. Find the cutter. Find the press. Find the ship, if it hasn't sailed. And do it before the *Morning Tide* makes port in the capital, because if those papers reach the colonial office, the next report I file will be explaining why I kept you on."

The silence afterward had texture. Voss's quill had stopped moving entirely.

"Your Excellency," Hale said, "there is a matter I would raise. Concerning the census office."

The Governor's eyebrow moved a fraction. "Wren?"

"Elias Wren, yes." Hale had turned the thing over all night, in the warehouse, in the carriage, in the grey hour before dawn when the fog thinned and the truth got easier to see. It fit. It had always fit, and he had been too busy hunting shadows in doorways to see the man standing in the open with a ledger under his arm. "Wren conducted the household survey in the Bellows Lane district personally. He visited Crane & Sons twice—once officially, once on his own time, which he entered in his log as a follow-up on an unoccupied dwelling. The dwelling was occupied. I have checked. And the compass-lantern mark first appears in print within a fortnight of his second visit."

"You suspect your own census taker."

"I suspect a man who has spent four months walking through every printer's household in this city with the authority of the Crown, taking down names, trades, apprenticeships, and the locations of back rooms and cellar doors." Hale let the sentence sit. "A census, Excellency, is a map of everywhere a press could hide. Delivered to whoever knows how to read it."

"And Wren reads it well?"

"Wren is meticulous. Wren is quiet. Wren has no debts, no vices, no family, and no friends—which I once took for diligence." Hale thought of the census taker's grey, careful face; the way he answered questions precisely and volunteered nothing; the way he had requested, months ago, access to the harbor shipping schedules *for verification purposes*, and Hale had signed the authorization himself because it had seemed such a small, dull thing. "It occurs to me that a man building a case for the Crown and a man building a case against it would look identical from the outside. Patient. Thorough. Invisible."

The Governor considered him for a long moment. Outside the tall windows, the fog was burning off the harbor, and the masts reappeared one by one, as if the water were giving them back.

"If you move against Wren," the Governor said at last, "and you are wrong, you will have driven a wedge between my civil service and my constabulary in the middle of an insurrection, and I will remember whose idea it was. If you are right—" the faintest pause, the ghost of something that was not quite a smile, "—then you may yet salvage this quarter. So do not arrest him. Watch him. Follow him home. Read his letters. Learn whom he speaks to and where he stops and how long he lingers, and bring me something I can act on. Quietly, Hale. If Wren is what you think he is, the moment he smells you, everything he knows walks out of this city in his head."

"Understood, Excellency."

"And Hale." The Governor returned to the window, dismissing him with his back. "The *Morning Tide* sails on the morning tide, as the name suggests. Three days. Whatever you intend to do, do it inside three days."

* * *

That afternoon Hale went down to the census office himself, without warrant, without escort, wearing his plainest coat.

The office smelled of paper and lamp oil and dust, that particular dry smell of a room where the Empire counted itself. Clerks scratched away at their desks. And there, at the corner table by the window, sat Elias Wren, bent over a household return, his pen moving in small even strokes, his collar clean, his cuffs worn smooth, everything about him arranged into a portrait of harmless industry.

He looked up when Hale's shadow fell across his page.

"Inspector." No surprise in it. No fear either—or none visible, and Hale reminded himself that invisible was precisely the man's trade. "How can the census office serve you?"

Hale smiled the way he imagined a fisherman smiles at water. "Routine verification, Mr. Wren. Cross-referencing the household returns against customs manifests. You understand. After last night's... disappointment."

"Of course." Wren turned back to his page, unhurried, and began to rule a fresh column. "The returns are public record. Help yourself."

Public record. Openly offered. Hale pulled a chair to the opposite side of the table—close enough to watch the man's hands—and began, with great ceremony, to untie the string on a bundle of returns he had no intention of reading.

Under the lamplight, Wren's pen scratched on. Steady. Even. The handwriting of a man with nothing in his chest but columns.

But Hale had interrogated enough liars to know the last thing they surrendered was their hands, and he watched those hands now, stroke by stroke, and told himself: *three days. Whatever you're carrying, census man, whatever you've buried and where—I will dig it up before the tide turns.*

Across the table, Elias Wren ruled another line, perfectly straight, and did not look up.
