## 24. The Shop Burns

The knock came at an hour when no honest knocker knocks.

Hazel was at the cutting bench still, though the candles had burned down to their collars and the wax had pooled into little white lakes around their bases. She had been working the same punch for three hours—a lowercase *g* for a commission she did not have, for a customer who did not exist—because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Word had come at dusk, carried by Tam breathless through the back alley: Ferro had been taken. Hale had him in the Customs House cellar. Whatever Ferro knew, he would know it by morning.

So she cut. It was what her father had taught her to do with fear, though he had never named it so plainly. *When the mind runs, give the hands a road,* he used to say, guiding her grip on the graver, warm behind her and smelling of lamp oil and steel filings. Give the hands a road and they will carry the mind somewhere quieter.

The road was not working tonight.

The knock came again—heavy, unhurried, the knock of men who expected doors to open for them—and Hazel blew out her candles in one breath and stood in the dark of the shop she had been born in, listening to her own heart like a clock in an empty house.

Then came the smell. Wrong and immediate. Smoke where no smoke should be, and under it something sweeter, tallow and pitch, the smell of fire *fed*, of fire that had been carried here in buckets and poured with intention.

She ran to the front windows and threw the shutters wide.

Fire was climbing the type rack wall.

Not the street-facing side—the alley side, where the old pine shelving held forty years of matrices, her father's cabinets, the drawers upon drawers of sorts sorted and re-sorted ten thousand times until each compartment's contents sang its own small chord when you ran your fingers through it. The flames had found the oilcloth they'd hung there against damp, found the linseed finish on the wood, found the resinous old pine itself, and they went up it the way ivy goes up a wall, quick and green and certain.

Hazel did not think. Thinking would come later, in terrible abundance; later she would have thoughts enough to drown in. Now there was only motion, only the body's dumb animal love of its own home. She was through the shop door before she knew she'd moved, keys already in her fist, and the night air hit her face and carried the fire's heat ahead of it like a wave carrying a drowned man to shore.

Behind her, up the lane, voices. Boots. She registered them the way one registers weather.

Inside, the smoke was already low along the ceiling, a grey river flowing backward toward the alley wall, and the heat had a texture she had never felt before—thick, pressing, like wading into a summer sea that wanted her dead. The great press stood in the middle of the floor, iron and black and immovable as an altar, and she put her shoulder to the cabinet beside it and dragged the first drawer out.

Father's sorts. Garamond, his first love. The letters rattled across her palms, cool, absurdly cool, each one a small metal fact in a world coming apart.

"Hazel."

Marta's voice, from the doorway. Marta who was seventy-one years old and walked with a stick and had set type for four decades with hands bent like gull wings.

"Help me," Hazel said. "The matrices—we can save the cases—"

"Child." Marta came into the smoke anyway, because Marta had never in her life done the sensible thing when someone she loved was being foolish. She grabbed Hazel's collar with a grip that had lifted quoins and chases and full forme weights for longer than Hazel had been alive, and she hauled her backward, and Hazel fought her, actually fought her, striking out with her elbows, screaming words she could not afterward remember.

"It's *wood*," Marta shouted into her face. "It's wood and it's metal and it will all melt or it won't, but *you* will not, do you hear me? You are not wood. You are not metal. Out."

"The punches—" And there it was, the true wound, said aloud without permission: her father's punches. His first punch especially, the one he'd cut at nineteen, the one he kept wrapped in flannel in the third drawer like a relic, because it *was* one—the small beginning of everything, the seed from which the whole shop had grown, the first letter of their family's long word.

"Out," Marta said again, softer now, terrible in her softness. "Your father is dead, girl. Your brother is dead. This shop is the shell of a thing that lives in *you*, and I will not watch you crawl into the shell because the living part frightens you."

The beam above the type rack cracked. A sound like a bone breaking in a giant's hand.

And Hazel understood, in the way one understands things in fires and floods and other languages spoken too fast—that Marta was right, and that being right changed nothing about how much it cost—and she let herself be dragged.

She did not go quietly. That must be said for her. She twisted once at the threshold and looked back and saw, in the light of the growing fire, the whole interior of her life made visible at last: the bench where she'd stood on a crate at six years old to reach the ink slab; the corner where Tomás had carved his initials and then, caught and thrashed for it, carved hers next to his so they'd share the punishment; the loft stairs where she'd hidden with pamphlets against her chest while inspectors' boots crossed the floor below, learning early the grammar of holding still. All of it lit up, every shadow banished, the shop more fully seen in its final minute than it had ever been in forty years of daylight.

Then the ceiling over the type rack came down in a bloom of sparks, and Marta pulled her through the door, and the night closed over them both like water.

* * *

They ended up against the pump across the lane, because it was the farthest Hazel's legs would carry her and no farther. The lane had begun to fill—nightshirts and shawls, faces orange in the firelight, the bucket line forming itself out of the crowd's old instinct even though everyone understood, in the way everyone understood such things, that this fire would not be put out, that it had not been started in order to be stopped.

"Whose shop?" someone asked. "Crane's? Who'd want to hurt Crane's?"

Nobody answered. Hazel looked down the lane toward the Customs House district, where the lamps burned steady and smug above the harbor, and thought: *Hale.* Thought it the way you think a name of God, without needing proof, without wanting any.

Three men in civilian coats stood at the mouth of the alley, watching. Not helping. Not leaving either. One held a dark lantern by its handle, unlit, swinging it gently, a man walking a dog he was fond of. Their stillness had a professional quality to it. They were measuring something—how fast the fire spread, whether the neighboring roofs would catch, whether anyone would be fool enough to run inside.

Hazel met the nearest one's eyes. He looked back at her with the mild, bored face of a man watching grain being weighed, and tipped his hat.

Something broke in her then—not the grief, which was already broken, already past breaking—but something else, some last thin membrane between herself and a truth she had been refusing since Tam's footsteps had faded up the alley. She had believed, all her life, that invisibility was safety. Be careful, be quiet, be no one, and no one will come for you. She had erased herself from ledgers and signatures and conversations; she had let a dead boy take credit for her living hands. And the fire said to her, in its vast indifferent voice: *they came anyway. They were always going to come. There is no small enough.*

"Your hands," Marta said.

Hazel looked down. Her palms were black with soot and sliced open in two places where the drawer's edge had caught her, and she was shaking—not finely, not trembly, but in great ugly waves that moved her whole shoulders, the way a rigging shakes in wind.

"I couldn't get them out," she said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "I got one drawer. One drawer out of two hundred, Marta. His first punch was in the third cabinet. I've known where his first punch was since I was nine years old, and I just—it's melting right now. Lead doesn't burn, they told me, lead doesn't burn, but it *melts*, it goes soft and loses its face, and he cut it when he was nineteen, he cut it before he ever met my mother, before—" The sentence went to pieces. She let it.

Marta did the unforgivable, merciful thing and held her. Hazel's face went into the old woman's shoulder, into wool and camphor and the ghost of printer's ink that forty years could not wash out, and she wept the way she had not wept at her father's funeral, had not wept at Tomás's grave, had not wept in fifteen years of careful, competent silence—for the shop, yes, for the drawers and the dies and the beautiful ordered drawers of letters, but underneath that, under the waterline, for all the deaths she had swallowed without ceremony because grief was a luxury for people who could afford to be seen crying.

The roof beam over the front room gave way with a sound like the end of the world in miniature, and a column of sparks went up into the fog, and the whole street gasped as one animal, and Hazel laughed—actually laughed, a cracked horrible sound inside her sobbing—because she felt it even through the ruin, unmistakable, absurd: *lighter.* Something in her had been carried out of the fire along with her body, and it was the weight.

Fifteen years of pretending to be her brother's ghost. Fifteen years of signing nothing, claiming nothing, standing one step behind her own life. And here was Hale's fire doing in one hour what she had never dared do for herself: burning the hiding place down. There was no shop now. There was no Crane & Sons to be the widow's, no dead brother's business to shelter behind, no ledger to erase herself from. When the embers cooled there would be nothing left of her camouflage but ash—and herself, standing in the street, visible to every soul on the lane, holding one drawer's worth of rescued type against her chest like a newborn.

"They'll say it was an accident," she said into Marta's shoulder. "A lamp left burning. Faulty flue."

"Yes," said Marta.

"And everyone will know it wasn't."

"Yes," said Marta again, and stroked her hair like she was a child, and neither of them said the rest: that knowing was useless if no one would say it, that the whole city could stand in this lane and know, and the knowledge would sit in their chests like swallowed stone, heavy and silent, until—

Until.

Hazel lifted her head. Down the lane, the watchers in civilian coats had turned away, their work done, strolling off toward the harbor with the unhurried ease of men whose evening had gone well. The bucket line worked on, saving the chandler's roof next door, letting Crane's burn, and nobody blamed them, and everybody understood.

"Marta." Her voice came out level. The shaking had stopped, which frightened her a little, the way calm always frightens the people who know what it costs. "Where is Odile tonight?"

The old woman went very still. "You know where."

"The Morning Tide. Loading at Number Four quay, sailing on the morning tide." Hazel watched the fire take the sign above the door—CRANE & SONS, her father's own hand-painted lettering, the paint bubbling gold at the edges of the C—and felt the strangest sensation, that she was watching her own cage door swing open in flames. "They need the decrees copied. They need matrices. They need a cutter's mark that the capital can trust. And now—" she heard herself say it, heard the words arrive from somewhere deeper than decision— "*now they need never know about the shop. There is no shop. There's only me.*"

"There was only ever only you," Marta said. "That was the whole tragedy, child. That was always the joke."

The fire roared through the interior behind them, and glass burst somewhere in the loft—Tomás's window, she thought, his little room under the eaves—and the heat washed over their faces like a tide, and Hazel stood in it and let it scorch away the last of the girl who hid.

Her hands had stopped bleeding. In the firelight the cuts across her palms showed black, and she looked at them and thought of gravers, of steel, of the punch waiting in her apron pocket at the cellar press—the compass in the lantern, her mark, the only thing Hale's men could not burn because it existed now in a hundred places, in wax impressions and stamped proofs and the memory of every runner in the network. You cannot burn a mark. You cannot arrest an idea wearing a symbol. Her father had taught her that letters were immortal in their way—that a well-cut punch could outlive its cutter by centuries, that she had printed words tonight with types descended from faces cut before either of their countries had names.

Let Hale have the wood and the glass. Let him have the shell.

She picked up the drawer she'd saved—the Garamond, father's first love—and stood it upright in the gutter at her feet like a headstone, like a cornerstone, she could not yet have said which.

"When the tide turns," she said, "I'm going down to the harbor."

"I know," said Marta. "I'm coming with you. Someone has to carry the drawer."

Above them the roof fell in entirely, a soft enormous sigh, and the sparks rose so thick and high that the fog itself seemed lit from within, and people in the lane raised their faces to it, and somewhere a church bell began to ring—not for alarm, Hazel realized, but the hour, two o'clock, ordinary time going on, the world insisting on itself even now—and she stood in the falling gold light with her family's whole history collapsing twenty feet away, and felt, for the first time in her life, entirely, dangerously, free.

Down the hill, muffled by distance and fire, came another sound: boots, many of them, moving in formation toward the harbor.

Toward the quays.

Toward the Tide.

Hazel picked up her end of the drawer without another word.
