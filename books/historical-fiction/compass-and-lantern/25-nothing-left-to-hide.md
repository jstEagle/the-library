## 25. Nothing Left to Hide

The smoke did not leave the sky all night.

Hazel stood in the street with the crowd and watched Crane & Sons burn, and she understood, with the strange clarity that grief sometimes grants, that she was watching two funerals. The first belonged to her father, whose presses and punches and forty years of patient labor were curling upward in black ribbons. The second belonged to the woman she had been for eleven years—the niece, the assistant, the shadow who swept the floor and set no type and cut nothing, who existed in every ledger only as a smudge of ink where a name should have been.

Marta's hand was iron around her wrist. Hazel had not even felt the old compositor drag her out of the doorway; she had been standing there, she realized, with her arms half-raised, as if she might catch the falling beams, as if she might reach into the fire and pull out the type rack the way you would pull a child from a river. Marta had hauled her back into the street and held on, and now neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the fire was not already saying, loudly, in the language of cracking timber and bursting glass.

The windows went first. She heard them go—the old crown glass her father had bought the year she was born, each pane warped so that the street outside had always looked to her like something seen through water. Then the sign. *Crane & Sons*, in gilt letters her father had polished every Sunday, swung once on its bracket like a man deciding, and then dropped into the flames with a sound like a bell falling down a stairwell.

*And Sons.* Hazel thought of the lie in those letters, the lie she had maintained with her own hands for more than a decade. Her father had never had sons. He had a daughter who could cut a letter finer than any man in Verrance, and he had known it, and the world had not permitted it, and so they had built the fiction together, the two of them, a conspiracy of small erasures. She had thought the fiction was a cage. Standing in the street with ash settling on her shoulders like grey snow, she understood that it had also been a wall, and the wall was gone, and there was nothing now between her and the whole cold wind of the world.

* * *

They walked back to Marta's rooms above the chandler's shop in the grey hour before dawn, when the fire was down to a red heart in the ruin and the Governor's men had gone, satisfied, leaving one dragoon on the corner to keep the curious away from the ashes. Hazel walked the way she had once seen a gull walk with a broken wing—straight enough, if you did not look closely, but every step cost something.

Marta's rooms smelled of tallow and old paper, which was to say they smelled of safety. There was a stove, and a kettle, and a chair by the window where Marta sat Hazel down as if she were a child, and put tea in her hands, and only then let her own hands shake.

"I saw them come," Marta said. "From the window. Four of them, with oil. They didn't even hurry. They knocked the watchman down first, and then the one with the red scarf—he looked at the sign before he poured. He read it, Hazel. *Crane & Sons.* He read it like he was checking a name off a list."

"Then it wasn't random," Hazel said. Her voice came out flat and far away, as if it belonged to someone across the room. "Hale."

"Hale." Marta spat the name with the economy of a woman who had lived through three governors and had stopped wasting breath on any of them. She poured her own tea and did not drink it. "They burned the shop because they couldn't find the press. They burned the shop because they think the mark lives there." She looked at Hazel over the rim of her cup, and her eyes, in the tallow light, were very old and very steady. "They're wrong, aren't they. The mark doesn't live there. It never did."

Hazel's hands tightened around the tea. It was too hot and she did not care. The heat was the only thing she could feel that was not ash.

"Marta—"

"I've known for six years," Marta said. "Don't look like that. I set type beside your father for twenty years, child. I know his hand in a line of type the way you know a voice in the dark. And I know yours. You think I didn't notice that the kerning changed after he died? That the serifs got finer? That the shop's work got *better*?" She set the cup down with a small, decisive click. "A compositor knows a cutter the way a wife knows a husband's footsteps on the stairs. I have known since the winter of the fever. I said nothing because you said nothing, and because a secret kept is a secret safe, and because—" she paused, and for a moment the iron in her voice softened into something almost gentle, "—because your father asked me to. The night before he died. He came up here with a bottle of the bad brandy and he said, *Marta, when I'm gone, my girl will try to disappear herself, and I need you to be the one who remembers she's there.*"

The tea trembled in Hazel's hands. She set it on the floor because she could not hold it and breathe at the same time.

"He knew," she whispered. "He knew what I'd do."

"He knew what the world would make you do." Marta leaned forward. "And here is what I have to say to you now, Hazel Crane, and I'll say it once, so listen. The shop is gone. The ledgers are gone. The name *Crane & Sons* is a pile of gilt letters in the street. There is nothing left to hide behind. Do you understand what that means?" Her eyes were bright and fierce. "It means you're free. It means the lie has burned up with everything else, and what's left sitting in my chair is not the niece, not the assistant, not the dead boy's ghost—it's the finest punchcutter in Verrance, and everyone in this city who matters is about to know it."

"That's not freedom," Hazel said. "That's a noose."

"It's both," Marta said. "Most true things are."

* * *

The cellar press knew before Hazel did. Word traveled underground faster than fire traveled up, and by the time she descended the chandler's steps the next evening, the whole room had rearranged itself around her grief the way water rearranges itself around a dropped stone. Tam sat on the stairs and did not chatter, which was worse than chatter. Odile stood by the press with her arms crossed, and for once she did not look like a woman calculating a price. Even Ferro was gone—sent away, Odile said shortly, before Hazel could ask, *for his own safety,* which was a phrase that meant something different in every mouth that used it.

They had heard about the shop. Of course they had. The whole harbor had watched it burn.

Hazel expected sympathy, and braced against it, because sympathy felt like drowning. What she got instead was Odile, crossing the room and stopping an arm's length away and looking at her with those flat grey sea-eyes.

"The plates," Odile said. "The matrices. What did you lose?"

"Everything that was in the shop," Hazel said. "The punches my father cut. The first matrix he ever made—he kept it on the shelf above the bench, forty years, never used it, just kept it. It's slag now. All of it's slag."

"And the compass punch? The one you cut for us?"

Hazel's hand went to her breast pocket before she could stop it. The punch was there, wrapped in oilcloth, where it had been since the night she finished it. She had carried it to bed. She had carried it to tea. She had slept with it under her pillow like a talisman, like a heart outside her body.

Odile's eyes tracked the movement and something in her face shifted—not softness, exactly. Recognition. The look of one professional acknowledging another's tools.

"Then we still have a press," Odile said, "and we still have a mark, and we still have the only pair of hands in Verrance that can cut another punch if we need one." She said it loudly, to the room, and Hazel understood that it was not a statement. It was a coronation.

Tam came down the last three stairs at that, unable to contain himself another second, and his chatter came back all at once like water through a broken weir. "I saw it from the rope walk, the whole harbor saw it, and there was this one moment when the roof went and the sparks came up like—like a tree, a burning tree, all at once—and everyone on the walk went quiet, even the gulls, even the *gulls*, Hazel, and old Pieter said—" he stopped, and looked at her, and his voice dropped for the first time in the entire span of their acquaintance. "I'm sorry about your shop. I'm sorry about your dad's things."

"His things," Hazel repeated. The words tasted strange. "Yes."

Because that was the truth of it, she was discovering. Not *my father's life's work*, though it was that. Not *my craft*, though it was that too. *His things.* Because the shop had never legally been hers, had never in the eyes of any clerk or magistrate or census ledger been hers, and the fire had not taken anything that belonged to Hazel Crane because Hazel Crane, on paper, had never owned anything at all. The fire had taken her father's property and her brother's reputation and the widow Crane's inheritance, and it had taken them cleanly, completely, leaving her—the actual hands, the actual eye, the actual eleven years of midnight labor—entirely unrecorded, unclaimed, unburned.

She had spent her whole life being the thing the fire couldn't find.

And now she was standing in a cellar full of people who had found her anyway.

* * *

It happened three days later, in the fish market, in front of God and the gulls and half the harbor.

Hazel had gone for salt fish because Marta's knee was bad and because she needed, desperately, to be somewhere with ordinary noise, somewhere the air did not smell of char. She was haggling with the fishwife over a pair of mackerel when she saw them—three of Hale's men, moving down the row of stalls with the unhurried menace of men doing a chore. Asking questions. Showing something to the fishmongers, the rope-walk hands, the chandlers. Showing a paper.

Her body knew before her mind did. It was the old knowledge, the eleven-years-deep knowledge of the hunted: the way her spine straightened, her face smoothed, her eyes found the middle distance. *Be the niece. Be the smudge. Be nobody.* She turned back to the fishwife with her heart hammering a rhythm her face did not betray, and she was reaching for her coin purse when Marta's voice cut across the market like a cleaver through a fish spine.

"THAT'S HER!"

Hazel turned. Marta was standing at the end of the stall row, small and grey-haired and immovable as a mooring post, and she was pointing—not away from Hazel, not past her, but *at* her, her finger steady as a type gauge.

"You want the mark?" Marta called to the men, and her voice carried the way a bell carries, the way twenty years of calling corrections across a noisy composing room had taught it to carry. "You want to know who cut the compass in the lantern? *She* cut it. Hazel Crane. Daughter of Aldous Crane, master cutter of Crane & Sons, the shop you burned. She cut every letter that shop set for eleven years. Not her brother. *Her.* The best cutter this city ever produced, and you people burned her house down over it, and now you can write the name down properly for once in your miserable bureaucratic lives: *Hazel. Crane.*"

The market had gone silent. Gulls hung on the wind. The fishwife's mouth was open. Somewhere a barrel rolled to a stop against a bollard.

The three men looked at Hazel. Hazel looked at Marta. And the strange thing—the thing she would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would tell her own apprentice one day in a shop that did not yet exist—was that she did not feel terror. She had expected terror. She had rehearsed terror her whole life; she knew its choreography the way she knew the cutting bench. But what rose in her instead was something she had no name for, something that started in her chest where the punch rode against her breastbone and spread outward like warmth into cold hands.

The men started toward her. And Hazel Crane, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of disappearing, stood her ground in the fish market with the salt wind in her face, and did not move.

"Write it down," she said, and her voice did not shake. "You've got the paper. *Hazel Crane.* Spell it right. I'll know if you don't—I'm rather particular about letters."

The tallest of the three—a sergeant with a red scarf, the same red scarf Marta had described, she realized, from the night of the fire—stopped an arm's length away. He looked at her the way men look at a puzzle box. Then he took out his notebook, and he wrote, and he read it back to her, and she corrected his spelling of *Crane*—*one e, not two*—and he closed the book and said, "You're to come to the customs house. Inspector Hale's orders," and she said, "I know where it is," and that was the whole of it. Eleven years of hiding, ended by an old woman's pointing finger and a fish market's worth of witnesses, concluded in the time it takes to spell a name.

Marta came and stood beside her as the men conferred at the end of the row. Her hand found Hazel's and gripped it, hard.

"You could have let me run," Hazel said quietly.

"You'd have run for the rest of your life," Marta said. "And they'd have burned the next shop, and the next, hunting a ghost with your hands. No." Her grip tightened. "Better they hunt a woman who stands still. Better they learn what your name weighs." Her eyes were wet and furious and proud, all three at once. "Your father asked me to remember you were there, child. I'm done remembering. Now the whole city knows."

The sergeant turned back. "Come along, then, mistress."

Hazel looked once more down the row of stalls—past the fishwife, past the silent rope-walk hands, past Tam, who had appeared from nowhere at the mouth of the alley with his eyes enormous and his fists clenched—and she felt the strangest thing of all, stranger than the freedom, stranger than the fear.

She felt *lighter*.

Eleven years of erasure, and it turned out the weight had been the hiding, all along. The name was heavy. The name was a noose and a target and a death sentence waiting for a signature. And the name was hers, and she would carry it into the customs house and out again, and whatever Hale did to her, he could never again make her small, because smallness had burned up with the shop, and what walked toward the harbor gate on the sergeant's arm was not the niece, not the ghost, not the smudge in the ledger—

It was Hazel Crane, master cutter of Verrance, and she had spelled her name for the Governor's men herself, one letter at a time, and she had corrected their spelling.

The customs house rose ahead of her against the grey morning, its clock ticking, its queues forming, Inspector Hale's shadow somewhere behind its windows. Hazel straightened her back and walked in through the doors of the building where, not two weeks before, she had smuggled plates past the very same inspectors in a coffin of lead type.

This time she was not smuggling anything.

This time she was the cargo—and she intended to be the most trouble they had ever unloaded.
