## 26. Odile's Change of Heart

The news reached Odile Verac at the tide tables, which was fitting, because Odile had long ago learned that all bad news traveled like water — finding every crack, pooling in every low place, arriving without knocking.

She was aboard the *Morning Tide*, moored at the outer quay where the harbor silt ran thick enough to smell it through the planks, going over her charts with a stub of red chalk when Tam came up the gangway at a dead run. She knew before he spoke. Boys who carry good news walk. Boys who carry bad news run, as if speed could leave it behind on the cobbles.

"Captain." He was breathing hard, salt-stained sleeves flapping, eyes too wide. "The print shop's burned. Hale's men. Last night."

Odile set down the chalk. Very carefully, so that the boy would not see how carefully.

"And?"

"They took her. Hazel. Marta got her out of the fire but they took her after — on the dock, after the cart. They marched her to the customs house." Tam swallowed. "Elias went in after them with papers. Nobody's seen him since either."

For a moment Odile said nothing at all. The harbor did what harbors do around silence: gulls screamed somewhere over the breakwater, a bell buoy clanked its dull iron note, water slapped the hull in that patient rhythm that wore stone down to sand given enough years. She looked at her charts. Thirty-two fathoms off the Serrated Rocks. A safe channel marked in her own hand. The route she'd been hired to run, carrying proof of a Governor's forgeries to a capital that might or might not care, for a payment that had been agreed upon in coin and had never once been questioned.

"Hazel Crane," Odile said, to no one, "is in the customs house."

"Yes, captain."

"And my cargo is not."

Tam shifted from foot to foot. He was fourteen and built out of elbows and urgency, and he had followed this woman into more danger than his mother knew about, mostly because she fed him well and never lied to him. He deserved the second thing now. Odile weighed it, the way she weighed everything — cargo, wind, risk — and found she did not want to lie to him today.

"Sit down, Tam," she said. "Eat something. Then you're going to tell me everything you saw, from the beginning, slowly, and you are not going to leave anything out because you think I'll be angry."

"I don't think you'll be angry," Tam said, taking the bench by the mainmast. "I've seen you angry. This isn't it."

*Perceptive child,* Odile thought. *Trouble later.* She poured him tea from the pot that never quite came to a full boil on her little spirit stove, and she listened.

* * *

He told it well, for a boy. The lantern-oil cart. The harbor gate. Hale waiting there personally, which Tam still seemed offended by, as though a man of Hale's rank should have sent underlings to be fooled. The moment when Hazel had stepped forward out of the crowd of dockworkers — *she just walked at him, captain, like she was walking into church* — and the bluff, God, the bluff: shouting that the cart carried the resistance's bomb, the real one, the one that would take the whole quay with it. Panic like a spilled bucket. Guards scattering, dragoons drawing pistols at shadows, stevedores stampeding.

"And while everyone ran," Tam said, "I ran the other way. With the case." His chin lifted. "I made the Tide. Barely. The dragoon chasing me fell in the mud by the fish stalls and cursed so bad I nearly stopped laughing."

"You didn't stop."

"Not till I was aboard."

"The case made it. The decrees made it. Elias's testimony, the matrices, all of it — it's in my hold right now," Odile said. "So the mission succeeded."

"Yes, captain."

"And Hazel Crane is in a cell."

Tam looked down at his tea. "Yes, captain."

Odile stood at the rail a long time, watching the grey water move beneath the grey sky, and let herself feel what she felt, briefly, thoroughly, the way she allowed herself to feel most things: privately, and then done with.

It would be easy to sail on the morning tide. That was the truth of it, the clean ugly truth that a mercenary learns early or doesn't survive. Her contract was fulfilled. Her cargo was aboard, sealed, dry, and worth more than the ship had cost. Every hour she stayed in Verrance harbor was an hour Inspector Hale — humiliated twice now, furious, hunting — could spend finding ways to impound the *Morning Tide*, search her hold, or simply put a ball through her waterline out of spite. A sensible captain sailed. A sensible captain had always sailed; it was the entire philosophy of her life, distilled: keep moving, keep paid, keep nothing that couldn't be replaced.

She had left things behind before. Whole ports. People. Once, a very long time ago, a coastline.

Odile Verac had been born on another coast entirely, in a village that no longer appeared on maps because maps were drawn by the people who burned it. She had been seven. Her family had gone to sea in whatever would float — her father, her mother, two brothers, an aunt whose name Odile had lost so completely that some days she doubted the aunt had ever existed. They had been carried by strangers. Fishermen, mostly. Smugglers. One naval deserter who wept the whole crossing and never said why. Nobody had been paid. Nobody had asked to be paid. They had simply looked at a boat full of frightened people from a burning shore and moved, the way water moves, because moving was what they did.

Her family had scattered further after that. Ports, ships, different names spelled differently on different manifests. Her father died in a place whose language Odile never learned. Her brothers — one wrote to her, sometimes, from three seas away, letters that took a year to arrive and said nothing that mattered. The other brother was a rumor. Her mother was a memory with a smell attached to it: tar and rosemary, inexplicably, forever.

And Odile herself had become this: a captain, a calculator, a woman whose word was iron precisely because she attached it to nothing she could not afford. Coin for services. Services rendered, coin collected, sails raised, horizon achieved. It was a good philosophy. It had kept her alive for thirty-odd years in a trade that ate sentimental people.

But it had never once kept anyone else alive. That was the flaw in it she tried not to look at, the way one tries not to look at rot in a hull plank. Standing at the rail of the *Morning Tide* with cold tea going lukewarm in her hand, she looked at it now, straight on, for the first time in years.

A sensible captain sailed on the morning tide.

* * *

Marta came aboard at dusk.

The old compositor climbed the gangway with the slow deliberateness of a woman whose hands had set ten thousand pages and whose knees had opinions about ladders. She carried a covered basket that smelled of bread and, underneath the bread, faintly, of ink. Odile met her at the rail.

"You shouldn't be seen here," Odile said.

"Nobody sees old women," Marta said. "That's the one advantage of my profession and my age combined. I can stand in front of a man's face reading a broadsheet and I am furniture." She lowered herself onto the coil of rope Odile kicked toward her, opened the basket, and began, maddeningly, to eat.

"Well?" Odile said.

"She's refusing to name anyone," Marta said, mouth half full. "You know this already or you wouldn't have sailed yet. But here's what your boy runner doesn't know, because I got it from the turnkey's wife, who buys her candles from my cousin." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Hale has offered her a bargain. Freedom for names. Standard practice. What's not standard is that she asked him for a graver instead."

"A what?"

"A cutting tool. Steel, small, sharp." Marta looked out at the darkening water. "They gave it to her, because Hale thinks a woman scratching marks on a wall has broken. She's cutting her compass into the cell wall. Letter by letter. The turnkey's wife says you can hear the steel in the stone at night. Like a clock that won't stop."

Odile turned away and gripped the rail.

There it was again, that feeling, inconvenient as weather. She thought of Hazel Crane the first time she'd seen her, down in the chandler's cellar, standing next to the underground press with ink on her fingers and her whole posture apologizing for existing. A woman built out of hiding places. And then Odile thought of the same woman at the harbor gate, stepping out of a crowd to shout a lie at a man with soldiers, spending her invisibility like a gambler shoving her last coins across the table.

And now she was in a stone room, cutting her own name into the wall in the only language she trusted.

"What are the tides?" Odile asked.

Marta blinked. "Tomorrow's low water at—"

"No. After. The day after. When does a prison transport make sense? When does the harbor open? Give me the shape of the week."

Marta's eyes sharpened. She was old and slow on stairs, but there was nothing slow behind her face. "You're going after her."

"I'm considering the freight costs," Odile said, "of leaving without her."

"That's a strange way to say it."

"I'm a strange woman."

Marta rummaged in her basket and brought out, wrapped in cloth, a folded broadsheet. She handed it over. Odile unfolded it in the last light and read it by the binnacle lamp: the first strike declaration of the Verrance printers, crude type, uneven margins, set in mixed fonts scavenged from a dozen ruined shops. At the bottom, printed large enough that even Hale would understand it, sat the mark. A compass rose inside a lantern.

"There are a thousand of these on the streets," Marta said quietly. "Every printer who could pull a lever pulled one yesterday. They're calling it her mark now. Not her brother's. Hers. The whole city knows the cut, even if they don't know the name." She paused. "Some of them know the name too. I may have mentioned it. To several hundred people."

"Marta."

"She earned it," the old woman said, without apology. "Besides — names are safer than secrets. A secret can be taken from you quietly. A name, everyone's already holding onto it. You'd have to take it back from the whole city." She smiled with all four of her remaining teeth. "I learned that from her, in a way. Or from watching what hiding did to her. Twenty years behind her brother's name, captain. Twenty years being excellent in the dark. You know what that costs a person. I watched you count your coin when she asked you to carry those decrees, and I watched you argue about collateral, and I thought: there's a woman who understands hiding. You hid behind your prices."

Odile looked at her sharply. Marta met the look with the serene insolence of the very old.

"And what," Odile said slowly, "do you propose I hide behind instead?"

"Crew," Marta said. "It's sturdier. Doesn't sink when the coin runs out."

* * *

Odile did not sleep that night. She told herself it was the tide tables.

She spread them out in the lamplit cabin and worked the problem the way she worked every problem: currents, depths, guard rotations, the geometry of the customs house quay, the draft clearance under the chain bridge, the sight lines from the fort. She calculated the weight of a prisoner transfer wagon versus a closed carriage. She calculated bribes — the turnkey, the harbormaster's clerks, always the clerks, the men whose signatures actually moved things. She calculated how much of her carefully hoarded reserve, accumulated over eleven years of honest smuggling and dishonest accounting, it would cost to buy one master cutter out of a colonial jail, and the number came back obscene.

Then she calculated what the *Morning Tide*, her hold, her crew of nine, her own self, was worth as forfeit if Hale caught them, and that number came back worse than obscene. It came back total.

A sensible woman would sail.

She put down the dividers and sat in the little rocking lamplight and let the memories come, because she was too tired and too honest to bar the door against them. Her mother's hands steering a leaking boat. The deserter weeping. The taste of rain on open water with a burning coast behind her and nothing ahead but strangers' mercy. She had spent thirty years repaying that debt in coin — paying pilots fairly, feeding runner boys, never once hauling a cargo of slaves or pressed men — as if fairness were the same thing as sacrifice. As if arithmetic could settle what had been paid for her in pure, unprofitable kindness.

Strangers had carried her family for free. And she had built a whole life on the principle that nobody rides unless they pay.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the bell buoy clanked. Odile Verac, owner and master of the schooner *Morning Tide*, put her head in her hands and laughed once, short and hard, at the magnificent joke the sea had finally played on her: she had survived every storm, every inspection, every betrayal the coast could offer, and she was going to be wrecked, in the end, by a quiet woman with ink stains and a graver, cutting a compass into a cell wall where nobody could even see it.

Cutting it anyway. That was the part. Cutting it where nobody would ever see it, because the mark was hers, and making it was not negotiable, and freedom was not the price of identity, not anymore, not for her.

"Damn you," Odile said affectionately, to an absent printer, and reached for fresh paper.

* * *

At dawn she summoned them. Tam first, because he was aboard. Then Marta, who arrived smelling of candle wax and conspiracy. Then, to her own mild surprise, she sent a runner to the census office with a message written in careful cipher: *Freight negotiation reopened. All parties attend. Bring your steady hand.*

Elias Wren came aboard at mid-morning looking like a man who had walked out of his own funeral — pale, rumpled, singed at one cuff, with the particular hollow-eyed steadiness of someone who had burned evidence in a stove in front of an inspector and walked out on procedure alone. He took in the assembled company, the charts, Odile's expression, and said only, "What's the freight?"

"Gone," Odile said.

"I'm sorry?"

"The price. It's gone. Waived." She planted both fists on the chart table and looked around at them — the boy, the old woman, the bureaucrat — these absurd, reckless, brave, unprofitable people who had somehow become, without her permission, the closest thing to a crew she'd had since she was seven years old on a leaking boat. "Listen carefully, because I'll say this once and I'll deny it in any court in the capital. The *Morning Tide* sails when Hazel Crane is aboard. Not before. Her passage, her rescue, her ransom if it comes to ransom, comes out of my pocket and my hold, and I will not be repaid, because debts between crew aren't debts. We're past that. I should have known we were past that the night I demanded collateral from a woman who'd already given a whole city her mark for free."

Tam's grin split his face. Marta nodded slowly, like a judge confirming a verdict she'd reached days ago.

Elias cleared his throat. "That's generous—"

"It's not generous," Odile snapped. "Generous is giving away money. This is me correcting an accounting error twenty-five years overdue. There's a difference, and if anyone repeats what I just said I'll put them ashore at the Serrated Rocks." She swept her hand across the chart table, and the charts came with it, fanning out across the scarred wood: channels, depths, guard posts, all of it annotated overnight in red chalk in her fierce precise hand. "Now. Here's the shape of it. Prison transports leave the customs house for the fort on Thursdays, by the north road — but Thursday's out, because Hale will expect exactly that, and he's expecting us anyway. So we don't go by road at all. We go by water."

She stabbed one finger at the harbor chart, at the seaward face of the customs house, where the old foundation stones dropped straight into twelve feet of green-black tide.

"There's a coal chute at the base, disused, big enough for a slim person. The grate's rusted — I checked it myself at slack water, an hour ago, before any of you were awake." She looked up. "Low tide tomorrow is at four in the morning. Dark until six. That's our window. After that, Hale moves her, or the Governor's commission arrives and none of this matters."

"And the harbor gate?" Elias asked quietly. "Hale will be watching it. He watches everything now."

"He'll watch it," Odile agreed. Her mouth curved, and there was nothing warm in it — only the cold bright joy of a sailor who has finally, after a lifetime of caution, chosen her storm. "Which is why the *Morning Tide* is going to create, tomorrow afternoon, a spectacular customs violation on the far side of the breakwater. Something that requires every launch, every lantern, and every dragoon in Verrance to investigate. A decoy, gentlemen and lady. I'm told this crew makes excellent decoys."

"And while they chase your ghost ship," Marta said, "we go through the coal chute."

"While they chase my ghost ship," Odile said, "one of us goes through the coal chute, and walks out through the front door with Hazel Crane on their arm, wearing the most audacious lie anyone's worn in this city since a printer shouted *bomb* at a quay full of soldiers." She straightened, and rolled the chart closed, and the matter, so far as Odile Verac was concerned, was decided, settled, and paid in full. "Now somebody tell me everything about that chute grate, and somebody else get me a list of every clerk in the harbormaster's office who owes me money, because by God, we're going to spend it all."

Above them, through the deck beams, they heard the harbor bell ring eight times, and beneath it, faint and constant, the turning of the tide.
