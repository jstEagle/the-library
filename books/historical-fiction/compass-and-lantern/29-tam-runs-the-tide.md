## 29. Tam Runs the Tide

The case was heavier than it looked.

That was the first thing Tam understood, standing in the shadow of the oil cart at the harbor gate while Hale's voice cut the fog behind him: that a thing no bigger than a bread box could weigh as much as a city. The leather was stiff under his arms, the brass seals cold through his sleeves, and inside — he had been told, though nobody had shown him, because you didn't show a twelve-year-old what the whole uprising hung on — were the decrees, the matrices, and the census man's written testimony, wrapped in oilcloth against the salt spray.

*Run when I nod,* Odile had said, and her nod was coming, he could feel it coming in the way her shoulders squared, in the way her hand drifted to the lantern hanging from the cart's rail like it might need lifting soon.

He counted his breaths the way Marta had taught him. In for four, out for four, the way she said kept your hands steady when you set type too fast. His hands were steady. His legs felt like they'd been filled with harbor water.

Behind him, Hazel's voice rose — not loud, but pitched strange, pitched wrong, the way a bell sounds wrong just before it cracks — something about *the real one*, something about *bomb*, and then the world came apart into shouting.

Odile nodded.

Tam ran.

* * *

The quarter-mile to the quay was the longest street in Verrance, though any map would tell him different. He knew it by his feet before he knew it by his eyes: the cobbles that dipped where the old drainage channel ran beneath, the seam of cobbles patched with newer stone after the flood year, the gutter outside the rope-walk gate that always stank of tar even at low tide. He had run this street a hundred times for a hundred errands — messages folded small and swallowed into his cheek if the wrong person grabbed him, coins sewn into his cuff lining, lies worn smooth as sea glass from use.

Never anything like this. Never anything that mattered this much.

His boots hammered the dipped cobbles. The case bounced against his hip and he hauled it up, clamped it under his arm the way Odile showed him, elbow locked, body curled around it like a gull around a stolen fish. The fog was thinning near the water — the tide was turning, he could smell the change, that green-silver breath the harbor gave off when the water began to pull back out toward the open sea. The *Morning Tide* wouldn't wait past the turn. Captain's rule, Odile's rule, the only rule in Verrance that nobody broke, because the breakwater passage went shallow as a dinner plate at slack water and stayed that way for two hours. If the tide turned before the case was aboard, everything they had done — the coffin at customs, the bridge of chains, Elias standing somewhere behind him doing whatever it was census men did with their steady hands and their lawful words — all of it would come apart like wet paper.

In for four. Out for four.

The rope-walk gate flashed past on his left. A dog barked once and thought better of it. Somewhere behind — far enough back that he couldn't see, close enough that he could hear — boots were hammering too, heavier than his, more of them than one.

A dragoon. Maybe two. He didn't look. Looking cost speed, and speed was the whole of his job now. That was the thing about being the runner, the thing nobody told you until it was your turn: you weren't the clever one, weren't the one who cut the marks or argued strategy or stood in doorways reciting harbor law. You were just the legs. And legs could not afford to wonder. Legs could only run.

But his mind ran anyway, ahead of him, sideways, everywhere, the way minds do when the body is fully occupied. He thought of Hazel's face last night in the cellar, watching him eat the stew she'd made, and how she'd looked at him like he was something worth looking at — not a convenience, not a pair of quick legs, but a person, an *almost-brother*, she'd said, and the word had gone into him like a coal into a stove and was burning there still. He thought of Marta saying *carry it like it's nothing, because nothing is the only thing nobody takes from a boy*. He thought of Ferro, broken Ferro, whose brother sat in a cell so that Tam's brother — the found kind, the made kind, the kind you got instead of the ones the fever took — could stand in a doorway tonight and hold off a man with the government in his pocket.

He thought, most of all, of the case under his arm. What was in it wasn't paper. He understood that now, running, really understood it for the first time, the way you understand things only when your blood is going too fast for lying. It was the Governor's signature, the forged one, matched against the true one. It was every farm in the valley, every family turned off land their grandfathers cleared. It was Hazel's name, the one she'd never been allowed to sign. It was the hangman's rope Elias had watched swing once before and sworn never to help again. A city's truth, Marta called it, and here it was, and it weighed less than a cat, and Tam carried it through the fish market with his lungs full of fire.

* * *

The market was empty at this hour except for the smell. Low tide always doubled it — fish and brine and the black mud breathing out its sourness between the pilings — and Tam ran through it with his eyes streaming, leaping a coil of net, skidding on mackerel scales gone slick as soap on the second-day cobblestones. A gull screamed overhead, outraged at being disturbed, and the sound tore a wild laugh out of him, high and cracked, a laugh with fear in it, and he let the fear stay in the laugh where it could do no harm.

The boots behind him were closer. He could pick out the rhythm now: one runner, heavy, iron-shod, the cadence slightly uneven — favoring the left leg, maybe, or just heavy with gear. Dragoons wore sabers and those long-barreled pistols, and neither of them made you faster. That was the arithmetic Tam ran by, the sum he did over and over with his feet: *I am lighter, I know the stones, I have farther to fall if I fall, so I must not fall.*

He cut left at the drying racks, down the slot between the salting sheds where the grown men couldn't follow two abreast, and for six glorious strides he was alone with his own breathing and the slap of wet wood under his palms as he steadied himself through the turn. Then the shed wall ended and the quay opened before him, gray and vast in the pre-dawn, and there it was —

— the *Morning Tide*, riding low in the outer roads past the breakwater, her boat already pulled up at the bottom of the stone steps, two of Odile's crew standing in it, faces upturned, waiting.

And between Tam and the steps, the tide was turning.

He saw it in the way the water moved around the harbor pilings — the eddies reversing, the scum-lines straightening out from their curls, the whole gray surface taking on that slow, muscular lean toward the sea. He saw it in the gap opening between the lowest step and the waterline, wet stone widening inch by inch. Time was leaving the harbor the way the light was: steadily, without ceremony, without caring what anybody carried or needed or loved.

Twenty strides to the steps.

Fifteen.

The boots burst out of the shed slot behind him and Tam heard the dragoon shout — *"STOP, in the Governor's—"* — and didn't stop, because stopping was a word and running was the world, and he took the last strides in a kind of terrible flying joy, the case hugged to his chest now, both arms around it, the way you carry a thing that is also a living thing, the way you'd carry a child, the way somebody must have carried him once, down a road, away from fever, too late for his brother but not for him —

The steps. Wet stone, descending. The boat below, rising and falling a foot at a time.

*"Boy!"*

The dragoon's hand closed on the collar of his coat.

Tam did the only thing there was to do, the thing every runner in Verrance knew without ever having been taught it: he let the coat go.

It happened slow in his memory forever after — the buttons tearing free one two three, the fabric sliding up off his shoulders like water, the cold air on his shirt-sleeves, the case swinging safe and untouched in his arms while the dragoon staggered backward holding nothing but a coat with a boy's name sewn into the collar in Marta's careful thread. *T. Voss, Crane & Sons.* A false name. She'd sewn it smiling, saying every runner should be someone else on paper, and now the someone else went to the floor of the quay in a soldier's fist while the real boy took the steps three at a time.

"Cast off!" Tam roared, and it came out half a scream, his voice breaking on it, and he didn't care. "CAST OFF!"

The boat crew surged. Tam hit the gunwale, vaulted — hands catching, someone hauling him in, the case passed overhead like a holy thing from grip to grip — and the painter dropped free of the ring and oars bit water and the boat swung out from the steps just as the dragoon reached them, saber drawn now, screaming orders at a stone quay and a turning tide and no boat within reach.

Tam fell onto the thwart, chest heaving, and laughed again, that same cracked laugh, and this time the crew laughed with him, short and hard, the laughter of men who knew exactly how many inches had just separated them from a rope.

Then he sat up straight, because the case was in the bow, and it was still sealed, and it was still theirs, and the tide was carrying them now — he could feel it under the keel even through the oar-strokes, the great patient pull of the harbor emptying itself seaward, bearing the truth of Verrance out past the breakwater toward the capital like it had been waiting all along to do exactly this.

* * *

The *Morning Tide* rose out of the fog proper as they came alongside, dark hull streaked with salt, rigging furred with droplets. Odile's bosun caught the case before Tam could climb, and for one lurching heartbeat Tam's hands were empty and his whole body panicked — a raw, animal *no* — before the bosun slung it down the hatch with two other men guarding it, and Tam breathed again.

They let him go up the side himself. Nobody helped, which he understood was the compliment.

On deck, the mate broke one seal and checked the contents by shuttered lantern — Tam watched from a distance he knew better than to cross — and then looked up, and nodded, and shouted the word forward, and somewhere in the dark of the ship the capstan began to turn and the anchor chain came up through the hawse dripping silver in the lantern light.

Tam stood at the rail with his torn collar flapping around his bare throat and watched Verrance recede. The city lay along its hill like embers banked for morning — a few lamps, a smear of smoke rising from the direction of the print shop district that he made himself look at, straight on, because Hazel would want to know someone had seen it and remembered. Behind the smoke, faint and far off, bells were ringing. Not alarm bells. Not yet. The other kind — the kind printers rang, the kind that meant lanterns were being lit in windows all over the lower town, one by one, in defiance of curfew, in answer to a mark stamped on a thousand broadsheets: a compass rose cut into a lantern.

The tide carried the ship past the breakwater. The swells changed, longer now, deeper, the rhythm of open water, and the *Morning Tide* leaned into them like a creature remembering its element.

"You're crying," said the bosun beside him, not unkindly.

"It's the spray," said Tam, which was true, and also not, and both of them let it go.

He thought about the quarter-mile behind him. About the coat in the dragoon's fist with T. VOSS stitched in Marta's thread — and then he stopped thinking, abruptly, coldly, the way a runner stops when the ground changes under him.

Marta hadn't sewn a false name into that coat. He'd seen it a dozen times. He'd traced the letters with his finger in the cellar, proud of it, his name, his real one, the one his mother had given him before the fever, the one he used for nothing else in this whole city of aliases.

*Tam Voss.* Real. On the quay. In the hands of the Governor's men.

The ship sailed on toward the capital, carrying everything — the decrees, the matrices, the testimony, the truth entire. And Tam stood at the rail with the wind pulling tears from his eyes whether he cried or not, and understood that the price of the morning had already been paid, quietly, in thread, weeks ago, and that somewhere behind him in the waking city, a dragoon was carrying his name to Inspector Hale.

The bells rang louder across the water. The tide ran strong and sure. And Tam Voss turned his face toward the capital and began, for the first time since the shed slot, to be afraid.
