## 32. The Uprising Ignites

Marta had set type for forty years, and in all that time she had learned one truth above the others: ink dries slower than blood, but it stains deeper.

She was at the cellar press when the smoke first showed above the grating — a thin gray finger rising through the cobbles of Chandler's Lane, curling into the morning like a question no one wanted asked. Tam had gone out for bread. He came back white as unprinted paper, the loaf still clutched against his chest like something he'd rescued from a fire himself.

"Crane & Sons," he said. "It's gone. Marta, it's *gone*. The whole front of it, the windows, the sign — the sign's melted."

Marta's hands stopped on the composing stick. A line of type sat half-set in her grip, the words *the Governor's decree* upside down and backwards, as everything true was before it was pressed. She set the stick down very carefully, the way her mother had set down knives when grief came to the door.

She had dragged Hazel out of that burning building not two hours past. She could still feel the girl's weight against her shoulder, still smell the scorch in her hair, still hear the sound Hazel made when the type rack went — not a scream, nothing so simple. A sound like a bell cracking. Marta had heard it once before, thirty years ago in Verrance's last uprising, when they broke a press in the street with hammers and the printer who owned it made that same sound, watching forty years of his life come apart in splinters.

"They took her," Tam said. "The graycoats. I saw them put her in a wagon. Odile's gone after the ship — she says the Tide sails with or without — and Elias is — I don't know where Elias is."

Marta nodded. Her hands, of their own old habit, had begun to sort type. *A*, *n*, *d*. She looked down at what they spelled and almost laughed.

"Put the bread down, boy," she said. "We're going to need our strength. Tonight the city learns to read."

* * *

By noon, Marta had walked every street between Chandler's Lane and the harbor, and what she saw she catalogued the way she catalogued a case of type — everything in its compartment, everything waiting for the hand that knew how to arrange it.

At the fish market, the wives of the net-menders were not buying. They stood in clusters with their arms folded, and when the harbor master's clerk came down the steps with his ledger, old Sef the net-mender spat at his feet and said, loud enough for the whole quay to hear, that no man who'd watched Crane's shop burn would load a Governor's ship, not this week, not this year, not while his hands remembered how to be empty.

At the ropewalk, the spinners had stopped. The great shed stood silent as a church, and the smell of tar hung in the cold air like incense over an unburied body.

And everywhere — everywhere — the lanterns.

It began, as these things do, with a single gesture no one could later trace. A chandler's boy hung an unlit lantern in his shop window at midday, in broad daylight, where it made no sense at all. By three o'clock there were lanterns in the windows of the bakery on Cordwainer Row. By four, the taverns. By dusk, when the fog came in off the water the way it always did, gray and patient as a debt collector, the lanterns were everywhere — hanging from signposts, from balcony rails, from the rigging of the fishing smacks at anchor, a hundred small flames bobbing on the black water like a congregation of souls.

They were Hazel's mark, most of them. Someone — Tam, Marta suspected, or one of the cellar press runners — had cut a stencil, compass rose inside a lantern, and the shape was being chalked on walls, scratched into doorposts, burned into barrel staves with a hot poker. Marta saw it daubed in fish-gut slime on the customs house steps, so high and so bold that scrubbing it away would take a man on a ladder and a day's shame in front of the whole queue.

*She is everywhere now,* Marta thought, standing in Cordwainer Row with the fog beading on her shawl. *They put her in a wagon thinking they'd made her small, and they've made her the size of the city.*

She had known Hazel Crane for three years — known her as the silent niece, the one who kept the ledgers, who spoke only when spoken to, who had a way of standing in a room as though she'd been typeset in invisible ink. Marta was a compositor. She knew invisible ink when she saw it. She'd known for two of those three years, from the way the girl's hands moved when she thought no one watched — the way she straightened a crooked sort without looking, the way she read a galley proof upside down at a glance, the way she'd once, drunk on one glass of Odile's smuggled brandy, described the exact bevel of a graver's edge with the tenderness other women spent on lovers.

And now the girl was in a cell, and the mark she'd cut in secret was burning in a thousand windows, and the city that had never known her name was saying it without saying it, in flame.

Marta turned up her collar and went to find the other printers.

* * *

They met in the back room of the Drowned Bell, because the Drowned Bell's owner owed Marta a debt from the old days and because its cellar had a second exit into the coal yards. There were nine of them. Once there had been forty printers in Verrance; the Governor's decrees on seditious publication had thinned them the way winter thins a flock, and the ones who remained had learned to keep their heads down and their presses printing wedding invitations and bills of lading.

Old Piotr came with ink still under his fingernails from his morning's honest work. The Voss sisters came together, as they did everything. Young Anders, who was not yet thirty and had never known a free press, came because his father's name was on the list of printers hanged in the last uprising, and because grief, Marta knew, looks for work the way water looks for a low place.

"We strike," Marta said, when the door was barred. No preamble. She was too old for preamble.

"Strike how?" Piotr asked. "They burned Crane's shop in an afternoon. What do you think they'll do to the rest of us?"

"Exactly that," Marta said. "Eventually. Whether we strike or not. Hale has his list — we all know he has his list, he's had it for a year, he's just been waiting for an excuse to work down it. The question is whether we're ash in our beds or ash on our feet."

Anders laughed — a short, hard sound, surprised out of him. The Voss sisters didn't laugh at all. The elder one, Greta, leaned forward into the lamplight.

"The harbor's already stopped," she said. "My husband works the grain quay. They refused the warship *Vigilant* this morning. Refused her powder and shot. The harbor master threatened them with the press gang and they stood there, four hundred of them, with their arms folded, and he looked at four hundred folded arms and went home."

"Then the city has voted," Marta said. "Before we did. We're only catching up." She put her hands flat on the table, and every eye in the room went to them, because every printer in Verrance knew those hands. Forty years of composition. Hands that had set the last free broadsheet of the old uprising, the one they'd hanged Joren Bell for distributing, the one Marta had set in a cellar by candlelight while the dragoons kicked in doors three streets away.

"Here is what we do," she said. "Tonight, every press in this room prints the same thing. One page. The compass and the lantern, and beneath it three lines: *They burned a print shop. They could not burn the truth. The decrees are forged.* Nothing else. No argument, no essay — the harbor workers can't read essays and don't want them. Three lines and a mark. We print a thousand. Two thousand. The runners take them out with the morning fish carts, and by noon every market stall in Verrance has one, and every window."

"And if the graycoats come?" Piotr asked quietly.

"Then we do what Hazel Crane did," Marta said. "We hide the truth inside something they don't think to search. Print the broadsheets inside a fake wedding announcement if you must — fold them into orders for mourning clothes. Grief is legal. Let's dress the truth as grief, because that's what it is."

She looked around the lamplight at their faces — old Piotr's fear, the Voss sisters' flint, Anders's burning — and she felt something she had not felt in thirty years, not since the night Joren Bell went to the gallows and the city went quiet and she had told herself, *never again, never again, the price is too high.* She felt the low place in herself filling with water. She felt useful.

"Set your type," she said.

* * *

The cellar of the Drowned Bell smelled of coal smoke, lamp oil, and ink — the smell Marta had slept inside, in one form or another, since she was nine years old and her father first put a composing stick in her hand. It was the smell of her whole life, and tonight it smelled like the first night of something.

They worked in shifts through the small hours. Anders ran the press — a battered hand-press smuggled down in pieces over three weeks, a thing of rust and stubbornness that groaned like a dying animal with every pull — while Marta locked the form and the Voss sisters fed and stacked. The broadsheets came off damp, three lines and the mark, the compass rose inside the lantern cut that very evening from a linoleum floor scrap by Greta Voss, who had never held a graver before tonight and whose first cut was crooked and whose second was true.

Marta pulled one from the stack while the press groaned on, and held it to the lamp.

*They burned a print shop. They could not burn the truth. The decrees are forged.*

And the mark. Hazel's mark. Marta had watched the girl cut the original punch in the cellar under the chandler's shop — had watched her bent over the steel for six hours, silent, her whole being narrowed to a point finer than a needle's eye — and she had understood then, watching, that she was seeing a kind of prayer. Craft as mourning. Craft as defiance. The girl had been saying goodbye to her father and her brother in the only language she had, and the language had come out as a compass rose inside a lantern, and now it was on two thousand sheets of paper and a hundred windowpanes and the customs house steps, and no power in the colonies could gather it all back up again.

"You knew her," Anders said, pausing between pulls. "Crane. The cutter. You knew what she was."

"I knew she was the best hand in this city," Marta said. "The rest was her business."

"They say she's in the Fort cell. The stone cells, under the water line."

"I know where they put her," Marta said, and her voice came out flat as a press bed, and Anders, to his credit, asked nothing more.

* * *

Toward dawn, Tam came down the coal chute with fog in his hair and news in his mouth.

The harbor was a bonfire of lanterns — every smack, every lighter, every moored hulk hung with flame, so that from the heights the whole anchorage looked like a spilled constellation. The *Vigilant* sat at her berth with empty holds and a skeleton crew, and the harbor master had posted a proclamation offering double wages, and the proclamation had been torn down before the paste dried and replaced with a broadsheet — *three lines and a mark* — and the harbor master had looked at it for a long moment and then gone into his office and shut the door.

At the rope walk, the spinners had marched to the Governor's house and stood in the fog, silent, holding unlit lanterns, four hundred of them, for one hour, and then dispersed without a word spoken. Greta's husband said the silence was worse than shouting. Greta's husband said the Governor's windows had gone dark one by one, as though the house itself were flinching.

And in the streets, Tam said, breathless, perched on a coal sack with the dawn gray at the chute's mouth behind him — in the streets, people who had never spoken to each other were nodding. The baker and the fishwife. The carter and the clerk. A nod, a glance at the lantern in the window, a nod back. A whole city learning a new alphabet overnight, and the alphabet had one letter, and the letter was flame.

"And Odile?" Marta asked. "The Tide?"

"Gone out on the morning tide," Tam said. "With the case aboard. Odile went with her — no, wait, no, Odile put the case aboard and stayed. She's at the chandler's cellar. She says —" Tam hesitated, and Marta saw the boy struggling with something too large for his vocabulary, the way he struggled with long words and with grief. "She says we don't leave people in cells. She says that's the whole point. That a crew is a crew, on a ship or off one."

Marta closed her eyes. In the dark behind them she saw the girl in the wagon, the scorch in her hair, the sound like a bell cracking.

* * *

They came for the Drowned Bell at midmorning — not the graycoats but the city watch, apologetic as a bill collector, with an order signed by Inspector Hale declaring the tavern closed for sedition. The owner paid the fine with coin he didn't have and stood in his empty taproom looking at the space behind the bar where the cask of smuggled brandy had been, and Marta, watching from across the street through the fog, felt the old fear settle over her like the fog itself.

Let them close the tavern. Let them take the press if they found it — they wouldn't; Anders and the Voss sisters had it in pieces in the coal cellar before the watch finished their breakfast. The broadsheets were already out. Two thousand of them, folded into fish carts and laundry baskets and hymnals, moving through the morning city like blood through a body.

By noon, Marta stood in the fish market and watched a harbor worker — a great gray-bearded man with hands like knotted rope — read one aloud to a circle of illiterate deckhands, his voice cracking on the third line, and the deckhands standing with their caps in their hands as though at a funeral. Which, Marta thought, was exactly what it was. A funeral for a print shop. And funerals, in Verrance, had always been where the city did its truest thinking.

By evening, the warships rode empty at anchor, the ropewalk was silent, the customs house steps had been scrubbed of the mark and re-chalked with it twice before the scrubbers gave up, and the Governor's house stood dark behind its gates while the whole harbor burned with small obedient flames.

Marta stood on the quay in the dark with the fog coming in, an old woman with ink in the creases of her hands that forty years of lye soap had never fully lifted, and she let herself feel it — not triumph, nothing so clean, but the fierce, aching, complicated thing underneath triumph. Grief, transfigured. Joren Bell's rope. Her father's broken press. Hazel's burned type rack. All of it gathered up now into four hundred folded arms and a thousand lit windows and a ship gone east with the truth in its hold.

They had taken the girl. They thought they had taken the girl.

Tomorrow, Marta thought, turning up the quay toward the chandler's cellar, where Odile was waiting with charts and coin and a face like a drawn blade, tomorrow we go and get her back.

She did not yet know how. She knew only that a city that had learned to read by lantern light would not sit quietly while its cutter sat in a stone cell under the water line, and that somewhere in the Fort's ledgers — she smiled at the thought, an old compositor's smile — somewhere in those ledgers there would be a line, a form, a technicality, a loose sort waiting for the right hand.

Every lock was type. It only needed setting.
