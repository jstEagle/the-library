## 36. Ashes and Sorts

The rain had come in the night, soft and indifferent, as though the sky had only just noticed the ruin below it and thought to dampen the ashes before they blew away. It had done neither. When Hazel Crane came down Lantern Row in the grey of morning, the print shop that had borne her father's name for thirty-one years stood open to the weather like a split chestnut shell, its blackened ribs holding up nothing but smoke-stained air.

She stopped at the threshold. She did not go in. Not yet.

There was a ritual to thresholds; her father had taught her that without ever meaning to. *Wipe your feet, Hazel-girl. A shop remembers who enters clean and who enters careless.* She stood a long moment on the scorched step where the brass bell had once hung, and found its melted puddle fused into the iron bracket, a dull teardrop of metal that would never ring again. Then she wiped her boots on the wet stone, twice, three times, out of habit or grief or some older superstition she had not known she carried, and stepped inside.

The smell was the worst of it. Not the sharp char she had braced herself for—that had mostly washed away—but something deeper and more intimate: ink burned into its component stinks, linseed oil gone acrid, the leather of the pressing boards rendered down to a sweetness like old blood. And beneath it all, faint as a ghost's cologne, the mineral tang of type metal. Lead and antimony and tin. The smell of her childhood. It had survived the fire the way a voice survives in a seashell, and it undid her more thoroughly than any flame could have.

She did not weep. That surprised her. In the weeks since the burning—weeks spent in a cell, weeks spent being freed by coin and cunning and law in equal measure—she had assumed this moment would break her. Instead she felt something closer to stillness, the hush of a house after a fever has passed. The shop was dead. Grief for the dead is a different animal from grief for what might have been, and it walks slower and demands less.

*Well,* she said to the ashes, in her father's cadence without intending it. *Let's see what you left us.*

She had brought tools: a stiff broom, a tarpaulin, two empty flour sacks, a pair of heavy gloves with the fingers worn thin, and the small iron rake Marta had pressed into her hands at the door of the chandler's cellar that morning with a look that said both *be careful* and *don't be long*, which were the same thing in Marta's language. Elias had offered to come. She had told him no—not unkindly, but firmly, the way one refuses help lifting something that must be lifted alone. Some weights are not weights at all when shared; they become ceremonies, and ceremonies lose their power when witnessed.

She worked first at the doorway, clearing a path. The floor had been oak once, scarred and beautiful, every plank carrying the ghost of a dropped quoine or a spilled measure of solvent. Now it was a field of rubble: slates from the collapsed loft, lath, the twisted iron of the stove door, great cakes of ash that held their shape like dark bread and crumbled at a touch into clouds that tasted of everything the shop had been. She raked gently. She had decided this on the walk over—gently, always gently—for beneath the wreckage there might be things worth saving, and the difference between salvage and destruction, in a ruined print shop, is often no more than the weight of a hand.

By midmorning the light through the broken windows had turned from pewter to pale gold, and Hazel had learned the geography of the ruin. The composing stone had cracked clean across but lay where it had always lain, and when she swept the debris from its surface she found, imprinted in the soot, the perfect negative of the last forme her father had locked up—a jobbing handbill, she could tell from the size of the letters, an advertisement for someone's patent cough drops. His last work, pressed into ash the way a fossil presses into shale. She knelt over it for a long time, not touching, memorizing.

The press itself was a wound. Her father had bought the Albion secondhand the year Hazel was born, and the two of them had grown crooked and strong together, the machine and the girl. Fire had warped the frame into a shape like a praying man struck down mid-devotion. The screw was seized. The platen hung askew. She put her palm against the cheek of the frame, expecting cold iron, and found it warm—the sun through the east window, nothing more—and yet she flinched as if the press had spoken.

*I know,* she told it. *I know.*

It was near noon, working along the wall where the type racks had stood, that she found the first of them.

A single sort, lying face-down in the ash. She turned it with a fingertip: a lowercase e, twenty-four point, bearded and beautiful, its face unmarked. The fire had taken the rack, the cases, the leads and quoins and half the matrices, but heat, it seemed, had poured past this corner like a flood around a standing stone. She set the e carefully in the tin cup she'd brought for the purpose. It made a small bright click against the metal, and the sound went through her like a struck tuning fork.

After that she worked on her knees, sifting handful after handful of ash through her gloved fingers, and the shop began to give up its dead.

An ampersand, its counters choked with soot. Three n's, an o, a question mark bent almost double. A capital C—her heart lurched, absurdly, possessively—that proved on inspection to be pitted and unsound. She kept it anyway. A whole line of ten-point body type, fused into a brick of metal by the fire's breath, reading backward and upside down: *GOODS DELIVERED PROMPTLY TO ALL PARTS.* Somewhere in the city there was a merchant who had paid for that promise and never known how it ended. She laid the fused line in the sack whole. Broken type could be recast. Everything, she was beginning to understand, could be recast, given a crucible and the patience to wait for the metal to flow.

Her knees ached. Her back spoke in a low constant complaint. She noticed none of it. There is a state that comes over her at such work, and it had come over her now: the world narrowing to the span of her two hands, time going soft and unhurried as tallow, every sense given wholly to the small country between her fingertips. It was the same state she entered when cutting a punch, and she recognized, with a small inward astonishment, that she was happy. Not glad—happy was a word for other people, other lives—but something adjacent. Purposeful. A woman doing precisely what her hands were made for, in the precise place her hands were made for, though the place was a ruin and the hands were filthy to the wrist.

The sun had crossed the yard and was laying long amber bars through the west window when she found the box.

It was under the collapsed bench—her bench, the cutting bench, she knew it by the iron holdfast still bolted to its leg—and it was a box she had not seen in eleven years. Oak, banded in brass, its lid scorched brown but intact, wedged beneath the bench as if someone had shoved it there against a coming disaster. Her breath went strange. She drew it out with both hands, and it was heavier than memory said, and lighter than fear said, and she sat back on her heels in the ash with it in her lap like a thing newborn.

The latch opened at a touch. Inside, nested in wool gone grey with age, lay her father's first punch.

He had cut it when he was younger than she was now—an a, lowercase, twelve point, the founding letter of the Crane text face, the letter from which all the others in that family had descended the way children descend from a mother. He had shown it to her exactly once, when she was nine, taking it from this same box with hands that shook slightly, though she had been too young then to understand why a grown man should tremble at a piece of steel the size of a thumbnail. *This is where we began, Hazel-girl. Everything else is elaboration.* He had let her look for the length of ten heartbeats, no more, and then he had put it away, and she had understood even at nine that she was being trusted with the sight of something sacred, and had carried the trust like a coal in her chest ever since.

Now she took it up with the gloves off, because some things must be touched skin to steel, and turned it to the light.

He had been magnificent. Even fire-scorched, even these eleven years blind in the dark, the a was flawless—the bowl true, the stem confident, that slight forward lean at the apex that gave Crane text its characteristic urgency, as though every word set in it were leaning into a wind. She saw, as she had not been old enough to see at nine, where he had corrected himself: a faint facet near the join of bowl and stem where he had re-cut, dissatisfied, and left the correction visible rather than polish it away. Her father, who preached perfection to his apprentices, had signed his own imperfection into his masterwork and kept it all his life.

She held the punch until her eyes blurred, and this time the tears came, two of them only, quick and hot, falling into the ash at her knees where they made two small dark planets. That was all. Her father had been dead six years and her brother four, and she had grieved them sideways and in secret and never properly, because proper grief requires a name and she had buried hers along with them. But kneeling in the ruin of everything they built, holding the letter that began it, she found she could finally say the plain words to the empty air.

*Papa. Tam. I'm the one who kept the bench. I'm the one who kept the letters. Forgive me the lying, and thank you for the steel.*

The shop received it without echo. Ruins are good listeners.

She wrapped the punch in the least-burned corner of the wool and returned it to its box, and set the box in the bottom of her sack above the sorted type, and then she sat a while longer in the gold light with her hands empty in her lap, doing nothing, which was its own kind of work.

* * *

Elias found her there in the late afternoon.

She heard him on the Row before she saw him—he walked like a man perpetually apologizing to the cobblestones—and she did not turn as he came through the doorway, wiped his boots on the wet stone with unconscious fidelity to her father's rule, and stood beside her surveying the ruin with the particular helpless expression of a man whose weapons are all paperwork.

"I brought bread," he said. "And tea, in a flask. And—" he hesitated, produced a folded paper from his coat, "—a permit."

"A permit."

"Occupational reconstruction. Provisional." He handed it over as though it were something volatile. "The new administration is issuing them to any trade displaced by the—by the troubles. It took me most of the morning and only three clerks, one of whom owed me a favor I intend never to collect." He paused. "It has a blank for the proprietor's name."

Hazel took the paper and read it slowly. Official stock, official seal, the new Governor-Regent's stamp still sharp-edged and hopeful. *Permission is hereby granted to the bearer, ________, to rebuild upon the premises of Lantern Row such works as were destroyed...*

"The blank," she said.

"It seemed wrong of me to fill it in," Elias said, ears going pink. "It seemed—it seemed a thing a person ought to write for herself."

She looked at him then, this man who had spent a career counting other people into columns and was learning, laboriously, to leave spaces blank instead, and she felt the fondness rise in her like water finding a channel. She folded the permit with care and put it inside her coat, over her heart, next to nothing, because there was room there now.

"There's tea in the flask," Elias said, "and bread, and if you want company for ten minutes I can be extremely quiet, and if you want to be alone I can be extremely gone."

"Company," she said. "Quietly."

They sat together on the cracked composing stone and drank lukewarm tea and ate bread dusted, inevitably, with ash, and Elias pointed out, in a voice pitched to disturb nothing, that the eastern wall was sound, and the cellar stairs looked passable, and wasn't that the cellar mouth there behind the fallen lintel, and had she considered that a press could be had from the estate of old Master Corvey, who died in the spring, if one knew whom to ask? She listened, and answered in monosyllables, and understood that he had already asked whom to ask, and that somewhere in his coat was a note with a price written on it, and that he would sit on that note until she was ready. This was how Elias loved: in advance preparations and unfilled blanks.

When the flask was empty he rose, brushed uselessly at his knees, and hesitated on the threshold.

"Marta sent a message," he said. "I was to tell you only if you seemed—" he gestured at her, sitting serene in the wreckage, "—well. Like yourself."

"Say it."

"The Morning Tide sent a pigeon. Odile. The decrees were read into the record Tuesday. There's to be a commission." He turned his hat in his hands. "Hale has been summoned to the capital. To answer questions." A pause. "Tam says"—the ghost of a smile—"that pigeons are unreliable narrators, but he checked with the harbor boys, and the harbor boys confirm."

Hazel nodded slowly, and looked at the wreck of the press, and the ash, and the small tin cup of rescued letters catching the last of the light.

"And Marta's message?" she asked. "The part after 'if she seems well'?"

Elias cleared his throat. "Marta says: tell the girl the cellar press ran three hundred broadsheets this week and every one of them wants to know who cut the compass. Marta says: tell the girl the city is asking by name. Marta says—" he faltered, and delivered it flat and exact, the way one delivers a load-bearing sentence, "—tell the girl a shop needs a name on the door, and the sooner the better, because paper is patient but the city is not."

The evening fog was beginning to come off the harbor, grey and familiar, threading itself through the broken windows like a tenant returning to check on an empty house. Hazel looked down at her palms, black to the wrist, scored with a dozen small cuts from the sifting, and turned them over as though reading them.

Three hundred broadsheets wanting to know her name. A blank on a permit. A punch that leaned into the wind.

"Tell Marta," she said, standing, taking up the sack of fragments in one hand and her father's box in the other, "that she should buy lampblack. Good lampblack, none of your tar-and-turpentine filth. And tell her I'll want the cellar press two days a week while the rebuilding's done, and that if Odile thinks she's getting the compass punch as collateral she can think again, but I'll cut her anything she likes for cost."

Elias wrote nothing down, which was how she knew he would remember every word.

At the doorway she stopped, as she had stopped coming in, and looked back once at the ruin—the warped press praying its broken prayer, the ash-printed handbill fading already from the composing stone, the gold light going out of it bar by bar. She had come expecting to close something. She was leaving having opened something, and the strangeness of it settled over her not like a shroud but like a shawl: warm, worn, hers.

"Crane & Crane," she said, mostly to herself, testing the words the way she would test a newly cut letter, turning them to the light, feeling for burrs.

Elias heard. He said nothing at all, which from Elias was a benediction.

Behind them, as they passed out onto Lantern Row, the fog moved gently through the shattered windows of the print shop, filling the ruin with soft white, and if a shop remembers who enters clean, then it surely also remembers who kneels in its ashes and rises with an armful of letters—and the shop remembered Hazel Crane, and began, in its slow iron way, to wait for her return.
