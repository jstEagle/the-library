## 37. A Name on the Door

The sign took three men, a length of rope, and most of the morning to hang, which seemed fitting to Hazel. Nothing worth naming had ever come easily to the Cranes.

She stood in the street below with her arms folded against the harbor wind and watched Odile haul on the line while Elias steadied the ladder and Tam—no longer a boy runner but an apprentice now, in a leather apron two sizes too large—shouted directions that were mostly enthusiasm and mostly wrong.

"Left! No—*your* left, Captain!"

"My left is the ship's left when I say it is," Odile called back, and gave the rope one last heave. The sign swung up against the fresh-painted fascia and settled: **CRANE & CRANE**, letters cut deep into quarter-sawn oak, gilded at the edges so they caught the morning light off the water and threw it back in small coins across the cobbles.

Two names. One door.

Hazel had carved the pattern herself, of course. She had cut each letter twice as large as any punch she'd ever made, working at the bench her father had built, using gravers her father had ground, and she had done it slowly, over six nights, because some things deserved slowness. The second *Crane* was not a copy of the first. Her father's name would have been set square and sober, without ornament. Hers leaned very slightly forward, the serifs sharpened like the prows of ships, because she had wanted anyone reading it to feel motion in it—a name going somewhere.

"There," Tam said, breathless, when the hooks held. "That's proper, that is. Looks like it's always been there."

"Everything does, once it's hung," Marta said from the shop doorway. The old compositor had come early, as she came early to everything now, her gray braid pinned up under a cap she'd taken to wearing like a banner. In the year since the Uprising, half the printers in Verrance had learned to read her silences the way sailors read weather. Today her silence was clear skies. She was smiling, which for Marta was practically fireworks.

Hazel looked up at the sign a moment longer. *Crane & Crane.* Passersby were already slowing to squint at it—the fishwives with their baskets, a customs clerk with his collar up, two dockhands who'd stopped mid-argument. Let them look. A year ago the sight of eyes on the shop had felt like frost coming through a cracked pane. Now it only felt like weather.

"Inside," she said. "We've ink to mix and a commission to pull before the client arrives, and I'll not have the first job out of this shop crooked."

"It won't be crooked," Tam said, holding the door for her with exaggerated ceremony. "You'd sooner die."

"Don't tempt the morning," Hazel said, and went in.

* * *

The new shop was smaller than the old one, and better.

The fire had taken everything on Harrow Street—the type racks, the press, forty years of her father's matrices gone to slag and smoke—and for a long winter after, Hazel had believed that was the whole story: a life's work reduced to a smell that clung to her hair no matter how many times she washed it. But ash, it turned out, was only another kind of paper, and paper could be printed over. The premises on Chandler's Row had stood empty since the chandler retired; Odile had known the landlord through shipping channels no honest person asked about; the rent was settled in trade and gratitude. They had built the benches themselves, Elias planing the tops level while Hazel checked them with a spirit level borrowed from a mason who owed Marta a favor. The press had come north by sea from a dissolved monastery printery, iron-boned and patient, its screw re-greased by Ferro himself, who had appeared one morning at the cellar door with his tools and his shame and asked, in a voice like a man at confession, whether he might work it off.

They had let him. That had been Hazel's argument, in the end—not forgiveness exactly, but arithmetic. A press needed hands. A broken man needed a bench. Some repairs ran parallel and never met, and that was allowed.

Now the morning light came through windows Hazel had washed herself until her shoulders burned, and fell across the sorted cases in their ranks, the galley trays, the stone where the forme lay waiting. On the walls hung what they had salvaged: the framed broadsheet from the first free press run, its compass-in-lantern mark stamped a thousand times over in a thousand shops across the city; and beside it, small and dark with age, her father's first punch, mounted on a card in Elias's careful census-hand: *Ambrose Crane, cutter, arrived Verrance 1798. Cut true.*

Below the frame, the commission waited on the stone.

It was a modest job, deliberately chosen. The Harbor Trust wanted five hundred copies of a notice—new tide tables, revised channel markers, the ordinary grammar of a working port. No decree, no manifesto, no forgery to undo. Just words, set clean, to be posted on walls where fishermen and pilots could read them. Hazel had picked it from three offers precisely because it was small. First jobs should be humble, her father used to say. Like first steps. Like first confessions.

But the forme held one thing no tide table required, and that was why her hands would not entirely steady as she checked the quoins.

In the lower corner, where printers traditionally placed their colophon if they dared place one at all, sat a single line of type:

*Printed by Hazel Crane, at the sign of Crane & Crane, Chandler's Row.*

Her name. Set by her own hand, in type she had cast from her own matrix, standing in metal like every other word on the page—as though it were ordinary. As though it had always been ordinary.

"You're staring at it again," Marta said, without looking up from the case where she was distributing sorts with fingers that moved like water finding its level.

"I'm proofing it."

"You've proofed it nine times. The tenth will not improve it."

"The tenth might."

Marta snorted. But when Hazel turned back to the stone, the old woman crossed the room and stood beside her, and looked down at the line of type with an expression Hazel had never quite seen on her before—something between pride and grief wearing the same face, the way they often did.

"Ambrose set his name like that," Marta said quietly. "On his first job out of apprenticeship. I pulled the lever for him; did I ever tell you? I was a girl of fourteen, strong for my size, and he was so nervous his hands shook, and he said, *Marta, you pull it, I can't trust myself.* And it came out perfect." She touched the frame above the stone, just briefly, the way one touches a shoulder. "He'd have set yours shaking too. It's the shaking that proves it matters."

Hazel swallowed. "I'm not shaking."

"Your quoins are tight enough to crack the furniture, child. Loosen them before you split your first form and shame us all."

Hazel laughed—short, surprised, real—and loosened the quoins.

* * *

They came at noon, as arranged, though none of them were clients.

Elias arrived first, of course; Elias was a man who arrived first to everything now, as though punctuality were a form of penance he intended to keep paying forever. He wore his good coat, the gray one without government buttons—he had resigned the census office in the spring, walking out of the Records Hall with his ledger under his arm and his resignation letter written in a fair hand that named no reason, because no reason was required. He carried flowers, which embarrassed them both.

"For the occasion," he said, offering a bundle of sea thrift and rosemary wrapped in brown paper. "The market woman said rosemary means remembrance and thrift means—well, thrift. I thought that was suitably printerly. Frugal, hardy, grows in poor soil."

"You brought me weeds."

"I brought you *symbolism.*"

Hazel took them, and hid her smile in the rosemary, and put them in the jar that lived on the cutting bench—the one spot of softness in a room of edges.

Tam came skidding in from the street smelling of fish and salt, still in his apron, a smudge of ink already on his chin that Hazel decided not to mention because mentioning it would mean admitting she noticed everything about him now, the way one notices weather. Behind him came Odile, filling the doorway, in a coat that had been fashionable in three ports and was now merely legendary. She surveyed the shop with a captain's eye—the press, the cases, the framed mark on the wall—and pronounced judgment.

"It'll do."

High praise. From Odile, *it'll do* was practically a christening.

Last came Marta, having doffed her apron and donned, over her plain dress, a shawl Hazel had never seen: dark wool, embroidered at the borders with tiny lanterns, worn soft as paper with years. Her good shawl. Her *church* shawl. She said nothing about it and nobody asked, but everybody understood.

"So," Odile said, rubbing her hands together. "Where's this famous signing? I've a tide to catch at four and a cargo manifest that lies creatively about its tonnage, and I refuse to miss the theater."

"No theater," Hazel said. "Just work."

"Just work," Odile echoed, grinning. "Says the woman who spent a decade pretending to be her own ghost. Pull the proofs, Crane. Let's see what honesty looks like in ink."

* * *

Hazel wiped the form, and it was like breathing.

There was no ceremony in the work itself—that was the secret she had never been able to explain to anyone outside the craft. The ball came down; the ink spread its blue-black skin across the face of the type; the dampened sheet lowered like a wing. The bar swung. The screw turned. The press spoke its long iron syllable, and then the sheet peeled back and there it was, black on white, permanent: tide tables, channel markers, and beneath them, small and steady in the lower corner—

*Printed by Hazel Crane, at the sign of Crane & Crane, Chandler's Row.*

She laid the first proof on the stone and stepped back.

Nobody spoke. Even Tam held still. The press ticked as it cooled, and outside a gull argued with the morning, and the light through the clean windows lay across the wet ink so the letters shone faintly, freshly, like things just surfaced from deep water.

Then Marta stepped forward, drew the compositor's rule from her apron, and laid it beneath the signature line—an old gesture, the formal acknowledgment of a well-set page. "True," she said. It was the highest word in the trade. *Cut true. Set true.* Her voice caught slightly on it, and she cleared her throat and pretended it hadn't.

Elias came next. He did not touch the proof; he had learned, over long months at the shop, the liturgy of wet ink and clean hands. Instead he read the line aloud, quietly, as though filing it into some record more permanent than any ledger he had kept. "*Printed by Hazel Crane.*" He looked up at her, and his eyes—those careful census-taker's eyes that had once counted households and hidden what they found—were bright. "Witnessed and entered. For the record, this time the record is true."

"Witnessed," Odile said, stepping up and pressing her thumb briefly to the margin, leaving the faint ghost of an ink-stained print beside the name. "And witnessed. There. Now it's binding. Try to escape your own fame now, little cutter."

Tam bounced on his toes until Hazel sighed and beckoned him forward. He studied the proof with immense gravity, lips moving over the words, and then pointed at her name. "That's you."

"That's me."

"Not your brother."

"Not my brother."

"And not a lie anymore."

Hazel knelt so they were level, this boy who had run a city's truth through gunfire in his arms, who had slept in her loft for a season after the fires until the flinching left him, who called her *Hazel* now instead of *ma'am* except when he forgot. "Not a lie anymore," she said. "That's the whole point of the door sign, Tam. So I stop having to remember which name goes where."

"Good." He nodded once, businesslike, and then ruined it by hugging her hard around the neck, ink chin and all. She let him. She held on a moment longer than strictly necessary, and found she did not care who saw.

* * *

They pulled fifty proofs that afternoon, because the Harbor Trust wanted five hundred and the press was slow and their hands were glad of the work. Hazel pulled the lever for most of them, but she made Tam take turns, and Marta, and even Odile, who worked the bar with the easy strength of a woman who hauled anchor lines and declared the machine "honest, for a landlubber contraption." Elias she taught to feed sheets, and he was clumsy at it, and improved, and by the fortieth impression his timing was nearly right, and something in his face when the sheet seated true made her think he had found a kind of quiet he had been hunting for twenty years.

Between pulls, while Marta washed the balls and Tam stacked drying sheets like a boy building a house of paper, Odile leaned against the press frame and told stories from the coast road—the new port at Sallow's End where the decrees had been publicly burned, the magistrate upriver who now required original documents countersigned twice, the way the word *compass* had become slang among harbor children for *the truth*. "They chalk it on walls," she said. "Little lanterns. Half of them drawn wrong—one point instead of eight—but the constables scrub and scrub and more come back." She smiled her wolfish smile. "You're a piece of the language now, Hazel Crane. You can't unsay yourself."

Hazel wiped ink from her fingers and looked at her hands.

They were her father's hands, or near enough—the same broad nails, the same callus ridge along the right forefinger where the graver rested. For ten years she had kept these hands out of ledgers and off signatures, had folded them behind the fiction of a dead brother's mastery, had trained herself to be a rumor in her own shop. She had thought invisibility was safety. Then she had learned it was only a slower kind of erasure, and that erasure, in the end, was what the Governor's forged decrees had done to whole villages—struck names from land, struck names from records, unmade people with a clerk's smooth stroke. Her hiding had never been neutral. It had simply been smaller.

No longer. The name stood on the door and in the type and soon on five hundred public walls, and the city knew the compass-lantern, and the compass-lantern knew itself.

When the last proof of the day came off the stone, Elias hung it by the window to dry, beside the framed broadsheet and her father's punch, so that three generations of Cranes' work faced the light together—except that the newest of them bore a name the older two had never dared. He straightened the pin twice, fussed, stepped back.

"Hazel," he said, and there was something in his voice that made her turn fully around. He stood by the drying rack with his hands clasped behind him, a man assembling courage the way he assembled testimony: carefully, in order, refusing to skip. "There's one more document wants your signature today. If you're willing."

"We've signed everything the Trust sent."

"This one isn't the Trust's." He produced a folded paper from inside his coat—good paper, rag paper, the expensive kind—and did not unfold it yet. "It occurred to me that a shop ought to have a ledger of its own. Accounts, commissions, debts and credits. Every business on Chandler's Row keeps one. And it occurred to me further that the first entry shouldn't be in my hand, or Marta's, or anyone's but yours." He unfolded it at last and laid it on the cutting bench, weighting the corners with type bodies. It was a fair copy, beautifully ruled, of a title page:

**Ledger of Crane & Crane, Printers — begun this day, in the first year of the free press.**

*Proprietor:* ________________________

The blank line waited, wide open, like a door.

Hazel looked at it for a long moment. Around the shop, everyone had gone quiet again—Marta with the ink balls stilled in her hands, Tam frozen mid-stack, Odile watching from the press with an expression she usually saved for horizons.

Her father had kept ledgers full of lies, entries in her dead brother's name because a daughter's name would have invited ruin—or so he'd believed, meaning kindly, meaning wrong. Her whole childhood lived in those false columns. She had inherited the lying and the columns both.

She picked up the pen. Her hand did not shake, quite; it trembled the way a plumb line trembles, seeking true.

*Hazel Crane,* she wrote. Not *Crane.* Not *niece of the widow Crane, late of Harrow Street.* Not *executrix of the estate of* anything. Just the name, whole, hers, in a hand that had finally decided what it was.

"There," she said, and her voice was steadier than she expected. "Entered into the record."

Elias blew gently on the ink, the way one does with important papers, and closed the ledger with the reverence of a man laying a wreath. "For the record," he said softly. "Which, I'm told—" and here his mouth curved, "—is now on your side."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the harbor, the thick white Verrance fog that ate rooftops and made lamps necessary at four in the afternoon. Tam went to light the shop lantern—the big one, the one they hung by the door, the one whose brass sides Odile had polished within an inch of its life—and when it kindled, its glow spilled through the windows onto the street and caught the gilt edge of the new sign, so that *CRANE & CRANE* burned faintly gold above the emptying row.

And it was then, as Hazel stood in the doorway with the smell of ink and rosemary around her and her family at her back, that she saw the figure across the street: a woman, travel-worn, standing in the fog just beyond the lantern's reach with a satchel at her hip and a look on her face as though she had been walking toward this door for a very long time.

The woman saw Hazel seeing her. She did not run. She did not speak.

She only raised one hand—weathered, inkstained to the wrist—and touched two fingers to her heart, the old greeting of the trade, cutter to cutter.

Hazel's breath went out of her. Because she knew that greeting. She had seen it once, years ago, in a woodcut her father had kept hidden in a locked drawer—a portrait of a woman printer, executed in the capital thirty years past for cutting seditious plates, a martyr whose name had been struck from every record in the colony.

A name that had been struck. A name that someone, apparently, remembered.

The woman stepped forward into the lantern light, and Hazel heard herself say, before she could think better of it, before she understood at all:

"You'd better come in. We were just opening."
