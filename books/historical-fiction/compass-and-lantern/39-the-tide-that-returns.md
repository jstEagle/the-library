## 39. The Tide That Returns

The press breathed before it spoke.

Tam had learned to listen for it in the year since the shop rose from its own ashes — that long inhale of the platen rising, the held breath at the top of the arc, and then the *thump*, deep as a heartbeat heard through a wall, when iron met iron and ink became language. He stood with his hand flat on the frame now, feeling it shiver up through his palm and into his wrist, into his elbow, all the way to the place behind his sternum where he kept the things he never said out loud.

"Mind your fingers," Marta said, without looking up from the stone.

"I know."

"You say you know. Your fingers do not always agree with you."

His fingers were stained black to the second knuckle. So were his cuffs, his collar, the inside of his left ear where he scratched when he was thinking, and — according to Odile, who had inspected him at breakfast with the gravity of a harbormaster inspecting a hull — his soul. Tam considered this a fair trade. A year ago his hands had been empty except for whatever they could carry away fast. Now they carried letters. Thousands of them. Whole rivers of them, cast in Hazel's type, set by Marta's quick old fingers, locked into forms so tight that no governor's men and no fire and no fear could shake them loose.

The rebuilt Crane & Crane did not look like the shop that had burned. The old one had been low and crooked, smelling of tallow and pipe smoke, its windows fogged with a century of breath. This one stood taller, its beams still pale and sweet-smelling with new pine, its windows wide enough to catch the harbor light and throw it across the floor in long gold bars. The scorch mark survived only on the doorframe, where Hazel had refused to let the carpenters plane it away. *So we remember what the wood cost,* she'd said. Tam ran his thumb over it every morning on his way in, the way other people touched a saint's medal.

Outside, Verrance was waking into the first morning of something none of them had a proper word for yet. The Governor had been gone eight months. The new administration's seal sat on documents instead of the old one, and the customs men at the harbor gate checked manifests because manifests needed checking, not because someone had paid them to find treason in the cargo. The Bridge of Chains came down at dawn now instead of staying raised against the city like a drawn knife. And on the corner of Rope Walk and Fishmonger's Row, where the fishwives had once whispered their grievances between gutting knives, there was a fresh-painted board: **THE TIDE — PRINTED DAILY BY THE HANDS OF VERRANCE**.

Today was the first day it would be true.

* * *

The broadsheet came off the press at half past six, and Tam caught the first copy as it slid from the tapes, still warm, the ink smelling of linseed and lampblack and — though he knew this was impossible — of the cellar under the chandler's shop, of the nights when printing had been a crime and everyone who did it had been a family anyway.

He read it standing there, though he'd read every word three times in proof. **THE TIDE**, said the masthead, and beneath it, small and sure as a signature, the mark: a compass rose cut into the heart of a lantern. Hazel's mark. Cut twice now — once in secret, once in daylight — and stamped this morning onto two thousand sheets that would go out into the city like seeds on the wind.

Below the masthead, the lead story, set in ten-point Caslon that Hazel had cast herself from her father's first matrix:

*On this day one year past, the print shop of Crane & Sons was put to the torch by order of Inspector Hale, in retaliation for the publication of certain land decrees forged under the Governor's hand. The decrees were true. The fire was real. The shop is rebuilt. The record stands corrected, and will stand corrected daily, in print, for as long as this city has hands to set it.*

Tam read it four times. The fourth time was for the rhythm, because Marta had taught him that a line of type was like a line of music — you didn't just read it, you felt where it wanted to breathe. Then he stacked copies into his canvas satchel until it hung heavy as a sack of wet sand off both shoulders, and Odile, who had come in with the dawn fog still in her hair, looked him over and nodded once.

"That's the whole city on your back, boy."

"No," Tam said. "It's lighter than that."

* * *

He went out through the fish market first, because that was tradition now, or superstition, or whatever you called a thing you did because it mattered and couldn't bear to call it nothing.

The stalls were loud with the morning's catch — silver mackerel laid out in gleaming ranks, crabs muttering in their barrels, the whole place sharp with brine and ice and the smoke from the kipper house. The fishwives knew him. They'd known him since he was seven years old and running messages through rope walks for pennies, back when a boy could go anywhere in Verrance if he was small enough, quick enough, and poor enough to be beneath notice. Being beneath notice had been his whole trade once. Now the fishwives called him by name and saved him the heels of the bread.

"First one's yours," Tam announced, slapping a broadsheet down on Widow Bren's cutting board, next to the mackerel.

She wiped her hands on her apron — a formality, her hands were never clean before noon — and read it right there, lips moving, while the market roared around her. When she got to the part about the fire, she stopped moving her lips. When she got to the part about the record standing corrected, she pressed the paper flat with both palms, careful as a woman smoothing a shroud.

"A year," she said. "Feels like yesterday I smelled the smoke."

"It feels like yesterday to everybody."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow there'll be another one." He tapped the masthead. "That's the whole idea. It comes back. Like the tide."

Widow Bren laughed — a short, barking laugh that scattered gulls off the pilings — and folded the sheet into her apron pocket, over her heart, which was where she kept everything that mattered. By noon, Tam knew, she'd have read it aloud to half the market, doing all the voices, the way she did. News in Verrance had always traveled by mouth. Now it traveled by mouth *and* by paper, and the paper was the part that couldn't be argued with later, couldn't be denied, couldn't quietly vanish the way things vanished when the only record of them lived in a governor's filing cabinet.

He worked the market stall by stall. The chandler took three copies, one for himself and two to send upriver to cousins. The net-mender read hers standing in her doorway with a half-mended trawl still hanging from one fist. Two boys about Tam's old size — runners, both of them, with the quick hungry eyes he recognized like a mirror — trailed him for three streets asking questions until he gave them each a copy and taught them the fold, how to snap a broadsheet open with one wrist so it cracked like a sail catching wind.

"You get paid for this?" the smaller one asked.

"Room and board. Lessons. People who'd burn a building down before they'd let anything happen to me." He shrugged, adjusting the satchel strap. "Best wages in the city."

The smaller one looked at him the way Tam had once looked at Odile stepping off her boat — like a door opening onto a room he hadn't known existed. Tam handed him a third copy. "Come by the shop Saturday. Ask for Marta. Tell her the tide sent you."

* * *

By midmorning he'd climbed the hill to the customs quarter, where the paper was read differently — not aloud, not with voices, but in silence, behind office doors, with expressions Tam collected like coins.

A clerk in the harbor registry took his copy with gloved hands and an expression of profound suspicion, as though the paper might be armed. A young magistrate's assistant read hers on the steps of the courthouse and then read it again, slower, and Tam watched her face do the thing faces did now when people encountered the plain truth in plain type after years of official fog: a kind of blinking, a recalibration, a person remembering that words were supposed to mean things.

At the top of the hill, outside the old census office — Elias's old office, though Elias hadn't sat behind that desk in months — Tam paused. The windows were dark. The new administration hadn't decided yet whether a city that had learned to count itself still needed counting, and Elias said, when asked, that he hoped not, that a census should be a lantern and not a net, and that until he was sure which one it would be, he preferred to work with ink rather than lists.

Through the glass, Tam could see the stove where Elias had burned his copy of the forged decree while Hale's questioners circled him like gulls over a dying fish. Tam had heard that story four times and would happily hear it forty more. Someday, he thought, somebody ought to print it. Someday, maybe, everything would be printed — every fire, every lie, every quiet brave stupid magnificent thing anybody in this city had ever done in the dark.

He left a copy wedged in the census office door, face out, masthead to the street.

* * *

He was halfway down Rope Walk when he saw the dragoon.

For one full second — one long, cold, tidal second — the year fell away. Tam's body remembered what it had been trained into by narrow escapes and Ferro's betrayal and the turning tide at the harbor gate: drop the satchel, run the alley, don't look back. His feet were already deciding.

Then the dragoon turned, and it was just a dragoon — a bored young man in a uniform that fit badly, buying chestnuts from the cart on the corner, paying with honest coin, complaining about the weather. The new garrison. Hale's men were gone; Hale himself had sailed for the capital in irons eleven months ago, and the last anyone in Verrance had heard, he was answering questions in a room considerably less comfortable than the ones he'd used to ask his own.

Tam stood in the middle of Rope Walk with his heart going like a press at full speed, and made himself breathe, and made himself notice that nobody else on the street had even glanced at the soldier. That was the change, he realized. Not that the uniforms were different. That nobody flinched anymore. Fear had been a tax levied on the whole city, and the new administration had abolished it, and the citizens of Verrance had discovered they'd gotten used to keeping their own money.

He bought chestnuts from the same cart, out of spite, and ate them walking, and delivered the rest of his route with the satchel lightening street by street until it hung empty against his hip, and even empty it felt heavier than it used to, because of what it had carried.

* * *

The shop, when he got back near noon, smelled of ink and hot metal and the onion rolls Marta brought on press days, and the sound coming through the open door was the sound Tam privately thought of as the sound of the world working properly: the press breathing its long inhale-thump, exhale-thump, Hazel calling measurements across the floor, Marta answering in the shorthand of compositors, and underneath it all the creak of the sign outside — **CRANE & CRANE** — swinging in the harbor wind like a slow pendulum marking a year.

Inside, the afternoon light lay across the stone in gold bars, and Hazel stood at the case with a composing stick in her hand, setting the morrow's edition, her sleeves rolled, her forearms black to the elbow. She looked up when he came in, and her whole face did the thing it did — the thing that had taken him months to learn to read, back when she was all edges and silence, back when being seen was a danger she'd trained herself out of wanting.

"Sold out?" she asked.

"Every copy. Widow Bren's reading it to the fish market right now. Doing the voices."

"She does my voice too high."

"Everybody's voice too high. It's part of the art."

Marta laughed from the stone, and Elias looked up from the proof table — proofs spread around him like fallen leaves, pencil behind his ear, spectacles pushed up into his hair — and gave Tam the nod that meant *well done*, which from Elias was practically a parade.

Odile arrived at the stroke of one, because Odile arrived everywhere at the stroke of something, carrying a crate of good paper from up the coast and news from every port between here and the capital. She dropped the crate, kissed Marta on both cheeks, stole an onion roll, and fixed Tam with her harbormaster's stare.

"Well, apprentice. How does it feel? First edition of the free press, distributed by your own two hands."

Tam thought about it honestly, the way she expected him to. He thought about the fishwives and the clerk in gloves and the two runner boys and the dragoon buying chestnuts and the scorch mark on the doorframe and the weight of the satchel and the sound of the press breathing.

"Like the tide," he said finally. "You know how it goes out and goes out and you think it's never coming back, and then it turns, and it comes in and in and in, and it was never gone at all, it was just gathering?"

Odile grinned. "I'm putting that in a song."

"You are not."

"I absolutely am. Royalties payable in onion rolls."

They were all laughing — even Hazel, whose laughter was still, after a year, a thing that arrived like sun after fog, worth standing in — when the knock came at the door.

Not a hard knock. Not the knock they all still, somewhere under their ribs, knew by heart. A soft one. Hesitant. The knock of someone who wasn't sure they were allowed.

Hazel opened it, and Tam saw a girl on the step — fourteen, maybe fifteen, work-worn hands, ink already ghosted into the creases of her fingers the way it ghosts into the fingers of anyone who's ever loved a press — holding a battered wooden box against her chest like a shield. Behind her the fog was rolling back in off the harbor, softening the rooftops, and the shop lantern above the door threw its circle of light down onto her face.

"Please," the girl said, in an accent from the upriver districts, the districts the old decrees had stripped bare. "I'm looking for the cutter. They said — they said the compass-lantern mark. They said if anyone could cut what I need cut—" Her voice cracked, and she lifted the box lid with shaking thumbs, and Tam leaned to see, and saw inside a handful of worn matrices, old ones, rescued ones, and a scrap of paper bearing a name crossed out and rewritten in a child's careful hand.

"They said you'd understand," the girl finished, barely above the fog. "About starting again from what's left."

The shop went very quiet. Tam looked at Hazel, and Hazel looked at the box, and the lantern swayed above the door, and the tide came in below the quay, patient as print, certain as morning.

"Yes," Hazel said. "Come in. Tell me your name."
