## 1. The Smell of Ink

The first thing Ulyana Eriksen did every morning, before the stove was lit, before the kettle, before she so much as untied her apron strings, was to stand at the door of the shop and breathe in.

Ink. Not the clean, sharp ink of a fountain pen or the cheap violet stuff Milo used for his letters home, but the deep, oily, animal smell of printing ink — linseed and lampblack and something older underneath, something that had soaked into the floorboards of Eriksen & Son over three generations and would soak into her bones if she lived to be ninety. Her father used to say you couldn't learn the trade until the smell stopped bothering you. Then he'd said you'd learned it when the smell started smelling like bread.

She breathed in. It smelled like bread.

"Ulyana." Milo's voice came from the back, muffled by machinery. "The kettle's doing that thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where it makes the noise like it's dying but doesn't die."

"That's called boiling, Milo."

"It sounds like grief," he said solemnly.

"You're sixteen. You don't know what grief sounds like."

"My grandmother says I have an old soul."

"Your grandmother also says you were switched at birth with a bishop's son, which means either she's wrong about one thing or the bishop's son is currently setting shipping manifests in my shop, and I don't think the church would approve of his kerning."

There was a pause, then Milo's head appeared around the press, hair sticking up in four directions as though he'd been electrocuted in his sleep and simply never recovered. "You never compliment my kerning."

"I compliment your kerning when your kerning deserves compliments."

"When has my kerning ever deserved—"

"The seventh of March."

"That was *once*."

"It was a beautiful *f*," Ulyana said, hanging her coat on its peg. "I think about it sometimes."

Milo grinned at her — he grinned at everything, the way only boys who had not yet been properly acquainted with the world could grin — and vanished back behind the press, and Ulyana went to fill the teapot, and this was how mornings began at Eriksen & Son: with the smell of bread and the kettle dying without dying and a boy who thought his soul was old.

Her father had bought that joke about the bishop's son. She had made it up herself, two years ago, to fill the silence Milo left when he asked, once, whether her father had ever set type as badly as Milo did. She made up jokes now the way other people made up beds — daily, briskly, smoothing the wrinkles down so nobody could see what lay underneath.

* * *

The morning's work sat waiting on the stone: shipping manifests for the Ostlund Shipping Company, four hundred copies, due Thursday, which meant they were already late because everything in Vessengard was late since the war ended, coal and bread and letters and men. Ulyana took her composing stick down from its hook and began.

There was a particular pleasure in setting type that she had never managed to explain to anyone who didn't do it. The world arrived at the shop in chaos — orders shouted across counters, prices changed by telegram, the government itself sending print orders amended twice in a week as though language were a thing you could keep re-stirring like soup — and Ulyana's job was to make it hold still. Letter by letter. Word by word. Each piece of type a small leaden certainty, each line built like a wall, course upon course, until here, look: sense. Order. A sentence you could lean against.

Her father had taught her at eight years old, standing her on a stool at the case. *"Left hand finds the letter, right hand sets it. Don't look at your hand. Your hand knows where things live. That's the whole secret of the trade, Ulla — hands that know where things live."*

Her hands knew. They moved through the case without her, f and g and the stubborn ampersand, and her mind wandered the way it always did while her fingers worked, out through the frost-furred window, down the hill, past the fish market and the customs house, to the harbor, where the Ostlund freighters sat loading herring barrels for Copenhagen.

History happened out there. History happened in capitals, in ministries, in the grand marble rooms where men whose names were printed in newspapers decided which provinces changed hands and which languages children would be schooled in and whether there would be peace or merely a pause between wars. History happened to generals and chancellors and kings in exile. It happened to countries, whole and entire.

It did not happen to girls who set type. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement. Vessengard shipped herring and iron and occasionally soldiers, and received in exchange news of history the way it received tea and sugar — packaged elsewhere, priced elsewhere, its production invisible. Ulyana had made her peace with this long ago, or rather she had never found the moment when making peace became necessary, because the war had come to Vessengard mostly as paper. Ration cards. Casualty lists. Print orders for recruitment posters, and then, later, print orders for memorial notices, and then, one gray Tuesday in her twenty-third year, an envelope with the government crest containing a form that said, in the beautiful, indifferent, perfectly-kerned type of the state: *Reported dead. Presumed. Remains not recoverable.*

Remains not recoverable. She had set worse words than that herself, in her time. Obituaries for merchants, memorial verses for drowned fishermen. She knew exactly how much space those words took up on a page. She could have told them how to save money on the form by tightening the leading.

"Ulyana? You've gone somewhere."

Milo was watching her over the frame of the press with that unguarded concern boys wore like a too-large coat.

"I'm deciding whether to give you the manifest order or make you sweep."

"Sweep," Milo said immediately.

"You want to sweep?"

"I want you to stop looking like that. Sweeping helps me think and I can't think near the press today anyway, the belt's squeaking and it puts a sound in my teeth."

"The belt squeaks because you won't wax it."

"The wax is *right there*, I can see it, but Master Eriksen always said—" He stopped. His ears went pink.

"He always said what?" Ulyana asked, evenly, sorting a quoins.

"That you should wax the belt on Thursdays because waxing it on Mondays makes the ink dust fly, and ink dust in the bearings wears the bearings." Milo recited it fast, the way one returns a borrowed thing one is ashamed to be holding. "He told me the second week I was here. Before."

Before. The word fell between them and lay on the floor like a dropped sort, and neither of them picked it up.

"He was right," Ulyana said. "Wax it Thursdays."

"Yes, miss."

"And stop calling me miss. I've known you since you were eleven and stole apples off the sill."

"You said miss was professional."

"I say many things. Sweep."

He swept. Ulyana set type. The stove ticked. Outside, the city went about its business of being cold, and somewhere above the harbor the gulls turned their white wheels in the gray air, indifferent as typeset words to the meaning beneath them.

* * *

At noon her mother came down — came halfway down, rather, stopping on the third stair from the bottom, which was as far as she ever came these days, as though the shop were a country she held a passport for but no longer wished to visit.

"There's herring salad," her mother said. "And bread that is mostly bread."

"Mostly bread is a triumph. Bring it down?"

"In a minute. I wanted—" Her mother's hands were doing the thing they did, folding and unfolding the apron hem, hem folded and unfolded, a little loom of worry working all day long. "The Lindqvists are asking after you. For the choir social."

"Tell them I'm busy."

"You're always busy."

"I'm a printer. Printing is busy work. It's the whole nature of the trade."

"Ulyana." Her mother descended one more stair, then another, an act of considerable courage, and stood in the doorway with the light from the street behind her, so that her face was in shadow and her thinness was not. Two years since the envelope, and her mother had gone soft-edged and quiet as a worn coin, her features rubbed smooth by grief until sometimes, looking at her, Ulyana felt she was looking at a photograph of her mother rather than the woman herself. "You could go out among people. It might—"

"Mama. There are four hundred manifests due Thursday."

Her mother looked at the forms of type locked in their frames, at the press, at Milo sweeping with tremendous concentration in the corner. "He loved this room," she said quietly. "More than he loved us, I sometimes thought. More than me, certainly."

"He loved you like a press loves ink, Mama. You couldn't see it working but nothing printed without it."

Her mother's mouth did something that was almost a smile, and then wasn't, and then she went upstairs, and the smell of herring salad drifted down after her, faint and brave.

* * *

The afternoon brought the mail: an invoice from the paper merchant, a bill for the gas that made Ulyana wince, and the corrected proofs from the harbor office, which she read twice and set aside, and then — last, thin, official — a printed circular from the Office of Public Information, stamped for distribution to all licensed trades.

Milo read it upside down as she skimmed it. "What's it say?"

"Censorship renewal. Same as October's circular. All material intended for public circulation subject to review; all printers responsible for content; penalties as previously enumerated."

"Have we ever been penalized?"

"We've never printed anything worth penalizing."

"Is that a boast or a confession?"

"It's the trade," Ulyana said, filing the circular in the drawer where such documents went to die. "We print manifests and menus and memorial cards, Milo. We print what other people need printed. Nobody needs a printer with opinions."

"Master Eriksen had opinions."

"Master Eriksen kept his opinions between his own ears, where they belonged."

But that wasn't true, and the lie sat oddly in her mouth, like type set backwards. Her father had opinions the way the sea had salt; they were everywhere in him, in the margins of his ledgers, in the way he'd set certain headlines with just slightly too much space around them, a small typographic shrug of disbelief. He used to say that every job that passed through a print shop carried the fingerprints of everyone who'd touched it, whether or not they signed their names. *Nothing comes off a press clean, Ulla. Ink gets everywhere. So does conviction.*

She had thought, these two years, that she understood what he meant. Standing now at the case, her hands finding letters in the dark of the box like animals finding their burrows, she realized instead that she had understood it the way you understand a foreign phrase you can pronounce but not translate.

Because her father's hands were still, and hers still moved, and the world still sorted itself the same way it always had: history out there, in the capitals and the ministries and the harbor offices; here only herring manifests, four hundred copies, due Thursday.

"Ulyana," Milo said, from the corner. "Can I ask you something?"

"You may ask. Whether anything occurs afterwards is another matter."

"Do you think things will change now? With the armistice and all the talking? Herr Lindqvist says there'll be a new age. My cousin says there'll be a war again inside ten years and he's putting money on it, which seems disloyal somehow."

"I think," Ulyana said, reaching into the case for an e, feeling for the little notch, the familiar wear of the metal, "that whoever is doing the talking is very far from here, and whatever they decide, on Friday morning I will still be setting manifests, and you will still be waxing the belt on Thursdays, and the gulls will still be stealing from the fish carts. Things change for other people, Milo. For us, things continue."

She heard him considering this, the broom slowing.

"That sounds sad," he said finally.

"It sounds realistic."

"My grandmother says realism is sadness wearing a hat."

"Your grandmother," Ulyana said, "should write pamphlets."

Later she would remember saying it. Later, when pamphlets became the whole of her life and the word itself grew teeth, she would remember tossing it out lightly over her shoulder, the way you toss a scrap to a dog, never imagining that somewhere out in the darkening city the word was already running toward her with its ears up.

But that was later. That evening there was only the stove and the herring salad and bread that was mostly bread, and her mother's chair drawn up close to the fire, and Milo let out at seven with a wave and a shout about seeing her tomorrow, and Ulyana washing the ink from her hands at the basin, watching the water run black, then gray, then clear, then black again where the cracks of her knuckles held it, ink that got everywhere, so did conviction.

She locked up at half past nine. She stood a moment in the doorway, breathing in the night version of the shop's smell — colder, mineral, the ink asleep in its pots — and looked down the empty street toward the harbor lights, and felt the peculiar comfort of a small life, well-fitted, like a glove.

History happened elsewhere. It had taken her father and it had left her alone, and it did not, in any event, concern the likes of Eriksen & Son, purveyors of manifests, menus, and memorial cards, established 1861.

She was pulling the door shut when she heard footsteps at the far end of the street — quick ones, uneven ones, the kind made by a man favoring one leg — and a shape detached itself from the shadow of the customs warehouse and started toward her, stumbling, holding something clutched to its chest like a satchel, like cargo, like something that must not be dropped.

Ulyana's hand found the key in the lock and stayed there.
