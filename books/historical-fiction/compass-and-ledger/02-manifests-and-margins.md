## 2. Manifests and Margins

The morning the trouble arrived, it came folded inside an ordinary envelope, which is how most troubles come — dressed in something dull so you'll let them through the door.

I was at the stone by half past seven, because ink dries differently in February and Father's rule had been that manifests were set before the stove warmed the shop enough to make the metal lie to you. *Type expands when it's comfortable, Ulyana,* he used to say, tapping his temple. *So do people. Keep both somewhere cold until you need them.* I had adopted the habit without adopting the wisdom, the way one inherits a coat that doesn't quite fit but is too good to throw away.

The Eriksen Print Shop sat on Binder's Lane like an old dog asleep on a doorstep: everyone knew it was there, no one expected anything of it. We did manifests for the herring fleet, bills of lading for the timber concerns, funeral notices, the occasional choir programme, and — this quarter — shipping schedules for the Provisional Maritime Authority, which paid late but paid, which in Vessengard in 1919 put them ahead of most institutions.

Milo came in at eight, snow in his eyebrows and a bun in each fist.

"You take one," he said, "or I eat both and weep about it after."

"Give it here." Milo was sixteen and my apprentice in the loosest sense — Father had taken him on two weeks before he left for the front, which meant Milo had learned his trade from me, which meant he'd learned it slightly wrong, cheerfully, and with opinions. He had a genius for making friends with everyone on Binder's Lane and a corresponding inability to keep a secret longer than a tide cycle.

We ate. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us our lodger Mrs. Kessel dragged her chair to the window for her constitutional observation of the street, which she performed aloud, like a commentator at a regatta.

"The manifest order," Milo said, licking his thumb. "Is that the big stack from the Authority?"

"Four thousand, when they bother to send the corrected figures."

"They sent a boy round yesterday. He had a very important hat."

"Everyone from the Authority has a very important hat. It's how they find each other."

He grinned and went to sweep, which he did the way he did everything — thoroughly for four minutes, then with increasing imagination. I unlocked the job case for the Authority work and drew out the copy they'd delivered: manifest forms, cargo declarations, tonnage tables. Dry as communion wafers. Perfect. I have always loved dry work. Dry work asks nothing of you but accuracy, and accuracy I could do all day; grief asks other things.

I set the first forme without incident. Herring barrels, salt weights, the *Vigilant*, the *Mercy of Kold*, the *Anna Sofia*. There is a meditation in it — the reach, the pull, the little steel click of each letter settling into its bed. People think printing is about ink. It isn't. It's about arranging absence so that what remains says exactly what you mean. Father taught me that too, though he phrased it less prettily and with more swearing.

It was on the third signature that I found the pages that didn't belong.

* * *

They were tucked into the middle of the copy packet — six leaves, same paper stock, same Authority letterhead, same officious margins. But they weren't manifests. They were personnel summaries: garrison returns, transport rosters, columns of names reduced to numbers, embarkation dates, and a heading stamped in red: *RECLASSIFIED — CIRCULAR 44*. And they didn't match the job ticket. The ticket said forty pages of shipping schedules. The packet held forty-six, and the extra six had been slipped in between signatures eleven and twelve like a razor in a loaf.

I stood there with my hands full of other people's arithmetic and felt the shop go very quiet around me, the way rooms do when they're waiting to see what you'll do.

"Milo," I called, because the silence was worse than company. "When the boy with the hat came — did he say anything?"

"That the figures were final and not to substitute last quarter's tables. Why?"

"Nothing. Hand me the quoin key, would you."

I locked up the manifest formes and set the extra six leaves on the stone beside me, face down at first, like something that might bite if watched. Then, being honest with myself in the way I am honest about everything except the things that matter, I turned them over and read them properly.

Most of it meant nothing to me. Numbers of men, numbers of cattle cars, dates in October and November of 1917 — the bad winter, the winter the telegrams came to every third door on Binder's Lane. There were place names I half knew from the newspapers: Rastok, the Veln marshes, the interior depot at Kalna. Column headings like *received* and *processed* and *disposed*, which are words for warehouses, for paperwork, for fish — not for men.

One line snagged me the way a nail head snags a stocking. In the remarks margin of the fourth page, someone — not the clerk who'd made the table; the hand was different, faster, angrier — had written in pencil: *these figures cannot be reconciled with the field reports. see attached. see NOTHING attached. — T.*

See nothing attached. Whoever T. was, he had been told to stop seeing. And instead of stopping, he had hidden his seeing inside a shipment of herring manifests bound for a print shop where nobody important would ever look.

That was the part that sat wrong in my chest, later. Not the numbers — those I couldn't make sense of anyway. It was that some stranger, some clerk with fast angry handwriting, had looked at our shop — Eriksen & Daughter, manifests and choir programmes — and thought: *nobody watches this place. Nobody will notice.*

We were furniture. We were safe because we were nothing.

I should have felt insulted. What I actually felt, standing at the stone with my fingers still smudged to the first knuckle, was relief, and then shame at the relief, and then a very strong wish for the kettle.

* * *

"What's that?" Milo asked at midday. He has the instincts of a cat where anything new is concerned.

"Extra stock from the Authority. Paper I can use for proofs."

"Looks official."

"Paper usually does. That's half its function."

He considered this, nodded profoundly, and went back to distributing type, which he did well now, most of the time, except for a persistent tendency to put the lowercase e where the c lived, so that any proof read slightly seasick. I slid the six pages into the drawer under the stone where we kept cancelled plates, and I shut it, and I heard the small wooden sound of it closing like a door being shut on a room I had decided not to enter.

Here is what I told myself, and I tell it to you exactly as I told it to myself, because a record that flatters the keeper is no record at all:

*I am a girl who sets type. The war happened at the front, the peace is happening in conference halls, and both of those places are a hundred train-changes away from Binder's Lane. If the Maritime Authority stuffs its secret papers into our envelopes, that is a matter between the Authority and its own conscience. If I print the manifests clean and ship them back, I have done my job. My father is dead — reported dead, twice over now, once by the army and once by the passage of time — and my mother sleeps upstairs with a bottle of tincture and a heart that beats like a faulty press, and I have neither time nor standing for other people's buried crimes.*

Every word of it true. That's the trick of it, you understand. You don't lie to yourself; you just select. Selection is the whole art.

* * *

Afternoon. Snow again, the fine dry kind that squeaks. Mrs. Kessel announced from upstairs that the tobacconist's boy had fallen off the curb again, "the same curb, that boy and that curb," and Milo went out to buy bread and returned having instead acquired a sled ride, a rumor about the armistice delegation, and no bread. I pulled proofs of the morning's formes and corrected Milo's seasick e's, and at four o'clock I did the thing I did every day at four o'clock, which was to look up at the ceiling and listen to whether Mother's floorboard creaked.

It creaked, twice, slow. Good days, the board creaked often. Bad days, not at all. Twice was a day with edges on it — she'd be sitting up, wanting tea, wanting not to say why she wanted tea.

I took her the cup. She was at the window wrapped in Father's old shawl, which she wore the way other widows wear lockets.

"Snow's easing," she said.

"For now."

"You've ink on your jaw. Left side."

"It's my war paint."

That got the almost-smile, the one that cost her something and paid it anyway. She looked at me a moment longer than the joke required — mothers have a second sight for their children's pockets, literal and otherwise — but she only said, "Don't stay at the stone past ten. Your father ruined his eyes doing exactly that," and drank her tea.

Your father. As if he were a cautionary tale and not a man. Two years since the telegram, three years this spring since he'd marched out with the reserve battalion singing something filthy adapted from a psalm, and grief, given enough time, stops being a wound and becomes furniture. You dust around it. You sit on it occasionally. You forget, in odd bright moments, that it's there at all, and then you put your whole weight on it by accident.

Back downstairs, the light going blue in the windows, I opened the cancelled-plate drawer. I don't know why. Yes I do. Because T.'s handwriting had reminded me of something and I'd spent the afternoon refusing to remember what, and refusing only works while your hands are busy.

Father's letters — the few that had come, before the ones that stopped — were in the desk in the back room, under the ledgers. His writing hand was fast and slanted downhill, and when he was irritated he pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. T.'s pencil note had that same downhill urgency. Half of Vessengard wrote like that, probably. Wartime makes everyone's hand hurry. I closed the drawer on that thought too, more firmly than necessary.

* * *

The manifests went to press the next day. Four thousand clean, accurate, unremarkable schedules for the herring fleet — and here is the detail that matters, the one I would take out and look at in later months like a coin I should never have accepted: the six extra pages went back into the return envelope, exactly as received, and I sealed it, and Milo carried it to the post, whistling.

I hadn't printed them. I want that understood, and I want you to hear how much it doesn't matter that I wanted it understood. I hadn't printed them, hadn't copied them, hadn't spoken of them. I had also not burned them, not written to the Authority asking why circulars were travelling in manifest packets, not mentioned to anyone at all that somewhere in the machinery of our new government there was a T. who had seen numbers that wouldn't reconcile and been told to see nothing.

I had done precisely nothing, efficiently, on schedule, and the shop smelled of oil and ink and the coffee Milo burned, and it was warm, and I told myself the cold feeling under my ribs was the weather.

Costs are quiet things at first. Nobody hands you a receipt. The cost of that Wednesday wasn't visible until later, when I learned what *disposed* had meant in those columns, what the winter of 1917 had been at Kalna depot, whose son walked past our window every morning to the chandlery. On that Wednesday it cost me only one thing, small as a splinter: I caught myself, pulling the last proofs, checking the margins of ordinary work for pencilled notes that weren't there. Looking where I'd decided not to look. A person who has truly decided nothing is wrong doesn't check.

"Ulyana." Milo, upside down over the imposing stone. "If the armistice really comes, will there be commemorative printing? Banners? Special editions? I'd like to set something large. Something with swash capitals."

"There'll be manifests," I said. "Peace runs on paperwork same as war. Worse, probably."

"One banner. With flourishes. Promise me a banner."

"I promise you a poster," I said, "if you promise to stop putting the e in the c's box."

"That was one week in November."

"It was the entire liturgical calendar."

He laughed, easy as breathing, and I envied him — sixteen, with his whole idea of history still shaped like banners and flourishes, big letters for big events, all of it happening to somebody else somewhere grander. I'd had that idea once too. I'd built my whole life inside it, comfortably, like a house with thick walls: revolutions happen to capitals, massacres happen to marshes, history is a parade that passes along the main boulevard and never turns down Binder's Lane.

The parade doesn't turn down Binder's Lane. I still believe that's true. What I learned — later, violently, at night, at my own loading door — is that history doesn't turn down your lane. It sends things to you in plain envelopes and counts on you being nobody.

The return envelope went out with the Thursday post. The Authority's boy with the important hat did not come again. Winter went on. And on Friday evening, five days after I sealed six pages back into their packet and called it cleanliness, there came a knocking at the loading door — three knocks, low, deliberate, nothing like the post — and a smell of blood and snowmelt, and a voice I had never heard before saying my father's name as if it were a password.

Which, as it turned out, it was.
