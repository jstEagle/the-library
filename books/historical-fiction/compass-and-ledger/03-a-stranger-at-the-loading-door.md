## 3. A Stranger at the Loading Door

The knock came at half past eleven, and it was wrong in every particular.

It was not the knock of the coal man, who arrived at six and hammered as though the door had insulted his mother. It was not the knock of Herr Dussek from the bindery next door, who tapped politely twice and then let himself in whether you answered or not, on the theory that printers were family whether they liked it or not. And it was certainly not the knock of a customer, because customers did not come at half past eleven at night, in November, in Vessengard, when the harbor fog came up the streets like something with intent.

This knock was three taps, spaced too far apart, with a long pause before the third — the knock of a man who had to think about each movement of his arm.

I put down my composing stick. I had been working late on the shipping manifests for Ostlund & Sons — forty crates of cured herring, God help us all, the most consequential document I would set all week — and the shop was dark except for the lamp over my stone. The press stood in its corner like a sleeping ox. Somewhere upstairs, Mother coughed once in her sleep and turned over.

Three more taps. Weaker.

I should have done nothing. That is what I would tell myself later, in the years when I told myself many things: *you should have done nothing, Ulyana, blown out the lamp, gone to bed, and let the night keep whatever it had brought you.* A sensible girl in a sensible city does not open her father's loading door to strangers after midnight. Vessengard in 1919 was full of strangers — demobilized men drifting home to houses that had been rented to other families, men with medals they sold for bread and wounds they mentioned never — and every one of them was somebody's tragedy, and none of them were mine.

But there are decisions the body makes before the mind is consulted, and I have come to believe that this is where character actually lives. Not in the things we deliberate over. In the things we do with our hand already reaching for the bolt.

I opened the door.

He fell against the frame rather than stood in it. Tall, or he would have been tall if he'd straightened; a greatcoat two sizes too big for him, military but stripped of insignia, the buttons mismatched — someone had dressed him in haste, or he had dressed himself out of a ditch. His face was the color of the fog. Under the stubble he could not have been more than twenty-five, and under the fever he could not have been more than a day from collapse.

And on his shoulder: a satchel. Canvas, army-issue, buckled shut, and gripped by his hand with the desperate force of a drowning man gripping driftwood. Even half-conscious, even swaying, that grip did not loosen. Whatever was in it, he had carried it further than he had carried himself.

"Frøkken Eriksen," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water.

"I don't know you."

"No." He tried to smile and produced something nearer to a grimace. "No, you don't. But I know—" and then his legs ended the sentence for him.

I am not a strong woman. Setting type does not build the shoulders, whatever Milo claims about the athletic glories of the trade. But I caught him, or mostly caught him, which is to say I slowed his descent enough that he landed against the loading step instead of through it, and then I stood in the doorway with the cold fog pouring past me into the shop and had my first real argument with myself.

*Close the door. He is nothing to you.*

*He said your name.*

*Everyone in this quarter knows the Eriksen name. There has been an Eriksen setting type on this street for sixty years. It is not a mystery; it is a sign above a door.*

*He is dying on your step.*

*That is a matter for doctors. Doctors are a matter for morning. Morning is a matter for people who mind their own business—*

The fog shifted behind him, and far down Krystalgade I heard boots. Not one pair. Four, at least, in step, which meant a patrol, which meant that whoever had dressed this boy in mismatched buttons had good reason to, and that reason might be minutes behind him.

I got him inside and threw the bolt.

* * *

Getting a feverish soldier across a print shop is not a graceful operation. The floor is a geography of obstacles: the stone, the imposing frame, the trolley of galleys, the crates of paper stacked two high because paper had begun, that autumn, to acquire the scarcity and price of a delicacy. Twice he tried to walk and twice his knees declined the invitation. In the end I took his arm across my shoulders and we progressed together in something between a waltz and a demolition, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *Father would laugh himself sick at this, look at you, Ulyana, dancing with herring manifests in your hand* — and then I remembered that Father was dead, and the thought went through me like a splinter, the way it always did, sharp going in and worse coming out, and I held the stranger tighter without meaning to.

I got him onto the old cot in the back room. Every print shop has a back room and every back room has a cot, because printing runs do not respect the clock and compositors have slept beside their presses since Gutenberg. Father used to nap there of an afternoon, hat over his eyes, snoring in a rhythm I could set time by, and Milo claimed the cot's springs had opinions about every part of the human body.

The cot took Anton's weight and complained. He did not. He lay where I put him like cargo, eyes half-open, following me around the room with a gaze that was fever-bright and yet, underneath the fever, watching everything. Taking inventory. My father's lamp. My father's cases. Me.

"The satchel," I said.

His hand closed on it again. Truly, I have seen fewer determined expressions on bank vaults.

"Give it here, then, if you love it so much. Under the cot it goes."

A pause while he considered this. Then, with the care of a man handing over a newborn, he passed it down, and I slid it into the dark beneath the frame of the cot, where it sat like a sleeping animal.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't what?"

"Read it." His eyes closed. Opened again. "Whatever happens. Don't read it. It isn't — it isn't yours." A breath that wanted to be a laugh. "Yet."

"Charming. A dying man who brings riddles to my door at midnight and instructions about my own back room."

"Not dying," he said, with the pedantic stubbornness of the very ill. "Feverish. There's a difference. Dying takes talent."

And then he was asleep, mid-sentence almost, the way soldiers sleep and sailors sleep and mothers of infants sleep — dropped through the floor of consciousness like a stone through water. I found a blanket that smelled of ink and thirty years of the shop and put it over him. His forehead, when my hand brushed it checking the fever, was hot as a galley just off the press.

I lit no lamp in the front room. I sat on Father's stool at Father's stone with the lamp turned low, and I listened to a strange man breathe in my father's back room, and outside, four pairs of boots went past along Krystalgade and paused — paused, I say — at the junction with the harbor road.

* * *

They came back. That is the part I want set down honestly, because in the stories a patrol passes once and the danger is done. In life, patrols double back. They know that anyone hiding anything waits for the boots to fade and then relaxes, and so they turn at the corner and walk their route again slowly, listening at doors.

The second time, they stopped outside the shop.

I heard them the way you hear thunder — first with the skin, then the ears. The boots halted. Metal sounds: a rifle butt adjusted, a buckle touched. Then voices, low, two men conferring, and the specific quality of silence that means someone is looking at your windows.

My lamp was hooded toward the stone. From the street it would show only as a faint warmth in the frosted glass, the kind of glow a shop left burning overnight might make, nothing remarkable — unless you knew that the Eriksen shop had not worked nights since the summer, since the owner died, since—

I stood up. And then I did not know what I intended to do, which frightened me more than the boots did, because I have never in my life not known what I intended to do. I am a compositor. My entire craft is knowing precisely what comes next, letter by letter, space by space. Uncertainty is for other professions.

From the back room, a sound. Anton, turning in fever, murmuring something in a language of half-words. If it reached the street — if one of those men had ears trained by three years of war to catch a human voice in a quiet building —

I did the only thing my hands knew how to do.

I started the press.

Not a run — God, no, nothing so loud. But Father's old Heidelberg had a voice, a great iron clatter-and-breath when it turned over idle, and I threw the clutch, fed it a dummy forme of blank lead I use for testing impression, and let it speak. The whole shop woke. The floor shivered. The windowpanes buzzed in their frames. To anyone passing, the message was unmistakable and boring beyond suspicion: *the Eriksen print shop is working a rush job. Herring manifests, probably. Good Lord, who cares.*

The boots conferred again. One of them laughed — I heard it through the glass, a short bark, the laugh of a man commiserating with a colleague about shift work — and they moved on, and this time they kept moving, and I stood at the press with my hands on its cold iron flank feeling my own heartbeat gradually descend from somewhere near my hairline.

That was when I understood I had made a decision. I had not decided anything, mind — no deliberation, no weighing of conscience, none of the solemn internal parliament that important choices are supposed to convene. I had simply hidden a stranger from a patrol, at risk of my shop, my mother's peace, and my own neck, for no better reason than that he said my name and gripped a satchel like it held the sun.

Instinct, not conviction. I want that recorded clearly, because later — much later — people would ask me when I chose, and the truthful answer is that choosing came afterward. First came the hands.

* * *

He slept through until the gray hour, when the fog lifts just enough to show you how dirty the windows are, and then the fever broke over him like a wave and he woke talking.

Not to me. To someone in 1917, apparently, someone named Larsen, someone he was telling — insisting — that the road was mined, *Larsen, the whole verge, look at the crows, the crows know* — and then he surfaced, saw the ceiling beams of a print shop, saw me, and had the grace to look mortified.

"How much did I say?"

"That the crows know something. About a road."

"Ah." He digested this. "Crows are reliable correspondents. Better than most newspapers." He tried to sit up, discovered what Milo's cot springs thought of the human body, and lay back with his jaw clenched. "Water?"

I brought water, and bread from the tin, stale, and he ate the way feverish men eat — mechanically, dutifully, food as logistics. Upstairs, Mother was stirring; I heard the floorboard outside her room complain its familiar complaint, and the small domestic clockwork of her kettle beginning. The world was resuming. Which meant I had perhaps ten minutes before I had to explain to myself, let alone anyone else, why there was a fugitive on the workshop cot.

"You knew my name," I said. "Last night. Before you knew anything else about me, you knew my name."

"Yes."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only part of the answer I can afford before breakfast." He looked at me properly for the first time, and I saw that under the wreck of him there was a steady sort of face, the kind that has decided things. "Frøkken Eriksen. Is this shop yours now?"

"It was my father's."

"And your father —"

"Dead. Two years at the front, and reported dead these two years past." I said it flatly, the way I had taught myself to say it, flatness being the only finish I could apply to that particular joint. "So if you've come about a funeral job, the timing is poor."

Something crossed his face. I would give a great deal, in retrospect, to have read it correctly then, instead of filing it away wrongly — filed it as pity, when it was something much closer to fear. He looked at the satchel's shape under the cot, just briefly, the way you glance at a door you're trying not to look at.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, and meant several other things besides.

"Who beat you to my door?" I asked. "Because men don't strip their own buttons. Someone dressed you. Someone sent you. And someone with four boots' worth of interest walked past this window twice last night looking for you."

He was quiet for a moment. Fog light came through the back-room's little window, gray and forgiving, and it made him look younger and more tired than lying about it ever could.

"If I tell you," he said, "the boots stop being out there and start being in here. With you. Permanently. You understand that? There's a version of today where you didn't open the door, and I'd like you to consider it seriously before I take it off the table."

I considered it. I considered it honestly, sitting on my father's stool with my arms folded, while upstairs my mother's kettle sang and next door Herr Dussek's early shutter rattled up and the whole ordinary machinery of Krystalgade cranked into motion around us, a street full of people who minding their own business had survived three years of war better than anyone.

Then I reached under the cot, and before he could finish saying my name — my full name, *Ulyana*, in a voice that suddenly wasn't asking permission anymore — I lifted the satchel out, weighed it in my hand, and set it on the blanket between us.

"You said it wasn't mine," I said. "*Yet.*"

He stared at me. Then he laughed — a real laugh, ruined halfway through by the cost of laughing with bruised ribs, so that it came out as a bark and a wince, and he pressed a hand to his side and shook his head at me.

"God," he said. "You set type like her."

The words landed before their meaning did. And then the meaning arrived, all at once, the way heavy type slides off an unsecured galley — everything shifting, crashing, too fast to catch — and I heard my own voice come from very far away:

"*Like who?*"

Anton looked up at me through the fever's wreckage, and his eyes were wet, and not from the fever.

"He talks about you in every letter," he said. "Every single one. 'My daughter,' he writes. 'My daughter keeps the cases.' Ulyana — your father is alive, and he sent me to find you."

The satchel sat between us on the blanket, canvas and buckles and weight, ordinary as a lunch parcel, and I could not move my hands toward it. Outside, Krystalgade rang with carts and shutters and the ordinary morning, and upstairs the kettle sang on, and the whole world I had arranged so carefully — dead father, small life, history happening elsewhere to other people — tilted slowly, like type slipping, toward the floor.
