## 4. Handwriting Like a Face

The letters came out of the satchel the way a snake comes out of a basket — slowly, and only when the person holding it has decided you are not going to scream.

Ulyana did not scream. What she did was worse, in a way: she laughed. One short, ugly syllable of a laugh, there and gone like a struck match, because the alternative was to let her knees stop being load-bearing structures in the middle of her own composing room at half past midnight.

"No," she said.

Anton — still propped against the stone doorstep of the loading door, still gray around the mouth, still smelling of river water and train smoke and something medicinal that Milo would later swear was horse liniment — said nothing. He simply held the envelope out another inch, as if distance were the problem.

"No," Ulyana said again, more firmly this time, the way one corrects an apprentice who has just suggested setting an entire hymnal in six-point. "My father is dead. He has been dead for three years. There is a telegram in my mother's bureau drawer with an official seal on it, and there is a grave marker in the churchyard at Sankt Ibs with his name cut into it, and I chose the quotation myself because I was twenty-two and grieving and thought *the pen outlives the hand* sounded wise instead of unbearable. So whatever those are—"

"They're in his hand," Anton said quietly.

And that was the whole catastrophe, right there. Because they were.

She had known it from the moment he drew the first envelope from the satchel, before he'd even offered it. She had known the way you know your own face in a shop window — instantly, unwillingly, without any possibility of appeal. The Eriksen hand was not a handwriting so much as a physiognomy. Henrik Eriksen had been setting type since he was eleven years old, and it had gotten into him the way coal dust gets into a miner's lungs. His pen moved the way his hands moved across a composing stick: economical, upright, with a peculiar little backward kick on the descenders, as though every g and every y were glancing over its shoulder to check the margin. He had a joke about it. He'd had a joke about everything. *"A man's letters should stand up straight, Lale, even if the man can't."*

The envelope in Anton's hand stood up straight.

The date in its corner — written in that same upright, margin-glancing script — was fourteen months ago.

* * *

"Sit down," Ulyana heard herself say, in what she privately thought of as her shopkeeper's voice, the one she used with delivery men who wanted paying in advance. It was a good voice. Steady. Reasonable. It belonged to a woman who was not currently watching a dead man write a date fourteen months into his own death. "You're going to fall over, and if you fall over you'll crack your head on the quoin stones, and then I'll have a body on my hands as well as—" she gestured at the envelope without touching it "—as well as whatever that is."

"There's a stool behind the proofing press," Anton said. His voice had a rasp in it, like a brayer running low on ink. "Your father used to keep one there. For customers who wanted to watch their jobbing done properly."

"You knew my father."

"For two years." A pause. "I'll tell you everything. But not standing at your open door with a patrol due past the harbor clock at one."

Ulyana looked at the loading door, hanging open onto the dark canal lane. Somewhere out there a fog was coming off the water, and somewhere beyond the fog was a world in which her life had been one thing ten minutes ago and was now some entirely other thing, and no one had consulted her about the change. Then she shut the door, threw both bolts, and turned around to find that her hands had already lit the lamp at the proofing bench and dragged out the stool, as if her body had made decisions her mind hadn't approved.

She fetched the medical kit from under the ledger counter while he talked. Not much of a kit: iodine, lint, a roll of bandage her mother had insisted on keeping stocked since 1915, when everyone kept bandages stocked. Anton's left shoulder was bound already, badly, the knot tied by someone using one hand and their teeth. She cut it away and did not let herself look at the wound longer than professionalism required, and rewrapped it, and all the while he talked in a low, flat courier's voice — routes and names and dates, most of which slid past her — until he said the sentence underneath all the other sentences:

"He asked me to tell you he was sorry about the pen."

Ulyana's hands stopped moving.

Because there was a pen. There was a specific pen, a stub-nosed black-handled thing her father had carried for thirty years and lost — actually lost, genuinely, furiously lost — the week before he marched out of Vessengard with the reserve regiment. Her mother had turned the shop inside out looking for it. Ulyana had once found her father at two in the morning, going through the type drawers drawer by drawer on the mad theory that he'd set it down among the twelve-em quads, and he had looked up at her with genuine anguish and said, *"Lale, I can fight a war, but I cannot fight it with someone else's pen."*

No living soul in Vessengard knew about the pen. It had never seemed important enough to tell anyone.

"I don't understand," she said, and was mortified to hear that her shopkeeper's voice had gone somewhere and left a girl's voice in its place. "If he's alive — if he's been alive all this time — why? Why would he let us think—"

"Read them," Anton said. "In order. Please. He wrote them for reading in order."

* * *

She read them sitting on the floor, because the stool had suddenly seemed too formal and the chair too far away, with her back against the cabinet of hand types and four envelopes in her lap arranged by date. The lamp made a small warm island. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like a customer who wouldn't leave.

The first letter was short. Six lines. It was dated eight days after the telegram announcing his death — after the battle at Kalvsund where his regiment had been reported destroyed — and it began, in that upright, margin-glancing hand: *My dearest Lale and Asta. By the time anyone can safely read this, you will have mourned me, and I am sorry for it, but you should know the mourning is premature and the report is a kindness I could not accept.*

Ulyana read that sentence perhaps nine times.

Then the second letter, longer, and here the story came out in pieces, in the careful, type-set prose of a man who knew exactly how much weight a sentence could bear. He had gone into the battle at Kalvsund and come out of it, barely, half-buried, and crawled away from the field into a marsh village where the people were not inclined to report deserters to an army that had burned their granaries the previous autumn. And in that village, and in the weeks after, he had seen things. Heard things. Been handed things — papers, testimony, lists — by men who trusted no one and had decided, for reasons he himself did not fully understand, to trust a middle-aged printer from Vessengard who happened to have a way with words.

*I could come home,* the third letter said. *Understand that. Every day for two years there has been a road home, and every day I have looked at it, and every day I have chosen not to walk it, because the things I carry die with me if I carry them into hiding, and they are worth more than my safety. I know what that sounds like to a wife and daughter. I know exactly what it sounds like, because I am a man who has spent his life choosing the precise word, and I am telling you I have searched for a gentler one and there isn't any.*

Ulyana put that letter down.

Her hands were shaking, which annoyed her enormously. She was a compositor; her hands were instruments; she had set forty pages of shipping manifests that very day without a single wrong fount, and now they shook over four envelopes like a drunkard's. She picked the third letter up again because putting it down felt like losing him twice.

There was anger coming. She could feel it assembling itself somewhere below her ribs, gathering sorts like a page being composed, but it wasn't ready yet. First there was just this — this awful, flooding, terrible hope, which was so much worse than grief had ever been. Grief was a settled account. Grief had a grave marker and a quotation and a drawer of her father's shirts that her mother had finally stopped opening in the night. Hope tore all that open again and scattered it across the floor and said, *well, now pick through it.*

He was alive. He had been alive at Christmas. He had written *give Milo my compliments and tell him his spacing is improving, though his justification remains a crime against the alphabet* — a joke, a bloody joke, in a letter written fourteen months ago by a dead man, and Ulyana found that this was the line that broke her. Not the atrocities, not the desertion, not even the apology about the pen. The joke. She sat on the floor of her father's shop with four letters in her lap and cried the way she had not cried at the funeral — loudly, gracelessly, into the crook of her elbow, while a wounded stranger sat three feet away holding a satchel and pretending, with great courtesy, to study the ceiling beams.

* * *

Which was when Milo came down the stairs.

They heard him first — the particular creak of the fourth step, the one that announced him to the entire household like a town crier — and Ulyana had approximately four seconds in which to become a dignified adult, an interval she used to wipe her face with her cuff and arrange the letters face-down beneath her palm.

Milo appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt with a candle, fifteen years old, hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog, wearing the expression of a boy who has heard crying through floorboards and is now committed to finding out why or dying in the attempt.

"It's half past one," he said.

"Yes."

"You're crying."

"No."

Milo looked at her. Then he looked at Anton — the stranger, the bloodied bandage, the satchel — with the unhurried thoroughness of a boy who had spent two years around printers and had learned that the truth is usually in the details nobody bothered to justify. Then he looked back at Ulyana.

"That's the stool from behind the proofing press," he said. "We moved it to the cellar in March."

"Milo—"

"And there's a stranger sitting on it with a hole in him." Milo set his candle down on the stone sill with great deliberation, folded his arms, and planted his feet in the stance he used when a delivery of type arrived short-weighted. "Frau Eriksen, I'm not stupid. I know I'm only the apprentice and it's none of my business what moves through this shop after dark. But you're crying, and you never cry, and he's got your father's satchel."

Ulyana blinked. "What?"

"The satchel." Milo pointed. "Oil-tanned hide, brass clasp replaced with a copper rivet after the clasp broke in the winter of '16, burn mark on the left flap from the paraffin stove. Herr Eriksen used to let me carry it to the bindery on Saturdays." His voice wobbled slightly on the last word, and he covered it by lifting his chin higher. "So either that's his satchel, or somebody went to a great deal of trouble to counterfeit a burn mark, and I'd like to know which."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Anton, who had been watching this exchange with an expression Ulyana would later describe to herself as *recognition*, said mildly, "He told me the boy would know it. He said — and I quote — 'tell Milo about the satchel, and he'll believe nothing else you say, because he trusts objects more than people.'"

"He said that?" Milo's voice cracked clean down the middle.

"He said you once accused a paper merchant of fraud over a single misaligned watermark, at age thirteen, and were proven right."

"I *was* right," Milo said, with dignity, and then sat down abruptly on the floor next to Ulyana as if his legs had resigned from the enterprise, and put one hand flat on the satchel as though taking an oath.

Ulyana looked at the two of them — the wounded courier and the hedgehog-haired apprentice, united in the lamplight by the testimony of a burnt leather flap — and felt something shift in the foundations of the evening. An hour ago this had been her catastrophe, hers alone, a private earthquake. Now there were witnesses. Now there was, absurdly, a *we*. She wasn't sure yet whether that made it better or infinitely worse.

"Milo," she said. "Go back to bed."

"Absolutely not."

"Milo."

"You're going to do something," Milo said, with the maddening certainty of the young, "and you're going to do it tonight, and I'm going to find out about it anyway, because I always find out. So you may as well tell me now, while I'm awake and can be useful."

"The fourth letter," Anton said quietly, to Ulyana rather than to the boy. "You haven't read it."

She looked down. The fourth envelope sat apart from the others, unopened, and it was different — thinner paper, written in a shakier version of the upright hand, dated only five weeks ago. Five weeks. He had been alive *five weeks ago*. Perhaps — her mind refused and then refused to refuse — perhaps he was alive *now*, tonight, at half past one in the morning, in some cell or cellar or ditch, breathing the same foggy Baltic air she was breathing.

Hope, she thought, was a cruelty dressed as a gift. Grief at least let you sleep.

She broke the seal. Inside was a single page, and it was not really a letter at all; it was instructions. It named a city official. It named a general — *Halvard*, a name she knew from the posters going up all over town, the victorious general's face already being printed by every shop that could hold a plate, including, last Tuesday, her own. And then, in the shakier hand, in lines that seemed to press harder as though the writer knew time was short:

*Everything I gathered is real, and everything I gathered will be buried unless it is printed, and printing is the family trade. Forgive me for asking this of you. I have searched for a way to ask it gently, and there isn't one.*

Ulyana read it twice. Then she looked up at the wall above the stone sink, where her father had hung, for as long as she could remember, a small framed motto over the wash basin, next to the dried-out pumice stone: *THE TRUTH, WELL SET, WILL HOLD A PAGE FOREVER.* She had grown up under that motto. She had never once, in twenty-five years, considered that it might someday constitute an order.

Downstairs, faintly, the harbor clock began to strike two.

"Well," said Milo, into the silence. "I'm making tea. This is clearly a tea situation."

* * *

He was alive. That was the fact she carried upstairs with her at three in the morning, past her mother's door, where a thin line of candlelight still showed beneath it — her mother slept badly these days, and Ulyana paused there with her hand half-raised to knock, and did not knock, and hated herself for both. *Not yet,* she told herself. *Not until I know what to say. Not until I know whether it's crueler to give her hope or to have given her false hope.* The lie sat uneasily in her mouth, because it was her father's kind of lie too — the calculated omission, the sentence justified to the margin — and she was beginning to suspect that families passed down more than handwriting.

Back in the composing room, Anton had fallen asleep sitting up on the stool, the satchel hugged to his chest like a child, and Milo had covered him with the canvas apron from the press and posted himself beside the door with the tea, refusing on principle to abandon his post.

Ulyana sat down at her case. It was an old habit, thinking at the type rack, her fingers moving over the cold brass rows the way other people counted rosary beads. *H-e-n-r-i-k.* She set his name in ten-point Caslon, absently, perfectly spaced, and held the little line of type up to the lamp.

Alive. Writing. Choosing, for three years, every single day, not to come home.

The anger finally finished composing itself. It rose in her with a beautiful, terrible clarity, and it was aimed in two directions at once — at the father who had chosen documents over daughters, and at the whole vast machinery of empire and army and official seal that had made his choice plausible, that had sent her a telegram with a stamp on it and expected the arithmetic to end there. Well. She came of printer's stock. They knew what to do with things that needed correcting.

"You're doing your angry face," Milo observed, from the doorway. "It looks like your father's angry face."

"Go to bed, Milo."

"In a minute." He hesitated, and for once the boy's relentless precision failed him, and what came out was simply young. "He's really alive? You're sure?"

Ulyana looked down at the line of type in her hand — *HENRIK*, upright, margin-glancing, standing as straight as the man who'd taught her to set it.

"No," she said honestly. "But I'm going to find out."

And then, because the night had already broken every rule it was going to break, she picked up the fourth letter again and read aloud, for the second time, the sentence she had not shown them — the one at the bottom of the page, added in a different ink, cramped against the margin as if written in haste, in a hand that trembled but never doubted:

*Ask Anton what happened at the fortress at Drenow. Ask him before you trust him with everything else.*
