## 6. The Ledger Room

The ledger room had been Father's kingdom, and like most kingdoms it was smaller inside than it had looked from the border.

Ulyana stood in the doorway with a lamp in one hand and a candle stub she didn't need in the other — a habit from childhood, when Father had insisted every visitor to the ledger room carry light in duplicate, because "the dark down here is greedy, Ulya, and it takes whatever flame you're willing to lose." She had been seven and convinced the room contained monsters. She was twenty-three now, and the monsters had moved upstairs into her chest, where they took up considerably more space.

Anton slept in the bindery with the satchel under his arm like a child's doll, bandaged, feverish, muttering now and then in a soldier's shorthand that she chose not to translate. Milo had gone home at midnight after asking three separate questions she'd answered badly. And her mother was asleep upstairs, or pretending to be, which amounted to the same ritual: the creak of the bedframe, the small cough, the long silence.

The clock over the stairwell said half past two. It had said half past two for eleven years; Father had never fixed it, claiming a print shop needed at least one thing in it that refused to obey.

"All right," Ulyana said to the empty room, because talking to herself had become unavoidable lately and she preferred to do it on her own terms. "Let's see what you were saying, Papa."

The ledgers lived in the long oak cabinet along the north wall, forty years of Eriksen & Son — which should have been Eriksen & Daughter, a joke Milo made once and would never be permitted to make again — arranged by year, spines cracked, corners furred with damp. She pulled 1913, the last full year before the war, and set it on the sloped reading desk Father had built from an old church pew. The lamp threw a warm circle. Outside, Vessengard breathed its harbor breath: brine, coal smoke, the far complaint of a gull that had clearly missed the memo about night.

She began to read.

This was, she realized within minutes, a strange kind of grief-work. Every page was his voice without being his voice. *12 March — 5 reams cream wove, Halvorsen Shipping, invoiced late again, the man is a thief but he is our thief.* *19 June — Milo dropped a full forme of nine-point. Charged him one krone and a promise. Promise pending.* She laughed out loud at that one, alone, at half past two, and the laugh came out wrong, wet at the edges. He had written these. His hand marched across every line, the same hand that had set type beside hers, the same hand that had ruffled her hair when ruffling hair was still allowed between grown people who pretended they weren't fond of each other.

And this man was dead. Officially dead. Reported at the front in '17, mourned in '18, memorialized in the very type he had cast himself — Ulyana had composed the death notice standing exactly where she stood now, choosing between *beloved father* and *devoted husband*, unable for an hour to decide, finally setting both.

Except Anton had put four letters on her kitchen table last night, each one in this same marching hand, and one of them dated eight months ago.

So either the dead wrote letters, or the world had lied to her with excellent typesetting, and Ulyana Eriksen had never yet met a lie that survived contact with a compositor.

* * *

She worked backward through the war years first, on instinct. 1917 — the year of his reported death. The entries thinned through autumn, as they would, as the shop's business thinned and Father's hands went to the front. But the thinning wasn't clean. In September there were orders she couldn't account for: *special job, cash, no invoice*. Again in October. Again in November, twice. Government work, the margins said, though no ministry name appeared, and government work always appeared with its name attached, because ministries wanted credit even for things they should have been ashamed of.

"Special job, cash, no invoice," she repeated. "You never did a special job in your life without invoicing God Himself."

Then, in the December ledger — the month after the notice of his death had arrived, which meant the entries must have been written before, or — she stopped. Her pencil, which she had begun chewing somewhere around the 1914 volume, hovered over the page.

There were numbers. Not sums. Numbers as entries, where client names ought to be.

*No. 14 — 40.*
*No. 15 — 40.*
*No. 16 — 60.*

Forty what? Sixty what? No unit, no price, no description. Just the numbers, entered in the debit column with the serene confidence of a man recording something perfectly ordinary. She flipped forward. The pattern continued into January, February — dates she knew, from Anton's documents still folded in her apron pocket, were dates that meant something. Dates that Anton had touched with a grimy forefinger last night and said, *these are the convoy days. These are when they moved them.*

She laid Anton's papers on the pew-desk beside the ledger, weighting the curling corners with the brass ink pots. The documents were copies — poor ones, made in haste somewhere with a bad roller, the ink ghosted and patchy — requisitions, transport manifests, a memorandum whose signature block made her stomach fold over on itself: the name of General Halvard, the architect of the armistice, the hero being chalked onto walls all over the city by children who did not know better.

The convoy dates matched. Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Forty men, forty men, sixty men. Not cargo tonnage. Men. Moved in November, in December, in January — moved *after* the date her family had held a funeral for a man who might have known about it.

"No," she said quietly, to no one. "No, Papa, you didn't know. Tell me you didn't know."

But the ledger only sat there, being a ledger, which is to say being honest in a way people are rarely brave enough to be out loud.

She turned back to 1916 and forced herself through it line by line, the way she'd proof a difficult forme, and there — buried in July, in the middle of a routine order for fish-crate labels, so ordinary she nearly skimmed past it — was a string of figures that stopped her heart and restarted it out of rhythm:

*f. 3/14 — k. 2/9 — s. 1/22.*

Nonsense. Except it wasn't nonsense to a compositor. Fractions. Em measurements. Type sizes and spacing values. Any printer reading those figures would see garbage; only a printer who knew *her father's hand*, his particular habits, would ever think to read them twice.

Her father had kept a second ledger inside the first, written in the language of his trade, hidden in plain sight among shipping manifests and crate labels. And if she was right about what she was looking at —

Upstairs, a floorboard groaned.

Ulyana went absolutely still, the way she had as a girl when caught reading under the counter: lamp dimmed, breath banked, ears straining. A pause. A small cough — Mother's cough, the thin one, the one Dr. Reval called "nothing to worry about" in a tone that worried everyone. Then footsteps, soft, crossing toward the water jug. Then silence again.

She hadn't told her mother anything yet. That was going to detonate on its own schedule and she could not afford it tonight; Mother's heart had run unevenly since the telegram came, and Ulyana rationed shocks for her the way the city rationed coal. One crisis per household per winter. The wounded stranger in the bindery did not count, because Mother did not know about him either, which was a house of cards Ulyana was maintaining with both hands and her chin.

When the silence upstairs had held for a full minute, she bent back to the ledger.

* * *

It took her until nearly four o'clock to break the code properly, and when she did, it was almost embarrassing how simple it was. Not simple to find — simple once seen, like all good hiding places. Father had converted letters into typographic measurements using a system keyed to the case layout itself. *f. 3/14* wasn't a fraction; it was a position — folio case, third row, fourteenth compartment. She fetched a spare jobbing case from the rack, ran her finger along the rows counting compartments, and felt the hairs rise on her arms.

Third row, fourteenth compartment: **T**.
Second row, ninth: **H**.
First row, twenty-second: **E**.

*T-H-E.*

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you clever, impossible man."

She copied the case layout onto butcher paper, built herself a key, and translated. The July entry resolved into a sentence fragment: *...CONVOY ROUTE VIA OSTMARK BRIDGE...* An August entry gave a place-name she didn't recognize followed by *WITNESS SAFE WITH HOLM*. September: *THEY BURN THE VILLAGES AFTER. I HAVE LISTS.*

She sat back. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere below the floor, in the cellar where they stored the solvents, a pipe knocked twice as the building settled, and she nearly went through the ceiling.

*I have lists.*

Lists. Anton's satchel was full of lists. Copies of lists — but someone had made originals, someone with access to military paperwork and a compositor's discipline and a deserter's motive, someone who had sent coded messages home through the only channel the army could never search: his own print shop's billing records, mailed monthly to his own address, read by no one because who reads invoices?

The past had been speaking all along. For two years it had been sitting ten feet from her bed, speaking in em-quads and fractions, and she had dusted its shelf and never once listened.

She thought about all the evenings she'd spent in this room after his death, doing the accounts because somebody had to, skimming past the odd entries with the efficient blindness of a woman who had decided that history happened elsewhere — to capitals, to grandees, to generals whose faces went on posters. She had looked directly at *No. 14 — 40* and seen nothing, because seeing nothing was comfortable, and comfort was the family business along with printing.

"I'm sorry," she told the ledger. It seemed important to say it to the correct party. "I looked away. You were shouting and I filed you under fish-crate labels."

The ledger, having no opinion, offered its pages to her like an open hand.

* * *

By five o'clock she had translated six full entries and filled four pages of notes in a cipher of her own devising, because if Father could hide truth in a ledger she could certainly hide it in grocery lists. Her eyes burned. Her neck had the permanent ache of the devoted. And she had arrived at the edge of something she did not want to look at straight-on: the *WITNESS SAFE WITH HOLM* entry.

Holm. There was a Signe Holm who ran a bookbindery off Weaver's Lane, a master of the guild older than the century, who had once told fourteen-year-old Ulyana that her kerning was "competent, which at your age is a tragedy." If Holm was a name in her father's code —

That was tomorrow's problem. Possibly the day after. She was learning, rapidly, that conspiracies run on postponement.

She was copying the final entry when the stairs spoke.

Not the groan of settling timber this time. Footsteps. Deliberate ones, in house slippers, descending at the pace of a woman who wanted to be heard coming. Ulyana swept the notes into her apron, closed the ledger, and had achieved a pose of innocent industry — checking stock counts, honestly, the picture of filial diligence — by the time her mother appeared in the doorway in dressing gown and shawl, holding the candle she no longer needed any more than Ulyana had.

Mother looked at the lamp, the open ledgers, the spread of papers, and the brass weights sitting on documents that were very obviously not invoices.

"You're doing the accounts," Mother said, "with a dead man's private papers."

"He isn't dead," said Ulyana's mouth, entirely without authorization.

The silence that followed was the longest of the night. Above them the unfixed clock declined to tick. Mother set her candle on the desk beside the lamp, lowered herself onto the pew bench with the care of a woman negotiating with her own heart, and pulled the 1917 ledger toward her.

"Show me," she said. "Start from the beginning, Ulya, and don't tidy it for me. I've been tidied enough for two years."

Which was when Ulyana understood, with a lurch, that her mother had heard the floorboards too — had perhaps been lying awake listening to this whole investigation breathe beneath her — and that the fragile health upstairs was not the only secret in the house that had been waiting patiently to be found.

Mother turned the ledger toward the light and began, in her slow schoolroom reading-voice, to sound out the first coded line.

"That word," she said, tapping it. "Is that an *f*? Your father always set his *f*s crooked."

"It's a position in the case, Mamma. Third row—"

"Never mind the mechanics." Her mother's finger stayed on the letter, steady as a compositor's. "Read me what your father wrote."

Ulyana took a breath, and reached for her key.
