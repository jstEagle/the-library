## 7. Milo Asks Questions

Milo Brandt — no relation to the inspector, he'd been asked that twice a week since the inspector started calling, and no, he did not find it funny anymore — had worked at Eriksen & Daughter since he was eleven years old, which meant he knew the shop the way other boys knew their own pockets.

He knew that the third stair to the flat above creaked in E-flat, and that if you stepped on the edge nearest the wall you could go up at midnight without waking Mrs. Eriksen. He knew that Ulyana pretended not to know he stole liquorice from the drawer under the composing stone, and that she moved the drawer two inches further from his elbow every month like a slow game of chess she intended to lose forever. He knew the press had a temper on damp days and a saint's patience on dry ones, and that Mr. Eriksen — the late Mr. Eriksen, God rest him, though lately Milo had begun saying it faster, the way you skip a stone you're tired of skipping — used to tap the frame twice before starting a run, for luck, and that Ulyana had caught herself doing it last winter and had gone out into the yard and stood in the snow until her ears went red.

So when things in the shop began to be wrong, Milo noticed. It was practically his job. Nobody paid him to notice, but nobody paid him to sweep properly either, and he did both anyway, out of pride.

The wrongness arrived in three installments.

The first was the back room. The room behind the storeroom where old plates and broken chases lived, the room that had been locked before but never *locked-locked* — the key had always hung on its nail by the door like a promise, and any apprentice worth his apron could get at it with a chair and forty seconds if the nail were ever empty. Now the key was gone from the nail. Now the lock had been oiled. Oiled! Milo had put his ear to the door one evening and heard, faintly, the sound of a man coughing the way men cough when they are trying not to cough, which is the loudest coughing there is.

The second installment was food. Ulyana had always cooked for two and a half — herself, her mother, and a generous allowance for Milo's appetite, which she claimed was the eighth wonder of Vessengard. Now she cooked for four and lied about it. There were three bowls of barley soup going upstairs one night, and Milo counted, because counting was also practically his job, and when he asked, Ulyana said, "Mother's appetite is back," in a voice that suggested Mrs. Eriksen had suddenly developed the table manners of a stevedore. Mrs. Eriksen, who took her meals in bed and managed six spoonfuls on a good day, would have needed to eat like a stevedore indeed to account for the extra bread. Milo bought a loaf himself the next morning and left it on the counter without comment, and watched Ulyana look at it the way a card player looks at a card she didn't expect but can use.

The third installment was the bandages.

He saw the man on a Thursday, or rather he saw the *evidence* of the man, because whoever was in the back room had been in the back room with a thoroughness that Milo respected professionally. But bandages leave traces. A bloodied rag balled up too hastily behind the coal scuttle. The smell of carbolic soap, which Ulyana had bought at the apothecary on Kessel Lane even though the apothecary there charged a kopeck more, because she was loyal to the wrong apothecary out of pure stubbornness, another thing about her that Milo had decided long ago was a virtue wearing an inconvenient coat. And once — only once — a boot printed in wet ash by the yard door, a boot much larger than Ulyana's, longer than her father's had ever been, with a military kind of heel.

A soldier's boot, hidden in a print shop, behind a newly oiled lock, fed by phantom soup.

Milo stood in the yard holding the ash-print question in his chest and thought, very seriously, about what he was going to do with it.

* * *

Here is what Milo knew about trouble, having read about it extensively in the penny serials he traded with the bindery boy next door: trouble came in through doors and windows and occasionally through beautiful widows, and it was always obvious to the reader from page one. The hero's best friend was always the last to know. The best friend usually died in chapter nine, saying something loyal.

Milo was fifteen and had no intention of dying in chapter nine, or any chapter, ideally living to a comfortable old age with a shop of his own and a wife who found his appetite charming rather than ruinous. Self-preservation was not cowardice; it was arithmetic. A boy with no family, no money, and a warrant against him was a boy who had done his sums wrong. The correct answer to a soldier's boot in the ash was to keep sweeping, keep stealing liquorice, keep his nose in a case of twelve-point bodoni where it belonged.

That was the arithmetic. He ran it three times. It kept coming out: *mind your own business.*

But then there was the other column, the one that wouldn't stay empty no matter how hard he tried not to write anything in it.

There was the fact that Mr. Eriksen had taught him his letters on the job, patiently, using pieces of furniture as examples — "the chase holds everything, Milo, like a mother holds a family" — while Milo's actual schoolmasters had taught him mostly how to stand in corners. There was the fact that when the telegram about the death had come, two years ago now, Ulyana had gone very still and then very busy, the way she did, and Milo had fetched tea and said nothing, and she had said, later, not looking up from her type, "You're a good boy, Milo," which was four words he had kept like coins in a closed fist ever since, taking them out only at night. There was the fact that this shop was the only place in Vessengard where Milo Brandt was somebody rather than somebody's problem.

And there was the fact — and this was the one that tipped the ledger, he admitted it grudgingly — that Ulyana had stopped tapping the press frame twice for luck.

She'd started again last Tuesday. Twice, every run, quick and embarrassed, like a person resuming a habit they'd sworn off. Which meant hope had come into the house. Hope wore a soldier's boot and coughed behind oiled locks, and whatever else that was, it was not the kind of trouble that wanted to hurt the Eriksens. You didn't start hoping again over something bad. Milo had done enough reading to know that.

He did his sums one final time, in the yard, in the cold, and got a different answer than before, and was annoyed about it, because the new answer involved courage, and courage had never once appeared on any of his previous calculations.

"Right," he said to the ash print. "But I'm doing it quietly."

* * *

His campaign of quiet help began the next morning with bread.

If there was a fourth mouth, then there should be proper bread, and not the grey war loaf that broke your teeth, so Milo rose early and queued at the bakery on Fiskargatan where the good rye came out at seven, and he bought two loaves instead of one and said, when the baker's wife raised an eyebrow, "Growing," and patted his stomach, which was already considerable and showed no signs of heroic growth. She gave him the second loaf half price out of respect for the joke.

He left one loaf on the counter beside the flour bin, angled so that anyone coming through the back stairs would pass it naturally, and said nothing.

Ulyana looked at the loaf. Then at him. He was very busy with a stick of furniture.

"New loaf," she said carefully.

"Had a coupon," Milo lied fluently, and discovered, with a small internal jolt, that lying was easier than he'd expected and that he disliked how easy it was. He resolved to save it for worthy causes.

* * *

The second act of quiet help was medical, sort of. Milo's aunt Halina had been a nurse in the epidemic year, and before she'd moved to the coast she'd left him a tin of things — salve, rolled linen, a needle and gut, iodine in a brown bottle that leaked a little if you held it wrong. He had kept the tin under his cot for years for reasons he couldn't articulate, some instinct that supplies might matter someday. He wrapped the tin in brown paper and tied it with string and left it inside the back-room door's reach, wedged between a crate of worn quoins and the wall, where a man moving quietly through the shop at night would be almost certain to see it, and where a man who wanted to ask questions would have to actually say the words *I found a medical tin,* which Milo was not yet prepared to do.

The tin was gone by Saturday. In its place, tucked under the string of the brown paper, re-tied neatly, was a coin. A whole mark.

Milo stared at it for a long time.

Then he took the coin down to the corner shop and spent it on liquorice and candles — candles being expensive and useful and therefore a legitimate expense of his conscience — and brought the candles home and put them in the cupboard by the lamp without comment, and ate the liquorice, because quiet help should not require total self-sacrifice.

* * *

It was the candles that undid him. Or rather, it was Ulyana finding the candles, and the conversation that followed, which wasn't a conversation at all and was therefore the most honest one they'd ever had.

She caught him Monday evening at the stone, setting a wedding announcement for the Sørland family, whose daughter was marrying a fish merchant's son in what everyone agreed was a merger more than a romance. His hands were doing the work while his mind did other work, which was the correct way to set type, according to Mr. Eriksen, who said a compositor's hands should know the alphabet better than his head did, freeing the head for higher things. Milo's head had been free for higher things all week and had mostly used the freedom to worry.

"Milo." Ulyana was in the doorway with the lamp. She had the candles in her hand. "These."

"What about them?"

"Nothing about them." She turned one over. "They just appeared."

"Coupons," said Milo.

"You don't have coupons for candles."

"I have many coupons. I'm a man of coupons."

She looked at him across the lamplit shop, and Milo looked back, and for a moment neither of them performed anything at all. He saw her weigh it — tell him, don't tell him — and he watched her decide, with visible effort, to protect him by leaving him outside the oiled door, and something in his chest went tight and stubborn, because being protected was very nearly the same as being dismissed, and he had spent fifteen years learning the difference.

"If you needed candles," she said at last, "you only had to ask."

"I know," said Milo. And then, because loyalty wars with itself and sometimes wins loudly: "Ulyana. If there was ever a thing I shouldn't ask about — I'm not asking about it. That's all. That's my whole sentence."

Her face did several things quickly, none of which she'd have permitted if she'd had time to rehearse. "That's a strange sentence."

"I know."

"It's a nonsense sentence."

"Yes," Milo agreed. "But it's set solid. Justified both margins." He held up the stick of type to demonstrate: perfectly flush. "You could print it."

Ulyana laughed — a short, cracked sound, half relief, half something else — and put the candles back in the cupboard, and went upstairs, and did not fire him, and did not explain, and Milo understood that this was the arrangement now: he would not ask, and she would not lie, and somewhere behind an oiled door a man with a soldier's boots would eat phantom soup with proper bread and dress his wounds with Aunt Halina's iodine, and all three of them would pretend the shop contained exactly the people it appeared to contain.

He finished the wedding announcement. He was pleased with it. The Sørlands would never know their happy news had been composed by a boy committing his first deliberate acts of conspiracy, and that he'd set their names with unusual care because it felt good, just then, to handle letters that meant what they said.

* * *

The test came on Wednesday, and it wore a uniform.

Not the inspector — Milo had learned to distinguish official footsteps the way farmers distinguished weather, and Inspector Brandt walked like a man who enjoyed his own authority and wanted the floorboards to enjoy it too. This was different: boots in the yard at dusk, two pairs, and a knock at the loading door with the particular flat rhythm of authority that expects to be obeyed by buildings.

Milo was alone downstairs. Ulyana had gone to buy ink; the man — and he was sure now, sure as type metal — was behind the oiled lock, and Mrs. Eriksen was upstairs, and the arithmetic Milo had done in the yard came due all at once, compounding interest.

He wiped his hands and opened the door with the expression of a mildly inconvenienced apprentice, an expression he'd practiced in the yard mirror of a morning, along with looking taller.

Two soldiers. Younger than he'd expected. One held a paper. "Print shop?"

"It says so on the window," said Milo, "in eight-point Grotesque, if you're admiring the lettering."

The paper was a requisition notice — confiscated stock lists, a search of premises on the lane, routine. "We're checking the bindery next door. We'll want to see your stock room after."

"Stock room's a mess," said Milo honestly. "We've a funeral order on. Coffin's in the yard, if you want to inspect that instead. Very well-made. Local oak."

The younger soldier blinked. The older one said, "After," and they moved off toward the bindery, and Milo shut the loading door and leaned against it and discovered his heart was going like a press on a fast run.

Then he did not think. Thinking was for people with time. He crossed the shop, took the chair, and had the back-room lock open in thirty seconds — forty seconds was for beginners — and stuck his head in and said, in the fastest whisper of his life:

"There are soldiers next door and they'll come here after, probably ten minutes, maybe less. There's a coffin in the yard, it's for the Hegge funeral, it's empty and lined in shavings. I'm just saying it's there. I'm not suggesting anything. I'm an apprentice who tells people where coffins are."

The man in the back room was sitting up against the plate shelves, one shoulder bound, face grey and handsome in the way ruined things are handsome, and he looked at Milo with eyes that did rapid arithmetic of their own.

"How old are you?" the man said.

"Fifteen and three quarters."

"You open locks fast for fifteen and three quarters."

"The nail trick is standard trade knowledge," Milo said, offended. "Ten minutes. Coffin. Shavings. I'll be at the stone setting type like innocence itself." And he was gone, resetting the lock, back at the case, pulling sorts with hands that shook only slightly and then steadied, because the hands, as Mr. Eriksen had promised, knew the alphabet better than the head, and the head could panic on its own time.

The soldiers came through ten minutes later, poked the stock room, admired nothing, made a joke about the coffin that Milo answered so earnestly and at such length about local oak versus imported pine that they left mainly to escape the conversation. From the yard gate Milo heard the bindery boy crying, heard furniture being broken next door, and stood very still among his safe, dull, beautiful letters, understanding for the first time that the difference between his shop and that one was currently hiding in a coffin, and that he had put it there.

* * *

When the soldiers were gone and the yard was quiet, Ulyana came home to a shop that was spotless, a candle burning steady, and an apprentice setting type with suspicious serenity. Behind the oiled lock, a coffin lid eased shut. She read the room the way she read a galley — fast, top to bottom — and her eyes came to rest on Milo with a question in them.

"Soldiers?" she said.

"Next door, mostly. They liked the coffin." Milo pulled a spacing sort and set it. "Also, Ulyana, I should mention — I've decided I'm not asking about anything. Formally. Consider it announced." He paused, then added, because honesty deserved at least one margin: "The nail trick takes me thirty seconds now. If that ever becomes relevant information."

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment, this boy she'd inherited along with the shop, this appetite with legs, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop pretending, all at once, like a press releasing.

"Come downstairs," she said. "Both of you. There's someone you need to meet — and afterward, we're going to need every candle in that cupboard, and probably more. Because we're going to print something, Milo, and we'll only get one chance to set it right."

Milo put down his stick. Somewhere in his chest, the ledger of self-preservation quietly balanced itself, and he followed her down the stairs, stepping lightly over the third step, which creaked in E-flat.
