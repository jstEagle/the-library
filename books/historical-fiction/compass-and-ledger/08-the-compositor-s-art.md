## 8. The Compositor's Art

Anton slept until nearly noon, which Ulyana decided was the least he could do. A man who had walked — she still didn't know from where, exactly; he was sparing with geography the way other men were sparing with confessions — deserved a mattress behind a locked back room and a few hours in which nobody knocked.

She gave him until one o'clock out of mercy and until half past out of impatience, and then she went down to the shop and set type for a funeral notice for the Hesselby girl, who had died of the influenza at nineteen, and tried not to think about how many of these she had set in the last two years. The shop had printed more obituaries since 1916 than wedding announcements, more ration cards than poems. She had stopped counting. Counting was a kind of grief, and she had budgeted hers carefully.

At half past one she climbed the stairs with two cups of chicory coffee and knocked on the back room door in the little pattern her father used to use — two quick, one slow — before she remembered that Anton wouldn't know the code, and that the code had been for customers, anyway, and that she was knocking on her own door like a stranger.

"Come in," Anton said. He was sitting up on the cot, bandaged shoulder propped against the wall, her father's letters arranged on the blanket in front of him like a hand of cards he'd been studying for hours.

"You should eat," she said, handing him a cup.

"There's bread?"

"There is always bread. There is nothing but bread." She sat down on the crate that served this room as a chair — it had once held type metal from Hamburg, according to the stamp, though it had held nothing but dust since before the war. "You've been reading them all morning."

"I've been reading them for three years," Anton said, and then seemed to hear how that sounded, and looked down at the coffee. "I mean — I know them by heart now. I could recite them. But seeing them laid out together, all seven—" He touched the topmost page with one finger, careful, the way he might touch a wound. "Your father has a way of ending every letter in the middle of a thought. As if he couldn't bear to finish any sentence completely."

"He does do that," Ulyana said. Her throat felt strange. "He did it in letters home too. Mother used to say he wrote the way he talked — always leaving the door open behind him."

They were quiet for a moment. Somewhere upstairs, her mother moved about the kitchen with the deliberate slowness that had replaced her old briskness — each pot set down gently, as if the house were made of glass. Milo's footsteps clumped above them too; he was ostensibly sorting the paper stock, and actually doing it badly, because Milo sorted anything badly when his mind was elsewhere, and his mind had been elsewhere since the night a bleeding stranger came through the loading door.

"Anton," Ulyana said. "If we're going to do this — the pamphlet — you need to understand something first."

"I'm listening."

"You can't write it. Not the way you want to."

His jaw tightened. She watched him decide whether to be offended, and appreciated that he at least considered the question before landing on offense.

"I've seen what happened at Kolvik," he said quietly. "I carried the testimony out myself. I think I've earned—"

"It has nothing to do with earned." She set her cup down on the floorboards. "Look at your handwriting."

He looked at it. It was a soldier's hand, she supposed — cramped, urgent, all slant and pressure, letters leaning into each other like men in a queue.

"Now look at his." She picked up the topmost letter and turned it toward him. "My father sets type. Has done for thirty years. It ruins you for writing — did you know that? Your hand forgets how to make its own shapes. Everything comes out wanting to be justified." She traced the lines without touching them. "But that's not the point. The point is: whatever we print, they will trace. Brandt's people will hold a sheet of this pamphlet up to the light and ask, who set this, where, on whose press. If your hand is anywhere in it, or mine in the wrong places, we're found inside a week. And worse —" she tapped the letter — "if the pamphlet doesn't read like *him*, then it isn't him, is it? It's just some stranger's account of what a dead man said. Nobody believes a ghost secondhand. It has to sound like Elias Eriksen wrote it, because he did."

Anton was silent for a long moment. When he spoke again, the edge had gone out of his voice. "So what do we do?"

"We set it," Ulyana said. "In type. Properly. Which means someone has to teach you how a composing stick works, because I am not doing three thousand words alone before the armistice lands on us, and Milo can't be told yet — not all of it."

* * *

The shop at night was a different animal from the shop by day. By day it belonged to invoices and the smell of coal oil and Milo's whistling, which was always slightly flat. By night it was hers — the great case of drawers with its hundred small compartments, each holding a letter of the alphabet in cold gray lead, and the stone-topped imposing table where pages grew line by line, and the old Albion press in the corner, squat and patient as a churchwarden.

She lit only two lamps, and shaded one of them with a tin funnel so the glow didn't reach the front windows. Then she took down an empty composing stick and put it in Anton's hands.

"That's the stick," she said. "It's held like this — see, in the left hand, notch toward you. The type goes in upside down and backward, and reads right way round only after it's printed. You'll fight that for an hour and then your brain will simply give up and accept it, the way sailors accept the horizon moving."

"And the letters themselves?"

"Come here." She pulled open a drawer halfway down the case. Even in the lamplight the compartments were beautifully ordered, each little steel wall dividing e from f, f from g, the most-used letters in the biggest boxes near the center where a compositor's fingers could find them without looking. "This is the lower case. Capitals live above — hence the phrase, upper and lower case, which printers have been saying so long that writers steal it without asking. Like everything else writers take from us."

Anton laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if laughter had to be smuggled past the rest of him. She noticed he guarded his ribs when he laughed, and filed that away.

"These are yours?" he asked, looking at the compartments. "All this order."

"The shop's. My father's system, my hands keeping it." She lifted out a piece of type between finger and thumb and handed it to him. "Go on. Look at it properly. Everyone looks at type properly once."

He held the little block of lead alloy up close to the funnel-shaded lamp, turning it. On its face, reversed and raised, stood a single lowercase *a*, crisp-edged, perfect.

"It's so small," he said. "And so exact. I never thought of letters as objects."

"Most people don't. That's rather the point of us." She took it back. "Now — the rules. There are only five, and you'll break four of them tonight. One: don't drop type face-down, ever; a scratched face prints like a scar. Two: distribute what you set — when a page is done and printed, every letter goes back to its own box, no excuses, no 'later.' Three: mind your measure — this little brass tab here is the width of our line, and every line must end exactly there, no sooner, no later. Four: don't lean on the stone. And five—"

She paused. He waited.

"Five," she said, "is that you never set a word you aren't prepared to stand behind, because type is evidence. Every letter is signed, even unsigned. Printers are the only writers who leave fingerprints in lead."

Anton looked down at the composing stick in his hand as though it had grown heavier.

"Good," Ulyana said, softer. "That's the right face for it. Now come and ruin some type."

* * *

He was, she discovered over the next hour, an attentive student and an appalling one at the same time — attentive to everything except the thing directly in front of him. His fingers were strong and careful when she reminded them to be, but his eyes kept drifting to the letters lying on the stone: her father's pages, which she had brought down and locked in a proofing frame so they could study the text itself.

"He starts every paragraph with a person," Anton said, for perhaps the fourth time. "Do you see it? Never an abstraction. Not 'the regiment was ordered to.' Always 'Sergeant Voss was ordered to, and Voss was a man from somewhere, with children.'" He shook his head slowly. "I tried to write it the other way. Numbers. Dates. The language of reports, because reports are what courts believe. He refused. He said numbers let the reader off."

"He's right," Ulyana said. "Numbers are how governments count. Persons are how people count." She reached past him — closer than strictly necessary, she noted, and did not note it further — and slid a line of type into the stick. "Here. You've got the em-quads backwards again."

"How can a space be backwards? It's empty."

"Empty things have direction in this trade. Ask any widow." She heard herself say it and winced, and Anton went very still beside her, and she realized the joke had landed somewhere in both of them at once — his losses, hers, the whole quiet continent of grief the war had left behind. Neither of them laughed. Neither of them apologized either. Some silences were a form of understanding, she was learning, and better left standing.

They worked. She demonstrated; he attempted. His first line — "Testimony concerning the conduct of the Third Brigade at Kolvik, gathered by Elias Eriksen, printer" — took him eleven minutes and contained, by her count, four errors: two upside-down letters, one n for an h, and a comma where an en-quad belonged. She fixed them without comment, sliding the corrected pieces in with her fingertips, and he watched her hands do it with an expression she couldn't name and didn't try to.

"Again," she said.

His second line took nine minutes and had two errors. His third took eight and had one. By midnight he was setting simple sentences at a pace that would have gotten him dismissed from any respectable shop in Vessengard, and knew it, and kept going anyway, and she found she liked him enormously for that.

"You're patient," he said at some point, concentrating hard on an *m*.

"I'm a teacher with a lamp. Patience is cheap after dark." She pulled the galley proof of their trial lines and dusted it, and there it was in black on white — clumsy, gappy, unmistakably alive. His first printed words. He stared at them a long time.

"It reads," he said wonderingly. "Backwards letters, wrong spaces, and somehow it reads."

"That's the whole secret of the trade," Ulyana said. "Print is very forgiving of everything except indifference. Ink forgives the clumsy hand. It never forgives the careless one."

* * *

They argued about the title for an hour, and the arguing was the best part, though she'd have bitten her tongue before admitting it.

Anton wanted something fierce. *The Truth About Kolvik.* Or *What Halvard Did.* Short, blunt, a hammer.

"A hammer gets confiscated," Ulyana said. "A hammer gives them a name to ban. You print *What Halvard Did* and by morning every gendarme in the district knows exactly which pamphlet to burn, because you've helpfully labeled it for them."

"Then what? Something meek?"

"Not meek. *Slippery.*" She paced along the stone, hands in her apron pockets. "Think like a fishmonger, not a martyr. What survives censorship? Things that look like something else. Shipping manifests get through roadblocks. Choir programs get through. Bills of lading, prayer sheets, school primers. Nobody stops paper that looks official or boring."

"So we disguise testimony as paperwork." His mouth twisted. "There's something fitting in that. They hid the crimes in paperwork. We unhide them the same way."

"Exactly." She stopped pacing. "Which means the format matters more than the title. Eight pages, sextodecimo, tight margins, set small — bourgeois, dull, dense. A cover that promises less than it delivers. Something like—" she cast around the shop, and her eye fell on the stack of shipping orders for the herring fleet, "—*Supplementary Report, Baltic Trade Commission.* God, even I almost yawned saying it."

"And inside?"

"Inside, my father's paragraphs. All of them. Sergeant Voss first." She looked at Anton steadily. "Every person, named. That was his rule and it becomes ours. If we're printing this, we print it whole. No trimming for safety."

Anton was quiet. Then he said, "You understand that whole makes it longer, and longer makes it slower, and slower makes it more dangerous."

"Yes."

"And you still say whole."

"I'm a compositor. We don't abbreviate other people's words. That's what censors are for." She meant it lightly and it came out otherwise, and neither of them moved to soften it.

He nodded slowly, and something settled between them — she felt it settle, like a forme being locked into its frame, all the loose pieces suddenly held fast. "Whole, then. How many pages of setting is that, honestly?"

"At my pace, four days. At yours—" she glanced at his stick, where his latest line leaned crookedly, "—a fortnight. So we divide it. You take the simpler paragraphs, straight narrative, and I'll handle the tables and the harder passages. Milo sets nothing, suspects nothing, and keeps the legitimate orders flowing, because if the shop stops printing manifests, Inspector Brandt will come sniffing before the week is out."

"Milo," Anton said. "The boy. Does he suspect?"

"Milo suspects everything and announces none of it. It's his one talent besides whistling flat." She began distributing Anton's practice lines back into the case, letter by letter, click and slide, click and slide — the oldest rhythm in her life, older than grief. "Watch. This is rule two. Type returns home. Everything in its place. A shop where type isn't distributed is a shop that deserves to be raided."

"Is that true?"

"No idea. My father said it, so I obey it."

Anton insisted on distributing his own lines, badly, and she stood beside him correcting the worst of it — an *e* into the *i* box, a whole word's worth of confusion — and somewhere in the middle of the alphabet she became aware that they were standing very close, both bent toward the same drawer, both hands in the same compartment, and that the lamplight through the tin funnel made a small warm country around just the two of them, and that neither of them stepped back.

She didn't step back. She didn't move forward either. She finished putting the *h* away and said, "En-quads go in the box beside the ems," in a voice that was almost steady, and Anton said, "Beside the ems," in a voice that was also almost steady, and that was all, and it was somehow a great deal.

* * *

By three in the morning they had planned the first two pages and set a third of one, and Anton's fever had crept back enough that she ordered him upstairs over his objections. At the foot of the stairs he paused.

"The title," he said. "*Supplementary Report.* It's good. Cold and boring and perfect. Where did you learn to think that way? To hide a fire in a ledger?"

Ulyana looked back at her shop — the case, the stone, the press, the little brass measures gleaming faintly in the funnel-light. Everything her father had built and she had maintained and never once believed mattered beyond the harbor's customs deadlines.

"I didn't learn it anywhere," she said. "Apparently I already knew."

Upstairs, Anton's door closed. Above it, her mother's light was still burning — late again, her mother sleeping less and less, moving through the nights like a woman listening for something. Ulyana banked the lamps, checked the bolts on the loading door twice, and was carrying the last cup to the scullery when Milo appeared in the stairwell doorway in his nightshirt, hair sticking up, holding the day's paper stock inventory in his hand.

"You're up," she said.

"You're down," he returned, with the flawless logic of a fifteen-year-old. Then he hesitated, and she saw him wrestling with something, and lost the wrestling. "Miss Eriksen — the stock count. We're short. Half a ream of the good manifest paper, and it's not misprints, I checked twice. I checked three times." His voice dropped. "I didn't put it in the book. Yet. But paper doesn't walk, miss. Somebody takes it. And if I can count it, so can the inspector's clerk, and Mr. Brandt counts everything."

The lamps were banked, the shop dark, the plan already locked in its frame — and somewhere across the city, apparently, the count had already begun.

Ulyana set the cup down very quietly on the stair.

"How short, Milo?" she asked. "Exactly?"
