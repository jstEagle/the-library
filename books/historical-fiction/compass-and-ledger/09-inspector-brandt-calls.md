## 9. Inspector Brandt Calls

He came on a Tuesday, which felt somehow characteristic of him. Mondays were for raids and Fridays for arrests; everyone knew that. A Tuesday caller was a man with leisure, and leisure in an inspector was worse than urgency.

I was standing at the composing stick when the bell above the door gave its small, polite cough. Milo looked up from the wash tank, hands black to the wrist, and I saw the question form on his face before it formed in his mind — *do we have anything to hide today?* — and I watched him answer it himself, carefully, the way one counts change.

"Good morning," said the man in the doorway. "Eriksen's Print Shop?"

"Eriksen's Print Shop," I agreed, and set my composing stick down with the deliberate slowness of a woman who has nothing in her hands worth noticing.

He was not at all what I had expected, which I later understood was his first and best trick. I had imagined censors as heavy men, men who took up doorways and breathed officialdom. Inspector Brandt was narrow, gray-suited, with the mild, apologetic face of a schoolmaster about to collect essays he already knew were copied. He carried a leather portfolio under one arm like a hymnal and held his hat — an ordinary felt hat, unremarkable in every particular — as though it had been issued to him with the position and he had never quite made friends with it.

"Brandt," he said. "State Censorship Office, Publications Division." He produced a card and offered it to me the way one offers a visiting card at a funeral: correct, regretful. "We're making routine calls on the printing houses. Nothing alarming, I assure you."

"Nothing is ever alarming until it is," I said, before I could stop myself, and then smiled to sand the edge off it. "Ulyana Eriksen. The shop is mine, since my father's death."

"Ah." Something moved behind his eyes — not sympathy exactly; filing, perhaps. "My condolences. Late?"

"Two years late. The post office finally admitted what the war had known all along."

"That's often how it goes," he said quietly, and I had the uncomfortable sense that he meant it as more than courtesy.

* * *

The inspection itself was theater, and we both knew our parts. He asked to see the press — Papa's old Heidelberg, squat and faithful in the corner — and admired its age without admiring it. He asked what we printed. Manifests, I told him. Shipping manifests, bills of lading, funeral notices when the season obliged. Funeral notices kept us honest; there was no market in Vessengard for seditious obituaries.

"And the government work?" He consulted the portfolio. Not opening it. Just consulting it, the way a priest touches his book. "You've held the contracts for customs forms since before the war."

"We have. My father bid low and died before he could raise his rates. I've chosen to honor both decisions."

That earned me something almost like a smile. It was a thin thing, his smile, worn at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands.

"You'll forgive the question, Miss Eriksen, but do you find the work —" he paused, selecting his word from a tray of words as carefully as any compositor selects type — "interesting?"

"Interesting?"

"Manifests. Forms. The same page, set again and again, year after year."

"I find it restful," I said. "There's comfort in knowing where every letter belongs."

"Mm," said Brandt, and wrote nothing down, which frightened me more than writing would have.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about that morning: three rooms above us and one flight of stairs away, Anton lay in the little storeroom off my mother's kitchen with a fever that had broken only the night before and bandages I had changed at dawn. The loading door was barred. Milo had moved the galleys — the innocent ones, the manifest galleys — into plain view, and moved everything else into the cellar behind a false wall Papa had built during another government's paranoia, back when paranoia had been fashionable rather than merely prudent. We were, on paper and in appearance, precisely the boring shop I claimed to be.

And yet my heart was going like a press at full run, and I poured the tea with a hand I willed to be steady and which was steady, mostly, if one did not look closely, and Inspector Brandt struck me as a man who looked closely.

"The kettle is army issue," I remarked, filling his cup. "From the Red Cross parcels. Everything useful in this city comes from somewhere else now."

"Everything true in this city, also," he said.

I laughed — genuinely, helplessly — and hated myself for it. He was funny. Nobody had warned me that censors could be funny.

"You have sugar," he observed, stirring. "Real sugar."

"My mother hoards like a squirrel with a university education."

"Then your mother is a wise woman. May I tell you something in confidence, Miss Eriksen?" He did not wait for permission; confidence-givers rarely do. "I have inspected eleven print shops in eight days. Every single owner has told me, unprompted, that they print nothing but manifests and wedding invitations. One of them was lying. Statistically speaking," he added, apologetically, "several of them must have been lying, since no city this size prints only weddings."

"And you can't tell which?"

"Oh, I usually can." He sipped. "Liars over-explain. They volunteer. They laugh at jokes that aren't funny." He glanced up. "Your laugh just now was real. That was almost disqualifying."

"Almost?"

"A truly guilty woman would have let me be wrong about her." He set the cup down. "You're enjoying this conversation. That troubles me professionally, because I'm enjoying it too, and I am paid not to enjoy things."

* * *

Milo chose that moment to come through from the back carrying a forme of type — legitimately, innocently, a manifest forme — and I watched the inspector watch him, and I watched Milo's ears go red, and I thought: *there, that's the tell, that's the whole case, right there in a boy's ears.* But Brandt only nodded at him.

"You'd be the apprentice."

"Milo," said Milo. "Yes, sir."

"How long with the shop?"

"Four years."

"Do you like the work?"

Milo looked at me — a flicker, half a second, asking permission or forgiveness — and then back at Brandt. "It's honest," he said. "Type doesn't lie. You set a word wrong, it stays wrong till someone fixes it."

"Doesn't lie," Brandt repeated softly. "But it can be made to say things its setter never intended. Or things he intended very much indeed and denies afterward." He turned back to me. "You'll forgive the philosophy. It's a slow morning."

"It's Tuesday," I said.

"It is Tuesday," he agreed gravely, and for one absurd moment I felt we shared the joke, and remembered just in time that sharing jokes with inspectors is how shops get closed.

* * *

He rose, eventually. He examined the job board by the door — funeral notice, choir concert, a poster for the fishmongers' benevolent society — and read each one fully, as though they might contain cipher, and perhaps in his profession they might. He paused at the small card announcing that the shop accepted guild scrip at par, which was technically generous of us and actually a favor to Papa's old guild brothers, and tapped it once with one finger.

"The Guild of St. Dunstan still meets, then."

"When they can find a hall nobody's requisitioned," I said.

"Old Master Holm still keeps her shop on Chandler Street? She set type longer than most men have been alive."

"She does."

"A remarkable woman. Stubborn." He said *stubborn* the way other men said *dangerous*. Then he put on his hat, badly, at the angle of a man who has better things than hats, and collected his portfolio, and thanked me for the tea with genuine warmth, and said the shop seemed well-kept and the type well-dressed, and that he hoped very much never to have cause to call again.

"I hope so too," I said, meaning it from four separate directions at once.

At the door he stopped. Turned. And said, in a tone entirely different from all his previous tones — lighter, almost gentle:

"One small housekeeping matter. A consignment of misprinted customs stock went missing from the government depot last month. Sixty reams, give or take. Good paper. It happens — war surplus walks out of warehouses the way water finds cracks — but paper finds its way to presses, and presses leave marks. Watermarks, weights, that sort of thing. If such paper ever happened to pass through your receiving door by accident—" he smiled his worn coin of a smile— "the Censorship Office takes a charitable view of accidents reported promptly. Charitable views are harder to come by afterward."

"I'll remember that," I said, "if any paper reports itself to me."

"See that you do." He settled the hat. "Good day, Miss Eriksen. My compliments to your mother. Squirrels with university educations are rare."

And he was gone, the bell coughing politely behind him, leaving behind him only the smell of cheap tobacco and the absolute conviction that I had just survived something and would not survive the next time.

* * *

Milo stood in the middle of the floor with the forme still in his arms, looking at the door.

"He knew about the paper," he said.

"He suspects about the paper. There's a difference, and the difference is evidence." I heard my own voice doing something new — flat, quick, arranging facts like type in a stick — and realized I had learned it from watching Papa.

"He knows about Signe Holm. He asked after her by name."

"He asks after everyone by name. It's his method. He throws names like stones into ponds and watches which ripples come back." I took the forme from Milo's arms and set it on the stone. "Ours didn't ripple. Yours went red at the ears, but he'll have filed that under apprentice-nerves, because apprentices go red at anyone in a good suit."

"And yours?" Milo asked. "What did he file yours under?"

I thought about it honestly. "Under 'woman who enjoys conversations she shouldn't.' Which is either my protection or my doom, depending on his mood in spring."

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked — Anton, moving to the window or away from it — and we both looked at the ceiling and said nothing, and in saying nothing said everything.

I sent Milo to the depot with a legitimate order for legitimate paper, and told him to count the reams twice and complain loudly about the price, because a shop that complains publicly about prices is a shop with nothing to hide, or so I had decided in the last ninety seconds. Then I stood alone in the quiet shop among Papa's letters and Papa's press and the ghost-smell of the inspector's tobacco, and I found my hands had gone, without consulting me, to the locked drawer of the sorting case.

Papa's private drawer. The one with the odd sorts in it, the spacers cut to strange widths, the habits of a man who had set type for forty years and left his fingerprints in the spacing the way other men leave them in wax. I had opened that drawer a hundred times since his death and touched nothing in it, the way one doesn't touch a shrine.

Today I lifted out the thin bundle of his letters — the early ones, the ones Anton had brought, which I had read and reread until the folds had gone soft as cloth — and I carried them to the window where the light was honest.

Because here was the thing Brandt's visit had shaken loose in me, rattling around like a loose quoine in the chase: if the inspector could read a shop through its paper, what was I failing to read in my father's? I had read those letters for their news — alive, sorry, working, watch — the way one reads a telegram. I had not read them the way a compositor reads.

Tonight, I decided, I would read them properly. Letter by letter, line by line, the way my father would have wanted anything of his read: slowly, and with respect for the spacing between the words.

Outside, the armistice preparations went on clattering through the street — hammering, bunting, a brass band rehearsing badly — history being built at high volume two doors down. Inside, I sat down at the window with my father's handwriting in my lap, and began, at last, to really look.
