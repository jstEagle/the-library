## 10. Letters to a Ghost

Ulyana waited until the house was asleep before she let herself feel anything.

It was a compositor's habit, though she had never thought of it that way until now. During the day you locked your grief in the job case alongside everything else — capital letters, fractions, the em-quads that filled awkward spaces — and you worked. You set type for shipping manifests and funeral notices and the annual report of the Vessengard Herring Cooperative, and you did not open the case marked *Father* because if you did, all those loose sorts would spill across the floor of your heart and there would be no picking them up again before supper.

But it was nearly midnight now, and Milo had gone home whistling something off-key, and her mother's cough had settled into its usual rhythm upstairs — two light, one deep, then quiet — which meant she was asleep, or pretending well enough that Ulyana could pretend too. The shop below smelled of ink and cold stove iron and the particular dustiness of paper that has been waiting a long time to be asked what it thinks.

Anton was asleep on the cot in the back room, or lying still with his eyes closed, which for a man with a fever and a bullet-grazed side amounted to nearly the same thing. She had changed his bandages herself an hour ago, her hands steady, her stomach somewhere up around her tonsils. He had apologized twice for bleeding on her floor. She had told him the floor had seen worse, which was true; Milo had once dropped an entire tub of scarlet ink the week before Easter and they'd been finding red footprints until Midsummer like a very pious crime scene.

Now she sat at her father's desk — her desk, she corrected herself, three years running — with the satchel's contents spread before her under the shaded lamp.

Seven letters. All in her father's hand.

She had not opened any of them yet. Anton had offered them to her that first night and she had taken only one, read only its opening line — *My Little Compass, if you are reading this then I have been a coward in more ways than one* — and put it down as if it had bitten her, because grief reopened is worse than grief buried, hope is crueler than mourning, and she had needed two days to stop shaking.

Two days, during which she had set type, argued with Milo about hyphenation, cooked soup, changed bandages, and otherwise conducted herself as a professional woman with no ghosts whatsoever.

Tonight the ghost was going to talk.

She picked up the first letter. The paper was soft at the folds from much handling — he had carried these a long way, or through a great deal. His hand was unmistakable, the way a face is unmistakable: the slight forward lean of his capitals, the way his *r*s trailed off at the end like a man leaving a room while still talking. She had spent her childhood watching that hand move. It had taught her to hold a composing stick. It had drawn a compass rose on her slate when she was six and told her, *that's north, Little Compass, and north doesn't care whether you're looking at it.*

She began to read.

---

*My Little Compass,

If you are reading this then I have been a coward in more ways than one, and you are entitled to throw the ink stone at my head, which I know you can do accurately to within a quarter-inch.*

She laughed. It came out of her like a sneeze, sudden and wet, and she pressed her hand over her mouth because of her mother upstairs.

He was right about the quarter-inch. When she was nine she had thrown a wooden quoit at his head — accurately, and on purpose, because he had sold her favorite drawing paper to a customer who wanted it for wrapping fish — and her mother had said a girl who could hit a moving target at four meters had a future in artillery or nothing at all.

*I deserted,* the letter went on, without decoration, without asking pardon. *I will not insult you by explaining it as anything nobler, yet. Read the others first. Then judge me. Or don't. Your mother always said you'd be a better judge than God, and faster about it.*

Ulyana set the letter down.

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the harbor a foghorn spoke once, low and patient, like a cow calling a calf that was late for supper.

She remembered being eleven years old, sitting on the counter swinging her legs while her father stood at the imposing stone, locking up a form for the church calendar, and asking him whether he was ever frightened of anything. He hadn't looked up. He'd said, *Yes. Every day. Fear is just information, Little Compass. It tells you where the edge is.* And she had said, *What do you do with the information?* and he had thought about it seriously, the way he thought about kerning and weather and other people's bad poetry, and said, *Mostly I stand near it and see what happens.*

That was not the man who wrote *I deserted* in a flat hand on cheap paper.

Or perhaps — she turned the letter over, as if the back might contain a different father — perhaps it was exactly that man, standing near the edge, seeing what happened.

* * *

She made herself tea she didn't drink and opened the second letter, dated fourteen months after the first.

This one was longer, and the handwriting had changed — tighter, smaller, as though he were trying to fit more into less space, or as though the world had stopped offering him much of either.

*They tell us we are winning, which is a strange thing to be told while carrying men who are losing. I am printing things here, did you know? Army broadsheets. Orders of the day. Victory counts. I set the type myself and I will tell you a trade secret, Little Compass: whoever controls the case controls the story. A comma in the wrong place is a mistake. A number in the wrong place is a lie, and I have been asked to place several numbers in places I did not like.*

Her hands had gone cold on the teacup. She wrapped both of them around it anyway.

*I want to come home. That is the whole truth and I will write it plainly because I taught you plainness: I want to come home and find you at the case and scold you about your leading and eat your mother's bread and be afraid of ordinary things again, like creditors and damp. But I have seen something I cannot unknow, and if I come home and say nothing, then I have chosen my own comfort over other people's lives, and I did not become a printer so I could choose badly with good handwriting.*

There was more in that letter — questions about Milo (was the boy still eating all the licorice and setting all the wrong sorts?), about the press (had she oiled the gib screws, had she checked the platen for warp, forgive him, old habits), about her mother (kiss her hair for me, tell her nothing yet, tell her nothing ever if I fail) — and each domestic question landed on Ulyana like a small stone dropped from a great height. He had been thinking of them. From inside whatever hell he was describing in careful, workmanlike sentences, he had been wondering about gib screws.

She found she was angry about the gib screws specifically, in a way she couldn't explain. Two years of believing him dead. Two years of her mother going grey thread by thread. Two years of Ulyana herself walking past his chair, his coat, his half-finished crossword in the drawer, learning not to look at them, and all that time he was alive and worrying about platen warp.

*You could have written,* she said out loud to the empty shop, and heard how young she sounded, and hated it, and said it again anyway, louder. "You could have *written.*"

The shop declined to answer. Shops are discreet that way. They've seen every kind of argument and take nobody's side.

* * *

The third letter was dated eight months ago. The handwriting had deteriorated — not shakily but hurriedly, a man writing fast because time was short or light was poor or both.

And here, at last, plainly, was what he had seen.

She read it standing up. She didn't remember standing. Some part of her had decided that this was not reading-one-sits-down-for; this was testimony, and witnesses stand.

He named the village. He named the date. He named the officers — one above all, *Halvard*, underlined once, hard enough to tear slightly. He described what had been done to the people of that village in language stripped of all art, because he knew, she realized, that art would be the first thing attacked. Nobody could accuse a list of embellishment. He had printed orders for years; he knew exactly how official lies sounded, and so he wrote the truth in their grammar instead — numbered, itemized, witnessed — and it was terrible precisely because it sounded like a shipping manifest.

Forty-one souls. He counted them. Of course he counted them; he was a printer's soul down to the marrow, and printers count everything, because a thing uncounted is a thing that can be denied.

Ulyana sat back down on the floor, actually on the floor, her back against the cabinet where they kept the display type, the brass ones that caught lamplight like small coins.

Milo was seven months old in the village records of that year, she thought irrelevantly, and then corrected herself — no, that was a different village, a different year, her mind was doing anything at all except stay in the room with the letter.

When she was twelve, her father had caught her altering a proof — just slightly, just smoothing an awkward sentence in a customer's obituary, making the dead man sound kinder than he'd been — and instead of scolding her he had sat down beside her and said, *Do you know why we don't do that? Even kindly?* And she'd said, because it's lying, and he'd said, *No. Because the record isn't ours. We're not the authors, Ulyana. We're the keepers. Somebody has to keep the door, even when what comes through it is ugly. Especially then.*

She understood, sitting on the cold floor with forty-one names in her lap, that he had spent three years keeping the door.

She understood also that she wanted to hit him with a quoit.

* * *

The fourth and fifth letters she read together, because they belonged together — plans, instructions, the courier network, the coded entries he had been leaving in the Eriksen & Son ledgers for *two years*, under her nose, under everyone's nose, hidden in freight weights and paper stock numbers like a message sewn into a hem.

"You absolute, insufferable, brilliant *man*," she whispered, and it came out half admiration, which annoyed her, because she was trying to stay angry.

Anger was easier. Anger kept a person upright.

But understanding kept creeping in around the edges of it like damp around a window frame, and by the sixth letter she had stopped fighting it.

The sixth letter was short. Dated five weeks ago. The handwriting was his own again — steadier, as if some decision had been made and the hand had agreed to it.

*By the time you read these I will likely be in custody or dead, and either way I will have made choices you have every right to resent. So let me say the things a father ought to have said sooner.*

*I chose the record over coming home. I am not sure it was right. I am sure that someone must make that choice, and I was placed — by accident, by trade, by temperament — to make it. You will be angry that I did not trust you with it sooner. Here is the truth: I could not bear the thought of you becoming me. I wanted you safe and invisible and bored, and I knew, even as I wanted it, that boredom is a luxury bought with somebody else's blood, and that you, of all people, were never going to accept stolen goods.*

*You are my daughter. You were raised among galleys and quoins. You know that a page, once printed, cannot be unsaid. Be careful, Little Compass. And be brave. In that order, if you can manage it, which knowing you, you cannot.*

*Your father,
who is sorry, and would do it again*

Ulyana read the signature three times.

Then she did the thing she had not done at his memorial service, nor at the empty grave with its polite official stone, nor on any of the thousand nights since — she put her face in her arms and cried for her father, properly, loudly, with her whole chest, the way she had cried at nine years old over the drawing paper, except that this time there was no one to throw anything at, and the target of her fury had walked to the far end of the world and left her his letters and his work and his terrible, magnificent stubbornness.

She cried until the lamp guttered, and then she cried a little more out of spite, and then she blew her nose on a misprinted manifest for the Herring Cooperative, which felt appropriate.

* * *

The seventh letter she almost didn't open. Her eyes were swollen slits; the clock over the case said twenty past three; common sense suggested bed.

But the seventh letter was addressed differently. Not *My Little Compass*. Just: *To whoever holds these papers.*

She opened it.

*Whoever you are — and I think I know who you are, because I chose you without meaning to — these pages are now yours. Not mine. Mine ended the day I started collecting them. A record belongs to whoever carries it forward, and a carrier is not a hero, whatever the ballads claim. A carrier is a person with sore feet and a job.*

*Print it. That is all. Don't improve it, don't soften it, don't add your indignation no matter how much it wants adding. Set it plain, set it honest, set it in a size that old eyes can read, because the people who most need to read it are older than me.*

*And when it is done — go home. Live loudly. Take up space. History does not happen elsewhere, whatever I told you to make you feel better about the fog. It happens wherever somebody writes it down.*

*The case is yours now. Mind your leading.
— E.*

Ulyana sat for a long moment in the cooling dark of the shop, holding the last words of a living man, feeling something rearrange itself in her chest with the heavy finality of a form being locked into the chase.

History does not happen elsewhere.

"Liar," she said softly. "You knew that all along. You just hoped I'd never need to."

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked — her mother turning over, or getting water, or simply being a woman who had slept beside that handwriting for thirty years and knew, perhaps in her bones, that it wasn't finished with any of them.

Downstairs, in the back room, Anton coughed once in his sleep.

And Ulyana Eriksen wiped her face, trimmed the wick, and reached for a pencil, because the letters had given her something beyond grief and beyond rage: they had given her a *specification*. Plain type. Honest setting. Large point size for old eyes.

Which meant she would need paper she could not legally buy, ink she could not account for, and hands besides her own — which meant, whether she liked it or not, that tomorrow she would have to start trusting people.

She looked at the locked door of the guild registry across the yard, dark against the grey beginning of the sky, and thought of Signe Holm, who owed her father a debt and hated owing anything to anyone.

"Well," she said to the empty shop, in her father's own tone of standing near the edge, "let's see what happens."
