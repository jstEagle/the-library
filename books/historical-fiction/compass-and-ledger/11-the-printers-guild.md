## 11. The Printers' Guild

Anton had learned in the army that a man's courage could be measured by how slowly he walked toward something he dreaded. The bravest soldiers he'd known moved deliberately, like men wading into cold water, giving themselves time to become someone equal to the task. So he walked slowly now, down Sildergade, past the herring barrels and the shuttered tobacconist, giving himself the full length of the street to become a man who could ask for help.

The guild hall of the Vessengard Printers' and Typographers' Brotherhood stood at the corner of Møntergade like a fat grey ledger dropped between two crooked houses. Its windows were tall and grimy. Above the door, carved in stone that had gone soft with a century of salt air, read: *The Word Endures.*

Anton stood across the street for a long moment, his bandaged shoulder aching under his borrowed coat, and considered the irony of that motto until it nearly made him laugh.

He had shaved that morning. He had borrowed Milo's second-best collar, which was slightly too small and gave him the feeling of being perpetually about to be strangled, which he supposed was appropriate. In his inside pocket lay a letter — one of Eriksen Eriksen's letters, folded twice, addressed not to Ulyana but to a name Anton had memorized: *Master Anders Holm, Guild Elder.*

Ulyana had pressed it into his hands at the loading door that morning with an expression he was beginning to know well — the set jaw of a woman handing over something she'd rather keep.

"My father trusted him," she'd said. "Or trusted him once. There are letters from 1911 thanking Holm for loans when the shop nearly went under. Men remember who helped them stand up."

"And if he remembers differently?"

"Then you run," she said simply. "You're better at running than I am."

He wasn't sure that was true anymore. He suspected that if she decided to run somewhere, she would arrive before anyone thought to stop her.

* * *

The guild hall's interior smelled of glue, tobacco, and the particular staleness of rooms where committees meet. A hall opened off the entrance, lined with the portraits of dead masters — men with magnificent beards holding specimens of their finest work, as though type were a child they were presenting for blessing.

A boy sweeping the floor directed him upstairs, where the guild secretary kept an office behind a door marked with a brass plate worn nearly illegible.

The secretary, a narrow man named Poulsen with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like a customs official, looked up from his desk and took Anton in with a single professional sweep: the coat, the collar, the pallor, the way Anton held his right arm close to his body.

"You're not a member," Poulsen said. It was not a question.

"I'm asking on behalf of a member. Eriksen Eriksen, of the shop on Krudtgade."

Something moved in Poulsen's face — a flicker, quickly smoothed. "Eriksen Eriksen is deceased."

"Eriksen Eriksen is mislabeled," Anton said.

Poulsen set down his pen with great care, as if it might go off. "Young man. Do you know what week this is? The armistice delegations arrive within the month. There are censors walking these halls twice a fortnight. Every master in this building has a government print contract, and every contract contains a clause the width of my hand about seditious matter." He laced his fingers together. "Whatever you're carrying — and you are carrying something, men who carry nothing don't ask questions in that register — take it elsewhere."

"I haven't shown you anything."

"You haven't needed to." Poulsen rose and opened the door. "I'll tell you what I tell every fool who comes through that door with a cause: paper remembers everything, and so do we, but only one of us can be arrested. Good day."

* * *

He tried six masters in four days.

It went, with minor variations, the same each time. He would present himself — sometimes at a workshop's back stair, sometimes at a master's home in the evening, once at a funeral where he knew the deceased printer's brother would be standing in receipt of condolences, which was either tactical genius or moral bankruptcy depending on how late one slept on it.

Master Kvist of the Akademie Tryk threw him out physically, which Anton permitted because resisting would have reopened his stitches. Kvist had two sons at the front, one buried and one not, and Anton understood, lying in the gutter outside the workshop collecting his hat, that grief had made Kvist's refusal a kind of armor he wore against his own despair. To help Anton was to believe his sons had died for nothing. No man wanted that arithmetic.

Master Lindqvist wept. That was worse. She sat him in her parlor among photographs of grandchildren, listened to everything, held the letter in her small trembling hands for a full minute, and then returned it and said, "I believed once, in 1905. I printed leaflets for the dock strike. They deported the apprentices, you know — boys of seventeen. I swore on my knees afterward that I was done making widows." She kissed his forehead like a grandmother. "Forgive me. Go somewhere with fewer photographs."

Master Ostenfeld said nothing at all, listened, poured tea, and then talked for forty minutes about the weather, the price of zinc plates, and the declining quality of imported rag stock, at the end of which Anton realized he had been dismissed without ever being asked to leave. It was, he had to admit, a masterpiece of the form.

By the fifth day, his shoulder had begun to seep, and Milo — who had appointed himself Anton's unofficial quartermaster without consulting anyone — changed the bandage in the shop's back room while delivering a stream of commentary about the guild that revealed the boy knew every master's drinking habits, debts, and marital difficulties.

"Kvist is done since his boy. Lindqvist won't cross a doorway she's sworn at. Ostenfeld would help if it didn't cost anything, which it does." Milo tied off the bandage with a flourish. "You want Signe Holm."

Anton looked up. "Anders Holm's widow? The letter is addressed to him."

"Died in '16. Everyone knows the widow runs the shop now, though she lets her foreman think he runs it. She pulled proof at the case herself until her hands got bad. And she—" Milo lowered his voice with the gravity of a boy revealing state secrets, "—she keeps the old press. The one the guild used before the new hall. Down in her cellar. My cousin delivered coal there once; he says the stairs have a door in them."

"Why would she still have it?"

Milo shrugged with elaborate innocence. "Maybe she's sentimental. Maybe printers are like cats — they don't like other people's presses but they remember their own." He grinned. "Worth finding out."

* * *

Signe Holm's shop occupied a narrow frontage on Bagerlane, wedged between a chandler's and a house that had been condemned so long it had grown ivy with squatter's rights. The window displayed wedding invitations and funeral cards in equal proportion, which struck Anton as either excellent commerce or profound philosophy.

The woman who answered his knock was seventy if she was a day, small and square as a type case, with white hair pinned in a crown and hands knotted with sixty years of labor. Her eyes went over him the way Poulsen's had — but slower, and kinder, and missing nothing.

"You'll be the courier," she said.

Anton felt the floor tilt very slightly. "Madam—"

"Eriksen's letters came through the fish trade, wrapped in waxcloth, addressed in a hand pretending to be a shipping clerk's. But a clerk doesn't kern his capitals, and he doesn't use the word *ledger* fourteen times in eleven letters unless he wants someone counting." She stepped back from the door. "Well. Are you coming in or do you intend to bleed on my step? The chandler sweeps twice daily and he's territorial."

Inside, the shop smelled of lead and lavender — metal and flowers, the whole of her, Anton thought. She led him past the public workroom, where three compositors bent over cases and a foreman glanced up with proprietary suspicion, and down a corridor to a private sitting room that doubled as her office. Only when the door closed did she turn to face him fully.

"Now," she said. "Tell me what Anders Eriksen found at the front that was worth deserting for."

So he told her.

He told it all — more than he'd told Ulyana, because some of it he had never managed to say aloud to anyone who hadn't been there, and something in Signe Holm's listening made silence impossible. He told her about the village called Zarechnoy, and the orders signed in Halvard's name, and the well. When he reached the well he stopped, and his voice did something he hated, and Signe Holm rose without comment and poured him a glass of aquavit and stood over him while he drank it.

"The general who signs the armistice," she said quietly, when he could speak again. "That's the man."

"That's the man. And the amnesty clauses will bury all of it. I've seen the draft terms. His crimes will be filed under *wartime necessity* and locked away with the rest of the century's embarrassments."

"And Eriksen?"

Anton hesitated.

"Young man," Signe said, settling back into her chair, "I am seventy-three years old. I have buried a husband, a son, and four pressmen's funerals' worth of colleagues. I have watched this city change flags twice and lie about both occasions. Do not attempt to protect me. Where is Eriksen?"

"He's alive. He's in the fortress, under another name, awaiting transfer. If the amnesty passes, there will be no inquiry, no prisoner list, no record. He will disappear administratively. Completely."

Signe was silent for a long moment. Then she stood, walked to the window, and looked out at Bagerlane with its gas lamps coming on, and Anton watched her shoulders move once, deeply, like a woman setting type — composing something in her body before speaking it.

"When I was a girl," she said, not turning around, "my father worked the guild press in the old hall. When the Brotherhood bought the new Wheland machine, the old Albion was going to be sold for scrap. My husband bought it instead, and hid it in our cellar, and for forty years we have kept it oiled and covered, and every year at midsummer I have pulled one sheet on it just to hear it speak. My husband called it foolishness. Then he died, and I called it mourning." She turned around. "Now I find I was keeping it for something."

"Frau Holm—" Anton began, and discovered he had no idea what he meant to say.

"Don't thank me yet. Listen." She counted on her fingers, the habit of a woman who had estimated job costs her whole life. "One: I will not print on my premises. The cellar is yours for composition, proofs, and storage, but the run happens elsewhere, because if they come here they take my shop and my people with the press, and I have three men upstairs whose children eat because of me. Two: I supply expertise, not materials. Paper is your problem, and God help you, because it will be the death of the whole enterprise. Three—" her finger stopped, and her voice changed, became quieter and harder at once, "—if I am taken, you finish it. You don't negotiate, you don't try to free me, you finish the printing. Swear it."

Anton looked at her. Seventy-three years old, offering herself with the same flat efficiency she'd use for a price quotation.

"I swear it," he said.

"Good." She crossed to the sideboard and produced a bottle of aquavit and two glasses, pouring with the steadiness of a much younger woman. "Then we drink to the Albion press, which has waited forty years for honest work." She raised her glass. "*The Word Endures.*"

"*The Word Endures,*" Anton echoed.

They drank. Then Signe set down her glass and fixed him with a look that had probably quelled generations of apprentices.

"One more thing. The girl. Eriksen's daughter."

"Ulyana."

"She's setting the pamphlet?"

"She's doing everything. Composition, planning, holding the household together, arguing with me about distribution routes. She hasn't slept properly in a week."

Signe nodded slowly, and something old and warm moved through her expression. "Her father used to write to me about her. 'My Ulyana sets a cleaner line than any journeyman in Vessengard,' he wrote, 'and she thinks history belongs to other people. One day she'll learn different, and God help whoever's standing nearest.'" She smiled, and for a moment looked twenty years younger and considerably more dangerous. "I suspect, young man, that whoever is standing nearest is you. So I'll give you the same advice I gave every apprentice on their first day: mind your measure, keep your margins even, and don't put your fingers anywhere you wouldn't put them if the press were running."

Anton felt himself going red at fifty-three years old, wounded shoulder notwithstanding.

"I'll bear it in mind," he managed.

"See that you do." She saw him to the corridor door, then paused, her hand on the frame. "Anton." It was the first time she'd used his name. "Five masters refused you this week. Don't grieve them too hard. Fear isn't cowardice — most of those people have more to lose than you do, and less practice at losing it. We ration our hope in this trade. A little each day, spent carefully, or it spoils." She patted his arm, gently, near but not on the wound. "Come Thursday. Bring the copy. We'll see what the old girl can still do."

He stepped out into the lamplit street, and the cold air hit his face like clean water. Behind him, through the shop window, the funeral cards and wedding invitations glowed softly in the gaslight, births and deaths arranged in tidy rows, and Anton found he was grinning like a fool — one press, one old woman, one cellar door, and somewhere across the city a girl who thought history belonged to other people.

It wasn't much. Rationed, as she said. Enough for today.

He was halfway down Bagerlane before he noticed the man in the cap who had been standing at the chandler's corner when he arrived — and who was still there now, an hour later, not buying candles.
