## 12. Paper Is Rationed

Milo Brandvold had been an apprentice compositor for eleven months, and in that time he had learned three great truths of the trade.

First: ink got everywhere. Behind the ears, under the fingernails, in places a man did not care to name in polite company. Second: Ulyana Eriksen could tell the weight of a sheet of paper by holding it to her ear, like a doctor listening to a chest, and she was never wrong, and it was unnerving every single time.

And third — learned just this morning, standing at the case while she paced the length of the shop with her arms folded — there was no such thing as a small crime when the crime was right.

"The problem," Ulyana said, for the fourth time, "is paper."

Milo set an em quad and did not point out that he'd heard her. She was talking mostly to herself anyway. He'd noticed that about her these past weeks, since the stranger Anton had come through the loading door feverish and secretive and had somehow become a fixture in the back room like a second press that occasionally coughed. Ulyana thought aloud. She walked figures in the air. She muttered margins.

"Three thousand pamphlets, eight pages each, decent stock so it doesn't fall apart in the rain or look like a laundry list. That's—" she did the arithmetic aloud, quick and bitter, "—nearly four reams of good paper. And every ream in this city is logged."

"Logged," Milo repeated, because it seemed wise to repeat things. It made him sound involved.

"Every ream sold to every printer comes through the Paper Commission since the rationing order. We buy what we're allotted, we account for what we print on it, and if I put in for four extra reams of fine stock a month before the armistice, someone at the Commission writes it down, and someone else asks *what is Eriksen's printing, exactly*, and then Inspector Brandt comes back with his tea and his questions and his terrible little smile." She stopped pacing. "So we can't buy it."

"We could steal it," said Milo, joking, the way you joke.

Ulyana turned around slowly.

"Oh," said Milo.

* * *

Here is what he had come to understand about the Eriksen shop, in his eleven months and especially in these last strange weeks: it looked from the street like nothing. A narrow frontage between a tobacconist and a shuttered milliner's, windows slightly fogged, a sign with faded letters that half the harbor couldn't read. Inside, it smelled of oil and lead and the particular dusty sweetness of old paper, and it hummed, faintly, all day, like a hive thinking.

It was the sort of place where a boy who had no father and a mother too tired to argue with him could be given a composing stick and told, *you'll ruin it at first, everyone does, keep your thumbs clean anyway.* The sort of place where Mrs. Eriksen came down on good days to dust the frames of the specimen books and say nothing at all, which was its own kind of blessing. The sort of place where a feverish stranger with a bandaged shoulder could sleep in the back room among rolls of packing felt, and Milo could bring him soup without being told why, and not asking had begun to feel less like ignorance and more like trust.

His cousin Lars worked at the government depot on Quay Street. That was the whole of the plan, really, before it became a plan. Everything else was details, and Milo supplied most of them himself, which surprised him more than anyone.

* * *

The misprints were the beautiful part. Even Ulyana said so, though she said it grimly, the way she said most true things.

The Harbor Office ordered its shipping manifests printed quarterly, and quarterly the shop pulled proofs, found errors — a tonnage column transposed, a date off by one, once the entire fleet of herring boats listed under *coal* — and pulped the bad run. Standard practice. The spoiled sheets went into the waste bin, the waste bin went to the rag collector, and nobody counted a single sheet because spoiled paper wasn't paper at all, legally speaking. It was trash. Trash was unlogged. Trash was free.

"They've already paid for it," Ulyana said, laying it out at the little table in the back room, where Anton sat propped against the felt rolls with his arm still bound and his eyes following everything like a man doing sums. "The Harbor Office pays for the full run whether we spoil two pages or two hundred. If we spoil rather more than usual this quarter — if our press develops a sudden, tragic inaccuracy—"

"You mean lie to the Harbor Office," said Mrs. Eriksen from the doorway. She came down rarely now; her good days had grown shorter, like autumn light. But she was here tonight, wrapped in her shawl, and she had heard everything, apparently, because she always did.

"I mean," said Ulyana, carefully, "print extra spoiled copies of a document nobody reads."

Her mother considered this for a long moment. Then: "Your father once printed a wedding announcement with the wrong bride entirely. He kept the misprints in a drawer for twenty years. He called them his most honest work." She adjusted her shawl. "I'll want my supper at the usual time regardless."

Which, in the currency of the Eriksen household, amounted to a blessing with interest.

Anton laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter. "You see?" he said to Milo. "You see where she gets it?"

Milo did not see, exactly, but he nodded anyway, because nodding was also part of his method.

* * *

There remained one difficulty, and it wore Milo's own surname.

Spoiled manifests went to the rag collector on Fridays. The rag collector was a wheezing old man named Poul who took the bales around the corner and weighed them and paid by the kilo, and between the shop's door and Poul's cart lay perhaps thirty meters of open cobbles, plainly visible from the street, plainly visible from the tobacco shop where, some mornings, a policeman took his coffee.

Four reams of fine stock does not hide in a coat. Milo knew this because he had tried, privately, in the cellar, walking past the mirror his employer used for checking galley alignment, and he had looked like a boy who had stolen a church collection plate and stuffed it down his trousers.

"So we don't carry it out Friday," Milo said, at the planning session — and it was a planning session now, with a pencil and everything, Ulyana drawing the street in strokes on the back of a spoiled manifest. "We carry it out Tuesday. Early. Before anyone's awake but us."

"And then where does it go?" Ulyana asked.

Milo took a breath. This was the part he'd been keeping in his pocket like a coin.

"Lars," he said. "My cousin. He works nights at the Commission depot on Quay Street — hauling bales, sweeping, whatever they give boys. There's a loading alcove behind the depot where they stack the incoming stock, and there's a gap behind the stacks where Lars says he hides when the night foreman makes his rounds." He turned the pencil over. "Lars owes me. And Lars doesn't read anything he hauls. He can't, mostly. We move it a bundle at a time, in with the scrap rags, and I collect it from the depot's rag output, which comes to Poul same as ours, only nobody weighs what's inside a rag bale, do they? Nobody in this whole city has ever once cared what's inside a rag bale."

Silence at the table.

"That," said Anton at last, slowly, "is genuinely clever."

"I'm not just thumbs," said Milo, with dignity.

"No," said Ulyana, and she was looking at him in a way she hadn't quite before — not as the apprentice, not as the boy, but as a colleague, one compositor to another across a conspiracy. "You are not just thumbs."

He would remember that exact look for the rest of his life, though at the time he only felt his ears go hot and pretended to be very interested in the pencil.

* * *

Tuesday came gray and cold, the harbor breathing fog up the streets, gulls arguing over something in the fish market gutter.

The scheme ran thus: Monday's manifest run would be printed with a deliberate fault — a transposed column, obvious enough to justify pulping, subtle enough that the Harbor Office clerk checking the proof wouldn't blink. They'd pull the "spoiled" copies on the good stock, set aside the required number to actually pulp, and hold the rest in the coffin.

Yes, the coffin. There was a genuine funeral job on the books — old Herr Vaske of the chandler's family, God rest him, a big man requiring a substantial box — and the empty coffin crate stood in the back room awaiting delivery. Milo had suggested it with the gallows humor that had lately become his signature, and Ulyana had gone pale, then laughed until she had to sit down, then agreed, and now every time Milo passed the thing he patted its lid respectfully, like apologizing to Herr Vaske in advance.

But the coffin stayed in the shop. The paper had to travel light and unseen. Which meant bundles sewn into the lining of the rag sack. Which meant Milo.

He rose at ten past five. He had never in his life risen at ten past five voluntarily and discovered, briefly, a whole secret country: streets wet and empty, lamplighters finishing their rounds, a milk cart, a cat with opinions. He carried two rag sacks, one honest and one not, and his heart went like a rabbit's.

Quay Street was longer in the dark. That was a fact; he was sure of it; distances stretched when you were committing your first crime.

The depot's rear alcove was exactly as Lars had described — a brick recess behind the loading dock, stacked with incoming bales, smelling of hemp and damp, dark as the inside of a hat. Milo stood in it with his back against cold brick and listened to his own blood.

Then a whisper from behind the stacks, sibilant and terrified: "*Is that you or the foreman, if it's the foreman I've died—*"

"It's me, you great eel."

Lars emerged looking like a boy who had slept in his clothes, which, Milo reflected, he probably had. Sixteen, gangly, a cowlick like a flag of surrender, and eyes that darted to the alley mouth every four seconds. He'd been excited when Milo had first laid it out — *a real scheme, like in the penny papers* — but excitement, Milo saw now, had curdled somewhere around hour two of waiting in the dark.

"This is theft," Lars whispered. "This is actual theft. Of government stock. You didn't say government stock, you said *paper*."

"Rationed paper is government paper, yes."

"They hang people for—"

"They do not hang people for paper, Lars. They fine them. Possibly. If anyone notices, which nobody will, because—" Milo leaned in, deploying the phrase he'd rehearsed in the mirror, "—nobody in this whole city has ever once cared what's inside a rag bale."

Lars stared at him. Somewhere above them a window banged open and both boys nearly ascended bodily to heaven; but it was only a woman shaking out a rug, muttering at the weather, gone again.

"Look," Milo said, quieter. In the dark he could just make out his cousin's face, young and scared and wanting to be brave. Milo remembered being sixteen. It had been last year, practically. "You know the Eriksen shop. You've had your boots resoled twice on Mr. Eriksen's account, and he never charged your mother full price, and when the flood took your street he let half the neighborhood dry their things in the back room. You know it's true."

"...Everyone knows that."

"And now there are men who burned villages, proper villages, Lars, and they're going to sign the peace and get medals instead of rope, and the only thing standing between them and the medals is a pamphlet, and the only thing standing between the pamphlet and the printer is paper. That's all this is. Paper." He held out the false-bottomed sack. "You're not stealing from the government. You're stealing *for* everyone the government's decided not to count."

That was Ulyana's phrasing, more or less. Milo had been carrying it around for days like a stone in his shoe, and it felt enormous relief to finally set it down somewhere.

Lars chewed his lip. Down the quay a bell buoy clanked. Then, with the sigh of a boy accepting his destiny, he took the sack.

"Bundle goes behind the third stack," he muttered. "Rag bale comes out Thursday, mixed in with the depot's scrap, Poul collects it same as yours. If the foreman comes, I'm checking stacks, and if he finds you, I never saw you in my life, and I mean it, I'll cry, it's very convincing."

"Lars."

"What?"

"Thank you."

His cousin paused in the dark. And then, gruffly, in the voice of their shared grandmother, the voice that meant *family*: "Bring the rest next Tuesday. And Milo — if they hang us, I want it noted I went first into the alcove."

"Noted," said Milo solemnly, "in the ledger."

* * *

Thursday afternoon, Milo walked to Poul's cart with a hand truck and a legitimate pickup — the shop's ordinary weekly rags — and loaded aboard, among the honest bales, one bale heavier than its fellows by exactly four reams of fine cream-colored stock, sewn snug in rag cloth, invisible to God and the weighmaster alike.

Poul squinted at it, wheezed, hefted one corner, pronounced it "bloody heavy for rags, someone's been washing blankets," and paid Milo nine öre extra for the weight.

Nine öre. Milo walked home with the coins in his fist and started, somewhere around the tobacconist's corner, to laugh — helpless, terrified, joyful laughter that he had to strangle into coughing before the coffee-drinking policeman looked over. Nine öre. He had committed crimes against the state and been *paid*.

* * *

That evening the paper lay on the back-room table, four reams of it, unwrapped and gleaming faintly in the lamplight like something rescued from a wreck. Ulyana lifted a single sheet to her ear, the way she did, and listened.

"Good stock," she said softly. "Heavy enough to survive a pocket. Light enough to run fast." She set it down and looked around the table — Anton with his arm in its sling, her mother's chair drawn up though she'd gone up to bed an hour ago and left the chair occupied only by her shawl, which somehow still seemed to count — and lastly at Milo.

"Milo," she said. "Tell me honestly. How much trouble is your cousin in if this goes wrong?"

"None," said Milo, "as long as nobody talks."

"Nobody talks," Anton agreed quietly.

"And how much trouble are *you* in?"

Milo considered the question properly, because she'd asked him like a grown-up and deserved the answer like one. Trouble meant the inspector with the tea. Trouble meant his mother's face. Trouble meant, possibly, a cell with poor drainage. He weighed it all in the scales of his eleven months of thumbs and quads and clean hands.

"Enough," he said, "to be worth it."

Ulyana nodded once, satisfied, and began dividing the reams into press-ready lots with quick, sure movements. "Then tomorrow we spoil some manifests," she said. "A great many manifests. The Harbor Office will weep."

They bent over the paper together, three heads and a shawl in the lamplight, conspirators all.

Which was precisely the moment, Milo would think later, that they should have heard the footsteps — the slow, deliberate ones stopping outside the loading door, followed by three soft knocks, and a voice through the wood that made Ulyana freeze with a sheet half-lifted:

"*Frøken Eriksen. It's Holm. Signe Holm. I believe you and I have a press to discuss.*"
