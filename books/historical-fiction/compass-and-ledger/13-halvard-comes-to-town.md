## 13. Halvard Comes to Town

The posters went up on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday they had multiplied like a particularly confident fungus.

Ulyana noticed the first one pasted to the wall of the fishmonger's on Saltmarket Row, at exactly the height where a man waiting in a queue would have nothing else to look at. It was a portrait in three colors — an extravagance of ink these days — showing a jaw like a ship's prow, a mustache groomed to the millimeter, and eyes gazing nobly off into a middle distance that presumably contained the future of the nation.

*GENERAL THE HONOURABLE EIRIK HALVARD. SAVIOR OF THE EASTERN FRONT. WELCOMED HOME BY A GRATEFUL VESSENGARD.*

"Well," said Milo, who had stopped beside her with a paper-wrapped bun halfway to his mouth, "he's got good bone structure. You could print him off that plate and never fix a thing."

"That's not what plates are for."

"I'm saying it's efficient."

She looked up at the face again. Somewhere behind those noble eyes, if Anton was right, were villages with names she was only now learning, and lists of men who had been entered into ledgers as *expended*, as though they were paper stock.

"Efficient," she said quietly. "Yes. That's rather the problem, isn't it."

Milo glanced at her sideways, chewing. He had gotten better lately at knowing when her silences were moods and when they were weather fronts. This one, he seemed to judge, was weather. He finished the bun, dusted his hands, and said, "Mrs. Voss wants four hundred wedding invitations by Friday, which is a laugh, because nobody in this city can afford a wedding unless it's to a sack of flour. Shall I start?"

"Start," said Ulyana, and turned away from the general's beautiful jaw.

But she felt it watching her back all the way down the street. She would feel it, in various forms, for the next six days.

* * *

By Thursday there was no escaping him. Halvard's face hung in the railway station, over the customs house, in the window of the confectioner's — who had, with a commercial instinct that deserved better times, begun selling chocolate portraits on sticks. Posters papered the pillars of the harbor arcade so thickly that the harbor itself had nearly disappeared behind them: the gray water, the cranes, the trawlers riding at anchor with their nets frozen into stiff black lace.

Even the shop got its share. A city clerk came in on Friday morning with a print order so large it required two men to carry it: ten thousand handbills for the welcoming ceremony, five hundred programs, and a banner run for the Guildhall facade. The job was legal, ordinary, paid on delivery. It was also, Ulyana thought as she read through the order form, the most obscene commission the Eriksen press had taken since someone had wanted commemorative plates for the Governor's birthday.

"We could decline," Milo said, not looking up from the type case.

"On what grounds? We're a business. Businesses don't have opinions; they have invoices."

"You could say the press is broken."

"It isn't broken."

"You could say *you're* broken."

"That's very touching, Milo, but no." She squared the order against the counter edge. "We take the money. Money buys paper. Paper buys—" She stopped herself before the sentence could go somewhere dangerous even indoors, and Milo, carefully, looked at nothing at all.

Her mother came down mid-morning wrapped in two shawls, moving slowly along the wall the way she did now on her bad days, one hand trailing the shelves like a woman navigating a ship in fog. She looked at the stack of order forms, then at Ulyana's face.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A government job. Ten thousand copies of a man I'd rather not meet."

Her mother picked up the topmost proof — Halvard's portrait, reproduced in halftone, staring out from the galley with that serene, distant confidence. She held it at arm's length for a long moment.

"He looks like a man who has never once been cold," she said at last, and gave the sheet back as though it might stain. "Well. Print your ten thousand. Just be sure you wash your hands after."

It was the closest thing to a political statement her mother had made since the war began, and it startled them both. She retreated upstairs with unusual firmness of step, and Milo whispered, "I like her angry. It's like spring coming early."

* * *

Setting ten thousand handbills was its own particular torture. The text was short — *VESSENGARD WELCOMES ITS HERO, SATURDAY NOON, CATHEDRAL SQUARE* — but short text means large type, and large type means every flaw shows, and the clerk from the Ministry had specified a face from their case that hadn't seen regular use since before the war, so that Ulyana spent Saturday morning fishing cracked sorts out of the job case and substituting letters from other sizes, which any proper compositor would notice and which she suspected was precisely why the ministry clerks always chose that face. Petty sabotage by typography. It satisfied something small and mean in her, and she was grateful for it.

They worked through the day with the shutters half-closed. Word of the job traveled, as word does among tradesmen; the binder across the alley sent over his boy with a loaf and a raised eyebrow. Old Krail from the chandlery came in ostensibly to settle his account and stayed twenty minutes talking about everything except the enormous stack of patriotic handbills drying on every flat surface in the shop, which was itself the loudest possible commentary.

"They've closed Cathedral Square," he said, examining his hat brim. "Carpenters have been at the dais since dawn. And the tram lines are rerouted. For a man who's supposed to be loved, they're taking a lot of precautions."

"People love him," Milo offered.

"Certain kind of love," said Krail, putting on his hat. "Same love you give a dog that bites the neighbors. You're proud he's yours." He touched his cap to Ulyana and left.

Anton heard none of this directly — he was still confined to the back room, his shoulder healing badly and slowly, and the shop's front rooms now received more official foot traffic than usual. But he heard the rhythm of it through the floorboards: the press running longer hours, Ulyana's footsteps heavier at night. When she brought his supper down that evening, he was sitting propped against the paper cabinets with her father's third letter open on his knee, and he looked at her with an expression she was learning to dread, because it usually preceded honesty.

"You printed his face today," he said. Not accusing. Worse — factual.

"Ten thousand of them. Twelve if you count the smudged ones we're supposed to destroy." She set the tray down. "The state pays cash."

"I know. I know what it costs you—"

"Do you?" It came out sharper than she'd intended, and she saw him flinch, and she hated that she'd put it there. She sat down on the crate opposite him. "No. I'm sorry. Yes, it costs me. But here's the arithmetic, Anton, and you'll appreciate it, you strike me as a man who does sums under fire: every krone they pay us for handbills is a krone that buys paper we don't have to steal. Every hour we spend being boringly, obediently profitable is an hour nobody wonders what the Eriksen shop is printing after dark. Their money funds their own undoing. There's a word for that in the trade. We call it a subsidy."

He looked at her for a moment, and then — she saw it happen — the grimness broke into something younger, something surprised.

"You'd have made a fine quartermaster," he said.

"I'd have made a magnificent criminal. I simply came to it late." She stood, brushed off her skirt. "Saturday. Noon. Are you well enough to hate a parade from a distance, or shall I hate it for both of us?"

"I'll hate my share," he said. "Go on. Save some contempt for me."

* * *

Saturday came up bright and hard, a wind off the harbor scrubbing the sky to a clean ceremonial blue, as though the weather too had been issued orders. Ulyana left Milo in charge — "If anyone official comes, I've gone to church; pick a church" — and walked into the city with her collar up and her heart going oddly, the way it did before a big pull on the press.

She had expected crowds. She had not expected *this*. Cathedral Square held perhaps forty thousand people on a market day; it held sixty thousand now, packed shoulder to shoulder beneath bunting in the national colors, and the colors themselves looked different — newer, brighter, mass-produced. Someone, somewhere, had run enormous print jobs for this day. She found herself estimating quantities without meaning to. Fifty thousand flags. Twenty thousand rosettes. Thirty thousand programs, probably from Lindqvist & Sons, whose press was bigger than hers and whose compositors were worse, she'd bet the shop on it.

The military band struck up at eleven, and the crowd's murmur changed key — anticipation, that low animal hum. Ulyana worked her way toward the edge of the square, up onto the steps of the corn exchange, where she could see over heads. From there the whole scene arranged itself into composition: the dais draped in velvet, the dignitaries ranked like cutlery, the cathedral's gray towers behind, and the wide avenue down which the general would come.

And come he did.

First the escort, horses stepping high, harness gleaming. Then open carriages of officials, each receiving applause of diminishing size. Then a pause, a hush with a texture to it, and then General Eirik Halvard, standing alone in an open landau, in a greatcoat that managed simultaneously to suggest hardship and cost four hundred kroner, one gloved hand resting on the carriage door as lightly as if the entire twentieth century weighed nothing.

The sound that rose from the square was enormous. It was cheering, mostly. It was also, Ulyana thought — listening the way one listens to a chord for the dissonance underneath — partly something else. Sixty thousand people do not all feel the same thing, but they can make the same noise, and that is the trick, she understood suddenly, standing there with her hands gripping the stone balustrade. That is the whole trick. The parade doesn't need everyone's belief. It only needs everyone's voice. A crowd is a press that prints whatever it's given, and Halvard had given them ten thousand copies of himself, and they were running the edition right now, free of charge, at full speed.

He was handsome. That was the sickening part. He was genuinely, photographically handsome, and he had a way of inclining his head to each side of the street that made ten thousand strangers feel individually saluted. Beside her on the steps, a woman in widow's black was crying — actually crying — and whispering to her neighbor that he had been at Kolvary, that he had slept in the mud with the men, that he remembered names.

"He remembers names," the woman said again.

Ulyana thought of a ledger entry in her father's careful hand — *testimony of L., private, 3rd company, re: the road east of Kolvary* — and had to close her eyes for a moment against the vertigo of it, two truths occupying the same square, the same minute, unable to touch.

The landau passed below her. For perhaps three seconds she had a clear line to the man himself, close enough to see the grain of his skin, the exact grooming of the mustache, the faint smile distributed evenly across the crowd like butter across bread. Close enough that if fate were crueler or funnier, he might have looked up and met the eyes of the girl who was going to end him.

He didn't look up. He looked at the cathedral, at the flags, at the middle distance containing the future. And the band crashed into the national anthem, and sixty thousand voices took it up, and Ulyana Eriksen stood on the corn exchange steps with her mouth shut, hands flat against cold stone, feeling the anthem move through the masonry into her palms like the vibration of a press bed — power as impression, history being set in type, line by line, whether anyone consented to proof it or not.

*I am not invisible,* she thought. *You think this square is full of witnesses, and it is. I'm one of them. Remember my face, General. No — don't. Better if you don't.*

She slipped away before the speeches. Behind her the roar swelled as Halvard rose to speak, and the words followed her down the side streets in fragments — *sacrifice… victory… peace with honor…* — bouncing off walls plastered with his own face, so that he seemed for a moment to be speaking from everywhere at once, from every wall in Vessengard.

* * *

Milo had locked the shop front and gone round to Signe Holm's with a message about "galley storage," which was the code they'd agreed on, which meant *come, something's happening.* Ulyana found them both in the bindery yard behind the Guildhall, where Signe kept her trap, and where the sound of the ceremony arrived muffled and intermittent, like weather from another country.

Signe was in her seventies and built like a binding press, with forearms that had pulled proofs since before Ulyana's father was born. She listened to Ulyana's report of the square — the crowds, the widow weeping, the head inclined just so — without expression.

"He came to the Guild dinner in '16," she said at last, tightening a strap on the trap. "Before things went badly for him to go well. Shook every hand in the room. Told me printing was 'the conscience of the nation.' Charming man. Genuinely charming." She snapped the buckle closed. "That's what nobody tells you about them, girl. They aren't monsters in person. Monsters in person you could fight. He's a man who believes his own poster, and has forty thousand people a day to confirm it."

In the muffled distance the crowd roared again. Milo shifted from foot to foot.

"Paper stock's getting low," he said. "Even with the handbill money. If we're doing the real run at Signe's place, we need to know how much, and where it's coming from, and — Uly. The manifest misprints from the customs office. Those crates in the cellar. That's good stock, just trimmed wrong. Nobody's counted it since autumn."

"Nobody's counted it," Ulyana repeated slowly, "because nobody expects anyone to want it. It's waste paper. It's going to the pulpers in spring anyway."

"So it's not stealing," Milo said, with the scrupulousness of a boy rehearsing a defense. "It's *rescuing*."

Signe snorted. Ulyana looked at the two of them — the old master and the apprentice, conspirators already — and above them the Guildhall flag snapping in the wind, and beyond that, faintly, the anthem starting its second verse.

"Tuesday," she said. "Customs office sends their misprints for collection the first Tuesday of the month. Milo — you'll be driving the cart. Congratulations on your promotion."

Milo's face did something complicated between terror and delight. "Tuesday," he said. "Right. Tuesday." And then, because he was Milo: "Do I get to wear a cap? Criminals should wear caps."

Above them the flag snapped; below the ground, somewhere under the city, the presses of Vessengard went on printing the world as ordered. On Tuesday, Ulyana thought, a small part of it would begin printing the world as it was.

"Come inside," said Signe Holm, unlocking the yard door. "Both of you. Before you plan felonies in my yard, you'd best see what I keep in my cellar."
