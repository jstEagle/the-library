## 14. Signe's Cellar Press

Signe Holm's cellar smelled of onions, machine oil, and forty years of secrets, and Ulyana decided within the first minute that she liked all three.

"Mind the beam," Signe said, ducking without looking, the way a person does who has struck her head on the same beam four hundred times and has finally come to an arrangement with it. "The press is down here. Mind her too, while you're at it. She bites."

The press was a Heidelberg platen, old enough to have opinions, squatting in the corner of a low brick room beneath Signe's stationery shop on Kirkegade. Somebody had hung sacking over the walls in hopeless strips, as if that would muffle anything, and somebody else — Signe herself, judging by the neatness — had built a false wall of storage crates along one side, behind which the machine could be hidden from anyone who came down for pickled herring.

"She was my husband's," Signe said, running a hand along the frame the way another woman might stroke a cat. "He printed union bulletins in the nineties when it was merely unpopular. Then he printed other things when it became dangerous. Then he died, and I kept both habits."

"You print things now?" Milo asked, eyes going wide. He had brought his tool roll the way a boy brings a dog everywhere — slung over his shoulder, thumping against his hip.

"I print wedding invitations," Signe said gravely. "Beautiful ones. The bishop's daughter ordered two hundred last spring. There is no finer cover than the bishop's daughter." She smiled, and Ulyana saw for the first time how much iron there was under the flour-sack softness of her. "But she has been idle since March, and a press left idle sulks. So. Show me what you have."

Ulyana set the forme on the stone and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that wanted to shake. Her father's words, locked into type — not the pamphlet yet, only the first page, the testimony of a corporal named Lindqvist about what happened at the Varnow road in November of 1916. She had read it so many times she could recite it, and still, seeing it in metal, it became something new. Type made words heavy. Heavy things could be dropped on people.

Signe put on her spectacles, bent close, and read. The cellar was quiet except for the tick of the gas lamp and Milo shifting his weight from foot to foot like a kettle deciding.

"Well," Signe said at last. She straightened, took off the spectacles, polished them on her apron, put them back on, and read it again. "Well," she said a third time, in an entirely different voice. "Your father set this?"

"He taught me. This one I set." Ulyana heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "He'd have pulled the 't' in *testimony* half a point tighter, but it would have looked worse."

Signe laughed — a short bark, surprised out of her. "Arrogance. Good. You'll need it." She rolled up her sleeves. "Anton — you're ink man until you prove you're a menace. Milo, you're on paper. And someone must be lookout, which means the youngest and most expendable—"

"That's me," said Anton mildly, from where he sat on a crate with his bandaged side propped against the wall. His fever had broken two days ago and left him thin and pale and stubbornly cheerful, like a plant that grows back after being stepped on. "I'll confess I can't lift much anyway. The army took my shoulder and never gave it back."

"You were army?" Milo asked, then went red. "Sorry. Stupid question."

"It's not stupid." Anton took the ink slab and knife Signe handed him. "It's the whole story, actually. Ask me someday when we're not about to commit crimes."

"Is printing crimes?" Milo asked. "It's just — paper."

"Everything is just something," Signe said, "until it isn't."

* * *

The rehearsal run began badly and improved only slightly from there.

The first pull produced a sheet on which Corporal Lindqvist's testimony of the Varnow road appeared as a grey smear, like the ghost of a word that had died before reaching the page.

"Ink's too thin," Ulyana said, leaning over the plate.

"Ink's fine," Signe said. "Pressure's wrong. She always drops off on the left. She's been doing it since 1911; Henrik swore he'd fix her and then he died, which I consider a coward's way out of the job." She cranked the pressure screws a quarter turn each, muttering what sounded like endearments and threats in the same breath, and they tried again.

Better. Not good — the left margin still faded as though the words were losing their nerve — but legible. Milo held the sheet up to the lamp and read his own small part aloud: "*...they marched us past the ditch twice so the new men would see it and understand.*" His voice slowed halfway through. He put the sheet down carefully, as if it might go off.

"That's the third time I've heard that line," he said. "It gets heavier every time."

"That's how you know it's true," Signe said. "Lies stay the same weight. Truth gains." She pointed at the stack. "Again. Two more pulls before we judge her temper."

The second pull tore. The third stuck to the plate entirely, and Ulyana spent five minutes peeling soggy paper off the type with a bone folder while Milo held the lamp and offered suggestions of increasing uselessness ("Could you — no? All right. What if — also no. I'll stand here.").

"The packing's damp," Ulyana said at last, feeling the tympan with her palm. "Cellar's too humid. Paper drinks the air down here."

"Henrik used to say the same about my mother-in-law," Signe said. "There's a stove behind the crates — light it, low. We'll be warm and visible, or dry and discreet. I know which I'd choose."

They lit the stove. They dried the packing over its open door, filling the cellar with the smell of scorched wool and, briefly, optimism. Anton cut ink with linseed oil by feel, watching Ulyana's hands more than the slab, and when she caught him at it he didn't look away, which somehow made it worse and better.

"More," she said. "A finger more. My father always said ink should be like gossip — thin enough to spread, thick enough to stick."

"Your father," Anton said, working the knife, "said a great many things to strangers in a trench outside Kovno that he apparently never said to his own daughter."

"He said plenty. Just none of it useful. 'Don't touch the quoins.' 'Measure twice.'" She snapped the last of the paper free. "'History is written by whoever owns the presses.'"

Anton stopped. Looked up. "He said that?"

"All the time. I thought it was shop talk."

"It wasn't," Anton said quietly. "It was a confession."

Before Ulyana could decide what to do with that, Signe barked, "Ink man, stop making eyes and ink. Compositor, to the lever. We are burning moonlight and packing paper."

* * *

By the eleventh pull they had a sheet Ulyana would have been proud to hang in the shop window, if hanging it in the shop window weren't precisely the surest way to get everyone in the cellar arrested.

"There," Signe said, holding it at arm's length. "There she is." She touched the corner of the printed page with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child you didn't want to wake. "Three thousand of these, you say."

"Three thousand," Ulyans said. "Eight pages. In nine days, if the armistice timetable Anton heard holds."

"Nine days." Signe sniffed. "Henrik printed eight thousand leaflets in a weekend during the dock strike and was home Sunday for supper. Of course, he was twenty years younger and so was the press, and neither of us has been properly oiled since." She patted the machine. "We'll manage. We always do. Milo — paper. Proper stock this time, not that manifest rubbish."

Milo went to the paper shelf, counted sheets onto the feed board, and got to the fourth sheet when the ceiling above them creaked.

Everyone stopped breathing. It is remarkable, Ulyana thought later, how completely a human body can agree to stop functioning when required. The gas lamp hissed. The stove ticked. Above them, floorboards complained under a slow, shuffling weight, followed by the unmistakable sound of a door opening and a voice — Mrs. Brandvik, who lived above the stationery shop and was, according to Milo's intelligence network, seventy-three years old, mostly deaf, and constitutionally opposed to all human activity including her own.

"Signe!" the voice called down the stairwell. "SIGNE! Are you down there burning something?"

Signe closed her eyes briefly, as if praying for patience from a deity she probably printed leaflets about. She crossed to the stairs and ascended halfway, calling up in a voice of perfect, placid innocence: "Stove, Astrid. It's cold. Are you all right?"

"I smelled ink!"

"Wedding proofs. The Engstrands moved their date up. Go back to bed, it's nearly midnight."

A pause. Floorboards shifted. Ulyana, frozen at the press with her hand still on the lever, watched Anton slide off the crate and put himself between the stairwell and the forme with a fluidity that reminded her, uncomfortably, of exactly what he had been before he was a courier.

"I don't smell weddings," Mrs. Brandvik announced. "I smell *newsprint*."

"Proofs are newsprint, Astrid. Everything is newsprint unless you pay for laid paper." A beat. "Would you like to see them? They've got gilt borders. Come down if you like."

This was either brilliance or madness, and from the sound of it — a scandalized little huff, a muttered *"at midnight, honestly,"* the scrape of furniture, the retreat of slippered feet — it was brilliance. Signe came back down the stairs wearing the expression of a woman who had just won a duel without unsheathing anything.

"Gilt borders," Milo whispered. "You don't have any—"

"Of course I don't. But Astrid would rather die than look at anything as vulgar as a proof sheet after midnight. Invoking gilt is like showing a vampire a cross." She took her place at the feed board. "Now. Where were we before the dragon at the door?"

"Pull eleven," Ulyana said. Her heart was still going like a drum corps, but her hands were steady, and she noticed — filed away — that steadiness under fear felt strangely like competence. Or perhaps it was the same thing. Perhaps that was the whole trick nobody told you.

* * *

They ran twelve more pulls. Each one better. By the last, Corporal Lindqvist's words sat on the page black and certain, and Milo stacked them with the reverence of an altar boy, and Anton read over each sheet even though he knew the text by heart, checking, always checking, the habit of a man whose life had once depended on noticing details.

"Stop," Signe said suddenly.

Ulyana's stomach dropped. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." Signe was looking at the sheet on the stone, then at Ulyana, then back. "Come look at your 'f'."

Ulyana leaned in. In *confessed*, mid-line — the f stood a hair proud of its neighbors, its crossbar kissing the adjacent letters, spaced with that particular greedy closeness her father had never trained out of himself.

She hadn't done it on purpose. She checked the next f. Same. And the next. Three years at her father's case, and her hands had absorbed his private arithmetic without her permission.

"He's in your spacing," Signe said softly. "Like a voice in a grandchild. You can't help it. That's inheritance, girl. That's the whole thing in miniature."

Ulyana looked at the letter for a long moment. Something in her chest did what the press did — took the pressure, transferred it, left a mark.

"Right," she said, when she could trust her voice. "Same time tomorrow. We'll do pages two and three. Milo, bring the manifest stock — we may need it sooner than I said, and I'll tell you why on the way."

"Why not tell me now?" Milo asked, gathering his tool roll.

"Because if I say it out loud I'll start organizing the fear instead of the work, and the work matters more tonight."

Anton snuffed the lamp. In the sudden dark, the press ticked as it cooled, like a clock counting down. Signe locked the crate wall behind them, and they climbed the stairs into the cold, ordinary night of Kirkegade, four conspirators smelling of ink, past a door behind which Mrs. Brandvik slept soundly beside a world that had eleven days left of believing itself innocent.
