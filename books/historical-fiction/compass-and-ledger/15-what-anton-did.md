## 15. What Anton Did

The rain came in off the harbor that evening the way it always did in October — sideways, with opinions — and drummed against the loading door like a creditor who knew we were home.

I was locking up the type cabinets when Anton said my name.

Not "compositor." Not "Miss Eriksen." Not one of his careful, formal addresses that kept a polite distance between us, as if we were two strangers sharing a railway compartment. Just *Ulyana*, spoken low, the way a man says a word he has been rehearsing.

I set down the composing stick. Something in his voice had changed since supper — he had pushed his bread around the plate and answered Milo's chatter with half-smiles — and now I understood that the half-smiles had been a wall, and he had decided to knock it down before it fell on both of us.

"You'd better sit," he said.

"I've been sitting all day. My father used to say a compositor's legs are ornamental."

"Ulyana." He was standing by the stove, and the firelight caught the fresh bandage on his shoulder, and his hands were hanging at his sides like things that had forgotten their purpose. "You need to know what I did. Before you trust me with any more of this." He gestured — at the shop, at the galleys of testimony, at the letters from her father that lay upstairs in a biscuit tin wrapped in oilcloth. At everything, really. At me.

I pulled up the stool from the proofing table anyway. Sat. Folded my hands, which is what my mother does when she is bracing for bad news delivered by a doctor or a bill.

"Tell me," I said.

* * *

He told it badly at first, which I have since learned is how honest men tell hard things. Liars give you a shape — beginning, middle, tidy end. Truth comes out in lumps.

"I wasn't always a courier," he said. "I was in the Ninth Infantry. Halvard's own. Three years."

"I know that much."

"You know I deserted. You don't know what I deserted *from*." He crouched by the stove and fed it a log with exaggerated care, as though the fire were a difficult apprentice needing occupation. "My first posting was a village called Ostrov. Grain country. Flat as a griddle, you could see the church spire from three kilometers in every direction. We were sent to requisition supplies."

"Requisition," I repeated. The word had a government smell to it, like sealing wax.

"That's what the order said." He watched the log catch. "There was no grain, Ulyana. There hadn't been since spring — the army had already taken it twice. And so Lieutenant Vassek, who was ambitious, decided we would take what there was instead. Livestock. Seed grain. The seed grain especially, because seed grain is *future* grain, and taking it means the theft repeats itself next year without anyone lifting a finger again. Very efficient. Halvard liked efficiency."

The rain filled the pause. I found I was holding my elbows very tightly, the way you do on a cold day.

"And you?" I asked, because not asking would have been mercy, and mercy was not what either of us wanted just then. "What did you do?"

"I held the lantern." He said it flatly, and somehow that flatness was worse than any crack in his voice could have been. "That was my job in the Ninth. I was steady, and I could see in the dark, and so I carried the light while other men did the — " He stopped. Started again. "While the requisition happened. A whole autumn of villages, Ulyana. I held the lantern at Ostrov, and at Kelmis, and at a dozen names I've tried to drink out of my memory and failed. And when a farmer at Kelmis grabbed Vassek's arm, I saw what happened to him, and I looked at the ground, and I adjusted my lantern, because a lit lantern is a *task*, you understand. A man holding a task has an excuse for where his eyes are pointing."

"Yes," I said quietly. "We understand that in printing too. The man who sweeps the floor during an argument."

He looked up at me then — startled, almost grateful — and I realized I had said something kind without meaning to, which is often how kindness works best.

"The desertion wasn't noble," he went on. "I want that understood too. It wasn't conscience rising up like a trumpet call. It was a night at a place called Zeleny Ford where I was given an order I couldn't hold a lantern through, and I ran. I ran like a rabbit, in the mud, and I vomited behind a fence post two kilometers away, and only *then*, lying in the mud being ashamed of myself, did it occur to me that shame might be put to use. Your father found me three days later. Half-starved. He fed me, and he asked questions, and I discovered that the only thing I had left — the only thing I hadn't spent at Kelmis and Ostrov — was that I had *seen*. Witnessed. That's a kind of property, apparently. It can be transferred."

"To paper," I said.

"To paper." He almost smiled. "Your father's idea, of course. He said — and this is nearly word for word — 'A soldier's memory rots with him, Anton. Ink keeps.' He talked about ink the way other men talk about God."

"He talks about ink the way *everyone* in this family talks about ink," I said. "It's a hereditary condition."

But my heart wasn't in the joke, because my mind was doing what it does when it's been handed a heavy parcel: turning it over, checking the weight, looking for the address.

Milo, I should say, had gone home an hour before, after an elaborate pantomime of forgetting his scarf that fooled no one. He had been watching Anton all week with the sharp sideways attention of a boy raised on stories about strange men who bring trouble through loading doors — which, in fairness, is exactly what Anton was. But tonight the shop was ours alone, and the rain had driven even the harbor cats indoors, and the confession sat in the middle of the floor between us like a piece of machinery nobody had ordered.

"So," I said at last. "You followed orders. For three years."

"For three years."

"And then you stopped."

"At Zeleny Ford. In the mud. Yes."

I thought about my father's ledgers downstairs — I had been through them twice more since finding the coded entries, and each pass revealed some new small signature of his: the way he ruled columns, the private shorthand in the margins, *waste 2 qrs — burn*, the little economy of a careful man. A ledger records what happened. It doesn't record what the bookkeeper wished had happened instead. I had been chewing on that all week.

"Anton," I said. "Do you know what my job was, before you came through that door?"

"Compositor."

"My job was to set, letter by letter, whatever was put in front of me. Shipping manifests. Customs notices. Last month, a government order I didn't read past the first line, because reading isn't my department — setting is. If someone had put the Ostrov requisition order on my stone, I would have composed it beautifully. Justified margins. Clean impression. And swept the floor afterward." I looked at my hands, which have ink in the creases no amount of pumice will ever fully remove, and which I have long considered the most honest part of me. "I'm not telling you this to make us even. I'm telling you because I don't think guilt divides up the way you're trying to divide it — into soldiers with lanterns and everyone else, innocent, at home, sweeping."

"It's not the same."

"No," I agreed. "It's not the same. You were cold and afraid and surrounded by men with rifles; I was warm and well-fed and bored. Yours is the heavier parcel. But you didn't come here tonight to be told your parcel is heavier. You came to find out whether I'd throw you out."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, my mother coughed twice — the light, dry cough that meant she was awake but resting, that I had learned to read the way sailors read weather.

"Yes," Anton said simply.

"Well." I stood, because this seemed like a moment requiring a person on their feet, and fetched down the biscuit tin from its hiding place behind the specimen books. Set it on the proofing table between us. "Then here is what I think. A guilty man carrying truth is worth ten clean men carrying nothing, because the guilty man knows exactly what the truth costs, having paid for it himself. But — " and I put my hand flat on the tin, and felt through the oilcloth the corners of the letters inside, my father's slantwise hand, alive under my palm — "you carry it *for* them, Anton. For Ostrov. For the man at Kelmis who grabbed the lieutenant's arm. Not for your own absolution. Absolution isn't yours to fetch. Understood?"

"Understood." His voice had gone rough.

"Good. Because I need help with something, and I'd rather ask a guilty man than a clean one anyway. Clean ones faint at the sight of solvents."

* * *

What I needed help with was the galley press — specifically, the stubborn old lock-up frame that my father maintained like a violin and that I had been fighting all afternoon. Two heads and four hands being better than one, we worked by lamplight, tightening quoins, testing the furniture, and it was during this labor — the particular intimacy of two people sweating over shared machinery — that Anton began, unprompted, to fill in the rest.

Not the atrocities; those were in the documents, catalogued with terrible precision. The ordinary things. How he had learned to sleep standing up. How the men in the Ninth sang on the march, badly, songs about girls in towns they'd never seen. How your father —

"How Eriksen taught me to read a page," he said, easing a quoin key a quarter-turn. "Properly, I mean. He said most people look *through* print at the meaning and never once look *at* it. Then he showed me a newspaper and pointed out how the spacing tightened near the bottom of a column where the compositor had run short, and once you see that, you can't unsee it. The whole world became typeset. Shop signs, proclamations, army field orders. I started noticing things in the field orders. Who they were printed for. What they assumed about the reader." He paused. "That's how the collection began, really. Your father taught me that documents have fingerprints."

"He'd be insufferably pleased to hear that."

"He'd deny it sternly and then preen for a week."

And there it was — the first time either of us had spoken of my father in the present tense, easily, like a man expected home for supper — and we both heard it, and neither corrected it. Some fictions are load-bearing. You leave them up until the real walls are ready.

We got the frame locked. I pulled a test proof onto scrap — a few lines of yesterday's manifest, *forty crates cured herring, port of registry Vessengard* — and the impression came up crisp and black and true, and Anton made a small sound of satisfaction that surprised us both with its boyishness.

"There," I said. "Now you know the shop's darkest secret: the frame sticks on the north side and you must speak firmly to it."

"I've survived firmer company."

He cleaned the roller with the unhurried competence of a man who had been shown once, weeks ago, and had filed the knowledge away with everything else. Watching him, I thought about parcels again — how you cannot know what a thing weighs until you try to lift it yourself — and I thought about the mud at Zeleny Ford, and about the sweeping I had done in my life during arguments, and I found that my verdict of an hour ago had not softened into sentimentality but hardened into something more useful: certainty. A guilty man who locks a press frame properly at one in the morning, in a stranger's shop, for no pay and considerable risk, is a man whose shame has been converted into fuel. I would rather have fuel than innocence. Innocence doesn't pull a lever.

"Ulyana." He had stopped cleaning. The rag hung from his fist. "One more thing. The reason I told you tonight and not last week. Brandt's office published a new censorship schedule. I saw a copy at the guild hall. Print shops get inspected on rotation — it's routine, supposedly — but Eriksen & Sons is on next month's list, early."

"Eriksen & Sons," I repeated. "There hasn't been a Son in this establishment since 1904."

"Nevertheless, that's the name on their schedule. Which means somebody official still thinks of this shop as a going concern with a male proprietor. Somebody official may also be wondering why its paper orders have been — " he chose the word carefully — "*irregular*."

The misprinted manifest stock. Twelve reams of it, diverted with Milo's help last week and hidden under the floor of the washroom, enough paper for a pamphlet run, obtained through petty theft dressed as clerical error. I had felt clever. I had felt, briefly, like the heroine of one of Milo's lurid serialized novels.

Now I felt watched.

"Routine inspections happen all the time," I said, and heard in my own voice the exact tone Anton had used for the word *requisition*, and hated it, and kept talking anyway. "Father handled dozens of them. You give the inspector tea, you show him the license, you compliment his coat if he seems the type, and he goes away."

"And if this inspector isn't the type?"

"Then," I said, picking up the roller he had set down, and beginning, with great calm, to clean it again, "we will have tea ready anyway."

* * *

Upstairs, later, I checked on Mother, as I do, and found her awake and sitting by her window with a shawl, watching the rain make silver worms of the streetlamps below.

"Milo's cousin was asking questions at the fish market today," she said, without preamble, in the mild tone she uses for remarking on weather or neighbors' hedges. "About whether our shop has been buying more solvent than a shop our size needs."

My stomach performed a maneuver I had previously only read about in naval memoirs. "Cousin Piet? The one who talks with his mouth full?"

"The same. Old Fru Lindqvist told me, between gossip and herring. She thought it was sweet, him taking interest in Milo's work." Mother turned from the window. Her face in the lamplight was thin and calm and entirely clear-eyed, and I remembered, not for the first time, that my mother had run this shop's accounts through two strikes and a customs scandal while I was still learning my letters. "Solvent, Ulyana, is a question about cleaning. Cleaning is a question about presses. Presses are a question about printing. Someone is walking toward us one question at a time, politely, in daylight."

"Then we'll answer the questions politely," I said, "in daylight."

She studied me for a long moment — the way she studies a proof sheet, I thought, hunting the flaw — and then nodded slowly and reached for her shawl-strings, and said only: "Buy solvent in smaller tins from now on. Two shops, alternating. And tell that young man his limp is back when he's tired. Inspectors notice limps."

I stood in the corridor outside her door for a while afterward, listening to the rain, doing sums I did not like: a censorship schedule with our name on it, a chatty cousin at the market, a limping courier, twelve reams under the washroom floor. All the small threads of an ordinary autumn, quietly tying themselves into a noose while we set type by lamplight and called it safety.

Three days later, when the bell above the shop door rang at eleven in the morning and a neat, gray, courteous man introduced himself as Inspector Brandt of the Censorship Office and observed that we must be the Eriksen establishment, and how interesting, he'd heard the name mentioned recently in connection with paper allocations — I understood that Mother's arithmetic had been correct, and that the walking had arrived.
