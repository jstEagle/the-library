## 16. Brandt Returns

He came on a Thursday, which Ulyana would later think was fitting. Thursdays were ledger days in the Eriksen shop — the day her father had set aside for balancing accounts, checking stock against orders, making the numbers agree with one another. It was, ironically, the one day of the week when she could not plausibly claim to have no idea how much paper was in the building.

The bell above the loading door gave its small brass cough, and Milo looked up from the stone where he was cleaning rollers, saw who it was, and developed an urgent fascination with the solvent tin.

"Frøken Eriksen." Inspector Brandt removed his hat and stood dripping just inside the doorway, because it had begun to rain the way Vessengard rain did in autumn — not falling so much as settling, a grey dampness that seemed less like weather than like the city itself exhaling. "You'll forgive the intrusion."

"Inspector." She set down her composing stick with the deliberate calm of a woman who had nothing whatsoever on her mind. "We're between jobs. I'm afraid if you've come about the church circulars, they went out Monday."

"They went out Monday," he agreed. "I know. I read them." He smiled — a thin, courteous movement of the mouth that never quite reached the pale eyes behind his spectacles. "Very sound doctrine. The Prodigal Son. One might say timely."

"One might," said Ulyana, and offered tea, because that was what one did, and because a man standing in the doorway was a man about to leave, and a man seated by the stove was a man she could watch.

* * *

The last time Brandt had called — six weeks ago, during the routine censorship sweep that every printing house in Vessengard endured each quarter — he had been almost apologetic about it. He had leafed through their proofs with the air of a man obliged to inspect a shipment of herring, asked two desultory questions about typefaces, complimented the coffee, and gone. He had struck her then as a tired instrument of a tired state: a clerk in a good coat, going through motions so worn they were nearly transparent.

This was not that man.

This man sat down at the workbench she cleared for him, accepted the tea, wrapped both hands around the cup as though genuinely cold — and then let the silence do his work for him. He looked at the cases of type. He looked at the press, old Berthold, her father's pride, its iron frame painted over so many times it had gone the colour of a storm cloud. He looked, for a moment too long, at the door to the back room, which was shut, which was always shut now, and which had a new bolt on the inside that Ulyana prayed daily he would not ask about.

"You've been busy," he said at last.

"Not so busy as we'd like. Times being what they are."

"Times being what they are," he echoed, mildly, as though agreeing to the weather. Then: "Your paper order from the Provincial Supply Office. September the eleventh. Forty reams of print stock, double-weight." He produced a small notebook from inside his coat, opened it, and laid it flat on the bench with the tenderness of a man laying out a patient for examination. "That's rather more than your manifest work requires, isn't it? By my arithmetic you should still be sitting on most of it."

Ulyana's heart performed a single enormous thump and then, remarkably, resumed its ordinary rhythm. *So that's what this is.* Not the back room. Not Anton. Not the pamphlet that did not yet exist except in her head and in Anton's fevered plans and in Signe Holm's cellar. Paper. Only paper.

Only everything, actually, because forty reams minus what they'd shown on their books equalled exactly the stock that had gone into three fish barrels on the night of the twelfth, bound — via a route she still couldn't think about without her stomach clenching — for a cellar under a chandlery on Saltmongers' Row.

"We returned half of it," she said.

"Mm." He did not look up from the notebook. "The return slip says thirty-five reams received back on the fourteenth."

"The fourteenth, yes."

"And yet the supply office's receiving ledger has no such entry." He turned the notebook around so she could see — though she made herself only glance at it, uncurious, bored, a woman looking at another person's paperwork. "Their clerk is a boy named Ostram. Sloppy boy. Writes like a spider fell in the inkwell. But even sloppy boys generally manage to record thirty-five reams coming back through the door." He closed the notebook. "It's possible he simply forgot. It's possible the entry will turn up next week. These things happen, in times like these."

"Constantly," Ulyana agreed.

"But it's also possible," Brandt went on, in the same mild voice, the same reasonable tone he might use to discuss the price of rags, "that no such return ever occurred, and that thirty-five reams of government-supplied print stock are currently sitting somewhere in this city, unaccounted for, in the hands of people who have some use for them." He sipped his tea. "Good tea. Your mother's blend?"

"Our supplier's," said Ulyana, who had bought it herself from the corner grocer and knew for a fact that it was mostly dust and chicory. "I'll pass along the compliment."

* * *

The trouble with lying, she discovered over the following hour, was that she was good at it.

She had never been good at it before. As a girl she had been hopeless — her mother could read a fib off her face at forty paces, and her father used to say she blushed like a beet whenever she told so much as a white lie about who had left the type case open. She had assumed this was a permanent feature of her character, like her crooked little finger or her inability to carry a tune. A moral feature, even. Honest Ulyana, whose face showed everything.

But here she sat, cupping her own chipped cup, explaining to an officer of the state precisely, fluently, and with a small regretful smile, how the discrepancy had arisen: the delivery had come short; she had complained; young Ostram had promised to correct the books and evidently never had; she could produce her own delivery notes if the inspector wished; she was sorry for the confusion, truly, these things were so easily muddled in times like these.

And every word of it was invention, delivered without a tremor, without a blush, with even a certain warmth — because warmth, she had realized somewhere around the third exchange, was the best disguise of all. A liar who seemed nervous was caught. A liar who seemed *pleased to help* was waved through.

Brandt asked to see the delivery notes. She fetched them — she had prepared them days ago, after Milo's cousin's drunken mouth at the Anchorline, after the first cold drop of dread — and he examined them while Milo, at the far end of the shop, made a heroic pantomime of cleaning a roller that had already been clean for twenty minutes.

"These are in your hand?"

"My hand. My shop. My responsibility." Let him make of that what he liked.

"Hm." He checked something. He held one page up to the light, briefly, as though checking for watermarks or alterations, and Ulyana felt a flicker of genuine fear and stamped it flat. "The stock that remains — the legitimate remainder. Might I see it?"

And there it was. The question she'd rehearsed for, alone, at two in the morning, setting imaginary type in her head because sleep wouldn't come. She rose without hurry. "Of course. Mind the step."

* * *

The paper store was off the back room's antechamber — a narrow stone room smelling of size and dust — and the trick of it, the trick she had worked out with Milo over two pots of bad coffee, was abundance. Hide a thing by showing its neighbours. They had bought, with money they could ill spare, six cheap reams of wrapping stock from a wholesaler on credit, and stacked them with artful care among the legitimate remainder, so that the room looked full, generous, prosperous — a shop with more paper than it knew what to do with, which was the very last thing anyone would believe of a print works in the winter of 1919.

Brandt walked the narrow aisle. He ran a finger along a stack. He counted — she watched him count, lips moving faintly — and she stood in the doorway with her hands folded and her pulse tapping away in her throat like a second heart.

"Double-weight," he murmured. "Yes." He turned. "You know, Frøken Eriksen, I visited your father once. Before the war. A licensing matter — nothing of consequence." He said it lightly, but his eyes were doing something she didn't like: reading the room the way she read a galley proof, top to bottom, letter by letter. "He showed me the press. Berthold, he called her. He talked about her the way other men talk about their wives." A small pause. "He was a careful man, your father. Every sheet accounted for. He told me waste was a kind of theft — from the craft itself."

"He believed that," Ulyana said. And it was true, and the truth of it went through her like a needle, because her father *had* believed it, and here was his daughter committing fraud in his own storeroom, perjury in his own tea cup.

"A careful man," Brandt repeated. "One wonders where—" He stopped himself, with visible professional discipline, and put his hat back on. "Well. These things happen, in times like these."

"So they do."

At the door he paused. The rain had thickened; the light beyond the loading door had gone the colour of dishwater. "I'll want to speak with young Ostram again, of course. And perhaps your apprentice." He glanced across the shop at Milo, who was suddenly extremely invested in the solvent tin. "Boys notice things. Sometimes they mention them. Sometimes to the wrong cousin." He settled the hat. "Thank you for the tea, Frøken Eriksen. Do give my regards to your mother."

And he stepped out into the rain, and was gone, unhurried, his umbrella blooming like a black flower.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Milo let out a breath that seemed to come all the way up from his boots. "He knows about Kasper," he said hoarsely. "About my cousin. He *knows*, Ulla, he said 'wrong cousin,' he—"

"He was fishing." She heard her own voice come out level, steady, someone else's voice. "If he knew, he wouldn't have hinted. Knowing men don't hint. Knowing men arrive with soldiers."

"You're sure?"

No. She was not remotely sure. She began gathering the teacups because her hands wanted occupation and her hands, unlike her voice, could still be trusted to tell the truth. "I'm sure he counted the stacks. I'm sure he'll come again. And I'm sure that from today, nothing goes through the loading door that hasn't been watched for a week first."

Milo nodded, twice, and she saw the fear in him settle into something harder and more useful — resolve, or its adolescent approximation. He picked up the roller and began, genuinely this time, to work.

* * *

Alone at the case afterward, distributing type, Ulyana found her thoughts refusing to stay sorted.

She had lied. Not evaded, not kept silent, not merely failed to volunteer — lied, fluently, warmly, with fabricated delivery notes in her own careful hand. Her father's daughter, forging documents in the shop he'd built, wearing honesty like a coat with nothing underneath. And the worst part — the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone — was that she hadn't hesitated. The words had simply been there, ready, polished, *composed*, like lines of type waiting in the stick. When had she learned this? Who had taught her? Some hidden case in her own character, stocked overnight while she slept, letters she'd never known she owned.

*Waste is a kind of theft,* her father used to say, laughing, ink to the elbows. Well, Papa. Here is your daughter, stealing. Here she is, good at it.

She thought of Signe Holm saying, in that cellar that smelled of grease and onions: *The truth needs hands, child. Dirty ones, usually.* At the time it had sounded like wisdom. Now it sounded like a bill come due.

She slid the last ligature into its compartment and closed the case, and the small familiar click of it steadied her slightly. The pamphlet needed her lies the way a roof needs scaffolding, she told herself. You took the scaffolding down after. You built clean.

You hoped the builder survived the building.

Upstairs, faintly, she heard her mother moving about the kitchen — the slow shuffle-step, the clink of the good cups. Her mother, who drifted through the shop some days like a ghost in her dressing gown, asking gentle confused questions, noticing nothing, missing nothing—

Who had asked her, only last week, at breakfast, with terrible mildness: *Is that inspector eating properly, Ulya? He looks thin. Men like that forget to eat.*

Ulyana stood in the lamplight with the stone in her chest and the lie still warm on her tongue, and realized, with a sensation like a floor tilting, that there might be considerably less in this house that escaped her mother's notice than any of them had ever supposed.
