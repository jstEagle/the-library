## 17. Mother Knows

My mother had a way of appearing in doorways that suggested she had been standing there for some time.

She did it on the third evening after Signe Holm's visit, while I was at the kitchen table pretending to reconcile the shop's accounts — a fiction I had maintained with increasing sloppiness for weeks, because the numbers I wrote down bore less relation to the truth than the government's shipping manifests did. Behind me, Anton's footsteps crossed the landing overhead, slow and careful of his bad leg, on his way to the cot we'd made up in the type room. Milo had gone home an hour before. The house was settling into itself, floorboards ticking as the stove cooled.

"Ulyana." My mother stood with her hands folded, wearing the gray dress she saved for doctors' visits and funerals. "Will you come upstairs? I should like to talk with you before I sleep."

There is a particular dread attached to that sentence from a sick woman. My stomach performed a small, cold revolution.

"Of course, Mamma."

She led me up to her room, where the lamp was already lit and the bed turned down, though it was barely nine. She sat in the chair by the window instead — her good chair, the one Papa had reupholstered with his own hands using fabric he called *dove gray* and everyone else called *faded*. From beneath the crocheted throw she took out a flat parcel wrapped in a linen napkin, and laid it on her knees, and looked at me for a long moment with those pale Ravensworth-blue eyes that I had inherited along with my stubbornness.

"I have been waiting," she said, "for you to tell me yourself. But you were always your father's daughter, and he would have carried a secret to the grave and then dug it up again to check on it."

"Mamma—"

"Hush. Let me do this properly; I have rehearsed it." She smoothed the napkin over her lap as though it were fine table linen. "I know about the letters. I have known since the second week of September, when you began locking the back room and speaking to that young man in the voice you use for strangers — which is my voice, incidentally, so I recognized it immediately. I know that your father is alive. I know what he sent home. And I know about the pamphlet, because although you whisper, Ulyana Eriksen, you whisper in a house with thin walls and a boy apprentice who hums when he is nervous, and lately everyone is nervous, so everyone is humming."

I sat down heavily on the edge of the bed. The mattress complained. So, eventually, did I.

"How much did you hear?"

"Enough. Not everything — my hearing is not what it was, and Anton has a soldier's habit of mumbling the important parts." Her mouth twitched. "But I heard your father's name spoken by a living voice's worth of hope, and I heard enough about General Halvard to be ashamed of every dinner party where I nodded politely while his name was praised."

"Why didn't you say anything?" It came out sharper than I intended — fear dressed as accusation, the family specialty. "All these weeks, you let me creep around like a thief in my own house. You let me lie to you."

"You did not lie to me. You *protected* me, badly, the way you protected me from the telegram two years ago by reading it first and hiding it under the sugar tin, as if I could not read a telegram myself." She said it without heat. "I said nothing because you needed to arrive at this without me pushing. Your father pushed people. Look where it got him — a fortress, if Anton's guess is right, and a war's worth of ghosts. You, my darling, pull. Like your grandmother, who could get a whole congregation to volunteer for the church roof without raising her voice. I wanted to see whether you would pull or push. You pulled."

I did not know what to do with that, so I did what I always did: argued.

"Mamma, you don't understand what we're doing. If it goes wrong — and it may, Inspector Brandt has been here twice and he counts things, he *counts*, it's his nature — if it goes wrong, everyone in this house is implicated. Milo. Anton. Me. You. They don't hang women, but they do other things to women, quieter things—"

"Ulyana."

"—and you are ill, you shouldn't even be *sitting* up this late, Dr. Voss said—"

"*Ulyana.*" She said my name the way she used to when I fell out of trees: not angry, merely ending the discussion. Then, gentler: "Come here."

I came. She took my face in both hands, and her fingers were thinner than I remembered, paper-thin, a compositor's wife's fingers gone soft at last, but they were still steady.

"In 1893," she said, "your father printed leaflets for the dockworkers' strike. You were four. He did it in the cellar, at night, and I sat on the stairs with a cooling pan and a pistol I did not know how to load, and I would do it again tonight if my legs allowed it. Do you imagine I married that man without understanding what he was? Do you imagine I raised you in a print shop without hoping some of the ink got into your blood?" Her thumbs brushed my cheekbones. "I have spent three years grieving a man who was not dead. That is a thing I do not forgive easily, and I do not forgive *him* easily either — he will answer for those years when I see him, and he knows it, and I hope it kept him warm. But he is alive, and he is trying to finish something true, and my daughter is the one carrying it across the finish line. I have never been prouder of anyone in my life. Including him. Don't tell him I said that."

I laughed, and it broke somewhere in the middle and became the other thing, and for a while my mother held me the way she had when I was small enough to fit against her shoulder entirely, and neither of us pretended I hadn't been crying.

When I could speak again, I said, "You rehearsed all of this."

"Every word. In front of the mirror, which felt ridiculous, but one owes the moment a little ceremony." She patted the bed beside her. "Now. Sit. There is business."

* * *

The business was in the parcel.

She unwrapped the linen napkin with the deliberation of a woman setting type, corner by corner, and inside lay her wedding silver. Not much of it — we had never been rich, only respectable — but respectable once counted for something: six forks, six knives, six spoons, two serving pieces, a small ladle with a curling handle, and the sugar tongs she'd refused to use for twenty-five years on the grounds that tongs were for people showing off. The candlelight moved along the handles, where a silversmith long dead had stamped a pattern of oak leaves.

"Mamma, no."

"Yes."

"That's — those are yours. Yours and Papa's. You've kept them through everything, through the *worst*—" I stopped, because the worst was precisely why she'd kept them; silver survives wars in cupboards the way hope does, unreasonably.

"They are mine," she agreed, "and therefore mine to spend. Silver in a cupboard buys nothing but polish. Silver in the right hands buys paper, ink, a night's lodging for a courier, silence from a fishmonger — whatever it is you need that I am not supposed to know about." She lifted the ladle, weighing it. "This was my mother's dowry piece. She crossed the sound with it sewn into her petticoat. It has done one useful thing in its life so far, and that was being smuggled. Consider it coming out of retirement."

"We can't sell it locally. A stranger asking after silver in Vessengard right now is a flag on a hilltop."

"Which is why," said my mother, with the mild triumph of someone who has anticipated the objection, "you will send it with Milo to his aunt in Havnvik, who owes me forty years of friendship and has never once asked a question she wasn't prepared to answer. She'll get a fair price and keep her mouth shut, and Milo gets a trip away from this house before he wears a groove in the floor with his worrying." She paused. "He is a good boy. Frightened, and does the thing anyway. That is the entire definition of courage, as far as I have ever determined."

"He doesn't know you know."

"He knows I'm not stupid, which frightens him more." She gathered the silver back into the napkin, unhurried. "There is one more thing, and then you will help me to bed and we will never speak of my weakness again."

She reached into the drawer of the bedside table — the drawer I had assumed held nothing but pills, thread, and the dead radio's fuses — and brought out an envelope, worn soft at the corners, addressed in my father's hand.

I went very still. "That's one of them. One of the letters. How—"

"The third one. It came in August, before Anton. Through a drayer who owed your father money and repaid the debt this way, apparently thinking he was doing me a kindness." She set it on the bed between us. "It arrived the week after Dr. Voss told me to prepare myself. I read it eleven times, and then I put it in the drawer, and I decided that if God was going to return my husband to me in the form of paperwork, I would simply have to stay alive long enough to file the complaint in person."

"Mamma—"

"I am not finished." Her voice cracked, just once, and she pressed on over it like a cart over a rut. "I am dying, Ulyana. We are both aware of it; we have been politely not discussing it for months, and I am tired of politeness. I may not live to see him walk through that door. If I don't, I want you to give him this letter back and tell him it is the eleventh reading that mattered — tell him the last line, he'll know. And tell him—" she stopped, and looked out the dark window a moment, and when she turned back her face was composed and fierce and entirely my mother's — "tell him the shop stayed open. Tell him the type was kept clean and the ledger was kept honest and the girl he left holding everything grew into the woman who holds it still. Can you remember that? Word for word?"

"Word for word," I said, and found I meant it literally; I had set ten thousand lines of copy, and some things take as permanent impression.

"Good." She tucked the letter into my hand and closed my fingers over it, folding my grip shut like a finished signature. "Then we understand each other. Now help me up. All this courage has made my hip ache abominably, and there is a limit to how much heroism one woman requires before bedtime."

* * *

I walked her the four steps to her bed, and she instructed me the whole way — pillow higher, shawl on the chair not the hook, window cracked exactly a finger's width because sealed rooms breed complaints — until she was settled, and then she caught my wrist.

"One more thing. I mean it this time; the last thing."

"You've said that four times."

"A mother reserves the right to amend." She pulled me down close. "The young man. Anton. He limps past my door every night at half past ten to check the draft under it, because he thinks I don't notice, and he flinches when the stairs creak, and he eats like a man apologizing for the food. Whatever he did over there that puts that look in his eyes — and men always think they're concealing it, and never have — you tell him from me that a person is not the worst thing they've done. I have buried a son and nearly buried a husband and watched my daughter turn grief into a career, and I am qualified to rule on the matter. A person is not the worst thing they've done. Say it to him when he needs it. He will need it soon; I could see it tonight at supper, the way he watches you decide things."

"I'll tell him," I said.

"And Ulyana—" the lamplight caught her smile, quick and sly as Milo's — "when this is finished and your father is home, *you* will be the one to decide what this family prints next. Not because he'll allow it. Because you'll have earned the case."

She closed her eyes before I could answer, and within minutes her breathing had gone slow and even, the performance of health she maintained even in sleep.

* * *

Downstairs, the shop smelled of ink and cold iron, and the lamp above the stone still burned where I'd left it. Anton was awake — I saw the light under the type-room door, and heard the small sounds of a man trying to be silent and failing at it, because soldiers are quiet outdoors and hopeless in a house.

I stood a while at my mother's letter in my hands, then put it in my breast pocket, over the heart, where documents go when they stop being documents.

Then I knocked on the type room door.

Anton opened it with the wary readiness that never quite left him, and relaxed when he saw it was only me, which was its own small compliment.

"My mother wants you to know," I said, "that a person is not the worst thing they've done."

Whatever he had expected, it was not that. He stood very still in the doorway, one hand on the frame, and I watched something move behind his face like ice shifting on a river — not breaking yet, but deciding to.

"She also," I added, "has given us her wedding silver, her blessing, a smuggling route through Milo's aunt in Havnvik, and a message for my father that I am now sworn to deliver word for word. Apparently she has known everything for weeks. She says I whisper badly and you mumble the important parts."

A breath escaped him that might have become a laugh in better circumstances. "She isn't wrong about you."

"No one in this house is ever wrong about me. It's a tyranny." I held up the silver bundle, the knives and forks chiming faintly inside the napkin like distant bells. "We sell this tomorrow, and with what it brings we buy enough stock for the full run. Which means—" I heard my own voice steady and strange, a voice making a promise — "the next proof we pull is the real one. The one that goes out into the world and never comes back."

Anton looked at the bundle, and at me, and at the press shadowed against the far wall, our great black sleeping animal.

"Then we'd better make sure," he said quietly, "it's perfect."

I reached past him and threw the switch, and the type room went dark, and somewhere above us the house creaked once, like a nod.
