## 18. The First Proof

The press in Signe Holm's cellar had a voice, and Ulyana had spent three nights learning its moods.

It was an older machine than anything in her father's shop — a handpress with a platen that groaned like a ship changing tack, and it wanted coaxing rather than commanding. Too much pressure on the left and the type bit into the paper hard enough to show through; too little and the letters came up pale, like a man speaking through a scarf. Signe, seventy years old and built like a barrel stave, adjusted the tympan with two fingers and the patience of someone who had been arguing with machinery since before Ulyana's mother was born.

"Again," Signe said.

Ulyana pulled the lever. The platen came down. The gears took her weight and gave back that deep, satisfying *thock* she had known all her life — the sound of her childhood, the sound of her father humming somewhere behind the composing stone.

"Closer," Signe said.

Milo, sprawled on a crate of rags with an oil rag going black in his hands, pronounced judgment from his throne. "Closer than last time, which was closer than the time before, which means by tomorrow morning we'll have achieved one perfect page, and we can spend the remaining eight days celebrating."

"You'll celebrate when I say you can," Signe said, without looking at him.

"Yes, Master Holm."

"And stop calling me that. It makes me feel like I've died."

Ulyana lifted the sheet. She had stopped expecting perfection around midnight of the second night and started accepting something better: near-perfection, honest imperfection, the slight kiss of ink where it should be. Her hands knew this work the way her heart did not know how to be brave — quietly, without deciding to.

The page in her hand was not a manifest. It was not shipping schedules for herring barrels out of Vessengard harbor, which was what the stolen misprinted stock had been destined for, and which was why no ledger anywhere would miss forty reams of it. The page in her hand was the first page of testimony given by Anders Eriksen, late compositor of this city, deserter of His Majesty's army, witness to what had happened in the winter villages along the front in the year 1915.

Her father's words, set in her own hands.

She read it standing there, though she already knew it by heart. She had set every word of it twice — once as galleys, once again after Anton had caught an error in the third paragraph that would have changed the number of households burned from eleven to seventeen, and neither of them could bear to guess which number her father had meant, so they had checked against the original letter three times until the figure resolved itself, and it was seventeen, and they had both sat very still for a while after that.

"How does it look?" Anton asked. He was leaning against the cellar wall where the lamplight didn't quite reach, which was his habit and everyone had agreed not to mention it. His wound was healing; he stood straighter now. But he still arranged himself in shadow the way other men arranged themselves in doorways.

"It looks like a book," Milo said, before Ulyana could answer. "It looks like an actual book, like the kind you'd buy, like the kind that has opinions about it printed in newspapers—"

"That's the point," Ulyana said.

"—and it's terrifying," Milo finished, undeterred. "I'm just saying. I've set type for you for four years, Ulyana, and I've never once been frightened of a page before. I don't care for it."

Signe held out her hand, and Ulyana gave her the sheet. The old woman held it at arm's length, tilted toward the lamp, and read it through in silence. She had insisted on reading every proof before it went further — *my cellar, my neck*, she'd said — and none of them had argued.

When she finished, she lowered the page slowly, and her face was not the face of a printer evaluating a job. It was the face of a reader.

"Well," she said. "That will do."

* * *

They gathered that evening because Ulyana asked them to, and if her voice shook slightly when she asked, everyone was polite enough to pretend otherwise.

The cellar had become, over the past week, a strange kind of home. Signe's press occupied the middle of the floor like a cathedral altar; the composing frame they'd carried over in pieces, wrapped in sacking, on three separate trips that Milo had narrated each time as though they were smuggling contraband angels. A stove ticked in the corner. There were crates to sit on now, and a kettle, because Signe held that no revolution worth having had ever been conducted without tea.

Anton sat closest to the stairs — always, Ulyana had noticed, positioned between the group and any door that opened onto the world. Milo perched on the crate nearest the stove, which he had claimed as territory early and defended with the ferocity of a small dog. Signe knitted. Nobody had known she knitted until tonight; the needles flashed like small silver fish.

And Ulyana stood by the press, holding the first clean proof of the first page, and understood that she was about to do the bravest thing she had ever done, which was simply to speak.

"I thought," she said, "that someone should hear it whole. Out loud. Before we print three thousand copies and lose control of it forever. Once it's out there—" She stopped, started again. "Once it's loose, it stops being ours. It becomes everyone's. So. Once, first, it should be spoken properly. By someone who set it."

Silence. The kettle ticked. Somewhere above them, in the ordinary world, a cart went by.

She began to read.

*"I, Anders Marius Eriksen, compositor, of Vessengard, do make the following statement freely and of my own hand, being alive, contrary to official record, and being a witness, which is worse."*

Milo made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost not.

*"In January of 1916 I was attached to the supply column of the Fourth Brigade under General Halvard. I state plainly that I deserted my post on the fourteenth of that month, and I do not repent of it, and any court that wishes may hang me for the desertion but must first hear what I deserted because of."*

Ulyana's voice held. She had wondered whether it would. She had set these words in metal, touched every letter of them, and still she had wondered whether saying them aloud — giving them breath, making them flesh in a warm room full of people who cared whether she lived — would break something in her throat.

It did not break. It steadied. That was the miracle of print, she realized, the thing she had never put into words in ten years at the case: type is courage you can hold. Someone had to be brave exactly once, at the moment of setting, and after that the bravery lives in the metal and transfers itself to whoever lifts the page. Her father had been brave enough to write this. She only had to be brave enough to carry it.

*"The village was called Zelany Most. I spell it as I heard it spelled by its people, who are gone. There were forty-one houses and a church with a green roof..."*

She read the testimony of the winter villages: the orders signed in Halvard's name, reprisals executed against civilians for the acts of partisans, the granaries burned in February so that there would be nothing to feed anyone in March, the column of prisoners who were marched out in the snow and fewer of whom marched back. Her father wrote like a compositor — precisely, without ornament, numbers and dates and names — and it was the precision that made it unbearable. No adjectives. Just arithmetic done in human beings.

Milo had gone completely still. The boy who could not sit through a fifteen-minute sermon, who fidgeted through everything, whose knee bounced like a metronome through even the most solemn funerals — Milo sat with his hands flat on his knees and his eyes fixed on the floor, and he did not move for the length of a full page.

Signe's needles had stopped. Ulyana hadn't heard them stop; she only noticed, midway through, that the room had no sound in it but her own voice and the stove.

When she reached the passage her father had written about the church — *"I helped bury no one, because there was no one left to bury and nothing left to bury them with; I write this so that the number stands somewhere other than in my memory, where it is wearing me away"* — she heard Anton exhale, long and slow, the way a man breathes when he has been holding it for years.

She finished. *"This statement is true. I am alive to be questioned about any part of it. — A. M. Eriksen, Vessengard, written in hiding, March 1919."*

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Signe laid her knitting down in her lap, folded her hands on top of it, and said, in the voice of a master addressing an apprentice, "Your father set type?"

"All his life."

"He writes like it. Every sentence sits square." She nodded at the page. "You read it well, girl. Now we make it so nobody ever has to read it aloud again — they can just hand it to their neighbor, and their neighbor can't look away, because it's right there in ink, and ink doesn't flinch."

"Ink doesn't flinch," Milo repeated softly, as if filing it away.

Anton rose and came forward into the lamplight, which he almost never did, and Ulyana understood it as what it was — a kind of salute. He held out his hand for the sheet, examined it the way a soldier examines a weapon, and then looked at her.

"Three thousand copies," he said. "By signing day. Do you believe yet that we can do it?"

Ulyana considered the question honestly. The press downstairs groaned. Her wrists ached. The stolen paper was stacked under sacking in the corner, and there was perhaps enough of it if nothing went wrong, and things went wrong constantly.

"No," she said. "But I believe it anyway. That's what the last week has taught me. Believing isn't the same as being sure. It's more like — setting type. You commit the letters to the stick before you know how the page will justify."

Anton smiled — one of his rare ones, the ones that rearranged his whole face and made him look briefly like a much younger man, a man from before. "Your father says something like that in one of his letters. About justification."

"He would." And then, because the warmth in the room had reached a point where she could risk it: "He never said anything that wise to me directly, mind. To me he said things like 'mind your quads' and 'you're holding the bodkin wrong.' Wisdom by post only."

"Perhaps," Anton said, "he found it easier to be wise at a distance."

"Most men do," said Signe. "Present company included."

"Present company included," Anton agreed gravely, and Milo snorted into his tea, and the room laughed — quietly, careful of the neighbors above, but genuinely — and Ulyana felt something settle in her chest that she had no better word for than *belonging*. Four people and a groaning press in a cellar, conspiring in ink. Her father would have called it a proper shop.

* * *

Later, when Signe had gone up to bed and the stove had been banked and Milo had been sent home with strict instructions to walk the long way round and look bored, Ulyana stayed to clean the press, and Anton stayed to help her, which meant mostly to hold lamps and hand her rags and talk, which was how they had begun to talk — sideways, while working, as if the work gave them permission.

"Do you remember the first thing you ever set?" he asked.

"Of course. Everyone remembers. It was my name. Father set me up at a spare stick when I was seven and told me to compose 'ULYANA ERIKSEN COMPOSITOR,' and I got the Y backwards, and he kept the misprint pinned above the stone for years. Said it was the best joke the shop ever produced free of charge." She wiped ink from the rollers, working the rag into the crevices. "What made you think of that?"

"Because tonight, when you finished reading, I thought — he taught her this. Every hour she spent learning, he was standing next to her. And he's sitting in a cell in the fortress right now, and he doesn't know that everything he taught her is down here, working. He doesn't know what she became." Anton paused. "I'd like him to find out. Not read it in a report. Find out."

Ulyana kept her eyes on the rollers. "We'll get him out."

"After signing day. When this is loose, when they can't simply make him disappear—"

"After signing day," she agreed, and hated the arithmetic of it even as she consented to it. Ten days. The pamphlet first, then the man. Truth before rescue, because rescue without truth would just be one old compositor released into a country that had agreed to forget. She had made her peace with that ordering six times this week and would probably have to make it sixty more.

She closed up the ink box. Anton doused the lamp down to a coal-glow. In the dark, before climbing the stairs, he touched the press once — flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a wall of a church — and Ulyana pretended not to see, and loved him a little for it, and filed that away too, in the drawer where she kept things too large to look at directly.

Outside, the night was raw and clear. Milo had left his bicycle chained to the rail, because of course he had, because the long way round apparently did not extend to walking home. Ulyana was still smiling at that when she noticed the light.

Across the lane, in the window of the corner house where the cousin lived — Milo's cousin Jannik, who drank, who was forever turning up at the shop asking after odd jobs — a curtain moved. Just slightly. The way a curtain moves when someone has been standing behind it and steps back.

Or the way it moves when someone is cold and shuts the window, she told herself. This was a city at armistice; half the population watched the other half out of windows; it meant nothing.

She locked Signe's door, checked it twice, and walked home through the empty streets rehearsing, for no reason she could name, the opening line of the pamphlet, over and over, like a prayer: *I, Anders Marius Eriksen, compositor, of Vessengard...*

*I am alive, contrary to official record.*

Soon, she promised the dark. Soon, everyone would know it.
