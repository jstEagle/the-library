## 19. A Betrayal Overheard

Milo Voss had three great talents in this world, and he counted them honestly: he could set type faster than any apprentice in Vessengard, he could eat a smoked herring in under a minute without crying, and he could tell when his cousin Joren was about to be an idiot.

The third talent had been failing him lately.

Joren worked the fish carts out of the harbor market — big shoulders, small thoughts, a laugh like a barrel going downstairs — and for eleven years he had been exactly the kind of cousin you wanted: loyal, uncomplicated, always good for half his bread. But lately something had gone wrong in Joren's chest, some slow leak of grievance. The war was over and nobody had thanked him for anything. The guilds were closed to outsiders. His girl had left for Copenhagen with a piano tuner. And now, on this raw Thursday evening in October, he sat at the corner table of the Salt & Anchor with four men Milo didn't like the look of, drinking aquavit like it owed him money.

Milo hadn't even meant to come in. He'd been sent out for glue — actual glue, for the binding job Mrs. Lundqvist had ordered for her church hymnals — but the tavern stood between the chandler's and home, and it had started raining sideways, and one drink to wait out the weather seemed like God's own reasonable suggestion.

That was the trouble with taverns. They were full of reasonable suggestions.

"—and I'm telling you," Joren was saying, loud enough that Milo heard it from the doorway before he'd even hung up his cap, "things *move* through that shop. Things that aren't manifests."

Milo's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for ships in weather.

He should have walked back out into the rain. He knew that immediately, the way you know you've stepped onto ice that's singing. But his feet did the cowardly-brave thing instead of either sensible thing: they carried him to the bar, where he ordered a small beer and turned on his stool so he could watch the corner table in the mirror behind the bottles.

There were four of them. Two he recognized by reputation — dockside men who loaded crates for whoever paid and asked nothing, which was a profession in Vessengard the way rain was a season. The third wore a railway uniform with the buttons polished too bright, which meant he cared about being seen as honest, which meant he wasn't. The fourth was thin and quiet and drank almost nothing, and it was the fourth man who made Milo's hands go cold around his glass, because quiet drinking men in taverns were never there for the tavern.

"So what kind of things?" the quiet one asked. Not eager. Just curious. The way a hook is curious.

"Paper," Joren said, grandly, as though he'd invented paper. "Whole reams of it. And ink — you can smell it on me some nights. Presses running when they shouldn't be. My cousin thinks I don't notice, but I notice everything." He drained his glass and signaled for another, which Milo noted with mounting horror was his fifth or possibly his ninth. "I've kept my mouth shut because he's family. Family's family."

"That's very decent of you," said the quiet man.

"It is, actually," said Joren, with the wounded dignity of the drunk. "It's extremely decent. Because what I could tell people — if I were the sort to tell —"

*Joren,* Milo thought, with the full futile force of prayer, *stop.*

"— is that there's a stranger living above the Eriksen print shop. Bandaged up like a Christmas goose. Comes and goes after dark. And the manifests they print don't add up to the paper they use." He leaned back, pleased with himself, master of information. "But I'm not the sort to tell."

"No," agreed the quiet man gently. "You're the sort to sell."

The table laughed. Joren laughed loudest, then seemed to hear what had been said, and his face went through several expressions like cards being fanned — amusement, confusion, the dawning suspicion of insult — before settling on belligerence. "I never said anything about selling."

"Of course not," said the quiet man, and put coins on the table, and stood, and Milo understood with terrible clarity that the conversation had achieved its purpose and the purpose had just left the building wearing a grey coat.

Milo paid for his untouched beer and followed.

* * *

The rain had settled into a fine malicious drizzle, the kind that doesn't fall so much as seep, entering your collar by negotiation. The quiet man went north toward the harbor offices at an unhurried pace, and Milo did not follow him, because following a man like that was how apprentices ended up as unexplained ballast in the harbor. Instead he ducked into the chandler's doorway and waited until Joren came weaving out with two of the dockside men, still talking, still loud, and cut across the street to intercept him.

"Joren!"

"Milo!" Joren's face opened like sunrise. "Cousin! Have a — no, you've had one, I saw you at the bar, sneaking about. Sitting with your back to everybody like a spy in a play."

"We need to talk."

"Then talk! I'm a talker." He waved farewell to his companions with both arms, generously, nearly losing balance. "Talk away."

"Not here."

"Where, then? Your shop of secrets?" And there it was again — that new hardness under the drunkenness, the grievance leaking through. Eleven years Milo had known this man's every mood, and this one he didn't recognize. "Your honorable mistress's shop? The great Ulyana Eriksen, may her ink never dry?"

"Don't call her that."

"What should I call her? She barely knows you're alive. You set her type and sweep her floor and sleep in your little room like a good dog, and does she ever once —" Joren stopped, swaying slightly, and Milo watched the alcohol and the anger fight for the wheel. "Does she ever ask about *your* life?"

"You're drunk."

"I'm honest! It looks the same from the inside." Joren jabbed a finger at him, missing twice. "You know what I get for honesty? Nothing. You know what I could get for —" and here he lowered his voice to a volume that would have been audible across a parade ground, "— for what I know? Real money. Real respect. There are men asking, Milo. Men with offices. Asking about the Eriksen shop, about comings and goings, and all they need is someone to confirm it, and they pay in silver, not gratitude."

The drizzle ran down Milo's neck, cold as fingers. "What men?"

"Wouldn't you like to know."

"Yes," Milo said. "That is literally why I asked. What men, Joren?"

His cousin squinted at him, and for a moment — one clear moment, like lightning showing a landscape — something frightened moved behind the drunkenness. Something that had already made its arrangements and was only now realizing the arrangements had weight. "Government men. Inspectors. How should I know? A man comes to the market, asks questions, buys my whole cart twice a week whether the herring's good or not. A man can feed his family, can't he? A man can be *paid attention to* for once in his miserable —"

"Shut up." Milo grabbed his sleeve. "Shut your enormous mouth and listen to me. Whatever you think is happening at that shop — whatever you smelled, whatever you imagined for the sake of feeling important — you will forget it. Do you understand? Because the men you're talking to are not buying herring, Joren. They're buying people. And the people they buy stop being people afterward. Ask anyone who sold."

Joren pulled his arm free. In the lamplight his face had gone ugly with hurt, which was worse than anger, because hurt couldn't be argued with. "Eleven years I've watched you belong to them," he said quietly — and this, finally, was sober, the truest thing he'd said all night. "You've got a place. You've got a trade. I've got a cart and wet feet, and you stand there telling me about *people.* You don't know anything about it, little cousin. You've never had to sell anything."

He turned and went, splashing through puddles, and Milo stood alone in the drizzle with the glue he'd forgotten to buy, understanding that he had just lost an argument by winning it, and that winning arguments against drunk men in the rain was worth precisely nothing if the drunk man had already talked to someone sober.

* * *

He took the long way home, which was to say he took every way, walking the streets of Vessengard in circles like a compass needle looking for north, trying to think.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out in his head with the neat precision of a man who spent his days justifying lines of type:

One: Joren had seen the night press runs. Not everything — he didn't know about Signe Holm's cellar, thank God, didn't know about the pamphlet, didn't know about Anton by name or nation or wound — but he knew *something moved*, and he knew a bandaged stranger lived upstairs, and he knew the paper count was wrong.

Two: Joren was greedy, yes, but greed was predictable; you could pay off greed. It was the *hurt* that scared Milo. Greed negotiated. Hurt testified. Hurt wanted to be seen, and there was nothing a man with grievances wouldn't say to be seen, especially by men whose whole profession was seeing people.

Three: Milo could not fix this alone, and telling Ulyana felt like handing her a lit match while she stood in a room full of everything she loved.

He climbed the stairs to his little room above the shop at half past ten and lay on his bed fully clothed, listening to the building breathe. Below him, faintly, the tick of the composing stone as Ulyana worked late — he could always hear her, the soft metal music of her sorting, quicker than anyone's, quicker than his own and he'd never told her that because some truths you keep like coins under the tongue. Through the wall, Mrs. Eriksen's clock, which ran four minutes fast and lied about it steadily. And somewhere below, behind the locked door of the back room, Anton's cough — quieter this week, healing, alive.

They were all so busy being brave, Milo thought. Ulyana brave with her chin up, Anton brave through his pain, Miss Signe brave in her cellar with grease to her elbows. Nobody had assigned Milo the bravery role. His role had always been to notice things and keep them, the way you keep spare type in a job case — sorted, ready, unremarked.

Well. Here was something to keep.

Except keeping wasn't enough anymore, was it? That was the whole point of everything they were doing, the pamphlet and the risk and the coffin trick with the galleys — that truth shut in a case was just metal. It had to be *set*. It had to go out into the world and justify itself line by line.

So he would tell her. Tomorrow. First thing, before the kettle, before the manifests. He'd say it plainly: *My cousin has a loose tongue and a grudge and friends with polished buttons, and we need to move the schedule up.*

Satisfied, he began, at last, to drift toward sleep.

Which was when he heard the knock at the loading door.

Not the coded knock — three-and-two, which Anton used, which Signe used. This was two knocks, flat and official, spaced like a signature. Then a pause. Then two more, identical, patient, the knock of a man who knew the door would open eventually and was prepared to stand there being right about it.

Milo sat up in the dark, heart slamming, and through the floorboards heard Ulyana's sorting stop mid-word, one letter lifted and never set down.

From the street, muffled by cobbles and drizzle, came the voice Milo had last heard laughing in the Salt & Anchor — but sober now, and careful, and carrying anyway, pitched exactly loud enough to reach every room in the building:

"Evening! Message for the Eriksen shop — from Inspector Brandt, with his compliments. He says to tell the lady: *the manifest doesn't balance, and he's begun counting.*"

Silence. Footsteps receding. Rain.

Milo lay back down and stared at the ceiling and thought, with a strange floating clarity, *too late, too late, I was too late by one whole evening* — and beneath that, colder, the thought he'd carry into tomorrow and every day after: that Joren hadn't needed to betray them at all. He'd simply needed to be *asked*. And somewhere in this city, tonight, men with offices had learned that asking worked.
