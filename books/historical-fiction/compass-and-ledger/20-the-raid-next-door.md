## 20. The Raid Next Door

The boots woke us before the shouting did.

I had fallen asleep at the composing stone again, cheek on a proof sheet that had left faint letters mirrored on my skin — Anton had laughed at them earlier that evening, pressed his thumb to the reversed *e* on my jaw and said I was becoming typography itself. Now it was past two in the morning, and the sound coming through the floorboards of the shop was not laughter or anything like it. It was boots. Dozens of them. The particular heavy rhythm of men who have been given permission to break things.

Anton was awake instantly. That was something I had learned about soldiers: they slept shallow, like cats in a house where dogs are expected. He was on his feet with his hand already on the satchel before my mind finished assembling the thought *what is that noise.*

"The bindery," Milo whispered from the doorway of his cot-room, hair wild, eyes enormous. "It's coming from Voss's place."

He was right. Through the front windows, by the light of lanterns held at arm's length, I could see figures moving across the cobbles toward the shop of Herr Voss, bookbinder, who for thirty years had glued spines and grumbled about the price of calfskin and once, when I was nine, made me a tiny blank book with my name stamped in gold because I had admired his tools too openly. A good man. A dull man, I had always thought, in the kindest possible sense. A man who had never in his life done anything more radical than complain about a late delivery.

They were going to break down his door.

"Cellar," Anton said. It was not a suggestion.

* * *

We went down with the lamp turned to its lowest wick, into the room beneath the shop that smelled of cold earth and the year's last potatoes. Mother was already there — she had come down ahead of us, wrapped in her shawl, moving with the eerie competence of the ill who know exactly how much strength each stair costs and refuse to waste a step. She had brought a chair for herself and a blanket for all of us, as though we were gathering for a story rather than hiding from one.

"Sit," she said, in the voice she used when the doctor visited and wanted to fuss. "All of you. If you must hover, hover lower."

Milo sat against the wall nearest the coal chute, which had the single virtue of being the only spot in the cellar where a boy could hear the street clearly. I knelt beside him without deciding to. Anton stayed standing, back to the wall, and I noticed he had positioned himself between the stairs and the rest of us without appearing to think about it at all.

Above us, the world ended politely, then impolitely.

First came the hammering on Voss's door — three sharp official knocks, then voices, then, when no one answered quickly enough for soldiers' tastes, the splintering crash of wood that has lost its argument with an iron bar. Mother's hand found mine in the dark. Hers was very steady. Mine was not, and she held it anyway.

"They'll search the whole row next," Milo whispered. "That's what they do. Door to door."

"Not tonight," Anton said quietly. "Listen. They came for something specific. If it were a sweep, there'd be a list read aloud first. There'd be procedure." He said the word *procedure* the way a man says the name of a country he has fled. "This is a hunt."

And then, muffled by floorboards and forty feet of night air, we heard Herr Voss's voice.

I want to set down what it sounded like, because I have thought about it nearly every day since. It sounded confused. That was the unbearable part. Not defiant, not frightened into silence — just the ordinary bewilderment of a man being pulled out of his bed and unable to locate the mistake. *"But this is a bindery,"* he kept saying, louder and louder, as though repetition might repair the situation. *"Gentlemen, this is a bindery. Look at the books. Look at them, they're hymnals—"*

One of the soldiers told him to shut up. Another laughed. Something heavy hit the floor above his shop, and I heard — we all heard — the sound of a press being kicked over. Milo flinched as though struck himself. A printer does not need to see a press fall to feel it.

"They've got the wrong man," I said. My own voice surprised me; it came out flat and certain. "Voss wouldn't print so much as a birthday card without a permit. He alphabetizes his glue brushes."

"He has paper," Anton said. "He has equipment. In times like these, having equipment is the crime. They don't need to be right, Ulyana. They need to be seen doing something."

I understood him. That was the terrible thing. Six months ago I would have called such talk paranoid — history happening elsewhere, to grandees, to capitals, to people in newspapers. But I had spent weeks now diverting misprinted stock and forging passes and lying fluently to Inspector Brandt over tea, and so I understood perfectly well that the soldiers upstairs were not hunting a criminal. They were performing vigilance, and Herr Voss had simply been standing in the theater when the curtain rose.

* * *

We listened for the better part of an hour. It is strange, what you catalog in the dark while bad things happen overhead. I remember the exact pitch of the lantern handle scraping Voss's iron railing. I remember Milo counting under his breath — he counted when he was frightened, always had, and got to somewhere past four hundred before he stopped. I remember Mother, at some point, beginning very softly to say the opening lines of an old evening psalm, not as prayer exactly but as ballast, and Anton closing his eyes to listen with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

Then the door of the bindery banged open one final time, and the voices moved out onto the cobbles, and we heard the thing we had been dreading without naming it: the specific scuffle-and-drag of a man being walked fast by two men holding his arms.

"No," Milo breathed. "No, they can't — he's sixty years old, he's got a bad hip, he can't even—"

"Milo." Anton's voice was low and hard. "Sit still."

"But we could do something. Open the door, say he's our neighbor, vouch for—"

"And then they take four of us instead of one," Anton said. "You saw the satchel, Milo. You know what's in this cellar. You'd be trading Voss for all of us, and the pamphlet, and everything your mother's silver bought." He paused, and I heard him choose his next words carefully, which he almost always did. "There are worse things than sitting still. I've done most of them."

Milo sat. But I put my arm around his shoulders anyway, because being right is cold comfort when you're sixteen and the only adult you trust within earshot is telling you to listen to a neighbor be taken away.

Through the coal chute, in fragments, came the last of it. Voss's protests had changed key — confusion giving way to something smaller and worse, a kind of pleading dignity. *"My wife is at her sister's. Someone will have to tell her. Someone will have to feed the cat."* And a soldier's voice, bored rather than cruel, which was somehow crueler: *"Someone will sort it out after. If you're innocent, you'll be released."*

*If.* I felt that word settle into my chest like a swallowed stone.

The boots receded toward the harbor barracks. A wagon rattled away. Then the street was empty, and the silence that replaced the noise was so complete it rang.

* * *

Nobody moved for a long time. Then Mother, who had survived a husband's false death report and a war economy and my father's silences and her own lungs' slow treachery, said: "Well. Someone will indeed have to feed the cat."

It was absurd and it broke the spell. Milo made a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh. Even Anton exhaled.

"I mean it," Mother continued, adjusting her shawl with terrible practicality. "Mrs. Voss is at her sister's in Halden, and the creature will starve among the ruins of the front parlor. And more to the point, someone will have to tell Mrs. Voss what happened, and look after the shop, because if that premises sits dark and unclaimed, the garrison will requisition it inside a fortnight, and then nothing of Voss comes back at all. Grief is a luxury, children. Presence is a duty."

"She's right," Anton said slowly. I watched the calculation move across his face — risk weighed against risk, the arithmetic he performed constantly and hated performing. "If neighbors see the Eriksen shop looking after the Voss bindery, it reads as innocence. Solid citizens minding a friend's affairs. It may even protect us."

"It may," Mother agreed serenely. "And if it doesn't protect us, it will at least have been the correct thing to do, which is more than can be said for most strategies."

We came up out of the cellar into a shop that seemed impossibly intact. Lamplight, type cases, the smell of ink — the small republic we had built, still standing. Across the yard, Voss's windows gaped. His door hung off one hinge. From where I stood at our loading door I could see straight into his front room, lit by the moon now, and it looked like a museum of a life examined roughly: drawers pulled out and upended, his workbench swept clean of tools onto the floor, the great binding press knocked askew on its bolts, and everywhere the pale litter of torn endpapers drifting like the aftermath of a very rude snowfall.

I crossed the yard before I had decided to. Anton followed. So did Milo, and behind him, wrapped in her shawl despite my protest, my mother.

Inside the bindery, the smell was Voss's own — paste and leather and the faint vinegar of his hideous lunchtime herring — and it undid me more than the wreckage did. Shops hold their people. Anyone who works with their hands knows this. This room knew him: his chalk marks on the bench, his spectacles folded neatly on the shelf where the soldiers had apparently deemed spectacles beneath suspicion.

Under the bench, wedged in the shadow where the leg met the wall, I saw a small shape. I reached in and drew out the tiny blank book he had made me when I was nine. *ULYANA,* it said in gold. The soldiers had missed it, or dismissed it.

"He gave you that?" Milo asked softly.

"For admiring his tools too loudly." My voice cracked on the word *tools*, which was humiliating, and I let it crack anyway.

We did what could be done that night, which was little enough. We closed his door as best the hinge allowed. We righted his chair. Milo found the cat — an enormous gray tom named Bismarck, indignant and unharmed, lodged behind the stove — and carried him across the yard to our cellar with the solemnity of a state ceremony. Mother wrote a note to be sent to Mrs. Voss in Halden in the morning, wording it with the gentle honesty she believed people were owed: *taken for questioning, premises under our care, cat fed.*

And I stood a moment longer in the middle of that ruined room, holding a child's blank book, and understood the lesson the cellar had been teaching all along, the one I had resisted learning for twenty-three comfortable years. I had thought staying invisible was safety. It was only safety deferred, and paid for by whoever stood closest when the boots arrived. Tonight it had been Voss. The ledger balances, whether or not we write in it.

Back in our shop, Anton banked the stove and looked at me across the low flame.

"This will happen to us," he said. No drama in it. Just fact, laid on the table like a proof sheet. "Sooner, now, because of tonight. Sooner because Signe Holm is pulling copies off her press every day, and because Milo's cousin talks when he drinks, and because Brandt counts his paper stock like a moneylender. I want you to hear me say it plainly while the sound of Voss's door is still fresh: they will come here."

I looked at the case where tomorrow's manifest type waited in its stick, orderly and patient. At the cellar door behind which three thousand sheets of diverted paper lay hidden. At my mother's chair. At the man who had carried a dead man's letters to my loading door and changed the length of my life.

"Then we'd best finish the pamphlet before they do," I said.

Anton nodded slowly. But I saw him glance once toward the window, toward the north of the city, where a cellar press ran in the dark under Signe Holm's respectable house — and I saw in his face that he had already begun counting the cost, and that the column marked *Signe* had just grown heavier than either of us was prepared to carry.

Two days later, we learned how right he had been. But that morning, we only swept the glass from Voss's threshold, fed the cat, and tried to believe that sitting still had saved anything at all.
