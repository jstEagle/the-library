## 21. Signe Pays

The knock came at half past six in the morning, which was the hour when respectable people were lighting their stoves and the less respectable were finally going to bed, and it was therefore the hour the police had chosen, because Inspector Brandt's people understood timing the way compositors understood kerning — that everything depended on the space between things.

Ulyana heard it from the stairs, where she was carrying down a tray of coffee for Anton, and she heard the second knock, the one that wasn't a knock at all but the flat of a boot against the door of the cellar flat on Krähengasse where Signe Holm had lived above her press for thirty-one years.

She did not go to the window. She had learned that much by now. She stood on the stairs with the coffee tray in her hands and listened to the city do a small, efficient violence two streets away, and she counted the sounds: the door, the voices, a woman's voice — Signe's, level and dry as ever, the voice of a woman who had once told a customs officer that his mother would be ashamed of his arithmetic — and then the sounds stopped being words and became furniture. Something heavy dragged across a floor. Something metal went down steps. A press, being carried out of a cellar, makes a particular complaint, and Ulyana knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat.

Milo came out of the type room, saw her face, saw the tray, and put his hand flat against the wall as if the building had tilted.

"That's the Holm cellar," he said.

"Yes."

"They're taking the press."

"Yes."

He was quiet a moment. "Should we — I mean, is there anything we—"

"No," Ulyana said, and hated the word, and said it again anyway. "No. There's nothing. If you go out that door this morning, you're a witness, and witnesses get remembered, and we cannot afford to be remembered this week."

Milo looked at the coffee tray as though it were an accusation. Perhaps it was. She carried it back up the stairs and set it down outside Anton's door without knocking, because knocking had come to mean something between them that she hadn't the time to examine, and went back down to the shop and opened the front shutters and set the day's type as though the world were a place where shipping manifests mattered.

Her hands were steady. That was the worst of it. Three months ago her hands would have shaken. Now they knew the work and they did it, and grief and fear had been filed away somewhere in the muscle memory, between the em-quads and the brass spacing, and she could not find them when she needed them.

* * *

Anton came down at nine, gray-faced, moving like a man whose body had been borrowed from someone older. He had heard it too; everyone on the street had heard it; the soldiers had made no effort at quiet, because quiet was not the message. The message was the noise.

"They took her at the door," he said. "I went as far as the corner. There was a van. Two men carried the press out between them — the small one, her proofing press, and then the big Heidelberg came apart in the doorway and they cursed at it." He sat down heavily on the composing stone, which no one was ever allowed to sit on, and Ulyana did not tell him to get off, which told him everything about the morning. "She saw me. At the corner. Across the street."

"And?"

"She looked at me the way you look at a bill you intend to pay." He put his hands over his face. "She knew what it cost. She knew when she agreed, and she did it anyway, and that is worse. If she had been a fool I could at least have — but Signe Holm has never been a fool in her life."

"Anton." Ulyana took the galleys off the stone beside him and began, without quite deciding to, to distribute the type back into the case, letter by letter, because her hands wanted occupation and because the sound of it — the small iron music of it — made it possible to speak. "Tell me what happened. Not the feelings. The facts. The feelings can wait until the facts have had their say."

He lowered his hands. She had learned that about him: he was a man who could be governed, if you governed him gently. It was the soldier in him, she supposed, and she was learning not to flinch from that word.

"The landlady's nephew," he said. "The one who carried coal for Signe. He was drunk on Tuesday — you heard about that, Milo's cousin heard it from him — and he let slip that something moved through the Holm cellar at night. He didn't know what. He didn't know why. But he knew the cellar door was locked on Tuesdays and Thursdays when it had never been locked before, and he knew there was a man who came after dark who didn't walk like a fishmonger." Anton's mouth twisted. "That's all he knew. And it was enough. Because they weren't looking for what we were printing. They were looking for anything at all, and a locked door is a confession in this city now."

"So it wasn't the pamphlet."

"It was the pamphlet. It was always the pamphlet. It just didn't need to be *named* to be found." He stood, and winced, and stood anyway. "They'll have found the rehearsal proofs. We burned the bad pulls, but the good ones — Signe kept two, she said a printer keeps her proofs, she said it like a law of nature—"

"Because it is one," Ulyana said quietly.

"Then they have her handwriting on the margin next to your father's testimony, and they have her press, and by tonight they'll have her in a room with a man whose job is arithmetic, and Signe Holm is seventy years old and her heart is not what her will is." He was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the length of the shop. "I brought this to her cellar. I stood in her kitchen and asked a seventy-year-old woman to commit treason because I had nowhere else—"

"You asked a *master printer*," Ulyana said, "to print. What did you imagine she'd say?"

"I imagined she'd say no! I hoped she'd say no! A decent man hopes to be refused!"

"Well," said Ulyana, "then you've been carrying the wrong hope in your pocket for some time, because Signe Holm printed pamphlets against the customs union in 1905 and lost three fingers to a press accident doing it, and she told me herself that the only thing she regrets about 1905 is the kerning. She was never going to say no. You didn't make her do anything. You gave her the thing she'd been waiting for, and if you apologize for that at her trial I will never forgive you, because she'd find it insulting."

Anton stopped pacing. He looked at her with an expression she couldn't read — something between gratitude and grief and the particular loneliness of a man who has just been told he is not the author of the world's suffering.

"You sound like her," he said finally.

"Someone has to, for a while."

* * *

The argument — because it was coming, and they both knew it was coming, and there is a kind of mercy in letting a thing arrive on schedule — began that evening in the back room, after Milo had gone home and Mrs. Eriksen had gone up to bed, and it began, as the worst arguments do, over something small.

Milo had left the evening's proofs in a stack on the stone, and Anton had picked them up, read the first page, and set them down again with his thumb pressed white against the paper.

"We should stop," he said.

Ulyana, who was cleaning the rollers, did not stop cleaning the rollers. "We should not."

"Ulyana."

"Anton."

"Signe Holm is in a cell on the Vesterbro with a heart that could stop from fright alone, and her press is in a warehouse being catalogued as evidence, and every hour we keep going we spend more of other people. Milo. Your mother. The fishmonger who's going to carry bundles for us. The choir." He turned the proof sheet over, as if the second page might argue his case better than the first. "I have done the arithmetic. I'm good at arithmetic. It's what they trained me for — how many men a position costs, how many a retreat costs. And this—" he tapped the proof, "—this costs people. Real ones. With names. And I cannot make the column balance. I have tried. It does not balance."

Ulyana set down the roller and wiped her hands very slowly on her apron, because she wanted the pause to be as long as he found uncomfortable.

"Whose arithmetic is it?" she asked.

"What?"

"The arithmetic you're doing. Who taught it to you?"

He blinked. "The army. The staff. What does that—"

"Because it's *their* arithmetic, Anton. That's my whole point. That ledger where you weigh a pamphlet against a woman's heart, where you put lives in one column and truth in the other and ask which is heavier — that's General Halvard's ledger. That's the ledger of men who decided in 1915 that a village was an acceptable loss and a report was an acceptable forgery, and they wrote it all down in columns just like that, and the columns balanced beautifully, and that's why we're printing at all." She took the proof out of his hands, gently, and held it up. "You don't get to borrow their arithmetic to talk yourself out of undoing them. It doesn't work that way. It was never meant to work that way. That's the trick of it — they taught the whole world to count in their currency, so that every honest man who tries to do the sums concludes the truth is too expensive. The truth isn't expensive. It's the only thing that was ever free. It's the *lie* that costs. The lie is what put Signe in that cell. The pamphlet is just the invoice coming due."

Anton sat down. It was not a graceful sitting; his legs simply stopped participating.

"You think I don't know that," he said, after a while. "You think I don't lie awake doing exactly that sum in reverse. But Ulyana — it's easy for you to say *free*. You haven't—" He stopped. She watched him decide, in real time, whether to say it. "I stood in a farmyard outside Drenow and I held a rifle and I did nothing, and the arithmetic they gave me said the farmyard was acceptable, and I *believed* it, for eleven minutes I believed it, and then I didn't, and by then it was too late for the people in the farmyard. So don't tell me the truth is free. I know exactly what it costs, because I've seen the bill. I'm trying to keep you off the bill."

"And you think stopping keeps me off it?" Ulyana crouched down so she was level with him, so he had to look at her. "Anton. If we stop, Signe Holm is still in the cell. Her press is still in the warehouse. Milo is still a boy who knows too much. I am still a girl who has read her father's testimony and can never unread it. Stopping doesn't take anyone off the bill. Stopping just means we pay it and get nothing. That's the only column that matters now — not what it costs, but what it *buys*. And what it buys is that Signe's arrest is not the end of the story. If we stop, they took her press and her courage and her fingers' worth of stubbornness, and got exactly what they wanted, and she'll know it from her cell, and that will be the cruelest thing we could do to her. If we go on, then she's not a woman who was caught. She's a woman who was *heard*. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference, and she would tell you so herself if her heart would let her climb the stairs."

The clock in the shop ticked. Somewhere upstairs her mother's floor creaked, the familiar small sound of a woman turning over toward the wall.

"You're not afraid," Anton said. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost a question, almost a wonder.

"I'm afraid all the time," Ulyana said. "I'm afraid so thoroughly that fear has become a kind of weather. You don't argue with weather. You dress for it and you go out." She stood, and offered him her hand, and after a moment he took it and let her pull him up. "Signe told me something the night of the rehearsal run, when the neighbor knocked and we sat in the dark smelling of ink like guilty children. She said: *a press is just a promise that keeps working while you sleep*. They've taken her press. They haven't taken the promise. We're the promise now, Anton. All of us. So we don't stop. We work faster."

* * *

Milo came back at eleven, which was against every rule they had made, and he came back because he had heard something, and he stood in the doorway of the back room with his cap in his hands and snow melting on his shoulders, and he said, in the voice of a boy trying very hard to be a courier of bad news and not the cause of it:

"The warehouse on Tannhauser Quay. Where they took the press. My cousin's foreman got the inventory job — cataloguing seized material, good money, he says. And he says the manifest lists the Holm press and the type and the galleys, but there's a second page. A separate order. Signed this morning."

Anton was on his feet. "Signed by whom?"

"That's the thing." Milo swallowed. "It's not a seizure order. Seizures get filed with the magistrate. This one's marked *Ministry of Armistice Affairs*. And the foreman says the crates going onto that page aren't for storage." He looked up, and his face in the doorway's lamplight was very young and very certain of what he was about to say. "They're for the fortress. Everything's going to the fortress. And the foreman heard the sergeant say the whole lot is scheduled for the furnace Friday — except the galleys. The galleys are going to somebody's desk."

Ulyana looked at Anton. Anton looked at the proofs on the stone — her father's testimony, Signe's marginal hand, the whole fragile argument of it — and then at her, and she saw the arithmetic running behind his eyes one more time, and this time she watched the columns come out differently.

"Friday," Anton said. "How many days is that?"

Milo didn't have to count. "Four."
