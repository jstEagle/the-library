## 22. Armistice Terms Leak

The paper came on Tuesday, and it was wrong.

Ulyana knew it before she knew it, the way a compositor knows a misaligned forme by the sound of the press alone — some small wrongness in the rhythm of the day that the mind takes a moment to catch up to. Milo had carried the bundle up from the cellar where it had waited since the theft, wrapped in sacking, and dropped it on the stone with the thud of a thing that weighed more than paper should.

"That's all of it?" she asked.

"All that was worth saving. The rest was waterlogged. Rats had been at some of it." He wiped his hands on his apron, leaving grey streaks. "Rats with opinions, possibly. There were pages about drainage assessments."

"Thrilling."

"There was one bit," Milo said, and here he hesitated, which was unlike him; Milo's relationship with hesitation was roughly the same as a cat's relationship with rain, "where the columns didn't line up. Same paper as the manifests, same watermark even, but the type is different. Finer. And there's no job number."

Ulyana set down her composing stick.

Everything that passed through the state press house bore a job number the way every cow in Vessengard bore the estate's mark. She had set thousands of such numbers herself over the years, filling orders that arrived on official letterhead and left as shipping manifests, customs schedules, notices about the price of coal. The numbers were how the government kept its ledger of itself. Paper without a number was either a mistake or a secret, and the state press house did not make mistakes. It was too well fed for mistakes.

"Show me."

He showed her. And she saw at once what he had meant about the type: it was finer, set tight and confident, the work of a senior hand — someone who had been given something important and told to be careful with it. She lifted a sheet to the window. The watermark matched the manifest stock exactly, which meant it had been pulled on the same day's run, which meant —

"They printed them together," she said slowly. "The manifests and this. Same press, same day. Someone used the manifest order as cover to run these off, and then the whole lot got spoiled — or misdirected — or stolen before anyone could sort it."

"Stolen," Milo said, with dignity. "By us."

"By us," she agreed. "Which makes it worse. Or better. I genuinely don't know."

There were forty-odd sheets of it. She spread them across the imposing stone, weighting the corners with quoins because the stove had a draught in it that took liberties with unsecured paper, and she began to read.

* * *

Anton came down when the light began to fail, moving better than he had two weeks ago but still favouring his left side, still with the careful economy of a man whose body kept a list of grievances. He stood in the doorway a long moment, watching her read, and she felt his attention like a hand on her shoulder.

"You've found something."

"I don't know yet." She held up a sheet. "This is a protocol summary. Delegation positions, proposed terms — it reads like notes toward an actual document, not the document itself. Drafted by someone at the armistice commission." She turned it over. "No name. No number. But look at the date references."

He took it. His eyes moved, and she watched him arrive at the place she had arrived at an hour ago, watched it land on him the way it had landed on her, heavy as wet sand.

"Nine days," Anton said.

"From the drafting date. Which was eleven days ago." She let that sit. "The commission expected terms to be final within a fortnight of drafting. That fortnight is nearly spent."

"The armistice is close." He said it flatly, the way a man says *the ice is thinning* while standing on the ice. "We always knew it was coming."

"We knew it was coming the way you know winter is coming." Ulyana gathered another handful of sheets. "You don't panic in October. But this—" she tapped the paper, "—this is October ending."

Milo, who had been pretending very hard to inventory the type cabinets, gave up the pretence. "Is it bad? The terms?"

"It's a draft," Ulyana said. "Half of it is boilerplate — troop withdrawals, prisoner exchanges, who pays for the bridges. But there are annexes." She found the one she wanted, fourth sheet from the left, and smoothed it on the stone. "Annex Three. Provisional measures regarding accountability for conduct of hostilities during the campaign period."

She heard Anton stop breathing.

"'Conduct of any proceedings against military personnel,'" she read aloud, "'shall be discontinued upon entry into force of the present agreement, and acts committed under lawful command authority during the period specified shall be subject to full and general amnesty, no proceeding to be instituted or resumed thereafter.'"

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs her mother's floorboard creaked, the familiar small music of the house.

"Full and general amnesty," Milo repeated.

"In an annex," Ulyana said. "In the annexes, Milo. Not in the body of the text. Not in anything any delegate will read aloud or any newspaper will reprint. In the fine print, behind a door marked *provisional measures*, written in language so dry you could store gunpowder next to it." Her voice was doing something she didn't like — climbing, sharpening — and she made herself stop, and breathe, and start again. "Every atrocity in my father's pages. Every testimony Anton carried three hundred miles in a satchel against his ribs. One sentence in Annex Three and none of it ever happened. Legally, formally, permanently — never happened."

Anton sat down. Not dramatically; he simply reached the end of what his legs would do, and lowered himself onto the stool by the stone, and looked at his hands.

"He warned me," Anton said. "Your father. One night near the front, when we were — I thought he was being morbid. He said, *They will not deny it. Denial invites inquiry. They will forgive it.*" He turned his hands over, palms up, as if checking whether they still belonged to him. "I argued with him. I said no one could sign away the dead like settling an account."

"He's a bookkeeper," Ulyana said quietly. "He knows how accounts get settled."

* * *

They worked until midnight, because grief is a poor organiser and fear is worse, and the only thing any of them could do with the feeling was turn it into information.

Ulyana sorted. Forty-three sheets, and once you understood their grammar they resolved into a coherent whole: position papers from two delegations, a summary of disputed points, and the annexes — four of them, of which the third was the poison. The type was uniform throughout, which told her the entire packet had been composed by one hand, and the hand was good enough that she found herself reading the setting rather than the words, the way one listens to a voice instead of a sentence. Whoever had set this had been frightened. She could see it in the spacing — slightly generous word gaps, the unconscious caution of a compositor leaving himself room, keeping his distance from the text. You set love letters tight. You set things like this loose.

"Somebody in the state press house has a conscience," Milo said, when she pointed this out.

"Somebody in the state press house is terrified," Ulyana corrected. "Conscience and terror look identical in the spacing. The difference is what happens after." She thought of the missing job number, the manifest order used as a blind. "They ran these off under our cover order hoping the spoilage would bury them. Hoping nobody would ever read the misprints. It's not leaking, exactly. It's — dropping something overboard and pretending it fell."

"And we fished it out," said Milo.

"We fished it out."

Anton had gone quiet in the way she had learned to distrust, his stillness not rest but calculation. Now he laid three sheets side by side on the stone — the annex, a delegation position paper, and a schedule — and drew his finger down each in turn.

"Ten days," he said. "If the drafting timetable holds — and timetables drafted by governments hold better than promises made to people — the commission convenes to initial the final text nine days from now. Signing follows within a day or two of that. Call it ten."

"Ten days to do what?" Milo asked. It was the obvious question and everyone had been avoiding it, and now it sat on the stone among the papers like a cat that had invited itself.

"To finish the pamphlet," Ulyana said.

"To do more than finish it." Anton looked up. "A pamphlet that appears after the signing is a complaint. A pamphlet that appears before it is a bomb. If these terms are amnestying Halvard while the city still has a say — while the delegations can still be embarrassed, while the crowds in the square still have ten days to learn what they're cheering — then the pamphlet isn't a record anymore. It's an intervention." He paused. "Your father understood this too. Why do you think he stayed? Why do you think he collected testimonies for two years instead of walking to a neutral consulate and telling his story to a comfortable chair? Because a story told late is a story filed. A story told now is a story that can change what gets signed."

Ulyana looked at the annex sheet again. *Full and general amnesty.* She thought about the phrase the way she thought about a badly kerned line — turning it, finding the flaw. An amnesty required a signature. A signature required men willing to put their names beneath it. And names, she thought, with the sudden cold clarity of her trade, could be printed beside other names.

"Halvard signs this," she said, "and he walks out of the war clean."

"Halvard dictates this," Anton said, "and walks out crowned."

* * *

Her mother came down at one in the morning, which she did sometimes now — appeared at the top of the cellar stairs in her dressing gown, descended with the slow deliberateness of a woman who refused to be hurried by her own lungs, and settled onto the cot they kept down there as if she had been summoned, though no one had called her.

"You'll ruin your eyes," she said, which was what she always said, and which was not, Ulyana had come to understand, about eyes at all. It meant: *I see you. I know you are awake and afraid and doing something about it.*

"Mother. It's late."

"It's late everywhere, dear. That's no reason to be rude about it locally." Astrid Eriksen peered at the papers strewn across the stone, and her gaze, dimmed though it was, snagged on the annex the way a practised reader's gaze will snag on the one important page in a pile. "What have you caught?"

Ulyana considered lying, and had learned too much about lies lately — their weight, their maintenance costs, their terrible fluency — to spend another. She handed her mother the sheet and summarized, plainly: the armistice, ten days, the amnesty, the fine print.

Her mother read it twice. Then she folded her hands on her lap over the paper, the way she used to fold them over a hymnal, and was silent for a long moment.

"When your father proposed to me," she said at last, "he did it in writing. A letter, delivered by hand, though we lived on the same street. And at the bottom, in small print, he'd added a clause — *the undersigned reserves the right to be wrong about everything except you.*" Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "I laughed until I cried, and then I signed it, and I have spent forty years discovering which half of the clause mattered more."

"Mother—"

"Governments propose the same way," Astrid said. "Everything important hidden at the bottom, in small print, where the laughing happens later." She gave the sheet back. "So. Ten days."

"Ten days," Ulyana confirmed.

"Then you'll want the good lamp oil, not the cheap stuff. Your father always said fine work goes blind on cheap oil." She rose, unhurried, gathering her dressing gown about her, and paused at the foot of the stairs. "And Ulyana. Whatever you're printing — print it large enough to read. The whole trick of small print is that it assumes nobody looks. Be somebody who looks."

She went up. The floorboards spoke her passage, room by room, back to bed.

Anton was staring at the stairwell. "She's remarkable."

"She's terrifying," said Ulyana. "It's the same thing, done well."

* * *

At two in the morning, with Milo asleep in the chair he always pretended he wasn't sleeping in, Ulyana stood at the composing stone with the proof of their own pamphlet — the first proof, pulled five days ago, read aloud among them like scripture — and laid it beside Annex Three.

Her father's testimony on the left. The amnesty clause on the right. Between them lay ten days.

She thought about what Milo had asked — *ten days to do what?* — and understood that the question had a second half nobody had said aloud. Ten days to finish the pamphlet, yes. To print it, distribute it, seed it through fish carts and choir lofts and whatever routes remained. But also: ten days in which Inspector Brandt might return, in which the cousin's drunken slip might travel from tavern to garrison, in which Signe Holm sat in a cell while her press gathered dust in a police warehouse, in which every hour was a door closing somewhere in the city without their hearing it.

And underneath everything, patient as groundwater: her father. Alive, as far as anyone knew, in the fortress on the hill. Waiting on the same clock.

She set the annex sheet in the chase beside the proof, just to see them together — the accusation and the acquittal, arranged in the same frame, the way history actually happened: not one truth defeating another, but types competing for the same page, and only so many lines that fit, and someone had to decide what got left off.

That was the part they never taught you about printing, she thought. Every act of composition was an act of exclusion. Somebody chose. Somebody always chose.

She picked up her composing stick.

"Anton," she said quietly, and he woke the way soldiers wake — all at once, silently, already upright. "How fast can you find out which delegations can still be embarrassed?"

He smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile. "Ambassador Lindqvist drinks at the Harbourside Club and cannot hold his brandy. Why?"

"Because in nine days they're going to initial this." She tapped Annex Three. "And I want at least one of those initials preceded by a man who knows what he's initialing. Get me names, Anton. Everyone who touches this document between now and then — clerks, couriers, secretaries. Anyone who might prefer a copy of it existing outside the commission's files."

"That's a great deal of asking," Anton said.

"Yes," Ulyana said. "We have ten days to ask the largest question there is, and I intend to be extremely rude about it locally."

Outside, the harbour bell counted three, and the clock started.
