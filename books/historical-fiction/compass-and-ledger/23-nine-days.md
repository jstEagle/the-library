## 23. Nine Days

The map went up on the wall of the locked back room at half past six in the morning, and by seven it had already acquired its first argument.

"We are not using the fish carts," Milo said, arms crossed, standing in front of the great sheet of brown paper that Ulyana had pinned up beside the drying rack. "I want it noted for posterity that I said we should not use the fish carts."

"Noted," Ulyana said, without looking up from the table where she was sorting three thousand proof sheets into stacks of fifty. "Posterity has noted it. Posterity also notes that the fish carts are the only vehicles in Vessengard that move before dawn without being stopped."

"They smell," Milo said.

"They smell of fish."

"They smell of *fish*, Ulyana. Whole streets smell of them. If a patrol stops a cart and finds bundles wrapped in oilcloth under the herring crates—"

"Then the patrol will conclude what every patrol in this city has concluded since the year dot," said Signe Holm from the doorway, arriving with a basket of rolls and the air of a woman who had been arguing with young men since before their fathers were apprenticed, "namely that it is a fish cart, and that no one in the history of tyranny has ever been arrested for transporting herring." She set the basket down on the ledger table, which was now permanently given over to the cause and no longer saw a legitimate invoice. "Take a roll. Sit down. We have nine days and you are spending the first one's best hour on the question of smell."

Anton was already at the wall, one shoulder still stiff under his bandages, marking routes in grease pencil. He had been at it since five, Ulyana knew; she had heard him moving about below her floorboards while she lay awake composing and discarding plans the way she composed and discarded lines of type, setting them up, seeing they didn't balance, distributing them back into the case.

"Here," he said, tapping the harbor district. "The fish carts come in from the north quays at four in the morning, load at five, and disperse to the market streets by six. Old Vasel owes Signe a favor."

"I collect favors the way other women collect lace," Signe said. "Vasel will take four bundles per cart, no more, and he will not take them past the Ringstrasse, because he says the market inspectors there look into carts, which is a lie — they look into nothing but their own lunch pails — but he is seventy years old and frightened, so we honor it."

"Four bundles per cart," Anton said, writing it. "Six carts. Twenty-four bundles in the harbor district before the city wakes." He stepped back and looked at the map the way Ulyana had seen him look at it all night: not satisfied, but counting. "The choir lofts?"

"That's mine," Milo said, and could not keep the pride out of it, and did not especially try.

* * *

The choir lofts had been Milo's idea entirely, and he had come by it honestly, which is to say through the least heroic possible chain of reasoning. His cousin Astrid sang in the Lutherplatz church choir; Astrid's choir rehearsed on Wednesdays and Saturdays; the choir loft of the Lutherplatz church overlooked a vestibule through which some eleven hundred people passed every Sunday; and, as Milo had put it on the first night of planning, with the sudden blazing certainty of a boy who has discovered that his ordinary life contains hidden machinery, "nobody looks *up*. Nobody ever looks up. You could hang a flag up there and nobody would find it until Easter."

By the second day of the nine, there were bundles in four lofts across the city — Lutherplatz, Saint Ansgar's, the fishermen's chapel on the south shore, and the Mennonite hall, whose elders had been told nothing whatsoever and whose sexton, a deacon of few words named Poul, had accepted one closed word from Signe Holm over a shared pipe and asked no questions forever after.

"The trick," Milo explained now, pacing before the map with a stick of chalk he'd taken to carrying like a conductor's baton, "is the hymn boards. You know, the boards they put up with the numbers of the hymns on them? Every church has them. Well." He drew a small rectangle in the air. "A bundle fits behind a hymn board if you flatten it properly. Ulyana showed me how to wrap them flat. And then on Sunday morning — or Saturday evening, at the rehearsal, which is better, because the sextons go home early on Saturdays — somebody who is legitimately in that building for a legitimate reason takes the bundle down, puts it in their music case, and walks out with the rest of the choir."

"And your singers know what they're carrying?" Ulyana asked. She had learned, in these two days, to ask the questions an inspector would ask, and it gave her no pleasure how good she was getting at it.

"Astrid knows. Astrid knows everything, she always has, I don't know why we bother keeping secrets in this family." Milo flipped his chalk, caught it. "The others just think it's choir business. Mrs. Iversen thinks Astrid is running a clothing collection for the widows' fund. Which, in fairness, she also is. Astrid says a good lie needs true walls to stand in."

There was a short silence in the back room, the particular silence of four people deciding whether to laugh at something that deserved laughing at and might get them all imprisoned.

"*Walls*," Signe said at last, delicately, and bit into her roll, and they laughed, quietly, the way people laugh in cellars during bombardments — briefly, gratefully, and with both hands pressed flat on the table.

* * *

The passes were the part that frightened Ulyana most, and Milo's part too, and perhaps that was fitting.

The armistice preparations had filled Vessengard with paperwork. Movement permits for carters. Vendor licenses for the square. Clergy credentials for the services that would surround the signing. Press passes — those were beyond them — but above all, in the last days before any great ceremony, the city sprouted a whole flora of small printed documents, each one a rectangle of authority, each one issued by an office whose clerks were drowning, and each one, crucially, printed somewhere, by someone, on machines exactly like hers.

"They're reprinting the carter permits Tuesday," Ulyana said, laying a specimen on the table — a genuine one, obtained by Signe from a carter who owed her money and expected never to see it again. "The government shop on Kirkegade. Same stock as ours now, since we've been eating through the misprint crate. Same size. Look at the border."

Milo leaned over it. He had been studying borders for two days with the devotion of a monk at scripture. The permit carried a decorative rule around its edge, a repeating pattern of small diamonds, and at each corner a fleuron — the little leaf-shaped ornaments that sat in every printer's type case like punctuation waiting to be used.

"Diamond rule, twelve point," he recited. "Fleuron number thirty-one in the Caslon case, turned ninety degrees, because the clerk's office doesn't know it's supposed to sit upright. They've been printing it sideways since 1911 and nobody's ever told them." He looked up, and there was something in his face Ulyana had not often seen there: the particular gravity of a craftsman taking his own work seriously for the first time. "I can match it. I can match it so well the clerk who issued the original couldn't tell them apart."

"You'll need a stamp," Anton said. "The office seal."

"The office seal is a rubber handstamp," Milo said, "ordered from Berg & Son, stamp-makers, Bremerholm 4, and I know this because I set the confirmation card for the order myself last spring when Father—" He stopped. The sentence had run him straight into his father's name, the way sentences still did, all of theirs, a hazard in the road they'd all learned to swerve for and sometimes chose not to. He took a breath and finished it differently. "When the shop did work for the clerk's office. Before."

"Berg & Son still exists," Signe said mildly. "And Berg & Son does not ask the purpose of a stamp. Stamp-makers never do. It would be bad for trade."

So it was arranged that a handstamp would be commissioned, cash, no receipt, and that Milo would spend his evenings carving a duplicate date-plug out of a cork stopper with Ulyana's gravers, because the date on the passes changed daily and a forger must be able to change with it. By the third night his fingers were stained black to the knuckle and he held the cork up to the lamp with the expression of a jeweler presenting a flawed diamond.

"It's not perfect," he said. "Look, the tail of the nine is too thick."

Ulyana took it from him and examined it under the lamp longer than she needed to, because she remembered being sixteen and holding her first imperfect thing up to her father, and she knew exactly what the correct answer weighed. "The tail of the nine is too thick," she agreed, "and the clerk's office will never notice, because the clerk's office sets its dates from a worn plug that's been dropping ink since summer. Yours is better than the real thing. That's the danger, actually — make it worse."

"Worse," Milo repeated, delighted. "You want my forgery to be *worse*."

"I want it to be believed in," Ulyana said. "Perfection gets questioned. Mediocrity gets filed."

She caught Anton smiling at her from across the room, and realized she had just quoted her father without meaning to, one of the shop sayings, one of the things he used to say over the case with a cigarette in the corner of his mouth, and that it had come out of her as naturally as breathing. Something moved through her that was not quite grief and not quite inheritance but had elements of both, and she bent back over the passes and said nothing about it, and saw Anton understand that she was saying nothing about it, and loved him a little for letting her.

* * *

On the fourth day it rained, which was a mercy, because rain kept patrols under awnings and made citizens hurrying along with their heads down invisible to one another, and there was much to move.

They worked out the arithmetic at the kitchen table upstairs, because Ulyana's mother liked the noise of it, liked having the young people around her even when she understood only half of what they discussed — or perhaps, Ulyana thought, watching her mother follow Milo's chalk marks on the back of a delivery envelope, precisely because she understood only half. Ignorance, in her mother's house, had never been a passive condition. It was a chosen courtesy, a curtain drawn halfway, and everyone in the room understood the arrangement.

"Three thousand copies," Anton said. "Say two hundred and forty bundles of twelve, plus loose copies for hand distribution. Harbor district, twenty-four bundles by fish cart. Church lofts, thirty-two. Hand-to-hand in the square itself, on the day — that's the dangerous part and the most important part, and it wants maybe forty people who will each carry ten copies and look like they're carrying nothing at all."

"Where do forty people come from?" Milo asked.

Signe Holm smiled the smile of a woman opening a very old cupboard. "Vessengard," she said, "has been governed badly for a hundred years, and in that time I have attended a great many funerals, christenings, guild meetings, and strikes. People owe me. Some of them don't even know yet that they owe me, but they'll remember when I remind them." She counted on her fingers. "Two of the fishwives will take bundles. The night watchman at the tannery — a Socialist, furious, reliable. My nephew and his sweetheart, who are theatricals and consider this the best role either of them has ever been offered. The organist at Saint Ansgar's, who has hated Halvard since the occupation levy confiscated the church's copper roof piping. I can give you twenty-five by Friday and the rest by Sunday, and you will not ask me for the names."

"No," Anton said. "We shouldn't have the names. None of us should have names beyond our own cell." He said it flatly, the soldier's grammar of conspiracy, and then looked around the table and added, less flatly, "I'm sorry. That's how it was done in the army. This isn't the army."

"It's a bit the army," Milo said.

"It's a bit the army," Anton conceded, "run by a novelist, a choirboy, and an old lady."

"A printer," Ulyana said, "an apprentice compositor, and a master of the Guild, if you please. Titles matter in this town. Ask anyone who's been refused credit."

Her mother, who had been silent through all of it, following the chalk marks with one crooked finger, spoke for the first time. "The wet nurses' bench in the cathedral close," she said. Everyone turned. She did not raise her eyes from the envelope. "Your grandmother sat there, Ulyana, and I sat there with you, and every woman on that bench has fed half the infants of the official class and heard everything said over their cradles. If you want copies inside the close itself, inside the ring where the delegates will lodge—" she pushed the envelope, with its chalked map of the cathedral quarter, toward Anton, "—no one searches a nursing mother, and no one remembers a nursing mother. That is the whole history of this country, as far as I've witnessed it. No one remembers the women on the bench."

The silence that followed was of a different quality than the earlier one. Anton picked up the envelope and studied the chalked streets as though her mother had drawn fortifications there, which, Ulyana reflected, in a sense she had.

"Mrs. Eriksen," Anton said carefully, "have you been listening to more than you've let on?"

"I listen to everything," her mother said placidly. "It's the only talent I've kept. Now. Who is going to explain to me what an amnesty is, and why it is written in the fine print of a peace document rather than said out loud like an honest sin?"

Nobody wanted to explain it, and everybody understood that it needed explaining, and in the end it was Ulyana who explained it, evenly, the way she set difficult type — one word at a time, trusting the line to hold — and her mother listened, and nodded once at the end, and said only, "Well. Then we had better be quick, hadn't we," and asked Milo whether he took sugar, and poured the tea, and that was that.

* * *

The nights were for the routes, and the routes were walked before they were committed to paper, because Anton had a soldier's superstition — learned, Ulyana guessed, in the worst classroom — that no plan survives contact with a street it hasn't been walked on.

They went out on the fifth evening, the two of them, separately and then together by prearrangement at the mouth of Fiskergade, walking the harbor district in the blue dark with their collars up. Rain had stopped; gulls muttered over the quays; the fish carts stood in their rows like patient animals.

"Vasel loads here," Anton murmured, "and turns left, which is wrong for us, so the bundles go aboard last, under the false floor of the salt barrel—" He stopped. Ahead, at the corner, a patrol lantern swung. Two military policemen, bored, wet-collared, checking the papers of a late carter.

Ulyana felt the old animal lurch of fear and, beside it, something new: she catalogued it, the way she'd begun cataloguing everything these days — *fear, eight-point, bold face, set solid* — because naming a thing measured it, and measured things could be handled. She took Anton's arm the way a sweetheart would, laughed at something invented, and steered them both into the light of Brask's tavern window, where lovers were permitted to stand and were beneath the notice of empires. The lantern passed. Her heart performed its drumroll against her ribs, and she observed to herself that she was becoming fluent in fear the way she had become fluent in lying to Inspector Brandt, and that both fluencies cost something she hadn't finished pricing.

"The square," Anton said quietly, when they moved again, "will be different. On the day, the square will be full of soldiers and cameras and men whose entire profession is noticing things. Whatever we do there has to be done by strangers, quickly, once, and without any thread leading back to Kirkegade."

"Then the thread leads nowhere," Ulyana said. "That's the whole design, isn't it? A pamphlet with no imprint, dropped by no one, appearing everywhere at once. Let Halvard's men chase a ghost through ten thousand hands." She looked out over the dark water. "My father used to say print was the only magic left that actually works. You put words in one place and they appear in ten thousand places and no one can say afterward where the first place was." A pause. "He said it about advertising copy, mostly. But I'm choosing to apply it upward."

Anton laughed — really laughed, the first she'd heard from him since Signe's arrest had been undone by his own grim accounting of it — and the sound startled a gull off the pilings, and for one moment, standing in the fish-smell dark of a city preparing to sign away its dead, Ulyana Eriksen felt what she could only describe as happiness: fierce, improperly timed, entirely hers.

* * *

On the sixth night, Milo forged his first pass, and it was beautiful, and it was terrible, and it worked.

He did it alone in the back room, because forging, they had all agreed, was a solitary art; witnesses were weight a forger couldn't afford. But when he came out at midnight, gray-faced, holding it at arm's length like something that had bitten him, the whole household assembled in the corridor to look, because they had all been waiting up, though none of them admitted it.

"There," Milo said hoarsely, pointing at the date with a shaking finger that was only half trembling from tiredness. "The tail of the nine. Too thin. I overcorrected."

Ulyana took it. Under the corridor lamp, the pass was indistinguishable from the government's own — border, fleurons, stamp, the smug little date-plug impression sitting slightly off-square exactly the way the clerk's office always sat slightly off-square. She looked at it a long time.

"Milo," she said, "this is the finest piece of composition ever produced in this shop."

"Don't say that," he said, stricken. "Don't joke. I made a *thing that lies*, Ulyana. I sat there making a thing that lies and I kept thinking about Father teaching me to pull my first proof, and how he said a printer's whole honor is that the page tells the truth twice — what's written and what's set — and I've broken it. I've broken the shop's honor tonight and I can feel it broken in my hands."

The corridor was quiet. Downstairs, the press stood under its sheet like a sleeping animal. Upstairs, Ulyana's mother's clock counted the small hours with its patient tick.

"Milo," Ulyana said, "come here." She led him into the back room, to the wall where the great map hung with its grease-pencil rivers of herring carts and choir lofts and nursing mothers, and she put the false pass down on the table beside the stack of true ones, and she pointed at the map, at all of it, the whole web of ordinary treachery they had spun. "This page tells the truth twice," she said. "Everything on that wall exists so that one document — the one we're printing — can tell the truth once, plainly, in public, where it counts. The pass is a lie in service of the record. Father's honor isn't broken tonight. It's *carried*. There's a difference, and you'll feel it in a day or two, and until you do, you'll do the work anyway, because that's what honor actually is — doing the work while your hands feel broken."

Milo looked at the map. Then at the pass. Then at her.

"The seventh cart," he said finally, his voice steadying as it found its technical ground, "should go by the Ringstrasse after all, actually. I've been thinking. Vasel won't, but Jørgensen might, if Signe asks him at the funeral tomorrow — the bindery man's, everyone will be there — and if Jørgensen goes by the Ringstrasse we cover the government offices themselves before eight o'clock."

And that, Ulyana thought, watching the boy bend over the map and begin redrawing the morning's arithmetic in careful chalk — that was the sound of an apprentice becoming a master, or near enough that no one would quibble on a point of ceremony.

* * *

By the eighth evening, everything was ready, and readiness turned out to be its own special torment.

The bundles waited in the cellar, wrapped flat, hymnal-shaped. The passes lay in Milo's tin, dated and dated and dated. Signe's roster of the willing existed, unwritten, in Signe's head, which was safer than any vault in the city. The fish carts were arranged; the choirs rehearsed; the organist at Saint Ansgar's had reportedly practiced his indignation. Even the weather cooperated, a hard clean cold forecast for signing day, good crowds, good tempers, thick coats with deep pockets.

They gathered in the back room for the final check, reading the list aloud item by item like a family saying grace, and it was in the middle of the list — between "*loose copies for the square, forty carriers*" and "*contingency, if the carts are stopped*" — that someone knocked at the loading door.

Three knocks. Even. Unhurried. The knock of a man with permission.

Everyone froze in the manner of people who had rehearsed freezing, and it occurred to Ulyana, absurdly, in the half-second before Anton reached the bolt, that eight days ago she would have frozen out of terror, whereas now she froze the way a compositor freezes — assessing, measuring, already reaching for the right type.

Anton opened the door a hand's width.

Inspector Brandt stood in the lamplight, alone, hatless, holding a folded newspaper under his arm against the cold, and he said, in the voice of a man calling on a neighbor to return a borrowed tool:

"Fräulein Eriksen. Might I have a word? Not about paper, this time."
