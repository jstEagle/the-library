## 25. The Letter That Isn't His

The trouble began, as so much trouble does in a print shop, with a piece of paper that didn't match the others.

It was nearly midnight, and Ulyana had sent Anton up to sleep three hours earlier — he still moved like a man whose body was keeping a ledger of debts — while she sat at the proofing table with her father's letters spread before her in the lamplight like a hand of cards. She had read them all a dozen times by now. She knew their rhythms the way she knew the psalms of her childhood, which is to say imperfectly but with her whole heart. She knew where his sentences ran long when he was frightened and clipped short when he was furious. She knew that he crossed his sevens, that he doubled his t's with a kind of impatient violence, that his margins wandered like a man walking home drunk and happy.

And she knew the smell of the ink.

That was the thing nobody thought about, unless they were born over a print shop. Paper has a smell; ink has a family of smells. The letters had come to Anton in batches over eighteen months, carried through lines and back channels, wrapped in oilcloth, passed hand to grubby hand — and every one of them smelled faintly of the same iron-gall ink, aged the same way, absorbed into the fibers the same way. Even the oldest had that softness of a page that has been handled.

Except this one.

She had it in her hands now — the letter dated the twelfth of March, the one that described the convoy to the fortress, the one that named names and dates with an almost helpful precision. It was the most operationally useful of all nine letters. It told them where things happened. It told them when.

It smelled wrong.

Ulyana held it to the lamp, then pressed her nose to the corner like some absurd bloodhound, and felt her stomach drop through the floorboards and into Signe Holm's cellar below.

Fresh ink. Or fresh-ish. Months younger than its date, at least. And beneath the ink smell, something else — a whiff of machine oil, the cheap mineral kind, the kind used on government office stamps. Her father had hated machine-oil ink. He'd once written three pages to a supplier on the subject, comparing it unfavorably to pond scum.

She set the letter down very carefully, as though it might go off.

Then she picked it up again and did what her father had trained her to do since she was tall enough to see over the composing stone: she stopped reading it as a letter and started reading it as *type*. Because handwriting, to a compositor, is not magic. It is a system. It has habits, and habits can be checked.

Her father's capital F — the one he used mid-sentence when he wanted a word to feel important — always leaned. Always. A little forward, like a man starting to rise from his chair. She scanned the forged page. Every F stood politely upright. And the spacing — she measured two words against each other with her thumbnail, then a third, and found what she had been half-dreading: the letters sat evenly. Beautifully evenly. Machine-even. Her father's hand had never been even in its life; he spaced words the way he told jokes, with a pause exactly where the meaning needed it.

Someone had written this letter slowly and carefully, imitating a hand they knew only from samples.

Someone who had never set type beside him. Someone who had never watched him write at all.

*Planted,* she thought, and the word arrived cold and complete, like ice slipping under a door.

*This one was planted.*

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the kitchen with the nine letters divided into two piles — eight and one — and made tea she forgot to drink, and listened to the house tick, and tried to think like someone cleverer than she was, because the alternative was to think like someone frightened, and frightened people make excellent instruments for other people's purposes.

Whoever forged the letter knew things. Knew the convoy route. Knew the fortress transfer schedule — or claimed to. Knew enough of her father's hand to pass a casual glance, which meant they'd had access to genuine samples. Which meant either her father's correspondence had been seized somewhere along its journey, or —

Or someone close to him had written it.

Or someone close to *her* had delivered it.

Anton had brought the letters in two lots. The first five had come with him across the border, sewn into his coat lining — she'd seen the stitches herself, seen the needle marks in the fabric. The last four had reached him through a contact in Gammelbruk after he arrived, passed along a chain he could describe only vaguely: *a fish merchant, then a seminarian, then a woman who never gave her name.* The March letter was in the second lot.

So the chain was broken somewhere, or the chain had never existed except as a delivery route with her shop sitting at the end of it like a marked grave.

And yet. And yet. Eight letters rang true down to the kerning. One was a counterfeit sitting among them like a forged coin in a purse — and whoever slipped it there hadn't needed it to survive scrutiny forever. They'd only needed it until the tenth day. Until the amnesty was signed and sealed. Unless, of course, the point wasn't the letter at all, but the doubt it would sow *when discovered.* Poison a well once, and men drink warily forever. She remembered her father saying something of the sort about a rival printer who had faked a competitor's invoice — not to ruin him, but to make his customers squint at everything he ever printed.

Doubt, her father said, is cheaper than slander and works twice as hard.

Upstairs, her mother coughed twice — the light, deliberate kind that meant *I am awake and I have heard you pacing, go to bed* — and Ulyana blew out the lamp and lay on top of the blankets fully dressed and stared at the ceiling and did not go to bed.

* * *

In the morning she was worse than no sleep had any right to make her, because no sleep plus certainty is a kind of fuel. She came down to the shop with the letters under her arm and found Anton already at the composing stone, sleeves rolled, doing the slow one-armed work of redistributing type into the case — Milo had assigned him the job as therapeutic and had been astonished to discover the man took to it like penance.

"Anton." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Where did the March letter actually come from?"

His hands stopped over the case. He didn't turn around right away, and she watched the back of his neck, and hated herself for watching it the way a stranger would.

"Gammelbruk. I told you. The fish merchant—"

"The fish merchant whose name you don't know. On a street you can't describe. Given to you by a woman who never introduced herself." She laid the letter on the stone between them, face up, like an accusation at trial. "Read the date on that."

He read it. She watched his face do several things quickly — confusion, then concentration, then the particular stillness of a man realizing the ground has shifted under him without making a sound.

"It's forged," she said. "The ink is wrong. The spacing is wrong. The F doesn't lean. My father's F leans, Anton. It has leaned since before the war. This was written by somebody working from copies — careful, slow, by someone who learned his hand the way you learn a foreign language. From examples. Not from memory." She kept her voice level because the shop door was unlocked and Milo was somewhere in the back with the coffin job, whistling. "It came with the four letters from your Gammelbruk chain. The ones I cannot trace to anything but your word."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life, and it lasted perhaps four seconds.

"You think I brought it here," Anton said quietly.

"I think somebody brought it here," she said. "I think somebody wanted it in this shop, in these hands, feeding into a pamphlet we are going to stake everything on. And the list of people who could have done that is short. It is roughly one name long, and I hate that it is."

"That letter is why we moved the timetable," he said. His voice stayed low, but she could hear the steel coming into it, the parade-ground core she sometimes forgot was there. "Everything in it matched what I saw at the front. The convoy schedules, the transfer rotation — Ulyana, I *lived* beside that information for a year. Why would I carry evidence of Halvard's crimes across a border in my own blood and then help forge more?"

"To control us. To steer us. To make sure we printed the thing *they* wanted printed — a version with errors in it, a version that could be debunked, so that the true parts drown with it." She heard herself speaking in her father's cadences and didn't care. "A pamphlet with one proven lie in it isn't a weapon, it's a gift. It hands Brandt everything. It hands Halvard's people the power to call the whole record a fabrication — my father included. They don't need to stop the truth if they can make it stink."

"And you think I'm their instrument." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "I walked eleven days on a wound that went to the bone to reach this shop, because your father told me — *told me*, before I ever saw his face — that if anything happened to him, his daughter would know what to do with paper. And now you look at me like Brandt looks at me."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "Probably not. But it's honest, and I'll take honest over fair."

Milo appeared in the doorway from the back room with the coffin-order clipboard in his hand and the whistle dying on his lips. He looked from one to the other the way people look at weather turning. "Should I — I can come back."

"Stay," Ulyana said, and surprised herself by meaning it, because whatever this was, it should not happen in whispers behind doors. Whispers were how they'd gotten here. "Milo. If I put two pieces of type in front of you, both casting the same letter, how do you tell which came from our foundry?"

Milo blinked. "The nicks. Ours have a chipped nick on the — wait." She watched the understanding arrive on his young face, watch it travel downward. "You're saying one of the general's letters is fake? The dead — the *not-dead* Eriksen's letters?"

"One of nine."

"Which one?"

"The useful one," Ulyana said bitterly. "Of course. It's always the useful one."

Anton pulled out a chair and sat down slowly, and for the first time since she'd known him he looked his age and a good deal older — a man who had run out of places to be believed. "Ask me anything," he said. "About the front, about the convoys, about your father — the real things, the things a forger wouldn't know to fake or dare to invent. Ask me until you're satisfied or sick of me. I don't know how else to fix this, and I understand that it needs fixing, because—" he gestured at the letter on the stone, and his hand, she noticed, was not entirely steady, "—because whoever did this understood exactly what you just said. They don't need to beat us. They only need us to stop trusting each other. And it's *working*, Mrs. Eriksen, and I've been on the other end of an army's cleverness before, and this feels exactly like it."

"Don't call me Mrs. Eriksen, I'm unmarried and it makes me feel like my mother."

Despite everything, the corner of his mouth moved. "Miss Eriksen."

"Milo," she said, "go get Signe's magnifier from the drawer. The big jeweler's one. And bring the file of my father's old business correspondence from the ledger room — the 1913 box. We're going to do this properly. We're going to check every letter, line by line, against forty years of his habits, and we are going to be *certain*, because certain is the only condition under which that press runs again."

"And if the others check out?" Milo asked, already moving.

"Then we hold the truth in one hand and this forgery in the other," Ulyana said, "and we ask ourselves a very interesting question — namely, what the forgers were so anxious to make sure we believed."

She looked at the false letter lying on the stone, upright-F'd, evenly-spaced, helpful beyond all decency, and felt the fear in her chest transmute slowly into something harder and more familiar. Anger. Compositor's anger. The clean kind, the kind that comes from a flaw found and a flaw to be corrected.

Somebody out there had studied her father's hand. Somebody had touched his letters, which meant somebody might be touching *him*. And somebody, somewhere in a government office smelling of machine-oil ink, had sat down at a desk and practiced and practiced until they could produce this — all to unmake a girl who set type in a small shop in Vessengard.

Well. They had picked the wrong trade to fight.

"Anton," she said, and he looked up, wary and tired and waiting to learn whether he was forgiven. "Tell me about the winter at the front. Everything. Start with the cold. Forgers always forget the cold."

He began to speak. And it was Milo, setting the magnifier down on the stone, who went white first — because halfway through the second sentence, Anton mentioned the name of a corporal, and Milo said, very quietly:

"He's lying about the cold."

Both of them turned.

"He's not lying," Milo said, and his voice had gone strange. "But that corporal — Anton, that corporal isn't dead. He's living two streets from here. And I've got three hundred of his funeral invitations sitting in the back room, waiting for tomorrow's press."
