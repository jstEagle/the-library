## 26. Eight Days

The forged letter sat on the composing stone between them all night, like a guest nobody had invited and nobody would feed.

Ulyana had not slept. She had lit every lamp in the shop — the good ones, the ones her father hoarded for fine work because "electricity is a rumor, Ulyana, and rumors flicker" — and she had laid out the six letters Anton carried alongside the one that had arrived by the hand of a boy who took his coin and vanished into the fog. Seven letters. Six true, one false. She was certain of it now, certain the way you are certain of a wrong note in a song you've known since childhood, but certainty of the ear is not proof, and proof was the only currency that mattered with eight days left on the clock.

Anton sat across from her, his bandaged shoulder propped against the paper cabinet, watching her hands. He had not argued when she'd told him to stop talking. That was new for him. He was a man who argued the way other men breathed.

"Tell me again what you're looking for," he said finally, which was not stopping.

"Quiet."

"You said that an hour ago."

"And it remains an excellent suggestion." She didn't look up. Her fingers were moving over the letters without quite touching them, hovering, the way her father had taught her to hover over wet type. *Don't smudge what you love,* he used to say, which she had taken at age nine to be advice about type and only much later understood to be advice about nearly everything.

The problem was this: the forged letter was *good*. Whoever had made it knew her father's hand — the slight forward lean of the capitals, the way his *r* trailed off like a man leaving a room mid-sentence. They had his phrases. They had his habit of underlining once for emphasis and twice for fury. They even had his private shorthand for *money*, which appeared in exactly two letters in the world as far as Ulyana knew, both of them addressed to her mother and hidden in the flour tin for eleven years.

So it wasn't a forgery made from ignorance. It was a forgery made from study. Someone had gotten hold of real letters — perhaps intercepted, perhaps bought — and copied them with a copyist's diligence.

But.

*But.*

"You're smiling," Anton said. "That's either very good or very bad."

"It's neither yet. It's professional satisfaction, which is its own category." She pulled the lamp closer. "Anton. When you were a soldier, could you tell your comrades apart in the dark? By footfall?"

"By breath, mostly. Jorin whistled when he slept. The sergeant cleared his throat before he lied."

"Yes. Exactly that." She turned the suspect letter toward him, angling it so the lamplight raked across the page sideways, shadows pooling in the valleys the pen had pressed into the paper. "Everyone has habits they don't know they have. Handwriting is full of them. A copyist sees the letters. A daughter sees the *gaps*."

* * *

Here is what Ulyana Eriksen knew about her father that no intelligence officer in Vessengard could have bought or tortured out of anyone, because she had never told a living soul, because it had seemed too small to be worth telling:

Halvard Eriksen was afraid of the letter *f*.

Not afraid in any way a doctor could name. He simply did not trust it. An *f*, he maintained, was a letter that took up more room than it deserved on the line and gave nothing back — a swaggerer, a letter that elbowed. Left to his own devices he set his *f*s tight against whatever followed them, crowding them, punishing them for their ambition. When he composed by hand, his *f* leaned hard into the next letter until the pair looked like conspirators sharing a secret. *Of*, in his writing, was practically one character. *After* looked apologetic.

And when he set metal type — this was the part, this was the treasure buried under the ordinary years — he did it with his thumbs.

Every compositor in the Baltic learned to space by eye and by the little brass wedges, and most of them were consistent within a hair's width, because consistency was pride and pride was the trade. But Halvard Eriksen, who could justify a column of shipping manifests so perfectly that merchants came in just to watch him do it, always, always thinned the space after an *f* by one hair below what the eye strictly required. Not enough for a customer to notice. Enough that if you had spent ten thousand hours standing beside him at the case, handing him quads, listening to him complain about the price of Swedish ink — enough that you felt it in your fingertips before your eyes confirmed it.

She had discovered it at fourteen, checking his proofs against her own, and had announced it like a scientific finding. He had been delighted rather than embarrassed. "A man's spacing is his signature," he'd said, tapping her nose with an ink-stained finger. "Anyone can copy my letters, sprout. Letters are public property. But the *silences* between them—" he'd waggled his hand mysteriously, "—the silences are where a man keeps himself."

She had thought, for twenty years, that this was just something printers said.

Now she spread the seven letters across the stone and read them the way her father had taught her to read proofs: backwards, slowly, aloud in a whisper, hunting errors the eye would glide over. And there — third paragraph of the true letter dated last spring — *after the convoy left, we counted what remained.* The *f* in *after* crowded its neighbor. The *f* in *left* huddled against the *t* like a man sheltering from rain. And in the forged letter, in its confident imitation of his voice, its correct phrases, its stolen shorthand: *I fear I cannot explain further by post.*

The *f* in *fear* stood at a polite, regulation distance from its neighbor. The *f* in *further* had room to spare. Whoever wrote it had beautiful manners and no blood relation to the spaces between Halvard Eriksen's words.

It was such a small thing. It was everything.

* * *

"The silences," she said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, which annoyed her enormously, because she had planned to deliver this like a verdict and instead it came out like a prayer.

Anton leaned forward. "Show me."

She showed him. She put his finger — his rough, bandaged-adjacent, soldier's finger — onto the gap after the *f* in the forged letter, then onto the gap in the true one. "There. Do you feel it? He never gave an *f* its due. Twenty years of setting type beside him. His hand crowds them. This hand flatters them. This isn't my father being careful. This is someone else being careful *about* my father."

Anton stared at the page for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, disbelieving bark, quickly strangled, as though laughter were also rationed these days. "A letter *f*," he said. "We staked everything on a letter *f*."

"We staked everything on twenty years of standing next to a man while he worked," Ulyana said. "The *f* is just where it showed."

She expected him to argue — that it wasn't enough, that Brandt's people would say a daughter's word was sentiment dressed up as evidence, that doubt was cheaper than proof and everyone spent doubt. Instead he went quiet in a different register entirely, the quiet of a man reorganizing himself around a new fact.

"I accused you," he said. "Last night. I stood in your shop and let them look at you like—" He stopped. Started again, which he almost never did; Anton's sentences usually arrived fully armed. "When the letter came, I watched your face and some part of me thought, *she could be part of it, the shop could be the trap, the whole thing could be bait.* I'm sorry. I'm sorrier than I know how to say in your language."

"You apologized better in mine than most people manage in theirs," Ulyana said. "And you weren't wrong to doubt. Doubt was the right instinct applied to the wrong person." She gathered the six true letters into their oilcloth wrapping with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. "Doubt the letter. Doubt the boy with the coin. Doubt the weather, the government, and the fish. But don't doubt the spacing. Spacing doesn't lie. Spacing can't afford to; the whole trade would collapse."

He smiled at that, tiredly. Then, because he was Anton and still learning her: "Your father taught you this? To read the gaps?"

"He taught me everything by accident. He thought he was teaching me to fetch quads." She tied the cloth. "That was his whole method, I think. Teach by pretending not to. You learn more from a man's habits than his lectures, and he knew it, and he arranged his habits accordingly." She paused. "Which means somewhere in the last three years, in whatever hell he was walking through, he sat down and wrote these letters knowing — *knowing* — that someday they'd be doubted, and that the only witness who could clear them was a girl he'd taught to read silence without ever saying he was doing it."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs her mother coughed, twice, and was still.

"That's why it held," Anton said softly. "Why the whole plan held. He built the proof into the letters the way you build a keel into a boat. For you specifically."

"For the record," Ulyana corrected, because her throat was doing something undignified. "For whoever checked. It happens that whoever checked was me."

"Ulyana."

"Don't make it a moment, Anton. We have eight days and a pamphlet to print and a general to embarrass, and I refuse to cry over kerning."

But she was crying, a little, over kerning, and Anton had the grace to pretend to examine the type case until she'd finished, turning over pieces of furniture with great scholarly interest, including — she noted later, when dignity returned — a piece of furniture that was actually a spoon.

* * *

They worked through the morning after that, and the work itself was the celebration. There is a particular joy, known only to people who make things with their hands, in returning to a task after terror has passed through it — the wood of the bench feels friendlier, the ink smells richer, even the bad coffee tastes like a decision you're glad you made.

Ulyana rebuilt the forme for page four of the pamphlet. Anton, whose left arm was still more ornament than instrument, sat at the case under strict orders to do nothing ambitious, and sorted type — the mindless, meditative work of putting every letter back in its compartment, and the first honest labor he'd done since the war that didn't involve carrying something dangerous.

"You put the *k* in the *h* box," she said, without looking up.

"There are two boxes that look identical."

"There are two boxes that *are* identical, and one of them is for *h*, and you have committed a crime against it."

"In the army we had a system—"

"In the army you shot people. Sit down."

He sat. He resorted the *k*. Ten minutes later she caught him reading the type in the compartments like a man reading tea leaves, lips moving, and she decided not to say anything, because there was something in his face she recognized from her own mirror on the day she'd first understood that the shop was hers.

Milo arrived at noon with bread and news, in that order of importance. "Brandt's office sent a clerk round to Kessler's bindery asking about paper weights," he reported, divesting himself of his coat. "Kessler told them he sells to anyone with coin and remembers no faces, which is a lie, because Kessler remembers the face of every coin. But it means they're asking. Also my cousin Soren says hello and also Soren is a boil on the neck of humanity and I may drown him in the harbor, in a friendly way."

"Noted on both counts," Ulyana said. Milo had been walking a strange line since the forged letter arrived — furious at it, she suspected, in the particular way apprentices get furious when someone threatens the shop, which Milo defended with the ferocity of a small dog defending a large territory it does not legally own.

"Is it settled, then?" Milo asked, jerking his chin at the oilcloth bundle. "The letter. Real or not?"

"Forged," Ulyana said. "And the others proven true."

"How?"

"She read the gaps," Anton said.

Milo blinked. "The what?"

"Come here." She beckoned him to the stone and showed him, quickly, the two *f*s — the crowded one, the polite one. "Your master's master had a signature made of empty space. Learn to see it. In five years you'll catch a supplier swapping stock by the grain of a margin, and you'll thank me."

Milo studied the pages with the intense frown he reserved for mathematics and injustice. "So the man who forged it," he said slowly, "copied the letters but couldn't copy the... the *not-writing*?" His face cleared, then darkened. "That's brilliant. That's horrible. That means whoever forged it got hold of real letters to copy from."

The shop went quiet again, the good quiet curdling into the other kind.

"Yes," Ulyana said. "It does mean that. Which tells us they've had access to his correspondence — intercepted somewhere between him and us, or taken from someone who carried it." She looked at Anton. "Which narrows things. Your route. Who else touched the letters between the fortress road and this door?"

Anton's jaw tightened. "Two dead men, a burned safehouse, and a woman in Lennvik I'd trust with my life, who is currently forty miles from anyone's reach." He rubbed his eyes with his good hand. "Or a courier I haven't accounted for. Or the garrison post. I'll write to Lennvik tonight. If the leak runs through her, we need to warn her before Brandt's clerks get clever."

"Do it from Signe's," Ulyana said. "Nothing leaves this building anymore that isn't printed."

* * *

Evening came down over Vessengard the color of diluted ink, and Ulyana found herself alone at the case for a few minutes while Anton walked Milo partway home — a precaution they'd begun without discussing it, the way you begin all the small mutual protections that eventually add up to a word nobody has said.

She stood in front of the job case, upper-left quadrant, where the *f*s lived, and ran her thumb along the row of them. Cold brass and lead. Forty little swaggerers, each one taking up slightly more room than it deserved, each one spaced by her own hand now, thinned after, punished gently, kept in line — kept the way he'd kept them, the way he'd taught her to keep them without ever once saying *this is how you will know me when I cannot speak for myself.*

*The silences are where a man keeps himself.*

She had thought, two weeks ago, that she was keeping the shop for a dead man. Then that she was keeping it for a ghost. Now she understood she was keeping it for a prisoner, and that every piece of type in the building was a message he'd already sent, waiting years in the dark for someone fluent enough to read it.

Upstairs, her mother's floorboard creaked — pacing, not falling. Good. Pacing meant appetite.

The street door rattled. Ulyana's heart performed its now-familiar gymnastics, and then Milo's head appeared around it, breathless, cap askew, wearing the expression of a boy who has run the last three streets and enjoyed none of them.

"Milo? You live the *other* direction."

"I know." He bent double, hands on knees, and got out between breaths: "Fish cart. Down by the harbor. The driver — one of Signe's routes — he didn't come to the corner. And there are soldiers at the fortress gate, more than usual, and they've brought wagons. Wagons, Ulyana. Prison wagons." He straightened, eyes wide. "Signe's girl got word to me. They're moving prisoners. *Tonight.*"
