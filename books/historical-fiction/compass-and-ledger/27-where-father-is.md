## 27. Where Father Is

The letter had been drying on the line above the stove for two days, pinned by wooden clothespegs Milo had whittled into the shapes of birds, and Ulyana had read it perhaps forty times. She knew it the way she knew a page of type — not by meaning anymore, but by shape. The way the *h* leaned. The way the ink had pooled at the end of the word *forgive*, as though the pen had hesitated there, or the hand had shaken, or both.

*If you are reading this, then Anton reached you, and the boy did not lose his nerve, which I confess I doubted, having met him for exactly nine minutes in a ditch outside Kernau.*

She was still holding it when Anton came down the stairs from the back room, moving carefully, favoring the shoulder that had not yet decided to forgive him for the bayonet. He had learned the house in three weeks the way a cat learns a house — which boards spoke, which doors stuck, which stair groaned its single note of complaint. He avoided the groaning stair now without thinking. Ulyana had noticed this and said nothing, the way she had stopped saying a great many things lately.

"You've read it again," he said. Not a question.

"It's a letter, not a loaf of bread. It doesn't go stale."

"It does, a little." He sat across from her at the worktable, in her father's chair, which he had begun doing without asking and which she had permitted without deciding to. "Every time you read it, it's a little further away from him. The ink stays. The man doesn't."

"That's a very printerly thing to say for a man who can't tell a quoine from a quoin."

"From a *what* to a *what*?"

"Never mind." She set the letter down flat and smoothed it with both hands, an unconscious gesture, the gesture of a compositor making a forme true. "You've been circling something for three days, Anton. Since the proofs came back from Signe's. You sit there like a man with a stone in his boot, and you keep deciding not to take it out. I've set type for shipping manifests for six years. I know when a page is carrying more than its weight."

He looked at her. Outside, Vessengard was doing its November business — gulls arguing over the fish market, a handcart rattling over the cobbles, somebody's radio, faint through the wall, playing a waltz as if the world were a place where waltzes still made sense.

"You told me everything," she went on. "Kernau. The village with the well. The orders Halvard signed. You gave me all of it in that awful flat voice, and I took it, and we built a pamphlet out of it. So whatever this is, it's worse. It's the thing you kept back because you thought it would break the run."

"Ulyana—"

"Don't. Don't do the thing where you decide what I can carry. My mother does it. Milo does it. The whole city does it to every woman who works a trade — *you set the type, love, you needn't read it.* I have read everything that has crossed this shop since I was eleven years old. I read the casualty lists before the officers' wives did. I set the type for them." Her voice had risen, and she brought it down deliberately, the way she'd bring a forme down onto the stone. "So. The stone out of the boot."

Anton put his good hand flat on the table. She watched him arrange his face, and understood, with a strange lurch, that he was arranging it *for* her — that this was a man who had spent a year delivering terrible things and had learned to package them.

"Your father is alive," he said.

The shop was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs her mother's floorboard gave its familiar sigh.

"I know that," Ulyana said. "That's what the letters—"

"No." He didn't let her finish. "You think *alive the way the letters mean it* — alive in some farmhouse in the east, writing when he can, coming home when the war ends. I let you think it. It kept you working. It kept me from watching you stop." He drew a breath. "He's alive the way a man is alive in the fortress at Vessengard. Cell block four. He was transferred there eleven weeks ago. Pending transfer again — to the interior, after the armistice. He's on a list, Ulyana. And the list is going to be made to disappear."

The words went into her the way a spike goes into a forme — one clean blow, and everything held fast.

"The fortress," she said. It came out very small. "The fortress is *here*. It's — you can see it from the fish market. I've seen it every day of my life. It's where they keep smugglers and debtors and boys who punched a sergeant." She was aware she was saying foolish things and could not stop. "He's been *here*? Across the harbor? While I've been — while I've been setting *manifests*?"

"He was brought through the city in a closed wagon. He was in the same city as you for eleven weeks, and I have spent those eleven weeks trying to work out how to tell you, and there is no way to tell you, so I am simply telling you." Anton's voice stayed level, but she saw his hand — the good one — press harder against the wood, as if the table might otherwise go somewhere. "I found out from a clerk in the garrison pay office who owed me. Cell block four. He's listed as *Eriksen, K., civilian detainee, sedition*. They didn't shoot him, Ulyana. That's the part you have to understand. They kept him alive because a dead witness is a martyr and a live one can be *managed*. And after the armistice—"

"After the armistice, the amnesty." She had read the leaked proofs herself. She had set a line of them with her own hands. *All acts committed under operational authority between the dates of—* The fine print, the beautiful, murderous fine print. "The amnesty erases the crimes."

"The amnesty erases the *witnesses*. That's the trick of it. You can't have a crime if the law says it never happened. Halvard walks into the new government with his chest full of medals, and your father becomes a seditionist who was detained under emergency statutes, and the emergency is over, so the file closes, and the man goes to the interior, and the interior is a very large word, Ulyana. Nobody comes back from the interior. Not in a way that can testify."

Ulyana sat with it. She was surprised at herself — she had expected to weep, or to shout, and instead she found she was doing what she always did, which was *composing*. Taking the elements, sorting them, finding where they belonged in the order of the thing. Father, alive. Father, near. Father, on a list. Amnesty, in nine days. Transfer, after.

"Pending transfer," she said. "That's the phrase you used."

"Yes."

"Which means he's not transferred yet."

Anton's jaw tightened. "Ulyana."

"Which means that for some number of days, my father is sitting in a cell across the harbor, alive, *findable*, while the ink on his own testimony dries in this room." She stood up. Her knees were steady, which surprised her. "How many days?"

"I don't know. The clerk didn't know. Somewhere between the signing and the end of the month."

"Nine days until the signing."

"Ulyana." He said her name the way one says *careful* to a person standing at the edge of a quay. "I have thought about nothing else for eleven weeks. I have walked past that fortress forty times. I have drawn the gate in my notebook like a schoolboy. And I am telling you, as a man who was a soldier and knows what a garrison looks like from the inside — we cannot get him out. Two of us, one of whom cannot lift his arm above his shoulder. It isn't a plan. It's a way to be in the next cell."

"Then it's fortunate," Ulyana said, "that I have never once considered you the brains of this operation."

For a moment — the first moment in eleven weeks — Anton laughed. It was a short, cracked thing, half cough, but it was real, and it changed the whole room, the way a well-inked roller changes a forme: suddenly everything legible.

* * *

Milo arrived at half past seven, stomping snow off his boots in the doorway and announcing the weather as though the shop had sent him out to gather it. "Sleet, then proper snow by dark. The fish carts are chaining up. Oh — and Herr Voss at the chandlery says if we want the good rag stock before the armistice rush, we'd best put in the order this week, because everyone and his uncle is printing commemorative something-or-others." He hung his coat, then stopped, because the room was wrong and Milo always knew when a room was wrong. He had the sense of a typesetter for misplaced sorts. "What's happened?"

Ulyana looked at Anton. Anton looked at her. It was, she thought absurdly, like watching two compositors each waiting for the other to distribute the type.

"Sit down, Milo," she said.

"I don't like it when you're polite to me. Polite means bad news or a funeral job."

"Sit down."

He sat, on the upturned crate he'd claimed as his own the first week of his apprenticeship, and wrapped his arms around his knees like a boy much younger than sixteen, and waited.

"My father is alive," Ulyana said. "He's in the fortress. Cell block four. And unless something happens in the next nine days, the armistice amnesty will make him into a man who was never arrested for anything worth remembering, and then he'll be moved somewhere none of us will ever find."

Milo took this in the way Milo took in most things — with a silence that looked like slowness and was not. Then: "Cell block four is the one with the little windows. You can see them from the harbor wall. My cousin said—" He stopped. His cousin was a subject he had been handling carefully since the incident at the tavern, and Ulyana watched the caution move across his face like weather. "My cousin says the fourth block is where they put people who aren't going to trial. Because trials make paperwork, and paperwork gets read."

"Your cousin," said Anton, "sounds like a useful idiot."

"My cousin sounds like a drunk who knows things," Milo said, "which is the most common kind of useful there is." He unfolded himself from the crate. "So what do we do?"

"*We*," Anton began, "are going to finish the pamphlet, and distribute it, and—"

"That's what *we* were doing yesterday," Milo said. "Yesterday it was a cause. Now it's Ulyana's da. So what do we *do*?"

And there it was, Ulyana thought. The boy had cut through the whole knot with the blunt little knife of his plainness. Yesterday it had been history — the general, the crimes, the record, the great cold nouns. Today it was a man with ink stains under his fingernails sitting in a cell with little windows you could see from the harbor wall, and the nouns had all turned warm and specific and unbearable.

"We do both," she said. "That's the answer, Milo, and I know it sounds like nothing, but it's everything. The pamphlet is not separate from him. The pamphlet is the only weapon that reaches inside that fortress. You can't bribe a garrison. You can't shoot your way past a gate with two people and a bad shoulder. But if three thousand copies of what he wrote are in the crowd on signing day — if the whole city reads his name and his testimony while Halvard stands on the platform — then my father stops being a file in a drawer and becomes a *scandal*. And scandals get reviewed. Files get opened. Men in cell block four get looked at by people who are not on Halvard's list." She heard her own voice steadying as it went, the way a press steadies on the second pull. "The truth is the plan. It was always the plan. It's just that now it has a face."

"Two faces," Anton said quietly. When they looked at him, he was looking at the table. "The clerk owed me, but he isn't the only one I've kept. There's a sergeant on the gate rotation — a Kernau man, a conscript who was there, who knows what he was part of. He's been feeding me the gate schedule for a fortnight. Not because he's brave. Because he can't sleep." He looked up. "I was going to tell you when I had something better than a schedule. I don't have anything better. But I have a schedule, and nine days, and a man across the harbor who taught you everything you know about making words permanent. So. The run continues. And I go back to the fortress wall, and I watch, and I count, and I wait for the day the transfer wagon is late."

"That's not a plan either," Milo said.

"No," Anton agreed. "It's a schedule."

* * *

That night, after Milo had gone home into the snow and Anton had climbed the stairs to the back room, Ulyana did not go up to bed. She sat at the stone with the letter before her and read it a forty-first time, and this time it was a different document entirely, the way a page is different once you know where it will stand in the book.

*If you are reading this, then Anton reached you.* Not *if you are reading this, come find me.* He had not asked for rescue. He had asked, in eleven pages of small careful handwriting, for exactly one thing: *see that it is printed, and see that it is printed true.* She had thought, all these weeks, that it was modesty, or fear, or a dying man's economy. Now she understood it was none of those. He had known where he was. He had known what the amnesty would do. He had written his testimony in a cell with little windows and smuggled it out through a feverish boy in a ditch, and he had asked not for a door but for a *press*.

"Stubborn man," she said to the empty shop, and her voice broke on the second word, and she let it, briefly, the way you let a forme sit before you lock it.

Then she wiped her eyes on her sleeve, took up her composing stick, and began to set the last pages.

She worked by the stove's light, and the type clicked under her fingers — the small, familiar, holy sound of her whole childhood, her father's sound, the sound he had made on the other side of the shop while she did her sums at the ledger desk. *Eriksen, K.* She set the name. She set it again in the colophon line. She would set it a thousand times if the run required it, and every impression would be identical, and identical was the point, was the whole art: that a thing said once, truly, could be said three thousand times without changing, and no general and no amnesty and no fortress wall could reach in and alter a single letter.

Upstairs, a floorboard sighed. Her mother, awake, or turning over, or listening to the same silence.

Ulyana was fitting the last line of the page when the door at the loading bay rattled — three knocks, then two, the signal Signe's boy used when he ran proof sheets through the snow. She went to unbolt it, and the boy pushed in a folded packet and a note, and the note, in Signe Holm's cramped master's hand, said only:

*They've moved the hearing on my arrest up to Thursday. The magistrate asking questions about the cellar. Whatever you're doing — do it faster.*

Ulyana stood in the doorway with the snow coming in past her shoulders, and looked across the black water of the harbor, where the fortress sat with its little lit windows, one of which, she now knew, was her father's.

Nine days had just become fewer.
