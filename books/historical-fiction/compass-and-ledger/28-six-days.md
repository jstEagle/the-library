## 28. Six Days

The plan, such as it was, had been drawn up on the back of a spoiled funeral notice — *In Loving Memory* across the top, which Ulyana considered either an omen or a joke, depending on how much sleep she'd had.

They sat around the kitchen table at four in the morning: Ulyana, Anton, Milo, and Signe's boy Tomas, who had come to say that his mother's arrest had not yet led anyone to the cellar press on Ravn Street, and who ate bread the way only a nineteen-year-old eats bread, as though famine were a rumor he refused to believe.

"Two things," Anton said. He had washed, and bandaged himself fresh, and in the lamplight he almost looked like a man instead of a convalescence with opinions. "The printing goes on. That doesn't stop for anything. And I go to the fortress."

"You can barely walk," Ulyana said.

"I can walk fine."

"You've been walking to the privy and calling it an expedition."

Milo snorted into his tea. Anton gave her the level look of a man who had marched forty kilometers on a shattered ankle and did not intend to be audited by a compositor.

"The transfer is set for after the amnesty is signed," he said. "After that, your father stops being a prisoner and becomes a clerical error. Nobody will ever have held him, because officially nobody ever held him. So whatever happens, it has to happen before Thursday week. I know the fortress. I delivered dispatches there for eight months. I know which gate the guards use, which sentry drinks, and which window is your father's."

"How do you know it's his?"

"Because he taught me to tap the pipe stem against the bars when the patrol passed. Twice, pause, twice. He said it was so I'd know he was alive." Anton looked down at his hands. "It was so *I'd* know. He knew perfectly well."

The kitchen was quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs Ulyana's mother coughed twice and then, deliberately, did not cough again, having made her point.

"So we split," Ulyana said at last. "You go and do something reckless. We stay and finish the run."

"Three thousand copies," Milo said. "We've got maybe nine hundred pulled."

"Then we pull twenty-one hundred more," Ulyana said. "Signe's press is seized. The shop's too hot for the big flatbed while soldiers are still sniffing down Ravn Street. So it's the cellar proof press and my father's old hand press, and it's slow, and it's us."

"Three of us," Tomas said. "Four counting me."

"Counting you?" Milo said. "You're a fish cart."

"Everyone's something," Tomas said cheerfully. "I'm a fish cart."

Anton stood, wincing in a way he pretended not to notice, and took his coat from the chair. At the door he stopped and looked back at Ulyana, and for a moment neither of them seemed able to locate the sentence they wanted.

"Six days," he said finally. "If I'm not back by the fifth day—"

"Print anyway. I know."

"If you're arrested—"

"Print anyway," Ulyana said. "That's rather become the whole philosophy of the establishment."

He smiled — that crooked, exhausted smile she had decided some weeks ago not to examine too closely, on the grounds that examination would be dangerous — and then he went out into the dark, and she listened to his uneven footsteps fade down toward the harbor, and told herself firmly that listening was not the same as worrying, and knew she was lying.

* * *

By seven she was in the cellar, and by half past she had learned the first great lesson of running a press alone: alone means alone.

With Milo oiling rollers and Anton hauling paper and even Tomas fetching, the work had a rhythm like breathing. Alone, every motion belonged to her — damping the forme, inking, laying each sheet true, pulling the bar — and between each motion stood a small waiting silence in which no one else's hands were moving anywhere in the world.

Her father had worked the hand press for thirty years. She had watched him ten thousand mornings, leaning into the pull with his whole body, wrist turning at exactly the right moment so the platen came down square. She had thought it was strength. Standing at the press now, she understood it was not strength at all. It was *listening*. The press told you when the ink was right; it told you through your palms when the packing had gone soft; it told you, in a whisper under the whisper, when a sheet was lying crooked before your eyes could see it. Her father had spent thirty years learning the language, and she had spent thirty years standing next to him without knowing he was speaking.

Well. She was learning it now.

The first hour was ruinous. She pulled eleven sheets and threw away six — ghosting, offset, one memorable disaster where the platen caught the tympan and printed a bold black rectangle with a small white hole where the word *amnesty* should have been, which Milo held up to the lamp and pronounced "honestly, more accurate."

"It's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Milo."

"Pulling six hundred sheets today," he said, stacking spoil paper for pulping, because they wasted nothing, not anymore, not with paper rationed and the clock running. "Or we die trying. Or possibly of boredom. Those are the options."

They fell into it. He fed, she pulled; she fed, he stacked; they argued about kerning in the way other families presumably argued about money. By noon the stack of good sheets had climbed past four hundred and her shoulders had found the angle, her wrists had found the turn, and somewhere around sheet five hundred the press stopped being her father's press and simply became *the press*, and something in her chest unlocked that she had been carrying clenched since the night Anton came through the loading door.

She was not minding the shop for a dead man.

She was running it.

* * *

The interruption came at two, in the form of boots on the floorboards overhead, which was normal, and then a knock at the shop door above, which was not.

Milo froze with a wet sheet half-lifted. Ink dripped onto his shoe.

"Delivery," Ulyana said, more steadily than she felt. "We're a print shop. People knock. It's practically our entire business model."

"And if it's soldiers?"

"Then you're setting type for a parish newsletter and I'm a widow's daughter doing her grieving, and the cellar door stays shut." She wiped her hands, climbed the stairs, arranged her face into something suitably weary, and opened the door on — not soldiers.

A woman. Sixty perhaps, in a good coat gone shabby at the cuffs, holding a parcel wrapped in brown paper against her chest the way one holds either a baby or a bomb.

"Fräulein Eriksen?"

"Yes."

"My name is Voss. I teach scripture at the cathedral school." A pause, in which a great deal happened silently. "I understand you sometimes do... small jobs. Private editions. Memoriam pamphlets."

Ulyana kept her face still. Behind her own eyes she saw Inspector Brandt's tea cup, and heard his gentle voice explaining what penalties attached to unregistered printing.

"We do funerals," Ulyana said carefully. "Wedding orders. Shipping manifests."

"Mm." The woman's eyes went past her shoulder, unhurried, taking in the composing stone, the cases, the galley of standing type. "My brother is a chaplain. At the fortress." She held out the parcel. "He asked me to bring this to a printer he trusted. He said — and I quote him exactly, because I memorized it — 'the Eriksen girl sets a clean page.'"

Ulyana took the parcel. It was heavier than paper. Under the string and wrapping her fingers found the shape of it: a book. An old ledger.

"He said you'd know what to do with it. He said—" and here Frau Voss's composure cracked, just at one corner, like ice over moving water, "—he said the record must survive the record-keepers. He said you would know what that meant."

"I think," Ulyana said, "that I might."

The woman nodded once, sharply, turned, and walked away down Ravn Street with the gait of someone who had done a brave thing and intended never to do anything brave again if God was merciful.

Ulyana closed the door, leaned against it, and unwrapped the parcel with hands that were not entirely steady. Inside: her father's second ledger — the twin of the one from the locked room, the one she had thought lost with the shop's papers when the army requisitioned everything — filled in her father's cramped hand with entries she had never seen. Dates. Names. Company numbers. And in the margins, in a code she now recognized the way one recognizes a face in a crowd, tallies.

Not of paper stock.

Of men.

* * *

She carried it down to the cellar and set it on the composing stone, and she and Milo read it in shifts, because reading it all at once was not possible; it was like drinking from a fire hose of grief. Between entries for legitimate jobbing work — invoices, real ones, her father's double books running both directions at once, honest commerce in the front and testimony in the back — ran the tally columns. Fourteen men shot attempting escape at Kolvik, the entry read, *no attempts occurred*. Thirty-one died of typhus, *no typhus existed at the camp*. Page after page. Her father had gotten this to the chaplain somehow — smuggled pages in confession, perhaps, or in the lining of returned laundry — and the chaplain had kept it through two years of war's end, waiting for a printer.

Waiting, specifically, for her.

"He knew," Milo said quietly. "All this time. He knew you'd end up doing this."

"He hoped," Ulyana said. "Knowing is different from hoping. Knowing is worse." She touched the margin where the tallies ran. "But he bet the truth on me anyway."

Milo was silent a moment. Then he said, "So we'd better pull a clean page, hadn't we. For a man who sets a clean page."

And they went back to the press, and Ulyana pulled the bar with her wrist turning at exactly the right moment, and sheet after sheet came off square and black and true, and by evening the stack of finished pamphlets stood at thirteen hundred, and her arms ached in a way she suspected she would remember for the rest of her life, and did not mind.

* * *

Her mother came down at dusk. She descended the cellar stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, wearing her shawl indoors against the damp, and stood watching them work for a long moment with an expression Ulyana could not read.

"You should be resting," Ulyana said.

"I have rested for three years." Her mother crossed the cellar, examined the drying rack of pamphlets, touched one corner of a sheet with one finger, the way she had once inspected Ulyana's schoolwork. Then she picked up the ledger, read one page, closed it, and set it down.

"Your father used to stand exactly where you're standing," she said. "Left foot forward. Elbow in. You even scowl at the same moment of the pull — just as the platen comes down, as if the machine has personally offended you."

"Mama—"

"Hush. I didn't come down to reminisce." She reached into the shawl and produced a small key on a ribbon. "The bottom drawer of my wardrobe. There is a box inside. You know the box."

Ulyana knew it. The wedding silver. Two candlesticks, a cream jug, spoons with her grandmother's initials, things that had survived two wars' worth of hard winters by being too meaningful to sell and too useful to keep.

"Sell what's needed," her mother said. "Keep the spoons. Your grandmother would want the spoons kept; she was sentimental about implements." She paused at the foot of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and did not look back. "Tell Anton that the tapping code works both ways, if he's clever. Your father invented it for a game we played through the wall of my mother's house, when we were courting and not supposed to be speaking. Two, pause, two. It means *still here*."

Then she went up, slowly, and Ulyana stood in the ink-smelling dark with the silver key in her hand and understood that she had been inheriting this shop for years — not the presses and the type, but the thing underneath them, the stubborn arithmetic of people who counted what actually happened and wrote it down.

* * *

On the fifth day, Anton did not come back.

Ulyana had promised herself she wouldn't count the hours, and had counted every one. The print run passed seventeen hundred. Milo forged passes at the kitchen table with a skill he refused to explain the origins of ("a gentleman needs mysteries, Ulyana"), and Tomas ferried bundles out in the fish cart and came back with the cart smelling of herring and empty, which was the whole idea. The city was filling up. Flags on the municipal buildings. Bunting going up along the harbor front for the signing, red and gold and festive, draped over the same stones that had known nothing but queues and coal dust for years.

On the evening of the fifth day, with the light failing and the harbor turning iron-grey, Ulyana climbed to the loading door and looked across the water at the fortress. Its windows were coming alight, one by one, little yellow squares in the dusk. She found the wing where the cells were — Anton had drawn it for her, obsessively, on the backs of spoiled proofs — and looked at the dark windows there.

No signal. No tap of a pipe stem carried across open water, which had been an unreasonable hope anyway, but hope had stopped consulting reason some time ago.

Behind her, in the cellar, the press waited with eighteen hundred sheets pulled and twelve hundred to go, and tomorrow was distribution on a scale that would make the city ring like a struck bell, and somewhere across that water her father was sitting in a cell not knowing whether his daughter had received his ledger, and somewhere in those same streets a courier with a bad ankle had vanished into the machinery of a state that was about to sign away his testimony in six-point type on the bottom of page four.

Milo came up behind her and stood in the doorway, hands in his pockets.

"He'll make it back," he said. "Men like him always make it back. It's their defining flaw."

"Milo."

"Yes?"

She pointed, because pointing was all she could do. Across the darkening water, in the fortress wing, one window that had been dark lit suddenly yellow — and then went dark again. Then lit. Dark. Lit.

Two flashes. A pause. Two more.

*Still here.*

Ulyana gripped the doorframe and laughed once, a strange broken sound halfway between laughing and the other thing, and pressed her palm flat against the cold wood as if she could push warmth across the water by sheer will.

"He's inside," she whispered. "Milo. He's *inside.*"

Milo squinted across the harbor, and his face went through doubt and arrived somewhere better than belief. But even as the signal blinked its patient message across the water, Ulyana's eye caught something else — movement below the fortress walls, where the supply road met the quay. A motor lorry, army grey, backing toward the water gate in the last of the light. Soldiers unloading it. Not supplies.

Timbers. Rope.

Scaffolding poles.

Tomorrow was six days, and the fortress was building a gallows.
