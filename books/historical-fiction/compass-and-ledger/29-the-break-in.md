## 29. The Break-In

Milo Jans had learned three things in his nineteen years, and he had learned all three of them in the Eriksen print shop, which said something either about the shop or about Milo.

First: never lick your fingers after handling red ink. Second: when Miss Ulyana said *just one more proof*, she meant four. Third — and this was the one that mattered this particular gray morning — the difference between a galley of type and a coffin was mostly a matter of what was inside it.

He was thinking about that because there was, at that very moment, an actual coffin standing in the middle of the composing room, and inside the coffin, wrapped in sawdust and sacking, were eleven galleys of type that could get every one of them shot.

The coffin had arrived two days earlier, ordered by the Vestergaard family for old Herr Ludvig Vestergaard, who had died at eighty-one of nothing more dramatic than being eighty-one. It stood waiting for its fittings, smelling of fresh pine and varnish, plain as Sunday. And Milo, who had been carrying galleys down to Signe's cellar press every night for a week, had looked at it the way a starving man looks at a bread cart.

"If we're caught," Ulyana had said, when he'd suggested it, "they'll open it."

"Nobody opens a coffin," Milo had said. "It's bad luck. Even soldiers have mothers."

She'd stared at him for a long moment, and then she'd laughed — a short, startled laugh, the first one in days — and said, "You are either a genius or a lunatic, Milo, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Both," Milo had said modestly. "It's why Father kept me on."

That had been yesterday. This morning, at half past nine, with Anton still gone and not back from the fortress where he'd gone scouting — and wasn't *that* a stone sitting in everyone's chest — the knock came at the loading door.

Not the ordinary knock. The ordinary knock was Mrs. Lindqvist's knuckle-rap, three quick beats, wanting her husband's business invoices. This knock was a boot. A boot applied by someone who considered doors a formality.

Ulyana's head came up from the case like a bird dog's. Across the room, Milo saw her hand move — small motion, almost nothing — toward the coffin, then away again. Too late to do anything now. Whatever happened next would have to happen as it fell.

"Compositors' entrance," Milo called out, because his mouth had always been faster than his sense of self-preservation. "Unless you're here about the funeral, in which case, my condolences."

The door opened, and soldiers came through it.

There were six of them. Milo counted automatically, the way his father had taught him to count sheets off the press — quickly, accurately, and without hope that the number would be better than expected. Six soldiers, mud on their boots, rifles slung with the lazy carelessness of men who knew exactly how much a rifle was worth in Vessengard in 1919. Behind them came a sergeant with a paper, and behind the sergeant came the smell of wet wool and gun oil, and behind *that* came the silence of Ulyana Eriksen deciding not to be afraid yet.

"Search," said the sergeant, waving his paper as though the word itself were the warrant. "By order of the Provisional Authority."

"Of course," Ulyana said pleasantly. "Mind the type cases. Some of them are older than the Provisional Authority."

* * *

Here is what Milo knew, in the part of his mind that stayed cold and clear while the rest of him performed the role of Nervous Apprentice, a role that required no acting whatsoever:

Eleven galleys of type sat in a coffin ten feet from the sergeant's left elbow.

Upstairs, his mother's cousin Anders had been told to stay away from the shop for a week, which meant Anders was probably drunk somewhere within sight of it, but that was Anders's problem.

And Anton had not come back.

That last fact kept trying to climb to the front of Milo's attention, and he kept pushing it back down, because if he let himself think about Anton — about checkpoints and prison cells and the fortress on the hill with its walls like a closed fist — then his hands would shake, and shaking hands were worse than lying eyes. A compositor's hands had to be steady. His father used to say it: *the hand confesses before the tongue does.* So Milo folded his hands into his apron and made them into hands that had nothing to confess except ink.

The soldiers fanned out through the shop with the enthusiasm of men searching a place they didn't understand. That was something, at least. A print shop to a soldier was a cave full of mysterious metal furniture; you could hide a cannon in a print shop and a soldier would step over it looking for rifles.

One of them pulled open a type case drawer and frowned at the little metal letters as though they might be ammunition.

"Sorts," Milo offered helpfully. "Forty-four point Caslon. There's a man in the Fish Quarter who'd shoot us both for spilling those."

The soldier looked at him. Milo looked back with the open, guileless face of a boy who had spent his whole life being the least important person in any given room. It was, he had found, a remarkably effective disguise. People looked right through a boy like Milo. He had once carried a stolen goose through a police checkpoint under his coat on that principle alone, though that story belonged to a different and hungrier winter.

The sergeant, meanwhile, had produced a notebook and was consulting it with the air of a man reading scripture very slowly.

"Eriksen," he read. "Printers. Manifest contracts, government orders." His eyes came up. "You've been short on deliveries."

"We've been short on everything," Ulyana said. "Paper, ink, staff, luck. The war took our compositor-in-chief, may he rest." She crossed herself, briefly, and Milo felt the floor tilt under him, because she was using her father — using his death, which might not even be his death — as a shield, and doing it so smoothly that even Milo nearly believed the grief was simple.

The sergeant wrote something down. "Name?"

"Milo Jans. Apprentice." He bobbed his head. "Third year. Well, third year-ish. There was a disruption."

"There was a war," the sergeant said, without humor, and moved along the row of cases.

* * *

The problem with hiding evidence in a coffin, Milo discovered, was that a coffin draws the eye eventually. It is, by its nature, furniture with ambitions. One of the younger soldiers drifted toward it, the way people drift toward anything shaped like death, and Milo watched him come with the peculiar double vision of terror: everything slowing down, becoming very detailed, the grain of the pine planks, the sawdust crumbs on the floor, the soldier's thumb hooked in his belt.

"That's the Vestergaard job," Milo said.

The soldier stopped.

"Old gentleman died Tuesday." Milo shook his head sadly. "Eighty-one. They're coming for the fittings this afternoon. You want to explain to the widow why the army delayed her husband's funeral?"

"I'm not explaining anything to anybody's widow," the soldier said, with feeling. He'd seen things, clearly. Or at minimum he'd refused things to widows before. "What's in it?"

"Air," Milo said. "And sawdust. And, God willing, Herr Vestergaard, Thursday."

The soldier snorted — the first human sound Milo had heard from any of them — and moved on. Milo exhaled through his nose, slow and controlled, and did not look at Ulyana, because looking at Ulyana would have been looking at the coffin, and looking at the coffin was forbidden by every law of nature.

But she had heard. Of course she had heard. And when he risked the smallest glance, he found her watching him with an expression he'd never seen on her face before — something between astonishment and pride, as if a piece of furniture she'd bought for two kroner at auction had turned out to be mahogany.

*The coffin,* he thought wildly, *cost more than that.* But the thought dissolved into the noise of boots on floorboards.

They searched upstairs. Milo heard them moving through the flat above — his mother's lodgings, the little kitchen, the room where Mrs. Eriksen lay most days now by the window with her blankets and her silence. He heard a door open, and Ulyana's voice go up the stairs after it, quick and firm: "My mother is ill. She sees no one. She has seen no one since the armistice began, and frankly neither have I, we're a household of hermits with a printing habit—"

Milo held his breath and listened to the house.

Mrs. Eriksen, to her eternal credit, played her part. Milo heard the thin, cracked voice — thinner than usual, pitched perfectly at the register of a woman too tired for the world — saying something about her heart, about papers, about whether young men had mothers of their own. Two minutes later the soldiers came back downstairs looking embarrassed, the way men do when they've been made to feel like bullies by somebody's grandmother.

One of them, the youngest, actually apologized.

"It's fine," Ulyana told him kindly. "You're just doing your job. We all do jobs we'd rather not." And Milo, sorting sorts with steady hands and a hammering heart, thought: *she has become terrifying.* Somewhere between the letters arriving and today, Miss Ulyana had turned into a person who could look a soldier in the eye and lie to him with her mother's health and mean every word twice. He wasn't sure when it had happened. He suspected it had something to do with the same thing that was keeping Anton out past sunrise.

The sergeant, to his credit, was harder to embarrass. He came back to the composing room and walked it again, slowly, and Milo watched him the way you watch a cat near a fish stall. The sergeant stopped at the locked back room — the one with the press fittings, the one whose key Ulyana wore on a string under her collar.

"This door."

"Store room," Ulyana said. "Furniture from when the shop was bigger than the business. Do you want me to fetch the key? It's upstairs, under—" 

"No." The sergeant looked at the door a long moment. Then he took out his notebook and wrote in it, and the scratch of the pencil was the loudest sound in the building. "We'll return," he said. "With the locksmith, if necessary."

"You'll find a lot of furniture," Ulyana said.

"And," the sergeant said, tucking the notebook away, "you'll find us thorough."

At the door, he paused. Turned. Looked directly at the coffin — directly, deliberately, the way a man looks at a thing he means to remember — and then at Milo, and then at Ulyana, and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anything he could have said.

Then they were gone, and the boot-knock faded up the street, and the shop was quiet, and Milo discovered that his legs had been holding more than he'd asked of them.

He sat down on the floor. Right there, next to the coffin, apron and all.

"Well," he said, to nobody. "That went well."

* * *

Ulyana locked the front door, then the loading door, then stood with her back against the wood and let out a breath that seemed to have been in storage since September.

"They knew," she said.

"They suspected," Milo said. "There's a difference. Suspecting is what sergeants are paid for. Knowing requires finding things, and they didn't find anything, because I am—" he gestured at himself, supine among the sawdust, "—a genius."

"A lunatic."

"The two are frequently confused in great men."

Ulyana slid down the door until she was sitting too, and for a while they just breathed, the two of them on the floor of the shop like children playing at shipwreck, the coffin looming over them with its cargo of treason and sawdust.

"He looked at it," Milo said, quieter now. "The sergeant. At the end."

"I saw."

"He didn't open it."

"No."

"Why not?"

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. Outside, a cart rattled past; the normal sounds of a normal street, indifferent to whether the world ended. "Because opening coffins costs paperwork," she said finally. "And because he wants to come back with a reason. With the locksmith. With a warrant that names us." She tipped her head back against the door. "Brandt," she added, half to herself. "This had Brandt's manners all over it. The courtesy. The writing things down. The leaving."

"You think the inspector knows?"

"I think Inspector Brandt knows more than he says, less than he could, and exactly as much as suits him." She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "Which means we have perhaps two days. Maybe three. After that, the coffin gets opened, and then the question isn't whether we're caught, it's only who's standing nearest when they are."

Milo looked at the coffin. Eleven galleys. Her father's testimony, set letter by letter in twelve-point Baskerville over four nights, every 'f' kerned the way only a dead-or-not-dead man's daughter could bear to kern it. Three thousand copies' worth of beginning, sitting in a box built for Herr Vestergaard, who had lived eighty-one years and died of it and never once, so far as anyone knew, committed an act of journalism.

"So we move it tonight," Milo said.

"To Signe's? The routes—"

"Are burned or watched, yes, I know." Milo got his legs under him and stood, and found, somewhat to his surprise, that they worked. "So we don't use a route. We use a funeral."

Ulyana looked at him.

"The Vestergaards come tomorrow for the fittings. Thursday for the burial. St. Ansgar's, full procession, six mourners and a choir." He was talking fast now, the plan assembling itself in his mouth the way type assembled under his fingers, one piece clicking against the next. "Nobody searches a hearse. Nobody stops a funeral for a dead old man who died of being old. We put the galleys in the undertaker's wagon under the wreaths, I drive it myself — I've driven the Lindqvist cart a hundred times, a wagon's a wagon — and we take it around by the harbor road, slow as Sunday, and Signe's nephew meets us at the chandler's yard with his barrow."

"That's insane," Ulyana said.

"It's also," Milo said, "Thursday."

She laughed again — that startled laugh, the rare one — and then pressed both hands over her mouth because laughing felt dangerous now, felt like something the sergeant's pencil had written down in advance. But her eyes above her hands were bright, and afraid, and alive.

"Milo," she said, lowering her hands. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, it's you in the wagon. Alone. With all of it." She said it plainly, without softening, because she had stopped softening things sometime around the raid next door, and he understood why, and he loved her for it in the way you love family — flatly, permanently, without needing to say so.

"Miss Ulyana," Milo said, picking sawdust off his apron, "I carried a goose through a police checkpoint when I was fourteen. A wagon of type is heavier, but it argues less."

He expected her to laugh. Instead she crossed the floor and put her arms around him, quick and fierce, ink-smelling and trembling slightly at the edges, and Milo, astonished, patted her back with the gentle awkwardness of a boy who had lost his own mother too early and forgotten what this was for.

"For the record," she said into his shoulder, "you're not the apprentice anymore. You're the junior partner. When this is over, I'm putting your name on the door."

"Under yours or over?"

"Don't push it."

They were still standing there, the printer and the junior partner, when the knock came again at the loading door — soft this time, three quick raps, a knuckle-rap, and Milo's heart leapt and sank in the same instant, because he knew that knock, everyone knew that knock, it was Mrs. Lindqvist wanting her invoices, except Mrs. Lindqvist had been dead wrong about invoices for weeks now, ever since the night Milo had watched her husband's fish cart roll out heavy and come back light.

Ulyana opened the door a crack.

Mrs. Lindqvist stood on the step, gray-faced, still in her market shawl, and she did not ask about invoices. She grabbed Ulyana's wrist through the gap and pulled her close and whispered, loud enough for Milo to hear every word:

"There are men at the fortress gate. Soldiers, unmarked wagon. And they've brought out a prisoner — tall, limping, bandaged under his coat — and the whole street's saying they're transferring him to the coast on Friday's boat." Her old eyes were wet and hard at once. "It's your courier, girl. They've got Anton. And there's a notice going up on the square this hour — offering information leading to the printers of *a certain pamphlet* — signed by Inspector Brandt himself."
