## 30. Four Days

He was late.

Anton had said dusk. He had said it the way he said most things — carefully, with his eyes on the middle distance, as though the words might detonate if he looked at them directly. *I'll scout the fortress approaches by day's end. Dusk. Don't wait supper.*

Ulyana had waited supper anyway. It sat under a folded cloth on the workbench between the composing stone and the ink slab — barley soup going lukewarm, a heel of black bread, two cups because she refused, out of pure stubbornness, to lay only one. The shop smelled of solvent and cold metal and, beneath that, the ghost of her father's pipe tobacco, which had soaked so deeply into the rafters over thirty years that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out. She had stopped minding that years ago. Tonight it felt like a hand on the back of her neck.

The clock in the front room struck seven. Then half past.

"He's been later," Milo said from the stone, where he was distributing type with more noise than necessity. Her apprentice had developed a whole vocabulary of clatters and thumps designed to fill silences neither of them wanted to inhabit. "Remember the night he came back from Signe's? We'd eaten everything. I ate his share. I'm not proud of it, but I don't regret it either."

"He wasn't scouting a fortress that night."

"No," Milo admitted. His hands stilled on the type case. "He wasn't."

They had agreed — all of them, in Signe's cellar three nights ago, voices low over the rehearsal proofs — that Anton would go alone. He had argued for it with the flat logic of a man who had been a soldier and knew exactly what soldiers did to strangers loitering near walls with guns on them. *One man walking is a man. Two men walking is a patrol or a plot.* Ulyana had hated the argument precisely because it was sound. She had spent twenty-nine years being the person who stayed behind while history went out the loading door, and she had sworn off it, and here she was again, standing at a window with a lamp turned down to a coal, watching a street empty of everything but wind and the paper-hawkers' leavings.

At eight o'clock there came a knock at the loading door. Three raps, then one — their signal, Milo's invention, which he had insisted upon with the gravity of a admiral reviewing a fleet. Ulyana crossed the shop in six strides and hauled the door open.

It was not Anton.

It was the boy from the fish cart — Joren, seventeen, cheeks wind-scoured, who had been carrying bundles for them since the planning began and who had never once asked what was in them. He stood in the doorway holding his cap in both fists, and his face told her everything before his mouth opened.

"Frøken Eriksen." He swallowed. "There were patrols. By the harbor road. They took him."

* * *

What they knew, in the first hour, amounted to almost nothing, and they turned it over and over like a coin too worn to read. Joren had been watering his horse near the fish market when he saw two gendarmes walk a limping man toward the fortress gate. Limping — that was something. A man taken alive could limp; a man shot could not. The coat had been dark. The man had not called out. Of course he hadn't called out; Anton would sooner bite through his own tongue than say a name aloud within earshot of a gendarme, and every name he protected was one of theirs.

Milo bolted the loading door and then, feeling that insufficient, dragged the crate of spare quoins in front of it, which would stop nobody but made him feel better, and Ulyana let him feel better.

"They'll question him," Milo said. "That's all. Question him and hold him."

"And when the questioning doesn't work?" Ulyana heard her own voice come out level, and was distantly surprised by it. "He has passes in his pocket with your forgery on them, Milo. He has the route memorized. He knows about Signe's cellar. He knows about this shop."

"He'll give them nothing."

"You can't know that."

"No," Milo said quietly. "But I can believe it very hard, and right now believing hard is the only trade open to me."

She laughed — a short, awful sound, surprised out of her — and then pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and let herself, for exactly sixty seconds by the shop clock, be a woman whose person had been taken. Grief and fear had shapes now, after these weeks. She knew their weights the way she knew the weight of a galley of set type, could lift them without dropping them if she braced properly. Sixty seconds. Then she took her hands down.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're going to see Inspector Brandt."

* * *

Brandt received them in his office at the district station at nine in the evening, which told her he had expected her before she arrived. He was a tidy man gone slightly untidy around the collar — the armistice preparations had everyone working long hours — and he poured tea without asking, which was either courtesy or theater, and with Brandt it was impossible to say where one ended.

"I understand you've lost something," he said.

"I understand *you* have," Ulyana said. "A courier. Taken near the fortress this afternoon. I'd like to know what he's charged with."

Brandt stirred his tea. The spoon clinked with terrible patience.

"Walking near a military installation during armistice negotiations," he said. "Which is an offense. Whether it's prosecuted depends very much on what else turns up in a man's pockets." He set the spoon down and looked at her, and for once he did not bother with the bland inspector's face. What was underneath was tired. "Frøken Eriksen. Sit down."

She sat. Milo, beside her, sat the way a spring coils.

"There are people above me," Brandt said, "who have known for some weeks that documents exist. Documents concerning General Halvard's conduct in the western campaigns. They have handled this knowledge the way such people handle things — by wanting it burned and being unable to find where it sleeps. Today they may have found a sleeping place." He drank his tea. "You should know that I am telling you this at some professional risk, because I want something understood clearly, and I'd rather it be understood by you than guessed at badly."

"Understood by me how?" Ulyana asked. "Are you offering to help us?"

"I am offering you arithmetic." He laid a slip of paper on the desk between them, folded once. "This arrived at my desk an hour ago from the commandant's office, misdirected, I'm sure quite accidentally, to a civilian censorship office rather than a military one. Sloppy staff work. It concerns a proposed exchange." He paused. "Both men for the plates."

The room got very small and very loud, the way rooms do when the floor moves. Ulyana did not touch the paper. "Both men."

"Your father is held in the fortress. Has been since spring. The transfer order that would have moved him out of the city was suspended pending the amnesty language — they intend him to vanish inside the general pardon, uncharged, untried, unnamed. A man erased by footnote." Brandt's voice stayed dry as ledger paper. "Unless, it seems, someone wishes to make him useful. Your courier gives them leverage they didn't expect to have tonight. The note proposes a simple transaction: the printing plates and all documentary materials, delivered intact, in exchange for both men released across the border into Finland. Safe passage guaranteed." He folded his hands. "Guaranteed, of course, by the same people whose guarantee the documents describe."

Silence. Somewhere below, a cell door closed; even the building seemed embarrassed by it.

"Why tell us?" Milo asked. His voice cracked on the second word and he pushed on regardless, sixteen years old and ferocious. "Why not just take the plates yourself?"

Brandt regarded him for a long moment. "Because I read the misdirected note before anyone else did, and I chose who else reads it. That is the entire extent of my power in this matter, young man, and I am exercising it." He rose, signaling an end, and at the door he added, not quite looking at her: "The exchange is proposed for tomorrow sundown. You will want to think about what a guarantee from those men is worth. And you will want to think about what your father would say. You know his hand better than anyone alive."

Then they were out in the street, and the cold hit like water, and Ulyana stood under the gas lamp with the folded note in her glove and discovered that her legs would hold her after all.

* * *

They did not go straight home. They walked, because walking was thinking, up past the harbor where the armistice bunting was already strung between poles — red and white and blue, cheerful as a festival, four days early. Posters of General Halvard covered the customs house wall, ten feet tall, jaw like a cliff, eyes fixed on some heroic horizon that did not include any of the villages he had burned. Someone had drawn a small mustache on him in charcoal. Milo looked at it with naked longing, as if contemplating a career.

"If we trade," Milo said, "it's over. Not just the pamphlet. Everything. Signe sits in a cell while they walk free with everything they need to bury it forever. And they won't release anyone, Uli. You know that. Once they have the plates—"

"I know."

"—I mean, I've forged documents for a living these three weeks, and even *I* wouldn't put a guarantee on paper that flimsy—"

"*I know*, Milo." She stopped walking. The harbor wind pulled at her scarf. Down the quay, a fish cart rattled home late, lamps swinging, and she thought of Joren's white face and Anton's dark coat and the sixty seconds she had allowed herself and could not afford again. "Here is what I keep arriving at," she said, more gently. "It isn't a choice between my father and the truth. That's the shape they've cut it into so that whichever way we jump, we're the ones who killed something precious. If we trade, they erase him anyway — a pardon buries him deeper than a bullet would. If we refuse and print, the truth does more for him than any trade could, because a public cannot be pardoned into forgetting what it has read." She was building the argument as she spoke it, plank by plank, and finding — this was the strange part — that it held her weight as well as her own. "They've offered us a bargain where we lose either way, dressed as a bargain where we win. Which means somewhere in it, they're frightened."

"Frightened of a pamphlet."

"Frightened of four days." She looked up at Halvard's enormous printed face, smug even through the charcoal mustache. "He negotiated for a year to get his crimes into the amnesty clause. He's four days from succeeding. People who are winning don't send notes through inspectors. People who are winning don't need to."

Milo was quiet the whole length of the quay. Then: "So we print."

"So we print."

"And Anton? And your da? We just—" His voice caught on the machinery of it, the sheer arithmetic of leaving two men inside a fortress.

"We make the truth worth more to them than the men are," Ulyana said. "Tomorrow I'm going back to Brandt with a counter-offer. And Milo—" she put her hand briefly on his shoulder, that bony, loyal shoulder, "—tonight we pull the forme off the press and carry every plate out of this city before sunrise. Let them offer exchanges for things that no longer exist."

* * *

The shop at midnight was a different country. They worked by shaded lamplight with the shutters barred: breaking down the forme on Signe's press — no, the plates themselves, the stereotype shells, wrapped in sacking and packed in a fish barrel that Joren swore on his mother's life he could move through three checkpoints blindfolded, having done nearly that sober. The documents went into a second barrel, padded with wood shavings. Milo worked with his jaw set, saying nothing, doing everything, and twice Ulyana caught him looking at the loading door as though expecting soldiers to come through it, and twice he looked away and lifted the next plate as if it weighed nothing.

Her mother came down the stairs at one o'clock, gray braid over her shoulder, dressing gown belted with terrible precision, and surveyed the dismantling of the family business without a flicker of surprise.

"The soup's still on the bench," Astrid Eriksen observed. "Cold. Whoever it was meant to eat it is eating worse tonight." She picked up the untouched bread, broke it in three pieces, and handed them around, and so they stood in the ruined print shop at one in the morning eating bread like a communion, and Ulyana found her eyes burning and blinked it away.

"Mama," she said. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't," her mother said. "And if it does, we are Eriksens. We know how to keep records." She brushed crumbs from her fingers, kissed her daughter once, hard, on the forehead, and went back up the stairs, and Ulyana understood suddenly where she herself had learned to be brave in a flat voice.

By three the barrels were gone into the night on a fish cart, and the shop held nothing incriminating but its own smell, and Ulyana sat alone at her father's old desk with Brandt's note unfolded under the lamp.

*Both men for the plates,* it said, in a clerk's anonymous hand. *Sundown tomorrow. Guarantee of safe passage. Commandant's office.*

She read it until the words wore smooth. Then she took a fresh sheet of the shop's best paper, dipped a pen, and began, in her clearest compositor's hand, to draft her reply — counting under her breath, the way she counted ems and ens, measuring out sentences like lines of type.

*Inspector — There will be no exchange, because there is nothing left to exchange. The plates left the city hours ago, in more places than you can search. But you should know that your name appears nowhere in them, and that this is the last hour in which you could have arranged for that to remain true. Sundown tomorrow, my office. Come alone. I have a proposition of my own.—U.E.*

She read it back. It was, she thought, the first truly dangerous sentence she had ever set — and she hadn't needed lead and ink to do it.

Outside, the sky over the fortress was going gray at the edges. Four days.
