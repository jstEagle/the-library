## 31. Three Days

My mother used to say that a woman should never visit the house of a man she intends to threaten without first eating something substantial. She said it about dinner parties, in the context of not drinking wine on an empty stomach, but I found it transferred reasonably well to the blackmailing of state inspectors.

So that morning I ate two slices of black bread with butter so thin it was more of an opinion than a spread, and I drank no coffee at all, because I wanted my hands steady, and then I put on my second-best coat — the best one being reserved, I had decided, for the day we won — and I walked to the Ministry of Information as though I went there every Tuesday.

Which, in a way, I now did.

* * *

Brandt's office had not improved since my last visit. It was one of those rooms that seemed furnished entirely from the estates of the recently deceased: a desk of dark oak, a filing cabinet with a dent in it shaped like somebody's knee, and a portrait of the Minister hanging slightly crooked, which I suspected was Brandt's only remaining act of rebellion.

He was standing at the window when I came in, watching the street below, where carpenters were already raising scaffolding for the armistice bunting. He looked, as always, like a man who had been up since four o'clock reading documents he wished didn't exist.

"Fräulein Eriksen." He did not sound surprised. Brandt never sounded surprised; it was his single greatest professional asset. "You've come about the paper stock."

"I've come about your tea," I said. "You promised me real coffee last time and produced chicory. I've come to collect."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile that had been audited and found non-compliant. He gestured to the chair across from his desk, and he made the coffee himself on the little spirit stove by the cabinet — actual coffee, I noticed, from a tin he kept behind the files marked OBSOLETE CORRESPONDENCE, which struck me as the correct place to hide anything worth having.

We sat. We drank. The scaffolding creaked outside.

"You have three days," Brandt said at last, setting down his cup. "Perhaps less. I want to be clear with you, because unlike some of my colleagues, I prefer clarity: there are men in this building who already suspect your shop. There is a warrant drafted. It is waiting for one more signature, and that signature will arrive when General Halvard's staff finishes reviewing the security arrangements for Thursday. If you give me the courier and the plates now, you walk away. Immunity for yourself, your mother, the boy. This is the last time I can make that offer. After tomorrow it leaves my hands entirely."

I had rehearsed this moment on the walk over. In rehearsal, my voice had been steady and my arguments arrived in elegant order, like type set by a master. In the room itself, my heart was doing something undignified against my ribs, and I heard myself say:

"May I see yesterday's printing order for the Ministry?"

Brandt blinked. Of all the sentences he had prepared for, I suspect that one wasn't among them.

"The what?"

"Your ministry placed an order with every commercial print shop in Vessengard last month. Commemorative materials for the signing. Programmes, broadsheets, a commemorative history of the war effort — forty pages, heavily illustrated." I reached into my satchel. "We printed our share of the run. Very patriotic. Very dull. And here is the interesting thing about printing, Inspector, the thing nobody outside the trade ever thinks about: paper has a memory."

I laid the proofs on his desk. Not the pamphlet — God, no, never the pamphlet — but the galleys of the official commemorative history, the ones we'd been legally given, the ones any compositor in the city could produce.

"Every sheet that passes through a press takes an impression of everything pressed before it," I said. "Set-off, we call it. Ghosts of previous pages, faint, but there, if you know where to look and hold the paper at an angle to the light. Your commemorative history praises General Halvard on pages six, eleven, and nineteen. Our ghost-impressions tell a different story about the same man. We pulled comparison sheets. They're very convincing. They're also, I'm told, admissible under the press-inspection statutes — your own statutes, Inspector, chapter nine. The ones written to catch printers who print lies. Nobody ever imagined they'd be used to catch a ministry that does."

This was, I will be honest, approximately forty percent true. The ghost impressions were real — they always are — but what they showed was mostly a smudged fragment of a shipping manifest and half a sentence from Milo's practice exercises about a farmer and a stubborn goose. But Brandt could not know how faint they were without seizing them, sending them to an examiner, and opening a formal inquiry into the Eriksen print shop — an inquiry that would ask why the shop's records were interesting enough to examine, which would lead, by crooked stairs, to everything.

Bluffing, I have come to understand, is simply typesetting with words instead of metal. You arrange what you have in the order that makes the strongest impression, and you trust the reader to fill in the rest with their own fears.

Brandt picked up the topmost galley. He held it toward the window. For a long moment the only sound in the room was the scaffolding and, distantly, a brass band rehearsing something triumphant.

"This proves nothing," he said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "It proves nothing beautifully. That's rather the point. Nothing is very easy to prove when the people who could disprove it keep getting arrested."

* * *

Here is what I had not told Anton, or Milo, or my mother, who had watched me leave that morning with the expression of a woman handing her daughter over to the tide: I had not come primarily to save ourselves.

I had come because Signe Holm was sitting in a cell on Kastanje Street, sixty-two years old, with grease permanently under her fingernails and a press she'd hidden through two wars now sitting sealed in her cellar, and because the last thing she'd said to the arresting officers, according to the neighbor who witnessed it, was *"Mind the type. It's hand-cast and worth more than any of you."*

Signe had looked at Anton's documents — testimony gathered at terrible cost, names, dates, villages, orders signed in Halvard's own hand — and she had said yes. Not quickly, not easily, but yes. And then soldiers had come for her because a drunk cousin in a tavern had a big mouth, and now the amnesty being drafted into Thursday's armistice would erase not only the crimes but the arrest, the cell, the whole record, as if none of it had ever needed courage at all.

I could not get Signe out of her cell. I was a compositor with flour-paste hands and a press hidden behind a coffin. But I could do what my father had done, what Signe had done, what the whole quiet machinery of ink had always done: I could put a name on a page and make it impossible to un-see.

Which is why, when Brandt finally set down the galley and looked at me with those tired archivist's eyes, I said the thing I had walked across the city to say.

"My name isn't in the document, Inspector. Yours is."

The band outside hit its crescendo, unfortunately drowning me somewhat, so I said it again.

"Yours. Inspector Henning Brandt, Ministry of Information, Censorship Division. Dated entries. Dates you inspected our shop. Dates you inspected Signe Holm's cellar — yes, we know about that too. Dates on which evidence passed across your desk and went into the cabinet marked obsolete." I tapped the dented cabinet. "The pamphlet calls you a man who looked away. Those are nearly its kindest words about anyone. Halvard gets worse. The generals get much worse. But you — you get something I think you'll find harder to survive than condemnation. You get a footnote. History loves a footnote, Inspector. Ask any printer."

"That is extortion," Brandt said. His voice was perfectly level. His hand, on the cup, was not.

"It's a proof sheet," I said. "It exists whether you read it or not. That's the difference between us, you and I. You can choose what to look at. Print doesn't get to choose. It just goes on existing, patiently, until someone holds it up to the light."

Silence. Outside, the band started again from the top, because bands always do.

"And what," Brandt said at last, very carefully, "would remove my name from this document?"

"Nothing," I said, and enjoyed, briefly and shamefully, the small collapse in his shoulders. "It's printed. Three thousand copies, distributed through channels you couldn't shut down with a battalion — fish carts, choir lofts, funerals. By Thursday morning it will be in ten thousand hands. That is not leverage, Inspector; that is weather. What I'm offering you is not removal. It's amendment."

I slid the last paper across the desk. One page. My own typesetting — you could tell, my kerning has gotten good under pressure — and unsigned.

"A second edition," I said. "Printed Friday, if there is a Friday for us. It contains everything the first one does, plus the inquiry file: the warrant that waited for a signature, the amnesty clause buried on page thirty-one of the armistice terms, the whole machinery, named. Unless, before Thursday, that warrant loses its signature. Unless the review board receives certain documents — the ones Signe Holm's press already photographed, the ones already abroad by now, in case you're wondering, in case someone decides to arrest everyone in this room — unless a certain inspector files his own report, dated properly, recording what he knew and when. Then the second edition tells a different story. It tells it about a man who, late, at cost, chose once."

Brandt stared at the page for a long time.

"You'd print my redemption," he said. It didn't sound like gratitude. It sounded like a man discovering the exact dimensions of his cage and noticing, with some wonder, that he himself had built most of it.

"We're printers," I said. "We print what's true. Which version is true is rather up to you between now and Thursday."

* * *

He saw me to the door. At the threshold he paused, and for a moment the inspector fell away and there was just a tired man of fifty-odd with coffee on his breath and a career folding quietly in his hands.

"Your father," he said. "Kristof Eriksen. I processed his file in 'sixteen. Death-in-service, standard form." He looked at the middle distance, at the years. "There was an annex to that file. A discrepancy report. I flagged it. My superior told me flags were for laundry." His jaw worked. "I was younger then. I thought the ledger balanced itself."

"It doesn't," I said. "Somebody has to carry it."

"Yes," said Brandt. "Well." He straightened his coat, and the inspector returned, buttoned up to the chin. "Tell the courier the eastern gate of the fortress changes its transfer schedule on Wednesday nights. Tell him the night guard drinks at the Anchor and cannot hold his liquor. And tell him—" he stopped, visibly surprised at himself, "—tell him to be careful. I've grown accustomed to being threatened by your family. It would be inconvenient to lose the tradition."

I walked out past the scaffolding, past the bunting going up in cheerful red and white, past the band, and I did not let my knees go soft until I was two streets away, at which point I leaned against a fishmonger's wall and laughed until the fishmonger came out to look at me.

Because here was the thing I hadn't dared feel upstairs: I had walked into the Ministry carrying nothing but paper and nerve, and I had walked out with the fortress gate schedule.

Power, it turns out, is mostly a printing problem. Somebody writes the story, somebody signs the story, and everybody else believes the story because it looks official — right typeface, right seal, right bunting. Change who holds the type, and the whole ceremony comes loose at the seams.

Milo was minding the counter when I got back to the shop, and he took one look at my face and dropped the composing stick he was cleaning.

"We're not dead?" he said.

"Not yet."

"Anton's back. He's been pacing a hole behind the coffin since noon. Also your mother has made soup, which means she's terrified." He hesitated, then, in the tone of a boy trying very hard to be a man: "Did it work? Whatever it was?"

"Ask me Thursday," I said, hanging up my coat. Then, because he'd earned it, because we all earn what we're given these days: "It worked, Milo. He gave us the gate schedule. Wednesday night, the eastern postern, guard rotation at eleven."

Behind me, from the doorway to the back room, came Anton's voice — hoarse, urgent, wrong in a way I felt before I understood it.

"Ulyana. Come here. Please."

And when I reached him, he was standing over the table where he'd laid out the fortress plan he'd drawn from memory, and his face told me the news had arrived ahead of me, and that three days had just become fewer.

"They're moving him tomorrow," Anton said. "Not Thursday. Tomorrow. First wagon after dawn."

* * *

Tomorrow. The word sat in the room like cold iron.

Tomorrow we were meant to be running the final plates. Tomorrow Milo was forging passes and my mother was sewing pamphlets into coat linings. Tomorrow, and every hour of it, already spoken for.

Anton met my eyes across the table. My mother appeared in the stairwell with the soup and, seeing our faces, set it down untouched, which may have been the gravest omen yet.

"Well," I heard myself say, reaching for my coat again, fingers steadier than they had any business being. "Then I suppose we'd better decide which of us is going to be at the eastern gate at dawn."
