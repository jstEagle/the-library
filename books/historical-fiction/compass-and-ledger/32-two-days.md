## 32. Two Days

The press had never been asked to work a miracle before, and it let them know about it.

Ulyana stood at the flatbed at half past nine in the evening, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to the second knuckle, and listened to the machine breathe. It was an old Heidelberg that her father had bought secondhand from a bankrupt newspaper in Kolvik, and it had opinions. Tonight its opinion was that three thousand copies of eight pages was an unreasonable demand for a machine of its years, and it intended to complain about every single one.

"Feed it gently," Signe had said when she'd arrived with her coat still smelling of cellar damp and coal smoke. "A press is like a horse. It knows when you're afraid, and it knows when you're lying to it."

"And what does it know now?" Milo had asked.

"That we're lying," Ulyana had said. "We told ourselves this would be easy."

* * *

They had moved everything down into Signe's cellar two nights before — type locked in chases, the galleys wrapped in oilcloth, the paper stock smuggled over in three trips disguised as a delivery of rags for the rag-and-bone man who owed Milo's family a favor and asked no questions because he was paid not to. The coffin trick had gotten the most delicate pieces through: the galleys lay inside a coffin ordered for old Herr Voss's funeral, under a layer of sawdust, while four soldiers at the checkpoint discussed whether Herr Voss had been a large man or merely a beloved one.

Now the cellar was lit by three lamps and one spirit of shared purpose, which was brighter than all of them but harder to read by.

Signe Holm worked the press itself. She was sixty-one years old and had been setting type since she was eleven, and she operated the Heidelberg the way other women of her age kneaded bread — without looking, without hurry, with a running commentary of small curses. Anton fed the sheets. Ulyana pulled proofs and checked them against the corrected copy. Milo stacked, collated, carried, counted, and talked, because Milo could no more work in silence than a kettle could boil quietly.

"Page four," he said, fanning a stack. "Page four is my favorite page. Do you know why? Because it has the sentence where your father says Halvard ordered the wells poisoned, and it says it in *our* typeface, on *our* paper, and somewhere tomorrow a fishmonger is going to hand it to a colonel."

"A fishmonger handing anything to a colonel," Signe said, tripping the press, "is more likely to be a knife fight."

"Then the pamphlet can be his weapon too. We're egalitarian."

"Feed," Signe said, and Anton fed.

The rhythm was the thing. Ulyana had learned that in twenty years around presses: find the rhythm and hold it, and hours stop being hours and become instead a kind of music you are inside of. Ink, sheet, pull, turn. Ink, sheet, pull, turn. The lamp flames leaned when the door draft came through, and the smell of the ink — her father's smell, the smell of her whole childhood, linseed and lampblack — filled the cellar until it seemed the walls themselves were made of print.

Somewhere above them Vessengard slept, or pretended to. Somewhere above them General Halvard slept in the Grand Hotel under guard, two days from signing his name to a peace that would also sign away the dead of three villages. Not tonight. Tonight the dead were going to get their say, three thousand times over.

"Ink's thinning on the left," Ulyana called.

"It's not thinning, it's *tired*," Signe said. "There's a difference. A tired press needs encouragement." She said something encouraging to it in what might have been Danish and might have been profanity, and the left margin came back dark as a confession.

* * *

At midnight they stopped for food, because Anton put his foot down — figuratively; literally he lowered himself onto a crate with the care of a man whose ribs were still negotiating their treaty. Mother had sent down a basket through Milo with instructions delivered verbatim and slightly terrifying: *eat it all, and if anyone is saving portions out of politeness, tell them I will know.*

There was black bread, hard cheese, pickled herring, and a jar of plum preserves that had survived two wars and one marriage and would presumably survive the apocalypse. They ate sitting on stacks of uncut paper, which Signe said was sacrilege and then did herself.

Anton ate slowly, watching the press the way a man watches a fire in winter. "I keep thinking," he said, "that I carried these words across four hundred kilometers in a satchel, and I thought the carrying was the hard part."

"The carrying was the easy part," Ulyana said. "Carrying is just walking. This—" she gestured at the galleys, the ink, the growing stacks, "—this is making it *true*. Words in one copy are a rumor. Words in three thousand copies are a fact. That's the whole secret of the trade, and governments know it, which is why they fear printers more than they fear armies."

"More than artillery?" Milo asked.

"Artillery kills you. Printers *embarrass* you. Armies recover from being killed. No general in history has ever recovered from being laughed at in front of his own troops."

Signe snorted. "Your father used to say almost exactly that. Word for word. You've become him, girl, did you know? Same hands, same sermon."

Ulyana looked at her own hands — black to the knuckle, a fresh cut on the thumb from a edge of tin type she hadn't seen in the lamplight. She didn't feel like becoming him. She felt like a woman very tired, very frightened, and happier than she had any right to be. Perhaps those were the same thing. Perhaps that was the whole secret of the trade too.

"Back to work," she said. "Two thousand to go."

* * *

It was at half past two, deep in the long middle of the run where the body forgets it has edges, that Milo hurt himself.

He was carrying a full stack of printed sheets from the press to the drying racks — forty pounds of paper, arms wrapped around it, chin holding the top sheet steady, talking as always ("—and when they read page six in the taverns, the ones about the supply officers selling the winter boots, there'll be *riots*, good riots, polite riots—") when his heel caught the leg of the drying rack and he went down.

He went down the way young men go down: entirely, instantly, and with the stack arriving on top of him like a small avalanche of truth. There was a crack — everyone heard it — and then a silence, and then Milo said, in a voice pitched carefully between pain and performance, "The sheets are fine."

"Milo—"

"The *sheets*, Ulyana. Check the sheets first. Priorities."

But his face had gone the color of the unprinted paper, and when they pulled the stack off him his left wrist was already swelling, bent at an angle wrists are not issued with. Signe took one look and began giving orders in the voice she must have used on apprentices for forty years: splint, board, the vinegar cloth from the first-aid box, and Milo sat on the floor cradling his arm and saying, "It's nothing, it's a sprain, I've had worse from falling off the hay cart—"

"You have not," Ulyana said. "You're white as type metal."

"I'm always white. I'm a printer. We're subterranean creatures."

Signe bound it fast and competent, two boards and a strip of linen, and pronounced it "cracked, not shattered, and you'll thank me in six weeks." Then she looked at Milo over her spectacles with an expression that dared him to say what they both knew he was going to say.

"I can still work," Milo said.

"You can't set type."

"I wasn't going to set type. One arm works fine. I can feed. I can stack. I can count. I can talk — *especially* I can talk. You need the talking, you'd all die of grimness without the talking."

"Milo." Ulyana crouched beside him. In the lamplight his eyes were wet, and not only from pain, and she understood suddenly that this wasn't stubbornness, or not only. This was a nineteen-year-old boy who had spent two weeks discovering that he belonged to something larger than a print shop, and who could not bear, with eight hundred copies still to pull, to be carried upstairs and put to bed like a child while history happened in the cellar without him.

She remembered being nineteen. She remembered believing history happened elsewhere, to grandees, in capitals. Milo had learned faster than she had.

"One-handed stacking," she said. "And if Signe says stop, you stop."

"If Signe says stop, I will stop so fast I'll leave a comet trail," Milo said solemnly, and got up, and swayed, and grinned at them all with his arm in its boards like a flag, and went back to work.

And the strange thing — the thing Ulyana would remember all her life — was that the work got better after that. Not faster. Better. As though the little accident had spent some of the room's fear, the way a storm spends the heat. Anton fed with a new steadiness. Signe's pulls came clean and dark. And Milo, one-armed, stacked each finished quire with the exaggerated care of a priest laying out relics, announcing the counts in a whisper like a litany: "Sixteen hundred. Sixteen fifty. Seventeen hundred, gentlemen and lady, seventeen hundred truths and counting."

At four in the morning, Ulyana pulled the sheet that made two thousand, held it up to the lamp, and found she was crying without having decided to.

It was only ink-smell and exhaustion, she told herself. It was only that the page in her hands contained her father's handwriting translated into type — his testimony, his fury, his grief for the men of the 14th who had frozen because their boots had been sold, his careful numbered list of dates and places that no amnesty clause could make unhappened. Two thousand copies of it. Her father had written these words alone in a fortress cell or a ditch or wherever he had written them, not knowing if they would ever be read by one living soul, and here they were multiplying in a cellar under a city that was about to pretend none of it had occurred.

"Hey," Anton said softly. He had come to stand beside her. Up close she could see the gray in his stubble and the fever-light finally gone out of his eyes. "He'd be proud."

"He'd tell me the kerning on page five is sloppy."

"He'd do both. Men like that always do both." Anton hesitated. "My mother was a laundress. When I was small I asked her how she could stand the work, boiling other people's sheets all day, and she said — *because clean is clean, no matter whose it was.*" He touched the edge of the proof sheet, careful, the way he touched everything now, as if the world were newly breakable. "This is clean, Ulyana. Whatever happens after. This is clean."

Ink, sheet, pull, turn. Two thousand and one.

* * *

They finished at ten minutes past seven in the morning, when the light through the cellar grate had gone from black to iron to the pale gold of a Vessengard spring, and Signe tripped the press one last time and then simply stood with her hand flat on its frame, breathing, like a rider dismounting after a long ride.

Three thousand and twelve copies. Twelve extra, because a press, like a horse, gives you more than you ask if you treat it right, and Signe declared the extras "for the archives, whatever archives survive."

The stacks stood along the walls in quires of twenty-five, wrapped in plain brown paper tied with string, looking for all the world like ordinary jobbing work — invoices, funeral notices, menus. Three thousand lies waiting to become the truth tomorrow.

Milo had fallen asleep sitting up against the paper bales, his splinted wrist resting on his knees, ink on his cheek, his mouth open, mid-sentence even in sleep by the look of him. Anton had draped his own coat over the boy an hour ago and said nothing about it. Now Anton himself was swaying on his feet, and Ulyana steered him to a crate and he went down onto it like a dropped marionette.

Signe banked the lamps. "Go home," she said. "Sleep. All of you. Tomorrow night the fish carts come, and the choir lofts, and whatever madness the boy has drawn up on his maps. You'll want your hands steady and your heads empty."

Ulyana climbed the stairs last. At the top she paused and looked back down: the little cellar glowing dim, the great silent press, the sleeping boy and the sleeping soldier and the old woman already sweeping, already humming, already beginning to clean the type for whatever came next.

Her hands were ruined — ink in every crease, the cut on her thumb throbbing, her shoulders a pair of hot wires. She had never loved them more.

She stepped out into the morning street. Vessengard was waking: milk carts, gulls, a bell somewhere counting the hour, posters of General Halvard on the hoardings looking noble and faintly absurd in the early light, as all posters do. Tomorrow he would sign. Tomorrow the fish carts would roll.

She was halfway home before she saw the notice pasted fresh on the customs-house wall, the paste still wet, the letters tall and official:

*BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY: effective immediately, all printing establishments within the city are subject to inspection of stock and records. Any person found in possession of unlicensed printed matter will be detained.*

Below it, the signature: *Insp. H. Brandt.*

Ulyana stood very still. Three thousand copies lay wrapped in brown paper in a cellar beneath a widow's house, and the man who knew her face, who had drunk her tea twice and watched her lie to him twice, had just made possession of them a crime.

Tomorrow the fish carts came. But tonight — tonight, perhaps sooner — so would Brandt.
