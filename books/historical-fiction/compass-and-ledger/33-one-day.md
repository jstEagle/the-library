## 33. One Day

The fish carts came at half past four in the morning, when the sky was still the color of dishwater and the gulls were arguing over the harbor's leavings as though they'd been personally wronged by it.

Ulyana had not slept. She had tried, once, lying down in her clothes on top of the counter covers, but her body had refused the offer on the grounds that it was being asked to lie down during the most important night of its life, and no thank you. So she had spent the hours re-checking bundles that did not need checking, counting copies that she had already counted twice, and drinking tea that had gone cold and bitter and which she drank anyway because her hands wanted something to do.

Milo arrived at five, gray-faced and moving with the careful stiffness of a boy whose ribs had been introduced to a press frame two nights ago and had not forgiven either party since.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You look like you lost an argument with a machine."

"I did. It won." He lowered himself onto a crate with the delicacy of an old man descending into church. "How is it that you look fine? You've slept less than I have."

"I'm running on terror," Ulyana said. "It's very efficient. Better than coffee. You should try it."

"I have terror. I also have ribs."

The cellar beneath Signe Holm's house smelled of brine and newsprint now — three thousand copies of *What Halvard Did*, wrapped in brown paper, tied with plain string, indistinguishable from any parcel of misprinted manifests or old ledgers if one didn't look too closely. Which was, of course, the entire idea. Nobody looks closely at paper. Paper is the least interesting thing in any city. That had been true all of Ulyana's life, and she had counted on it, and been counted invisible herself because of it, and today she intended to weaponize it thoroughly.

Anton came up from below with the last six bundles stacked against his chest, his shoulder still bound, his color better than it had been two weeks ago. He looked, Ulyana thought, like a man carrying firewood, which was precisely what he was supposed to look like, and there was something almost funny in how little ceremony the truth required. Three months of hiding, of coded ledgers, of kerning arguments held in whispers — and at the end of it all, the revolution fit inside ordinary brown paper and rode to town in a cart that smelled of herring.

"First cart's here," Anton said. "Jorunn's man. He wants to know if we're sure."

"We're sure," Ulyana said.

"He asked twice."

"We're sure twice."

Jorunn's man was a wide, weathered fellow named Peder who had fished the same waters for thirty years and had opinions about generals roughly equivalent to his opinions about weather: that both came and went, and neither should be trusted near small boats. He loaded the bundles under the false floor of his cart among the ice and the crates without ceremony, tamping them down with a practiced hand, and then he straightened, wiped his palms on his apron, and looked at Ulyana for a long moment.

"My sister's boy was at Kolvik," he said. "He didn't come back. They wrote us he died well." He spat off the dock, with feeling. "Well, then. Let's see what dying well actually meant."

Ulyana had prepared several reassuring things to say about the safety of the operation. She said none of them. Some transactions don't take receipts.

Peder climbed onto his seat, flicked the reins, and the cart rolled off into the gray morning, and Ulyana stood on the dock watching it go and felt her heart do something acrobatic and unauthorized in her chest.

"That's one," Milo said. "Two hundred copies. Out of three thousand."

"One," Anton agreed. Then, because he was who he was, he took out his notebook and made a mark in it, and Ulyana laughed — a real laugh, surprising them all, including herself.

"What?"

"Nothing. You're keeping a ledger of the revolution."

"Somebody has to," Anton said mildly. "Your father taught me that. Everything important goes through a ledger eventually."

* * *

By eight o'clock, Ulyana had learned the first great lesson of distribution, which was that after the planning ends, there is nothing whatsoever left to do.

The second cart had gone out at seven — choir loft route, via the Lutheran sexton who owed Signe a favor dating back to some scandal in 1904 that nobody would explain to her. The third was due at nine. Between these departures there was only waiting, and waiting in a cellar while your life's work drove away under fish ice turned out to be its own particular torture.

She went home to open the shop, because the shop still had to be the shop; that was the whole architecture of the alibi. She set type for a wedding invitation — the Bergstrom girl, marrying a flour merchant's son, the happy couple desiring *gold ink if possible* — and her mother sat by the window doing hem-stitching with hands that shook less than they had in months, and outside the window Vessengard went about its business in the pale spring light, entirely unaware that its history had changed owners overnight.

Or perhaps not unaware. That was the thing. By noon she would know.

At half past ten, Milo came in the front door faster than his ribs approved — she could see him pay for the speed in his face — and leaned on the counter with elaborate casualness.

"Taverns," he said.

"What about them?"

"The Anchorstone. The Gull. Old Marek's place by the customs house. They're reading it." His voice cracked slightly on *it*, and he covered by coughing. "I heard it myself. A stevedore stood up on a bench and read the whole first page out loud, and nobody stopped him, and then everybody started talking at once, and the barman — Ulyana, the barman *poured him a free one*."

She gripped the counter edge. The shop around her seemed suddenly very bright and very far away, the cases of type glittering like cities seen from above.

"Anyone stopped? Anyone detained?"

"Not that I heard. There's soldiers at the square for the armistice preparations, but they're standing around looking decorative. Brandt's men are checking licenses at the print shops." Milo grinned, and it hurt his face and he grinned anyway. "Checking the shops. While it's in every tavern in the harbor district. It's like searching the river for rain."

"It's like," Ulyana agreed, and then had to sit down on the stool, because her knees had filed notice of their intentions.

Her mother looked up from her hemming. "Girls," she said, meaning, apparently, *what has happened*, in the language of mothers.

"They're reading it, Mama," Ulyana said. "Out loud. In public."

Her mother considered this. Her needles paused, resumed. "Then mind you're home before dark," she said, which was not, Ulyana understood, really about dark at all.

* * *

In the afternoon, unable to bear the shop another minute, Ulyana walked down to the harbor with a marketing basket she had no intention of filling, and saw it for herself.

The Gull was loud. That was the first thing. Taverns were always loud, but this was a different register of loudness — not the dull roar of drunk men but the jagged, overlapping noise of people arguing about something that mattered, voices climbing over each other, chairs scraping, someone laughing hugely at something someone else had said with fury. Through the low doorway she could see a knot of men around a table, and in the center of the knot a sheet of cheap paper, gone soft at the folds from handling, passing from hand to hand like communion.

"—says here, testimony, sworn testimony, names and units—"

"—Kolvik, my God, my brother-in-law was at Kolvik—"

"—amnesty. Read that bit again. They're going to sign it *away*, put it in the fine print like a shipping surcharge—"

A woman near the door caught Ulyana's eye and jerked her chin toward the table. "You believe this?" she said. Not hostile. Hungry.

Ulyana's heart was going so hard she could feel it in her ears. "I believe," she said carefully, "that paper doesn't lie. People lie. Paper just repeats what somebody set down."

"And somebody set this down?"

"Somebody set it down letter by letter," Ulyana said, and discovered she could not keep a small, fierce pride out of her voice, and decided not to try.

The woman studied her a moment longer, then nodded slowly, as if a suspicion had been confirmed and filed away for later, and turned back to the noise. Ulyana bought nothing. Her basket stayed empty. She walked home along the quay with the wind coming off the water cold and clean, and felt simultaneously lighter than air and heavier than the harbor itself — exhilarated and terrified in exactly equal measure, so exactly balanced that she couldn't tell which one was winning, and suspected the answer was *neither, permanently, from now on.*

Because here was the other thing she had understood at the tavern door, the thing that put the weight in her chest: once read aloud, those pages belonged to everyone and no one. She couldn't recall them. She couldn't correct them. Three thousand copies were loose in a city of ninety thousand mouths, and by tomorrow they would be ten thousand copies, retold, argued over, misquoted, embroidered — alive. And things that are alive can grow, and things that are alive can be killed, and the soldiers standing decoratively in the square would not stand decoratively forever.

Water into sand. But sand shifts. And so does water.

* * *

Evening brought the tally, delivered to the shop's back room in the code of ordinary commerce: Jorunn reporting her fishmongers had moved their allotment "with the mackerel, no trouble"; the sexton reporting the choir lofts of three parishes supplied and "one verger asking questions, handled"; Peder sending word, via a boy and half a crown, that he'd left forty copies at the fortress workers' canteen, "where they'll do the most good, and don't ask me nothing else."

Anton spread the notebook on the worktable and drew his lines and columns, and Ulyana watched his pen move and felt something in her go quiet and steady.

"Sixty percent placed by nightfall," he said. "The rest by morning. And after that—" He set the pen down. "After that, it's out of our hands. Whatever happens now happens to all of them, everywhere at once."

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"Brandt will know within a day where it was printed. Maybe less. He'll know the paper stock."

"He already knows the paper stock. He inspected it himself in March and complained about our margins."

Anton looked at her across the table, and there was in his face something she had learned to read over these weeks: the soldier doing arithmetic about casualties. "Tomorrow we should move the plates," he said. "And you and your mother should have somewhere to be that isn't here."

"Tomorrow," Ulyana said, "is soon enough. Tonight I want to sit in my own shop with my own tea and know that somewhere in this city a man is reading my father's words aloud to strangers, and that I made the letters that let him do it." She paused. "Also I want supper. Terror is efficient but not filling."

That earned the smile she'd been fishing for — tired, real. Outside, the lamplighter was working his way down the street, and the lamps came on one by one like typeset words, each in its proper place.

They were clearing the table when Milo burst in from the street, hatless, breathing hard, and the look on his face emptied the room of air before he said a word.

"There's a crowd," he said. "At the Anchorstone. Soldiers came to confiscate the sheets, and the regulars — the regulars wouldn't give them up. They're standing in the door. Fishermen, dockmen, the lot of them, arms linked, singing, of all the stupid brave things, some old hymn, and there's more people arriving every minute, and Ulyana—" he caught his breath, "—there's a lieutenant down there asking who printed it. Asking. And nobody's answering. Nobody's going to answer."

For a moment nobody in the back room moved. Then Ulyana reached for her coat.

"Where are you going?" Anton said sharply.

"To see," she said. "It's mine. Whatever it is out there tonight — I'm not watching it from behind a window anymore."

The streets near the harbor were thick with people moving the same direction, drawn the way crowds always are toward the sound of trouble and song, and Ulyana let the current carry her until the Anchorstone's lamplit face swam up ahead, jammed with bodies, and above the din she heard it clearly:

Not the hymn. Something else. A voice — a woman's voice, young, standing on a barrel, papers held high — reading, in a ringing, furious, joyful voice, the sworn testimony of Eriksen Eriksen, compositor, of Vessengard, concerning what he saw at Kolvik.

And the crowd was repeating the worst lines back to her, line by line, so that the words rolled up the street like surf.

Ulyana stopped at the edge of the light, invisible among hundreds, holding an empty basket, and let her father's voice wash over her.

Then, at the crowd's far edge, she saw the officer push forward through the linked arms — saw him look up at the girl on the barrel, and then past her, scanning faces, hunting the crowd for the one person in Vessengard who would recognize the spacing of an 'f' —

and his eyes found hers.
