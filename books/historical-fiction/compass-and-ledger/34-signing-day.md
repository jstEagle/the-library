## 34. Signing Day

The bells began at dawn, all of them at once, as though the city had been holding its breath for four years and had finally been given permission to exhale.

Ulyana stood at the case in the shop with a stick of type in her hand and did not set a single letter for a full minute. She simply listened. The bells of Saint Aldric's, the harbor bell, the little cracked thing on the guildhall that Milo swore had been rung by a deaf man since 1911 — all of it tumbling over itself, out of rhythm, joyful.

"They'll wear themselves out before noon," Milo said from the doorway, already dressed in his good coat, which was to say his only coat, brushed twice.

"Let them." Ulyana slid the type back into its compartment. "We've earned some worn-out bells."

They had slept — if it could be called sleeping — perhaps three hours each. The last bundles had gone out at four in the morning, carried off in Signe's cousin's fish cart under a respectable layer of herring ice, and Milo had come back smelling like a wharf and grinning like a man who had gotten away with something enormous, which he had. Three thousand copies. Ulyana kept turning the number over in her mind the way she might turn a coin she suspected was too valuable to be real.

"Mother's coming down," Milo said. "She insists. She says she's spent two years being told what's happening elsewhere, and today it's happening here."

*Today it's happening here.* Ulyana thought of the girl she had been in autumn, setting shipping manifests and believing history was a thing that happened to capitals, to grandees, to other people's sons. She could hardly find that girl now without feeling tenderness toward her, the way one feels toward a photograph of oneself in clothes that no longer fit.

Her mother came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, wearing her gray wool coat and the expression of a woman attending church. "Well," she said. "Is it done?"

"It's done," Ulyana said. "It was done before we started, really. We just made sure everyone else would know."

"That," her mother said, "is the most sensible thing anyone has said in this house in weeks." She patted Ulyana's cheek with a cold hand. "Come along, then. I didn't sell my wedding silver so my daughter could spend signing day indoors."

* * *

The square in front of the guildhall had filled by nine, and kept filling after that, spilling down the side streets like water finding its level. Bunting had gone up overnight — the victors' colors, ordered in bulk from a printer across town who, Ulyana noted with professional interest, had used an inferior stock and let his registration slip badly on the red. The flags sagged. The crowd did not.

There were soldiers in dress uniform lining the steps of the guildhall, and there were old men in the caps of regiments that no longer existed, and there were children sitting on their fathers' shoulders eating roasted chestnuts, and there were women in black who had come, Ulyana understood, not to celebrate anything but simply to stand where the ending was being announced, because endings deserved witnesses even when they arrived late.

General Halvard appeared at eleven o'clock precisely, and the square roared.

Ulyana stood well back, near the fountain, between a fishwife and a schoolteacher she half-recognized from her own schooldays. Her mother held her arm. Milo hovered at her shoulder, and somewhere in the press of bodies — she could not see him, but she could feel him the way one feels weather — Anton stood in a borrowed coat and a cap pulled low, watching the man whose orders he had once carried out.

On the platform, Halvard looked exactly as he did on the posters: silver-haired, square-jawed, modest in his decorations, a man carved by a sculptor who had been paid in advance. He raised a hand and the roar swelled. He let it swell. He knew precisely how long to let it swell; you could see the counting behind his eyes.

"He's good," Milo murmured. "You have to admit it. He's very good."

"So is a well-set page," Ulyana said. "That doesn't mean the words are true."

The ceremony proceeded. An official read the terms in a voice pitched for the back rows, a long litany of ceasefires and boundaries and exchanges, clauses folding into clauses, and the crowd cheered at intervals it judged appropriate, the way congregations do when they have lost the thread of the sermon. Ulyana listened hard. Clause fourteen. *All actions undertaken in the conduct of military operations prior to this date shall be regarded as closed matters, subject neither to inquiry nor prosecution.* There it was, read aloud in broad daylight, in festival weather, and the crowd cheered it, because cheering was what one did at ceremonies, and no one had told them what they were cheering.

Her hands curled into fists inside her coat pockets. Beside her, she felt her mother go very still.

"Closed matters," her mother repeated softly. "My husband is a closed matter."

"Not yet," Ulyana said.

* * *

It happened at twenty past eleven, though afterward everyone would disagree about the hour, and several people would insist it happened at several places at once, which was nearly true.

A boy near the fountain — twelve, maybe, with a chestnut-seller's tray abandoned under a bench — climbed onto the fountain's rim and began throwing papers into the air. Not one paper. A fistful, then another, then more, produced from a sack that must have weighed as much as he did, and the wind off the harbor took them and spread them across the square like a strange snowfall, white pages drifting down onto uniforms and bunting and upturned faces.

Then a second source, from the colonnade on the east side. Then a third, from somewhere near the tram stop. Fish carts, choir lofts, a man dressed as a municipal lamplighter. Nine days of planning, unfolding in ninety seconds.

Ulyana watched a single copy spiral down and land against the boot of a staff officer standing guard below the platform. The officer looked down at it. He did not pick it up. He looked at it the way a man looks at something that has fallen into his life from a great height and cannot be put back.

The title ran across the top of every page in type Ulyana herself had set, letters she had chosen and spaced and locked into a chase with her own hands:

**THE RECORD OF THE EASTERN MARCHES.**
*Testimony concerning the conduct of General Halvard's command, 1916–1918. Set down by those who saw it. Printed so that no one may say they did not know.*

Below it, the testimonies. Farmer Lindqvist's account of the burned village at Kessel, given in a voice that shook on the page. The nurse's ledger of names. The sergeant's confession. And at the bottom of the first page, in smaller type, the line Ulyana had argued for and Anton had resisted and her father — wherever he was — would surely have approved:

*Printed in Vessengard. The presses are many. The ink does not answer questions.*

For a moment the square simply absorbed it, the way ground absorbs rain. People caught pages out of the air. People picked them up off the cobbles. A woman read aloud to the man next to her because he could not read, her voice rising over the tail end of the official reading, and the official reading faltered — Ulyana heard it falter, heard the reader lose his place, heard the pause stretch — because it is difficult to pronounce an amnesty over a square full of people reading your name in a different document.

"Halvard," someone called. Just the name. Then another voice took it up, not as a cheer but as a question, and then a dozen voices, and the quality of the sound changed entirely. It had been a roar, and now it was a murmur, and a murmur, Ulyana knew — she had learned it from her father at the case, explaining why a misprint caught the eye faster than a perfect page — a murmur is far more dangerous than a roar. A roar spends itself. A murmur spreads.

On the platform, Halvard turned his head slightly, following a falling page with his eyes, and for one unguarded instant his face did something the posters had never captured. It was not fear, exactly. It was the look of a man discovering that the room he has been speaking in all these years has had other people in it the whole time.

"There," Milo breathed. "Did you see—"

"I saw."

An aide whispered to Halvard. Halvard straightened, resumed his posture, gestured grandly as though the papers were confetti, as though this were part of the program. But the ceremony was over in all but name. The band played something patriotic and half the crowd sang and half the crowd read, and the reading half was growing.

The fishwife beside Ulyana had caught a page and was squinting at it. "This true?" she demanded, of no one and everyone.

"It's testimony," Ulyana said. "Under names. You can ask whether the names are lying."

The fishwife looked at her, then at the page, then folded it with surprising care and put it inside her coat, against her body, the way one carries things one intends to keep. "Kessel," she said. "I lost a brother near Kessel." She said nothing else. She didn't need to. That was the whole arithmetic of it, Ulyana thought. Every name in the pamphlet was attached to somebody's empty chair.

* * *

They drifted apart by design — Milo toward the tram lines to watch the second distribution wave, her mother home to rest, Anton vanishing into the crowd with the ease of a man who had spent years being invisible in uniform. Ulyana stayed near the fountain, anonymous among thousands, doing nothing more remarkable than standing in a square on a historic day, which was, she reflected, exactly what she had always believed history required of her. It was astonishing how much could be accomplished by people appearing to do nothing at all.

By noon the pamphlets were everywhere. She saw one lining a birdcage — briefly, until the bird's owner actually read it and took it back out. She saw a priest on the cathedral steps quietly collecting copies from his parishioners, not to destroy them, she noticed, but to stack them neatly, as though archiving. She saw two students from the technical college copying the text out by hand into a notebook, presumably to re-set and reprint it, which meant the pamphlet was about to reproduce itself, which meant it had become what her father would have called *a living edition* — one that no raid, no seizure, no amnesty clause could ever fully recall.

Near the guildhall steps, a squad of military police moved through the crowd gathering pages off the ground. They worked diligently and achieved almost nothing, like men bailing the sea. For every page collected, fifty hands held fifty copies, and the hands passed them along, and someone had begun reading the nurse's testimony aloud on the fountain steps in a clear carrying voice, and the crowd around the reader had stopped being a crowd and become a congregation.

Ulyana bought a bag of chestnuts from the boy with the abandoned tray — he had returned to commerce the moment his sack was empty, with the adaptability of the young — and ate them slowly, burning her fingers, watching the general's ceremony puncture and deflate like a parade balloon with a pin in it, and felt no triumph at all, or rather felt a triumph so large and quiet that it had no triumph-shaped edges. It felt more like setting a difficult page and pulling a clean proof. *There,* her father would have said, tapping the sheet. *There it is. That's what it was supposed to say.*

She wondered, not for the first time that morning, whether he knew. Whether word had reached whatever cell held him. Whether he had heard the bells at dawn and known they were ringing for something he had set in motion with a pen in a frozen dugout three years ago.

* * *

She was halfway home through the celebrating streets when Milo found her, running, hatless, breathless, his face carrying an expression she couldn't immediately file — joy and urgency fighting for possession of it.

"You're smiling," she said. "And running. One of those is wrong."

"The fortress," he gasped. "Ulyana — the fortress. There are wagons. I came past the causeway road — army wagons, and a clerk with a manifest, and people shouting at the gate — the pamphlet's there, it's everywhere, even the sentries have read it — and there's a crowd forming, and someone's saying—" He bent double, hands on knees. "Someone's saying they're transferring the prisoners. For inquiry. Today. Because of *this*." He slapped his coat pocket, where a folded copy rustled.

Ulyana stood very still in the middle of the rejoicing street while fireworks — someone had fireworks, where had anyone found fireworks — burst green and gold above the rooftops.

Transferring the prisoners. For inquiry. Which meant wagons. Which meant a gate opening. Which meant that somewhere behind those walls, in a cell he had occupied for three years while his daughter set other people's words in type, her father might be walking toward a door.

"Milo," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her, calm and enormous, "fetch my mother's shawl. And tell Anton."

He stared at her. "Where are you going?"

She was already moving, pushing gently through the celebratory crowd, toward the causeway road, toward the gray shape of the fortress on its hill.

"To the gate," she said. "Someone in this family ought to be there when the record comes home."
