## 35. The Fortress Gate

The causeway road to the fortress was not built for crowds, which was fortunate, because there wasn't one — only a thin procession of people who had heard the same rumor and could not sit still under it. A fishwife with her baskets still on her arm. Two students from the technical school walking with the deliberate slowness of young men pretending not to be excited. An old soldier on a bicycle he never quite dismounted, circling back every hundred yards as though the road might change its mind.

Ulyana walked fast. Milo half-ran beside her, carrying the shawl even though the morning was mild; Anton came behind with the uneven gait that three years of bad bandaging had given him, refusing all offers of an arm.

"You'll wait behind me," Ulyana said. It was not entirely a plan. It was mostly a hope wearing the shape of an instruction.

"And if they recognize him?" Milo said. "If they take him back?"

"Then I will be standing at the gate," she said, "which is more than I have managed for three years."

The fortress had watched over Vessengard so long that the city had stopped seeing it, the way one stops seeing a birthmark or a scar. It sat on its hill above the harbor like a paperweight set down on a corner of the map, gray stone going darker where the sea damp climbed it. Ulyana had passed beneath its walls a thousand times without once wondering what it kept. Now every arrow-slit seemed to look back at her.

A handful of people had gathered by the gate before the causeway bent toward it: a woman in mourning black, a clergyman, a boy selling chestnuts out of season and out of luck. Word traveled the way water finds cracks. By the time the first wagon sound came — iron rims on cobbles, somewhere behind the wall — there were perhaps forty of them, and none of them speaking above a whisper, as if the fortress might reconsider if it heard them hoping.

Anton came up beside her. "It may not be him."

"I know."

"It may be worse than not-him." He said it gently, the way one delivers news to oneself. "Three years is a long time, Ulyana. Men come out of places like that folded up small."

"Then we will unfold them," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it, and surprised again that the sentence had arrived in her father's cadence, as though his voice had been waiting in hers all along, the way type waits in the case.

Milo spread the shawl over his arm like a waiter presenting a napkin. "I've decided," he announced to no one in particular, "that this is a ceremonial occasion, and someone ought to carry something."

* * *

They waited two hours.

The gate did nothing dramatic. Gates, Ulyana reflected, never did; that was their whole character. It stood twenty feet high, studded, barred, flanked by sentries who looked cold and slightly embarrassed, soldiers detailed to a duty that had changed shape underneath them. Somewhere beyond the wall came the sounds of preparation: wheels, orders called flat and bored, the clank of a chain being unhooked from somewhere.

Ulyana discovered that waiting has chapters too. In the first chapter you are calm. In the second your hands begin to notice things — the seam of your glove, a loose thread on Milo's sleeve — because the rest of you refuses to stand still and the hands must be given work. In the third chapter you stop being able to remember the man's face properly. She had been doing that for years, of course, losing him by increments: first the way he tilted his head when reading proof, then the exact color of his apron, then, cruelest and most gradual, the sound of his laugh, until what remained was less a father than a description of one. *Compositor, middle-aged, spectacles, ink under the nails.* She had set his obituary herself. Twelve point. She had chosen the type with care, because it was the last thing she could do for him, and she had thought at the time: *this is the worst job I will ever take.*

She had been wrong about that twice since October.

"He'll squint at us," Milo said suddenly. "That's how I'll know him. He always squinted coming out into daylight from the composing room. Used to say the sun was over-inked."

Ulyana laughed — a short, broken sound she hadn't authorized — and pressed his shoulder, and Milo went pink and studied the chestnut boy with great interest.

Anton said nothing. He had taken off his cap, which for Anton was roughly equivalent to another man kneeling.

At half past eleven the gate opened.

Not wide — a leaf of it, enough for a wagon, and a soldier stepped out first and faced the little crowd with the weary authority of a man told to keep order among nobody. The wagon came through slowly. It was a plain transport, canvas-topped, and it carried six men sitting on the floor boards because there were no benches, and every person in the crowd leaned forward at once, forty bodies tilting like grasses in one wind.

The first man down was young and shaking. The second wept openly and thanked everyone, the sentries included. The third needed help. The fourth and fifth came down together, holding each other's arms, blinking.

Ulyana could not see the sixth man yet. The wagon's shadow held him.

And she found, in that held moment, that some part of her mind had detached itself entirely and was observing, coolly, professionally, that fear and hope are set from the same case, letter for letter identical, differing only in how the compositor spaces them. She thought: *I am about to learn which one I have been setting all these weeks.*

Her father stepped down from the wagon.

He was thinner than the description she'd been carrying. His hair had gone almost entirely white, and his beard, which she had never seen on him, made him briefly a stranger. He moved carefully, like a man relearning that floors are trustworthy. He wore a prisoner's coat too big for what he had become, and his hands — she saw them first and knew them instantly, before the face resolved, before anything — his hands were held slightly away from his body, fingers curled, and she understood with a jolt that after three years without type he still carried the habit of reaching for a composing stick that wasn't there.

He blinked at the daylight. Squinted, rather. Over-inked.

"Dad," Milo said, beside her, and then clapped both hands over his own mouth.

Her father turned toward the sound. His eyes went across the crowd the way a proofreader's go across a page — quick, systematic, missing nothing — and Ulyana watched them pass over the fishwife, the students, the clergyman, and arrive at her, and stop, and go back, and check.

She saw him do the checking. She saw the moment when verification completed. And she understood that he did not cry out, did not lurch forward, because he was a careful man, a man of corrections and errata, and some deep habit of precision made him want to be certain before he trusted the reading.

So she gave him the only proof she had ever needed of him. She took off her work apron — she had put it on that morning without thinking, out of the shop, out of habit, out of love — and she held it up slightly, the ink-stained front of it facing him, the way he used to identify himself jokingly at the door: *the uniform.*

Her father made a sound. It was not a word. It was small and rough, like a press engaging, and then he crossed the distance between them, and it was not graceful and it was not slow, and Ulyana Eriksen, who had spent nineteen years believing that history happened elsewhere, held her father in her arms on the causeway road while forty strangers pretended very hard to look at the sea.

He was lighter than she remembered. That was the detail that undid her, more than the whiteness, more than the beard: he weighed less than a memory, and her arms had known the true weight and registered the error at once.

"There now," he said into her hair, absurdly, as though she were eight and had skinned a knee. "There now, little bird. You're smudging."

She pulled back and looked at him and laughed and cried at once, because he was right — her face was leaving marks on his coat — and because he was exactly, precisely, infuriatingly himself.

"You got my letters," he said. It wasn't a question. "All of them?"

"Every one." Her voice cracked in the middle and she let it. "Dad — why didn't you write that you were alive? Three years, we had a funeral, there was a—"

"A stone," he said. "I know. I paid for it. Best money I ever spent." He touched her cheek with two fingers, gently, the way he handled fresh proofs. "Letters travel, little bird. Stones don't testify. If they knew I lived, you became a door they could knock on. This way you were only a daughter grieving. Forgive me. It was the least bad page in a badly printed book."

She wanted to argue. She had three years of arguments queued up and rehearsed. But Anton had come close, and her father saw him, and everything in her father's face reorganized around what he saw — recognition, guilt, gratitude, and underneath it something older and quieter that Ulyana, watching, recognized as shame arriving to pay its standing debt.

"Eriksen," Anton said, and tried to salute, and couldn't finish it, and settled for standing straight.

"Courier." Her father took his hand and held it in both of his. "You brought them home safe. All of them?"

"Every page."

"Then you've done the one thing I asked God for nightly," her father said. "Which I did not deserve to ask, but did anyway." He looked around at the crowd, at the wagon already rumbling off toward the inquiry offices, at the gray wall behind him, and something in him seemed to decide, visibly, that he was finished looking backward. "Well. Is there tea? I would commit minor crimes for tea. Not major ones. I've developed standards."

* * *

They walked home slowly, because her father's legs had opinions, and he refused the offer of an arm twice before accepting Milo's shoulder on the hill down with the air of a man granting a great favor. The old soldier on the bicycle rode past them, saw who it was, dismounted, and stood bareheaded at the roadside until they'd passed, which embarrassed everyone including the bicycle.

"The shop?" her father asked, as the rooftops of Vessengard came into view below. "Still standing?"

"Thriving," Milo said. "Well. Standing. Well — standing."

"That's the trade entire," her father said gravely. "Standing."

Ulyana watched him take in the city as they descended: the harbor, the bunting left over from armistice week sagging between lampposts, the poster of General Halvard on the customs house wall, ten feet tall, jaw like a monument. Her father stopped walking when he saw it. Milo shifted nervously under his arm.

"That's still up," her father said quietly.

"They haven't taken it down," Ulyana said. "The inquiry is opened, but he's — Dad, he's in Vienna. Dining. There's talk of a command in the new army. The amnesty—"

"I read the fine print," her father said. "Even in there, they'd talk. Even in there." He looked at the poster a long moment, and Ulyana braced herself for rage, for the terrible arithmetic of a man counting his three white years against one printed jaw.

Instead he smiled. It was a small smile, tired and absolutely dangerous.

"But they took the prisoners out," he said. "Through a public gate. Before witnesses. Because a pamphlet made them." He patted the poster's lamppost companionably as they passed, as one pats a rival who has lost. "History's a stubborn ink, girl. It doesn't wash out just because the powerful wish it would. Sometimes you have to burn it in properly first."

* * *

Her mother was waiting at the loading door.

Ulyana had written ahead — Milo had run, actually run, through half the city — and so it should not have surprised her, and yet it undid her entirely: her mother standing in the doorway in her good dress, upright by visible effort, hands folded, holding herself together with the same discipline she applied to linens and to grief. She did not run to him either. Ravensworth restraint, Eriksen stubbornness; between the two of them, Ulyana thought, no wonder she'd turned out unable to stand aside from anything.

"Joren," her mother said.

"Marta." Her father's voice failed on the second syllable and he cleared it and tried again. "You kept the shop open."

"You kept writing letters," her mother said. "We appear to have kept our respective promises." And then the discipline broke all at once, beautifully, and she crossed the yard, and Ulyana turned discreetly to supervise Milo supervising the street, because some pages belong to other readers.

Later — tea achieved, minor crimes forgiven — her father sat at the composing stone with his hands hovering over the type cases the way a musician hovers over keys he fears he's lost. Ulyana watched him from the office doorway and did not offer help, because she understood that this was the true homecoming, more than any gate.

His fingers found the case. Moved along it. Stopped.

"You've reorganized," he said, wounded.

"I improved it," Ulyana said.

"You *vandalized* it. Where are the em quads?"

"Third row. Where they should always have been."

He looked at her over his spectacles — his old spectacles gone, these new ones from the relief office, but the look was unchanged, four decades of it, the look that had corrected her childhood homework and her adult proofs alike. Then he drew a composing stick from the rack, selected a piece of type blind, and set it on the stone, and read it, and laughed — and there it was, the laugh she had lost, restored to the record like a recovered line.

"'Eriksen & Daughter,'" he read aloud. The slug in his hand. Fresh-set, hers, the imprint from the pamphlet's final page, kept in the case like a treasure. "Well." He set the stick down with ceremony. "I see the firm has undergone certain amendments during my absence."

"The colophon of the pamphlet," Ulyana said. "Signe insisted. Before—" She stopped.

Her father's face did the thing faces do when they hear a name in the past tense. "Before what, little bird?"

Ulyana sat down across the stone from him. Outside, Vessengard went about its morning; somewhere a gull complained; the presses of the world waited to be fed. She took a breath, and reached for the drawer where they kept the newspaper clippings, and the account of Signe Holm's arrest, and everything that had been bought and everything that remained unpaid.

"Dad," she said, "there's something you need to read."
