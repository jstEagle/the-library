## 36. What Truth Buys

The winter after the armistice came in like a creditor — politely at first, then all at once.

Ulyana noticed it first in the shop, which was fitting. The cold crept under the loading door the same way it always had, curling across the stone floor toward the press as if it too wanted to read what was being set. She had taken to working with her coat on and a brick warmed on the stove at her feet, and she had stopped apologizing to customers for the state of the place. The Eriksen print shop was famous now, in its small way. People came in just to look at her.

"Are you the one?" a woman asked on a Tuesday in January, a woman with flour on her sleeves who had never once ordered anything printed in her life.

"The one what?" Ulyana said, though she knew.

"The pamphlet. You're the printer."

"I'm the compositor," Ulyana said. "The press did the printing."

The woman laughed as if this were wit rather than deflection and bought nothing, and Ulyana went back to setting wedding invitations — *Mr. and Mrs. Anders Vik request the honour* — and thought about how strange fame was when it arrived. It did not feel like visibility. It felt like being a window that people pressed their faces against.

Three months had passed since the square. Three months since three thousand sheets of paper had gone into the crowd like water into sand, since General Halvard's ceremony of ribbons and brass had been punctured by ordinary hands passing ordinary-looking folded pages down through ten thousand coats. Three months since the fortress gate, since the wagon, since her father had stepped down into the slush an old man where she remembered a tall one, and had looked at the shop above her head before he looked at her — and then had looked at her, for a long time, without saying anything at all.

He was upstairs now. He took his meals at the table and his tea by the window and he read everything the papers printed about the inquiry, which was, in these three months, quite a lot and also almost nothing.

That was the arithmetic of it, Ulyana had learned. Quite a lot and almost nothing could be true at once. The Commission of Inquiry into Conduct of Forces in the Eastern Marshes existed. It had offices. It had a letterhead, which she knew because they had written to the shop twice asking her to surrender her plates, and both times she had written back explaining, with citations, why the plates were evidence and not property and would be surrendered to no one but a court. It had witnesses — eleven of them now, men and women who had come forward after reading their own neighbors' testimony in a smuggled pamphlet and deciding that shame was heavier than fear after all. Eleven. Her father had gathered forty-three accounts in the marshes and the villages beyond them. Forty-three had been enough to shake an armistice. Eleven more had been enough to open a commission.

And Halvard?

Halvard had been promoted.

Not formally — the word *promoted* appeared nowhere, because words mattered to people like Halvard and his people were careful with them. What happened instead was that the general who was to be examined by the Commission of Inquiry into Conduct of Forces in the Eastern Marshes was appointed *chairman of the demobilization board*, which meant that every soldier whose testimony might reach the commission passed first through his office on the way out of uniform. It meant budgets. It meant staff. It meant a carriage and an escort and photographs in the illustrated papers shaking hands with ministers, looking grave and statesmanlike, the very portrait of a man shouldering burdens.

"They've made him the doorman," Anton said bitterly, the evening the appointment was announced. He stood at the case beside her, sorting type into the job stick with more force than the work required. "You queue at his door to accuse him, and he takes your hat."

"That's not how doors work."

"It is how his works."

She could not argue. She had read the announcement four times hoping the words would rearrange themselves. They hadn't. Politics, Signe Holm had said when she'd heard — Signe, out on her own recognizance after six weeks in custody, thinner, coughing, undefeated in the specific stubborn way of old printers — politics is the art of surviving your own exposure. Halvard had been exposed as thoroughly as a man can be short of a gallows, and he had survived it by becoming, overnight, indispensable to the peace everyone was tired of fighting for. There would be no trial that inconvenienced the demobilization. There would be an inquiry that issued, in two years or five, a report of nine hundred pages, of which eight hundred and ninety would concern procedure.

Ulyana had raged about it for a week. Then she had done what she increasingly did with rage: she set it in type, and looked at it, and found it smaller than it felt.

* * *

Her father found her at the case one grey afternoon, watching her fingers hover over the f's.

He did this now, her father — moved quietly through the shop like a man revisiting a house he'd sold long ago, touching things, putting nothing back where he found it, because his hands shook. The doctors at the fortress had done something to him that they had not documented; there was a numbness along his left side that came and went, and a tremor that worsened when he was tired. He had been at the shop eleven weeks. He still rose at five out of thirty years' habit and sat downstairs before anyone else woke, and twice she had come down to find him standing before the case with his hand flat on the frame of the press, the way other men touched shrines.

"You're doing the spacing again," he said.

"I'm always doing the spacing."

"You do it when you're angry. When you're calm you set loose and fast and you make mistakes, which I never told you, because the mistakes were usually interesting." He lowered himself onto the stool Milo had long ago claimed and Milo, magnanimously, had ceded to the founder of the firm. "Tell me what's wrong, and don't say nothing, because you only space like that when it's nothing and you mean everything."

"Halvard gets a chairmanship." She kept her eyes on the type. "Eleven witnesses and a commission and forty-three testimonies, and he gets a carriage. Papa, we did it — we put it in front of the whole city, thousands of them read it in the square while he stood there with his sash — and it bought us a report nobody will read and a war criminal with a budget. What was it worth? Tell me what it bought."

Her father was quiet a while. In the quiet she heard the stove tick and, upstairs, her mother's slow footsteps crossing the kitchen — stronger now, steadier, since the silver had gone and the worry had come out with it.

"Your grandfather," he said at last, "kept a ledger his whole life. Every job, every debt owed him, every debt he owed. And the year he died I went through it, thinking I'd find the sum of a man. Do you know what I found? That in 1861 he was cheated of forty crowns by a man named Ostergaard, and he wrote it down, and he underlined it, and there is no other record in God's world that Ostergaard ever lived. Forty crowns. A hundred years from now, that line will still say: this happened, and it was not right, and someone saw." He turned his palm up, the trembling hand, and studied it without embarrassment; he had decided, Ulyana thought, that embarrassment was a luxury for men with steady hands. "Halvard will die comfortably. I have accepted this the way one accepts weather. But there are eleven witnesses because of you. There are clerks in the ministry who copied our pamphlet and passed it hand to hand, and some of those clerks will be ministers someday, and they will remember what a compositor's daughter did with a hand press and no permission. That's what truth buys, Ulla. Not verdicts. Witnesses. It buys the next round."

"And the eleven who came forward? If Halvard's board gets to them first—"

"Then we get to them first." He smiled, and for a moment the tremor stilled and he was entirely her father, the man who had spaced an f like no one else alive. "We're printers. We've always gotten there first."

* * *

Milo, of course, had opinions, and expressed them the way he expressed everything: while carrying something heavy.

"They should hang him," he announced, coming through the loading door with a ream of stock for a bakery order. "In the square. On the same platform they built for the ceremony. Let him look at the bunting while he does it."

"You've thought about the logistics," Ulyana said.

"I've thought about nothing else for a month." He dropped the paper, dusted his hands, and perched on the stone bench where Anton used to sleep when he'd first arrived, feverish, with a satchel he wouldn't open. That seemed years ago. Some days it seemed yesterday. "My cousin Jorgen says — you remember Jorgen, the one who nearly got us all—"

"I remember him fondly and from a great distance."

"He says the ministers aren't protecting Halvard because they believe him innocent. They're protecting him because if a general can be tried for following policy, then policy has to answer for itself, and half the men who signed the armistice wrote the policy. So it's not a cover-up, it's—" Milo made a gesture like a man smoothing a tablecloth. "Tidying."

"When did you become a political philosopher?"

"Since I started forging passes, I suppose. It focuses the mind, crime." He grinned, and then the grin faded into something more careful, the face of a boy who had hidden galleys in a coffin and never quite stopped hearing the knock of that night. "It doesn't seem fair, is my point. We nearly died. Signe *did* nearly die. And the man it was all about gets to ride around in a carriage deciding which regiments go home first."

"No," Ulyana said. "It isn't fair."

She let it sit there. She had learned — was learning, badly, slowly — not to rush in and soften every hard sentence for Milo's sake. He was twenty now. He deserved unsoftened sentences; he'd earned them in grease and panic in a cellar press with a neighbor's boots on the stairs.

"What do we do, then?" he asked. "Print another pamphlet? Call it *What Truth Buys*? Because I'll tell you honestly, missus, nobody buys a pamphlet titled after a disappointment."

Ulyana laughed despite herself — laughed until her eyes watered, which told her how much of the last three months she had been holding somewhere below the ribs. Milo watched her with the satisfaction of a man who considered humor his department.

"We keep records," she said finally. "Every witness who comes forward, we help them write it down properly. Dated, signed, witnessed, copies made — one for the commission, one for us, one somewhere Brandt doesn't even know about. Halvard can outrun a trial. He can't outrun a filing system."

"A filing system," Milo repeated, disgusted. "Your revenge is stationery."

"My revenge is *permanent*," Ulyana said, "which is better."

* * *

Anton took it hardest, and hid it best, and failed at hiding it only in the evenings, when the light went and he thought no one could see his face.

He had stayed through the autumn on the pretext of testifying — he was, after all, the courier, the deserter, the man who had carried the satchel through three borders in his boot and his shirt — and the pretext had stretched into winter the way pretexts do when neither party wants to name what they actually are. He testified to the commission for six hours. They had thanked him, given him a numbered receipt for his statement, and explained that his own record — two years served, orders followed, a village outside Drassen where he had stood in a cordon and done nothing while — would be examined "in due course." Due course. He had walked out of that room, Ulyana suspected, the way he'd walked out of the army: at a normal pace, looking straight ahead, dying quietly the whole way.

He came to the shop less now. When he came, he worked — he was passable at imposing forms and hopeless at kerning and knew it, and their arguments about it had become the shape of something neither of them would call by name.

Tonight he sat on the stairs between the shop and the flat, eating bread and cheese off his knee because her mother insisted he eat and he insisted he wasn't staying long enough for a table.

"The board summons witnesses to 'pre-interviews,'" he said, staring at the dark end of the corridor. "Before the commission hears them. To 'clarify inconsistencies.' Two of the eleven have already withdrawn. One emigrated. The other just stopped answering letters, and when Signe went to the house, his wife said he'd been visited by officers from the demobilization office offering to expedite his pension."

"Expedite."

"In exchange for discretion. Their word." He turned the bread over without eating. "I did this, Ulla. I carried the papers, so I know exactly what they weigh, and now I watch them get lighter every week, and I can't—" His voice caught and flattened itself, soldier-smooth. "Forty-three people trusted your father. I promised him distribution or destruction, nothing in between. And we distributed, and it was magnificent, and now it's dissolving, and the man responsible is choosing curtains for his office. Tell me what that was *for*."

Ulyana sat down on the step above him. For a while she said nothing, because she had learned that Anton trusted silence more than comfort; comfort made him stand at attention.

"You want justice to be a door," she said finally. "You walk through it with the evidence and on the other side everything's corrected. I wanted that too. I still want it, most days, and most days I don't get it, and here's what I've got instead." She counted on her fingers, deliberately, the way she'd count type. "A commission that didn't exist last winter exists. Eleven people who were silent are not silent. My father's name is in the colophon of a document the national archive is legally obliged to hold, and Halvard cannot amend it, ever, no matter how many chairs they give him. And a baker's wife knows my name and a fish-cart driver knows my route and somewhere there's a girl setting shipping manifests in some smelly little shop who heard what happened here and will not believe, ever again, that revolutions happen only to capitals." She nudged his knee with hers. "That's what truth buys, Anton. It buys badly. It pays in installments. But it pays."

"And if the installments stop?"

"Then we start the presses again."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he had at the loading door that first night, that strange recognition she'd felt before either of them had words for it. "You know," he said, "when I came through that door, I thought I was bringing the truth to safety. I never once thought the truth had a family."

"It does. It's upstairs arguing with my mother about whether you're staying for supper."

"Am I?"

"My mother made dumplings. You can attempt to leave. I've seen it fail."

He almost smiled — the full smile was still in storage, reclaimed piece by piece — and upstairs the door opened and her mother's voice came down the stairwell, warm and utterly without mercy: "Anton Vasilievich, the dumplings are getting cold and I have known you were not leaving since October."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, the household asleep except for the stove and herself — Ulyana sat at the case with the lamp low and did not set anything at all.

She thought about prices. She had become, in the past year, a person who counted costs the way merchants did, and she forced herself to do the honest accounting now, because dishonest ledgers were the whole disease she'd been treating.

Signe Holm: arrested, held six weeks, her cellar press confiscated and melted down, her health broken enough that she coughed from November onward and swore it was nothing. Cost, paid. She'd do it again; she said so weekly, loudly, to anyone.

The neighbor's bindery: raided, wrecked, its owner questioned for three days over nothing and compensated with an apology worth nothing. Cost, paid, by the wrong person.

Milo: a scar along his forearm from the night run, a forged-pass habit he'd need to outgrow, and eyes that were older than twenty by several wars' worth.

Her mother: the wedding silver gone to a dealer in Nordside, the fear gone with it mostly, though some nights Ulyana heard her on the stairs at odd hours, checking.

Her father: something done to him in the fortress that medicine could describe and not repair, and a grief behind his eyes when he looked at his own shaking hands that no pamphlet would ever reach.

Herself: fluent at lying to inspectors, good at it, *too* good, and some mornings she caught the fluency in her voice mid-sentence — with suppliers, with the tax clerk, once, terribly, with her mother — and hated the ease of it, the way the year had polished her like a tool for a purpose she hadn't chosen.

And against all that: a report that would take years and change nothing, a general in a carriage, two witnesses withdrawn.

By any honest ledger, she thought, it didn't balance. By any honest ledger, the costs ran heavy and the returns were promises.

And yet.

She pulled open the drawer where she kept the original proof sheet — the first one, the one she had read aloud to four frightened people in Signe's cellar while the press dripped — and laid it flat in the lamplight. Her father's testimony, in readable form, set by her own hands, spaced in his private way so that any compositor who knew him would know it was true. Beneath it, faint as breath, the ink ghost of the next sheet pressed against it in the pile: mirror-writing, backwards words, *the record, the record, the record.*

Somewhere in the fortress of her chest, alongside the bitterness, there was this: they had done it. Whatever the ministers tidied, whatever Halvard survived, the words were out and could not be recalled, and every single person in Vessengard who read them was a place where the truth now lived, unsuppressible, multiplying. Justice would come late and incomplete and wearing someone else's face. But the record was real. The record was hers. She had inherited it at a loading door on a night in autumn, and she would not bequeath it diluted.

She closed the drawer gently, like tucking in a child.

And that was when she saw the envelope on the mat by the door — slipped under it sometime during the evening, cream-colored, official, with no stamp at all, which meant it had been brought by hand, by someone who did not want the post to remember it.

Her name on the front, spelled correctly, in a small precise hand she recognized at once.

Inspector Brandt.

She stood a long moment with the lamp guttering, the envelope cold in her fingers, and then she sat back down at the case, laid it flat before her beside the type, and reached for the letter knife.
