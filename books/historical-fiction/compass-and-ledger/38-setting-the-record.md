## 38. Setting the Record

The trouble with printing the truth, Ulyana discovered in the first week, was that the truth was long.

The file Brandt had left them ran to two hundred and forty pages when she and her father transcribed it properly — testimony, requisition ledgers, a quartermaster's arithmetic of men who had ceased to be counted as men. Her father insisted on every word. When she suggested, gently, that perhaps the middle section might be summarized for the reader's sake, he looked at her over his spectacles with an expression she remembered from childhood, the one that preceded lectures about kerning.

"Summarized," he said, as though tasting something spoiled. "Ulyana. If a girl died at Kalmenmoor, her name goes in. If a mule driver saw something and spoke of it and was shot for it, his name goes in. We are not writing a pamphlet anymore. Anyone can print a pamphlet." He tapped the stack of pages. "This is a record. Records are not summarized. Records are *kept*."

She had wanted to argue. She found, instead, that she wanted to cry, which would have been inconvenient at the composing stick, so she went upstairs, cried for four minutes into a dish towel, and came back down and set the names.

* * *

Fru Vasmer took her lunch at one o'clock precisely, which made her predictable, which made her useful. She was the archive clerk who had, over eleven months, misfiled a truly remarkable quantity of documents into the wrong boxes — boxes that happened to pass through the Eriksen shop on their way to nowhere, because the city still used small printers for odd jobs and never thought about what else a printer might copy while a job sat overnight on the stone.

"We don't steal them," Ulyana's father said, the first time he watched her ink a proof of one of the misfiled pages. "We copy. There is a difference."

"There is," Ulyana agreed. "It's the difference between theft and setting type, and I intend to hide behind it forever."

He laughed — a rusty sound, as though the mechanism needed oil, but functional. He was laughing more these days. Not the way he used to; there was a slowness in him now, a habit of pausing before he answered, as if checking each word against some ledger only he could see. Prison did that, Anton said. Or rather, Anton didn't say it, but grew quiet whenever the fortress was mentioned, and quiet from Anton said plenty.

On Thursdays Fru Vasmer brought her lunch to the bench by the canal, and Milo — who had discovered a genius for loitering — would be there with a meat sandwich and a newspaper he could not read very fast, and by half past one the wrong box would be on its way to the shop, and by morning the right copies would be back in it, filed correctly, plus or minus one sheet.

"It's not even lying," Milo marveled once, walking home through the fish market. "It's *filing*. I've become a criminal through excellent penmanship. My cousin would weep with envy."

"Your cousin can stay weeping," Ulyana said. "Preferably in another city."

* * *

The question of the imprint came up in February, on a night when snow ticked against the windows and the four of them — Ulyana, her father, Milo, and Anton, propped on his good side near the stove — were arguing about the title page.

"The shop has always printed anonymously for the state," her father said. "Compositors' names go in no colophon. That's tradition."

"Tradition," said Ulyana, "kept us invisible for three years while they wrote you into a grave."

The room went still. Anton looked into his tea. Milo developed a sudden fascination with the quoins.

Her father removed his spectacles and polished them, which he did when he was thinking or cornered, and possibly both. "And what would you put?"

"Eriksen & Eriksen," she said. "Vessengard. And below it: *Testimony gathered by Anders Eriksen, 1916–1919.*"

"That will get me arrested again."

"You're not going anywhere," she said, and heard her own voice crack slightly, and pressed on anyway. "Father. They amnestied Halvard. The inquiry will grind along for years and end in pensions for everyone involved except the dead. The one thing they cannot amnesty is the colophon. A book without a name is a rumor. A book with your name on it is a man saying *I saw this*, and men have been hanged for less, and men have been remembered for more, and I would rather you be remembered than safe, because safe nearly buried you."

The stove ticked. Snow ticked. Milo, very quietly, stopped pretending to look at anything else.

Her father put his spectacles back on. He looked at the case of type, at the stone, at the press their family had owned since before either of the Eriksens presently breathing was born. Then he looked at his daughter.

"Set it in fourteen point," he said. "If I'm going to be hanged, I'll be hanged legibly."

Milo cheered. Anton smiled down into his tea, and Ulyana pretended not to see him blink twice, quickly, the way a man does when something has gotten into his eye from a completely different direction than eyes are usually bothered from.

* * *

Setting the full edition took nine weeks. It should have taken six, but they kept stopping.

Not from shortage or danger — though there were shortages, paper being paper, and danger being Vessengard — but from the material itself. Some evenings her father would read a page of transcript aloud for correction and stop halfway through a sentence, and the shop would hold its breath until he finished, and then everyone would go back to work too carefully, the way people walk on ice they've just seen crack.

Other nights were lighter than they had any right to be. Milo turned out to have a gift for reading testimony aloud in the voices of the witnesses — a farmer from Kalmenmoor rendered as a man perpetually indignant about weather, a young sergeant whose account became, in Milo's mouth, a lecture delivered to an unruly class — and her father scolded him for irreverence and then asked him to do the farmer again.

Anton, whose shoulder was healing and whose future was assembling itself into train tickets and tribunals in a country whose name he still said carefully, worked the press with Ulyana. They had developed a rhythm over the winter that needed no discussion: he pulled, she fed, and between sheets they talked about everything except the things they couldn't talk about, which somehow meant they talked about everything.

"My mother kept bees," he said once, wiping ink from the platen. "Before the war. She said you could tell the mood of a hive by the sound. Not the volume — the *pitch*. Angry hives hum higher."

"This shop hums lower," Ulyana said. "What does that make us?"

"Content," he said. "Or cold. With bees you have to check."

He checked. He was like that. He refilled her tea without being asked and noticed when a galley was locked a finger-width off true and said nothing about her father's nightmares but slept in the chair nearest the loading door for the whole of January without anyone asking him to. Ulyana caught herself cataloguing these things the way she catalogued type — this much lead, this much space, fitted here — and understood that she was setting him somewhere permanent in the composition of her life, and that in three weeks he would leave, and she set the thought aside with the discipline of a woman pulling a perfect sheet, and got back to work.

There would be time. There was, in fact, exactly enough time for everything, if you did it in order. That was the whole religion of the trade.

* * *

In March the first unexpurgated edition of *The Kalmenmoor Record* came off the press under the imprint **Eriksen & Eriksen, Vessengard**, and Ulyana stood at the stone with the first finished copy in her hands and did not move for a long moment.

It was a plain book. Good paper stock, sewn signatures, no ornament but a rule above and below the imprint and the fourteen-point line beneath it: *Testimony gathered by Anders Eriksen.* Two hundred and forty pages. Three hundred copies in the first run, five hundred in the second, and orders arriving — through Milo's choir connections, through Signe Holm's old networks, through channels nobody discussed — from three cities and a university and, improbably, a naval chaplain.

Her father picked up the second copy off the pile and held it at arm's length, the way he held everything he was trying not to be moved by.

"Hm," he said. "The 'f' on page forty-one is spaced badly."

"That's your 'f'," Ulyana said. "That's your famous private kerning. I set it exactly as you taught me."

"I know." He turned the page. "I'm criticizing myself. It's the only criticism I trust anymore." But his thumb was resting on the colophon, flat, the way a man rests his hand on a doorpost of a house he thought he'd lost.

Her mother came down the stairs — slowly, one hand on the rail, but down, which was itself a victory the doctors hadn't promised — and took the third copy, and read the title page, and read it again.

"Anders," she said.

"Yes, Asta."

"Your name is in a book."

"So it appears."

"Well." Their mother set it down on the stone with great care, aligning its edges with the edge of the table, which was how she corrected the entire world. "Then it's true, and it can't be undone." She looked around the shop — at the ink-stained daughter, the apprentice, the quiet soldier by the stove, the husband holding his own testimony at arm's length — and added, in the tone she used for announcing dinner or fate: "I'll make the good coffee."

* * *

They distributed the edition themselves, which meant Milo distributed it and invented elaborate cover stories for the exercise. Copies went into law libraries wrapped as hymnals. A crate marked FISH went to the teachers' college. The naval chaplain collected his in person, saluted nobody, and paid in coin counted out onto the stone like a man settling a debt rather than buying a book, which, Ulyana thought, was exactly what he was doing.

By April, the Record was being read aloud in rooms Ulyana would never see, in voices she would never hear, and quoted in a newspaper in the capital that spelled her father's name wrong in the article and right in the footnote, and a deputy in the Riksdag stood up with a copy and read page ninety-one into the official record, where it could no longer be summarized away by anyone.

Halvard's name continued to appear at dinners. Halvard would likely die in a bed with a view. Ulyana had made her peace with that in the winter, or near it — peace being, she had learned, less a settlement than a subscription, renewed daily. The general would keep his pension. But the mule driver's name was in a book now, and the girl from Kalmenmoor, and the sergeant with the unruly class, and no dinner in the world could take them out again.

That was the arithmetic she had chosen. It didn't balance. It had never been going to balance. But it was *hers*, entered in her own hand, and there was a fierce comfort in that, the same comfort as a perfectly locked forme, as a sheet pulled clean on the first try: the world would not cooperate, and she would set it straight anyway, line by line, as far as her reach went.

* * *

On a soft evening in late April, with the second run drying in racks and the light going amber across the canal, Anton came to stand beside her at the case. He had a letter in his hand — the tribunal's, thick-papered, official — and he held it the way people hold documents that have already changed their lives and are waiting for permission to change them again.

"They want me in Liden by the ninth of May," he said. "To testify. In person. On the record."

Ulyana slid the stick into her left hand so she could face him fully. "You'll go, then."

"I'll go." He looked out at the water. "It's just — I've been telling the truth for three years to people who didn't want it. I don't entirely know how to tell it to people who've asked for it. It may come out crooked."

"Then they'll correct it," Ulyana said. "That's what proofs are for. Nobody gets it clean on the first pull. Not even Father. Especially not Father — have you seen his 'f'?"

Anton laughed, and then was quiet, and then said, in the voice of a man checking each word against a ledger: "I'll come back. After. If the shop — if you—"

"Ulyana," called her father from the stone, without looking up from the galley he was correcting, "if that boy is going to keep standing at your case blocking the light, he should at least learn where the em quads live. He'll be useless to me in Liden and worse here."

Anton went pink. Ulyana bit down on a smile and pointed to the job case, demure as a schoolmistress.

"Bottom left," she said. "Mind the thins. They roll."

He bent to look, and his shoulder brushed hers, and neither of them moved away, and outside, along the canal, the lamps of Vessengard came on one by one, ordinary as ever, while inside the shop the last light fell on a page still wet with ink — a page with a name on it that could never now be unwritten.
