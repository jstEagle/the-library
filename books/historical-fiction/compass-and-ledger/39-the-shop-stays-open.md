## 39. The Shop Stays Open

The herring season came in on a Tuesday, and with it the smell that Milo swore was worse than solvent and I swore was worse than Milo. Vessengard in late spring was a city rinsed clean and hung out to dry — fishing nets on the quay like enormous grey hair, bunting left over from the armistice festivities sagging between lampposts, and everywhere the sound of hammers as the city repaired what two wars and one revolution's worth of shortages had knocked out of it.

The Eriksen print shop had needed its own repairs. The window Brandt's men had cracked during their last search — politely, apologetically, the way everything Brandt did was polite — was replaced with glass slightly greener than the rest, so that if you stood in the right light, the street outside took on the tint of the sea. I had decided not to have it matched. A shop ought to show its scars the way a face does: honestly, and without apology.

Business, absurdly, was thriving. Word travels faster than any courier, and the word was that the Eriksen shop printed true. We had contracts now from three merchant houses, the fishermen's cooperative, the Lutheran charities, and — this was the detail Milo delighted in recounting to anyone who would stand still — the Office of the Armistice Commission itself, which sent us its administrative printing because, as the clerk who delivered the order put it with a straight face he could not entirely maintain, "your shop has demonstrated an exceptional commitment to accuracy."

I set that order myself. Every comma of it. If they wanted our accuracy, I would give them accuracy until it choked them.

Milo had been apprenticed properly by then — papers signed, guild dues paid, his name entered in the rolls in Signe Holm's old hand, which Signe herself had come out of custody thin but unbowed to inscribe. She had spent six weeks in detention and referred to it thereafter, in mixed company, as "my sabbatical." The guild had restored her mastership with a haste that suggested embarrassment, and she wore her embarrassment of the guild like a brooch.

"You're pressing too hard," she told him now, from her chair by the stove, where she presided over our mornings like a retired admiral watching young officers learn to reef sails. "The paper is not your enemy, boy. You are not at war with the paper."

"Everything else is at war with the paper," Milo said. "The ink is at war with the paper. The weather is at war with the paper."

"The weather," said Signe, "is not wrong."

It was a good morning. My mother came down before noon — she came down most days now, her strength returning in the slow way that spring returns, imperceptibly and then all at once — and sat with Signe shelling peas and offering opinions about the shipping manifests in the tone of a woman who had once believed print shops were beneath her and had since revised her views on nearly everything.

And Anton was packing.

He had been packing for three days, which meant the shop had acquired that particular atmosphere of a house where someone is leaving: his spare shirts folded into the same canvas satchel he'd arrived with, his few books stacked on the workbench, and around all of it a careful cheerfulness that fooled no one, least of all my mother, who kept finding reasons to feed him.

The tribunal in Marholm had summoned him to testify. Formal inquiry, public sessions, foreign observers — everything we had printed into existence, arriving now in the shape of a railway warrant and a letter written on paper so fine it seemed embarrassed by its contents. He would take the stand with his testimony already in every bookstall in the country; the pamphlet had done its work before the lawyers opened their mouths. But a pamphlet is evidence, and Anton was a witness, and witnesses must stand up in rooms where men like Halvard's advocates could look at them.

"He'll be back by Michaelmas," Milo said, for perhaps the fourth time that week, tightening a screw on the press that did not need tightening. "Back by Michaelmas, and I'll have the second press reassembled by then, and we'll run the full edition before winter."

"Michaelmas," Anton agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing to a weather forecast.

* * *

I found him at the loading door in the late afternoon, when the light came down the alley sideways and turned everything the color of brass. It was, of course, the only place he could be found. He had arrived at that door half-dead, fourteen months ago now, leaning against the frame with a fever in him like a fire in a wet house, and he had gone to it a hundred times since — to smoke, to think, to watch the gulls contest some item of garbage with the dock cats. Doors remember. I think he knew ours remembered him.

"Packing," I said.

"Sorting." He held up a shirt as evidence. "There is a difference known only to soldiers and compositors."

"Compositors sort type. Soldiers sort excuses."

"There," he said, wounded. "And after I taught you the quoins."

I leaned beside him against the frame. The alley smelled of tar and brine and, faintly, of someone's dinner. Somewhere behind us in the shop, Milo dropped something and blamed the cat, who had died years ago and whose reputation Milo maintained anyway, out of respect.

We had never named the thing between us. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole trade. There had been moments — teaching him to set type, his hands over mine on the composing stick; the night of the great print run when he had looked at me across three thousand sheets and something had passed between us that needed no language, having found language inadequate; the evening after Signe's arrest when he had gripped my shoulder and I had let my hand stay on his a beat longer than comfort requires. Anyone watching would have named it. We had not. Some things, unnamed, stay possible. Named, they demand a future, and neither of us could promise each other a future — not with a tribunal between us, and a fortress, and a father still learning how to be alive in a room above a bakery on Sankt Ida's Street.

"I've been thinking," Anton said, "about the first night. When you hid me."

"You were dripping on the floor. Hiding you was mostly housekeeping."

"You didn't know me. You didn't know what I carried. The patrol came down the alley and you blew out the lamp and stood in front of me, and I remember thinking—" He stopped, and looked at the opposite wall where the ivy had begun its annual conquest of the drainpipe. "I thought, this woman doesn't know yet whether I'm worth saving, and she's decided to save me anyway, and decide later. That was the bravest thing I saw in two wars. Not at the front. At the loading door."

"You were feverish," I said. "You may have hallucinated the lamp."

"Ulyana."

"I know." I watched a gull win its argument. "I know."

Because here is what I had understood, slowly, the way one understands a letter one has read four times: that he loved me, and that I loved him, and that we had both been waiting for the other to say it first out of a courtesy so complete it had become its own kind of cowardice. And that saying it now, on the eve of his departure, would be either a promise or a farewell, and we could not yet know which, and pretending otherwise would poison whatever we actually had.

So I said it plainly. That is what the year had taught me — plainness. Truth set flush, no kerning tricks.

"I love you," I said. "I expect you've noticed. I'd rather say it while there's time to hear it answered than spend Michaelmas composing clever ways not to."

Anton did not move for a moment. Then he laughed — a short, unsteady laugh, the kind that has to get out of the way before anything truer can follow — and he took my hand, and turned it over, and looked at the ink stains on my fingers as though reading them.

"I wrote you letters," he said. "In my head. All winter. Terrible ones. I'm a soldier; I can march forty kilometers and I cannot write a sentence that says what you just said."

"Don't write it, then. Say it."

"I love you." He said it easily, which told me he had practiced. "I will love you at the tribunal, and on the train, and wherever they send me after. Whether I come back by Michaelmas or by next Michaelmas or not at all — that part doesn't change. What changes is whether you should wait, and I won't ask you to. I've seen what waiting does. Your mother waited six years for a death certificate."

"My mother," I said, "waited badly. She waited by pretending you were dead and my father was dead and the whole world outside the kitchen was dead. I don't intend to wait at all. I intend to work. If you come back, you'll find me at the case, probably arguing with Milo about leading. If you don't come back, I'll find you — in the record, in the testimony, in every line of it. I am my father's daughter and a compositor's apprentice twice over. I know how to keep someone."

He pressed my hand to his mouth, briefly, and I let him, and then we stood shoulder to shoulder looking out at the alley like two people keeping the same watch, which is, I have since decided, most of what love is.

Inside, my mother was loudly not eavesdropping.

* * *

He left on the morning boat, because the morning boat was cheaper and because Anton, who had crossed half of Europe on other people's risk, still counted coins like a man who did not trust luck to keep being lucky.

The whole shop walked him to the quay, which is to say: five people and one handcart of supplies Milo had volunteered to "deliver to the fishmonger" along a route that passed directly through the departure crowd. My father came too. He had walked down from Sankt Ida's Street unannounced, hat brushed, coat mended at the elbows, and when Anton saw him he stopped as if struck, and my father put out his hand, and they shook like men concluding a transaction older than either of them.

"Bring back the truth," my father said, "in good order. I've read the draft testimony. Your third section needs restructuring — chronology, not theme. Juries think in calendars."

"Yes, sir," said Anton, who had learned within a week of my father's return that this was the correct answer to everything.

My mother kissed his cheeks and told him the Marholm boarding houses were damp and pressed a parcel on him that clinked. Signe told him that if any Marholm printer offered to help him and mentioned the guild, he should under no circumstances trust the man, and gave him a name to trust instead. Milo shook his hand solemnly and then hugged him quickly, the way boys hug, as if the embrace owed money.

Then there was nothing left but the gangway and the whistle, and Anton stood a moment with me at the foot of it.

"Michaelmas," he said.

"Or whenever the truth is done with you." I straightened his collar, which did not need it. "Send proofs. I'll mark them. Your commas are sentimental — you put them wherever you feel strongly."

"I do feel strongly about my commas."

"Go," I said. "Before the tide gets ideas."

He went. I watched him up the gangway and along the deck, a tall man with a soldier's shoulders and a printer's new stoop, and he did not look back until the very end, at the rail, and then he raised one hand — not waving, exactly. More like setting a line of type. Placing one small mark, deliberately, in the upper corner of the day.

The boat drew its long pencil-stroke across the harbor and out past the breakwater, and I stood among the fish crates until Milo appeared at my elbow with the empty handcart and the expression of a boy deciding whether a question was safe.

"You'll write him," he said. It was not quite a question. "Letters. Long ones."

"Copies," I corrected. "Every letter twice. Draft and file. I am keeper of the record now; even my correspondence keeps records."

"And the file copy?"

"Stays in the ledger room. Bottom drawer. Where nobody looks because everybody thinks that's where nobody looks."

Milo nodded gravely, absorbing this as one more law of an universe whose laws multiplied monthly. Then he said, in the careful voice he used for the questions that mattered: "And if the tribunal goes badly? If Halvard's people twist it, and Anton's name ends up mud, and none of it — none of the pamphlet, none of the gates, none of it — counts?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved that. Out on the water the ferry was a smudge now, merging with the horizon the way all departures do, becoming ordinary, becoming weather.

"Then it will count the way the manifest counts," I said. "Not today. But someday someone will pull the file and check the entry against the page, and find that somebody, somewhere, wrote down what actually happened — and got the spelling right."

Milo considered this, and found it insufficiently cheerful, and solved the problem in the traditional manner.

"Well," he said, grabbing the cart handles, "the Halvard Commission order's due Thursday, and if we're late with it, accuracy can go hang itself. Coming, Miss Eriksen? Signe says the new apprentice starts Monday and I intend to be better than him by Sunday."

"The new apprentice," I said, falling into step. "You've met this apprentice?"

"Milo Jans," he said, with tremendous dignity, "does not scout the competition. Milo Jans simply intends to crush it."

Behind us the harbor closed over its morning; ahead, the green-glass window of the shop caught the sun and threw it, briefly, all the way down the lane. My mother was already at the door, holding it open, and inside I could see the case gleaming, tomorrow's job ticket pinned above it in my own unmistakable hand.

There was type waiting to be set. There always would be.
