## 40. Compass and Ledger

The shop smelled of ink again.

Not the way it had before — not exactly. Three years of gunpowder gossip and pamphlet smoke had worked itself into the grain of the floorboards, into the mortar between the bricks, so that now when Ulyana pushed open the loading door at seven in the morning she caught the old familiar bitterness underneath a new one, like a chord with an added note that no one had written but everyone could hear. She had stopped trying to sort the two smells apart. They belonged to each other now, the way her father's handwriting belonged to his crooked left-leaning f's, the way Milo's laugh belonged to the hour before lunch.

She hung her coat on the peg — her peg, third from the door, which Milo still occasionally usurped and which she still occasionally made him regret — and lit the stove, and stood a moment in the grey light watching dust settle over the cases.

Tomorrow was the first of November. The *Vessengard Courier* wanted four hundred shipping manifests by Thursday, the city registrar wanted ballot notices, a widow on Kirke Street wanted funeral cards for a man who had, according to his own instructions printed at the bottom, "finally stopped arguing." And beneath all of it, stacked in reams behind the locked back room whose lock nobody bothered to turn anymore, sat the last unbound signatures of the second edition — the full edition, the one with her father's name in the colophon where it had always belonged.

Ulyana Eriksen, compositor, took down her apron and tied it, and considered how strange it was that a life could change completely and still begin every morning the same way: with cold type, a cold stove, and the small stubborn work of making marks mean something.

* * *

Milo arrived at half past seven, out of breath, which meant he had run, which meant he had overslept, which meant he would blame the tram.

"The tram," he announced, before she could say anything, "was a conspiracy."

"Against you specifically."

"Against punctuality as an ideal. It never came. Then two came at once, like they'd been waiting around the corner laughing."

"That is how trams work, Milo. You wait a long time and then everything arrives together. Like trouble."

He grinned, hung his coat on *her* peg out of pure habit and malice, and went to the stone to wash up. He was seventeen now, or claimed to be; there had been some confusion during the war about his papers, and he had discovered that a boy who could set type and forge passes could forge almost anything, including a birth year more flattering than the truth. Ulyana let it stand. The war had taken enough accurate records.

"You've seen the proofs?" he said, quieter, over the sound of the tap.

"Last night. Twice."

"And?"

"And they're good." She weighed a slug of type in her hand, testing its truth by feel, an old habit. "Better than good. Signe's sister did the binding samples — the blue boards?"

"With the compass stamped on the front."

"With the compass stamped on the front." Their imprint. Drawn by Milo, argued over by committee — the committee being four people and a stove — and chosen in the end because, as her father had said when he finally saw it, thin and grey and squinting through spectacles that were newer than his eyes, *"A ledger tells you where you are. A compass tells you what direction. A printer should keep both."*

He was upstairs. That was the fact Ulyana still turned over each morning like a coin she couldn't quite believe was real. Not restored, exactly — nothing about him was restored; the fortress had gone through him the way water goes through limestone, leaving channels — but present. Eating breakfast. Arguing with Astrid Eriksen about whether the good butter was for guests or family, and arriving each morning at the case nearest the window, where he had stood for thirty years, and setting his hands among the type as if greeting old colleagues who might reasonably have expected never to see him again.

He had wept, the day the first edition came off the press. Ulyana had never seen him weep. She had looked away, because some things are private even in a room with four people in it, and because she had learned — late, expensively — that looking away is a choice too, and she intended to make her choices deliberately from then on.

* * *

Anton came at nine, from the harbor, where the inquiry commission had rooms. He walked with a cane now and pretended he didn't need it; he leaned it against the doorframe the way he once leaned his rifle, with the air of a man who has made peace with an instrument. His testimony had been given over eleven days in a room with bad ventilation, and he had come out of it each evening greyer and somehow lighter, as if words were a weight he had been carrying in a satchel for three years and could finally set down page by page.

"They've adjourned until Tuesday," he said, taking the coffee Milo poured without being asked. "Halvard's counsel spoke for four hours."

"About?"

"Jurisdiction. Precedent. The fog of war." Anton's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Mostly about how large the fog is, and how unfortunate that anyone should be expected to see through it."

"And the commission?"

"Listened. Wrote. Adjourned." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something in his shoulder, and Ulyana saw him refuse to wince and admired him for it and hated that he had to. "It will go as it goes. He will probably dine well this winter somewhere warm. But it's in the record now. All of it. Spoken aloud, under oath, in a room with stenographers. Whatever else happens, a man in 1970 can pull those pages and read what Halvard did, and no counsel's fog will cover it."

"A man in 1970," Milo said. "You think people will still care in fifty years?"

"I think," Anton said, "that I cared, and I am nobody, and there are more of us than there are of him. That arithmetic doesn't expire."

Ulyana turned back to her case. There was a lump in her throat that had no business interrupting a working morning, and the best cure for unbusinesslike feelings was work.

* * *

At noon her mother came down with soup — there was always soup; Astrid Eriksen had decided that if the world insisted on producing crises, she would meet them nourished — and stayed to argue mildly with her husband about butter, and lost, and won anyway, because he ate the good butter while complaining about it, which in thirty years of marriage amounted to surrender.

Then the shop was quiet, the way shops are quiet after lunch: a held-breath quiet, the machines cooling, the city outside doing its slow afternoon business. Ulyana stood at the composing stone with tomorrow's type laid out before her — the manifests, the ballot notices, the ordinary freight of an ordinary port town — and she found herself, instead of sorting, remembering.

She remembered being eleven, standing on a crate at this very case, her father's hand over hers guiding a composing stick. *"Letters face the right way or they don't speak, Ulya. Everything in this trade is backwards until you print it. Remember that. Most true things look backwards until they're brought out into the light."* She had thought he meant type. Perhaps he had only meant type, then. Men say things in workshops that turn out, decades later, to have been prophecies they didn't know they were making.

She remembered the years she had spent believing history was something that happened elsewhere — to capitals, to grandees, to men whose names arrived in newspapers already typeset by strangers. She had been comfortable in that belief. Comfortable the way a person is comfortable in a house with a cracked foundation: nothing has fallen yet, so why look down?

But the crack had been there. It had been there in the government order whose pages didn't match the ledger, in the manifest stock that vanished into wars, in every polite sentence she and Inspector Brandt had ever exchanged across cups of tea, each of them holding a whole unwritten novel behind their teeth. History had been happening to her the entire time. It simply hadn't bothered to introduce itself.

And here was the thing she had come to, slowly, the way type comes to the stick letter by letter, backwards and upside-down until the moment of printing:

That wasn't history's fault. It was a question of authorship. History happens to everyone — to the girl who sets type and the general who burns villages and the inspector who files reports he knows are incomplete and the apprentice who runs a stolen ream of paper through the night rain because it was the only decent thing to do and happened to be illegal. Everyone is in the story. The only variable, the single lever the whole future rests upon, is who writes it down. Halvard had understood this; he had commissioned his own histories before his crimes were finished, had bought historians the way other men bought boots. Her father had understood it too, alone in a deserter's garret three hundred miles away, writing testimony in a coded ledger because paper was rationed and truth was contraband. Between those two understandings — one armored in medals, one hidden in margins — a city, a country, a century gets decided.

She had chosen her side. She chose it again every morning.

* * *

"Ulya." Her father's voice from the window case. "Come look at this."

She came. He had before him a proof of the new edition's title page, and he was looking at it the way he had once looked at her mother across a dance floor — with an expression he believed was neutral and never had been.

"The spacing on 'testimony,'" he said. "Second line. Something's wrong with it."

She bent. She looked. She saw it at once — a word-space a hair too wide, invisible to any reader alive except the two people standing at this stone.

"That's mine," she said. "I kerned it loose on purpose."

"Why on earth would you—"

"So you'd notice. So you'd come to me and complain, and I could watch your face while you did." She straightened. "I wanted to know it was really you checking my work. Some habits are worth keeping."

For a moment her father said nothing. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a press turning over after a long idle — and put his arm briefly, awkwardly around her shoulders, and said, "Fix it anyway. Craftsmen don't get to be sentimental in the word-spacing. Sentimental in the margins all you like."

"I learned margins from you."

"Then I've no one to blame." He turned back to the case, and she watched him for a moment — this man who had chosen survival and shame and the long slow labor of witness over a clean death and a clean silence; who had cost his family two years of grief to buy them a document; whom she had cursed and forgiven and cursed and forgiven until cursing and forgiving wore smooth into something simpler, which was love without illusions, the only kind worth having.

* * *

In the afternoon Brandt came.

This required explanation, the first time it happened, and had required several explanations since, and had settled at last into requiring none. He came monthly now, in a civilian coat, with a folder under his arm, and he drank tea and discussed censorship schedules that no longer much mattered, and once, without preamble, he had set on the table the complete file of the Eriksen shop's surveillance — three years of informants' notes, including his own — and said, "These were destroyed in the administrative reorganization. Sloppy filing. Terrible shame. I've come to tell you they're destroyed," and had drunk his tea while Ulyana burned them page by page in the stove, both of them watching the informants' careful handwriting curl and blacken like small confessions.

Today he had no folders. He had a newspaper, and an obituary circled in pencil — a minor official, dead of pneumonia, who had once signed the transfer orders for the fortress prisoners.

"I thought you'd want it for your records," Brandt said. "You keep better ones than the ministry does."

"We do," Ulyana agreed pleasantly, and poured his tea, and neither of them said anything about the amnesty, or the inquiry, or the general dining warmly somewhere this winter, because there was nothing left to say that wasn't already in print, three thousand copies deep, in cellars and choir lofts and the pockets of fish-cart drivers from here to the capital.

At the door he paused. "Your father's colophon," he said. "Eriksen and Daughter."

"Eriksen and Daughter," she confirmed.

"It's a good name for a firm." He cleared his throat. "Historically speaking, firms that keep honest ledgers outlast the ones that don't."

When he was gone, Milo emerged from behind the presses where he had absolutely not been eavesdropping and said, "Was that—"

"That was a man choosing, once, late, the only way he could," Ulyana said. "Set the ballot notices."

* * *

Evening came early in November. By five the lamps were lit and the windows had gone dark-gold, and Anton had fallen asleep in the chair by the stove with an uncorrected proof rising and falling on his chest, and Milo was whistling something from the choir loft route — he still helped with distributions sometimes, though there was nothing clandestine left to distribute; the whistling was nostalgia — and Ulyana stood at her case and began, at last, to set tomorrow's type.

Manifests. Ballot notices. The widow's funeral cards with their triumphant footnote. Ordinary work, honest work, the kind she had once thought was all there was and had learned was the ground everything else stands on. She set the letters backwards and upside-down, facing the right way in their secret mirrored country, and her hands moved without her asking them to, and she thought: *this is the whole of it.* Not the raid, not the pamphlet, not the crowd in the square where Halvard's ceremony had been punctured by ten thousand small rustling papers like sleet. Those had been the printing. This was the press.

History happens to everyone. To girls who set type most of all, because girls who set type are the ones nobody watches, and the unwatched can see clearly. The question — the only question, asked fresh every morning by every regime and answered fresh every morning by somebody — is who writes it down.

Her father closed his ledger at six o'clock precisely, as he had done for thirty-five years, and put on his coat, and paused at the door to look back at the lamplit shop: at Anton asleep, at Milo whistling, at his daughter at the case with ink to the second knuckle.

"Lock up when you're done," he said. Which was what he had always said. What he had never said before, in all those years of saying it, was what he said now, quietly, in the doorway:

"I'm glad it's you keeping it."

Then he was gone up the stairs, and Ulyana Eriksen stood alone among her letters, and reached for the big ledger — the shop ledger, the compass-and-ledger, the book of what came in and what went out and what was owed — and ruled it off at the bottom of the page. Orders filled. Debts paid. Names restored.

She opened a fresh page, dated it, and began to write.
