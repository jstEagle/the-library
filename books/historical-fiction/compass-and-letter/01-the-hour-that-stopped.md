## 1. The Hour That Stopped

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Mara Dunmore would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days nobody wanted anything from her.

It arrived as a note carried by a boy who didn't wait for an answer, delivered to the workshop of Herr Brandt & Sons, Clockmakers, where Mara had been an apprentice for nine years and a presence for eleven. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, with a crest she didn't recognize and handwriting that slanted forward like a person leaning into the wind.

*The regulator at Halvorsen Observatory has stopped. We are given to understand your hands may be of use. Come Tuesday. Ask for no one. The door will be open.*

No signature. No payment terms. In the trade, this was either an insult or an honor, and Mara genuinely could not tell which.

Herr Brandt read it twice, holding it at arm's length the way he held everything now, his eyes having surrendered their near focus two winters past. He looked at her over the rims of his spectacles with an expression she had learned to distrust: something like pride, crossed with something like apology.

"They asked for you," he said. "Not for me. For you."

"Perhaps they misspelled your name."

"Perhaps." He handed the note back. "Or perhaps someone told them that the girl in my back room can hear a escapement lie from across a bench."

Mara said nothing to that. She had long ago stopped explaining that she couldn't *hear* anything special; she simply paid attention, which was different, and less romantic, and got called a gift anyway by people who never had to be invisible to survive.

Invisibility was a craft like any other. Mara had been apprenticed to it before clockwork—raised into it, really, in the Foundling Home on Wexler Street, where the matron's one commandment had been carved above the lintel where all sixty children passed under it daily: *BE UNREMARKABLE.* Not *be good.* Not *be useful.* Unremarkable. It was the only scripture of the place, and Mara had studied it devoutly. She walked at the pace of nobody in particular. She wore coats the color of wet pavement. When spoken to, she answered in few words and let her eyes rest slightly left of her interlocutor's face, close enough to seem polite, far enough to invite forgetting.

It had kept her fed through lean years. It had kept her untouched through dangerous ones. But standing in Herr Brandt's workshop that Tuesday morning, folding the cream-colored note into quarters, Mara felt the first small tremor along the foundations of her invisibility, though she would not have named it then. Something had needed her. Not Brandt. Not the firm. Her.

She did not know yet what to do with that. She suspected it was a trap. Most things that wanted you specifically were.

* * *

The observatory sat on Kessel Hill like a chess piece forgotten mid-game—a white dome and its square tower rising above a terraced garden gone half wild, overlooking the harbor and the gray sprawl of the city below. Mara climbed the switchback road on foot because the tram fare was three coppers and she had decided, without consulting anyone, that whoever had sent that note owed her nothing.

That was another rule of hers, older than the matron's: owe nothing, be owed nothing. Debts were hooks. Favors were hooks. Kindness, most of all, was a hook.

The gate at the foot of the drive stood open exactly as promised, which unsettled her more than a locked gate would have. A locked gate meant someone cared who came in. An open one meant either trust or ambush, and Mara had met far more ambushes than trusts.

She catalogued herself as she climbed, the way she always did entering unfamiliar ground. Twenty-three years old. Small even for a woman, which in the clock trade was an advantage nobody admitted aloud—small hands, narrow fingers, the wrists of a person who could hold still for hours. Brown hair pinned back without ceremony. A face that witnesses consistently failed to describe. Her tool roll rode against her spine inside her coat, forty knives and tweezers and files of her own maintenance, worth more than everything else she owned combined. If the day went badly, that was what she would protect first. She had made her peace with what that said about her years ago.

The observatory's front entrance faced the sea. Mara went around to the tradesman's door instead, because unremarkable people entered through tradesman's doors, and found it—as promised—unlocked.

Inside smelled of cold brass, lamp oil, and dust that had settled slowly enough to become architecture. She stood a moment in the dim corridor letting her eyes adjust, listening to the building the way she listened to any mechanism before touching it. Every house has a pulse. This one had several: a tall-case somewhere to the left ticking a quarter-second slow, a carriage clock dying quietly upstairs, water moving in pipes. And beneath all of it, so deep she felt it in her sternum before she heard it, a great silence—the silence of a very large thing that ought to be making noise and wasn't.

She followed it down the corridor, past a stair, through a door marked with a brass plate worn nearly blank, into the base of the tower.

The regulator filled the room the way a cathedral fills a plaza.

Mara had read about it—every clockmaker had. The Halvorsen Regulator, built in '61 by old Anders Halvorsen himself, nine feet of black walnut case and gilded works, the master clock from which every chronometer in the observatory, every telescope drive, every tide table published in this city for thirty years, took its authority. It had kept time to a fraction of a second a day. Astronomers had supposedly wept at its funeral.

It hung on the tower's inner wall behind glass, and it was dead.

Not broken-dead. Not damaged-dead. Stopped. The hands stood at 4:17 precisely, the minute hand just past the hour mark but not yet to the four-minute tick, frozen there like a man photographed mid-stride. Dust lay over the case in a smooth gray fur, undisturbed, and Mara's professional eye read the strata of it before she consciously decided to: this clock had not been opened, touched, wound, or breathed upon in a very long time. Eleven years, if the gossip sheets she half-remembered were right. Eleven years ago the great regulator had stopped for no reason anyone published, and the observatory had declined from public institution to private oddity, and here it stood, keeping its one stubborn hour.

4:17.

Mara set down her bag and rolled her sleeves, and made herself do the thing she always did first, which was to look without wanting anything.

Wanting ruined observation. That was true of clocks and, she suspected, of people; she'd just never had enough data on people. So she circled the case once, slowly. She noted the hairline crack in the lower left pane of glass—not struck, *stressed*, the glass had been cold and something had been hot behind it. She noted that the winding crank socket showed bright metal at its edges, meaning someone had tried to wind it after it stopped, repeatedly, and then given up. She noted that the pendulum hung not straight but cocked maybe two degrees to port, which no free-swinging pendulum does when it stops naturally.

A pendulum stops where friction catches it, or where a hand places it. This one had been placed. Or trapped.

Her hands were already warm. That was the thing about the work—her mind could maintain its little fortifications, its rules about debts and hooks and being owed nothing, but her hands were honest laborers and they wanted this job the way a thirsty woman wants water. Nine feet of master regulator, dead eleven years, and someone had finally sent for her.

"First time," she said aloud to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it smaller, "that anything has needed me."

The room did not argue. The room had the particular quality of rooms that are listened to by other rooms—she had the sense, sudden and certain, that the tower was not empty, that somewhere above or below her, someone knew she had arrived and had decided, deliberately, not to come down. Ask for no one, the note had said. The door will be open.

Fine. She could work with strange. Strange was easier than kind.

* * *

Opening the case took her twenty minutes, and she resented every one of them, because the lock was trivial and the difficulty was entirely ceremonial—someone had fitted it with a cheap replacement escutcheon sometime in the last decade, the work of a handyman, not a horologist. Whoever maintained—or failed to maintain—this clock had not been worthy of it. She filed that away with the cracked glass and the bright crank socket. Buildings keep records too. You just have to know how to read them.

The case swung open on a breath of air that had been sealed since before her apprenticeship began. It smelled of dry wood and old machine oil and, faintly, unexpectedly, of lavender—which puzzled her, and she put the puzzle aside with the discipline of long practice, though some part of her mind tagged it *return later* and refused to release it.

The movement rose before her in tiers: the great barrel, the going train, the striking train with its hammers raised against silent bells, and above them all the escapement, the beating heart, where a wheel with fifteen teeth should have been releasing the train tooth by tooth, second by second, for eternity.

Instead the pallets were jammed. Not worn, not broken—jammed. Something small and pale was wedged between the third and fourth escape teeth, stopping the wheel, stopping the train, stopping the barrel, stopping nine feet of walnut and gilt and human genius at 4:17 and holding it there like a hand around a throat.

Mara fetched her loude from her roll, fitted it to her eye, and looked closer.

It was paper.

A scrap of paper, folded to the density of a fingernail, wedged into the escapement of the finest regulator on the coast by someone who had known exactly what they were doing—because to stop this clock cleanly, without damaging a single tooth, you would have to know precisely where to insert a shim, precisely how thick, precisely how deep. This had not been vandalism. This had been surgery. Someone had performed a deliberate, skilled, irreversible arrest of time itself, and chosen 4:17 as the hour to arrest.

Her hands, hovering with tweezers at the threshold of the movement, had gone very still. Stiller than they had ever been at any bench.

Eleven years, the clock had hung here at 4:17. Eleven years, someone had presumably known why. And now a cream-colored note with no signature had summoned an orphan apprentice across the city, through an unlocked gate, into an empty tower, to find it.

Ask for no one.

Mara became aware, then, of the full weight of the silence around her, and understood for the first time in her working life that silence is not an absence. Silence is a held breath. The tower was holding its breath. Whatever was wedged in this escapement had been wedged here on purpose, by hands, for reasons—and reasons that stop clocks are rarely gentle reasons.

The sensible move was obvious. Extract the obstruction, service the movement, restart the pendulum, bill whoever sent the note, go home to her rented room over the fishmonger's, and remain a woman whom witnesses cannot describe. That was the move the matron above the lintel would have approved. *Be unremarkable.*

But her hands wanted the clock.

And beneath the wanting, quieter, in the place where she kept things she refused to look at directly, another thought had already formed and would not dissolve: *someone stopped this hour on purpose, and I am the first person in eleven years to stand close enough to ask it why.*

"I see you," she told the clock softly—an absurd thing to say to a machine, and she said it anyway. "Whatever you're holding, I'm going to have it."

She reached in with the tweezers.

The paper came free with a whisper of friction, and the escape wheel shivered—actually shivered, one tooth's width, the whole vast machine twitching like a sleeping giant disturbed—and then resettled, dead again, waiting.

The paper was folded eight times and waxed shut, and the wax bore the impression of a seal she did not recognize: a compass rose, and beneath it, pressed deep, a single letter.

*M.*

Below, somewhere above her in the tower, a floorboard creaked—once, deliberately, like a signal.

Mara closed her hand around the letter and did not run, because running is remarkable, and because whatever waited up those stairs had been waiting eleven years too.

She climbed toward it.
