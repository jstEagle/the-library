## 2. Household of Strays

The summons had come by boy on a bicycle, which was the first strange thing. Nobody sent boys on bicycles for clockmakers' apprentices; they sent them for doctors and for deaths. The note was written in a hand that pressed too hard on the page, all its letters leaning forward like men in a hurry, and it said only: *The regulator at Halvorsen Observatory has stopped. Come Tuesday. Ask for Vey.*

It didn't say who I was. It didn't say what the pay would be. It said *come*, as if my coming were a settled matter, as if I were a person whose Tuesdays could be claimed.

I went anyway. A stopped master clock is a year of wages to an apprentice, even a split of one, and Master Ellery had been letting me take outside work for two winters now because his own hands shook too badly for fine adjustment and he liked the cut of my silence. "You've got the two virtues," he'd told me once, tapping my knuckles with a screwdriver handle. "Patience and no curiosity." He'd been wrong about the second one, but I hadn't corrected him. Correcting people is a way of being seen.

The observatory sat on the hill above the harbor like a chess piece nobody had moved in forty years — white dome, black railings, a drive of gravel gone soft and weedy at the edges. I climbed it on foot because the tram cost twopence and I had counted out my week already. The wind up there came straight off the water and smelled of salt and iron, and it pushed at my back the whole climb as though the hill itself wanted me to hurry up and get it over with.

I had catalogued myself long ago, the way you catalogue tools: Mara Dunmore, seventeen, apprentice, small enough to pass unnoticed through any door, quiet enough that servants forget she's in the room, useful enough to be hired and forgettable enough never to be thanked. It wasn't self-pity. It was inventory. In the orphanage we learned early that the girls who were noticed were the girls who were blamed, and the girls who were blamed were the girls who were sent away, and there was nowhere further to send you than where you already were. So I made myself a shadow with steady hands, and it had served me well for seventeen years.

I expected it to serve me here.

* * *

A man answered the side door before I finished knocking — a big man, gray-stubbled, wearing a waistcoat buttoned wrong and an expression of a man interrupted mid-thought some years ago and never quite returned from it.

"You're the clock girl," he said.

"I'm the apprentice," I said. "Mara Dunmore."

"Tomas Vey." He didn't shake hands. He looked at mine instead, at the oil stains worked into the skin around my nails, and something crossed his face that I couldn't read and decided not to try. "You'd better come in. It's worse than the letter said."

Inside smelled of paper and cold tea and, underneath both, the particular mineral smell of a building full of brass. We went down a corridor lined with framed star charts so old their ink had gone brown, past a room where a fire had been laid but not lit, and into the clock chamber.

I had repaired regulators before. I want to say that plainly, because everything after this will sound like exaggeration and I need the record straight: I had seen great clocks. Ellery kept the city hall works; I had stood inside the drum of the exchange clock with my arm buried to the shoulder in gearwork while merchants shouted three floors below. But the Halvorsen regulator was different. It filled the end of the chamber floor to ceiling, a walnut case tall as two men, glass fronted, and behind the glass hung a pendulum the length of a church pew, still as a corpse. Eleven dials of silvered brass. A dead weight of a thing, gorgeous and silent, and the silence coming off it was the kind of silence a room makes when someone has just left it.

"Stopped eleven years," Vey said. "Four-seventeen, the evening of the ninth of March, 1882. It's been four-seventeen ever since."

"Why hasn't anyone—"

"Three firms have looked at it. Two refused the job outright. One took the commission, opened the case, closed it again, and returned the fee." He said it flatly, but I caught the thing under the flatness, the way you catch a draft under a door. "Halvorsen wouldn't hear of replacing it. So it sits."

I put my palm flat against the case. Walnut, cold, sound wood. Through the glass the pendulum hung plumb and perfect, waiting. Clocks don't wait — I know that better than anyone, they're machines, they have no intentions — but standing in front of that one I felt watched anyway, felt *summoned*, and I didn't care for the feeling. Being needed is a debt you incur before you know the terms.

"I'll need the case open," I said. "Full access. And I'll need to know why three firms walked away, because if there's something in that clock besides a broken escapement, I'd rather hear it now than find it at midnight."

Vey looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed — one dry syllable, more cough than laugh.

"Ellery trained you?"

"Ellery trained everyone worth hiring."

"He did." He turned toward the corridor. "Supper's at seven. Astronomer eats at eight. You'll eat at seven with me."

"I haven't agreed to stay past the assessment."

"It's stew," he said, over his shoulder. "Nobody assesses over my stew."

* * *

I meant to refuse. Let me be clear about that too, since the record matters: I had a rule, learned in the orphanage dining hall and confirmed in every house I'd worked since. Never eat with them. Eat alone, in your room, off your own bread, because a shared table is where they start asking questions and questions are where shadows get found out. I had turned down suppers from grander households than this one. I had a whole liturgy of refusals, polished smooth with use.

But Vey set the bowl down in front of me without asking whether I wanted it, and the smell hit me — barley and onion and something smoked — and my body committed treason against my rules before my mind could convene the committee. I told myself I'd take one spoonful, to be civil, and leave.

The kitchen was low-ceilinged and warm, nothing like the marble corridors of the houses I usually worked. There was a scarred pine table and mismatched chairs and a shelf of chipped crockery that had clearly been chosen for holding capacity rather than appearance. Vey ate across from me with the concentration of a man who regarded meals as maintenance. Neither of us spoke for a while, and it was, strangely, comfortable. Silence with strangers is usually a negotiation. This was just silence.

Then the door opened and a girl came in carrying a stack of star charts, and I understood immediately that she was not what I expected, mostly because I hadn't expected her at all.

She was perhaps my age or a little older, dark-haired, dressed in a man's old coat cut down badly at the shoulders, and she moved through the kitchen the way I moved through other people's houses — silently, watchfully, taking the layout in. She set the charts on the dresser, saw me, and stopped.

"Ingrid," Vey said. "This is the clock girl. Mara Dunmore."

Ingrid looked at me. Then she looked at my mouth — directly, deliberately, the way nobody looks at anything — and said, "Say that again? Slower."

I said it slower.

Her eyes stayed on my lips the whole time, tracking, and then she nodded once, satisfied, and pulled out a chair and sat down across from me, close, closer than a stranger sits, and began signing at Vey with quick fluid hands. He signed back, halfheartedly, mouth full.

"She reads lips," he explained to me, when Ingrid paused for breath. "Has done since she was six. Reads them through glass, across rooms, upside down in reflections. Don't bother whispering in this house; it insults her."

"I heard that," Ingrid said, which startled me until I realized she'd been watching my face while I looked at him, reading the shape of my reaction off my mouth. She grinned. She had a quick, crooked grin that rearranged her whole face. "You're wondering if I'm going to stare at your mouth all supper. Yes. Get used to it. Everyone does."

"I don't mind," I said, and found that it was true, and found that the truth of it unsettled me more than a lie would have.

"The clock," she said, leaning forward. "Is it true you're going to fix it? Papa says three firms ran away. He says it like it's funny. It isn't funny. That clock used to sing the whole hill to sleep."

"Sing."

"You'll hear it. Or—" she caught herself, and something flickered across her face, fast, gone. "You'll feel it. The whole observatory hums when it runs. My bed used to buzz at the quarters. I haven't slept properly in eleven years and I didn't know why until last month when I worked it out." She said it lightly, but her hands, I noticed, had gone still in her lap.

"Your father is the astronomer."

"Halvorsen. Yes." She studied me again, that direct lip-reader's stare, and whatever she was looking for she apparently found, or didn't, because she shrugged and started signing at Vey again, faster now, and this time he put his spoon down and actually attended.

I caught maybe one sign in ten. But I caught the shape of it: her hands made a motion like a pendulum swinging, then stopped dead, chopped flat. Then a word repeated twice, sharp, both times with her chin lifted. Vey's face did something complicated. He signed back one-handed, dismissive, and she slapped the table — not hard, but precisely, the way you tap a glass to make it ring — and pointed at him, and he subsided.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"Nothing," Vey said.

"She said the clock stopped at 4:17 and that you know why, and she said it twice," I said. "I don't need the signs. Her face does most of the talking."

Both of them stared at me. Ingrid recovered first, and grinned that crooked grin, and pointed at me with real approval, and said something to Vey in signs that made him go faintly pink.

* * *

The astronomer came down at eight exactly, and I knew it was eight exactly because Vey checked his pocket watch and muttered "there he goes" with the resignation of long habit.

Dr. Halvorsen was not what the word *astronomer* had built in my head. I'd imagined someone crisp and remote, a man shaped like his instruments. What came down the stairs was a tall, stooped figure in a cardigan with one elbow darned a different color, white hair standing up as though he'd recently been electrocuted, spectacles pushed up on his forehead and a second pair already on his nose. He was carrying a mug of tea he appeared to have forgotten about, because he set it down on top of a stack of books and then, a minute later, looked around for it with genuine bewilderment.

"Papa," Ingrid said, "your tea."

"So it is." He retrieved it, blinked at me over the rims of both spectacles, and said, "Ah. The clock person."

"Mara Dunmore, sir. Apprentice to Master Ellery, of Ellery and Sons."

"Ellery." He pronounced it the way other men pronounce the names of old friends they've quarreled with. "He re-escaped the trainee escapement problem in '79, did he? Tell him the answer was in the recoil all along and he owes me a letter." He lowered himself into a chair, drank his tea, and looked at me with an expression of sudden, piercing attention that was completely at odds with everything else about him. "You've seen the clock."

"This afternoon."

"And?"

And I gave him the honest answer, because lying to a man with that much attention aimed at you seemed physically impossible. "I haven't opened the case yet. But whoever stopped it didn't break it. The pendulum's hanging plumb. The weights are seated. Whatever killed it, it died clean, or it was stopped clean, and those are different things."

The kitchen went very quiet. Ingrid had stopped eating. Vey was looking at the table.

"Stopped clean," Halvorsen repeated softly. "Yes. That's the phrase. That's exactly the phrase." He took his spectacles off, rubbed his eyes, put them back on. "Miss Dunmore. Three firms have stood in that chamber and told me the mainspring failed, the escapement wore out, the pivot galled — mechanical deaths, natural deaths, deaths with paperwork. You're the first person to look at that pendulum and tell me it was murdered."

"I didn't say murdered."

"No," he agreed. "You said *stopped*. Which is worse." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, but underneath it was something that surprised me: relief. Actual relief, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Fix it. Take as long as you need. Charge what's fair and add a third, and if anyone asks you what you found in there, you come and tell *me* first. Not the patrons. Not the parish constable. Me."

"Sir, I only repair—"

"Yes," he said, standing, gathering his forgotten books, already halfway back into whatever sky lived behind his eyes. "That's what I'm paying you for. To only repair." And he went back up the stairs, mug abandoned on the book stack again, leaving the kitchen ringing with a conversation I couldn't account for.

* * *

I washed my bowl. It was a small domestic act and I did it partly to have something to do with my hands and partly because in every house I'd ever worked, making yourself useful was armor. Invisible hands can't be dismissed.

Ingrid dried. She stood beside me at the basin, hip to hip, passing dishes between us without either of us arranging it, and after the third bowl she said, without preamble:

"They'll feed you tomorrow too. And the day after. Tomas cooks like it's a religious duty and Papa forgets to eat unless there's a witness. You should know that before you get attached."

"I'm not getting attached. I'm repairing a clock."

"Mm." She stacked a plate with unnecessary precision. "That's what the last firm said. Well — they said 'assessing the mechanism.' They left in nine days. One of them cried in the carriage."

"Why?"

She was quiet for a moment. Through the kitchen window the dome of the observatory caught the last light, white against a bruising sky, and beyond it the harbor lamps were coming on one by one, little orange points strung along the black water.

"Because it's a house of strays," she said finally. "Papa inherited it and can't run it. Tomas was thrown out of the city archive for something nobody will name and he won't defend himself against. I'm—" she waved a hand at herself, at her ears, briskly, the way you'd wave at weather. "And now there's you, who looks at a warm kitchen like it's a trap." She glanced sideways at me, and her eyes were quick and unembarrassed. "I read faces better than lips, if you want the truth. Lips are just where faces keep their loudest words. Yours said *don't ask me to stay* the entire supper, and your hands said *please let me wash one more bowl.*"

I had no answer to that. I had spent seventeen years constructing a face that said nothing, and this girl had walked through it in an evening like it was fog.

"I'll take the room over the clock chamber," I said, which was not an answer, and was therefore the best one available. "If it's offered. Early starts."

"It's offered. It's been offered since Tuesday's letter went out. Tomas airs it every morning." She handed me the last dish, dry, warm from the cloth. "Nobody here will ask you questions you don't want to answer, Mara Dunmore. That's the house rule. We're all hiding from something; it'd be poor manners to compare notes."

* * *

The room was small and clean and smelled of cedar, and the bed was narrower than my shoulders and more comfortable than anything I'd slept in since the orphanage dormitory, which is not a high bar but is a bar. I unpacked my tools onto the sill — loupe, drivers, pegwood, the little brass oiler Ellery had given me when I aged out — and lay down and listened to the house settle.

Through the floor, faint but unmistakable, came the tick of smaller clocks. A mantel clock somewhere below, a carriage clock, something with a bell. The observatory was full of timekeeping, all of it running, all of it honest, except the great one at the heart of it, struck dead at 4:17 eleven years before I ever climbed the hill.

Stopped clean, I'd said. I turned the phrase over in the dark. A spring fails messy — it lets go, it tears, it leaves wreckage. A pivot wears messy — grit, scoring, a slow staggering death. But a clock that stops with its pendulum plumb and its weights seated is a clock that was *put* to rest, gently, by hands that knew how. Someone had stopped that clock the way you'd close a dead friend's eyes.

And three firms had looked at it and lied to the old man's face, and taken their fees, and gone.

I lay there cataloguing myself, the way I always did at the end of a day, keeping the inventory current: Mara Dunmore, seventeen, apprentice, fed stew she didn't ask for, given a room that had been aired for her before she arrived, warned by a deaf girl that the household would not ask questions. Every item on the list was wrong. The list was supposed to say *invisible, temporary, safe.*

There was a knock — two knuckles, brief — and Vey's voice through the door, low.

"Miss Dunmore. Tomorrow, when you open the case. Open it slow. And if you find anything in there that isn't clockwork—" a pause, long enough that I thought he'd gone, "—you send Ingrid for me first. Before you touch it. Promise me that."

I stared at the ceiling. Somewhere below, the mantel clock ticked toward midnight, honest and small.

"Mr. Vey," I said, "what's in the clock?"

Another pause. Longer.

"That," he said, "is the question this house has been sitting on for eleven years. Sleep well."

His footsteps went down the corridor. I lay in the cedar-smelling dark with my heart going harder than it had any reason to, and told myself the fear was just the strangeness, just the stew and the welcome and the girl who read faces, just a job like any other job.

Tomorrow I would open the case, and I would find a broken escapement, and I would fix it, and I would leave.

I fell asleep rehearsing it, the way you rehearse a lie you intend to believe.
