## 3. What the Case Held

Morning came up the tower stairs sideways, in slices of grey light through the slit windows, and I was awake before it finished arriving. I had slept badly and deeply at once, the way you sleep in strange houses—body exhausted, mind standing watch. By half past six I had dressed, eaten nothing, and talked myself into a mood that could open a clock case.

The tools went into my roll the same way every morning of my life: loupe, drivers, tweezers, the brass-handled pliers Mr. Ferrant had left me in his will along with the debt and the shop key. The oiler. The pegwood. A stub of wax for marking. I checked the roll twice, which was not about the tools.

Then I climbed.

* * *

The clock room held its cold like a grudge. The regulator stood where I'd left it, tall and patient and dead, its hands still pinned at 4:17 by eleven years of nobody's business. Morning light came through the east window and lay across the case in a long pale bar, so that the dust on it looked almost deliberate, like something applied by a craftsman.

I set my roll down on the cloth I'd spread the day before and stood in front of the thing with my arms crossed like a woman negotiating.

"Right," I said to it. "Let's not make this difficult."

Clocks don't answer, which is most of why I like them.

The case came apart the way good old cases do, honestly, if you know where the joints are hiding. Two locking wedges under the crown moulding, one hidden catch behind the pendulum door that someone had worn smooth with use—worn smooth meant opened often, once upon a time, and I filed that away without deciding what it meant. The hood lifted off its pins and I carried it to the window ledge, walking carefully, the way you carry anything that has outlived everyone who made it.

Inside, the movement sat in its cradle like a heart in a chest.

I've heard men describe movements as beautiful, and they were always the men who'd never been hungry, but there is something in a fine regulator that stops even my mouth. This one was three-train, weight-driven, with a deadbeat escapement and a mercury compensating pendulum hung on a rod gone nearly black with age. Brass everywhere, but brass that had been loved—the plates polished at their edges by hands checking, adjusting, checking again, year after year, until the habit itself became a finish. Somebody had wound this thing faithfully through years I couldn't imagine, and then one day hadn't.

I put my loupe in my eye and leaned in.

The escapement was jammed, as promised—or rather, stopped, because when I got the light right I saw there was no breakage at all. The pallets sat against the wheel in a position no running clock would ever find them in, and the maintaining power had been let down, deliberately, all three weights run down and the great barrel braked with a wooden wedge that was still seated firm. Nobody had neglected this clock. Somebody had retired it. There's a difference, and the difference is intention, and intention is the part of any machine that doesn't show until you go looking.

"Who wedges a barrel?" I asked the room. The room kept its opinions to itself.

It didn't matter, yet. First I needed the movement out, or near enough to work, and for that I needed the seat board free and the cradle bolts back, and for that I needed my hands inside the case up to the wrist, feeling along the frame rails for the bolt heads in the dark where eyes won't fit.

That is how I found the letter.

My fingers knew it before my mind did—a wrongness in the geography, an edge where there should have been flat metal. Wood and then paper. Paper pressed flat and waxed over, sealed down against the inside of the back frame rail where no hand would go unless it was a hand like mine, small, patient, reaching blind into the dark for a bolt head that wasn't where the design said it should be.

I drew my hand out slowly. Then I took the lamp off its hook and held it inside the case, angling it up along the back rail.

There it was. An envelope, ordinary cheap rag paper by the look of it, laid flat against the wood and sealed down at all four corners with sealing wax gone the deep brownish red that old wax goes, the color of a dried wound. It had been put there by somebody who wanted it found only by somebody who had earned their way inside—whoever keeps the time, the address said, in a narrow slanted hand, ink faded to the brown of old tea:

*To whoever keeps the time.*

Not *to whom it may concern.* Not *to the authorities.* Whoever keeps the time. Which was, as of four days ago, me.

* * *

I did what any sensible person does with a mystery sealed into the guts of a stranger's clock.

I worked around it.

For two hours I worked around it. I freed the cradle bolts, all present, all sound. I eased the wedge from the barrel and inspected the great wheel tooth by tooth under the loupe, finding nothing worse than thickened oil gone to varnish. I stripped the escapement, cleaned the pallets, found the pivot on the third wheel scored where grit had ridden it and burnished the score smooth with tin and tripoli until my shoulder ached. I did honest, careful, billable work within six inches of an envelope addressed to me, and if you think that shows restraint, you have never spent your childhood learning that curiosity is the fastest road to a beating.

But here is the trouble with invisible people: we see everything. It is the whole trade. You cannot keep your head down and your eyes closed both; you'd walk into walls. So while my hands burnedished pivots my eyes read that envelope the way Ingrid read faces yesterday through the glass—every line of it, the seal's impression (a compass rose? some kind of star?), the corner where the wax had cracked and lifted a hair's width, the way the paper beneath had yellowed except where the wax shielded it, leaving a pale cross-shaped ghost. Eleven years of dust had settled over everything in that case evenly. Over the letter, evenly too. Whatever it was, it had been in there since before the dust, since before the stopping.

Since 4:17, some eleven years ago.

I set down the burnisher and wiped my hands on my apron and stood there hating myself for what I was about to do, and knowing I would do it, and knowing the exact moment I had decided—yesterday, probably, or ten years ago, in an orphanage dining hall, the first time I understood that being unseen was only shelter if there was nothing worth seeing.

Mr. Vey's question came back to me. *What's in the clock?*

"That," I told the empty room, "is none of my business."

The room, notably, disagreed.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I ran, standing in that cold with my hands hanging useless at my sides. Running it cost me twenty minutes and I want credit for every one.

Item: the letter was not mine. Nothing in this house was mine; I was hired hands, paid by the day, expected to fix a mechanism and be forgotten by supper. Item: opening correspondence addressed to another person was theft, or near enough that a magistrate wouldn't split hairs, and girls from St. Agnes's Home for Foundlings did not survive magistrates; we survived by being exactly as interesting as furniture. Item: whatever this letter was, it had frightened somebody once—it had been hidden, and things are hidden by the afraid—and fear in a house like this wore good coats and had lawyers. Item, item, item.

And then the last item, the one I kept trying to strike from the ledger: *whoever keeps the time.*

Eleven years this envelope had waited in the dark, waxed to the bones of a dead clock, waiting for precisely one kind of person. Not the astronomer, who owned the clock and surely must have known—or must he? Not the patrons who came to gaze at it. Not the servants, if there had ever been servants, who would no more reach inside the movement than inside the communion cup. One kind of person only: someone who opened cases. Someone whose work it was to go where the design says no hand goes. Someone who kept the time.

Someone like the person who had sealed it there in the first place, come to think of it. Clockmaker's wax, that brown-red. I'd handled its like in Mr. Ferrant's scrap box a thousand times.

So it was a message passed between keepers. Left by one and found by the next, across however many years, the way you'd pass a tool down a line of hands. And I was next in the line whether I consented to be or not, because I had climbed these stairs and put my hands in the works and that made me the keeper now, and keepers do not leave things lying in the mechanism. Any clockmaker will tell you: foreign object in the train, you remove it or you note it, but you do not pretend you didn't see it. Pretending is how clocks stop. Pretending, I thought, looking at the hands frozen at 4:17, is maybe how everything else stops too.

I ran out of arithmetic. That's the truth of it. I didn't decide; I just reached the end of my reasons and my hand was already moving.

* * *

The wax came away in flakes under my pegwood knife, brittle as biscuit, raining little rust-colored crumbs onto the frame rail and the cloth below. I worked corner to corner, slow, breathing through my mouth, absurdly careful of the paper as though it could feel. Three corners freed. At the fourth the wax held on stubbornly and I had to slide the knife flat beneath and lift, and the whole envelope came up in one piece with a faint sucking sound, like a page turning in a book nobody wrote yet.

It was heavier than an envelope should be. Something folded inside it besides paper.

I turned it over in the lamplight.

*To whoever keeps the time,* in that narrow slanted hand. And below the direction, smaller, pressed hard enough that the pen had nearly cut the paper, three more words I had to hold close to the lamp to read:

*Read it aloud.*

Below that, no signature. Only the seal mark again, clear where the wax had pooled—a compass rose, I decided now, sixteen points, and at its center not the usual fleur or initial but something small and square that might have been a house or a chart case or a book.

I stood there holding eleven years in one hand and my livelihood in a room around me, and I did not open it.

Understand: I wanted to. My thumbs were already at the flap. But a thought had arrived with the weight of it in my palm, a thought shaped exactly like Mr. Vey's face last night when he asked his question, and like Halvorsen's pause before answering me yesterday—*a matter for Miss Dunmore alone*, he'd said, and I had taken that for courtesy. Had I? Or had the whole household been waiting, all this time, each of them, for the clock to be opened, and unable to do it themselves, unwilling, forbidden—and had they hired me, a girl with no name worth saying, precisely because I could touch what they could not?

If I opened this alone, whatever it was, it would be mine alone. And if it was what the weight suggested—not a note, a dossier, a charge, a case—then the moment I broke that seal, invisibility stopped being a shelter and started being a liability. You cannot disappear once someone has watched you carry a thing. I knew that the way I knew escapements. It was the first law of the life I'd built.

So I did the bravest cowardly thing available, which was to fold the letter into my apron pocket unopened, brush the wax flakes into my palm and drop them in after it—evidence of tampering, I supposed grimly, or just tidiness—and turn back to the movement with my heart going like a detached balance wheel.

I gave the clock another hour. Honest hours, measured ones. Cleaned the varnish from the pallet faces, dressed the click spring, tested the winding square. The mechanism was sound underneath it all, sound and fine, a prisoner rather than a patient, and it wanted only permission to run.

At half past ten I climbed down, washed at the scullery pump, and went looking for the household.

They were easy to find. They were all in the breakfast room pretending not to have been waiting for me—Halvorsen behind a newspaper he wasn't reading past the second column, Ingrid drawing at the table with her back straight as a plumb line, Tomas Vey buttering toast with the concentration of a man defusing something.

I stood in the doorway with the letter's corners pressing through my apron pocket against my hip, and I heard myself say, in the flattest voice I own:

"There's something waxed to the inside of the clock case."

The newspaper did not go down. It stopped rustling, which was somehow louder. Ingrid's pencil stopped mid-stroke, and she looked not at me but at her father, quick as a bird. Tomas set down the knife with exaggerated care, as if it might go off.

"It's addressed," I said, "to whoever keeps the time."

Silence, of the kind that has mass.

Then Halvorsen folded his newspaper with terrible precision and looked at me, and his face was doing something complicated, grief and relief fighting over the same ground, and he said:

"And did you read it?"

"No." The word came out harder than I meant. "I brought it to you."

Ingrid was watching my mouth the whole time, and her eyes went from my face to my pocket and back, and I understood with a lurch that she had known before I spoke—that she'd seen it in how I stood in the doorway, probably, in the set of my shoulders, the way other people hear it in a voice.

Tomas pushed back his chair. He looked older than he had at supper, and younger too, somehow, the way men look when something they've been carrying sets them down at last.

"Sit down, Miss Dunmore," he said. "Please. Before you show us—sit. Because what's in that envelope decides what happens to this house, and I'd rather you heard it sitting down."

"I don't want to hear anything," I said, and heard the tremor under the words and hated it. "I fix clocks. I want the escapement parts I ordered, and my day rate, and the door."

"Yes," Tomas said gently. "That's what everyone in this house has wanted, for eleven years. Sit down."

I sat. The letter shifted in my pocket, and I took it out and laid it on the table between the toast plate and Ingrid's drawings, and four faces—three watching, one reading—turned toward it the way iron filings turn.

Halvorsen reached out and stopped, his hand an inch above the paper, trembling slightly.

"You first, Tomas," he said hoarsely. "You knew her. I can't—" He withdrew his hand. "I find I can't."

Tomas picked up the envelope and turned it to the light, and I watched him recognize the seal—I mean truly recognize it, the way you recognize a face across thirty years of street crowds, whole body flinching—and he said, in a voice scraped down to the grain:

"Oh, Lise. You did hide it in the clock."

"Who," I said, "is Lise?"

Tomas set the envelope down and looked at me with something like apology, already reaching for the wax-cracked flap.

"Lise Dunmore," he said, "was the finest cartographer this city ever failed to deserve. And you, Miss Dunmore, have just carried her letter across a room."

He broke the seal.
