## 4. The Name in the Wax

Tomas Vey had spent thirty-one years learning to read wax the way other men read faces.

It was a useless skill for an archivist, or it should have been. Seals were meant to be broken, not studied. But in the archive of the Ministry of Records, where he had once been the youngest keeper of the cartographic collections, the seals told you who had touched a document before you ever lifted the paper. The chancery used a dull red, pressed hard, always slightly off-center because the clerks were hurried. The Admiralty used black and pressed it twice, a habit of men who trusted nothing once. The private houses of the city's great families used colors like declarations—oxblood for the Vosses, deep green for the Merricks, a gold-flecked crimson that no one else dared imitate for the Halvorsen patrons, the Consortium of the Northern Approach.

The wax on the letter Mara Dunmore had carried down from the clock case was none of these.

It was a gray-violet, almost the color of a bruise healing, and it had been pressed with a seal no larger than a thumbnail. Tomas turned the letter in the lamplight of his cramped room beneath the observatory's east stair, and his hands, which had not trembled in years, began to tremble now.

The seal showed a compass rose. Not the ornate compass roses of the chart houses, with their fleur-de-lis pointers and their ribboned cardinal letters. This one was plain, almost severe: an eight-pointed star, and in the center, where a lesser engraver would have put a monogram, a single letter *L*, cut so finely it might have been mistaken for a flaw.

He knew it. God help him, he knew it. He had catalogued forty-one documents bearing that seal in the spring of 1881, and he had watched the last of them come into the collection the way you watch a bird fly into a room with the windows shut.

"Lise Dunmore," he said aloud, to no one, because the room was his alone and he had earned the right to say names to empty air.

The letter was addressed, in a hand he also knew—quick, slanting, the descenders bitten short—to *whoever keeps the time.* Mara had read that much to them at supper, her voice flat, her eyes moving from face to face as if checking each of them for a trap. Halvorsen had gone gray. Ingrid had gone still in the particular way she went still when she had understood something the rest of them were still circling. And Tomas had said, *May I look at the seal?* in the voice of a man asking to hold a snake.

Now he held it, and the snake was coiled in his chest where his conscience used to live.

He should take it to Halvorsen. That was the orderly thing, the honest thing. The letter concerned the observatory, its patrons, its dead. Halvorsen was the master of the house, whatever the house had become. But Tomas sat with the letter in his lap for a long time without moving, because he knew what was written inside it—not the words, he had not broken the seal and would not, not yet, not without her—but the shape of what was written. He had read its shape before, in 1881, in a logbook that no longer existed entire.

He had burned it. That was the sentence his mind kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. *You burned it.* Twelve pages of Lise Dunmore's coastal survey log, and he had fed them into the stove in the records annex on the fourth of November, 1881, while a man from the Consortium stood behind him with his hat still on.

* * *

Tomas had been thirty then, and hungry in the way that clever young men in unimportant positions are hungry—not for bread, but for consequence. He had been the one who first flagged the anomaly, and he had been proud of it. A set of coastal charts, soundings and headlands and the hidden depths of the northern approaches, the work of the surveyor Lise Dunmore, commissioned by this city's own Geographical Institute—those same charts had surfaced in the hydrographic archive of a foreign navy. A clerk's error, the Institute said. A copying accident. Tomas had known better. The charts in the foreign archive were not copies. They were originals, annotated in Dunmore's own hand, with corrections dated after the Institute's official delivery of the fair copies. Someone had given her the working originals to a foreign naval power, and the working originals were the more valuable by far, because they held her marginalia: where the channels shifted, where a hull could pass that no chart showed, where the harbor guns could not reach.

He had written his memorandum carefully. He had named no one, because he did not yet know whom to name, but he had laid out the dates and the discrepancies like a prosecutor laying out a knife. He had brought it to his superior, Keeper Aldous Crane—young then too, already rising, already the Consortium's favorite son in the ministry—and he had expected, God forgive him, a commendation.

What he had gotten was a lesson.

Crane had read the memorandum twice, unhurried, and then set it down and looked at Tomas with an expression of genuine, almost paternal disappointment. "Do you know what the Institute's budget is, Vey? Do you know who votes it?" And then, when Tomas had begun to stammer something about the public trust: "The public trust. Yes. Sit down."

He could reconstruct the rest of that conversation from memory the way he could reconstruct a cataloguing error from a shelf list. Crane had not threatened him. Crane had done something far more effective: he had made Tomas complicit in the shape of the silence before asking him to keep it. He had shown him the Consortium's subscription rolls—the observatory's founding patrons, the men whose money had built the great dome and the great clock and the careers of everyone within a mile of the hill—and he had shown him that three of the Institute's five governors were also Consortium men, and he had said, mildly, "Miss Dunmore's contract was renewed in March. Her funding continues through her good standing with these gentlemen. If her work were found to have been compromised—if her *loyalty* were in question—well. A surveyor without funding is a woman with a boat and a pencil." A pause. "And a great many people have invested in her pencil."

"You're saying she sold the charts," Tomas had said. "You're saying *she's* the leak."

"I'm saying nothing. I'm saying that a memorandum like yours, in the wrong hands, would *become* that. And that Miss Dunmore, who has no family, no patrons of her own, and a habit of writing to newspapers about the underfunding of coastal survey, is not a woman whose interests align with yours." Crane had smiled then, and it had been the smile of a man who has already won and is being kind about it. "Burn the memorandum, Vey. Not for me. For her. Think of it as protection."

And Tomas—thirty years old, hungry for consequence, and cowardly in the ordinary way, the way most men are cowardly, one small reasonable surrender at a time—had burned the memorandum.

That should have been the end of it. That was the bargain he had made with himself: one memorandum, one fire, one small cowardice, and then a long honest life of shelving other people's papers.

But in October, Lise Dunmore had come to the archive herself.

* * *

He remembered the rain that day, and the way she had stood dripping in the annex doorway with her oilskin beaded gray and her hair plastered to her cheek, and how she had not wiped any of it away, as if she had forgotten she was wet. She had been thirty-four. She had laughed easily, people said, though she did not laugh that day. She had asked to see her own file—the deposit receipts, the delivery records for the fair copies—and Tomas, who had burned his memorandum and told himself the matter was closed, had shown her, because refusing would have required an excuse and he was not yet a good enough liar to build one on his feet.

She had read the receipts standing up, fast, the way she read charts. Then she had said, "The second fair copy was never delivered. The receipt is dated the ninth of March, but the ninth of March I was at sea. I signed nothing on the ninth of March." She had looked up at him. "Who countersigned?"

And Tomas, who had the shelf list in his head, had said the name before he could stop himself. A Consortium man. One of the three.

She had gone very quiet. Then she had thanked him—thanked *him*—and said, "You've just done more for the northern coast than the Institute has in ten years," and left, and Tomas had sat in the annex with his heart hammering and told himself he had said nothing that mattered, that she would do nothing, that a woman alone with a boat and a pencil would do what sensible people did, which was nothing.

Three weeks later she was gone. Her boat was found hull-up off the shoals past the light. The verdict was misadventure. The tide, the weather, a lone woman's error. The Consortium's men attended the memorial in force and spoke movingly of her dedication, and the Geographical Institute retired her survey with honors, and the northern approaches were charted, at last, to everyone's satisfaction.

And in November, Aldous Crane had come down to the annex with a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth and set it on Tomas's desk, and said, "Her effects. The family—" there was no family; Crane had let the word hang and moved on— "the Institute wishes the personal papers disposed of. Irregular material. You understand."

It was her logbook. The working log of the final survey season. Tomas had opened it with the oilcloth still on his desk and read, in that quick bitten hand, entries that were not soundings. Entries about the delivery that never happened. Entries about a meeting with a Consortium man on a fog-bound pier. Entries about being followed. And on the last written page, dated nine days before her boat was found: *If anything happens to me, the charts speak for me. I have left the truth where the truth is kept. Whoever keeps the time will keep it.*

He had not understood that last line then. He had understood it eleven years later, standing in the observatory's clock room while an orphan girl with ink-stained fingers pried a letter off the inside of the great regulator's case.

But in November of 1881, Tomas Vey had carried the logbook to the annex stove, because Aldous Crane was standing behind him with his hat still on, and because Tomas had already learned that the distance between one small cowardice and the next was no distance at all.

Twelve pages. He had told himself he would read them all first, that he would at least bear witness. He had gotten through four before his hands began to shake too badly to hold the paper steady, and Crane had said, not unkindly, "The fire will do it faster," and Tomas had fed the rest in unburned, unread, and stood watching the bitten descenders curl and go black, and the compass rose on nothing, because the log had no seal, only her hand, which was the same thing.

He had kept one thing. He had not planned to; his hand had simply closed on the last gathering of pages—the ash-stained remainder, the final quire of the log, water-marked and scorched at one corner—when Crane had turned to speak to someone in the doorway. It had lived for eleven years in a false-backed folio box in his own quarters, catalogued in his own private index under a number that meant nothing: *Misc. 4:17.* He had chosen the number without understanding why, the way you choose a word in a dream. It was the time of the tide-table entry for the day her boat was found. It was the time, the coroner had ruled, of her drowning.

* * *

Now the letter lay in his lap, and the seal was her seal, and the address was her hand, and the number in his private index was the hour stopped in the great clock above his head, and Tomas Vey understood that he had spent eleven years standing in the wreckage of a sentence he had helped write.

He rose. His knees cracked—he was sixty-one now, and the archive had taken its toll in damp and stooping—and he climbed the east stair with the letter held away from his body, the way you carry something that might go off.

The clock room was cold. Mara Dunmore was there, as he had known she would be; she seemed to live in the clock's case the way a snail lives in its shell, half in, half out, her sleeves rolled and her fingers black with old grease. She had taken the escapement apart and laid it out on a cloth in perfect order, and even Tomas, who knew nothing of mechanisms, could see that the order was a kind of devotion.

She looked up when he came in. She had a way of looking at a person that was not shy and not bold but *accounting*—taking your measure, entering you in a ledger. He had seen it in her the first day and it had unsettled him, because it was not the look of a girl who had been taught to keep her head down. It was the look of a girl who had been taught to keep her head down and had decided, privately, to keep her eyes up instead.

"You've been sitting with it all night," she said. Not a question.

"I have."

"And?"

Tomas held the letter out to her, seal up, so the compass rose caught the lamplight. "Do you know this mark?"

She took it. She turned it over once, twice. And he watched her face do what his had done: the small collapse of recognition, the vertigo underneath it. "It was on her charts," Mara said. "The ones in the—" She stopped. She looked at him sharply. "You've seen her seal before."

"I catalogued it," Tomas said. "Forty-one times, in the spring of '81." He made himself go on, because he had decided on the stair that he would not do this the coward's way, in installments, each truth pried out of him like a tooth. "Mara. The cartographer who disappeared in 1881. The one whose charts were found in a foreign naval archive. Her name was Lise Dunmore."

The clock room was very quiet. Somewhere above them the dome ticked as the metal cooled, a sound like a patient finger tapping.

"That's not a common name," Mara said at last. Her voice was level. Her hands were not; the letter trembled against the gray wax.

"No," Tomas said. "It isn't."

She didn't ask. He watched her decide not to ask—not yet, not here—and he understood that she was doing what he had done at thirty: postponing the thing that would change the shape of her life, on the reasonable ground that it would still be there in the morning. He had learned what that ground was worth.

"There's more," he said. "And you should have all of it before that seal is broken, because once it's broken, there's no standing outside this. The letter accuses the Consortium. It accuses them of selling her charts to a foreign navy and of what happened to her after. I know because—" and here his voice did what voices do when they carry eleven years of ash— "because I was the archivist who handled her file. And because Aldous Crane, who is now the Consortium's chairman, stood behind me in November of 1881 while I burned the last of her logbook in the records annex stove."

Mara stared at him. The lamp hissed. In the case behind her, the dead clock stood at its stopped hour, patient as a witness.

"I kept one quire," Tomas said. "Scorched. Water-stained. It's in my room, and it's yours, and I will fetch it now if you tell me to. But I wanted you to hear it from me first, and I wanted to hear myself say it, because I have spent eleven years not saying it." He drew a breath that hurt. "I was a coward, Miss Dunmore. I am telling you so that you will not make the mistake of trusting the wrong part of me. Trust what I know. Verify the rest."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she looked down at the seal in her hand, at the fine-cut *L* at the center of the compass rose, and when she spoke her voice was strange and small and absolutely steady.

"Whoever keeps the time," she read. "That's what it says. That's who it's for." She looked up. "Mr. Vey. What time does the coroner's record give for my mother's drowning?"

And Tomas, who had chosen a catalogue number in a dream and lived under it for eleven years, heard himself answer with the only hour that had ever mattered.

"Four seventeen," he said. "The clock stopped at four seventeen."

Mara closed her hand around the letter—not crushing it, he noticed; protecting it—and turned to look up into the great regulator's case, at the hands frozen at the hour that had ended her mother and begun, in a way no one in that room yet understood, everything else.

"Then we'd better break the seal," she said, "and find out what she wanted kept."
