## 5. Four Seventeen

The wax cracked the way old wax does—not cleanly, but in flakes, like bark off a dead branch—and Mara found she was holding her breath and had been since before the knife touched the seal.

She set the pieces on the workbench with a care that surprised her. Two crescents of dull red, stamped with a device she didn't yet have a name for: a compass rose, or something close to it, its north point broken off. She would ask Tomas about it later. She would regret asking.

Then she unfolded the letter.

It was written in a hand that slanted hard to the right, the ink gone the brown of dried blood but still legible, the paper soft as cloth from eleven years pressed against the clock's inner frame. Whoever had written it had written fast. Mara could see it in the way the letters crowded at the ends of lines, as if the thoughts were outrunning the pen.

*To whoever keeps the time—*

Mara read it once standing up. Then her knees decided otherwise, and she read it twice more sitting on the repair stool, the lamp pulled close, while Halvorsen and Ingrid and Tomas waited in a silence so complete she could hear the pendulum's cradle ticking faintly where it hung stopped in its case.

*If you are reading this, then the clock has failed, or someone has been brave enough to open what should not be shut. Either way, you deserve to know what was done in this building and buried in it.*

*My name is Lise Dunmore. I am a cartographer by trade, and I am, I believe now, a woman whose government has decided she is worth more silent than living.*

*In the spring of 1880 I surveyed the northern approaches—the soundings, the channels, the deep-water routes the navy charts omit by policy. My patrons here, the gentlemen whose names are carved above the observatory door, commissioned the work under a civil seal. What they did with it afterward, I learned only by accident: copies of my charts went into the hands of a foreign naval attaché, sold for sums no civil survey could justify, along with letters of mine they had no right to sell because they were never theirs. If war comes to these coasts, it will arrive steering by my hand, and the men who profited will call it misfortune.*

*I have made a full record. It is hidden where only someone who understands instruments would think to look. I mean to take it to the inquiry sitting this winter. If I do not reach it—if anything prevents me—then know that whatever they say happened to me happened at their arranging, and at four seventeen in the morning, because that is the hour the tide turns at the harbor mouth and the hour they believe no honest witness is awake to mark.*

*Keep the time. Someone must.*

*—L.D.*

Mara read the last line a third time without taking it in, the way you can look at your own face in a dark window and see only the street behind you.

"Read it aloud," Tomas said quietly. "Please."

So she did. Her voice came out steadier than she expected, flat as an inventory list, which was how she'd been taught to say things that mattered: name, number, condition, no embellishment. The orphanage had trained it into her so thoroughly that even now, reading her own mother's last words, she catalogued them. *Full record. Hidden where only someone who understands instruments would think to look.* The weights cabinet. The false back behind the seconds pendulum's rating nut. She knew before she finished the sentence that she knew.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a long moment. Ingrid, who had been following on Mara's lips through the lamplight, wrote on her slate and held it up: *She sounds like you.*

"I don't sound like anyone," Mara said. "I'm nobody."

Even as she said it she heard how it landed—a coin dropped in an empty room—and Ingrid's face did the thing it did when someone said something deaf people hear perfectly: a small, precise wince, as though the words had been spoken too loudly.

Tomas took the letter from her hand with a gentleness that bordered on ceremony and carried it to the lamp. He read it twice. Then he turned it over, and his thumb stopped on the wax fragments she'd left on the bench, and he picked one up and held it to the light.

"The compass rose," he said. "With the north point struck through." He looked up at Mara, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen there before—something being dragged up from a depth. "I've seen this seal. Eleven years ago, in the provincial archive, on documents I was ordered to catalogue and never permitted to copy. Lise Dunmore's papers. They came in sealed, and they left sealed, and the manifest said *drowned cartographer, effects unrecovered.*"

"She drowned," Mara said. It came out automatically, the fact sitting where facts sat in her mind, worn smooth by handling. "At the harbor mouth. April 1881. Four seventeen in the morning."

She heard herself say the hour and felt the room tilt.

Not the room. The hour.

*Four seventeen in the morning, because that is the hour the tide turns at the harbor mouth.*

And the great regulator above them, dead for eleven years, jammed—jammed, not wound down, jammed, its escapement locked as if a hand had been put inside it and told it *stop*—stood at four seventeen.

"That's nothing," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"The clock stopped at some hour. Any hour. It stopped when someone wanted it stopped, and the hour means—" She was aware of her own voice speeding up, the inventory-flat tone cracking down the middle. "Clocks stop. It's mechanical. A lever fails, a weight runs out, you get a random time on the dial and people read meaning into it because people are always reading meaning into—"

"Mara." Halvorsen's voice, low. He had come forward from the doorway where he'd been standing, hat in his hands like a man at a graveside. "The escapement isn't broken. I've had three horologists look at it across eleven years. Whatever stopped this clock was done deliberately, from inside the case, by hands that knew what they were doing. The question was never *what hour did it happen to stop at.* The question was who chose four seventeen, and why."

*Because that is the hour they believe no honest witness is awake to mark.*

Mara stood up. Her legs worked, which seemed like an impertinence. She walked to the clock case and looked up at the dial, at the two hands frozen in their small eternal argument—one minute shy of the quarter, seven past four—and thought, with a strange cold lucidity: *I have walked past this clock every morning for a week and never once looked at the time it was telling.*

She had spent her whole life believing history happened elsewhere. That was the orphanage's deepest lesson, deeper than sewing or scripture or how to be small in a dining hall of forty girls: history was something that happened to other people, in capitals, in wars, in families that had things worth writing down. Her own history began at a foundling gate with a blanket pin and no note, and would end wherever she stopped, and between those points the world's great events would pass overhead like weather. She had arranged herself around that belief the way a plant arranges itself around a wall. It was shelter. It was also, she understood now, standing under the dead clock with her mother's handwriting still warm in the lamp light—it had also been a kind of blindness, and the blindness had been assigned to her.

"You said drowned at four seventeen," Tomas said carefully. He had come to stand beside her, an old man's careful distance, as though approaching a bird. "How do you know the hour? Drowning times aren't generally—"

"They are when they're in the file." She kept her eyes on the dial. "I bought my file when I turned eighteen. Cost me two months' wages. Orphanage records, coroner's extract, everything they had. Mother: Lise Dunmore, cartographer, lost by drowning at the harbor mouth, body recovered, time of death recorded as approximately four seventeen in the morning, witness statement attached." She paused. "There wasn't a witness statement attached. There was a blank page with a heading on it. I remember thinking that was strange, and then I remember thinking, well, clerks are careless, and I put it away, because putting things away is what I'm good at."

The room held very still around her. She could feel Ingrid watching her profile, reading her mouth sideways, missing nothing.

"So either," Mara said, and her voice was almost steady, "it is an astonishing coincidence that the clock my patrons asked me to repair was sabotaged to the exact minute my mother died—or it is not a coincidence at all, and I have been hired by the people who killed her to fix the thing they used to bury the evidence, because I was cheap, available, and famously incurious."

"Mara—" Halvorsen started.

"Don't." She raised a hand, not looking at him. "Don't tell me it isn't certain. I know it isn't certain. Certainty is for inquiries." The word came out before she meant it to—*inquiries*, already, as though the letter had put the word in her mouth—and she heard Tomas make a small sound, almost a laugh, almost a prayer.

Here was the vertigo, and she gripped the edge of the workbench against it. It wasn't grief, quite; grief required a shape and hers wouldn't hold one yet. It was more like standing on a high gallery and feeling the floor continue when your eyes insisted it shouldn't. All her life she had been the girl at the gate, the blank in the record, the person history had declined to happen to. And now a dead clock and a dead woman were telling her that history had happened *through* her—that she had been standing inside it all along, the way you stand inside weather, the way you stand inside time, and had mistaken the standing for absence. Her mother had hidden a record where only someone who understood instruments would look. Mara had been taught instruments before she was taught prayers. Somebody had counted on that. Somebody dead eleven years had reached through the case of a clock and chosen her hands.

Or nobody had chosen anything, and she was an orphan inventing a mother out of wax and coincidence, because orphans do that, because it is the oldest temptation there is.

Both thoughts lived in her at once, and she let them. That was the training too, the real training nobody had intended to give her: keep your head down, keep both possibilities in view, commit to nothing until the mechanism tells you the truth. Mechanisms don't lie. People do. Wax lies. Files lie. But a mainspring either has force or it doesn't; an escapement either releases or it locks; and somewhere inside this clock was the reason it had been made to die at the exact minute a cartographer drowned, and reasons are physical things, findable, touchable, fixable.

"Show me the file," Tomas said. "The coroner's extract. Word for word, if you have it."

"I don't have it with me. But I have most of it here." She tapped her temple. "I memorized it at eighteen. It was the only document in the world with my name in it. You memorize things like that."

"Word for word," Tomas repeated, and got out his notebook, and the small absurd domesticity of it—an archivist taking dictation from a memory, by lamplight, in the belly of a stopped clock—loosened something in her chest that had been tightening all evening.

She recited it. *Lise Dunmore, cartographer, age thirty-four. Recovered at the harbor steps. Cause: drowning. Time: approximately four-seventeen ante meridian. Witness statement:* and the blank, the headed blank, and Tomas's pen stopped when she said that, and he wrote *headed blank* in the margin and underlined it twice.

"Coroners don't leave headed blanks," he said, mostly to himself. "A headed blank means a statement existed, was removed, and someone wanted the removal visible—to whom it might concern. That's not carelessness. That's a message in an envelope nobody dared mail." He closed the notebook. His hands weren't quite steady. "Miss Dunmore. Mara. I have something to tell you, and it can't wait for a better hour, because I no longer believe better hours exist. It concerns 1881, and what was in that archive, and what I did with it. And you are going to want to hate me, and you should have the chance properly fed and seated when you take it."

"Later," Mara said.

"It shouldn't—"

"*Later.*" Not loud. Just final, the way a detent is final. Because if Tomas Vey confessed now, whatever he had done would become the largest thing in the room, and Mara needed—for one more hour, one more night—the largest thing in the room to be the letter, and the hour, and the possibility she was not ready to either claim or refuse. Half-denied. She knew that's what she was doing. She let herself do it anyway. There is a mercy in postponement, and she took it the way she'd taken suppers unasked: awkwardly, gratefully, waiting for the bill.

Ingrid tugged her sleeve. Held up the slate. She had erased *she sounds like you* and written something else, in her round deliberate hand:

*4:17 + 11 years = now. The tide turns again tonight at 4:17. Papa says so.*

Mara looked at the slate a long time.

Eleven years. A full cycle and one day over, the harbor tide returning to its same turning, the hour coming round again like a hand completing its circuit—and arriving, of all the nights in the year, tonight, the night the letter was opened, the night an apprentice clockmaker stood under a stopped dial and finally read the time.

"Then we have until four seventeen tomorrow morning," Mara said, "before the hour comes back around. And I intend to be awake for it this time."

She climbed the ladder into the clock case before anyone could answer, because her hands needed work and her heart needed a task, and there was a sabotaged escapement up there with a story locked in it. The lantern threw her shadow huge against the case's inner wall. Somewhere below, she heard Tomas say quietly to Halvorsen, "Tomorrow is Thursday," and Halvorsen answer, "Yes. The patrons come Thursday. Crane comes Thursday."

Mara's wrench stopped against the first retaining screw.

She had seen Aldous Crane's name once before—engraved small on a brass plaque inside this very case, among the list of observatory patrons, second from the top.

Her mother's letter named no names. It didn't need to. The names were carved above the door, and screwed to the frame, and coming Thursday, in broad daylight, to stand beside the clock that had kept their secret for eleven years.

She set the wrench-head into the screw slot, and pressed, and began.
