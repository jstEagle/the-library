## 6. The Patrons' Visit

Erik Halvorsen had dressed for the ceremony the way he imagined a man dresses for his own funeral: correctly, and without hope of enjoying it.

The black coat still fit, which surprised him. He'd worn it last for Ingrid's presentation at the Philosophical Society, four years ago, when she'd stood at the lectern with her charts of variable stars and let the room discover, slowly and to its own astonishment, that silence could be eloquent. He had been proud that day in a way that hurt. Today he felt only the coat's weight, and beneath it the older weight of what was hidden forty feet below him in the dome's shadow, ticking toward whatever it meant to tell them.

The girl—Dunmore, Mara Dunmore, a name he kept almost saying aloud—had been at the clock since dawn. She'd come down once for bread and gone back up without a word, her sleeves rolled, her fingers gray with brass polish. Tomas had spent the morning in the archive with the door locked, which was either prudent or suspicious, and Halvorsen had decided not to know which.

He stood now at the observatory's entrance hall, adjusting the knot of a tie that seemed to have opinions of its own, and watched the carriages come up the hill road one behind another like beads on an abacus. Six of them. The patrons came in threes this year; there was some arithmetic of prestige involved that he had never bothered to learn.

*You will smile,* he told himself. *You will pour the sherry. You will say the observatory is thriving. You are very good at saying things that are true.*

That last thought arrived with a sour taste attached, because the observatory was not thriving, and because the letter sewn into the clock's case had made liars of them all before they'd ever agreed to be.

* * *

Aldous Crane came through the doors first, as he always did, as though buildings were merely rooms waiting for him to enter them. He was sixty and carried it like a promotion: silver at the temples arranged rather than grown, a waistcoat the color of good claret, hands soft from decades of signing things. Behind him trailed Mrs. Ashworth of the Harbor Trust and young Pemberton, whose family's money had built the east wing and whose interest in astronomy extended to the word *astronomy* being carved over his own name in stone.

"Halvorsen." Crane took his hand and held it a half-second longer than warmth required. "You look well. The mountain air agrees with you."

"The air is free," Halvorsen said. "It agrees with everyone who can afford the carriage."

Crane laughed, a short bark with no floor under it. "Still sharp. I've always liked that about you. It's why we keep you."

There it was, then. The sentence that explained twelve years of polite underfunding: *we keep you.* A telescope was kept the way one kept a dog—not for its sake.

"Sherry?" Halvorsen said.

* * *

The ceremony itself was mercifully brief. There were speeches on the terrace, where the whole city lay below them in its noon haze, harbor and rooftops and the thin silver line of the river, all of it looking from this height like something worth protecting or something worth selling, depending on the man. Pemberton spoke about progress. Mrs. Ashworth spoke about the harbor. Crane spoke about legacy—the founding families, the great gift of the observatory, the debt owed to men of vision—and Halvorsen stood with his hands folded and thought about Lise Dunmore's hands, which according to Tomas had been stained blue from ink and salt from the coast wind, drawing the shoreline true while the men of vision stayed home and counted.

Then the plaque was unveiled—rededication of the great regulator, restored to service after eleven years—and everyone applauded a clock none of them had cared whether it ran.

"It's remarkable," Mrs. Ashworth said, peering into the hall where the case stood gleaming. "I remember when it stopped. 1882, wasn't it? Such a strange thing. A clock simply dying."

"Clocks don't die," Halvorsen said. "They wait."

She gave him the smile one gives an elderly relative who has said something faintly alarming, and moved on toward the sherry.

* * *

He saw it happen the way one sees weather coming over water.

The guests drifted toward the dining room in their twos and threes, and Crane did not drift. He turned instead toward the regulator clock, unhurried, and stopped before it the way a man stops before a painting he has come specifically to see. His hands went behind his back. He tilted his head. And he looked—not at the dial, not at the craftsmanship, but at the case, at the joinery, at the seams, with the particular patience of someone searching luggage.

Halvorsen crossed the hall at the pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be. Every step he took, he was aware, was the most important step he'd taken in years, and so he made it look like nothing.

"She's a beauty," Crane said, without turning. "Eleven years dead and she comes back looking new. Your repairer is gifted."

"She is. Better than gifted. Thorough."

"Thorough." Crane tasted the word. "Thoroughness can be inconvenient, in my experience. People who open things that were closed." He touched the case then, one finger, tracing the molding near the base—near, Halvorsen noted with a coldness spreading through his chest, near the place where a letter might be waxed to an inner frame. "Tell me about her. The repairer. She came from where?"

"The guild hall sent a list. She was first on it."

"The guild hall sends lists. Someone reads them." Crane turned at last, and his eyes were pleasant, and they missed nothing, which was the same thing as being dangerous. "An orphanage girl, I'm told. No family. No history. The sort of person who appears out of nowhere and starts opening cases."

"The sort of person," Halvorsen said, "who fixes clocks."

"Yes. Well." Crane smiled. "I confess I've taken an interest. An idle one. I knew the previous owner of this instrument, you might say. Its history. When it stopped, why it stopped. There are very few people left who remember, and fewer every year."

"I remember," Halvorsen said quietly.

Something moved behind Crane's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Do you," he said. Not a question.

And here it was, then. The moment Halvorsen would think about later, lying awake beside a wife eleven years dead, in a house that smelled of cold brass and chalk. Twelve years of honesty, and honesty had bought him a declining observatory, a shrinking staff, and a daughter the Philosophical Society praised in absentia because inviting her was awkward when she couldn't hear the applause. Honesty had been his religion and his albatross both, and here stood Aldous Crane offering him, in front of a clock that held a dead woman's letter, the chance to be something else.

"The clock stopped," Halvorsen said, "because the escapement failed. Escapements fail. It happens to instruments and institutions alike." He heard his own voice go smooth and easy, a voice he hadn't used since he'd retired from committee work in disgust. "We had it assessed years ago. The cost of repair exceeded the value. Sentiment kept us from selling it for parts. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," Crane echoed.

"Nothing more."

It came out clean. That was the horror of it—it came out clean, without a single stumble, the lie sitting in the air between them polished and plausible, and Halvorsen understood with something close to grief that the skill had been in him all along, dormant, like the clock. Eleven years he could have been doing this. Eleven years he'd chosen not to, and been punished for the choice by men exactly like this one.

Crane studied him for three seconds that lasted considerably longer.

"Well," Crane said finally, brightly. "Then I'm glad. Glad it runs again. A working clock in an observatory—there's a symbolism in that, wouldn't you say? Time moving forward. Institutions remembering only what serves them." He glanced at the dial. "What time does she keep? Accurate?"

"To the second. She'll be better than accurate. She'll be public." Halvorsen smiled, and found, distantly, that his face knew how. "When she strikes, the whole hill hears it. We're having her strike the hours again starting this week. There's something fitting about it—a city being able to set its watches by an honest instrument."

"A curious choice of phrase."

"I'm an astronomer. We deal in observable phenomena."

For a moment—one breath, maybe two—Halvorsen believed Crane might simply nod and move along. Then Crane leaned in slightly, close enough that the claret waistcoat filled Halvorsen's vision, and said, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather:

"Old instruments hold old histories, Erik. Sometimes it's kinder to everyone to let sleeping escapements lie. A man your age, with your... obligations—" his eyes flicked, delicately, toward the terrace where Ingrid stood talking with her hands to a patron's bored son, "—should think carefully about what thoroughness costs. Repairs. Funding. A certain restoration of standing. These things are available to institutions that know how to keep time discreetly."

"Discretion," Halvorsen said, "is a kind of stopping. I've just had the clock fixed."

Crane straightened. The pleasantness returned to his face like water filling a footprint. "Of course. Forgive me—I wax sentimental at ceremonies." He stepped back, raised his voice to its public register, and called across the hall, "Halvorsen, you must show Mrs. Ashworth the transit instrument before we go. She has ambitions for the harbor lights."

* * *

They left at four o'clock, the carriages grinding away down the hill road in reverse order, and the house exhaled. Halvorsen stood on the terrace until the last of them vanished into the haze below, and then he went inside and poured himself the sherry he hadn't drunk in company, and drank it standing up, alone, in front of the clock.

His hand, he noticed, was steady. That surprised him more than anything else that had happened today. He had expected to shake.

"You lied well," said a voice from the doorway.

Tomas Vey stood there in his rumpled coat, gray-faced, holding a catalog book against his chest like a breastplate. Behind him, halfway up the stair, Mara Dunmore had stopped too, a rag in one hand and grease to her elbow, watching him with those eyes that took everything in and offered nothing back.

"I did," Halvorsen said. "First time in thirty years. I find I haven't forgotten how, which is the most discouraging discovery I've made since my wife died."

"He knows," Tomas said. "Or he suspects enough. He was measuring the case with his eyes, Erik. Men like that don't linger over furniture."

"No," Halvorsen agreed. "They don't." He set down the glass. Above them, faintly, he could hear Ingrid's footsteps cross the gallery—she'd been at the window the entire latter half of the visit, he realized now, positioned where she could watch the terrace conversations, reading lips through glass the way other people read newspapers. She came down the stairs now two at a time and signed something rapid to her father, and Halvorsen watched her hands and understood, in a slow drop of cold water down his spine, that the afternoon had gone worse than even he had calculated.

"What did she say?" Mara asked.

Tomas had learned enough sign over the years to follow Ingrid when she slowed down, but she wasn't slowing down. Halvorsen caught only fragments—*window*, *Crane*, *the word inquiry* shaped twice, emphatically—and then Ingrid stopped, faced Mara directly, and spoke with her hands in short, deliberate strokes, as though dictating a telegram.

Mara went pale in the specific way of a person receiving news she had already been bracing for.

"He wants the letter stopped before it reaches anyone," she said flatly. "Before the parliamentary session. He told you as much upstairs, didn't he—he wrapped it in funding."

"He did," Halvorsen said. "Wrapped in courtesy, with a ribbon on it."

"Then we have less time than we thought." Mara looked up at the clock—at its face, its stopped-and-started heart—and Halvorsen saw her make some internal calculation and file it away behind those gray eyes. "How soon can the household move? If he means to act, he'll act through channels—customs, magistrates, paperwork. Things with timetables."

"Paperwork," Tomas said bitterly, "is the one weapon he never misses with."

"Then we'll need better paperwork," Halvorsen said, and heard, with a strange lightness, the lie-teller's ease already settling into him like a tool finding its old groove. "Gentlemen. Miss Dunmore. Come into the library. All of you. If Aldous Crane intends to use timetables against us, then it occurs to me that an astronomer knows rather a lot about timetables."

But Mara had already turned back to the stairs, the rag still in her fist, and she said over her shoulder words that stayed with him long after the lamps were lit:

"He'll come for the clock first. They always do. So tonight I open the case—and tomorrow, we learn exactly what we're carrying downhill."
