## 7. Ingrid Reads the Room

My father thinks I miss things.

He says it kindly, the way he says everything, his mustache moving in slow careful shapes while he looks at my face instead of his own hands, because someone taught him when I was small that I read mouths and not ears. *Ingrid sees less of the world,* he tells visitors at supper, apologetic, and then catches himself and adds, *I mean only that she hears it differently.* But what he means, underneath both sentences, is the first one. He believes the world arrives to me in fragments. He believes there is a whole country of sound where things happen that never reach me at all.

He has believed this for twenty-two years and in twenty-two years he has never once been right about anything that mattered.

Sound is not the only wire into a room. There are hands. There are shoulders. There is the angle a man's hat takes when he lies, the way a woman's thumb presses white against her teacup when she is afraid of her own husband. I learned early that people broadcast themselves constantly, shamelessly, in every part of their bodies except their voices, and their voices are the part they guard. So I got the true versions. I have been reading the true versions since before I could write my own name.

The morning Aldous Crane came back to the observatory for the second time in a week — unannounced, without ceremony, with only one attendant instead of four — I knew before he reached the door that this visit was different from the last.

I saw it from the north window of the schoolroom, where I do my charts, because the gravel drive curves past that window and no one remembers that. People remember to be careful in front of me when we are in the same room. Nobody is ever careful on the drive.

* * *

He came up the path too fast for courtesy and too slow for confidence, which is a specific walk; I had seen it once before, years ago, on a man from the bank who was about to tell my father the observatory's account had been moved under review. Crane's attendant stayed by the gate. That detail alone told me more than anything else. When a man brings an attendant to a friendly call, the attendant is furniture. When a man leaves the attendant at the gate, whatever happens next is not meant to have witnesses.

My father met him in the hall. I came down the stairs the way I always do when there are visitors — quietly, on the edges, a ghost with a household's permission to haunt — and took up my post behind the linen shelf in the dining room, where the door stands open six inches and the hall is visible in the mirror of the sideboard.

I want to explain something about reading lips through a mirror at forty feet, because my father would say it cannot be done and Tomas would say it cannot be done and even Mara might say it, gently, the way she says all doubtful things. It can be done. You lose half the words and you spend the other half like coins you've hoarded: context here, a guess there, the shape of a man's grudges filling in everything between. I have spent twenty-two years learning which words a man like Crane keeps in his mouth. They are not complicated words. Men like Crane use perhaps two hundred words in their entire lives, and most of them are *funding* and *reputation* and *unfortunate* and, if you wait long enough, *disappear.*

They talked for eleven minutes. I counted by the case clock in the hall, whose tick I feel in the floorboards through my shoes if I stand still enough, and whose pendulum I can see swinging in the mirror's far edge, so that time itself kept me honest while I worked.

The first minutes were nothing. Ceremony words. My father's back doing its hospitable arch, Crane's mouth making the shapes of weather and the city and the repairs to the east road. I almost went back upstairs.

Then Crane said my name.

I felt it before I placed it — a cold drop down the back of my neck, the body knowing danger ahead of the mind, as the body always does. I rewound, went back over the shapes. *Your daughter.* Yes. Then: *remarkably observant girl.* A compliment. Compliments from men like Crane are invoices. I stood very still and waited for the amount due.

*I understand she spends time with the clockmaker.*

There it was. Not a question mark anywhere in the shape of it, and men like Crane do not make statements about other people's children unless the statement is a move.

My father answered something long and deflecting — I caught *apprentice arrangements* and *perfectly ordinary* and *the clock, of course, the clock* — and Crane let him finish, and smiled the smile I have seen on exactly three faces in my life, each time just before something was taken from someone.

*What she reads, she reads nowhere else,* Crane said. And then, and this is the sentence I will carry out of that hall and into my grave or his, whichever comes first: *See that it stays that way. Paper is patient, Halvorsen, but so am I — and I am better funded.*

Paper. The word landed in me like a struck bell. Paper. The letter. Mara's letter, waxed inside the clock for eleven years, the letter that had turned our quiet house into a house with a secret — and secrets, I understood in that moment, looking at Aldous Crane's patient, well-funded mouth, have a smell, and men like Crane have hounds.

My father lied to him.

I saw it happen and I nearly gasped aloud, which would have been absurd, since no one in that hall could hear me gasp, but some habits of the hearing world stay in the body anyway. My father — who once returned extra change to a fishmonger through a rainstorm; who signed his name to an error in his own published tables that cost him a year of standing among his peers because correcting it publicly was the only honest course — my father looked at Aldous Crane and said, in smooth, warm, entirely false shapes, *You have my word, sir, that nothing of the kind exists in this house.*

His mouth made the lie cleanly. His shoulders did not. His shoulders were up around his ears like a man walking under falling masonry. Crane watched the shoulders. I watched Crane watching them, and Crane's eyes narrowed by perhaps a millimeter, and then he stepped back, and bowed, and said goodbye-words, and left.

And on his way to the gate, he passed the window of the schoolroom — my window, the north window — and stopped, and looked at the chalk marks on the glass.

I had left my star chart there. Tuesday's work. Jupiter's path against the ecliptic, drawn in my own notation, which uses symbols no one else in this city reads, because the notation books for the deaf are locked in a library across town and I long ago invented faster ones for myself.

Crane looked at those symbols for a long moment. Then he took out a small notebook, and copied three of them down, and walked on.

He thought they might be a code. He thought my astronomy was possibly a cipher. I stood in the empty hall with my heart going like the great clock's escapement and thought: *he is afraid of a woman's handwriting.* And beneath the fear, cold and clean as well-water, I thought: *good. Let him be. Fear makes careless men, and careless men show you everything.*

* * *

Mara worked late in the clock room that night. Everyone in the house knew she did, though nobody said it; her lamp burned behind the frosted pane until the small hours, and the tick-tick-tick of her tools came through the floor into the kitchen where Tomas sat pretending to catalog and actually staring at nothing, and into the study where my father pretended to correct proofs and actually stared at the same nothing. We were a household of people staring at nothing. Even the dog had given up on us.

I waited until the house settled. Waiting is a skill too; I began learning it before I learned letters. Then I went up.

The dome stairs are stone and spiral and my feet know them the way my hands know chalk. The clock room opens off the gallery beneath the dome itself, where the great telescope sleeps under its canvas shroud like a horse blanketed for winter. Mara had carried the regulator's works up here weeks ago — the escapement, the dead heart of it, laid out on velvet like surgery — and the lamp was burning low over the bench, and she was bent over it with a jeweler's glasses pinched to her eye, and she did not see me come in.

That is usual. Being unseen is usual. What was unusual was the moment I stepped into the lamplight and she looked up and did not look away.

Most people, upon noticing me, perform a small arithmetic: *deaf, therefore speak louder, therefore decide against it, therefore smile and wave and retreat.* Mara skipped the arithmetic. She set down her tool, took the glasses out of her eye, and said my name — she had practiced it, I could tell; the shape was careful, slightly too precise, the way a word goes when someone has rehearsed it alone — and then she simply waited, facing me, lamp tilted toward her own mouth so I could read.

Nobody tilts lamps toward my mouth. People tilt lamps away, toward their own work, and expect me to lean into the dark.

*You're up late,* she said. Or: the shapes of it. Her accent puts a hard edge on *late* that I like.

I sat down across the bench from her, on the stool Tomas uses, and I did something I had planned through three floors of stairs and was still frightened of doing, which is the truth about all courage as far as I can tell — the plan survives intact right up until the moment of the act, and then the act is done blind.

I said: *We need to talk about Aldous Crane.*

Her hands went still on the brass. I watched her decide whether to pretend otherwise, and watched her decide against it, which took her perhaps two seconds and which I will hold against every other person in this city forever, because in two seconds she chose not to waste my time.

*What about him?*

*He was here today. Unannounced. One attendant, left at the gate.* I gave it to her flat and fast, the way I compose when the words matter — short phrases, full stops, none of the flowing dependent clauses the hearing use to hide inside their own sentences. *I read the conversation. Through the mirror in the dining room. Don't ask how. He knows about the letter, Mara. He doesn't know where it is. But he knows it existed, and he used your name, and mine. He said paper is patient. He said to my father: see that it stays where it is. That was a threat shaped like a courtesy. Men like him don't waste courtesies.*

She stared at me. And here is the thing I braced for and did not receive: disbelief. The little furrow people get, the polite squint that means *surely you misread, surely you imagined, surely the deaf girl gathered fragments and assembled a ghost.* She didn't do it. She asked instead:

*Through a mirror? At that distance?*

*Forty feet. Half the words. The other half I filled in.* I tapped the bench, on the wood, twice, so she'd feel it and look at my hands. *I have been reading this house since I was four years old. I know what my father's lie looks like from the inside. He lied today for the first time in a decade. That is how I know Crane believes him — barely — and will come back to check.*

Mara took off her apron, folded it, unfolded it, refolded it. Working-class hands, always needing an object. Then she said the thing that made me know I had come to the right person in this house, the only person:

*Show me. The shapes he made. Show me exactly.*

So I did. I shaped Crane's threat with my own mouth, exaggerated, slowed, the way I teach myself new words from books — *see that it stays that way; paper is patient but so am I; I am better funded* — and I watched her watch me, and I watched the color go out of her face and come back on purpose, dragged back by will, like hauling a bucket up from a deep well.

*He'll have someone at the inquiry already,* she said. *Or he'll have someone watching us now.* She glanced up at the dome, at the dark curve of it, as if the sky itself might be retained by the patrons.

*Then we should assume we are being watched from tonight onward,* I said. *Which means we should also assume the letter cannot sit in the clock another week. It has to move soon, and it has to move along a route nobody watches, and it cannot move in any shape Crane expects.*

*You've been thinking about this.*

*Since eleven o'clock this morning. I think quickly. It's the one advantage of a head that never gets a moment's silence.*

That startled a laugh out of her — a real one, brief, bent over the escapement wheel — and something in my chest unlocked that I had not known was locked. I had not heard laughter since childhood, not the sound of it; I know it only as a shaking in other people's bodies and a brightening around the eyes, and hers did both, and I found I had missed it with an ache that surprised me.

*Why did you come to me?* she asked. And then, catching herself, honestly: *I'm glad. But why me? Why not your father, or Tomas?*

Because, I almost said, my father will protect me by excluding me, which is the same motion as Crane's protection of the city, and Tomas will protect his guilt by managing everything himself until it crushes him, and both of them love me and both of them have spent my whole life translating the world down into smaller, safer pieces before it reaches me. But what I actually said, because she deserved the plain version:

*Because everyone underestimates you. Like me. And people who are underestimated learn to move without being seen, and we are going to need to move without being seen.*

She looked at me for a long moment across the scattered heart of the great clock. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the case clock ticked into a new quarter hour, and I felt it faintly through the floor, our house keeping time under our feet.

*All right,* Mara said. *Partner, then.*

She put out her hand across the bench. A handshake. Nobody had ever — my father kisses my forehead, Tomas bows, the world pats my shoulder like patting a horse. Nobody had ever put out a hand and offered the grip reserved for colleagues.

I took it. Her hand was small and hard-muscled and smelled of oil and graphite, and I held on maybe a second longer than handshakes require, and neither of us mentioned it.

*One condition,* I said, as she turned back to the works. *Whatever the plan is — the route, the checkpoints, all of it Tomas is surely already scratching onto napkins somewhere — you tell me everything. No protected version. No smaller, safer pieces.*

*Agreed,* she said, and then, with the corner of her mouth moving in a way I was learning to read as fast as speech: *Though I warn you, most of the plan so far is: don't get caught.*

*A rigorous strategy. I'll draft improvements by morning.*

She laughed again, quieter, and bent back over the escapement, and I should have gone to bed. It was past two. Instead I stayed, and fetched chalk from my pocket, and drew on the flagstones at her feet where the lamplight fell — the drive, the gate, the position of Crane's attendant, the sight-line from the schoolroom window — a map of everything I had seen, in my own invented shorthand, teaching it to her symbol by symbol while she pointed and asked and learned faster than anyone I had ever taught.

That is how we found it, later — the two of us crouched over chalk at three in the morning, her fingers suddenly stopping on the stones. She tapped the drawing. The harbor gate. The route any letter leaving this house would most naturally take toward the ministry.

*If I were Crane,* she said slowly, shaping each word for me, making sure I caught all of it, *this is where I'd put my man. First checkpoint of three, if Tomas's rumors hold. Harbor gate. Customs house. Ministry stair.*

*Then we don't take the natural route,* I said.

*No,* she said, and her eyes had gone bright and dangerous in the lamplight, the eyes of a woman who had spent her whole life invisible and had just discovered what invisibility was worth on the open market. *No, we take one nobody's ever thought to guard.*

She wiped the map from the flagstones with her sleeve, all of it, every line, leaving only one mark: a small chalked circle, right at the base of the great clock's case.

And that was when we both heard — she heard, and I felt through the floorboards — footsteps on the dome stairs. Someone climbing. Slowly.
