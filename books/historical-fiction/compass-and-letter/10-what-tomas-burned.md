## 10. What Tomas Burned

Tomas Vey had rehearsed the confession for eleven years, which was eleven years longer than anyone had asked him to give it.

He practiced it in the archive stacks when the archive still wanted him, muttering it between catalogue drawers where no one could hear. He practiced it in his rented room above the chandler's shop, facing the cracked mirror, watching his own mouth move around the words like a man chewing gravel. He practiced it in the bath, in bed, walking the harbor wall at dusk. *I burned it. I burned the page. She asked me to keep it and I burned it.*

He had never once said it aloud to another living soul.

Now he stood in the observatory's lower workroom with a lamp turned low and Mara Dunmore sitting across from him, her hands folded on the table as though she were the one taking a deposition. The letter lay between them. The wax seal — Lise Dunmore's compass rose, pressed into crimson — had been broken that morning, and Tomas had spent the hours since avoiding both the letter and the girl whose name was written into its accusations in everything but ink.

She had cornered him after supper. Not loudly. That was the worst of it. Ingrid had gone up to the dome, Halvorsen had pleadingly pleaded an early night, and Mara had simply stayed seated when the others left, and looked at him, and said: *You knew her. Don't tell me you didn't. You said her name yesterday before you caught yourself.*

Tomas had spent thirty years in archives learning how records lied by omission. It seemed fitting that his own life should be convicted by the same method.

"Her name is Dunmore," he said now. "Your name is Dunmore. I'm not a clever man anymore, Miss Dunmore, but I can do that arithmetic."

"Do you want to say it plainly?" Her voice was level. Too level. He recognized the technique; he'd invented half of it. A witness kept calm was a witness who wouldn't break before the important questions.

"I want to tell it in order," he said. "If you'll allow me. Confessions are like catalogues. If you file things out of sequence, they're never found again."

Mara nodded once. She did not blink enough. He wondered if she knew how much she resembled her mother when she did that — Lise at the reading desk, pen moving fast, eyes fixed on some middle distance where the coastline hadn't been drawn yet.

*In order,* then.

* * *

"In 1879 I was assistant archivist at the Admiralty Records Office," Tomas began. "Third desk from the window. It doesn't sound like much, but that window mattered. Light to read by, and a view of the harbor, and the understanding — unspoken, but understood — that the men at the first two desks noticed who sat where. Promotion in that office was not earned. It was *observed*."

"Young, then," Mara said.

"Young, ambitious, and poor, which is the worst possible combination in a civil servant. Ambition without money is a fever. It makes you agreeable to anyone holding the medicine." He turned his cup on the table, not drinking. "Your mother came to us in the spring of '79. She was already known — coastal surveyor, self-taught, which the gentlemen pretended to admire and privately resented. She'd been charting the northern approaches under commission from the Geographical Society, and the Society had agreed to lodge her working papers with us. Surveys, soundings, logbooks. Everything she did for three years passed across my desk."

"And?"

"And she was brilliant." He said it simply, because there was no other honest way. "You'll have heard it from strangers who didn't know what they were saying. Let me be the one to tell you properly: she was brilliant, and funny in a dry way that took people a moment, and utterly without fear in a profession built on flattery. She treated admirals like errand boys when the charts demanded it. I was twenty-six and half in love with her, the way clerks are half in love with women who are kind to them out of habit."

Mara's hands tightened slightly on the table. She said nothing.

"The logbooks came to me in batches. I catalogued them, indexed them, shelved them. And here is the thing I need you to understand, because everything else follows from it." He leaned forward. "The logbook of a cartographer is not a diary. It's a record of position, tide, weather, bearing. Dry as sand. But Lise — your mother — she wrote in the margins. Observations. *Saw three steam colliers taking on coal at the eastern anchorage, flying no colors.* *Harbor pilot questioned me about my northern soundings; seemed ill-pleased.* Little notes. A careful mind noticing careless things."

"She was watching someone," Mara said.

"She was watching everyone. Because someone was watching her. And by the winter of 1880, she knew it. She started bringing me copies instead of originals. Started asking whether the registry index could show who had requested her files. I told her requests weren't logged by name." His mouth twisted. "That was true. It was also a lie, because requests weren't logged by name precisely so that no one would ever ask who had requested them."

The lamp fluttered. Somewhere above them the great dead clock ticked faintly through the floors — no, it didn't tick, it was stopped, it had stopped for eleven years; it was only the house settling, only the building breathing. Eleven years of hearing time where there wasn't any. Tomas steadied himself.

"In February of 1881 she came to me after hours. She'd bribed the night porter — or persuaded him; she had a way. She put a sealed packet on my desk and said, *'Vey, if anything happens to me, this goes to the parliamentary record. Not the Society. Not the Admiralty. The record.'* And inside—" he closed his eyes— "inside was her master logbook. All of it. Margins and all. Every careful note about colliers and pilots and foreign buyers and the names of two patrons of this very observatory who had arranged introductions between her commission and certain naval attachés passing themselves off as trade delegates."

"Aldous Crane," Mara said quietly.

"One of them. The younger, then. Hungry the way I was hungry, but with money behind his hunger." Tomas opened his eyes. "She trusted me. That's the whole of it. Out of every man in that building, she chose the third desk from the window, because I'd once stayed late to help her collate soundings, and because I brought good coffee, and because kindness is cheap and she overpaid me for it her whole life."

* * *

He stood then. He couldn't tell it sitting down; he never had been able to, even alone in the rented room. He walked to the workbench and stood with his back to her, looking at the tools hanging on their pegs — Mara's territory, this room, all those delicate instruments of truth-telling — and spoke to the wall.

"On the fourteenth of March, 1881, your mother's boat was found washed up near the point below the north cliff. Empty. The official finding was accidental drowning. The inquiry lasted one afternoon." He heard Mara's breath change and made himself go on. "On the sixteenth — two days later, before her body was even released — I was summoned to the office of the under-secretary. Not my superior. *His* superior. There was a man standing by the window whom I would later see at every observatory patronage dinner for a decade: Aldous Crane. And the under-secretary said, in the gentlest voice, that questions had arisen about irregularities in the Dunmore deposit, and that the office wished to assist me in resolving them, and that my career — which was, he noted, very promising — depended entirely on the speed and discretion of my assistance."

"What did they want?"

"The logbook. The real one, with the margins. They'd received word — from whom I never learned, though I have my guesses and Crane's face confirms them nightly — that Lise had lodged something sensitive. They couldn't simply seize it; the deposit was technically Society property, and the Society had a conscience or at least a secretary with one. So they needed a hand inside. A young hand. An ambitious, poor hand." He picked up a screwdriver from the bench and set it down again, unable to feel the weight of it. "They told me the book contained material damaging to *national security*. They told me she had been, implicitly, a seller of secrets — which was a lie so precise it cut, because it was exactly what they were doing to her memory that she had died trying to expose. And they told me that if I cooperated, the first desk by the window would open. Eventually."

"You took the book out of the deposit," Mara said. Not a question.

"I took it home. I sat with it all night. I read every margin note, and I understood — completely, finally, at the age of twenty-seven — that the woman was murdered, that these men had done it or caused it, and that I was being paid in a window seat to bury the proof." His laugh came out wrong, a dry click. "And I want to be exact with you, Miss Dunmore, because exactness is all I have left to offer: I did not burn it because I believed them. I burned it because I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up afterward as prudence. *If I refuse, they'll find another clerk, and the book will vanish anyway, and I'll lose everything.* That is what I told myself. It is the speech every coward gives himself, and I delivered it beautifully."

The silence stretched. Mara did not fill it. She let him stand there in it, and he understood that this too was mercy — that she would not soften the ground he had to cross.

"They wanted it destroyed in front of a witness," he went on. "A formality. The under-secretary himself came to my rooms — that's how frightened they were of it — and watched. But a whole logbook burns slowly, and it fights. The cover goes first, then the stitching, and wet paper — I'd spilled coffee on the back section days before, stupidly, providentially — wet paper doesn't catch. It chars. I fed the fire pages from the front, the dry ones, the position tables and the tide records, everything harmless, and I let the wet end — the last gathering, the final weeks of her notes — sit at the edge of the grate until the under-secretary grew impatient and declared it done." He turned around at last. "Then I signed a certificate of destruction, and eight months later I was promoted to the first desk by the window, and I never once enjoyed the light."

Mara's voice came thin. "You said you kept something."

* * *

Tomas crossed to the old cabinet in the corner of the workroom — Halvorsen had given him a drawer for his effects, one drawer, after the disgrace — and knelt before it. His hands shook. He let them. Some tremors had been earned.

Behind his spare shirts and a bundle of cataloguing slips, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with the waxed string he'd replaced twice in eleven years, lay a shape the size of a hymnal.

"When the ash was cold," he said, still kneeling, "I went back to the grate. I don't know why. Cowardice has strange appetites. And among the char I found the surviving gathering — scorched at the edges, water-stained, but legible. The last three weeks of her hand." He rose and turned, holding it in both palms like an offering at an altar he had no right to approach. "I couldn't restore it to the record. The certificate of destruction existed; producing it would have undone me and gotten it re-buried within the week. I couldn't burn it either. So I hid it, and I carried it through six houses and one marriage and one disgrace, and every year I told myself I would find the right moment, and every year the moment required more courage than I had. This is the arithmetic of shame, Miss Dunmore. It compounds."

He laid it on the table between the letter and the lamp.

Mara looked at it the way she must look at a stopped movement inside a clock case — assessing, careful, afraid of what touching it might start. Then she untied the string with fingers far steadier than his, and opened the oilcloth, and drew out the charred remainder of her mother's logbook.

She turned the first leaf. The paper was brown-edged, brittle, the margins crowded with that quick slanted hand. Tomas watched her lips move as she read — silently, the way her mother used to read — and saw her stop on one line, and go pale, and read it again.

"What does it say?" he asked softly.

Mara didn't answer at first. When she did, her voice had gone somewhere flat and far. "*Soundings taken off the north cliff, 4:17 by watch. Water unusually deep for the chart. Someone has moved the marker buoy.*" She looked up. "Dated the twelfth of March. Two days before she drowned. Off the north cliff. At 4:17."

The number hung in the lamplight between them. Tomas felt it settle into place like a tooth into a jaw — the stopped clock upstairs, frozen at 4:17 for eleven years, waiting in its own dead language for this exact table, this exact girl.

"She marked the hour herself," he said. "Whatever happened to her, it began at 4:17, off that cliff, and she wrote it down because writing things down was how she fought. And then—" he gestured at the letter, the map fragments locked in the strongbox upstairs, the whole buried architecture of it— "and then she sealed the rest away for whoever kept the time."

Mara closed the logbook gently, the way she'd close a clock case. When she raised her head, something had changed in her face — the wariness burned off it, or rather burned *down* into something harder and clearer, a metal brought to temperature.

"You're giving this to me," she said. "Not lending. Giving."

"I'm returning it," Tomas said. "There's a difference, and it matters, and it's the only sentence in thirty years I've earned. I cannot testify well — I fold under pressure, I've proven it. But paper doesn't fold. Paper holds the line you couldn't. Take it to the inquiry. Let it say what I was too small to say."

For a long moment Mara studied him, and he braced for the verdict he deserved — contempt, or worse, forgiveness given freely, which weighs more. Instead she reached across the table and placed her hand briefly, firmly, on the scorched cover, over his hand, and said:

"Then we carry it together. You knew her. You owe her. That makes you the closest thing I have to a witness, and I'm not leaving you behind to punish yourself in peace." A ghost of something — not quite a smile — crossed her face. "Besides. You know the records offices. We'll need papers for three checkpoints, and none of us can forge worth a damn."

Tomas laughed — really laughed, for the first time since 1881 — and the sound startled them both.

It was Ingrid who ended the moment, appearing in the doorway with her chalkboard held up, her eyes wide and urgent, the chalk strokes still trembling:

**CRANE'S CARRIAGE AT THE GATE. HE'S ASKING FOR THE ARCHIVIST BY NAME.**
