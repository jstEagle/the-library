## 11. The Harbor Gate

The delivery cart smelled of fish and tar, and I sat among the clock weights with my hands folded in my lap like a lady going to church, which was the first lie of the morning and by no means the worst.

We had argued about this the night before, in the dome, voices kept low because low voices felt safer even though there was no one left at the observatory to hear us. Tomas had wanted to carry the letter himself. He had maps of official procedure in his head—manifests, seals, the exact weight of paper that made a thing *real* to a clerk—and he believed, with the touching faith of a man who had spent thirty years inside archives, that paperwork could save them.

"They'll be watching for an archivist," Halvorsen said. "A disgraced one. You are the most legible man in this city, Tomas. Your face is practically stamped."

"And what is she?" Tomas had asked, nodding at me—not cruelly, only the way you'd note a flaw in an otherwise sound argument. "A girl with ink on her cuffs."

"A clockmaker's apprentice delivering clock weights," Ingrid said, and she said it aloud, which she almost never did, her voice flat and rough from disuse, and it settled the argument the way a struck bell settles a room. "Nobody looks at a delivery. Nobody has ever looked at a delivery. That's the whole point of deliveries."

So it was me. Of course it was me. I had spent nineteen years learning how to pass through a room without disturbing the air in it, and now the skill finally had a use, which felt like a joke told at my expense by someone who knew me very well.

The weights were real—two crates of them, brass cylinders wrapped in straw, bound for a merchant on Wexler Street who had paid Halvorsen's household a small fee for the use of his name on the manifest. Under the top layer of straw, waxed canvas against the lead of the largest weight, taped flat where no hand would go looking unless it already knew: the letter. Lise Dunmore's letter, addressed to *whoever keeps the time*, which was me, which had apparently always been me.

I touched my knee to the crate once more as the cart jolted down toward the harbor road. Still there. It would still be there in ten seconds; I would touch it again anyway.

"You're pale," said the carter. His name was Brem. He was a friend of a friend of Tomas's, which meant we trusted him exactly as far as the coin in his pocket reached, and no further, and everyone on that cart understood the arithmetic.

"I'm always pale," I said. "It's the indoor trade. Clockmakers come out of their workshops like mushrooms."

Brem grunted. He had not laughed at any of my jokes since the observatory, all four of them, and I had decided this made him trustworthy. A man immune to bad jokes has fewer levers for other people to pull.

Then the harbor gate rose up ahead of us and I stopped making jokes even inside my own head.

* *

I had seen the harbor gate before, of course. Everyone had. It was one of those structures the city used to tell itself stories about its own importance—a stone arch wide enough for three carts, crowned with the city arms and two carved figures, Commerce and Navigation, who gazed out over the water with the serene stupidity of marble. What I had never done was approach it carrying something that mattered. That changed the gate entirely. It was the same arch, but it had grown teeth.

There were soldiers at it. Not harbor police—soldiers, in government grey, with a table set up in front of the arch like a picnic nobody wanted to attend. A queue of carts ahead of us. A clerk with a ledger. And beyond the table, leaning against the stones with the boneless patience of a man being paid by the hour, a second guard with a carbine slung across his back and his cap tipped forward against the morning sun.

"Manifests out," Brem said, around the pipe in his teeth. "Don't talk unless they ask. If they ask, answer short. Deliveries get waved through nine times in ten."

"What's the tenth time?" I asked.

He sucked on his pipe. "You don't want to be the tenth."

The queue moved. I watched it move the way I watch an escapement—the tick, the catch, the release, each cart approaching the table, papers presented, a glance, a stamp, a wave onward. Tick. Catch. Release. There is comfort in mechanism, even when the mechanism exists to grind you. A system with rules is a system you can hide inside. I had hidden inside the orphanage's rules for eleven years. I had hidden inside the workshop's rules for eight more. Rules had always been a shelter.

I understood, sitting among the clock weights with my mother's letter six inches from my shin, that the rules had never once been a shelter. They had been a hiding place, and hiding places and shelters are different things. One keeps the weather off. The other just delays the moment they find you.

Two carts ahead, a soldier opened a crate with a bayonet and did not apologize to the driver for the wreckage. Tick. Catch. Release.

"Brem," I said quietly. "If they open the weights—"

"They won't open the weights."

"If they do."

He shifted the pipe to the other side of his mouth. "Then you're a clockmaker's girl with a broken delivery, and you cry about it, and you demand compensation, and you make such a boring nuisance of yourself that they wish they'd never lifted the lid. Weights are dull, girl. Dullness is armor." He glanced at me sideways. "You strike me as someone who learned that early."

One cart ahead. The soldier at the table had a voice like gravel poured slowly and a habit of repeating the last word of every answer the drivers gave him, as if daring them to take it back. *Onion sacks? Onion sacks.* *Tuesday delivery? Tuesday.* I filed the habit away. People who repeat your words back to you are listening for hesitation, not information. So don't hesitate, and give them nothing worth repeating.

And then it was our turn, and I saw the guard leaning against the stones straighten up and push his cap back, and my stomach dropped through the bottom of the cart, through the cobblestones, through the harbor silt, all the way down to wherever things go when they stop pretending to float.

Because I knew him.

* *

His name was Pell. When I was eleven years old—no, twelve; the spring after the Whitmore boy broke my arm and I set it myself in the wash-house rather than explain how—he had been a porter at Saint Ambrose's, hauling coal and crates and once, memorably, a piano nobody knew how to play. He had been the only adult in that whole grey building who looked at children as if they might contain something. Not kindness, exactly. Interest. He'd caught me reading a salvaged navigation manual behind the boiler rooms—I remember it precisely, *The Mariner's Practical Companion*, third edition, water-stained—and instead of confiscating it he'd squinted at the diagrams and said, "What's that contraption?"

"A sextant," I'd said. "It measures angles between stars."

"Why would anyone need that?"

"To know where they are."

He'd thought about that longer than the sentence deserved, and then he'd nodded and gone back to his coals, and I had kept the book, and that was the entire sum of our acquaintance. Two conversations, maybe three, across a single year. He had left Saint Ambrose's before I did. I had assumed he'd gone the way everyone went—into some other city, some other life, thinned down to nothing in my memory.

But memory doesn't thin. It waits. And here he came around the table with the manifest in his hand, squinting at Brem first and dismissing him in half a second, and then looking at me, and I watched recognition arrive in his face the way frost arrives on glass—from the edges inward, sudden and total.

"Dunmore," he said.

Not *Mara*. Not *girl*. The surname. My mother's surname, the one the orphanage gave up trying to scrub because I refused to answer to anything else, and my whole body went still with the particular stillness of prey.

"Do I know you?" I said, and hated how thin it came out.

"Saint Ambrose's," Pell said. "Years back. You're the one with the books." He held up the manifest. "Says here you're clock weights."

"Clock weights," the soldier at the table repeated, without looking up from his ledger.

"For Wexler Street," I said. "Merchant named Corvey. Weights for a tower clock. They're lead and brass, you can weigh them if—" and I heard myself about to offer, about to volunteer, the oldest mistake of the guilty, and shut my mouth so hard my teeth clicked.

Pell looked at me. Behind him, the second guard shifted against the stones, and the soldier at the table waited with his pen poised, and somewhere in the queue a horse stamped twice, and the whole apparatus of the checkpoint hung suspended like a wheel with the pallet engaged, everything holding, everything waiting on the release.

I thought: *he knows nothing. He remembers a child with a book. Whatever Crane's people have circulated—and they will have circulated something, a description, a name, a reward—it won't say 'the one with the books.' The world has never paid enough attention to me to describe me accurately.*

I thought: *but he's looking at the crates.*

I thought: *my hands are shaking, and hands are the one part of me I have never been able to disguise.*

"Corvey," Pell said slowly. "Wexler Street." He turned the manifest over, as though the back of it might hold different words. And then he said, in a completely different voice, quieter, aimed at me alone as though he'd drawn a curtain around our end of the table: "There was another Dunmore. Before you. At the harbor office, they still—"

He stopped himself. The second guard was watching now, openly, bored but watching, and boredom plus attention is the most dangerous combination there is.

"There was another Dunmore," Pell finished, carefully neutral, "on the fishing registers. Years ago. Coastal charts."

My mouth was dry as old cork. Every instinct I owned said: deny, deflect, bore him. Say *I wouldn't know, sir*, drop my eyes, become furniture. I had done it a thousand times. It worked a thousand times. Invisibility was the only inheritance the orphanage ever actually gave me, and it had kept me alive, and here, at a checkpoint with my mother's letter strapped under straw six inches from my shin, every trained reflex in my body reached for it—

And I didn't.

I don't fully know why. Later, in the dark of many retellings, I have tried to make it principle, strategy, courage. It wasn't. It was simpler and worse than any of those. It was that he had said her name—*charts*, *coastal*, *Dunmore*—and for twelve years her name had existed nowhere outside my own skull, spoken by no one, recorded in nothing, and hearing it in a stranger's mouth at a guarded gate was like taking a breath after nearly drowning. I could not pretend it hadn't happened. The word was already in the air. Denying it felt like letting her drown twice.

"My mother," I said, "was Lise Dunmore. She was a cartographer."

Tick, said the machinery of the world. Everything hung on the catch.

Pell looked at me for a long moment. Then he turned to the soldier at the table and slapped the manifest down with a brightness he had not possessed ten seconds earlier. "Weights check out. Corvey's been using Halvorsen's foundry connections for years—good brass, that lot. Wave it through before the queue backs up past the fish market again. You know what the fish market queue is like."

The soldier, who plainly did know, and plainly cared about nothing else on earth, stamped the manifest. "Move along."

*Move along.*

Brem clicked his tongue at the horse. The cart lurched. The gate's shadow slid over us, cold as well-water, and released us into the morning light on the far side, and the harbor opened out ahead—all rigging and gulls and the brown-green slap of water—and I sat rigid among my crates until the arch was a finger's width behind us, then a knife's width, then gone.

Only then did my hands start properly shaking, the big shakes, the kind that belong to the body and not to me.

* *

"He let us through," Brem said, after a quarter mile of silence. "That guard. He looked right at you and let us through, and I've run this gate eleven years and I know what that pause cost him. Somebody told him to look for somebody, and he decided the somebody wasn't you." He spat over the wheel. "Why?"

"I knew him when I was a child."

"That's not why a man risks his neck."

I looked at the water. A dredger was working the channel, its chain rattling up muck from the bottom, things surfacing that had lain under the gray for years, brought up dripping into the light whether they liked it or not.

"He said my mother's name," I said.

Brem waited for more. There wasn't more. Or there was all of it, everywhere, filling the cart and the morning and the rest of my life: that she had a name in other people's mouths. That the harbor registers still carried it. That the past I'd kept locked at 4:17 for twelve years had been moving the whole time, quietly, in ledgers and registers and the memories of coal porters, waiting for me to come within earshot. History had happened elsewhere—that had been my religion, the orphanage catechism, the workshop creed. Big things happen to other people, in other rooms, and you keep your head down and wind the clocks. But the letter under my knee had already proven the catechism a lie. Now the gate had proven it a lie out loud, in daylight, by a man who owed me nothing.

She had been recognized. Which meant, someday, I might be.

"Wexler Street's the next turning," Brem said. He was quiet a moment longer, then added, almost gruffly, "Whatever you're carrying in those weights, girl—you want to deliver it quick. Gates get smarter. Guards get replaced."

He dropped me at the corner with the crates—both crates, letter and all, because the plan required the delivery to be real and the plan, so far, was the only thing standing between me and catastrophe. I watched the cart rattle off toward the fish market and stood a moment on the cobbles with the sun full on my face and my heart going like a badly adjusted balance wheel.

Wexler Street first. Unload, stack, sign Corvey's book, walk out the front door like a mushroom returning to the cellar. Then the cut-through to Candle Lane, where Ingrid would be waiting at the corner with a market basket and, folded inside the false bottom of it, the map fragments, and where the second leg of the day would begin.

I lifted the crates. Halfway down the street, a boy detached himself from a doorway and fell into step beside me, unhurried, familiar, as if we'd arranged it—which we had not—and said, pleasantly, in a voice pitched only for me:

"Miss Corvey's expecting you. Though she asks—" and here he smiled with every tooth he had, "—why a clock delivery needs an escort of two."

Behind us, at the corner, a man in a grey coat folded his newspaper and began to follow.

The customs house was three streets away, and someone had known to wait for me there.
