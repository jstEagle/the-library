## 13. The Raid

Ingrid felt them before anyone announced them.

She was at the long window of the library, copying figures from the transit ledger onto her slate, when the gravel drive began to hum. Not carriage wheels — boots. Many boots, in step, which meant they had drilled for this, which meant it had been planned in rooms she would never be invited into. She set down the chalk and watched six men come up the drive in the grey light before breakfast, coats buttoned to the chin, and behind them a seventh in a better coat who walked like he owned the gravel: Mr. Pell, Crane's man, whom she had lip-read through this same window three nights ago saying *the letter must not reach the inquiry*.

Her father was still asleep. Tomas was in the archive pretending not to sleep there. Mara —

Mara was already on the stairs, hair braided wrong, the letter in her hand, moving fast and quiet the way she did everything, as if she were a habit the world might break if it noticed her.

Ingrid crossed the hall and took her wrist. *They're here,* she signed, and then, because Mara's hands were full and slow with fear, she said it aloud too — her own voice, flat and unpracticed and loud enough that Mara flinched, which Ingrid was used to and didn't mind.

"Where?" Mara's lips said.

Ingrid pointed through the glass. Six men. Pell. Twenty minutes, perhaps less, before they reached the door.

Mara looked at the letter in her hand as though she'd forgotten holding it, and Ingrid saw the whole calculation happen — the girl's eyes going door, window, clock case, stairs — every road ending at the same wall: nowhere for a letter to go that men with a warrant wouldn't look.

Ingrid took her arm again and pulled her toward the schoolroom.

* * *

The schoolroom had been hers since she was seven, when her mother was alive and the tutors came and went like weather. One wall was hers entirely: three blackboards framed in oak, her father's gift the winter he finally understood that his daughter's silence was not emptiness but a room with the door shut. She had covered them for twelve years. Diagrams of Jupiter's moons. Tide tables copied out until her wrist ached. A rotating orrery drawn in white and yellow chalk so careful you could almost see it turn. The lower boards held her older work, layered and smudged and gone over again, because chalk is cheap and boards are honest: whatever you last wrote is what everyone believes.

Pell and his men would search drawers. They would take up floorboards if instructed. They would unpick book spines with penknives. They would stand in front of a wall of a deaf girl's schoolwork, see a lifetime of sums and planets, and look elsewhere — because she had watched men do exactly that her whole life. Silence reads, to hearing men, as absence. Let them.

She was already chalking over the tide tables on the lowest board when Mara caught up to her, letter still clutched like something rescued from a fire.

"What are you doing?"

Ingrid held up one finger — wait — and finished erasing a column of figures with her sleeve. Then she turned and signed, slowly enough to be followed: *Give me the map.*

"We can't just—"

*The map.* She put out her hand. *They'll search everything with an edge. Paper hides badly. Chalk doesn't have to hide. It only has to be boring.*

Mara stared at her, and Ingrid watched the understanding arrive the way she watched everything arrive — through the skin, through the eyes, a little late but all at once. Mara knelt, pried open the clock-weight crate she'd carried from the workroom, and lifted out the oilcloth packet. Inside, the surviving sections of her mother's coastal chart lay cut into four pieces, the ink faded to the color of old tea, the soundings marked in a hand Ingrid had never known and now recognized instantly, because it was Mara's hand, older.

"You'll ruin them," Mara said. "Chalk dust. The moisture in the chalk. It could—" Her voice did the thing it did when she was frightened, going thin and precise. "It's the only map left."

Ingrid took the pieces from her. *So we don't ruin them.* She signed with one hand while the other laid the first fragment face-down against the board's ledge. *Paper in front. Chalk behind. I draw the coast over it — my version, from memory of your mother's lines — and the real map sits flat against the wood where no knife goes. Men don't scrape schoolroom walls. They read what's written and move on.*

"And if they wipe the boards?"

Ingrid let herself smile, small and sharp. *Then they wipe it themselves, with their own hands, in front of me, and I will look devastated, and underneath they will find four pieces of paper they were kind enough not to tear.*

Mara made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by fear. "And the letter?"

The letter was harder. The letter was words, and words couldn't be redrawn. Ingrid thought for a moment, then pointed upward, at the dome, and signed: *Father's sphere. The orrery base is hollow. He built it to hide his tobacco from me when I was nine. It has fooled one person for nineteen years.*

"He knows you knew?"

*He suspects. He has never once asked, because asking means admitting the tobacco.* Which was its own small lesson about powerful men, and one Ingrid intended to apply all morning.

Boots on the porch now. The knock came the way official knocks come — heavy, rhythmic, entitled. Mara pressed the letter into Ingrid's second-best drawing portfolio — misdirection, a first layer — and went downstairs to open the door, because it was her name on the repair contract and because, Ingrid was beginning to understand, Mara Dunmore had spent twenty-two years being knocked for and had no intention of standing behind doors anymore.

Ingrid picked up her chalk and began to draw the coastline.

* * *

They brought the cold in with them. Six men fanning through the hall in their grey coats, breath steaming, gloves on — gloves, Ingrid noted, so nothing they touched would carry prints of them, though everything they touched would carry prints of their going. Pell stood in the center of the hall reading the warrant aloud to her father, who had come down with his collar crooked and his eyes entirely awake, listening with the terrible courtesy of a man cataloguing every sentence for later use.

"—upon information received, all papers, charts, correspondence, and instruments pertaining to—"

"My instruments," Halvorsen said mildly, "are the property of the Royal Astronomical Trust, and removing them without a Trust steward present is theft, Mr. Pell, whichever warrant you're holding."

"This warrant covers theft too," Pell said, and Ingrid, watching his mouth from across the hall, saw how much he enjoyed it.

Two men went upstairs. Two went to the archive, where Tomas Vey stood in the doorway in a nightshirt over his clothes, arms folded, wearing the expression of ruined dignity he did so well and which Ingrid now suspected was half armor and half performance. One stayed in the hall to watch the family. The last — young, red-eared, uncomfortable — drifted toward the schoolroom doorway and stopped.

He looked at the boards.

Ingrid kept her back to him, shoulders bent over the lowest board, chalk moving. She was drawing the headland north of the harbor, the one with the twin stacks her mother's — no, Mara's mother's — chart had marked with a depth sounding of eleven fathoms where any honest survey showed seven. She drew it wrong on purpose, then right, then annotated it in her round schoolgirl hand: *corrected from memory, M.H., age 19*. Around the fragment pressed flat beneath her forearm she built a fortress of boredom: tide corrections, a practice problem in navigation, a marginal sketch of a gull done badly on purpose. The paper lay against the wood with her sleeve over it, and her heartbeat was so loud in her chest that she checked, absurdly, whether the boy could hear it from the doorway.

"Miss Halvorsen." His mouth moved uncertainly, the way hearing mouths did when they spoke at a deaf person — louder shapes, no more meaning. "You'll need to step away from the boards, miss."

She turned, chalk in hand, and gave him the look she had perfected at fourteen: polite, patient, faintly pitying. Then she pointed at her ear, shook her head, and gestured at her mouth with elaborate apology.

"I said—" he began, and colored, and tried again, shaping each word hugely: "STEP. AWAY."

From the hall, her father's voice arrived, dry as winter air: "She reads beautifully, constable, when people face her like civilized beings. Try it."

The boy flushed darker, stepped around so he was in her line of sight, and repeated, mortified, "Step away from the boards, please, miss. We have to examine the walls."

Examine the walls. So Pell had thought of that much. Ingrid stepped back with folded hands and watched two hours of her life get inspected by a man with red ears.

He read the orrery diagram. He read the tide tables. He read *corrected from memory, M.H., age 19*, and glanced at her, and she arranged her face into the blankness people expected — not defiance, nothing interesting, just a girl waiting for permission to return to her lessons. He ran one gloved finger along the board's ledge and found only chalk dust, because she had spent ten minutes before opening the door dusting the ledge and pressing the fragment's edges flat with the flat of her thumb until the paper surrendered to the wood.

He moved on to the windowsill. Behind her back, where no one whose attention belonged to the room could see it, Ingrid's hand closed around the chalk hard enough to bite.

* * *

Fury is a strange thing in a silent house. It has nowhere to shout, so it goes downward, into the hands, into the feet, into the pulse that ticks at her wrists like an insolent little clock. Ingrid stood in the schoolroom doorway under the escort of nobody — the red-eared boy had been called away to help haul books from the archive — and watched through the banister rails as her home was taken apart by men who had never once, in all the years they'd passed her father on the street, bothered to learn her name.

They had her father's correspondence out on the dining table, sorted into piles by a clerk with quick fingers. They had Tomas's archive cartons in the hall, forty years of other people's history being rifled by boys who thought an index was a kind of hat. They had pulled up three floorboards in the study and found nothing, and the finding nothing seemed to annoy Pell, who stood turning his gloves in his hands and looking at the ceiling as if the house itself were lying to him.

It was. The house was lying beautifully. The house was her.

Upstairs, a drawer slammed. Downstairs, the clerk called out, "Got a portfolio here!" and Ingrid's heart performed one complete revolution before her mind caught up: the second-best portfolio, the decoy, Mara's failed watercolors of the harbor from last summer, which Mara had insisted on adding ("they need something to find") and which now emerged into the hall light to a moment of genuine anticipation among six grown men.

Pell leafed through them himself. Harbor. Harbor from the mole. The harbor in rain, which was honestly the best of them. Pell's mouth did something between disappointment and contempt, and he tossed the portfolio onto the pile of things deemed worthless, and Ingrid breathed out through her nose, slow and even, and understood for the first time in her life why her father loved his work: there is a particular clean joy in watching the sky behave exactly as your mathematics said it would.

Mara stood near the cold fireplace with her arms wrapped around herself, playing the hired tradeswoman, bewildered, inconvenienced, wanting only her tools returned. She was good. Better than good — she had the servants'-entrance slouch down perfectly, the apologetic half-smile, the whole vocabulary of a person assembled to be overlooked. But Ingrid had spent three weeks learning her real face, and she could see the other one burning underneath, and when Pell's gaze crossed the hall and snagged on Mara for a half-second too long, Ingrid signed, low, behind the banister rail, where only eyes raised to exactly the right height could read it:

*Stop looking guilty. You look like a woman with a secret. Look bored instead.*

Mara's eyes flicked up, found her, and — this was the thing about Mara, the thing Ingrid liked more than she could easily say — she took the correction instantly, shoulders dropping a quarter inch, face slackening into tedium, a tradeswoman counting the minutes until she could bill someone. Pell looked away.

Then Pell looked at the clock.

* * *

The great regulator stood in its niche at the end of the hall, case dark, pendulum still, hands at 4:17 where they had hung for eleven years. Mara had opened it two days ago; the escapement lay disassembled on wool in the workroom upstairs. The case was empty of anything but old air and, waxed to the inside frame, nothing at all anymore, because the letter had come out and was currently rolled in a copper tube inside an orrery base that had once hidden pipe tobacco from a nine-year-old girl who missed nothing.

But Pell didn't know any of that. Pell looked at the dead clock the way a man looks at a dog that bit someone once.

"Open it," he said.

"It's an empty clock case," her father said. "Miss Dunmore has the movement apart in the workshop. You may verify that."

"Oh, I intend to verify everything." Pell snapped his fingers at the red-eared boy. "Both. Case and workshop."

The boy opened the case with awkward reverence, shone a lamp inside, ran his glove along the frame where the wax seal had sat for eleven years, and reported, "Nothing, sir." He fetched the disassembled movement piece by piece while Pell watched, and Ingrid watched Pell, and Pell's mouth said, to the clerk, *escapement... pallets... where are the weights?* and the clerk's mouth answered, *workshop inventory, sir, she's got a delivery due,* and Pell's face said what his mouth carefully did not: that a delivery of clock weights, arriving at an observatory under warrant, was a thing he intended to intercept.

Ingrid went very still, and filed it, and hated how useful filing things was becoming.

Because that was the plan. That was tomorrow. Mara's scheme, Tomas's papers, the harbor gate at dawn: the letter riding inside a shipment of brass clock weights through the first checkpoint, ordinary freight among ordinary freight. And Pell had just, in her own hallway, told her he would be waiting for freight.

*Say nothing,* her father's face said, because he had followed the exchange too, because he was an astronomer and had spent fifty years reading very small signals at very large distances. He turned to Pell with his most academic expression and offered him tea, and Pell declined with the rudeness of a man who believed rudeness was authority, and the search went on for another hour and found, in total: one portfolio of bad harbor paintings, a tin of tobacco that made her father go briefly, sincerely pale, and forty years of Tomas Vey's archives, which they carried out in cartons like plunder while Tomas watched from the archive steps with his hands clasped behind his back and his face composed into something Ingrid recognized, belatedly, as grief.

* * *

By noon the gravel was quiet and the house stood ransacked and breathing, drawers half-closed, chairs at wrong angles, the particular atmosphere of a place that has been handled by strangers. Her father poured brandy into four cups without asking, and they drank in the kitchen because the dining table still wore its piles of sorted letters like a rash.

Tomas spoke first, his voice rough. "Forty years of cataloguing. Gone in a morning. And the worst of it is they've taken the dull volumes and left the ones that matter, because they can't tell the difference, and I don't know whether to laugh or lie down."

"Lie down later," her father said. "First tell me what they actually found."

"Nothing," Mara said. "They found nothing."

"They found the delivery schedule," Ingrid said aloud.

The kitchen went quiet the way it always did when she used her voice — four faces turning with that mixture of respect and discomfort she had decided, somewhere around her sixteenth year, to stop minding. She set down her cup and signed as she spoke, giving them both, because fear deserved redundancy:

*Pell asked the clerk where the weights were. Workshop inventory. Delivery due. He's going to watch the freight. Tomorrow, the harbor gate, he'll have men on the shipments — maybe every gate on the route. Your plan is in his pocket, Mara. He wrote it down himself.*

Mara's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. Ingrid watched her set it down very carefully, watched the fear come and get gripped and put to work, that swift internal machinery she was coming to admire the way her father admired a well-made transit instrument.

"Then the weights go through clean," Mara said slowly, "and the letter doesn't travel with them."

"Then where—" Tomas began.

Ingrid was already signing, hands steady, heart hammering its insolent tick. *Where does no one look? Where did they search today and find nothing?* She jabbed a thumb at her own chest, then swept her hand around the kitchen, the house, the schoolroom above.

*A deaf girl's drawings. A dead clock. An old man's planet machine.* She let the smile arrive, the sharp one, and saw Mara answer it with the ghost of her own. *They came with a warrant and left with bad watercolors. Let them come back tomorrow and search the silence again.*

Her father raised his cup to her — to her, first, before anyone — and Tomas laughed his dry rust of a laugh, and Mara said, "The customs house is tomorrow at noon. If the freight's watched, we'll need a second way for Tomas's logbook pages, and we'll need it tonight."

"There's always a second way," her father said. "There is astronomy in it, if nothing else — there's always another window."

But Ingrid was looking at Mara, and Mara was looking at the ceiling, in the direction of the dome, where a hollow brass sphere sat keeping its nineteen-year secret, and Ingrid signed one last thing, small, just for her:

*Whatever you're planning — I'm coming with you.*

Mara read it, and didn't argue, and that was its own kind of triumph — but below the triumph, ticking away under her ribs like the mechanism upstairs waiting for its spring, the fury was still there too, patient now, aimed: tomorrow the city would learn what happens when it mistakes a quiet woman for an absent one.

Above them, through two floors and a dome of iron and glass, the empty clock stood with its hands at 4:17, holding the hour for all of them.
