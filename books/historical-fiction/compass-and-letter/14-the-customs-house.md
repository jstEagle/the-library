## 14. The Customs House

The customs house at Vire Street had been built by men who wanted the city to feel small when it entered. Everything was stone and echo, arches tall enough to swallow a ship's mast, windows set so high the light fell in gray ropes that never quite reached the floor. Even the queue for papers moved in silence, as though the building charged a tax on speech.

Mara kept her eyes down and her hands still. That had been her posture since St. Ambrose's—the orphanage taught it early—but now there was a difference, and she felt it like a splinter under the skin: she was not invisible anymore. She was pretending.

Under her coat, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, lay the letter. Under Ingrid's shawl, folded into the false bottom of her sewing basket, lay the map fragments Tomas had spent two sleepless nights arranging. And walking beside her, hands jammed in his pockets like a man trying to look casual and failing in four directions at once, came Tomas Vey, muttering his route under his breath.

*Harbor gate. Customs house. Ministry stair.* Two down. One to go.

The harbor gate had cost them an hour and a miracle—a guard named Pell who had shared a dormitory with Mara twenty years ago and recognized her by the way she held her chin, which apparently was her mother's way too. He had looked at her a long moment, said *Lise Dunmore's girl* so quietly only she heard it, and waved the delivery of clock weights through without opening a single crate. Mara was still carrying the weight of that name, unopened, the way you carry something hot with no place to put it down.

Now there was the customs house.

"The clerk we paid is named Ferrow," Tomas murmured. "Third window from the left. Gray whiskers. He took eleven crowns to stamp us through without inspection."

"And if he's changed his mind?"

"Then I've been swindled before and survived it." But Tomas's jaw worked around the words like they were gristle. He had not slept. In the lamplight of the observatory kitchen, going over the route again and again, he'd looked older than his years—except when he talked about the logbook page he'd given her, and then he'd looked younger, scrubbed somehow, like a man who had finally set down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight until it left him.

Ingrid tugged Mara's sleeve. She signed, quick and clean: *Third window. Your turn.*

They had worked out a system on the walk over. Ingrid would go first—women with baskets were furniture to clerks, and deaf women even more so; no one interrogated what they assumed couldn't hear them. Then Tomas, whose forged transit papers were his best work, good enough to pass at arm's length. Then Mara last, with the letter, because if anything went wrong at the front of the line the person carrying it needed time to turn around.

*You'll be through in ten minutes,* Ingrid signed. *Then we buy you dinner that isn't Halvorsen's porridge.*

*Deal,* Mara signed back, and Ingrid smiled—the whole smile, the one that made her look like weather about to change—and walked into the customs house with her basket on her hip and her chin exactly where Mara's was.

Through the glass, Mara watched her reach the third window. The gray-whiskered clerk barely glanced up. A stamp rose and fell. Ingrid passed through into the far hall, where the light from the high windows caught her and turned her briefly gold, and then she was through, waiting on the ministry side, real as anything.

"Next," said the second window's clerk.

Tomas stepped forward. Mara drifted toward the wall, close enough to see, far enough to look unconnected. This too was craft. You learned it in orphanages and clock shops both: how to stand so that no one's eye snagged on you.

Tomas laid his papers on the counter. The clerk picked them up. And then the clerk did not put them down.

He read them. Actually read them, turning the first sheet over, holding the second up against the light. His mouth moved around numbers. Then he raised two fingers—not a gesture, just a summons—and another man detached himself from the shadowed end of the counter hall with the unhurried gait of someone who had been told this might happen.

Mara's stomach dropped through the stone floor.

The third window's clerk—Ferrow, gray whiskers, eleven crowns—did not look up from his ledger. He looked very hard at not looking up, which was its own confession. The money hadn't bought passage. It had bought a list, and Tomas's name was on it, and somewhere behind the marble a man named Crane was paying for names.

The second clerk spoke to Tomas. Mara couldn't hear the words across the hall, but she could read the shape of them: papers, come around the side door, just a formality. Tomas's shoulders did the thing they did when he was frightened and refusing to show it—he squared them like a man buttoning a coat against wind.

And then, before he followed the second man toward the side door, Tomas did something worse than anything the clerks could have done.

He looked at her.

Not at the letter. Not at the map. At *her*—a quick, deliberate glance, level and calm, and he shook his head once, small, and touched two fingers to his chest, and mouthed three words she had no trouble reading through forty feet of marble: *Go. Don't wait.*

Then he was gone around the corner, and the line shuffled forward, and the customs house swallowed him the way it swallowed everyone: without interest.

* * *

She got ten steps toward the exit before her legs stopped working.

It wasn't drama. There was nothing dramatic about it. Her body simply declined, the way a clock declines when a tooth breaks somewhere deep in the train—one moment the mechanism turns, the next it doesn't, and you cannot argue with it. She stood in the gray rope-light between the second and third windows, holding a letter against her ribs, and understood that she had arrived at the exact center of the thing she had been walking toward for days.

The letter or the man.

If she walked out that door and up to the ministry stair, the inquiry would get Lise Dunmore's letter. The commissioners would read it. Her mother's name would enter the record, and the hour would finish striking, and everything they had bled for these two weeks would arrive where it was meant to go. That was the mission. That was the arithmetic. Every one of them had agreed to it in the dome at midnight: the letter mattered more than any single one of us. Tomas had said it first, and said it like a man reciting penance.

But Tomas had also shaken his head at her through the glass and said *go*, and she thought about why. He wasn't being noble, or not only. She had seen his face when the clerk's fingers summoned the other man. She had seen what lived underneath the squared shoulders. Tomas Vey had spent thirteen years being the man who burned the page—who did the convenient thing in 1881 and called it survival ever after. He was not shaking his head at her to protect the mission.

He was shaking his head at her because he believed, all the way down, that he was the expendable part. That old men with burnt reputations were the ballast you cut loose. That history had already written him off and the kindest thing was to stop arguing with the sentence.

Well. Mara Dunmore had spent twenty-six years believing the same thing about herself. She knew precisely how that lie was built, because she had helped build it, plank by plank, and she knew the sound it made when it broke.

It sounded like this: a deaf girl across the hall, watching her through the crowd, signing one word.

*Mara?*

Ingrid had come back inside—of course she had, she'd seen the whole thing through the window from the ministry side, lip-reading the clerks' shapes at forty feet better than Mara had managed at ten. She pushed through the queue, basket tight against her hip, and stopped in front of Mara, and her face asked the question again without signs.

"They're taking him through the side door," Mara said, low. "Detainment, probably. Papers 'under review.'"

Ingrid's eyes flashed. She signed fast, angry: *Crane's list. Ferrow sold the list, not the stamps.*

"I know."

*Then we go. Now. You carry it.*

"We." Mara caught her hands. "No. Listen to me."

*I am listening. I always listen. Nobody notices.*

A laugh tried to get out of Mara sideways and died. She bent closer, so Ingrid could read her lips clearly, and said each word like setting a tooth in a wheel.

"You take the letter. You take the map fragments. You go up the ministry stair and you put that letter in front of the commissioners even if you have to put it in your testimony in chalk. You're the only one of us they don't have a file on. They think you're nobody. Let them keep thinking it until quarter past four."

Ingrid pulled back, affronted, furious, magnificent. *And you?*

"I'm getting Tomas."

*Why? He said go. He wants you to leave him.*

"Yes," Mara said. "That's why."

Something moved across Ingrid's face—recognition, maybe, the same vertigo Mara herself had felt standing in front of a stopped clock at 4:17. The moment when a person you love shows you the lie they believe about themselves, and you realize you cannot leave it standing, because you know exactly how heavy it is.

Ingrid looked at the letter in Mara's hands. Then she held out the basket.

They worked fast, two women trained by clocks and telescopes, which is to say by patience. The oilcloth packet slid into the basket's false bottom beneath the map fragments, beneath the chalk and the needles, beneath a layer of darning that would make any searching official's eyes glaze. Ingrid signed the plan as they went: *Ministry stair. Quarter past four. If you're not there—*

"I'll be there."

*If you're not there,* Ingrid repeated, stubborn, *I tell them everything anyway. All of it. My father writes it down while I sign it and they can't stop me because they'll be too busy being shocked that I have opinions.*

"You do have opinions."

*All of them correct. Go.*

And then Ingrid Halvorsen took Mara's face in both hands—briefly, firmly, the way you steady an instrument before a measurement—and kissed her forehead, and turned, and walked out of the customs house with the letter of a dead cartographer under her darning and her chin exactly where her grandmother's must have been, and Mara stood in the gray light and let herself feel it for exactly one breath:

She was not doing this alone. She had never, since the harbor gate, been doing this alone.

Then she turned toward the side door.

* * *

The corridor beyond was narrower than the hall, meant for function—stone flags, gas brackets hissing, a smell of ink and wet wool. Two doors down, a uniformed man sat at a deal table with a ledger open, and beyond him, through an inner doorway, she could see Tomas in a wooden chair, hat on his knee, being questioned by a thick-necked official who turned the forged papers over and over like a man looking for the trick in them.

"—stated purpose, cataloguing, but your transit seal is dated two days after the restriction order, Mr.—what was the name?"

"Vey."

"Vey," the official repeated, with the satisfaction of a man who has found the trick. "Funny thing. We've had a circular about a Vey."

At the table, the uniformed guard noticed her. "Madam? Business?"

"Papers," Mara said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. She had spent her life making herself small in doorways; now she used every hour of it, the shy apprentice, the repairwoman, the girl from nowhere. "I'm meant to meet my employer. He's forgotten his luncheon parcel." She lifted the paper-wrapped bundle she'd assembled from the counter of her own bag in the corridor—bread, an apple, a cold sausage, arranged with heartbreaking domesticity. "Only, they've told me he's detained, and I don't understand why. Tomas wouldn't trouble anyone. He's an archivist. Retired."

The official looked up at her through the doorway, and here was the danger, here was the tooth that could break the train: recognition. Crane's circular might have her description too.

But the official didn't see a courier of contraband letters. He saw a tired young woman in a mended coat holding a luncheon parcel, and something in his face—some memory of wives, mothers, sisters who waited outside official buildings with parcels—softened by a degree.

"He's not lunching anywhere today, madam. His papers are under review pending verification."

"Verification of what? He's seventy years old. What can he possibly—" She let her voice crack, just slightly. Craft. Everything was craft. "Who do I appeal to? There must be someone."

"There's an inquiry process," the official said, with the flatness of a man reciting a wall. "Forms. In due course."

"In due course." Mara looked at Tomas. Tomas looked back, and there it was again—the head shake, faint, almost invisible, *go, girl, go*—and she looked directly at him and refused it. Out loud, to the official, she said: "Then I'll wait. He's my family. We don't leave people behind."

The word landed in the room like a struck bell.

Tomas's face did something she had never seen it do. It came apart, just at the edges—the careful archivist's composure cracking along lines that had been drawn in 1881 and never repaired—and he had to look down at his hat brim for a moment, and when he looked up his eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Family," the official repeated, uncomfortable. Men in stone buildings were equipped for smugglers and radicals; grief in a luncheon parcel was outside their training.

"My late father's colleague," Mara amended smoothly, because she was a liar now, apparently, and getting better at it. "The only family I have left. Am I to leave him sitting in a chair while clerks lose his papers?"

The official sighed. He looked at the guard. The guard looked at the ceiling. Somewhere above them all, in the ministry on the hill, a parliamentary inquiry was convening on a question of cartography and treason, and the machinery of the state ground forward with a luncheon parcel standing in front of it.

"Verification takes until the afternoon session reconvenes," the official said finally, which meant nothing, which meant everything, which meant: *we have orders to hold him past four o'clock, and we are telling you so in the language of forms.* "You may wait in the corridor. There's a bench."

"Thank you," Mara said. She sat down on the bench. She unwrapped the parcel and began, deliberately, slowly, to eat the apple, as though she had all the time in the world and no letter burning a hole through a basket across the city.

Through the doorway, Tomas watched her. After a while he stopped shaking his head.

"Mr. Vey," the official resumed, shuffling his circulars, "let's begin again. Where were you on the night of the eleventh?"

Mara bit the apple and counted seconds against her pulse, the way she counted a pendulum's beat, and thought: *Hold on, old man. Ingrid's climbing your stairs right now. Four o'clock is coming. The clock will strike, and when it does, we will all of us be in that room—every stray, every coward, every daughter—because that is the whole point of the mechanism. Every tooth turns together or none of it turns at all.*

Down the corridor, a door opened, and boots approached—new boots, expensive ones, with a familiar unhurried stride.

Mara did not need to look up to know who had come to see Tomas Vey. But she looked anyway, because she was done not looking.

Aldous Crane filled the doorway, smiling like a bill of sale.
