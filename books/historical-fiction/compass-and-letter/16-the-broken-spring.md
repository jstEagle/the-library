## 16. The Broken Spring

The cab let them off at the bottom of Observatory Hill because the horse would not go further in the wind. Mara paid the driver with Halvorsen's coins and did not wait for change. She took the path at a run, Tomas laboring behind her, his breath coming in ragged white flags in the dark, and she did not slow down for him and hated herself a little for that and kept running anyway.

The observatory rose against the sky like a fist. One window lit — the dome, where Ingrid would be — and every other window dark, and the dark told her things before she reached the door. Halvorsen had gone to the ministry to bargain for Tomas's release, which meant he was not here, which meant the house stood half-empty, which meant anyone who wanted to come inside tonight would find nothing standing in their way.

She smelled it before she saw it. Lamp oil, faintly, under the salt of the wind.

"No," she said, out loud, to no one, and shouldered through the door.

The workshop was intact. The bench was intact. Her tools lay exactly where she had left them, which was the first wrong thing, because Mara never left her tools exactly anywhere; they scattered when she worked, they lived in drifts, and now they sat in a neat row as though a stranger had tidied them. She crossed the room without touching anything and put her ear — her hands, her palms flat against the clock case — to the great regulator, and felt it the way a physician feels a chest.

No beat.

She got the case open in forty seconds, faster than she ever had, her fingers working the panels by memory, and swung the lantern up into the works, and there it was.

The mainspring had been broken. Not snapped by age, not failed by fatigue — broken. The fracture was bright and clean at one end and torn and ugly at the other, and the torn end had been wound down hard against the barrel wall, which meant it had gone under tension, which meant someone had winched the great spring tight past all sense and let it tear itself apart inside its own housing. Eleven years of careful preservation, ended in ten minutes of deliberate cruelty. And around the wreckage, everywhere, the fine gray dust of shattered steel teeth — the winding gear's pinion had gone with it, chewed to gravel.

Lamp oil, too. A cupful poured over the base of the case and not lit. Not lit because burning the clock would prove sabotage. Leaving it whole and dead would only prove failure. *Your clockmaker could not do the work,* the broken spring said. *Perhaps your clockmaker was never any good.*

Mara stood very still and let the cold walk up through her boots and into her spine.

"Mara?" Ingrid's voice came down the stairwell — Ingrid's voice was always slightly too loud, pitched by habit for people who weren't listening — and then Ingrid herself, chalk dust on her sleeves, her face going from hope to question to understanding in the space of three steps. She looked at the clock. She looked at Mara's face. "How bad."

"Bad," Mara said, and made herself say the rest of it plainly, because Ingrid read lips and deserved lips that didn't flinch. "The mainspring is destroyed. The winding pinion too. I don't have a spare. There is no spare. That spring was cast in 1859 by a man who is dead, and the foundry that rolled the steel closed six years ago." She heard her own voice going thin and stopped it. "The inquiry convenes at four. It is now—" she checked the small watch pinned inside her sleeve, the one thing she owned that had never belonged to anyone else— "twenty past eleven."

Ingrid set down the slate she'd been carrying and folded her arms, and Mara watched her think. People underestimated Ingrid Halvorsen the way they underestimated locked doors — they assumed that because she heard nothing, she knew nothing, and Mara had watched her take apart that assumption the way a crow takes apart a beetle, methodically and without mercy. Now Ingrid crossed to the workbench, picked up a piece of chalk, and wrote in her big square hand:

**WHAT DO YOU NEED.**

"Steel," Mara said. "Spring steel, thick enough for a barrel this size, long enough to give me eleven days of run after winding, tempered so it doesn't brittle. Or a miracle."

Ingrid wrote again: **TOMAS'S ARCHIVE HAS THE OLD REPAIR LEDGERS. EVERY CLOCK THE OBSERVATORY EVER OWNED.**

Mara stared at her. Then at Tomas, who had come up behind them both, hat in his hands, breathing hard, taking in the open case and the ruined spring with the expression of a man watching a second funeral.

"The ledgers," Mara said slowly. "Every decommissioned clock. Where are they stored?"

"Sub-basement," Tomas said. "Racks and racks. Half of them are junk, but—" he stopped, and she watched the archivist wake up in him, the man who remembered where everything slept. "There were two regulator clocks retired in the eighties when the dome instruments were rebuilt. They'd have springs of comparable mass. If they weren't stripped for brass—"

"They'll be stripped for brass," Mara said. "But nobody strips the spring, because nobody knows what a spring is worth except another clockmaker. Go. Both of you. Bring lamps. Bring everything down to the last screw, because I don't know yet what I'll need and I'd rather carry up too much than climb those stairs twice."

They went. Mara stayed.

She stayed because someone had to look at the wound before anyone tried to heal it, and because her hands needed work or they would start shaking. She cleared the lantern glass and held the light into the barrel and read the break the way she'd learned to read everything — closely, hungrily, assuming nothing.

The spring had been broken by someone who understood springs. That was the part she kept circling back to, the part with teeth. A vandal would have smashed the dial, bent the hands, tipped the case over. This had been done by a person who knew that a regulator clock is not its case or its face but its heartbeat, and who went straight for the heart, and who chose a method — overwinding, deliberate, controlled — that left no tool marks and no fingerprints and no story anyone would believe. *Crane didn't send a thug,* she thought. *He sent a tradesman.* Someone paid by the hour, competent, discreet. The kind of quiet violence the powerful purchase when they want a thing to look like an accident of time itself.

Well. Crane had made one mistake, and the mistake was arithmetic. He had calculated how long a repair should take and concluded it could not be done. He had never watched an apprentice in an orphanage mending the matron's mantel clock overnight with a file, a candle, and stolen wire, because keeping her head down had never meant keeping her hands idle. Mara Dunmore had been making something out of almost-nothing since she was seven years old. This was only a larger almost-nothing than usual.

*Four hours,* she told herself. *You will not get four hours. Work as if you have two.*

Her hands had stopped shaking. Good. Desperation, she discovered, was a material like any other: useless raw, but under heat and hammer it could be worked into something with an edge.

* * *

Tomas and Ingrid came up laden like pack animals — Tomas with a rack of disassembled movements balanced on his shoulder, Ingrid with two clocks slung in a blanket across her back, her chin set at the angle that meant she had carried more than anyone and would hear no commentary about it. They laid it all out across the workshop floor, and Mara knelt in the middle of the hoard like a queen of scrap and began the sorting.

Two retired regulators. The first was hopeless — its spring already pulled, its steel blue with rust, good for nothing but weight. The second opened up under her screwdriver like a held breath released.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you beauty."

The spring inside was a twin of the broken one in everything but length — same rolling mill's mark, same temper, same thickness, cut perhaps a hand-span shorter. Shorter meant less reserve. Shorter meant the repaired clock would run perhaps thirty hours on a full wind instead of eleven days. Shorter meant nothing at all, because the inquiry was in four hours and the rest was somebody else's problem.

"I can splice it," she said, and heard Tomas make a small skeptical sound, and turned on him. "I can. You weld a new hook to each end — hard solder, not soft, it has to hold under full torque — you anneal the joint so it doesn't crack, you re-temper, and then you pray to whichever god governs steel. It's not elegant. It's not even good practice. But it will hold for one striking of the hour, and after that it can fail in the street for all I care."

"And the pinion?" Tomas asked. "The winding gear is gravel, you said."

Ingrid was already holding up the other clock's movement, turning it toward the light, tapping the winding arbor. She caught Mara's eye and grinned — quick, fierce — and Mara felt the grin land somewhere under her ribs and catch fire.

"Yes," Mara said. "That yes. Take it out. Carefully — the teeth are fine as needles."

They worked. The night organized itself around the work the way water organizes around a stone. Mara took the center of the floor with fire and flux and the spliced spring, heating the joint to cherry-red, laying the solder, watching the metal flow the way she watched everything — with her whole self — then plunging it into oil and listening to it hiss its anger away. Tomas cleaned parts at the bench, unhurried and exact, an old man's hands finding an old competence, sorting screws into saucers by some taxonomy only archivists understood. And Ingrid — Ingrid became her second pair of eyes. When Mara's hands were buried in the barrel, Ingrid held the lamp. When Mara needed to test the temper of a strip of steel, Ingrid flexed it between her own strong fingers while Mara watched the bend, and nodded, and Ingrid felt the nod without looking up, because Ingrid missed nothing, least of all the language of hands.

Once, near two o'clock, Mara realized her eyes were wet and her fingers hadn't stopped working, and that grief had gotten into the mechanism somehow — into the rhythm of her filing, the stroke of the burnisher, the little circular motions her mother's hands must also have made once over brass and steel, a hundred years of women's hands compressed into one night's repair. Lise Dunmore had drawn coastlines. Mara mended clocks. Same work, maybe. Measuring the world. Keeping it honest. Making sure the lines met where they promised to meet.

She wiped her eyes on her shoulder and kept filing.

At half past two the splice went into the barrel. At quarter to three the reassembled winding gear took its first cautious turn, and Mara felt the resistance come up through the key — smooth, even, alive — and had to close her eyes for a moment against the sheer animal relief of it.

"It turns," she said. "It turns."

Ingrid, who couldn't hear the click and whirr that were filling the room like rain, put her palm flat against the case instead, and felt it, and smiled the way hearing people smile at music.

Then the clock struck — the trial strike, the half-hour, the single flat note of the bell — and the sound went through the floor and up through Mara's boots and she thought, absurdly, *it sounds like my mother clearing her throat before she says something important*, a woman she could not remember, whose voice she had invented entirely out of need, and the invention rang in the bell all the same.

Three o'clock. One hour.

Mara began the final assembly with the deliberateness of a surgeon closing. Hands on the dial. Bezel seated. Case panels swung shut, one by one, until the great regulator stood whole in the lamplight, ticking, its pendulum swinging its patient arc as though the past twelve years had been a brief nap.

Tomas brought the letter up from its hiding place in the flour bin. The map, wrapped in oilcloth. The scorched logbook with its ash-stained margin. He laid them on the table between them like cards played in a final hand.

"There's just the matter," he said quietly, "of getting them to the ministry."

And that was when Ingrid, who had been watching the window the way she always watched windows — reading the street, reading the light, reading the world that forgot she was reading it — went very still. She touched Mara's arm and pointed.

Down the hill, along the harbor road, a carriage was climbing. No lamps lit, but the moon gave enough: a closed carriage, moving fast, drawn by a horse being driven harder than any sensible person drove a horse at three in the morning. And behind it, further down, two more.

Mara looked at the clock, ticking beautifully, honestly, uselessly beautiful. She looked at the letter in its wax seal. She looked at the people the night had given her — an old coward with a straightened spine, a deaf girl who heard everything — and felt desperation finish its transformation into something colder and cleaner.

"They're not coming for the clock," she said. "The clock is fixed. They're too late for the clock. They're coming to make sure we don't leave this house." She swept the letter, the map, and the logbook off the table and into Ingrid's arms. "So we don't all leave together. Ingrid — the garden wall, the goat track, the back way to the ministry road. You know it better than anyone; you've walked it since you were six. Take these. Don't stop for anyone, don't speak to anyone, and if they stop you, be deaf and be empty-handed and be sixteen years old, because they won't search what they pity."

Ingrid hugged the bundle to her chest, and for one second her composure cracked and Mara saw underneath it, the frightened girl, the girl whose father was still somewhere on the night roads, the girl about to run alone through a dark city carrying the only truth anyone would accept.

"You won't be alone," Mara told her, shaping every word so there could be no mistake. "We'll be right behind you. And at four-seventeen, that clock is going to ring out the truest hour this city has ever heard — whether I'm standing in the chamber or not. Now *go*."

Ingrid went — through the kitchen, into the dark of the garden, gone like a swallow.

Mara turned to the window. The first carriage had reached the foot of the hill and begun to climb.

"Tomas," she said, "put the kettle on. If we're going to be arrested, let them find us civilized."

He laughed — actually laughed, a rusty astonished sound — and went for the kettle, and Mara stood with her hand resting on the warm wooden flank of the clock she had raised from the dead, feeling its heartbeat against her palm, and counted the seconds until the knocking came.
