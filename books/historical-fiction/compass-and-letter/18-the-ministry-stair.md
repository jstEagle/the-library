## 18. The Ministry Stair

The Ministry of Records stood at the top of the city like a man who had never once been told no. White stone, black iron, a stair of forty-one steps—I counted them, because counting was what my hands did when my chest went tight—rising in two broad flights to doors tall enough to admit a carriage and the horse that pulled it.

Ingrid walked beside me with the satchel strapped flat against her ribs, under her coat, where the letter and the scorched logbook rode against her heartbeat. The map fragments were already inside, chalked over on both sides, folded into drawings of tides and stars that no clerk would think to unmake. We had learned that much from the raid: men searched for what they assumed could speak. They did not search silence.

Tomas was still sore from the holding room, one wrist wrapped, moving like a man apologizing to his own body for the trouble. Halvorsen had gone ahead an hour before us, alone, in his good coat, because a commissioner's door opened faster for a name than for a crowd. Our plan was simple and therefore fragile: arrive separately, arrive early, arrive before Crane's people could find a third door to close.

We came up Harbor Street through the fish market, past the customs house where Tomas had been taken and released, past the harbor gate where a guard named Pell had said my mother's name out loud as if handing me something I hadn't known I'd dropped. The city felt different today. Not kinder. Just—watchful, the way a clock is watchful, all its parts agreed on one thing.

The stair was not empty.

Aldous Crane stood at the base of it, alone, hat in hand, gray at the temples now in a way that looked arranged rather than earned. Two of his men leaned against the balustrade further up, close enough to matter, far enough to pretend they didn't.

I stopped three steps from the bottom. Ingrid stopped with me. Behind us, Tomas drew breath through his teeth.

"Miss Dunmore." Crane said it gently, the way you'd address someone you'd rehearsed addressing. "May I have a moment? It won't cost you more than that."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in my body first, because the record should hold it even if no commissioner ever reads it.

My legs wanted to run. Not forward—away. Twelve years of orphanage training rose in me like water finding its level: make yourself small, make yourself dull, let the moment pass over you the way weather passes over a stone. There is a version of me that has spent her whole life stepping sideways out of rooms, and she woke up on those stairs with her heart going like a loose escapement and every instinct screaming *be nothing, be nothing, he hasn't seen you yet.*

But he had seen me. That was the whole point of him standing there.

"Mr. Crane," I said.

"You've been busy." He turned the hat in his hands. "I'll be brief, since I know you're a woman who values time. You've been told a story about your mother. A tragic one. A wronged cartographer, sold out by powerful men, drowned at 4:17 by the clock's own cruel arithmetic." He smiled without warmth. "It's a good story. Stories like it win inquiries."

"And the true one?"

"The true one," he said, "is that Lise Dunmore was not drowned."

The word landed somewhere under my ribs and sat there.

"She was arrested," Crane continued, quiet, almost kind, which was somehow worse. "Not by us. By the Admiralty of the country whose navy bought her charts—no. Let me be precise, since precision is your trade. She was *taken*. She lived. For some years, at least. There are records. There is—" he paused, savoring it, a man laying a card face-up, "—a place. An island hospital, north. She was alive as recently as 1889, Miss Dunmore. Eight years after your city decided she was dead. I can give you the file. The whole file. Names, dates, the ship that carried her."

The stair seemed to tilt. I gripped the cold iron rail because the world had begun doing what the clock used to do—stopping at a bad hour and refusing to move.

Alive. My mother had been alive while I ate orphanage bread and learned to keep my head down and told myself history happened elsewhere. Alive while I stopped believing she'd come back. Alive while I built a life around the shape of her absence, and the absence had been a lie with furniture in it.

"Why tell me this now?" My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Because I'm offering you a trade." He stepped closer, and up close his courtesy had seams in it, like cheap upholstery. "Walk away. Today. Take Ingrid and go home. The inquiry convenes without you; the commissioners hear a great deal of procedural noise and reach no conclusions; the Halvorsen Observatory keeps its funding and its name. And you—" he held out a card, cream-colored, an address written on it in a fine hand, "—get the truth about your mother. All of it. Perhaps, if she lives still, the chance to find her. Think of it. Everything you've wanted since you were six years old, in exchange for one afternoon of silence."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being invisible: you get very good at watching people when they think no one is looking.

So I watched Aldous Crane while he made his offer, and I saw what he thought he was hiding. His thumb pressed white against the hatband. His eyes flicked—not to me, but past me, to Ingrid, to the coat, to the shape beneath it. He wasn't offering me my mother. He was buying time, and he was afraid, and fear was leaking out of him the way oil leaks from a cracked case: slowly, invisibly, until everything it touches stops running clean.

He had done the arithmetic, same as me. If the letter reached the inquiry, he was finished. If it didn't, he survived, and a girl with ink-stained fingers was a small price. He had chosen the girl. He had probably chosen her weeks ago, back when the clock first stopped being a clock and started being a witness.

And underneath all of it—the offer, the card, the careful voice—he believed it would work. Because it had always worked. Because girls like me took trades. Girls like me had been taking worse trades their whole lives: silence for supper, smallness for shelter, other people's versions of their mothers for no version at all.

I looked at the card in his hand. Cream-colored. Fine paper. An address that might have held my mother's last known bed, her last known morning, her last known breath or word or window.

"I have a question," I said.

"Of course."

"If she was alive in 1889—who kept that from me? Not you. You weren't powerful enough to matter to a girl in an orphanage. Someone smaller did it. Someone who filed her away and locked the drawer." I watched his face do something complicated. "Was it you, Mr. Vey?" I didn't turn around. I didn't need to. "No. You burned a page. You didn't build the lie." Back to Crane. "Then it was the patrons. Your patrons. The same men whose names are in the letter riding against my friend's ribs right now."

Crane's smile thinned.

"So here's my counter-offer," I said. "You give me the file. Now. Free. And I walk up these stairs anyway, and I put it on the table next to the letter, and the commissioners read both. That way everyone gets what they want. You get the truth out—you clearly want it out, or you wouldn't be carrying it around like a stone—and I get my mother's name in the record where it belongs. Everyone wins."

"That isn't—"

"It's the only trade on the table where nobody has to disappear," I said. "You've made a career of people disappearing, Mr. Crane. I've made a career of things that refuse to. Clocks come back. Springs get replaced. Escapements get cleaned. You'd know that if you'd ever fixed anything instead of just owning it."

His jaw tightened. One of the men on the balustrade shifted his weight, and I heard Tomas behind me say, very quietly, "Mara," in the tone of a man watching weather turn.

"No," I said, to both of them.

* * *

What I did next was so small that Crane's men didn't even understand it until it was finished.

I reached out and took the card from his hand.

He let me—startled, maybe, or maybe some part of him relieved to be rid of the weight of it. I looked at the address. North. An island. A hospital. Words that meant my mother had woken on mornings I hadn't known existed, in a place I could find on a map, in a country I could reach on a ship.

Then I folded the card once, precisely, the way I fold anything that matters, and put it in my pocket next to my tools.

"Thank you," I said.

"You're still going up there." It wasn't a question. His voice had lost its upholstery entirely.

"Yes."

"You understand what happens if you do. To the observatory. To the old man. To your archivist friend with his burned page and his bad wrist. I don't need to threaten any of you, Miss Dunmore. I only need to stop being generous."

"I know," I said. And I did know—that was the terrible, clarifying thing. I knew exactly what it would cost, because Halvorsen had paid part of it already at his own front door, refusing Crane's money with the calm of a man setting down something heavy. Cost was not a reason. Cost was just the price, and prices were for paying.

"My mother spent eleven years as a rumor," I said. "Drowned. Lost. Unrecorded. Every hour of that, somebody chose the rumor because it was easier than the truth. I'm done choosing easy." I looked at him straight on, and for once in my life I didn't shrink, didn't soften, didn't make myself into something a powerful man could step over without noticing. "You think you're offering me a trade. You're offering me the same deal she got. Quiet in exchange for vanishing. I've been vanishing my whole life, Mr. Crane. It doesn't suit me."

Then I stepped past him.

Not around him—past him, close enough that my shoulder nearly brushed his coat, close enough that he could have grabbed my arm if he'd dared, and we both knew he didn't dare, not on a public stair with witnesses and a commissioner's door forty-one steps above us. I climbed. Ingrid fell in beside me without a word, her chin up, her eyes bright and furious and alive. Tomas came behind us, breathing hard, his wrapped wrist cradled, and I heard him mutter something that sounded like a prayer or a curse or possibly both.

Behind us, Crane said my name. Once. Just the surname, sharp, the way you'd call a dog.

I didn't stop.

Forty steps. Thirty-eight. The men on the balustrade looked at us and then at Crane and then, decisively, at nothing, because men like them only act when they're certain, and certainty had just walked up a staircase without them. Thirty steps. Twenty-five. The doors grew larger. The stone grew colder under my palms where I gripped the rail. Twenty. Fifteen.

At ten steps from the top, Crane's voice came again, quieter now, and it was the quietness that frightened me—not anger but arithmetic, a man recalculating.

"You'll wish you'd taken the trade," he said.

"Probably," I called back, without turning. "But wishing is honest work. Vanishing isn't."

* * *

The doors were taller than they'd looked from the street. Black iron handles worn smooth by a century of hands. Through the frosted glass I could see the long hall beyond, the commission chamber doors, the brass plate with the day's business engraved upon it, and somewhere in that building a room full of men waiting to decide whether my mother had existed.

Ingrid touched my elbow. When I turned, she signed—slowly, deliberately, the way she did when she wanted to be certain I caught every word: *He'll send someone. After.*

*I know,* I signed back.

*Good,* she signed, and something in her face was almost a grin. *Let him.*

Tomas came up beside us, wheezing slightly, and patted my shoulder with his good hand. "That," he said, "was either the bravest or stupidest thing I've witnessed in thirty years of archives."

"Which?"

"Both. Definitely both." He glanced back down the stair, where Crane still stood at the bottom, hat crushed in his fist, watching us with the expression of a man watching a clock he cannot stop. "He'll move now. Whatever he was going to do quietly, he'll do loudly. We have minutes, Mara. Maybe less."

"Then we spend them well."

I pushed the door.

It swung open onto marble and echo and the smell of paper and lamp oil and old decisions, and ahead of us, at the end of the hall, the commission chamber doors stood closed with a clerk posted beside them checking names against a list. Somewhere deeper in the building—a sound I felt before I understood it, a vibration coming up through the stone floor and into my boots, into my bones, into the place where my mother's blood and my father's absence and twelve years of orphanage bread had made me—

The ministry clock. Striking the quarter hour.

Counting down toward 4:17 whether anyone was ready or not.

We had made it past the third checkpoint. Now we had to survive the room behind the doors.
