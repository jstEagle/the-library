## 21. The Clock Keeps Time

The observatory reopened on a Tuesday in March, because Tomas insisted that institutions which reopened on Mondays did so out of arrogance, and my father agreed with him mostly to have something to argue about that wasn't the inquiry report.

I stood at the gate an hour before anyone was supposed to arrive and read the new plaque through the iron bars. It was bronze, and it said HALVORSEN OBSERVATORY — EST. 1867, and beneath it, smaller: REOPENED BY ORDER OF PARLIAMENTARY COMMISSION. There had been a long argument about whether the second line should mention Lise Dunmore by name. My father wanted it to. The commission's clerk said plaques of a certain size could only hold so many letters. In the end her name went inside, on the wall of the entry hall, where people would actually stop and read it, and where I could stand next to it and feel the letters' shapes without having to explain myself to anyone.

That was the thing nobody understood about being deaf in a hearing world's buildings: everything had a sound it also had a shape. A bell had its vibration in the floorboards. An argument had its shadow in the way shoulders set. A name carved into a wall had a weight you could put your hand against, like a door.

Mara arrived first, because Mara always arrived first now, carrying her toolbox the way other women carried handbags and walking past me so quietly that I only knew she was there when the air moved. She touched my elbow twice—*good morning* in the little code we'd made, two taps for good, one for bad—and held up the toolbox and grinned.

She looked different since the inquiry. Not taller. Nothing about Mara got taller; she'd spent nineteen years learning to take up less room than she occupied. But she walked like someone whose feet knew where they were going, and when she passed mirrors she didn't flinch the way she used to, quick as a bird startled off a wire.

*Did you fix it?* I signed, because her hands were free enough to answer.

*Almost,* she signed back. *One more day.*

She'd been saying *one more day* for eleven days. I didn't mind. There are clocks you repair and clocks you court, and the great regulator in the stairwell hall was the courting kind. It had stopped at 4:17 once for eleven years and again for three days after Crane's man snapped the mainspring, and Mara treated it now with the wary tenderness of someone who has learned that the things she loves can be broken on purpose by people who mean it.

My father came down the hill at half past, hat crooked, arguing already.

"—and if Vey has rearranged the archive again according to his own private cosmology, I want it stated for the record that the alphabet is not a suggestion—"

*He'll hear you,* I signed.

"He never listens, that's the tragedy." Then he kissed the top of my head, which he had done every day since the raid, as though confirming I was still there, still his, still unbroken by men who assumed silence meant emptiness. I let him. I had decided, sometime during those terrible weeks, that being loved fussily was a condition I could live with.

Tomas was already inside, of course. He had been living half in the archive since before we were allowed to be, cataloguing by candlelight with the shameless air of a man doing penance and enjoying it. When my father found him, Tomas straightened a shelf that didn't need straightening and said, "The alphabet, Professor, is a colonial imposition on the natural order of knowledge."

"The natural order of knowledge is alphabetical, you disgrace, that's what makes it useful—"

I left them to it. Some arguments were household pets; you fed them daily and they kept the place feeling lived-in.

* * *

The public came at ten. I had expected a handful—the curious, the sentimental, a newspaperman or two—but they came all morning, a slow river of them up the hill through the wet grass, and I stood by the entry door with my chalkboard and watched their faces do the same thing over and over.

They came in cold. They saw the clock.

They went quiet.

The regulator stood in the stairwell hall where it had always stood, but it wasn't the same instrument. Its case had been stripped and re-oiled until the walnut glowed like something alive. Its face was clean. And behind the glass, the pendulum swung with the deep unhurried patience of a heart that intended to go on beating whether anyone watched or not. Mara had re-engraved the maker's plate herself and added a second line beneath the original: REPAIRED BY THE HANDS THAT FOUND IT.

People read that line. I watched a woman in a green coat read it and then look at Mara, who was pretending very hard to be adjusting something unimportant nearby, and the woman didn't say anything—people rarely did, they just wanted to look—but she pressed her hand flat against the case for a moment, the way you'd touch a church wall, and then she went up the stairs to see the telescopes.

By noon I'd filled four slate pages answering questions. *Yes, this is the clock from the inquiry. Yes, it really struck 4:17 while she testified. No, I don't know how she timed it. Yes, she really is that young.* That last question I answered honestly: *she is exactly the right age to have done it.* People seemed satisfied. There is a kind of authority in small women who don't explain themselves, and Mara had become, without noticing, the most authoritative person in this city.

A boy of about nine planted himself in front of me while his mother read the wall. He pointed at my slate, then at his own ears, then made a gesture halfway between a question and an apology.

I wrote: *I'm deaf. It's fine. I hear plenty.*

He frowned at that—it was the kind of sentence adults found charming and children found suspicious—so I took his hand and put it flat against the clock case, low, near the base of the pendulum.

His whole face changed.

That was the lesson, the one I'd tried to give Mara in the dark of the dome weeks ago and had never needed words for: the clock doesn't care which of your senses finds it. It beats into wood and floor and bone. You only have to be touching something real.

When the boy let go he was grinning, and he wrote on my slate, in enormous childish letters: IT SOUNDS LIKE A GIANT'S HEART.

*I know,* I wrote back. *I've been listening to it my whole life.*

* * *

In the afternoon the crowd thinned and the four of us did the thing we would do every day after, which was nothing dramatic whatsoever.

My father climbed to the dome and uncovered the great refractor and spent two hours cursing at the collimation screws, joyfully, loudly, in Latin, so that God would understand him. Tomas received the archive's first official donation under the new administration—a crate of survey documents from the parliamentary commission, including the restored coastal charts, Lise Dunmore's charts, returned from evidence storage with their seals intact—and handled them with cotton gloves and the expression of a man holding his own pardon. Mara disappeared into the clock case with a lamp and her tweezers and did not emerge for three hours except once, silently, to steal bread from the kitchen.

And I worked. Because the observatory reopening meant more than visitors; it meant students. The commission's charter included a teaching mandate—my father's insistence, fought for line by line—and among its clauses, tucked there by a clerk with a conscience, was a provision that the observatory "shall make its instruments and instruction accessible to persons of all conditions," which Tomas had drafted to mean deaf girls, poor girls, orphanage girls, any girl the city's schools found inconvenient.

We started Monday. Four of them to begin: a fisherman's daughter, a printer's apprentice, twin sisters from the asylum's discharge ward who communicated in a whistled language I understood instantly and loved completely. Mara had built the curriculum's practical section without calling it a curriculum—*they'll learn by breaking things and fixing them, that's the only honest pedagogy*—and I would teach them what I taught everyone: how to read a room, how to read a face, how to listen with your eyes until listening stops being a metaphor.

But that was Monday. Today was Tuesday, opening day, and at four o'clock the household gathered in the stairwell hall for no reason any of us admitted to.

We stood around the clock the way people stand around a fire.

"You know," my father said, "for eleven years I told visitors the mechanism had failed beyond economical repair. I let them believe it. I believed it myself, some days." He touched the case the way the woman in the green coat had. "It's strange, isn't it, how a lie can be load-bearing."

"It wasn't a lie," Tomas said. "It was a concealment maintained under duress. I've written extensively on the distinction."

"You've written extensively on everything."

"Someone had to. The archives were in chaos."

I tapped the case twice and pointed at Mara, and Mara smiled the small closed smile that was hers alone and said, "It wants another week before I trust the strike train. The hour hand is honest but the chime is still learning manners."

"Like its keeper," my father said fondly.

"Like this entire establishment," Mara said, and for once she didn't duck her head when we laughed.

At 4:17—I checked my father's pocket watch against the regulator, an old habit, our private joke—we let the moment pass without ceremony. No strike yet. Just the pendulum, swinging, keeping its count, and the four of us standing in its reach like plants toward a window.

* * *

Evening came down over the harbor the color of tarnished brass. The last visitors left. My father cooked, badly, and we ate anyway, because that was also becoming tradition: food eaten together without apology, bread torn rather than sliced, wine that Tomas brought up from somewhere he refused to specify and my father pretended not to ask about.

Afterward I washed dishes while the men argued about the teaching schedule and Mara sat at the kitchen table with her notebook open, drawing gear teeth in pencil, her lips moving faintly. I dried my hands and sat across from her and waited until she looked up.

*Thank you,* I signed. For the clock. For staying. For the way you fixed more than the clock. Our signs were crude, home-made, half pantomime, but she read them better than anyone outside this house.

She shook her head slowly. Then she reached across the table and took my slate and wrote, because some things she preferred to write even though her hands knew the signs now:

*I spent my whole life thinking I was nobody's. Turns out I was everybody's. They just hadn't filed the paperwork.*

I laughed the way I laugh, too loud and wrong-shaped, and didn't care, and she grinned at the sound of it—or at the shape of it, she'd learned to watch my throat—and for a while neither of us did anything but sit in the warm kitchen of a building that had tried to destroy us and instead had become the only place in the world shaped correctly.

Later, when the others had gone to bed, I made my rounds. It was my habit now, mine alone: a last walk through the dark rooms, checking latches, reading the house by lamplight. Entry hall, where my mother-of-sorts watched from her bronze letters on the wall. Archive, where Tomas slept sometimes amid his papers like a fox in its earth. Dome, where the telescope breathed under its canvas.

Stairwell hall last. Always last.

The regulator stood in the moonlight from the tall window, pendulum flashing silver, steady as a promise. I put my palm flat against the case and felt it speak into my bones—that giant's heartbeat, patient, enormous, going on.

And that was when I felt it. Not heard—felt. A hesitation in the beat, small as a missed breath, there and gone. I stood very still and counted. It came again, four swings later, a tiny catch in the escape wheel, the kind of flaw no one else in the world would ever notice because no one else in the world listened to this clock through solid walnut with their whole hand.

Mara had said one more day. She had been saying it for eleven days.

I took my slate from my apron and wrote in the moonlight, carefully, so she would find it in the morning:

*Your clock is limping. Fourth beat of every fifth swing. Don't pretend you knew. Come downstairs. Bring the lamp. Bring the tea. We'll be here all night, and I wouldn't want to be anywhere else.*

Then I sat down on the bottom step to wait for her footsteps—for the particular soft quick rhythm I could pick out of a hundred, the rhythm that meant family coming down to join me—and began, out of habit and happiness, to keep time against the floor with my hand, counting toward whatever hour it turned out to be.
