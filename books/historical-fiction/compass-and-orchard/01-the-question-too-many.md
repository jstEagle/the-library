## 1. The Question Too Many

The workshop had not been warm since Aldous died.

Mara Montrose knew this was impossible — stoves did not grieve, and the coal in the scuttle was the same black lump it had always been — and yet she could not shake the conviction that the cold had entered the room with the news of his death and simply never left. It sat in the mortar of the walls. It pooled in the inkwells, so that she had to warm the bottle against her palm each morning before the nib would flow. Eleven days now she had come down before dawn to finish what he could not, and eleven days the chill had greeted her like a colleague who disapproved.

She worked by three lamps because one was not enough for the hatching, and hatching was everything. The atlas lay open beneath her hands at Plate Forty-One — *The Meridian Valley, Agrarian Survey* — and she was coaxing out the orchard rows with lines fine as eyelashes, each tree a stippled crown, each crown shadowed to the southeast so that the whole valley seemed to hold the light of an afternoon that had ended twenty years ago. Aldous had taught her that trick. *A map is a lie that aspires,* he used to say, tapping her knuckles with the bone folder when her shading went lazy. *Make yours aspire honestly.*

Eleven days, and she still caught herself listening for his step on the stair.

Outside, Vellamare was doing what Vellamare did on February mornings: coughing itself awake in a haze of brine and coal smoke, the harbor bells tolling flat across the water, fishwives crying the night's catch in voices like gulls with grievances. Mara heard none of it. She heard only the small silk sound of her brush clearing dust from an engraved line, and the tick of the long-case clock, and — after a while — footsteps.

Not his. Lighter. Slower at the landing, the hesitation of a man reading the brass plate on the door even though he must already have read it downstairs.

She did not look up. "The Guild office is across the square. Commissions through the side entrance."

"I'm not here about commissions." The door opened on a draft that made the lamps gutter. "Elias Thorne. Crown Census Office. Third district."

He stood in the doorway with snow melting on the shoulders of a coat too thin for the season, a leather document case under one arm and his hat in his hand as though the room might contain someone worth doffing to. He was younger than she expected — census men were usually gray and upholstered — with wind-scoured cheeks and the particular careful stillness of a person who spent his days being shouted at by strangers and had learned to make himself small first.

"We surveyed your household in autumn," he said. "There are discrepancies."

"Then you should speak to whoever keeps the household rolls. That is not me."

"Madam—" He corrected himself, visibly recalibrating. "Miss Montrose. You are the late master's sole apprentice and, I understand, executor of his papers. There are questions about the valley district. The pre-famine returns." He came no further into the room, but his eyes had already begun their work, moving over the great flattening press, the racks of plates wrapped in oiled cloth, the wall of drawers labeled in Aldous's cramped hand. Eyes like that belonged to a man who counted things for a living. She distrusted him immediately and thoroughly. "I've been sent back to reconcile them."

"The famine was nineteen years ago." She kept her voice level and returned to her stippling. The little crowns needed her. "Whatever ghosts are troubling your ledgers, they've had two decades of practice."

"Two decades is precisely the problem. The returns for Meridian Valley list the population of the lower parishes at"— he opened the case; paper whispered —"four hundred and eleven souls. But the tithe rolls from the same years, held at the diocesan archive, give nine thousand. And the parish registers—"

"Are wrong, or forged, or misfiled. Archives are full of arithmetic that disagrees with itself." Her nib had gone dry. She dipped it, too hard; a blot bloomed at the valley's southern edge like a wound. "I'm finishing an atlas, Mr. Thorne. Aesthetic geography. Nothing in it concerns your soul counts."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Nothing in it does."

Silence then, except the clock. Mara cleaned her nib with unnecessary care and told herself the heat climbing her neck was the stove finally catching. He had not moved from the doorway. Snowwater was dripping off his coat hem onto Aldous's floorboards, and some stubborn instinct — the one that fed stray cats and re-inked faded legends nobody would ever read — made her jerk her chin toward the stool by the press.

"If you're going to drip, sit. Master Aldous hated puddles more than he hated the Revenue."

Something shifted in his face at the name — not surprise. Recognition, and under it something that looked grimly like relief. He sat. He set the document case on his knees and did not open it again.

"You knew him," she said. It was not a question; she was out of the habit, lately, of asking them.

"I took the third district census from his shopfront four years running. He'd answer every question, then ask six of his own. Where I'd studied. Who drew my forms. Whether I understood what a population figure *did* once it was written down." Thorne looked at his hands. "I thought he was testing my patience. He was testing whether I understood the weight of ink."

"He weighed everything." Her voice betrayed her on the last word, thinning. She bent over Plate Forty-One and stippled three trees that did not need stippling.

*History happening elsewhere.* That was how she had been taught to hold the famine — at the Guild, at her master's elbow: a calamity of the interior provinces, distant in time and topography both, useful chiefly as a cautionary tale about incomplete surveys. Nineteen years safe. Nine thousand souls reduced to a moral. She had drawn the Meridian Valley a dozen times in exercises and never once wondered where the people had gone, any more than she wondered where the rain went. Geography swallowed them. Contour lines were very good at swallowing.

"Miss Montrose." His voice had dropped, and the dropping of it raised the hairs on her arms. "I have to file my reconciliation report by Friday. My superiors have instructed me — in writing — to enter the valley's lower parishes as *uninhabited wasteland, prior and current.* Not depopulated. Uninhabited. As if no one had ever—" He stopped, jaw working. "Four hundred and eleven became zero, and zero becomes *never was*, and *never was* becomes legal grounds. There are land instruments pending. Sequestration orders. The orchard lands."

The brush slipped.

A single grain of pigment rolled across the plate and lodged in the groove of a contour line, and Mara stared at it as though it had spoken. Because her hand — her traitorous, careful hand — had already known something her mind had refused to read for eleven days. Aldous had worked the valley sheets obsessively in his final months. Had pulled old plates from the deep drawers, the ones that creaked, the ones she'd been told — gently, firmly, in the only instruction he ever gave her as a dying request — *not to touch until the atlas was done.*

"Mr. Thorne," she said slowly, "you said there are discrepancies in the returns."

"There are discrepancies everywhere in this district, Miss Montrose. That's rather why I came to a cartographer."

She rose. Her legs felt borrowed. She crossed to the deep drawers — his drawers, the ones whose key now hung around her own neck on its ribbon, warm from her skin — and stood a moment with her hand flat against the cold iron.

*A map is a lie that aspires.*

She unlocked them.

Inside, wrapped in oiled cloth against the damp, lay plates she had never seen in eight years of apprenticeship — survey plates of the Meridian Valley, but not hers, not the Guild's, not the crown's. These showed farmsteads dense as freckles, mill races, cider presses, chapelyards. And in the margins, in Aldous's unmistakable hand — smaller than his legend script, hurried, pressed hard enough into the copper to bite — figures. Dates. Corrections. Cross-references to documents she did not recognize, and beside the population column of the official 1828 survey, one word scored through the false number with such force the line had nearly split the plate:

*LIE.*

Behind her, Elias Thorne had risen from the stool. She heard his breath change.

"That one," he said, very quietly. "May I see that one?"

And Mara, holding her dead master's most dangerous inheritance up to the lamplight, understood at last why the workshop would not get warm.
