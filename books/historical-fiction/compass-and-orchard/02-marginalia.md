## 2. Marginalia

The knuckle that rapped the workshop door at midnight was too polite to be a soldier's.

Elias Thorne knew this because he had spent the walk from the census office rehearsing the knock — firm enough to seem official, soft enough not to wake the street. It came out apologetic anyway. Everything about him had come out apologetic for thirty-one years, and tonight was no exception, though he carried in his satchel the least apologetic document of his life: a census form, half completed, with a line drawn through it in his own careful hand.

No lamp answered. But he could see, through the frost-feathered glass of Aldous Virel's workshop, a candle guttering somewhere deep inside, and a shadow bent over the great drafting table like a heron over winter water.

He knocked again.

The bolt slid back. Mara Montrose stood in the gap with a burnishing stone in one fist and murder in her face, and Elias understood he had never in his life been so thoroughly expected.

"You," she said.

"Me." He held up the satchel like a man surrendering. "I've come to show you something. And then I'd like to ask you not to hate me quite so efficiently."

She did not move. The candle behind her breathed, and the smell of the workshop rolled out over him — linseed and iron gall ink and cold ash, the smell of a dead man's patience. Somewhere in that dark, forty years of Vellamare lay flattened onto paper, and he had come to tell her what the crown intended to do with it.

"Three minutes," she said, and stood aside.

* * *

Mara watched the census taker cross her master's floor as if it were thin ice over deep water. Good. Let him feel it. She kept the burnishing stone in her hand because its weight was honest, and because the alternative was to wring her hands, and she had promised herself at the funeral that she would never again be caught wringing them.

He set the satchel on the table beside the unfinished atlas and drew out a folio bound in the grey of government issue. "Form Eleven," he said. "Valley district, population survey, autumn enumeration." He opened it flat and turned it toward her, and his finger — ink-stained, bitten at the nail, a working clerk's finger — rested on the entry line.

Where should have been numbers, where every other district in her master's atlas bloomed with tallies, there was a single word in the official stamp-ink of the Interior Ministry: *Uninhabited wasteland.*

"I didn't write it," Thorne said quietly. "I wrote the truth. Nine hundred and forty souls in the valley parishes as of last spring, orchard families, cider presses, a school at Ferrow Bridge. My supervisor read my figures, thanked me, and gave the form back with this." His finger moved to the second sheet beneath, an instruction on ministry letterhead, unsigned but sealed. *Log per attached classification. Deviation is a records offense.* "They ordered me to strike nine hundred and forty people from the count, Miss Montrose. Not to fudge them. To erase them. Because erased people can't own orchards."

The candle flame leaned. Mara found she was gripping the burnishing stone hard enough to print its shape into her palm.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said. "You don't know me. For all you know I'll run to the guildhall at dawn and trade your confession for my master's good name."

"I know what I saw yesterday." He met her eyes then, and up close they were the grey of rain over slate, tired and stubborn at once. "When I was measuring your workshop for the property register. Your master's valley plates were on the side bench. The ones with the orchards still on them." A breath. "Miss Montrose — the maps the crown published show that valley empty. Barren. As if the famine came to a wilderness. But your master drew it full. If anyone else in this city ever put those two documents side by side, the whole edifice falls down. And I think you know it."

Silence filled the workshop the way water fills a lock, pressing against all sides. Mara thought of Aldous in this room through the last winter of his life, coughing, correcting, hunched over the valley sheets until dawn while she slept badly upstairs and told herself grief explained everything. Grief explained nothing. He had known. He had been building a case the way other men built coffins.

"The marginal notes," she heard herself say. "His marginalia. I found them three weeks ago and I can't make them cohere. They're in shorthand — guild cipher, the old compression notation, but the references don't resolve. He's citing things I can't find."

Thorne leaned over the table before she had decided to let him. That was the thing about careful men; they moved only when they were certain, and their certainty was worse than another man's recklessness.

"That's not compression notation," he said slowly. "That's *census* shorthand. Registry cipher. I log six hundred forms a month in it." His finger traced a cluster of hooked figures in the margin of the valley plate. "This is a parish tally. Ferrow Bridge, harvest of '41. This—" another cluster, "—is a tithe record. Orchard yields. He wasn't writing notes, Miss Montrose. He was writing *citations*. Every scribble is a document, filed somewhere, that proves those orchards were alive and laden when the crown says the valley was empty."

Mara looked down at the margins she had studied for twenty-one nights and saw, for the first time, not her dead master's ghosts but his footnotes. Her eyes burned. She would not allow them to do anything about it in front of a stranger.

"He couldn't cite openly," she said. "So he hid the evidence in the map itself."

"In the one place they'd never burn," Thorne agreed softly. Then he stopped, and she saw him hear what he had just said, and neither of them mentioned the smoke she had smelled on his coat when she opened the door.

* * *

They worked until the candle drowned in its own wax and he lit another from the stub, and neither remarked on how naturally his hand shielded the flame toward hers.

It went like this: she would lift a plate from Aldous's chest — the corrected valley sheets, the ones the guild had never approved, never seen — and read aloud a marginal cluster in her master's crabbed cipher. Thorne would decode it, lips moving, translating forty years of registry habit into plain speech, and Mara would enter each citation into the atlas margin in red, a thread being sewn stitch by stitch through the dark.

*Tithes, Parish of Ferrow Bridge, 1841: 3,100 bushels pears; 2,400 apples; press-house at Millward operating four vats.*

*Census, valley district, spring 1846: 940 souls, 112 households, school enrollment 61 children.*

*Military requisition order, October 1846: all stored fruit and seed stock confiscated "for the relief of the realm."*

She read that last one twice. Thorne said nothing, but his pen stopped.

"They didn't fail to feed the valley," Mara said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, level and terrible. "They emptied it first. Took the food, took the seed, logged the land as empty, and then let the famine do the paperwork."

"Yes," Thorne said. Just that. Some truths deserve no decoration.

Toward the fourth hour she reached past him for the magnifier and their hands crossed above the plate — his cold, hers cold, both of them startled by contact as though the workshop had grown warm without notice. He did not pull back quickly. Neither did she. For a moment the atlas waited under their joined shadows, and Mara thought, absurdly, of how many nights she had worked alone in this room believing that loneliness was simply the price of precision.

"Your master trusted someone to find this," Thorne said, low. "The cipher, the citations hidden in plain sight. He wanted it decoded eventually. By someone patient enough to look."

"He wanted it decoded by someone who mattered," Mara corrected. "I'm only the apprentice."

"You're the one holding the map," he said, "and I'm the one who knows where the bodies of the paperwork are buried. Between us, Miss Montrose, we are extremely dangerous to very powerful people." He smiled — the first time she had seen it, small and crooked and exhausted — and she discovered that a smile could be a kind of citation too, entered into evidence against her own caution.

She should send him away. Every instinct trained into her by the guild said so: finish alone, verify thrice, trust the archive and no living soul. But the archive was a lie now, the guild was a lie, the beautiful finished world of the atlas was a lie with a wasteland stamped across the valley — and this tired clerk with his bitten nails was the only person in Vellamare telling the truth at cost to himself.

"Come back tomorrow night," she said. "And the night after. We'll need every citation before we have anything worth calling proof."

"Yes," he said, and gathered his satchel, and at the door he paused. "Miss Montrose. There's one more thing. The reason I came tonight instead of walking past your door like a sensible man." He turned the grey folio in his hands. "My supervisor wants Form Eleven returned by Friday. Sealed. Filed. After that, the amendment can't be challenged — the falsified count becomes the permanent record, and everything your master hid in these margins becomes a madman's doodles."

Something in his voice made her go still. "And?"

"And this morning the Interior Ministry requested the Cartographers' Guild surrender all of Aldous Virel's original plates. For review." He met her eyes. "For *review*, Miss Montrose. They're coming for the atlas. I don't know when. Days, perhaps less."

The candle between them shivered, though no door had opened.

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell rang once — the night watch, or perhaps something else — and Mara stood among her master's papers in the drowning light and understood that whatever they built here, they would have to build fast.
