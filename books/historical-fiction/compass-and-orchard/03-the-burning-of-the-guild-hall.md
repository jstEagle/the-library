## 3. The Burning of the Guild Hall

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy too young to know what he carried. The paper bore the Guild's seal pressed deep into black wax — deeper than custom required, as if the stamp itself were trying to crush something flat.

*By order of the Master Cartographers' Guild of Vellamare: all apprentices, journeymen, and members shall present themselves at the Hall by the ninth bell, there to witness the adjudication of the late Master Aldous Renner's works.*

*Adjudication.* Mara read the word four times while the workshop went grey with morning light around her. Not *review*. Not *examination*. Adjudication was what you performed on a corpse.

She had not slept. The corrected valley sheets lay across Aldous's workbench in a careful fan — nine plates, each one inked in his hand and amended in hers, each one carrying the marginalia that would hang the crown's historians with their own arithmetic. Nine plates. Three years of his nights and two weeks of her desperation. She had copied them once already, hidden the copies inside the false back of his atlas case, and still she woke tasting copper, certain someone had come in the dark to take them.

She dressed in her apprentice blacks, and then, standing before the cracked mirror, took them off again. She put on her mother's grey traveling coat instead. If she was going to watch them murder her master a second time, she would do it out of uniform.

* * *

The Guild Hall had never held so many people. Every tier of the lecture theatre was filled — apprentices pale in their rows, masters roosting above like crows in formal velvet, and along the back wall, men who wore no Guild colors at all. Crown men. Mara knew them by their stillness, by the way they watched the doors rather than the podium. One of them held a leather satchel, and she could not stop looking at it.

Guildmaster Corvane stood beneath the great rose window, and the light came down through it blood-red and gold, pooling on the empty easel where Aldous's atlas had stood for twenty years.

"Aldous Renner served this Guild with distinction," Corvane began, and his voice did not shake, which was the first obscenity. "He mapped three coastlines. He taught two generations. It is therefore with particular grief that we must find his final work — the so-called *Grand Atlas of the Northern Provinces* — to be materially falsified."

The word fell into the hall like a coin down a well. Mara heard it drop past every row.

"The survey plates recovered from his workshop show systematic alteration of population figures for the Vellamare valley. Alterations made, we must conclude, either by the late Master himself or—" Corvane's eyes found her, found her grey coat among the black, and rested there with terrible gentleness, "—by persons with access to his materials. This Guild does not traffic in sedition. This Guild draws coastlines. Let the historians argue about orchards."

An apprentice somewhere behind her began to cry, quietly, the way children cry when they have been told not to.

Mara's hands had gone cold in her lap, but her mind was doing something worse than grieving; it was counting. The plates on the easel — the ones they held up now, turning them to the room like evidence of a poacher's snare — those were her corrections. Her hand. Her ink. Which meant the copies they had seized were the copies she had *not* hidden well enough, and if they had found one set—

Then Elias was being brought in.

Two crown men walked him down the side aisle, not dragging him, worse than dragging him: escorting him, formally, his census ledger open in one of their hands like scripture. His collar had been torn at the seam. There was ink on his fingers — he had been made to write something, sign something, and she watched him hold his face very carefully still, the way a man holds still when the alternative is screaming.

"Elias Thorne, census officer, third district," the clerk read out, "is charged with conspiracy to falsify crown records, in company with the apprentice Mara Montrose."

Her name, spoken aloud in the rose-light, in front of everyone she had ever wanted to admire her. She felt it land and break, and understood, with a strange clarity, that this was how a name died. Not with drama. With paperwork.

"Montrose," Corvane said, and now his gentleness was the cruelest thing in the room. "Step forward."

She did not step forward. She stood, slowly, and the whole theatre turned with her.

"You will renounce the altered plates. You will state, for the record, that your late master acted alone and deceived you. Do this, and your apprenticeship is suspended — suspended, girl, not revoked — pending the Guild's forgiveness. Refuse, and you are named in the indictment beside him."

Above, the crown men had not moved. They did not need to move. The trap had been built to her exact measurements, and everyone in the hall knew it, and everyone in the hall was watching her decide whether her life was worth more said aloud or lived in silence.

She thought of Aldous's marginal notes, cramped in the gutters of his maps like a man whispering against a wall. *The valley was full,* he had written. *I counted the chimneys myself. Say it true or say nothing.*

"I saw his hand and I copied it faithfully," Mara said, and her voice carried further than she intended, further than she had ever dared let anything of hers carry. "Both hands. His truth and mine. The valley held eleven thousand souls before the famine returns were rewritten to say four hundred. You are not burning a fraud today. You are burning the witness."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. Somewhere in the tiers an old journeyman crossed himself.

Corvane closed his eyes briefly — and in that half-second, Mara saw something flicker across his face that was not contempt. Grief, maybe. Or envy. Then he opened them and spoke the sentence that unmade her.

"Mara Montrose is struck from the rolls. Her works are stricken. Let the record show the Guild has no member by that name."

*No member by that name.* Twenty years old, and she had just been subtracted from the only country she had ever belonged to.

* * *

They burned the plates at dusk in the Hall's courtyard, in the great bronze brazier that was meant for warming pitch.

Mara should not have been there. Struck names were not summoned anywhere. But the city had heard, and half of Vellamare had come anyway — shopkeepers, fishwives, clerks from the record houses — standing in the cold to watch the crown's arithmetic made flesh in fire, and no one stopped a woman in a grey coat at the edge of the crowd.

The smoke was sweet and wrong, linseed oil and sizing and burning paper, a smell she had loved all her life because it meant work being finished. Now it meant work being erased. Plate after plate went in, and she watched the flames take coastlines she had inked at seventeen, soundings Aldous had taken with his own lead line, the whole beautiful patient architecture of a truthful world curling to ash on the wind.

And then she saw them bring out the last bundle, and her heart stopped: nine sheets, tied in cord. The valley plates. *Not her copies — the originals.* The ones with Aldous's own marginalia, the primary evidence, the thing that could not be redrawn from memory. They had missed them in the workshop search. They had missed them, and now a junior clerk was carrying them to the fire.

She moved before she decided to. The crowd was dense, the light failing, every eye fixed on the brazier, and twelve years of apprenticeship had taught her exactly how to slide through a press of bodies without disturbing a single shoulder. She reached the clerk's blind side as he bent to feed the bundle in. Her fingers found the cord. The crowd surged — someone shouted about the sparks, a horse shied somewhere near the gates — and in that knot of chaos she drew the nine sheets free and folded them flat against her ribs, under the grey coat, over her heart, and stepped back into the dark between strangers.

The clerk fed the cord into the flames alone, frowned at it, and shrugged.

On the platform by the brazier, flanked by crown men, Elias turned his head — slowly, deliberately, the only movement they had left him — and looked out across the courtyard until his eyes found hers. He did not nod. He did not smile. He simply looked at her the way a surveyor looks at a fixed point, taking his bearing, and then he was marched away toward the Guild cells, and the crowd began, horribly, to disperse as though a justice had occurred.

Mara stood in the thinning square with stolen evidence pressed against her ribs and no name, no guild, no craft, and no man. The ashes rose. Above the rooftops, the first stars were coming out, indifferent and exact, and she thought: *they kept the stars, at least; no one has falsified the sky yet.*

Then she turned toward the workshop, walking fast, because there were copies in the atlas case and perhaps one night left to move them — and because somewhere in the record house, a sympathetic clerk might still be found before morning.
