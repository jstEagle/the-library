## 4. On the Record

They released him on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the lamps were trimmed, and the clerk who did it never gave his name.

Elias had spent nine days in the cellar beneath the Provost's Registry — not a cell proper, for he was never charged, merely *held*, as though the machinery of the state could not decide what to call a man who had done nothing but read aloud from a dead cartographer's margins. The damp had gotten into his knees. His interrogator, a soft-voiced man named Ferrant who smelled of ink and clove oil, had asked the same question forty ways: *Where are the sheets? Where is the atlas? Where is the woman?*

To all of which Elias had answered, truthfully, that he did not know. Mara had vanished into Vellamare's underside the night of the burning, and the city kept its secrets the way the sea kept drowned men — thoroughly, and without malice.

Then, on the ninth morning, boots on the stair, and a small stooped clerk with spectacles fogged by the cellar's breath, carrying an armful of ledgers he plainly did not need.

"Census amendment," the clerk announced to the turnkey, in the bored voice of a man reading a laundry list. "Thorne, E., surveyor second class. Records dispute under Statute Eleven. He's required at the filing desk within the day." He set the ledgers down, opened one at random, frowned at it. "Statute Eleven," he repeated, and looked up over his spectacles, and let Elias see in his eyes something that was not boredom at all.

The walk out took eleven minutes. In all of them the clerk said nothing except, at the door to the yard, quietly, without turning his head: "My mother came from Harrowmere. Before the famine. She used to say the valley smelled of cider in September." A pause. "The Registry archive room has a window that doesn't latch. Filing clerks are careless people."

Then he was gone, a stooped shape dissolving into the morning mist, and Elias stood in the yard with the cold on his face like open water.

* * *

The Registry archive room smelled of vellum, dust, and the ghost of smoke that had come through the windows two nights past when the Guild Hall burned. The great map cabinets still bore their brass labels — *NORTHERN REACHES, COASTAL SURVEYS, DISPUTED TERRITORIES* — and in the last, where the valley folders should have been thick as hymnals, there was a thinness now, a gap one could fit a hand through. Someone had already been here. Someone would come again.

Elias latched nothing. He opened the window onto the courtyard and waited, and near dusk she came — hood up, a market basket over her arm, moving through the archway with the unhurried gait of a woman buying onions. But beneath the cloak's hem her hands were gray to the wrist with charcoal and fixative, and when she pushed back the hood in the shadow between the shelves, her face was thinner than he remembered and her eyes burned like the hall had burned.

"You're free," she said. Not a question. She touched his jaw where the bruise had yellowed, lightly, as if checking whether he were real or one of Aldous's proofs — something drawn and then erased.

"A clerk freed me. One whose mother remembered the orchards." He covered her hand with his own. "Mara. Are you—"

"No." She said it fast, the way one refuses a offered chair. "Don't ask me that yet. Ask me when we're on the water." From the basket, wrapped in oilcloth that still smelled faintly of the workshop's linseed, she drew the sheets.

He had seen them before, glimpsed them across the candlelit wreckage of that first evening — but seeing them whole was another thing. Six plates, corrected in Aldous Montrose's crabbed hand and finished in Mara's finer one: the valley as it had been. Orchard rows inked tree by tree, four hundred and eleven holdings, wells marked, a schoolhouse, a mill, the census tallies from 1839 copied out beside the crown's 1846 figures so that the lie stood naked between them — *2,300 souls* struck through and replaced with *2,296, of whom 2,101 perished or dispersed.* And in the margin, in red, Aldous's last annotation, the one Mara had decoded at midnight while the fire glowed through the shutters: *Survey altered by order of R.— the plate was cut twice. I kept the first cut.*

"He kept the original wax impressions," Mara said softly. "All these years. Hidden in the frame of his elevation drawing of Mount Cael." Her thumb moved along the margin. "He couldn't publish. They'd have ruined him, and he knew it, and he hated himself for knowing it. So he wrote the truth where only someone reading *closely* would ever find it. He made the atlas into a letter to whoever came after." Her voice caught. "I thought I was finishing his masterwork. I was only finishing his confession."

"Then finish it properly," Elias said. "File it."

She looked up. "You understand what you're holding. This ends your career. Your name. Perhaps more — Ferrant will not stop because a cellar door opened."

"Ferrant asked me where the sheets were," Elias said. "Forty times. I'd like to hand him the answer in triplicate."

And here — in the dust-colored light of the archive, between the shelves where the victors' maps stood fat and unquestioned — Mara laughed, a short broken sound, and leaned her forehead against his shoulder, and for a long moment they simply stood, two careful people in the wreck of everything careful, holding the truth between them like a coal passed hand to hand.

* * *

The filing itself took less than an hour, which seemed obscene to Elias afterward — that nine days of cellar and a lifetime of consequence should compress into ink, wax, and a clerk's rubber stamp.

But procedure, he reminded himself, watching the amendment slide into the great ledger, was the one weapon the powerful could not confiscate without admitting it existed. Statute Eleven was plain: any licensed surveyor could lodge a correction to the public record, supported by primary evidence, and no amendment could be removed without a hearing before three magistrates and publication of the grounds in the *Vellamare Gazette*. The crown's historians could burn Guild Hall. They could not burn a numbered entry in a bound ledger without the whole city watching them do it.

The desk clerk — a different one, young, nervous, chewing his pen — read the cover sheet twice.

"This says the famine maps were falsified," he said. "By the Survey Office. It says so *on the record*."

"It does," Elias agreed.

"And these attachments—" The boy lifted the corner of the oilcloth, saw the orchard rows marching down the valley in their patient thousands, and went very still. His grandfather, Elias would learn later, had driven a cider cart out of Harrowmere.

"The Registry will want copies," Elias said. "Make six. Make twelve. Statute Eleven requires preservation of all supporting evidence in duplicate, and I'm told filing clerks are careless people."

The stamp came down like a small, decisive thunderclap: *AMENDED — SEE ENTRY 4471.*

* * *

They left at dawn three days later, before Ferrant could find a magistrate willing to hear the matter *quietly* instead of publicly.

Mara carried the atlas itself — the finished book, Aldous's life's work, its boards scarred, its spine re-sewn with her own hands — wrapped in sailcloth against the salt air to come. Elias carried one bag, his license surrendered by post in an envelope addressed to no one in particular, and the memory of the archive window that didn't latch, which he intended to carry to his grave or at least to the coast.

At the harbor gate they stopped. Behind them Vellamare climbed its hill in tiers of slate and plaster, chimneys just beginning to breathe, the burnt shell of the Guild Hall visible above the rooftops like a pulled tooth. Ahead lay the packet boat, the grey water, the coast road south where no one knew their names — which was, Elias thought, both the cost and, perhaps, the point.

"I keep waiting to feel ruined," Mara said. She watched a gull hang motionless over the mastheads. "I lost the guild. My standing. The atlas was going to make my name, and now my name isn't safe to say aloud in this city. And instead I feel—" She searched for it, frowning the way she frowned over a difficult coastline. "*Lighter*. As if I'd been carrying the falsified maps myself, all this time, without knowing."

"That's not ruin," Elias said.

"No." She took his arm. "It's the opposite. History wasn't something happening to other people — to ministers, to historians, to masters dead in their workshops. We *made* some. Entry 4471. It's terrible and small and true, and it's ours."

They walked down to the water. The tide was turning; he could feel it in the slack of the mooring lines, the whole harbor breathing differently, the way a record breathes differently after amendment.

Only, as the packet cast off and Vellamare began its slow retreat into haze, the harbormaster's boy came over the gunwale-side breathless with a folded paper. "Asked to give this to the surveyor gentleman. Man in a grey coat. Paid a penny."

Elias unfolded it. Five words, in a small stooped clerk's careful hand:

*They are printing copies too.*

He read it twice. Then he folded it away inside his coat, over his heart, and turned to find Mara watching him with the wind pulling strands of dark hair loose across her face.

"What is it?" she asked.

Elias looked back once at the fading city, then south, toward the open water and whatever waited on the coast road — because if the crown's presses were running, then the race was not finished, only relocated, and somewhere ahead of them the truth and its enemies were traveling toward the same shore.

"More wind than we thought," he said, and went to help the sailors with the sail.
