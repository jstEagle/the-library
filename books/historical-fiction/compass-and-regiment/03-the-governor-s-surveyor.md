## 3. The Governor's Surveyor

The knock came at half past nine on a morning of thin silver light, and Sable knew before opening the door that the day had changed shape.

There was a grammar to knocking. The baker's boy rapped twice, quick and careless, already turning away. Maren from next door scratched softly, a laundress's knuckle worn polite by years of other people's doors. The rent collector pounded. Drunken sailors sang at doors rather than knocked them. But this — three measured falls of a gloved hand, spaced like the ticks of a fine clock, patient enough to wait all morning if patience were required — this was the knock of a man who had never once been kept waiting and found the novelty instructive.

Sable set down the pen. The line they had been drawing — the eastern shore of the Bay of Sainte-Lune, its cliffs rendered in Corvane's stippled shadow-work — remained wet at the cliff's crown, and they would not blow on it, because blowing was an apprentice's habit and they had stopped being an apprentice in every way but name ten years ago. They looked at the door. They looked at the atlas, open beneath a linen cloth disguised as work on a harbour chart, and their hands, entirely without instruction from their mind, smoothed the cloth flat and laid a dividers case on one corner as if it had lain there for hours.

Then they went to answer the knock.

* * *

The man on the step was grey in the way that expensive things are grey: coat of good wool cut close, boots polished to the colour of wet slate, hair powdered just enough to signal that powdering was a choice he made rather than a fashion imposed on him. Forty perhaps, or fifty with excellent posture. His eyes were pale and did the whole of his looking for him; his mouth was merely furniture.

"Master Corvane's workshop?" he asked, though the shingle above the door — *E. Corvane, Cartographer & Surveyor* — had answered him already.

"The workshop, yes." Sable stood in the doorway the way Corvane had taught them to stand in doorways: filling it. "I'm Sterling. The apprentice."

"Ah." Something moved behind the pale eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "The apprentice. Then you'll do." He produced a leather folio from under his arm and opened it with one hand, extracting a document sealed in red wax heavier than any seal Sable had ever been asked to break. "Josiah Hale. Royal Surveyor, Commission of Lands and Harbours. I've come about the master's papers."

Sable heard themselves say, "What papers?" and marveled distantly at how easily the question came out, how much it sounded like innocence. It should have sounded like guilt. Perhaps it did to him. His face gave nothing back.

"The complete corpus," Hale said. "Charts, field notes, correspondence, fair copies, drafts. Everything in the master's hand and everything bearing his mark." He consulted the folio, though Sable would have wagered the workshop he did not need to. "By order of Governor Vane, acting for the Crown, the estate of Edmun Corvane is subject to recovery of commissioned works. Works paid for by the office of the Governor remain, I'm afraid, the property of that office. Death does not transfer title."

"He died owing nobody," Sable said. It was true, and truth was safer than invention; Corvane had taught them that too. *Never lie when a truth will serve, Sterling. A lie has edges. Truth is all weather.*

"I don't doubt it. May I come in?"

The question arrived so smoothly that refusing it would have been like refusing to breathe — noticeable, memorable, the sort of thing a polite man reports. Sable stepped aside.

Hale entered the workshop the way water enters a hull: everywhere at once, quietly, finding every low place. He did not go to the drafting table first, which Sable had expected, and which frightened them more than if he had. He went instead to the walls. He read the instruments — the brass meridian circle, the plumb rules, the long ruling pens hung in their oiled leather sleeves like a surgeon's knives. He read the two finished charts framed behind glass, the ones chosen years ago to be seen: Vellamare harbour as the Admiralty wanted it, and the county interior with its roads fat and honest. He read the smell of the place, almost; his nostrils flared at the lamp-black and gum arabic, and Sable thought absurdly, *he is counting the inks by scent*, and then thought, *no, he is simply a careful man, and careful men are worse.*

"You keep the shop working," Hale observed. "Commissioned work?"

"Harbour charts. Insurance surveys. A wedding gift for a merchant's daughter — her father wants the family estates drawn with the orchards in bloom." Sable gestured at the table, at the cloth, at the dividers keeping their false vigil. "Small work. The master took the grand commissions with him when he—" They stopped. Ten years, and the sentence still had a hole in it shaped like a man.

Hale let the hole sit there between them, courteous as ever. Then: "You were with him long?"

"From the age of nine. Debt-bond, originally. He bought out the bond." All true. All weather.

"A generous master."

"A precise one."

That earned the ghost of something across the grey face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would be filed if such things were kept. Hale turned from the walls at last and approached the drafting table, and Sable's heart performed a slow, cold manoeuvre, tightening like a sail taken in against weather. He looked down at the cloth. He looked for longer than cloth deserves.

"The eastern shore," he said. "Sainte-Lune?"

"Insurance work," Sable said. "A shipping concern. Cliffs, soundings, nothing of interest to anyone who doesn't fear shipwreck."

"Most men fear shipwreck." Hale lifted the corner of the cloth a hand's width — not rudely, not even curiously; the gesture of a man checking whether a delivery matches its manifest — and looked at what lay beneath: the shore, the cliffs, the honest little soundings, three days of careful misdirection Sable had built around a gap in the paper where the atlas's true leaf would sit when the night's work resumed. He let the cloth fall. "Clean stippling," he said. "Corvane's school. You hold your shadow-work well."

"He'd have wanted that noted," Sable said, and heard their own voice do something terrible: warm itself on the praise. Ten years of silence and the first stranger through the door finds the softest room in the house.

Hale turned to face them fully then, and for a moment the politeness thinned and Sable saw the instrument beneath it, the way one sees the keel beneath a painted hull. "Master Sterling. I will be plain, since plainness costs me my wages. The Governor believes Corvane held papers beyond his commission. Coastal soundings, chiefly. Land claims along the drowned quarter." The words came out level, unhurried, each placed like a stone in a wall. "He believes it because Corvane billed the office for vellum he cannot account for. Eleven skins of calf, autumn of 'ninety-one. Fine stock. The kind one reserves for work meant to last."

*Eleven skins.* Sable stood very still and counted in their head, the way Corvane had taught them to count — not the number of things, but the weight of the person naming them. Eleven skins. The atlas was nineteen leaves. Some skins cut two folios. Whoever had done this arithmetic had done it with love, or with hatred, and could not tell them apart.

"The household accounts went to the creditors' clerk," Sable said. "Vellum is vellum. Some spoiled. Some I practised on, as apprentices do. You're welcome to the shavings." They said it steadily. They even believed it, in the moment, which was the trick Corvane had never taught because he had never needed it: believe your lie while you speak it, grieve honestly for things that didn't happen, and no examiner alive will find the seam.

"I may do that," Hale said. He returned to the centre of the room, drew a card from the folio, and laid it on the drafting table beside the dividers with the exactness of a man setting a piece on a board. "I'll return. Inventory takes time, and the office prefers thoroughness to haste. If anything of the master's surfaces — in a drawer, a loft, a memory — the office looks kindly on those who assist it." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and offered the first true thing Sable had seen in his whole grey person: a fatigue, brief and bottomless, gone as soon as glimpsed. "Vellamare is a small city, Master Sterling. Small cities drown slowly. One learns to notice the water rising."

Then he was gone, his footsteps receding down Rope Walk with the metronome patience of his knocking, and Sable stood in the middle of their own workshop feeling the walls move closer by degrees, like a tide coming in over ground that had always been dry.

* * *

They did not touch the atlas for an hour. That was discipline, not fear — or fear wearing discipline's coat, which in Sable's experience amounted to the same garment cut by a better tailor. Instead they swept a floor that did not need sweeping, banked the stove, fed the neighbour's cat that had adopted the yard and considered itself payment for Maren's gossip. Only when their hands had remembered they were hands did they lift the cloth and look at what lay beneath, and understand, all at once and completely, the thing they had refused to understand for ten years.

The atlas was not unfinished.

They had told themselves the story so long it had worn smooth as a beach pebble: *I am completing the master's work. Leaf by leaf, in secret, I am finishing what death interrupted.* Every night the candle, the pen, the slow devotional labour of inking another coastline, another sounding, another marginal note in a hand learned so faithfully from Corvane's that their own had nearly vanished inside it. And every night the same quiet terror: *so much left, so many years left, will I live to see it done?*

Nineteen leaves. Nineteen beautiful, damning leaves. And tonight, lifting the cloth, Sable counted the gaps and found none. The smuggling routes with their tide-tables and lantern signals. The garrison plans with the gates' blind arcs marked in Corvane's patient red. The land grants, traced and annotated, showing where the Governor's signatures predated the lands they claimed — impossible dates, impossible arithmetic, forgery laid bare in the gentlest possible medium, ink on calfskin. The drowned quarter charted as it had been before the sea came in over the roofs, because Corvane had surveyed it before the sea was invited.

It was finished. It had been finished — when? Sable sat down on the stool, slowly, the way old men sit down. Not tonight. Not this year. The last leaf bore a date in the lower margin, tiny, tucked into the border's scrollwork where only a cartographer would think to look for a signature of time: *finished the 21st of December, 1791.* Four days before Corvane died.

Finished. Hidden. Every night for ten years Sable had been adding flourishes to a completed thing — coastlines re-stippled, borders re-inked, the endless polishing of a jewel already cut — because finishing had been the story that kept them at the table, and stopping would have meant asking what the table was for. Corvane had known. Of course he had known. He had finished it, hidden it in plain sight beneath an apprentice's busywork, and died four days later on the harbour stairs with the coastal charts still locked in his strongbox, refusing to the last to give up even the decoys.

*A map is a confession,* the master used to say, tapping the table with his pen-holder until the apprentices recited it back. *Every line confesses: here I walked, here I measured, here I cared.* And what had he confessed, in nineteen leaves of the finest vellum in Vellamare? That the Governor was a thief, a forgerer, and — the eleventh leaf, the drowned quarter, whose full horror Sable had copied out at fifteen without understanding — a murderer of villages. Confessed it in the one language that could not be contradicted: measurement.

And now a royal surveyor with tired eyes and eleven skins of arithmetic in his head had come to ask after it, politely, and would come again.

* * *

That evening a boy arrived from the Government House with a letter, and Sable signed for it with a hand that did not shake because they had spent the afternoon teaching it not to.

The letter was brief. It was written in a secretary's copperplate, but one line strayed from the copperplate's discipline into a broader, harder script, and Sable understood that this line, at least, the Governor had written himself.

> *To the keeper of the late E. Corvane's premises,*
>
> *The Crown, having cause to recover certain commissioned works, has directed its surveyor, Mr. J. Hale, to make inventory of the said premises. You will afford him every facility.*
>
> *The Governor desires me to add his personal regard for the late master's diligence, and his confidence that whatever remains in that house will prove worthy of his memory — being, as the Governor recalls it, chiefly exercises and spoiled stock.*
>
> *Facility, Master Sterling, is a virtue in servants. So is discretion. The Governor has long observed that the two are commonly found together, and rewards them accordingly.*
>
> *(in the broader hand:) There is nothing in that house worth a life. See that we agree on this. — A.V.*
>
> *Given under hand at Government House, Vellamare, this 9th day of March, 1799.*

Sable read it four times by the light of the storm lamp, Elowen-not-yet-Elowen still months from the door that would change everything, the atlas closed beneath its cloth, the city outside settling into its evening noises — gulls, rigging, Maren's washtub, the watchman's bell. *Nothing worth a life.* The Governor had answered a question Sable had not yet asked him, which meant the question had been asked before, by someone, somewhere, and the asking had gone badly.

They carried the letter to the stove and watched the copperplate curl and the broad hard hand blacken last of all, the A.V. holding its shape longest in the flames like a crest refusing to melt.

Then Sable unlocked the floor cache beneath the drafting table, lifted out the nineteen leaves wrapped in oilcloth, and began — for the first time, knowing what it was they held — to study them not as a work to finish but as a hiding to defend. Where would a grey man look? Under floors. In lofts. Inside map presses, inside the linings of trunks, inside the hollow leg of the big table. Everywhere a clever person would hide a treasure, and therefore nowhere at all.

It came to them near midnight, arriving the way the best solutions always arrived: not invented but noticed, as if it had been waiting in the room the whole while. Corvane's marginalia on the drowned-quarter leaf, the note Sable had copied a hundred times without reading: *what men value they frame; what they fear they bury; show a man his vanity and he will never search your cellar.*

The atlas did not need to be hidden. It needed to be dressed.

Sable pulled a fresh skin of vellum from the stock — one of the last, they noted, of the eleven — and pinned it to the board, and began, with the first clean stroke of the night, to draw a very different map: the gardens of the Governor's new pleasure-grounds at Ashcombe, commissioned two years past and never completed, all parterres and gravel walks and ornamental water. Harmless. Flattering. Exactly the sort of work a dead man's workshop might honestly owe its Governor.

Somewhere out in the dark, a coach rolled up Rope Walk slow enough to be counting doorways. Sable did not look up. The pen whispered. The ornamental lake took its curve, and beneath the curve, in Sable's own hand — the hand no one knew, the hand that had spent ten years pretending to be Corvane's — they wrote the date, small, tucked into the scrollwork of the border, where only a cartographer would ever think to look for a signature of time.

Three days later, Josiah Hale would knock again.
