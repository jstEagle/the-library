## 4. What the Silk Says

The scrap of silk had lived for eleven days in the drawer of Sable's drafting table, folded into a square no larger than a wafer, beneath the blotting papers and the broken nibs, as if burying it among the instruments of Corvane's trade could make it a matter of cartography rather than of blood.

Sable took it out each night after the candle was lit and the shutters barred, unfolded it, and looked at what could not be unseen.

It was fine work. That was the first thing—the quality of the embroidery declared itself before the design did, the way a forged coastline declares its forger in the confidence of its curves. The silk itself was the color of old cream, soft enough that it had gathered along the fold lines into ridges like dunes, and the thread was silver and a blue so deep it read almost black in low light. Someone had stitched a crest: a shield divided by a chevron, and upon the chevron three stars, and rising from the chief a hand holding a compass rose. Around the shield ran a motto in letters small enough to demand good eyes and better lighting:

*QUOD TENEAS TENEBRIS* — what you hold, hold in darkness.

Sable had inked that crest once. Only once, four years ago, on a map that no longer existed except in memory and in the atlas's careful accounting of its own suppressions. It had been a chart of the harbor approaches, commissioned under Vane's seal, and the crest had gone into the cartouche at the lower right because the Governor's steward had delivered written instructions regarding its placement, dimensions, and prominence—two inches, gilded borders, *the stars to be rendered with particular accuracy*. Then the order had come down to destroy the plate, and the chart with it, because the harbor soundings recorded there were not the soundings any ship's captain should trust, and because a map that flattered could not also be honest, and Corvane had chosen, in his cold methodical way, to keep the honest version and burn the flattering one.

But Sable remembered the crest. Remembered inking the stars, one two three, remembering the peculiar weight of the compass rose in the hand—remembered thinking, even then, that it was an odd device for a man whose relationship to truth was famously negotiable. A compass. On the arms of a Governor who bent every needle in the province toward himself.

Now the same device, stitched in silver and midnight, wrapped around an infant abandoned on a doorstep in the dead of winter.

Sable did not need the heraldry alone. There was the note too—*forget the face*—in a hand that was educated but hurried, the ascendants clipped, the ink blotted where the writer's glove had dragged through it. And there was the arithmetic, which Sable performed nightly the way other people said prayers. The child was perhaps ten days old when she arrived. Count back through her gestation, through the seasons, to a spring when Governor Aldous Vane had spent three weeks in residence at the harborside customs house overseeing the new tariff schedules—and when a young woman of the Vane household, a cousin's daughter or a lady's maid, depending on which rumor you subscribed to, had been sent away abruptly to relatives in the northern counties. Sent away thin. Returned in autumn, thinner still, and without explanation, and never spoken of again.

Vellamare did not speak of such things. Vellamare laundered them.

Sable set the silk down on the drafting table and pressed it flat with both palms, and felt the familiar vertigo of standing at the edge of a map that showed too much. Every rule Corvane had taught came forward like soldiers called to formation. *What is given to you in secret belongs to the secret.* *A workshop survives by knowing nothing.* *The man who carries another man's confession will die of it.* Ten years Sable had kept those commandments, through the grief and the suspicion and the slow solitary years of finishing the atlas by candle-end, and now the secret had been delivered to the door wrapped in lamb's wool and smelling of milk, and it slept in a crate lined with Maren's old shawls, and it had a name that Sable had not yet dared give it because names were inheritances and this inheritance might get them both killed.

The fire needed tending. The baby—she—would wake within the hour, hungry, and the ritual of her hunger had already begun to structure the days the way the tide tables structured the charts. Sable folded the silk back into its wafer-square, returned it to the drawer, and sat for a moment in the lamplight with hands that had drawn coastlines for kings and could not stop trembling.

There was a knock at the door. Three knocks, spaced evenly—not the knock of authority, which hammered, nor the knock of trade, which rattled. The knock of someone who had stood at this door many times and knew its acoustics.

Maren.

* * *

Maren Quill had washed Vellamare's linen for nineteen years, and in nineteen years of boiling other people's sheets she had learned the town's true ledger. Blood came out in cold water if you got to it fast enough. Wine required salt. And secrets, in Maren's experience, always left a stain somewhere—they turned up in collar frays and hem repairs, in the shirts a man sent out twice weekly though he claimed to be a bachelor, in the child-sized smock that appeared in a great house's wash one month and vanished the next.

She had known Sable Sterling since the boy—no, she corrected herself, since *they*; the word had settled on Sable in the last years and Maren was too old a woman to argue with how a person named themselves—had come to the workshop a debt-orphan with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like a cat's before dusk. She had washed for Corvane too, and had formed her own conclusions about the master cartographer's death, conclusions she kept in the same locked place she kept everything: behind a face as expressive as a washtub.

So when the baby's crying had started drifting over the wall between the workshop yard and her drying ground, eleven nights ago, Maren had not gone knocking immediately. She had done what she always did. She had observed.

The crying stopped quickly each time. Too quickly for a novice. Someone in that workshop had learned to soothe a child with a speed that spoke of desperation rather than experience—which meant they were afraid of the sound being heard, which meant the child was a secret, which meant, in Vellamare, that the child was dangerous. Maren had counted the visits to the chandler for oil, noted that Sable had begun buying milk from the farm two streets beyond the usual dairy—a small vanity of anonymity, easily seen through—and had waited until the eleventh day, because eleven days was long enough to prove the thing would not simply vanish, and because a woman who moved too fast on other people's secrets became a woman who had to be handled.

Tonight she knocked three times and stood in the cold with her shawl pulled tight, a basket on her arm.

Sable opened the door on the second knock's echo, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across the threshold: the mapmaker who knew every line of the coast and none of the lines of a human face, and the laundress who knew every face in Vellamare and drew no maps at all.

"You'll want to come in," Sable said.

"I know," said Maren. "I brought bread."

Inside, the workshop smelled as it always had—ink and paper and lamp-oil and the faint mineral breath of the sea that got into everything in this city—but underneath it now was another smell, newer, softer. Milk. Ash and clean wool. Maren set her basket on the drafting table, saw the drawer close an inch too late, and said nothing about it, filing the closed drawer away in the ledger behind her face.

"She's asleep?" Maren asked.

"For now. She feeds at moonrise and again before dawn. She's—" Sable paused, and Maren watched the younger person grope for vocabulary, this creature fluent in latitude and longitude, hunting for words that had no coordinates. "She's small. But she's strong. She grips fingers like she means to keep them."

"They all do," Maren said. "It's how they hold on to the world before they can carry anything." She unpacked her basket with the economy of long practice: bread, a jar of rendered fat for chapping, a bundle of linen squares cut down from sheets that had worn through their centers, and a knitted cap the color of undyed wool. "From my sister's boy. He never wore it. Cold winter took him before the cap warmed."

Sable's hands went still over the bread.

"Why are you helping me?" Sable asked. "You haven't asked me one question. Not whose she is. Not why."

Maren considered the fire for a moment. "I've been asking questions for nineteen years," she said finally. "I've just never asked them out loud. I know whose sheets boil white in this quarter and whose go grey in the poorhouse. I know which houses send linen out the back gate before the magistrate's men come in the front. Eleven days ago a baby started crying over that wall, and nobody came asking after her, and in Vellamare a baby nobody asks after is either dead or expensive." She looked up, and her eyes were the flat grey of the harbor in February. "I came to find out which."

"And?"

"And I've been listening to her breathe through the wall at night," Maren said. "Alive, then. Expensive, I'd wager. Whose?"

Sable walked to the drafting table. This was the moment, and Sable knew it as a threshold, the way a surveyor knows a river crossing—one bank or the other, and no wading back. Every instinct trained by Corvane said: *say nothing, show nothing, let the neighbor remain a neighbor.* But the instinct had been losing ground for eleven days against something else, something that had arrived swaddled in cream silk, and Sable understood suddenly that the rules of silence had been written for a person with nothing to lose, and were therefore obsolete.

Sable opened the drawer and laid the silk square in the lamplight between them.

Maren bent over it. She did not touch it—nineteen years of handling other people's fabric had taught her reverence for evidence—but her eyes went over the stitching slowly, reading it the way she read cloth, and Sable watched the recognition arrive in stages: first the workmanship, then the design, and then, unmistakably, the whole of it, because Maren Quill had washed for the Government House for six years in the lean early ones, and she had seen this device a hundred times, embroidered on table linens and pressed into napkin rings and once, memorably, stitched in gold on a christening gown that had come to her wash and gone back unpressed because the household was in mourning for reasons never stated.

"That's Vane," Maren said quietly. "That's the Governor's own crest."

"The compass rose in the hand. Three stars on the chevron." Sable's voice was level, but only just. "I know because I inked it once. Onto a chart that officially never existed. His household sent instructions on how the stars were to be rendered."

Maren straightened up slowly. In the firelight her face did something complicated—pity and calculation and an old anger turning together like gears.

"There was a girl," she said. "In the Vane household. Lady's maid to the Governor's sister, or cousin, or whatever woman he's installed over there since the wife went quiet. Dark-haired thing, quick hands, laughed behind her palm. Sent north last spring. Everyone said consumption." Maren's mouth thinned. "Consumption doesn't send letters back saying *forget the face*, love. Consumption doesn't wrap its babies in the family silk."

"You're certain it's his? The crest could have been stolen. Servants steal embroidery all the—"

"Look at the stitching on the border," Maren interrupted, and turned the silk toward the light without touching it. "See how the silver's pulled tight at the corners and loose along the bottom edge? That's hasty work over finished work—someone unpicked a monogram and stitched the full crest in its place, in a hurry, by lamplight, with the wrong weight of thread for the border. Now ask yourself what a lady's maid owns that bears the full achievement of the Governor's arms. Nothing. Servants don't get crests. They get initials. Somebody *gave* her this silk, or somebody near her cut it from something of his, and either way it traveled from his hand or his household to that child's wrapping within the year." She set the silk down. "It's his. As sure as tide."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the crate by the hearth, the baby stirred and made a small sound—not quite waking, a sound like a comma in a long sentence—and both of them went silent until she settled.

"Then you understand," Sable said, when the breathing evened out again, "what I'm holding. What's in this workshop. If it isn't the child alone—it's the child *and* the atlas, and either one of them would hang us, and together—"

"Together they're worth more," Maren said flatly. "That's not a question, that's arithmetic. A Governor's bastard is leverage. A Governor's bastard raised in a room full of proof of every crime he's committed—that's not leverage, that's a verdict waiting for a court." She looked at Sable for a long moment. "You could still take her to the foundling wheel. Anonymous. Tonight. Before anyone's tracked her here. I'd carry her myself and say nothing."

The words hung in the air, and Sable found—to their own quiet astonishment—that they weighed nothing at all. They were a shape, a form, a courtesy of an option offered and declined, and beneath them lay the true answer, already fixed, already surveyed, as immovable as a headland.

"No," Sable said.

"You're certain?"

"I've been certain since the second night. When she gripped my finger." Sable almost smiled. "She means to keep things. I've decided she can start with me."

Maren nodded slowly, and something in her posture shifted—a settling, like ballast finding its place in a hull. She had come prepared, Sable realized, for either answer. She had brought the cap and the linen regardless.

"Then here's how it will be," Maren said, and her voice took on the brisk tone of a woman arranging a washing roster. "Nobody in this quarter sees you buy milk again. I've four cows' worth of custom at the Hart Street dairy and Mrs. Tallow there owes me for a mended featherbed—I'll draw your share through my own account and bring it over in the basket, under the bread, where it's been tonight. The crying: she cries at moonrise, you said? Moonrise the whole street's at supper and the shutters are closed and the wind comes off the water and carries sound the wrong way. You're lucky. But luck dries up. When the weather warms and windows open, we'll need a plan for the noise, and I have one—there's a cellar under my drying shed with a brick oven nobody's used since the fever year. Warm. Quiet. Thick walls." She paused. "And when the midwives come asking—you mark me, they will, a baby's birth gets whispered about even when it shouldn't be—I'm a widow what lost her own, and widows are permitted a boarder's brat if they say it's their daughter's. From now on, if anyone asks, she's mine. My daughter's girl, staying with the mapmaker because my daughter's sick and I can't nurse and mind my own wash both. It's a good lie. It has sad bits in it. Sad lies hold best."

Sable stared at her. "You'd lie for her? For me? You barely know—"

"I know exactly what I know," Maren said. "I know Corvane didn't fall off that jetty by accident, whatever the coroner wrote. I know you've kept this shop alive ten years alone in a city that eats shops like yours. And I know that a man who'd drown a village to steal its land"—she tapped the silk square once, lightly, with one work-hardened finger—"will not hesitate over a baby girl who grows into a claim. You're not hiding a bastard, love. You're keeping evidence with a heartbeat. So am I, now."

The words fell into the room and lay there, and Sable understood that something had just ended and something else had begun: the long solitary decade, the single set of footprints in the snow outside the workshop, the whole architecture of silence built for one occupant—all of it dissolved in the space between the fire's crackle and the baby's next breath. Complicity had become alliance. A laundress with a locked face and a ledger full of the town's stains had just signed herself into a conspiracy with a mapmaker and an infant, over bread and rendered fat, and the treaty had taken less than an hour to draft.

"Why?" Sable asked again, softly. "Truly."

Maren was quiet for a while. She looked at the crate by the hearth, at the edge of a shawl visible above its rim, moving faintly with breath.

"My boy would be nine," she said at last. "Cold winter, like I told you. The doctor came too late and cost too much, in that order. Nobody came late or cheap for him because nobody important needed him alive." Her jaw worked once. "This one, somebody important needs her *not* to exist. Well. I've spent nineteen years making important things white and clean and folded, and I can tell you the one truth of the trade: what's been washed can be washed again. Whatever they're trying to scrub out of the world—" she nodded at the crate, at the silk, at the whole shadowed architecture of the workshop's secrets— "it comes through my hands now too. And I don't lose things I'm holding."

Above the hearth, the baby woke properly then, and announced it, and the conspiracy sprang into its first coordinated action: Maren moved to the crate with hands that remembered everything, lifting the child with the practiced sweep of nineteen years, murmuring the low nonsense syllables that calm them, while Sable warmed the milk pan and measured the fire and thought, absurdly, of contour lines—of how a landscape of loneliness could be remapped so completely in a single evening, new features rising where the old charts showed empty water.

The child fed, gripping Maren's little finger with the whole of her fierce small strength, and Maren looked down at her and then across at Sable, and her face—the washtub face, the locked ledger—did something it had not done in years. It unlocked, briefly, from the inside.

"She'll want a name eventually," Maren said. "And it can't be a name anyone in this city would recognize."

"I know," Sable said. "I've been thinking about names."

"Don't tell me yet." Maren tucked the linen square more snugly around the small shoulders. "Names said too early get carried off with wishes. Wait till she's a year old and the century's turned. New year, new name, new hundred years. Old folk wisdom, but the old folk were usually hiding from somebody, so they knew."

They sat then, the three of them, in the circle of firelight, the mapmaker and the laundress and the evidence with a heartbeat, and outside the wind came off the water carrying the wrong-way sounds of a city settling into sleep—a watchman's bell, a dog, the creak of ships at their moorings in a harbor whose true soundings existed only on a hidden chart, in a hidden atlas, in a hidden drawer, beneath a hidden silk.

It was later—much later, when Maren had gone home through the cold and the candle had burned to a thumbnail of itself—that Sable opened the drawer one final time and looked at the crest in the dying light. *Quod teneas, tenebris.* What you hold, hold in darkness.

"Well," Sable said aloud to the empty workshop, to the sleeping child, to the decade of silences and the decade to come, "you held me in darkness, Governor. Let's see how you like the dawn."

And it was in this mood—defiant, exhausted, tender, afraid—that Sable trimmed the wick and retired, and did not see, because the shutter was closed and the hour was late, the figure who had paused across the lane in the black gap between the chandler's and the cooper's sheds: a man in a plain coat with a surveyor's case at his hip, who stood looking at the workshop's lit window for a long moment, making some private notation against some private chart, before walking on into the dark with the measured tread of a man who counts his paces.
