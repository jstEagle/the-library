## 6. The Atlas's Missing Leaf

The atlas had twenty-three leaves. Sable knew this the way other people knew their own hands—not as a fact remembered, but as a thing felt, always, in the dark.

For ten years the count had been a ritual. Every leaf of Corvane's great forbidden work had its place in the false bottom of the drafting cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp that came off the harbor each autumn, and every night before Sable banked the fire and climbed the narrow stair to the loft, they counted. Twenty-three. The count was a prayer, a heartbeat, a lock. On the night of the spring tide, working late over the chart of the eastern garrison with Elowen asleep in the old fish-creel Maren had lined with wool, Sable lifted out the leaves one by one to lay them in order for the final collation—and counted twenty-two.

They counted again. Their fingers were steady. They were proud of that, later, that their fingers did not shake: twenty-two, and then twenty-two once more, and then a third time performed slowly, leaf by leaf, naming each in Corvane's own ordering as the master had taught it—*coastlines first, then roads, then what men have built and what men have buried*—and the name that would not come, the leaf that was not there, was the twelfth. The land grants. Vane's grants.

Sable sat down on the drafting stool very slowly, the way a person sits when the floor has become untrustworthy.

* * *

Understand what the twelfth leaf was. Understand why the room seemed to tilt without it.

Two years before his death, Corvane had been commissioned—or so it was framed—to survey the inland parcels along the river road, the orchard country above Vellamare where the fog thinned and the soil ran black and generous. Sable had been sixteen then, allowed to carry the chains and hold the staves, allowed to ink fair copies at night while the master drank his bitter tea and read the day's numbers aloud. And somewhere in the third week of that survey, Corvane had gone quiet in a way Sable had never heard before or since. He had spread three documents on the workbench—the Governor's grants, sealed in red wax heavy enough to sink in water—and laid his fresh charts beside them, and said nothing for the better part of an hour.

It was Sable, young and careless and proud of a newly trained eye, who saw it first. The boundaries did not agree. Not by a furlong, not by a surveyor's tolerable error. The grants described parcels that reached, in places, a full half-mile beyond anything any legitimate instrument had ever measured—swallowing commons, swallowing a freehold here, a mill there, swallowing whole stretches of land whose families had held them since before Vane's grandfather had come to the colony. The seals were right. The script was right. The hand that had drawn the boundary lines was almost perfect.

Almost. A forged line, Corvane taught afterward, betrays itself the way a liar does: not in what it says, but in what it avoids. The false boundaries always bent around something. Around wells. Around graves. Around the parcels of men who had quarreled with the Governor and would find their land quietly annexed by ink.

Corvane had made the twelfth leaf himself, alone, locking the workshop door even against his apprentice. It was a single folio comparing the true surveys to the granted ones—a confession drawn in lines, every theft made visible, every forgery annotated in his small fierce copperplate. He never let Sable copy it. He only said, the one time Sable asked, *That leaf will outlive us both or ruin us both, and I have not yet decided which,* and he laughed, and the laugh had frightened Sable more than the words.

Now it was gone. Not lost—gone. The oilcloth was folded back precisely as Sable left it. The false bottom sat flush. Nothing else had been disturbed. Someone had come into the workshop, known exactly where to look among all the hiding places a cartographer accumulates across a lifetime, taken the one leaf worth hanging for, and closed the drawer like a courteous guest.

Except.

Sable knelt at the cabinet for a long time, breathing the smell of linseed and cold ash, and forced the thought through in order, the way one plots a position from bearings.

No one had come in. Not through the door, whose hinges Sable oiled and whose sill wore a hair-seal of wax each night, unbroken. Not through the shutters. Not down the chimney, which still held its winter draw. Which meant the leaf had not been taken last night at all. Which meant—

Which meant Sable could not say when it had been taken. Days ago. Weeks. Perhaps longer. The nightly count confirmed presence, not timing. The thief could have come and gone while Sable slept above, while Elowen cried in the creel, while Maren clattered tubs in the yard. The leaf might have been gone since the snow.

And if someone knew about the twelfth leaf—if someone knew the atlas well enough to take exactly that and nothing else—then that someone knew the atlas existed at all. And Sable Sterling, who had spent ten years being no one, invisible, meticulous, safe, understood with a cold settling clarity that the hunting had been running the other direction all along.

* * *

Morning came gray off the water. Sable did not sleep. There had been years, in the beginning after Corvane died, when sleeplessness had been grief; now it was discipline. By lamplight Sable examined the workshop the way the master had taught examination of any chart claimed to be true: trust nothing because it looks familiar, measure everything, believe the evidence over the memory.

The evidence was maddeningly thin. Dust undisturbed on the window ledges. The wax seal on the door sill intact—no, wait. Sable crouched. The wax was intact, but it had been *pressed*, faintly, by a fingertip testing rather than breaking, the print of a glove's stitching ghosted into the surface and then smoothed. Someone had stood at the door, tried it gently, found it fast, and moved on—or come back another way. Sable followed the floorboards on hands and knees, a lamp low, until near the yard-side wall the light caught it: a scuff in the decades-old wax polish, a half-moon, fresh, beside the iron ring of the old coal chute, bricked up since before the war. Except the bricks were not original. Sable had never looked closely before. The mortar was paler, newer, and one course, at knee height, sat proud by the width of a knife blade.

Loose. It came away with a fingertip's pull, quiet as a page turning, and behind it darkness breathed out—the smell of old coal, harbor rot, and, faintly, unmistakably, fresh air. The chute ran under the yard into the culvert that drained toward the drowned quarter. Corvane had surveyed those tunnels once. Of course he had. Of course he had known this door into his own workshop and told no one, and either he had never known someone else knew it too, or—

Or he had left it findable, for whoever came after him. Sable put the brick back and sat in the gray light with their arms around their knees and refused, for one full minute, to think about which possibility was worse.

Elowen woke at dawn with her particular imperious cough, and the morning became milk and porridge and the small tyranny of a child who had decided the brass dividers were hers by right of reaching. Sable worked one-handed through the early hours, holding her on a hip while pretending to work, and it was not pretense really; the work steadied both of them. But beneath the ordinary motions the mind kept returning to the arithmetic of danger. Someone had entered and exited without disturbing so much as a shaving on the floor. That was not a burglar's skill. That was a professional's—someone trained in entry, in silence, in leaving rooms as arguments rather than crimes: *I was here. I could have taken everything. I took one thing.*

An argument aimed at whom? At Corvane, dead these ten years? Or at Sable, whom the intruder perhaps believed did not exist?

* * *

Maren arrived with the mid-morning washing, and Sable watched her come up the lane and understood, watching, how much the workshop had changed in four months. Once Sable had known every approach to the door out of fear. Now there was a face Sable was glad to see coming, and the gladness was its own vulnerability, and there was nothing to be done about it. Some things you chose knowing the cost. Corvane had taught maps; life had taught the rest.

"You've the look of a man who's found a wrong coordinate," Maren said, setting down her basket. She said *man* the way she always did, lazily, having decided years ago that Sable was whatever shape suited the sentence, and Sable had long ago stopped correcting anyone. Names were currency. Sable spent sparingly.

"The atlas," Sable said, and stopped, glancing at the open door, the lane, the gull-hung sky. "Inside."

They spoke in the back room with the kettle going, Elowen on Maren's shoulder, and Sable laid it out plainly because Maren was the only person alive who could be lied to usefully and therefore the only person who must not be lied to at all. The missing leaf. The brick in the wall. The argument-shaped absence of the theft.

Maren listened the way she did everything, with her chin slightly down and her eyes doing the real work, and when Sable finished she was quiet for the space of two waves against the quay.

"Who knows the atlas has that leaf in it?" she asked.

"No one living, I thought." Sable turned the cooling kettle handle without drinking. "Corvane made it alone. He showed me nothing of it. But Hale—" The name came out before the decision to say it. "Hale asked for Corvane's papers in the winter. He knew the word *atlas*. He used it like a man handling a knife he'd been given by someone who'd used it before."

"The surveyor." Maren shifted Elowen, who was gnawing the collar of her shawl with governmental seriousness. "He had soft boots on, that one. You notice when you wash for a living—men's boots tell you where they go at night, and his went places boots like his don't admit to." She frowned at the wall shared with the yard, the wall with the coal chute behind it. "You'll want to know whether they took it or whether they're telling you they know it's hidden. Those are different animals."

"How?"

"If they wanted it, they'd have searched till they found it. Nobody digs a tunnel into a workshop and stops at one drawer." Maren bounced Elowen absently, an old rhythm worn into her bones that she performed without seeming to know it, and Sable had learned not to look directly at her face when she did. "Taking one leaf and leaving the rest—that's a message. *We know your book.* Meant to make you run, or burn it, or come to heel. Question is, who's the message for, if the master's ten years dead."

The question sat between them like a fourth person. Sable rose, crossed to the drafting cabinet, and stood with a hand flat on its lid.

"There's one more thing," Sable said. "If the leaf is gone, then the atlas is incomplete. And an incomplete proof is worse than none—it invites doubt, and doubt protects him." Vane's name went unsaid, as it always did, a stone in the mouth of every sentence about him. "But if Corvane hid the leaf elsewhere—if he *always* kept it elsewhere, and what was in my cabinet was only ever—" Sable stopped, recalculating ten years of assumptions. "He hid things from me. He loved me and he hid things from me. Both of those are true, and I don't know yet which one matters."

Maren looked at her for a moment, and something moved behind her eyes—pity, maybe, or recognition, the look of a woman who had also been trusted imperfectly by the world.

"Then you'd best learn to think like him," she said. "Where does a mapmaker hide a map?"

Sable almost laughed. Almost. "In plain sight. In another map. In a place so honest no one thinks to check it." And then the thought arrived, fully formed, the way Corvane's lessons sometimes returned years late and perfectly preserved: the master's habit of mounting finished commissions onto linen-backed boards, the backs of which were never examined because backs are nobody's business. The commissioned charts of Vellamare's harbor improvements, framed in the workshop, hanging on the walls where any visitor could see them, where *everyone* had seen them—Vane's own surveyor among them—for ten years.

"I need to check something," Sable said.

* * *

The harbor commission charts hung three deep on the north wall, glazed, gilt-cornered, dull with respectability: the new quays, the customs house frontage, the planned esplanade. Corvane's finest public work, praised in the town records, signed by the Governor himself in the lower margin. Sable had dusted them weekly for a decade and never once thought past the glass.

The frame of the middle chart came apart with the same knife that fit the brick. Behind the backing board, glued flat to the linen, lay a second sheet—one Sable had never seen, in a hand Sable knew better than their own.

Not the twelfth leaf. Something older. A survey of the drowned quarter, dated eleven years back, in Corvane's hand, showing the old harbor village street by street, hearth by hearth—every household marked with a small careful symbol, and beside the symbols, in margins grown cramped with anger, figures. Compensation paid: nothing. Orders issued: *by His Excellency's direction.* Dates. Depths. The tide tables for the night the sluices were opened.

Sable read it standing in the middle of the workshop with the chart frame gaping open like a wound, and understood two things at once, in the order they needed understanding. First: Corvane had hidden the truth of the drowned quarter behind the Governor's own portrait of himself, where it had hung in plain view through every inspection, every visit from Hale, every year of Sable's guardianship—and Sable had dusted it.

Second: this was not the twelfth leaf. This was a different confession entirely, which meant the twelfth leaf—the grants—was hidden somewhere else, by some other logic, and the person who had taken it from the cabinet might have taken a decoy. Or might have taken the true original and left Sable guarding an empty shell. Or—

The knock at the street door came at dusk, two blows, unhurried.

Sable's hands did not shake. Ten years of practice held them still as they reassembled the frame, wiped the knife, set Elowen's creel in its place, and crossed the workshop with the lamp raised. Through the shutter slats: the lane, empty. The door, dark. And pinned to the door with a fine silver pin, at the height of a standing man's eye, a single sheet of good paper, blank except for a compass rose drawn in faded brown ink—drawn in the exact style of Corvane's own mark, the mark that appeared on the atlas's title leaf, the mark that no living person outside this workshop had any earthly reason to know.

Behind Sable, in the creel, Elowen stirred and began, softly, to cry.
