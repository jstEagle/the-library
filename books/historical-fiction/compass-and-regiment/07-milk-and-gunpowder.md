## 7. Milk and Gunpowder

Maren Quill had washed the linens of half the great houses in Vellamare, and she had learned this: every household kept one drawer that was not to be opened, and the size of the drawer told you everything about the size of the sin. The Governor's house on the hill kept a whole wing. The workshop below the mapmaker's stair kept only a cradle — but Maren had never in her life seen a drawer so heavy.

She rocked it with her foot now, one heel drumming the floorboards, and watched Elowen's face screw itself toward a cry. Outside, boots on cobbles. Not two men's worth of boots. Six, at least, moving in the unhurried rhythm of authority, the rhythm of men who had never once been made to run.

"Hush," Maren breathed, though the baby had not yet cried. "Hush now, little ember. Not yet. Not for them."

The solstice child — three months old and round as a moon, with her father's jaw already asserting itself beneath the milk-fat, God help them all — opened her mouth. Maren did what her own mother had done, what laundresses and fishwives had done since there were mouths in the world to fill: she pressed the child to her shoulder, tucked the shawl close, and began to walk. Walk, walk, walk. The floorboards of the workshop were mapped by now in Maren's feet as surely as any chart on the walls above her. Third plank from the hearth sang; avoid it. The corner by the drying racks held its breath and let you pass silent. She walked the room like a woman crossing ice.

The boots stopped outside.

Through the fogged glass she saw the lanterns swing, saw the yellow smear of light cross Sable Sterling's careful shelves, where the atlases stood spined and innocent and full of treason. Sable themselves was up the stairs, at the workbench, playing at sleeplessness over a chart of the northern coast — playing, because Sable played everything, even fear, with the same maddening precision they gave to ink lines. That left the baby to Maren. It always left the baby to Maren. She had agreed to it in the first week with a nod and no words, because some bargains are signed in the body rather than on paper.

Elowen's cry came anyway — a small furious squall, quickly smothered against Maren's breast — and the knocking started.

* * *

She had lost a child once. She did not say it. In Vellamare you did not say such things; grief was a linen you wrung out in private and hung where the neighbors could not read it.

Her boy had been called Tam. He had lived eleven days, and she had counted each of them the way misers count coins, and on the twelfth morning she had woken with her arms full of nothing and gone back to washing other people's sheets because the world does not pause its dirt for your dead. Ten years now. Ten years, and she could still feel the specific weight of him — not the general weight of a baby, which is a weight everyone knows, but his weight, seven pounds and a shrike's-bone lightness at the wrists, the way his head had fit into the cup of her collarbone like something drawn to scale.

When they put Elowen into her arms the first time — Sable holding the bundle out at arm's length as though it might go off, which, Maren thought, it very well might — her arms had known the weight before her mind did. Her arms had said *yes, this one, I remember how*. And she had taken the child, and looked down at the absurd scrap of embroidered silk tucked beneath the blanket, and understood that she was already lost.

Because here was the thing Maren knew and Sable only suspected: keeping a child was not a kindness. Keeping a child in Vellamare, in the year of our Lord 1800, in a workshop stuffed with a Governor's secrets — keeping a child was an act of war. Every feed was ammunition carried across open ground. Every hushed cry in the night was a shot fired and hoped unheard. You could dress motherhood in aprons and lullabies all you liked; underneath, it was gunpowder, measured out in spoonfuls, stored in the warm dark, waiting.

The knocking became pounding.

Maren climbed the back stair with Elowen pressed beneath her heart and settled into the alcove behind the drying racks where the plaster held warmth, and she listened while Sable descended, unhurried, to answer the door.

* * *

They were polite, these ones. That was worse than shouting. A lieutenant with red cuffs and a clerk with cold-chapped fingers, asking after shipping manifests, asking whether Master Corvane's establishment had kept records of coastal soundings, asking — with the terrible casualness of a man reading from someone else's list — whether there were children on the premises.

"No children here," Sable said. Their voice did the thing Maren admired and distrusted in equal measure: it went flat and clean as fresh paper. "A quiet house. Maps don't cry."

The lieutenant laughed at that, the laugh of a man who has been ordered to find something and has not found it and will report whatever is convenient. His eyes went past Sable's shoulder, along the shelves, across the rafters, and Maren stood in the dark of the alcove with Elowen awake against her chest and played the oldest game of her trade: she became linen. Neutral. White. Hung out to dry, noticing nothing, meaning nothing. She had done it in the Governor's own house when the steward's wife wept behind doors; she could do it now.

But Elowen could not do it. Elowen was three months old and had no talent for neutrality, and Maren felt the cry gathering in the small body like weather — felt the indrawn breath, the clench of the fists against her breastbone — and she did the thing her body knew before shame could object: she put her nipple in the child's mouth.

There was no milk worth mentioning anymore. Ten years dry. But the baby did not care about milk; the baby cared about silence, and the shape in her mouth bought it, and Maren stood in the dark with her bodice open and her heart hammering and fed a lie to a soldier's child while soldiers searched the house of maps.

*This*, she thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. *This is what I am for.* Not the wash-tubs, not the white linen, not the ten thousand sheets wrung for other people's beds. This. Standing between a child and the world's noise, armed with nothing but her own body and its stubborn refusal to be finished with mothering. Let them come with their manifests and their lists. She had hidden cries before. She had hidden a whole grave's worth of crying, alone, at nineteen, with no one to knock on any door at all.

The door closed. The boots receded. After a decent interval, Sable's voice came up the stair, low:

"They're counting our windows, Maren. Did you see? The clerk wrote them down."

"I saw." She emerged, tucking herself away, and handed the drowsy bundle back — and then, on impulse older than sense, didn't hand it back. Kept her. "They'll be back."

"They'll be back," Sable agreed, and for once the flat paper voice failed, and underneath it Maren heard the thing she had heard the night Sable first put the baby in her arms: not fear exactly. Want. A want so long unowned it had rusted over, like a key in a lock nobody turns.

"Then we'd best decide what they find," Maren said, and bounced the child, and did not give her back just yet.

* * *

Days took on the shape of siege, though a gentle siege, a domestic one. Maren came at dawn and stayed past dusk. She washed diapers in the same copper she used for Sable's shirts and refused to feel anything about it. She learned the geography of the child: the dimple at the base of Elowen's spine like a thumbprint of some approving angel; the milk-rash on her cheek that flared when she was overtired; the way she slept best with a hand fisted against Maren's collarbone, gripping the fabric as if to say *mine, this one is mine, I have chosen her.*

Sable drew charts by day and burned the drafts by night, and watched Maren nurse with an expression Maren could not name until one evening she could.

"You look at her like a cartographer looks at an unmapped coast," Maren said.

"I look at her like someone who was never on any map at all," Sable answered, and went back to the ink, and that was the closest either of them came, in those weeks, to saying what the workshop had become: not a hiding place. A country. Small, illegal, defended by a laundress and a liar of an apprentice, population three.

Maren taught Sable the things ink could not teach. How to swaddle so the child felt held and not buried. The five cries and their meanings — hunger, wind, wet, tired, and the fifth one, the wordless one, the one that meant only *be near me*. Sable took to it the way Sable took to everything, with a terrifying studiousness, making notes in the margin of a scrap paper Maren pretended not to see: *fifth cry = proximity.* As if love were a legend to be translated.

And Sable taught Maren things too, though neither called it teaching. Which neighbors reported and to whom. Which knock meant a customer and which meant a question. That the man who sold chestnuts on the corner had watched the workshop for two years, and that his chestnuts were, in Maren's professional opinion, terrible, and that this was no excuse.

Once — only once — Maren asked it aloud.

"The Governor's blood, in her. You know it. I've seen the silk. What happens when she grows up looking like him?"

Sable cleaned their pen with terrible care. Then: "Corvane used to say a map is a confession. Everything on it is a choice about what to admit." A pause. "She isn't his map. She'll draw her own."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." And Sable looked up, and Maren saw that under the flat paper surface the apprentice was drowning quietly, had been drowning since the solstice, and would drown forever rather than put the child down. Well. Maren knew about that. She had drowned ten years ago and never bothered surfacing.

"Then it will do," she said gruffly, and that was the end of the conversation, the way such conversations end in Vellamare: not concluded, merely shelved, like a dangerous folio in a forbidden atlas.

* * *

The inspections came again — twice more before the month turned. Each time the ritual: boots, knocking, flat paper voice, alcove, and the old lie offered at Maren's breast. Each time the questions narrowed, the way a channel narrows toward a weir. First they asked about Corvane's papers. Then about Corvane's clients. Then, on the third visit, the clerk asked — without looking up from his list — whether the workshop had ever received deliveries of infant linen.

Maren, in the alcove, felt the question go through her like a needle through folded cloth.

Infant linen. Someone had done sums. Someone knew a washerwoman came daily and stayed late, and someone had reasoned that a woman who stays is a woman who tends, and someone — a steward, most likely, a man paid to notice drawers that stay shut — had written *child?* in the margin of a list, and the margin note had traveled downhill until it reached this cold-fingered clerk.

Afterward she told Sable. She laid it out plainly, the way she laid out washed linen, everything smoothed and visible.

"They're not hunting the atlas anymore. Or not only. They've guessed there's a child."

Sable sat very still. Outside, the chestnut man coughed on his corner, faithful as a clock.

"Then we change the pattern," Sable said at last. "You can't come daily. It marks you."

"Don't be stupid."

"Maren—"

"I said don't." She heard her own voice crack on it, heard ten years of Tam in the crack, and mastered it. "You'll change the pattern when the pattern needs changing. Not before. And not by putting that child further from me." She gathered the sleeping baby, the folded diapers, her shawl, her fury, her tenderness, all of it, into her arms. "I buried one already. I know the exact weight of what I'm carrying. Do you?"

Sable had no answer to that. Nobody did. It was the one question in Vellamare with no honest chart.

At the door, wrapping herself against the harbor wind, Maren paused. Down the lane, two figures stood beneath the lamp that never worked properly — and she noticed, because noticing was her trade, that tonight it worked perfectly, and that they were not lovers and not drunkards and not waiting for anyone who might plausibly arrive. They were simply standing. Watching the workshop's windows. Counting, perhaps, again.

One of them turned his head as she stepped out, and touched his hat — courteous, unhurried, the gesture of a man confirming a detail for a report — and Maren walked home through the dark with Elowen's breath warm against her neck and her mind already working like a washtub, churning everything over, looking for what would come up.

By the time she reached her own door she knew two things with a laundress's certainty. The raid was coming soon — days, not weeks. And whoever those men answered to, he had begun to suspect that the Governor's bastard slept under a mapmaker's roof.

She kissed the small dimpled head and whispered it, so the child would grow up knowing her first lesson: "We see them before they see us, little ember. That's how we'll win."
