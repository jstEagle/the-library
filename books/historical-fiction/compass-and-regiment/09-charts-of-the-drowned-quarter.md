## 9. Charts of the Drowned Quarter

The marginalia had been waiting for Sable all along.

They found it on a Tuesday of grey rain, hunched over Corvane's working copy of the coastal survey—the one with his cramped annotations in the gutters, the one Sable had read a hundred times and thought they had exhausted. But grief and fear had made them a careless reader for ten years, and it was only now, with Elowen asleep against Maren's shoulder across the room and the atlas's missing leaf gnawing at them like a rotten tooth, that Sable saw what the master had left in plain sight.

In the margin beside the chart of the old harbor, in ink so pale it might have been water itself, Corvane had written: *The quarter keeps its own records. Ask the church that isn't there.*

Sable sat back. The rain ticked against the shutters. Across the workshop, Maren looked up from her mending, reading Sable's face the way she read everything—laundry lines, harbor weather, the lies men told their wives.

"You've found something," she said. Not a question.

"Maybe nothing." Sable traced the words with a fingertip, not touching the ink. "Maybe everything."

* * *

The drowned quarter lay south of the new harbor like a secret the city had agreed to stop telling. Once it had been Saltmere—a fishing village older than Vellamare's charter, if the stories were true—and then the Governor's engineers had built the new breakwater, and the sea had been invited to do what the law could not. The houses still stood, or the upper storeys of them did, rising out of green-grey water at low tide like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. The church tower was the last thing to go under; now even its weathervane was a rumor beneath the surface.

Children were told the village had flooded by accident. Children were told many things.

Sable went at first light, before the fisherfolk who scavenged the shallows came out with their hooks and nets, rowing a borrowed skiff through mist so thick it seemed the century itself had not yet decided to arrive. The oars made no sound worth mentioning. The water made no sound at all. It was the silence of the place that undid Sable—not emptiness but *withheldness*, the silence of a room where something has been said and everyone present has agreed never to say it again.

Corvane had surveyed here. That much Sable had always known; the master's early work bore the Governor's commission seal, the elegant coastal charts that had made his reputation. What Sable had never asked—what an apprentice does not ask—is why a man who could draw beauty like that had stopped taking commissions, and why every chart he drew afterward seemed to carry a small weight of anger in its lines, visible only to someone who loved him enough to look.

*Ask the church that isn't there.*

The tower's crown broke the surface at dead low tide, a knuckle of black stone furred with weed. Sable shipped the oars and let the skiff drift close, then closer, until the hull kissed stone. Below, through water the color of bottle glass, the ghost of a doorway was just visible—an arch, a threshold, steps descending into green dark.

Sable had brought rope, a lantern, oilskin wrapped around the lantern's glass. They had brought, though they would not have admitted it aloud, the small iron pry-bar Corvane had kept beneath the floorboards, the one whose purpose no apprentice had ever learned.

The water was cold as a held breath.

* * *

Down was easier than down should have been. The flooded nave opened around Sable like the inside of a bell, and when their boot heel struck stone, the sound rolled away into darkness and came back changed, deeper, as though the church were answering in a language it had kept for thirty years. Sable stood chest-deep on the submerged floor of Saltmere's church and felt the whole drowned village listening.

The lantern threw a trembling coin of light. Sable turned slowly. Pews, or the shapes of pews, soft with silt. A font like a sleeping bird. And above the arch where the chancel had begun—there. Faint under the weed and the years: carved letters. Sable scraped with a thumbnail, with the pry-bar's flat, holding the lantern close, and the letters surfaced one by one out of the green like bodies rising.

*RECORDARE.*

Remember.

Not a motto. An instruction.

Sable's hands were shaking, and it was not entirely the cold. Because now they understood the shape of Corvane's marginalia—not a riddle but a route. The master had known this place. Had stood where Sable stood, or near enough, with his instruments and his commission papers and the Governor's money in his purse, and he had seen what the water covered, and he had come back afterward, alone, in whatever year it was that his charts turned angry, and he had hidden something here the way a man hides a knife under his tongue.

The crypt stairs were behind the chancel, half-collapsed, running up rather than down—which meant the crypt itself sat above the waterline, sealed in its stone pocket like air in an upturned cup. Sable climbed with the lantern held high, breath loud in the narrow dark, and at the top found a door of iron-banded oak that gave to the pry-bar with a groan that seemed to come from Sable's own chest.

Inside: dry air, thick with the smell of old paper and pigeon lime and time.

And shelves.

* * *

Not treasure. Records.

Sable stood in the little round chamber at the tower's heart and turned in a slow circle, lantern high, and understood that they were standing inside the thing the Governor had paid to erase. Deeds. Baptismal rolls. A lease signed in 1741. Tithe books with their leather spines cracked like old bread. The parish register of Saltmere, open to a page where generations of names ran down in a fair brown hand—families who had held these lands since before Vellamare had walls, families whose claims the new harbor's engineers had needed to make vanish, because paper claims are only dangerous while the paper exists.

Someone had saved all of it. Carried it up these stairs, plank by plank, book by book, before the water came—or after, Sable thought, after, in the nights following, when the village was already drowning and the saving of paper was the only rescue left anyone could manage.

On the shelf's end, squared and neat, lay a document Sable knew without touching. Knew it the way you know your own handwriting in a stranger's letter.

It was the missing leaf.

Or rather—it was the original. The map of the Governor's land grants, drawn in Corvane's hand, the hand Sable had spent ten years learning to imitate stroke by stroke. And beside it, folded twice, a surveyor's field book in a different hand entirely, with dates and measurements and, on the flyleaf, a name: *A. Hale.*

Sable did not move for a long moment. The lantern hissed. Somewhere below, water lapped the drowned pews with the patience of a creditor.

Then they opened the map, and the horror arrived not as a shout but as a slow cold understanding, the way floodwater arrives—first at the ankles, then the knees, then past any height you can stand in.

The grants were forged. Sable had suspected; suspicion is a cartographer's constant companion. But here was proof drawn in the master's finest hand, each forged signature rendered exactly, annotated in Corvane's small furious script with the registry numbers of the true deeds—deeds that named Saltmere's families, deeds that predated every grant by decades, deeds that had been declared lost in a fire that had never happened. And in the margin, in the same pale ink as the working copy at home, one line:

*They did not drown a village. They drowned forty-one witnesses. I measured the water myself.*

Forty-one.

Sable counted the names in the baptismal roll without meaning to. Forty-one adults of age to hold title, the year the breakwater closed. The arithmetic of it sat in Sable's stomach like swallowed stone. This was what the beauty had been for. All those luminous coastal charts, those commissions that made Corvane's name—the elegance had been the payment, the price paid *to* him for drawing the drowning beforehand, measuring the water levels, marking the depths where a church tower would vanish. He had been hired to make murder look like weather.

And he had done it. That was the part Sable could not turn away from, kneeling in the dry dark with the master's confession in their hands. He had done it, taken the coin, signed the beautiful charts—and then he had spent the rest of his life building this: a cache, a record, a *recordare*, a debt of paper he intended the world to collect. Every rule he taught—*a map is a confession*—had been a man teaching his executioner's trade to the child who would finish his penance.

Sable thought of Elowen asleep against Maren's shoulder, and had to put a hand flat against the cold stone wall.

* * *

They took nothing at first. Then took everything, because leaving it was leaving it to the water and the worms and the next fire that never happened. The documents went into the oilskin satchel in careful bundles, the way Corvane had taught: flat, supported, never folded along old creases. The forged-grant map went last, wrapped separately, against Sable's chest.

Rowing back through the thinning mist, Sable passed the scavengers coming out—two men and a boy poling a flat barge among the rooftops, hooking up lead pipe and door fittings. The boy waved. Sable raised a hand and could not speak. The boy's grandfather might have been one of the forty-one. The boy would never know there had ever been anything to know.

That was how erasure worked, Sable thought. Not with soldiers. With silence, generation over generation, until the drowned quarter was just a place where children lost hooks and old men said the fishing was better before the breakwater, and no one asked *before what?*

By the time the skiff bumped the workshop's private stair, the sun was fully up and ordinary, and Maren was waiting at the door with Elowen on her hip and a question already hardening in her face.

"You're white as sailcloth," Maren said. "And you're carrying that bag like it's a baby."

"It is," Sable said. "Sort of."

They spread the documents on the long table, weighted at the corners with inkstones, and Maren—who could not read more than her name and prices—read the situation perfectly anyway. She touched the baptismal roll with one launderer's finger, gentle as touching a sleeping child.

"What is it?"

"A village," Sable said. "Saltmere. There was a village where the drowned quarter is. It didn't flood. It was *flooded*. To destroy the deeds." Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "The Governor's deeds. The ones in the missing leaf—they're forgeries, Maren. Built on graves."

Maren was quiet a moment. She had buried a child herself; Sable knew it the way one knows such things about a friend, without it ever being said entire. When she spoke, her voice had the flat calm of deep water.

"And nobody stopped him."

"Nobody knew."

"Somebody knew." Her eyes came up, sharp. "Somebody always knows. Laundresses know. Gravediggers know. Somebody carried these books up that tower, and that somebody didn't drown."

Sable had already thought of it, and the thought frightened worse than the forgery did. Whoever had saved the records had also, presumably, saved copies of their own—or remembered—or told someone. And Corvane's marginalia pointed here like a finger bone. If Sable could follow it, so could Hale. So could the Governor's steward. So could anyone who had ever watched the master too closely.

"We have to move all of it," Sable said. "Tonight. Out of the district."

"Where?"

Sable looked at the table, at the atlas lying closed beneath its loose boards, at the child watching them both from Maren's hip with the grave, unblinking attention infants give to matters of state.

"The cathedral," Sable said slowly, the idea assembling itself even as they spoke it. "The crypt. Magistrates keep their archives there—records even the Governor can't burn without burning the law itself. Corvane trusted the church that isn't there. Maybe the church that *is* there will hold what's left."

Maren snorted softly. "You trust priests now?"

"I trust stone older than Aldous Vane."

But even as they began wrapping the bundles again, Sable's eye kept returning to the field book with *A. Hale* on its flyleaf. The surveyor's own notes. His measurements of the water, his dates, his initials—proof that Hale had walked this ground before the drowning, or shortly after, and had written down what he saw. Either Hale had helped bury the truth, or he had recorded it and spent his life since as a keeper of a confession he'd never delivered.

Either way, Sable realized, folding the field book into their coat, the surveyor was not merely hunting the atlas.

He was tied to it. Bound to Saltmere the way a man is bound to a crime scene he once measured—and somewhere in Vellamare, sooner than Sable liked, he was going to learn that someone had just opened the church that wasn't there.

The knock, when it came, was three days later—but Sable would remember, forever after, that they had already known who it would be before Maren turned the latch.
