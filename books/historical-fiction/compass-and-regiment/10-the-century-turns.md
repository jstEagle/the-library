## 10. The Century Turns

The last day of the year began with frost on the inside of the workshop windows.

Sable found it there at dawn, ferning across the lower panes in patterns no cartographer could have improved upon—coastlines of ice, bays and inlets drawn by cold on glass—and stood a long while watching them fade as the fire took the chill from the room. Behind them, the harbor lightened by degrees: the masts first, black and bare as winter trees; then the water, the color of old pewter; then the roofs of the Lower Town climbing the hillside toward the workshop door, smoking already, alive with the small sounds of a city that had decided, against all evidence, that the coming year would be kinder than the last.

Elowen was awake. Sable could hear her before reaching the crib—not crying but talking, in her private language of vowels and clicks, addressing the wooden horse Maren had carved for her from a broken yardstick. The child stood gripping the rail, her hair catching the firelight, and when she saw Sable she raised both arms with the absolute confidence of someone who has never once been refused.

"Up," she said. Her first true word after *no*, and Sable had privately ranked them accordingly.

"One year old today," Sable told her, lifting her into the warmth against their chest. "One whole year. Do you know what that makes you?"

Elowen patted Sable's face with a hand that smelled of milk and sleep, and answered, "Up," again, with the same conviction. It was, Sable thought, not a bad philosophy for the century ahead.

There would be no celebration worthy of the name. That was the arithmetic they all lived by now—a birthday measured against risk, a festival weighed like cargo. Maren had argued for candles at least, a cake of the good brown sugar she'd been hoarding since autumn, and Sable had agreed because refusing Maren anything on this of all days would have been its own kind of cruelty. But the shutters would stay closed until dark. No visitors. The atlas, bound now in its false covers of *Commissioned Surveys of the Public Gardens of Vellamare*, Volume Three, sat in plain sight on the shelf where boredom could protect it, and tonight—while the whole city looked up—Sable intended to do nothing more dangerous than watch the sky with their daughter and let the century turn without them.

They were almost through the morning when they saw the man with the chalk.

* * *

He came up Rope Walk at half past ten, unhurried, a clerk's coat and a clerk's gait, and he stopped three doors down to examine a drainpipe with the absorbed interest of a man who has never once cared about drainage. Sable saw him from the upper window, where they had gone ostensibly to check the state of the eaves.

Maren saw him too. She appeared at Sable's elbow without having been called, dish towel still in her hands, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below, the man finished with the drainpipe. He crossed to the wall of the cooper's yard—the low stone wall that ran along the side of Corvane's workshop, half-hidden by the ivy that died back every winter and returned every spring despite everything—and he leaned there, casually, as a tired man leans. He was there perhaps the length of six breaths. Then he straightened his coat and walked on down the hill toward the water, whistling something with the shape of a hymn and none of its devotion.

Sable did not go out until noon. When they did, they walked past the cooper's yard at a stroller's pace, hands in pockets, eyes anywhere but the wall—and there it was, low on the stone, in chalk the color of old bone, rubbed faintly at the edges as if its author had wanted deniability:

**⟁ III — W**

A triangle, a numeral, a letter. Sable had spent ten years learning the language of marks—the surveyor's benchmarks, the tide-gauge notations, the smugglers' signs scratched into harbor pilings—and this one read plainly enough to anyone who knew the grammar. A place identified. A priority assigned. A watch ordered.

*W*. They turned the letter over all afternoon while measuring out flour and pretending an appetite. *Watch*, most likely. Or *wait*. It hardly mattered which. What mattered was the triangle itself, which Sable had seen before—inked in the margin of a requisition draft in Corvane's files, years ago, beside a note in the master's tight hand: *the Governor's mark for works in progress. He stamps it on everything he means to own.*

Vane's men had marked the workshop the way a surveyor marks a plot he intends to clear.

"You knew," Maren said that evening. Not accusatory. She was dressing Elowen in the good wool—blue, the color of a chart's deep water—while Sable stood in the doorway holding the candle. "When you went out at noon and came back with your face arranged like Sunday. You knew."

"I know a mark on a wall," Sable said. "I don't know what it means yet. It might mean nothing."

"It might," Maren agreed, folding a blanket with terrible precision. "And the tide might not come in tomorrow." She lifted the baby, settled her on her hip, and looked at Sable with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing in any quarter of the city. "So we'll have our evening anyway. We'll have the cake and the candle and the silly song you pretend not to know, and we'll open the door at midnight exactly long enough to hear the bells. And tomorrow—" she paused, and something moved behind her face like weather over water, "—tomorrow we decide what to do about a triangle."

She carried Elowen toward the fire. Over her shoulder she added, quietly, in the voice she used only for truths too large for emphasis: "Nobody takes a year from that child. Not a Governor, not a century. Nobody."

* * *

Three streets down and a world away, in the taproom of the Anchor & Chain, a man named Josiah Creech drank the Governor's health in the Governor's coin and tried not to think about the child.

It was not going well.

Creech was not, by his own accounting, a wicked man. He collected debts, followed men, marked walls—honest work, in the way that hauling offal was honest work, in that somebody had to and nobody thanked you. He had been told to mark the mapmaker's shop and note who came and went, and he had done it, and he had been paid for it, and that should have been the whole of the matter. But he had also, three weeks past, watched through a window as a woman had danced a red-faced infant around a kitchen to stop her crying during a soldiers' inspection, making faces like a woman possessed, risking all their necks on a lullaby—and something in Creech had come loose and never seated itself again.

"She's just a babby," he'd said to his employer's steward, and been told that it was precisely not a babby, it was a problem with legs, and problems with legs grew up to ask questions.

So now he drank, and watched the taproom fill with year-end revelers, and listened to the talk that ran through every table like current through a strait: the century dying tonight, they said, a hundred years of the world burned down to ash and a new one kindled at midnight. There'd be fireworks from the citadel. The Governor himself would show his face on the ramparts. Free wine at the quays, bells from every steeple, and the whole of Vellamare packed shoulder to shoulder looking up at the sky.

*Whole city looking up,* Creech thought, and found no comfort in it. In his experience, when everyone was looking up, that was exactly when the knife came from below.

He signaled for another cup and told himself, for the fourth time that night, that whatever happened at the mapmaker's shop would happen whether he drank here or drank at home, and that a man could not be hanged for the contents of another man's conscience.

He believed it the way sailors believe in fair winds: sincerely, and against all the evidence.

* * *

At nine o'clock they lit the single candle.

It was not a birthday candle in any honest sense—it was half of a dinner taper, salvaged, stuck upright in a squat little cake brown with sugar and studded with dried cherries Maren had traded two washings for—but when Maren set it before Elowen and the flame stood up between them, the child went utterly still, the way she went still before any new thing, studying it with the ferocious attention of her father's blood.

"One year," Maren said softly. "One year of her, in spite of everything."

"In spite of everything," Sable agreed.

They sang—or Maren sang, and Sable hummed the parts they pretended not to know, and Elowen banged both fists on the table in what all parties agreed was applause. When the moment came to blow out the flame, Sable lifted her up so she could feel her own breath puff against it, and the candle died, and in the sudden dark the child laughed—one bright syllable, startled out of her—and Sable felt the sound go through them like a nail driven into living wood, fastening something permanently in place.

*I will finish it for you,* they thought, the old vow reshaping itself around her name. *Not the atlas. Everything. All of it.*

They ate the cake. They let the fire burn low and gold. Outside, the city began its slow crescendo—drums somewhere in the Upper Town, ship bells counting the hours, the rising murmur of ten thousand people flowing downhill toward the quays like a tide of its own. Through the gaps in the shutters the night kept flashing: rockets already, early ones, thrown up from some private rooftop by people too impatient to wait for midnight, bursting green and gold above the harbor.

Sable watched the door.

They did not mean to. Their body simply arranged itself that way as the night deepened—seated where the latch was visible, the poker within reach, one ear tuned beneath the fireworks for the particular silence that meant boots stopping outside. Maren noticed, of course. Maren noticed everything. She said nothing, only moved her chair closer, so that the three of them formed a small constellation around the dying fire: the laundress, the mapmaker, the child asleep now across both their laps, breathing in the slow deep rhythm of perfect trust.

"Do you remember the night she came?" Maren asked, very low, under the drums.

"I remember everything about it. The frost was on the windows like tonight. I almost didn't open the door—I told myself whoever it was would come back in the morning."

"But you opened it."

"I opened it." Sable watched a rocket bloom red above the rooftops, heard its report arrive late, like thunder remembering itself. "Ten years of learning never to open that door after dark, and I opened it. Best decision of my life. Worst timing imaginable. Both things are true."

Maren's hand found theirs across the sleeping child, briefly. "Then hear my decision," she said. "Tomorrow, you take that triangle seriously. You tell me what needs doing, and I do it, and neither of us pretends anymore that watching is the same as waiting. Because I've done the washing for half the houses on this hill, and I know what gets said over linens, and I'm telling you: something is coming to this door, Sable Sterling. Soon. Before spring."

The clock in the corner gathered itself and struck eleven.

Outside, Vellamare lost what remained of its composure. The bells of the Lower Town broke loose first, then the citadel guns—signal, not salvo, white smoke blooming ghost-gray above the ramparts—and then the sky proper opened: chrysanthemums of green and gold, cascades of silver falling slow as sifting flour, the whole harbor lit up like a page held to a lamp, every mast and roofline and upturned face printed sharp and black against the glare. The crowd's roar rolled up the hill in a wave that rattled the shutters. A hundred years dead; a hundred years born. Somewhere on the quay, strangers were embracing. Somewhere a fiddler had climbed onto a barrel.

Sable opened the door at midnight exactly, as promised—just wide enough, just long enough. Cold poured in, and light, and sound: the bells, the guns, the sea of voices chanting the new year's number, and above it all the fireworks unfolding in the smoke-hazed dark like illuminated manuscripts written by a careless god.

Elowen woke, and instead of crying stared past Sable's shoulder at the colored fire reflected on the wet cobbles, and reached one small hand toward the doorway—toward the light on the stones, or perhaps, Sable thought later, toward something beyond it that none of them could see.

"Happy birthday," Sable whispered against her hair. "Happy new century. We're going to give you both something worth the trouble."

They closed the door. The bolt slid home with a sound, in the sudden quiet, like a full stop.

And that was when they saw it—the thing that had not been there at dusk. Slipped under the door while the whole city watched the sky, lying on the frost-silvered boards where the firelight found it: a single sheet of paper, folded once, unmarked save for a pressed wax seal.

Sable knew the seal. Everyone in Vellamare knew it. A triangle, stamped deep in scarlet wax—

—the Governor's mark, for works in progress. For everything he meant to own.

Maren had gone still by the fire, Elowen clutched to her breast. Neither of them breathed. Down the hill, the celebration roared on, oblivious, and Sable bent, and picked up the letter, and broke Governor Aldous Vane's seal.
