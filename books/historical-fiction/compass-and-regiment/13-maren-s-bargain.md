## 13. Maren's Bargain

Maren Quill had washed the linen of half the great houses in Vellamare, and she could read a household the way a sailor reads weather. She knew which wives wept into their pillowcases and which husbands drank themselves senseless on imported brandy; she knew, from the state of a collar or the stain on a sleeve, who had been promoted and who had been dismissed and who had been laid in the ground before their time. Laundry did not lie. People brought her their most private ruin every Tuesday, folded in baskets, and paid her to make it disappear.

So when a man in a dove-grey coat came to her washing line on a grey Thursday morning, saying he was the steward of Governor Aldous Vane, she did not need his introduction. She knew him already. She had laundered his shirts for six years—stiff collarless things, always ironed badly by his housekeeper—and she knew that this man's sweat smelled of fear even when his face smiled.

"You're Maren Quill," he said. Not a question. He stood at the edge of her yard with his boots carefully placed on the flagstones that were cleanest, as though dirt itself might report on him. "You do work for the cartographer's workshop. The old Corvane place, off Lantern Row."

"I wash for them," Maren said. She kept folding. Her hands knew the work so well they could lie for her: steady, unhurried, innocent. Inside her chest something else was moving fast, quick as a mouse behind wainscoting. *He knows about the workshop. He knows I go there.*

"Good work, I'm told." He produced no papers, no credentials. Men like Vane's steward never did; their faces were their letters of introduction. "Trusted work. Discreet work."

Maren set a folded sheet on the pile with exaggerated care. "If you've linen needing doing, you can leave it with Mrs. Tarn at the corner. I don't take new customers in my yard."

"No linen." He stepped closer, and now she caught it under the smell of clean wool and fear: money. Not coin exactly, but the particular scent of a man carrying a great deal of it, leather and metal and greed warmed by skin. "A small matter. A delicate matter. There's a child."

The wind chose that moment to come off the harbor and push through her drying lines, setting the white sheets swaying between them like sails, like veils, like the ghosts of everything Maren had ever lost. She let herself look at him then—full, flat, the look she gave men who tried to short her on the count.

"Children aren't my business," she said. "I wash for grown folk who can't be bothered to do it themselves."

"This one is unregistered." The steward's voice dropped as though the words themselves needed hiding. "No parish record. No christening. A child living in a mapmaker's workshop with no name on any roll in this city. The Governor's office finds such irregularities troubling. Disease. Sedition. Worse." He paused, and Maren understood the pause was rehearsed; she had heard better actors stumble through worse lies in her day. "The office only wishes to confirm the child exists. Where it sleeps. Whether it's being... cared for properly."

"And if it does exist?"

"Then arrangements would be made. For its welfare." His hand went into his coat, unhurried, and came out with a purse that made a sound Maren knew better than her own heartbeat—the heavy soft clink of gold, not the thin chatter of copper. "For those who help us make such arrangements."

*Where does the child sleep.* That was the question under the question. Not whether the child existed—they knew it existed, of course they knew, some laundry-maid or milk-girl or neighbor with a grudge had already told them—but where, at night, behind which wall, in whose arms. And everyone in Vellamare knew what the Governor's office did with irregularities it confirmed. It had done it before. The whole city remembered the fires that took the warehouses on Cooper's Slip two winters back, though nobody said aloud how conveniently they had burned.

Maren thought of Elowen asleep in the drawer-cot Sable had built into the map cabinet, tucked among the rolled charts of coastlines the child would someday inherit. Thought of the way the baby gripped Maren's thumb with her whole fist, ferocious as an anemone, as though letting go were a thing she refused to learn.

She thought of another child, twenty years gone, in a grave behind the chapel of Saint Ferrol, that she had not been permitted to hold even once after the fever took him, because the parish register listed him under a name that was never hers.

"Gold," the steward said gently, reading her silence however he wished to read it. "Ten sovereigns now. Ten more when the office has what it needs."

The sheets billowed between them. Maren reached out and took down a shirt pin, folded it, pinned it to the line again—a woman with nothing on her mind but washing.

"Which child?" she asked.

* * *

He gave her the description as though reciting from a paper: age near a year, dark hair, a birthmark—no, no birthmark, that detail he stumbled over, and Maren filed the stumble away. He did not know the child well enough to describe her. Whoever had sent him had sent him blind, trusting to coin rather than knowledge, and that was worth knowing too.

"The workshop off Lantern Row," he pressed. "There's a room above the drafting floor. Or perhaps below. You'd know, coming and going with your baskets. Which room?"

"Ten sovereigns buys a lot of questions," Maren said. "It doesn't buy them answered wrong."

His smile thinned. "Madam, I'm offering you a kindness. Others will ask less politely. When they do, your name will surface—you visit that workshop twice a week, everyone on Lantern Row knows it—and I would rather be able to say you helped."

*There it is.* The threat dressed as shelter, the oldest coat in Vellamare's wardrobe. Maren held out her hand.

She watched his face as the coins dropped into her palm—one, two, ten, heavy and warm from the purse—and she let her eyes do what poor women's eyes were expected to do when gold appeared: shine. Let him see hunger. Let him see a laundress counting a year's wages against a sleeping child who was, after all, nothing to her. He wanted to see that. Men like him needed to see that, or else the world was full of enemies instead of instruments.

"The child sleeps above," she said. "Back room, over the drafting floor. There's a stair from the alley side—the old coal stair, boarded at the bottom but loose where the board's nailed twice. Second board up." She said it smoothly, precisely, a woman selling a door. Every word of it true, every word of it useless: Sable had moved the cot to the front room a month ago, after the break-in, after the searched drawers and the untouched valuables, because Sable read a room's silence the way other people read letters. But the steward didn't know that, and the coal stair was real, and men who came at night loved a real door even when it opened onto nothing.

"Over the drafting floor," he repeated. "And the atlas? The old master's papers? Where are they kept?"

Maren laughed then, short and hard as a cough. "Governor's office pays ten sovereigns and asks a laundress to read maps. I couldn't tell you an atlas from a tablecloth, sir, except the atlas costs more to bleach." She jingled the coins deliberately, letting them speak her character for her. "You want the child's room, there it is. You want more than that, hire someone who owns a looking glass."

He studied her a long moment. She met it, breathing the rhythm of the wash—soak, scrub, wring, hang—breathing the way she had breathed through her son's fever, through the burial, through every Tuesday basket of other people's shame. Fear could live in a body without showing, if the body had practice.

"Second board up on the coal stair," the steward said at last, pocketing his notebook. "You'll have the rest when it's done."

"When what's done?"

"When the office has satisfied itself about the child's welfare." He tipped his hat, turned, and walked out of her yard with his careful boots, and Maren stood among her sheets until he was gone and longer, the gold burning a hole through her apron pocket like a coal.

* * *

She did not go to the workshop that afternoon. Twice a week, Tuesdays and Fridays—that was her pattern, and patterns were what watchers fed on. If the steward's people were watching, let them see nothing changed. So she washed. She wrung. She hung the Governor's own steward's shirts on her line and snapped them flat against the wind and hated each one individually.

But her mind was laying cloth beside cloth, matching seams.

They knew about the child. They knew enough to send a man with coin. They did not know the child's face—the steward's fumbled description proved that—but they knew the shape of the secret, and men like Aldous Vane did not pay ten sovereigns to *confirm* anything. They paid to locate it. And once located—

Once located, the welfare arrangements followed. A fire, most likely. Fires were Vane's preferred instrument; they destroyed records along with children, questions along with questioners. The workshop would burn with its atlases and its ledgers and its drawer-cot built into the map cabinet, and the city would shake its head over careless candle flames, and the Governor's land grants would rest easy in their forgery.

Not days, Maren realized, wringing a sheet until her knuckles went white. The raid would come soon—within days, perhaps tomorrow, perhaps tonight. A steward who had scraped together courage to approach a washerwoman was a steward on a deadline. Someone above him was pressing. Someone above him was afraid.

She finished the last load. She put away her soap and her boards. She took the ten sovereigns from her apron and did not bury them, did not spend them, did not throw them in the harbor as her first furious heart demanded. Instead she wrapped them in a scrap of linen and set them aside, because Sable would need money soon, and flight, and luck, and Maren Quill had bought all three with the only currency she had ever been allowed: her own apparent venality.

Courage, she thought, was mostly bookkeeping. It was deciding what you could afford to lose and then losing it without screaming.

At dusk, when the lamps of Lantern Row came up one by one and the harbor fog began its evening climb through the streets, she filled her laundry basket with clean linens—too many linens, linens enough to hide a bundle if a bundle needed hiding on a bad night—and carried it toward the workshop, taking the long way, doubling back twice, watching the doorways and the corners where watchers liked to stand.

Sable opened the door before she knocked. Sable always knew her step; ten months of a child in the house had sharpened whatever instinct already lived there.

"You're early," Sable said. Then, reading her face the way Sable read everything: "What happened."

"A man came to my yard this morning." Maren set down the basket inside the doorway and looked past Sable's shoulder, to where Elowen sat on the floor pulling at the fringe of an old chart, dark-haired, alive, utterly ignorant of the price her sleep was fetching in gold. "Vane's steward. He bought the child's room from me." She pressed the wrapped coins into Sable's hand and closed the other woman's fingers over them. "I sold him the coal stair and the boarded door, and I sold it well. But Sable—" and here her voice finally cracked, just once, like ice underfoot— "he counted that money like a man settling accounts before a journey. Whatever they mean to do, they mean to do it soon. Days. Maybe less."

Above them, on the landing, Elowen banged a wooden spool against the floorboards, twice, three times—a small, imperious knocking at the door of the world.

"We leave tonight," Sable said.

"No," Maren answered, and picked up her basket, and carried it in.
