## 15. Interrogation by Candlelight

They did not take Sable to the garrison. That was the first thing worth noting, and Sable noted it the way one notes a coastline that has shifted since the last survey—a small wrongness that means a larger one beneath.

The soldiers had come at dusk, polite as customs officers, and walked them not toward the citadel on the hill but down through the Old Town to the Customs House, and from there not into it but around it, along the seaward colonnade where the wind came off the water carrying salt and the last of the fish-market stink. At the far end stood a door Sable had never seen open, though Sable had drawn this building three times, once for the harbor chart and twice for practice, and knew its dimensions to the width of a hair. A door that did not appear on any plan. Corvane would have appreciated that. Corvane would also have said: *when a building keeps a secret, ask what the secret weighs.*

Inside there was no dungeon, no iron, nothing so theatrical. There was a room with a hearth already laid, a table of dark island walnut, two chairs, a decanter of amber wine, and Governor Aldous Vane standing at the window with his back turned, watching the harbor lights come up one by one like a man counting his own money.

"Master Sterling," he said, without turning. "Or is it Mistress? I find your paperwork delightfully ambiguous."

"Sable will do, Your Excellency."

"It always has, apparently." He turned then, and up close he was smaller than his portraits, broader in the hand, with the weathered complexion of a man who had once actually worked out of doors—years ago, before the governorship lacquered him over. His eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and they moved over Sable's face the way Sable's eyes moved over an unfamiliar chart: reading features, judging depths, marking hazards.

"Sit," he said. "Wine?"

"No, thank you."

"Good. Neither shall I. It puts us both on our honor, and honor at a table makes for poor conversation." He sat himself, gestured to the opposite chair, and waited until Sable took it. The candle between them was beeswax, expensive, burning with a clean steady flame that threw neither of their shadows anywhere useful.

For a long moment the Governor simply looked at Sable, and Sable looked back, and the silence between them was not empty. It was crowded. It held a drowned village and a burned atlas and a child asleep three streets away under Maren's rough warm arm, and neither of them mentioned any of it, because mentioning it would be the first false move, and both of them were cartographers of a kind. Both of them knew that the most important things on a map are the ones you do not label.

"You have been in Corvane's workshop eleven years," Vane said at last. "Nine as apprentice, two as—what? Custodian? Executor? Ghost?" He smiled faintly. "The guild rolls still list him as living. You pay his guild dues every quarter. Out of whose purse?"

"His," Sable said. "He left instructions. And funds."

"He left *instructions*." Vane turned the word over like a coin of doubtful mint. "Yes. He was fond of instructions. Fond of leaving things precisely where they could be found by the right person and not by the wrong one. I employed the man for nineteen years, Master Sterling. I know how he thought. Which is why I am finding this conversation so unsatisfying." He laced his fingers on the table. "You see, my men burned forty-one atlases in your workshop. Forty-one. Corvane never made more than six copies of anything in his life—he was vain about his originals and stingy with his labor. Forty-one atlases means either he changed his habits late in life, or someone in that workshop has been very busy making things that look valuable enough to burn."

The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Sable kept their hands flat on the table, loose, the way one keeps one's hands when a cat is deciding whether to strike.

"Fire spreads carelessly," Sable said. "We lost stock. The fire took whatever was near the front room. I couldn't tell you what was destroyed. I'm still cataloguing."

"I imagine you are," said Vane. "I imagine you have been cataloguing for two years." He rose, crossed to the sideboard, and came back not with wine but with a flat leather folder, which he set on the table between them without opening. It was old. The leather had gone dark and hard at the corners, and Sable recognized the stitching before anything else—the double-stitched spine with waxed linen thread, the particular way Corvane tied off his ends. The heart did something painful and complicated, a whole knot of feeling pulled tight in one motion.

"Do you know what this is?" Vane asked.

"It appears to be a folio."

"It appears to be. It is, in fact, empty. Or rather—" he opened it, turned it so Sable could see, "—it is a folio built to hold something of exactly these dimensions." From his sleeve he drew a card, heavy rag paper, and laid it in the depression where the missing leaf should sit. On the card, in Corvane's unmistakable hand, was written: *For A.V., being the truth of the matter, which he may read when he is prepared to be governed by it.*

Sable looked at it for a long time. The handwriting was real. The paper was aged correctly. And none of it mattered, because Sable had spent two years learning Corvane's hand well enough to forge it, which meant learning it well enough to detect forgery, and the descender on the *p* was wrong. Too confident. Corvane had been many things, but never confident in that particular letter; he had suffered it.

"The leaf is gone," Sable said quietly. "Whatever it held."

"Yes. So it would seem." Vane closed the folio. "And yet here is my difficulty, Master Sterling—or may I say Sable? Thank you. Here is my difficulty. A folio exists. A note in Corvane's hand exists. Therefore a leaf existed, and was made for me, and has been withheld or removed or hidden. Someone knows where. My surveyor believes you know where. My surveyor is, I have begun to suspect, a man who tells each party what that party wishes to hear, which makes him nearly useless and occasionally dangerous." A flicker crossed the colorless eyes—not anger; something more like fatigue. "So I have come to ask you myself. Not through Hale. Not through stewards and laundresses and informers in the fish-market, though I will note that you have been better served by such people than I have. Directly. As one professional to another."

"And if I don't know where the leaf is?"

"Then we have had a pleasant evening and wasted it." Vane leaned back. "But let me tell you what I think first, because I have earned the courtesy of being heard, and because I want you to understand that I am not a fool, whatever else may be said of me. I think Corvane surveyed the drowned quarter before the sluices were opened. I think he documented the holdings of thirty-seven families whose claims predated every grant I hold. I think he made a record of it, because he was incapable of *not* making records—it was a disease with him—and I think that record, properly read, would be inconvenient to me in ways that magistrates could act upon. And I think that when he died—"

"When he died," Sable said, and their voice came out lower than intended, "he fell on the north stair with a lantern in his hand after refusing to surrender the coastal charts. Everyone says so."

"Everyone says so," Vane agreed mildly, "because everyone who might say otherwise is dead, retired, or illiterate." He watched Sable across the candle. "You may draw whatever conclusion pleases you from that sentence, Sable. I notice you have already drawn several. Your face does an interesting thing when you decide not to speak—it happens just here—" he touched his own jaw, "—a small tightening, like a hand closing a clasp. You've done it four times tonight. A mapmaker who cannot lie with his face should at least know his own tells."

*Five times now,* Sable thought, and did not tighten anything.

* * *

The fire needed another log. Vane added it himself, kneeling at the hearth with the unhurried competence of a man who had camped on surveys in his youth, and while his back was turned Sable studied the room the way one studies a room for exits, because that is what Corvane had taught, and because some lessons live deeper than grief.

There was one door. Two high windows, barred. A chimney too narrow for anything but smoke. But the room itself told a story: the walnut table was not Customs House furniture; it had legs like a chapel table, like a refectory. The floorboards were new around the hearth and old everywhere else, which meant this room had been assembled recently inside an older shell. Vane had built himself a private chamber where no plan showed one. He conducted business that did not exist on any official record, in a room that did not officially exist, and he had brought Sable here instead of the garrison—which meant whatever happened tonight must never appear in any report. Either Sable would leave quietly, or Sable would not leave at all.

"There is a version of tonight," Vane said, still crouched at the fire, "that ends well. I'd like you to hear it before you decide anything with your jaw."

He returned to his chair and folded his hands again, and when he spoke it was in a different register entirely—not the interrogator, not the magistrate, but something older and more dangerous, because it sounded sincere.

"I was born in Salt Lane," he said. "Do you know it? Of course you do—you've been buying ink there. My father tanned hides two doors from where the printers are now, and I grew up smelling of lime and shit, and the first time I saw a surveyor's chain stretched down our street measuring it—for tax purposes, they said—I understood something that has governed my entire life: whoever holds the map holds the street. Not the sword. Not the law. The *map*. The law arrives afterward, obediently, following lines someone else drew." He spread his hands. "I am sixty-one years old, Sable. I have no legitimate children. My wife is buried in the cathedral crypt beside a son who lived nine days. What I have is a province, a fortune, a name that will outlive me by perhaps a generation, and a great many documents whose survival depends on the goodwill of whoever comes after me. And what do I find, when I look into this city that I have, in my fashion, loved? I find Corvane's apprentice. An orphan of no name, keeping his workshop alive like a votive lamp, paying dues on a dead man's guild seat, and knowing—knowing in that precise, patient, unforgiving way of yours—exactly what I buried in the flooded quarter twenty years ago."

The candle had burned down a full inch. Sable realized their own hands had not moved at all, and made themselves move—one small adjustment, human, unguarded—because a perfectly still opponent frightened men like Vane more than any outburst.

"What are you offering?" Sable asked.

"A post," Vane said simply. "Provincial Surveyor. The office Hale occupies and disgraces. A salary, a warrant, the right to sign charts with the crown's authority. A name, if you want one—there are procedures, patronage can accomplish remarkable things, and *Sterling* is a trade-name, not a lineage; even you must feel the difference. And a place." He let the word sit. "Belonging. You have spent your whole life in a workshop that was never yours, guarding a legacy that was never left to you, sleeping with one ear open in a house where you are tolerated at best and suspected at worst. I am offering you what Corvane gave you in scraps: work worthy of your hands, and a city that acknowledges them. Legitimacy, Master Sterling. It is the only inheritance anyone can actually give, and I am offering mine."

It was expertly done. That was the horror of it. Every word landed on something true—Sable felt each one strike home like a sounding-weight tapping bottom, *eleven fathoms, twelve, thirteen*, mapping the depth of a hunger Sable had spent years pretending was shallow. A name. A post. To walk into the guild hall and be announced rather than admitted through the side door. To sleep without listening for boots on the stair. To belong somewhere the way other people belonged, casually, without earning it hourly.

Vane watched all of this cross Sable's face, and Sable watched him watch, and understood suddenly that this was the actual interrogation—that the questions about the leaf had been the pretext, and this offer was the blade, and it was sharper than any question because it was honest. He meant it. He wanted an heir of skill if not of blood, a keeper of his secrets who had a stake in their silence, and he had found the one person in Vellamare whose loneliness he could price to the penny.

"That's a generous gift," Sable said slowly.

"It is."

"Gifts bind the receiver to the giver." Corvane had said that too—about patronage, about the fine vellum the Guild sent once, which he locked away and never used. *Nothing given freely stays free. Ink remembers, but gratitude forgets.* "If I accept, the drowned quarter becomes yours forever. Whatever the leaf says, whatever Corvane proved, becomes a story no one can act on, because the one person who could read it aloud is sitting at your right hand, signing your charts."

"Yes," Vane said. Without flinching, without softening. "That is the arrangement. That is every arrangement, ever made, anywhere. Belonging costs. You have simply never before been able to afford the currency."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, out on the water, a ship's bell rang the second watch, and both of them glanced involuntarily toward the sound—an old reflex, shared, and Sable hated the sharing of it.

"If I refuse?" Sable asked.

Vane considered the question honestly, which was worse than evasion.

"Then tomorrow there will be a routine inspection of the workshop's structural safety, and it will be found wanting, and you will be given a week to vacate, and the lease will pass to the customs service, and every stick of paper in that building will be inventoried by people whose inventories I read. No violence. Nothing for any magistrate to examine. Simply an ending, orderly and legal, of a workshop that has outlived its master." He paused. "I am told there is a child sometimes seen at your neighbor's. Laundress. Widow. I mention it only to observe that children complicate relocation. Growing bodies need stability. Milk. Warmth in winter. All of which is easier to guarantee inside an arrangement than outside one."

And there it was. Not a threat—a gift offered twice, with the second wrapping thinner than the first. Sable sat very still and let the silence stretch, because the silence was the only weapon in the room that belonged to them, and because somewhere behind the stillness a decision was being made that had actually been made weeks ago, in a printer's shop on Salt Lane, in a crypt-shadowed agreement with Maren, in the moment a certain leaf had been copied line for line and the copy carried out of the city in the false bottom of a laundry basket. Vane was negotiating for a thing that had already slipped his reach, and Sable knew it, and he didn't know that Sable knew it, and that asymmetry—the whole secret architecture of the coming months—rested entirely on what happened to Sable's face in the next ten seconds.

Sable tightened the jaw deliberately. Let him see the conflict. Let him believe the wound was working.

"Your Excellency is generous," Sable said. "More generous than I expected, and more honest. Give me until the new year to answer. A man shouldn't accept an inheritance in the same season he burned out of his house."

Something moved in the pale eyes—satisfaction, carefully rationed. "The new year," Vane agreed. "Twelve days. Consider it a survey period. Map your own depths, Master Sterling. I'm told you're skilled at that sort of thing."

He did not escort Sable out. A soldier in plain clothes did, through the colonnade, past the door that appeared on no plan, and released them at the corner of the fish-market with the courtesy owed to a guest rather than a prisoner, which was its own kind of message: *you were never here, and neither was he.*

Sable walked home the long way, along the sea wall, letting the wind strip the Customs House warmth off their skin. Behind them the city glittered and rang with early festivity—twelve days to the century's turning, lanterns going up along the mole, fireworks already being tested in barrels on the breakwater. Ahead lay the workshop, dark, searched, gutted of everything burnable except the truth, where Maren waited awake with the child.

Twelve days. Vane thought he had set a clock running.

He had. Just not the one he imagined.

Sable touched the door, felt Maren's shadow shift behind the peephole, and spoke the night's first honest words to anyone:

"Light the candle. We start copying the rest tonight."
