## 16. What Sable Sterling Wants

They let Sable out through the Governor's House garden gate rather than the front doors, and Sable understood the courtesy for what it was: a message. *You were never here. No one saw you. You owe us your invisibility, and we can make you visible whenever we choose.*

The gate closed behind them with a soft click that sounded, in the frost-still morning, like the first stroke of a pen on fresh vellum.

Sable walked home the long way, along the harbor road, because the long way took past the fish-market clock and the clock told no one anything. Two men in plain coats fell into step a street behind—Vane's watchers, clumsy enough to be meant to be seen. Let them count the windows of the workshop. Let them watch Maren carry Elowen to the wash-house on her hip like any laundress with any child. The atlas was gone from every place a man might look for it; it slept between boards bound in gilt, titled *The Pleasure Gardens of Vellamare*, commissioned by the Governor himself, and there was a joke in that which Sable intended to live long enough to enjoy.

But the walk was long, and the walk gave time, and time was the one thing Sable had asked the candle not to give.

The interrogation had ended—not in victory, nothing so clean—with Vane setting down his own glass of wine untouched and saying, almost gently: *Think on it. A man without a name is a man without a grave.* And then he had offered, in the same voice he might have used to describe the weather, the thing.

A post. Cartographer to the Governor's survey, a salaried position, rooms within the Governor's House precincts. A stipend retroactive to Corvane's death—back pay for ten years of orphaned devotion, as if grief could be invoiced. And a name. Not Sterling, that thin alias Corvane had hung around an unknown child's neck like a coat against the cold. A real name. A recorded name. Legitimization by the Governor's own seal, entered in the rolls at the cathedral, witnessed by magistrates.

*Every map needs a cartographer's name in the corner,* Vane had said. *Yours has none. I am offering to put one there.*

And Sable had sat in the candlelight with their hands flat on the table—hands that had learned stillness before they learned letters—and had felt the offer go into them like warmth into frozen fingers. That was the horror of it. Not temptation as the priests described it, all smoke and sweetness. Temptation as heat after cold. It had felt like coming home.

* *

The workshop smelled of tallow and wet wool when Sable let themselves in. Maren was at the workbench feeding Elowen bread softened in milk, and she looked up with the whole of her face, the way she did—the quick inventory: alive, unhurt, followed? Sable shook their head slightly: *not now*, and she nodded and went on mashing bread as though the world were only ever bread and milk.

"You've the look," she said after a while, when Elowen had been wiped and set on the floor with a wooden horse to gum. "Of someone who's been given something."

"I have."

"Bad or good?"

"That's what I can't tell yet."

Maren wiped her hands on her apron and waited. She had been a laundress in this quarter for twenty years and she knew that some things needed wringing before they could be folded, and she never rushed the wringing.

"He offered me a name," Sable said. "Legitimate. Entered in the rolls. A post in his survey. He said—" and here Sable's voice did something unfamiliar, thinned and went younger, "—he said every map needs a cartographer's name in the corner. Mine has none."

Maren was quiet. Outside, the fishwives were calling the morning's catch, and the sound came through the warped glass rounded and soft, like everything in Vellamare that didn't know it was living inside a forged document.

"And what did you say?"

"I said I'd think on it. Which is the truth." Sable sat down at the bench across from her, in the chair that had been Corvane's, that still bore the groove of his shoulder blade worn into the wood over forty years. "That's what frightens me, Maren. It isn't a lie I want to refuse."

She looked at the baby, at Elowen solemnly offering the wooden horse to no one, and then back. "You wanted a name since I've known you. Don't look surprised—I do washing for half this street. People talk over tubs. I've heard how you sign things. *Apprentice to the late Master Corvane.* Never a Christian name of your own before Sterling, and Sterling only these ten years. A woman whose mother nobody names. I've heard the clerks at the customs house say *the apprentice* the way you'd say *the broom*."

"Yes."

"So say the rest of it. Why won't you take what he's giving?"

Sable turned their hands over on the bench, palms up, ink in every whorl, the way it had always been. Ink under the skin in patterns no soap would lift, so that even stripped and searched and held to the light, Sable's body was a document.

"Because I know where the name comes from," they said. "I've drawn the deeds myself. Or their ancestors. The grants he wants recognized—the ones my atlas proves are forged—they sit on land that belonged to other people once. To the quarter under the harbor." The drowned village rose in Sable's mind the way it always did now, unbidden: the bell tower visible at low tide, the field boundaries Corvane had surveyed and then refused to surrender, the marginal note in his hand—*they were paid in nothing and called it gratitude*. "He's offering me a place in a house built on a floor he sank. If I take the name, I'm not just safe. I'm *his*. Every chart I draw after that will be true in its lines and false in its heart, and I'll have chosen that. With my eyes open. For warmth."

Maren nodded slowly, folding the edge of her apron over and over, a hem within a hem.

"When my Thomas died," she said—it was the first time she had said the child's name aloud in Sable's hearing—"the priest told me God had taken him to a better place. And I thought, no. *Someone* took him somewhere. It wasn't God and it wasn't better. But everyone wanted me to accept the prettier story because the true one couldn't be borne." She looked up. "Your master taught you a map is a confession. Well then. What does a name confess, if you buy it from the man who drowned the proof?"

* *

For three days Sable did not decide, and the not-deciding was itself a kind of torture Vane had designed without knowing it, because Sable had spent ten years finishing an atlas alone in the dark and had thought themselves acquainted with wanting. They had not been. They had known longing the way a prisoner knows the sky—through a grate, abstractly. Now Vane had opened the grate and the whole sky came pouring in: a name in the rolls. Witnesses at a christening. Legal standing to hold property, to testify, to *sign*. Sable lay awake at night cataloguing what legitimacy would mean and found the catalogue endless. To walk into the customs house and be addressed as *Master Sterling, cartographer to the Governor*. To buy vellum on credit. To stand before a magistrate and have words weigh what words weigh when spoken by a person who exists.

On the third night Sable got up from bed and lit the lamp at the workbench and took out the atlas—or took out the harmless gilt-bound twin of it, the parks and promenades, and beneath the pleasant engravings the hidden folios sewn into the spine—and turned, not to the forged grants, but to the oldest sheet in the collection. Corvane's first survey, done the year he took in a debt-orphan with ink-stained fingers: the coastline north of Vellamare, drawn in his sure hand, and in the lower margin, small as a signature, the exercise lines of a child learning letters. *S-a-b-l-e.* Over and over, the letters marching crookedly across the margin like ants finding their way. His student. His heir in everything but law.

Corvane had never legitimized them either. Sable had never wondered why until now, holding the page, feeling the old question turn over like a stone showing its underside. Corvane had money, standing, witnesses. He could have gone to the rolls. He had not. Had he feared the attention? Had he suspected even then what his charts would cost him?

Or—Sable's thumb stilled on the crooked letters—had he understood something Sable was only now learning? That a name given by the powerful is not a gift but a leash dressed as a collar of honor. That belonging, purchased from the house that owns the ledger, means the house can always revoke the purchase. Vane had offered Sable a name the way he offered surveyors commissions: as a debt incurred. Everything Vane gave, he later collected with interest. Hale was proof. Corvane's grave was proof. The village under the harbor was proof beyond all proof.

And beneath the fear, quieter, the real thing, the thing Sable had circled for three days like a surveyor circling a summit:

If Sable took Vane's name, then everything Sable was would date from that moment. Before it: nothing, a blank margin, an unnamed apprentice useful for inking other people's claims. After it: Vane's creature. But if Sable refused—if Sable stood in the cathedral crypt or the magistrate's court or the printer's back room on Salt Lane and said *my name is Sable Sterling and I made this atlas*—then the name began *there*. Not inherited. Chosen. A name with a birthdate and a birthday, born the instant it was spoken as a claim instead of accepted as a gift.

Corvane had left them the atlas instead of the name. Perhaps because the atlas *was* the name—one that couldn't be struck from the rolls because it was written in evidence.

* *

The watcher outside the workshop changed shift at dusk, and Sable, watching from the upstairs window, watched the new man settle against the cooper's wall across the lane and marked how he stood: weight on the left foot, arms crossed not for warmth but to hide his hands, eyes on the door and never the windows. A man following orders he hadn't written. Everyone in this story was following orders they hadn't written. Hale. The soldiers. Even Vane, perhaps, some old hunger wearing him the way he wore his office.

Elowen was asleep in the drawer-cot Maren had lined with flannel. Maren sat below the window mending, and Sable came down and sat opposite, and for a while neither spoke, and the silence was the good kind, the kind that holds weight without breaking.

"If I refuse him," Sable said finally, "we lose the workshop. Maybe more. There'll be no legitimate name to appeal to when they come. We'll be exactly what they call us—nobodies, squatters, a liar and a laundress and a foundling."

"Mm," said Maren, pulling thread.

"And if I take it, we keep the walls and the roof and the legal person of Sable Sterling, Esquire, and I spend the rest of my life drawing his harbors and deepening his channels and drowning whoever else he needs drowned, politely, in ink."

"That's about the size of it." She bit the thread. "Which do you want?"

And Sable found that underneath three days of torment, the answer had been sitting the whole time, patient as a buried boundary stone, waiting to be surveyed.

"I want the name," Sable said slowly, hearing each word as it was set down like a line on a chart, checked twice, inked once. "I've wanted it since I was seven years old watching other children get called home to supper. I'm not going to pretend the want isn't real—that's his trick, making us ashamed of wanting, so we're grateful to whoever feeds it. But I want it *mine*. Earned or claimed or carved, but not conferred. A name he gave me would be his forever. A name I speak for myself—" Sable looked toward the drawer-cot, where Elowen slept with one fist curled against her cheek, heir to nothing but the truth, "—a name I claim is the one thing in Vellamare he cannot forge."

Maren smiled then, slow and wide, the smile of a woman who had bet coin on the right horse. "So it's no."

"It's no. And more than no." Sable stood, and the tiredness fell away like a coat removed, and what remained underneath was not comfort but direction—the old surveyor's certainty of bearing. "He thinks the atlas burned. He thinks he bought my silence with an offer I'm too hungry to refuse. While he thinks that, we have days. Maybe a week. And I know a printer on Salt Lane who owes Corvane a debt, and I know plates can be engraved in a cellar faster than a Governor can change his mind."

Maren was already gathering her shawl. "Salt Lane's a night's work. You'll want light and you'll want the original, and you'll want someone walking ahead of you on the street to see who follows."

"Maren."

"Don't thank me. I've lost one child to men who kept tidy ledgers. I'm owed."

They went out together into the dark, past the watcher who noted the workshop door closing behind lamplight and reported, an hour later, that the premises appeared quiet—while two figures moved south through the lanes, carrying under a laundress's bundle the most dangerous documents on the coast, toward the printing houses where the century's new machinery was waiting, and where the name *Sable Sterling* was about to appear in public for the very first time.

Not in the rolls.

In print.
