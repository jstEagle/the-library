## 17. The Printers of Salt Lane

The printer's shop did not announce itself. That was the first thing Sable liked about it.

Salt Lane ran behind the tanneries like a vein behind skin, narrow and slick, smelling of lime and lye and the low-tide rot that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift from Vellamare's older streets. The houses leaned toward one another overhead until the sky was a ribbon of dirty grey, and the signboards — when there were signboards at all — advertised things no one wanted traced back to them: a bindery, a rag merchant, a dealer in secondhand boots. At the lane's far end, wedged between the boot-dealer and a doorway that breathed out the sour heat of a dye-house, stood a door with no number and no name, only a small brass plate polished blank by years of rain and deliberate anonymity.

Sable had walked past it three times before knocking.

Maren stood at their shoulder with Elowen wrapped against her chest beneath a fishmonger's oilcloth cape, the child's dark eyes peering out from the folds like something wild and patient. Maren had insisted on coming. *You cannot do this alone*, she had said, and she had not meant the engraving. She had meant the standing in strange doorways; the handing over of your only treasure to a stranger's hands; the moment afterward, walking home empty, when a person needed someone to hold their elbow and tell them they had not just made a fatal mistake.

"You're sure it's this one?" Maren murmured now, as Sable raised a knuckle to the brass plate. "The laundresses say the man who works here prints things that get men hanged."

"The laundresses," said Sable, "see everything."

"That they do," said Maren, and there was something in her voice — pride, almost, fierce and unexpected — that made Sable glance sideways. Maren Quill had spent thirty years being invisible in plain sight, wringing other people's secrets out of their collars and bedsheets and saying nothing, because saying nothing was how a poor woman survived. Now those silences were being spent, one coin at a time, and she seemed to have decided that if she was going to be hanged for complicity, she would at least choose the cause herself.

Sable knocked: twice, then once, in the pattern the rag merchant's boy had described for the price of a hot pie.

A long silence. Then a slot in the door scraped open, and one eye appeared — brown, bloodshot, unimpressed.

"We don't do wedding invitations," said the eye.

"I'm not here for invitations." Sable drew the package from under their coat: a thin folio, wrapped in oilcloth, containing three drawings on translucent paper. "I'm here for plates. Copper. Engraved deep enough to survive five hundred impressions and a magistrate's inspection."

The eye moved from Sable's face to the package and back. Behind it, somewhere in the dark interior, a press could be heard — no, not a press, Sable realized. A lathe, or a grindstone, the sound of metal being worked.

"Five hundred impressions of what?"

"Of the truth about Lowry's Reach."

The slot began to close. Sable put their fingers against the door.

"They told me you were careful," Sable said quickly. "They told me you were the only man in Vellamare who'd touch the truth. If I've come to the wrong door—"

"The wrong door is the only safe door on this street," the eye said. But it stayed open. "Lowry's Reach is a fairy story. A drowned fishing village. There's nothing there but eels and the Governor's new sea-wall."

"There are eleven families under that sea-wall," Sable said. "There were land grants that were never surrendered, and a survey made in '94 that proves the water rose before the papers were signed. Corvane made that survey. I finished his atlas. And I am asking you — *asking* you, Josiah Creed, whose press turned out the dockworkers' pamphlet in '97 without his name ever once being traced — to engrave what Corvane measured."

Silence again. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, and all three of them — Sable, Maren, the eye in the door — went still as prey until it faded.

Then the bolts were drawn back, one two three, and the door opened onto a long low room lit by a single lamp hung over a workbench scarred with the marks of forty years' graving. Tools hung in ranks above it, burnished bright. Along the walls, paper was stacked in bales like cordwood, and the smell was ink and vinegar and metal filings, and to Sable it smelled more like home than any place since the workshop.

Josiah Creed was sixty if he was a day, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had spent a lifetime leaning into heavy machinery, with forearms like mooring rope and ink worked so deep into the creases of his hands that they would never come clean. He looked at Sable for a long time. He looked at Maren. He looked, briefly and more gently than anything else he had done, at the small face watching him from the oilcloth folds.

"You brought a baby," he said, "to a conspiracy."

"She doesn't sleep through them yet," said Maren. "Thought she should start learning."

Something cracked in Creed's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile had been quarried out and filled back in. He stepped aside.

"Shut the door. Both bolts. Then show me what Corvane measured."

* * *

He spread the three transparencies across his bench and weighted them with gravers, and read them the way Sable had seen Master Corvane read charts: silently, thoroughly, his head tilted, one finger tracing coastlines without quite touching the paper. The lamplight fell yellow across the drowned quarter — the crooked streets of Lowry's Reach rendered from Corvane's field notes, the little harbor, the church with its bell still legended in the margin *ringed for last time 11 March 1794* — and beside it, overlaid in red, the footprint of the Governor's sea-wall, drawn precisely where the village had stood.

Creed read for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed. It was quieter, and older.

"My mother's people were Reach folk," he said. "I was told they moved inland for the herring. I was seven." His finger stopped at the church. "Who else has seen these?"

"No one who matters yet. That's why I've come to you." Sable took a breath, and laid out the bargain the way they had rehearsed it in the dark of the workshop with Elowen asleep against their chest. "I need key plates cut. Not the whole atlas — we couldn't cut the whole atlas in time, and most of it would be wasted breath anyway. Three leaves. The drowned quarter. The forged grants, with the dates that prove the forgery. And the coastal chart showing where the garrison's guns can't reach, because the dockworkers will want to know it, and they'll be the ones who keep the presses running when the soldiers come." Sable heard their own voice steady itself. "In exchange: the plates are yours. Your name goes nowhere near them. And when it's done, you print first, and free, for Salt Lane and the docks — every impression the magistrates' copies leave you free to make. The truth about Lowry's Reach belongs to the street it drowned."

Creed was quiet again. He picked up a transparency and held it to the lamp, and the light came through the paper and lit his face from behind, so that his expression was hard to read — though Sable, who had spent ten years reading the hesitations of hands, saw his thumb press flat against the margin where the drowned village's names were written. Eleven families. He was counting them.

"You know what happens to me if this goes wrong," he said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I know exactly what happens to everyone in this room if it goes wrong."

"And you came anyway. With a baby." He set the transparency down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something fragile so that his hands won't do anything else with it. "Corvane taught you that?"

"Corvane taught me that a map is a confession. The rest I learned from the man who killed him."

That landed. Creed looked at Sable sharply, and whatever he saw — the fear, the ten years of it, worn now down to something smooth and load-bearing — seemed to settle the account for him. He rolled his sleeves higher.

"Copper takes me four days a plate if I don't eat or sleep. I'll take six days each and stay alive. Bring me the rest of the drawings tomorrow, and bring them folded inside something dull — almanac pages, tax schedules, anything a soldier wouldn't steal." He was already clearing space on the bench, already reaching for his best graver, the motion of a man committing himself with his body before his mouth caught up. Then he stopped. "One condition. Mine."

"Name it."

"When it's printed — the drowned quarter leaf — my mother's family name goes in the margin. Harrow. There were Harrows in that churchyard. Corvane would have known them." His voice didn't waver, but his jaw did, briefly. "Eleven families, you said. I'll engrave every name you have. That's my price. Not money. Names."

Sable thought of Corvane's marginalia, the tiny sure hand recording what the official charts erased. *Ringed for last time.* They had carried those names for months like stones in the chest, waiting for somewhere to set them down.

"Every name," Sable said. "In your hand or mine. They go on the plate."

* * *

They came out of Salt Lane into a wind off the harbor that smelled of rain coming, and Maren did not speak until they had turned twice, unhurried, in the manner of people who have learned that hurrying draws the eye.

"Well," she said finally. "Either we've found our printer or we've handed the atlas to the Governor's cleverest trap, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"It wasn't a trap. Traps don't ask for their dead relations' names."

"Mm." Maren shifted Elowen higher against her shoulder. "No. They don't." She was silent a few steps. "My grandmother used to buy her soap from a Harrow woman. Before the Reach. Said she cheated her honestly, which was more than you got from most merchants." A pause. "Small world, isn't it. Drowned, and still turning up in the wash."

It was the closest Maren came to saying *this is right, this is why we're doing it,* and Sable took it as such. They were halfway to the fish market, working out how to smuggle the remaining drawings past the corner watch-post, when Sable felt Maren's hand close on their sleeve.

"Hold up," Maren said softly. "Man by the cooper's shed. Been behind us since the tannery turn. Don't look."

Sable did not look. Their heart performed its old practiced trick — the slow cold drop, the sudden clarity — and their hand found the handle of the portfolio under their coat. Ten years of knowing which rooms creaked, and it came down always to this: the moment between identifying a sound and deciding whether it was wind.

The man detached himself from the shadow of the cooper's shed and crossed the lane toward them with an odd, lurching haste, coat collar up, hat pulled low. Maren stepped in front of Elowen with a movement so fast and fluid it was almost beautiful, and Sable had one absurd instant to notice that the fishwives' capes made excellent disguises and terrible armor—

"Sable."

Hale pushed back his hat. Up close he looked wrecked — three days without shaving, a cut over one eyebrow gone greenish with age, and the hands. Sable read the hands first, as always, and the hands were shaking.

"Master Surveyor," Sable said carefully, loudly enough for anyone watching. "You're far from the customs house."

"I'm nowhere near the customs house ever again." Hale's voice was low and cracked. "Can we walk? Quickly? Please. I have maybe an hour before I'm missed, and after tonight I won't be missed, I'll be *sought*." He glanced at Maren, at the bundle in her arms, and something in his exhausted face flinched. "You have the child with you. Good. Better she hears this young."

"She hears nothing but bells and nonsense," said Maren, unmoving. "Talk."

Hale talked as they walked, fast, keeping to the middle of the lane where foot traffic was thickest, and the story came out of him in pieces, like cargo jettisoned in a storm. He had been ordered, he said — ordered in writing, which was itself a confession, because Vane's steward had grown careless — to locate and destroy the remainder of Corvane's atlas and to report any person connected with it. He had complied for two years because the alternative was ruin, and worse than ruin. But yesterday he had been shown the next order in the sequence: his own name, entered in the steward's ledger, marked for the same silence he had been dispensing. *Redundant instrument*, the notation said. He had understood, finally, with the clarity that comes too late to everyone, what Corvane must have understood on the night he died.

"I reported him," Hale said. The words came out flat and terrible, a stone dropped on stone. "Twenty years ago — no, ten. Ten. I told the steward's office that Corvane held suppressed charts. I believed — God forgive me, I *believed* — that if I gave them the old man's maps they would let everything else lie. Let the Reach lie. Let the grants lie. They burned his workshop wing instead, and they let him die in it, and I filed my reports and slept at night for ten years, and yesterday I read my own name in the same ledger in the same hand." He laughed, a short awful sound. "I've come to defect, Miss Sterling. Except you can't defect from a side you were never properly on."

They had reached the fish market's edge, the great reeking chaos of it, gulls screaming over the gutting tables. Maren steered them into the crowd, where watchers lose their quarry, and Hale fumbled inside his coat and produced a packet wrapped in sailcloth, thrusting it at Sable with both hands, formally, like a man surrendering a sword.

"His field notes," Hale said. "The original surveys. '93 through '96. The measurements in Corvane's own hand — depths, tide tables, the boundary stones of the Reach households, all of it, taken *before* the flood, when he was still working for Vane and did not yet know what the water was for. I took them from the survey office years ago and told the clerk they were misfiled." His mouth twisted. "I kept them the way a coward keeps evidence. In case. Well. The case has arrived."

Sable unwrapped one corner. The handwriting hit them like a blow — that upright, patient, faintly ornamental script, the script of every marginal note in the forbidden atlas, the script that had written *Elowen* in a margin before Sable had ever heard the name. Corvane's hand. Corvane's numbers. Proof notarized by a dead man's fingers.

"With Creed's plates and these notes together," Hale said, "it isn't a pamphlet anymore. It's testimony. Sworn measurements, original documents, a Crown surveyor willing to attest—" He stopped. Swallowed. "If anyone will still hear a Crown surveyor."

Sable looked at him — at the shaking hands, the ruined face, the ten years of complicity finally cracking him open — and thought of Chapter upon chapter of the atlas, of grief reframed as inheritance, and understood that this was what Corvane had died refusing to prevent: not the destruction of paper, but the manufacture of men like this. Men who helped drown a village one honest measurement at a time.

"You'll give your attestation to the magistrates," Sable said. "Not to me. To the ones Vane hasn't bought — Justice Amory's circuit, the ones who still sign their names in public. And you'll do it under your own seal, Hale, because anonymous truth is a rumor and we need a record."

"I know," Hale said quietly. "I've been composing it for two days. It reads like a confession, because it is one." He managed something that was nearly a smile. "Your master would have hated me."

"He'd have mapped you," Sable said. "Accurately. Which is worse."

They arranged it in whispers among the fish stalls: Hale would come to Creed's by the rag merchant's route, after dark, carrying his sworn deposition. The notes would go to the engraver tomorrow, folded inside tax schedules. In three weeks — less, if Vane's men moved faster than fear predicted — the plates would be cut, the press would run, and the drowned quarter would speak with eleven families' worth of voices.

Hale touched his hat brim, turned, and was absorbed into the market crowd, a stooped grey figure going toward whatever cellars a defector rents by the night. Maren watched him go with her arms around Elowen and her chin set.

"Do you believe him?" she asked.

Sable looked down at the sailcloth packet, at the corner of a tide table visible within, dated March 1794 in a dead man's hand. Fragile hope, conspiratorial hope — hope with wet feet on a slippery lane — but hope nonetheless, and heavier than doubt.

"I believe his hands," Sable said. "And I believe he's terrified. And terrified men who've just crossed sides are either our best witness or our fastest way to a scaffold." They tucked the packet deep beneath their coat, against their heart, where the atlas used to ride. "Come on. We need to get these notes to Creed before nightfall — and we need to move, because if Hale's ledger has his name marked redundant, it won't be long before someone reads it and starts asking who he's been seen talking to."

They turned for Salt Lane, walking quickly now, and behind them over the harbor the first drops of rain came down, and far off across the roofs of the Governor's quarter, in a window high above the sea-wall that covered eleven families, a light was burning late — and beside it, though they could not know it yet, Josiah Creed's name was already being written down.
