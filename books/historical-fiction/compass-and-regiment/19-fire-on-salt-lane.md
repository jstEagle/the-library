## 19. Fire on Salt Lane

The printer's shop smelled of lead and lamp oil and the particular sourness of ink worked too long in a small room. Sable had come to know it well in the weeks since the plates had gone under Josiah Quill's needle—had learned the rhythm of his press the way one learns a heartbeat, the clatter-and-breath of it, the way the whole floor shuddered when the screw turned home and left another leaf of truth damp and black on the felt.

Tonight the press was running hot. Nine sets printed. Eleven. Quill worked with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms black to the bicep, humming something tuneless through the pipe clenched in his teeth, and every finished sheet went into the drying rack like a candle set in a window for someone not yet arrived.

"The drowned quarter first," he said around the pipe, tapping the stack of completed leaves. "Let them read what lies under their harbor before anything else. A city that doesn't know its own floor is a city that can be sold twice."

Sable stood at the workbench collating, hands moving by memory now—the forged grants, the marginalia in Corvane's hand, the survey notes Hale had brought folded against his heart like a love letter from a dead man. Outside, Salt Lane was quiet in the way of streets that are only pretending. Maren sat near the door with Elowen asleep against her shoulder, the child's breath a small warm metronome in the cold room.

"Nine more sets," Quill said. "Then we scatter them like seed."

Maren lifted her head. She had been listening to the lane all evening the way she listened to everything—with her whole spine. "Wagons," she said quietly. "Two. They stopped at the corner and they haven't started again."

Sable's hands stilled on the paper.

"Could be the milk trade," Quill said, but he was already reaching for the lamp to turn its wick low.

"Milk wagons don't wait," Maren said.

* * *

Hale saw the fire before he understood it.

He had been walking toward Salt Lane with his coat collar up and the last of Corvane's original survey notes sewn into the lining—a final gift he meant to deliver and then, God willing, disappear across the harbor with the tide. He was halfway down Rope Walk when the sky over the rooftops turned the color of an open wound.

Not dawn. Not yet. The bells hadn't rung the hour.

Then came the sound that every port town knows and no one forgets: the crackle-roar of dry timber taking flame all at once, and beneath it, thin as a needle, screaming.

Hale ran. He was forty-six years old and he ran the way he had run at seventeen along the gunwales of a survey sloop, because some calculations the body makes before the mind consents. The lane was already filling with people in their nightclothes, faces orange in the firelight, and none of them moving toward the burning shop but away from it, because everyone in Vellamare understood by now what kind of fire this was—not mischance, not a lamp knocked over, but a verdict.

The door of the print shop was a wall of light. Through it, silhouetted like figures burned into paper, he could see movement: a man at the press—heavyset, unmistakable—doing something at the back of the shop instead of coming out. Going *toward* the thing, not away.

And at the mouth of the alley beside the building, a woman with a bundle in her arms, turning back toward the fire, held at bay by—

No. Not held at bay. Waiting.

Hale found Sable Sterling in the crowd's edge, soot-striped, wild-eyed, being physically restrained by two neighbors who seemed less to be holding them back than keeping them from walking into the flame.

"The plates," Sable was saying, over and over, to no one, to the fire itself. "He went back for the plates."

"He gave you time," Hale said, and his voice came out strange and flat. "That's what he was doing. Buying the door."

Sable turned. In the firelight, Hale watched understanding arrive on that face the way weather arrives—total, and impossible to argue with.

* * *

Quill had known, in the way that men who work with fire know fire, that he would die badly someday. Lead and oil and paper; a printer's life is a slow negotiation with combustion, and he had always assumed the negotiation would end the way negotiations with governors end.

He had perhaps four minutes. Maybe five. The front of the shop was already lost; the heat pushed at him like a hand on his chest. But the plates were iron, and iron does not burn—it only needs someone to carry it past the burning. He had wrapped them in the heavy felt blankets from the drying rack, soaked them in the water butt, and gotten six of the nine onto the sledge before the smoke found the soft tissue of his throat and began, with the patience of a craftsman, to take him apart.

Six of nine. The forged grants, the drowned quarter, the coastal charts. Enough. It would have to be enough.

He dragged the sledge toward the coal hatch at the rear of the shop—the one concession to caution he had ever made, a way to lower stock into the cellar—and the world went orange, then white, then a kind of dark that was darker than ordinary dark. His last coherent thought was not of pain or of God but of the feel of type set correctly, the small satisfying click of lead letters seated in their chase, order imposed against chaos, one character at a time.

The plates went down the hatch. Quill did not follow them.

* * *

Maren ran.

She had Elowen inside her coat, the child's face pressed to her breastbone where she had hidden cries through inspections and searches and one terrible afternoon when Vane's steward had stood close enough that his breath moved the baby's hair. She ran the way she had learned to run in the years after her own child—empty-armed women run strangely, apologetically—but tonight her arms were full and her feet were sure.

Behind her the print shop became a torch visible from the harbor, and she knew what it meant: every eye in Vellamare would be on the fire, which meant every soldier too, which meant the alleys were hers if she was fast enough.

She cut through the tanner's yard where the dogs knew her smell and let her pass. Through the gap between the cooperage and the seawall where the tide slapped black and loud enough to drown a footstep. Down the drowned quarter's old stair, the one from Sable's maps, the steps worn hollow by feet a hundred years dead.

At the bottom, in the salt-eaten arch where the cache had been found, she stopped and breathed at last, and felt Elowen's heart hammering against her own, twin drums.

"You're all right," she told the child, and told herself. "We're going somewhere. That's all this is. We're going somewhere."

But her arms shook, and she let them, alone in the dark with the tide, because there was no one left in Vellamare whose fear could be fed by seeing it.

* * *

They should have scattered. Every instinct, every rule Corvane had ever taught, said scatter. But Sable stood in the crowd watching the roof beams fall inward in a fountain of sparks, and Hale stood beside them, and neither moved.

"Where is the original?" Hale asked finally.

"Where no one looks." Sable's voice was ash. "It survived. It will survive."

"Then listen to me, because I won't say this twice." Hale took Sable's arm and turned them away from the fire, into the shadow of the ropewalk, speaking low and fast. "Vane didn't send soldiers to arrest. He sent them to burn, and burning doesn't stop at one shop. There's a list. I've seen it—I wrote half of it before I understood what I was writing. Your workshop. Maren's lodging. The houses of anyone who bought paper from Quill in the last month. And my name is on it too, at the bottom, in his own hand." He laughed once, a terrible small sound. "I spent twenty years making his thefts look like law. Apparently the ledger has balanced."

"Go to the magistrates. Testify—"

"I'm marked, Sterling. Marked men don't testify; they're found floating. But you—" his grip tightened "—you can still be heard. You have the atlas. You know which magistrates can't be bought; I gave you those names myself. Do it. All of it. Everything we found in the drowned quarter, everything Corvane died keeping, everything I—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Everything I helped bury."

Then Hale's head turned, sharply, the way a surveyor's head turns when an instrument reads wrong.

Three men had detached themselves from the crowd's far edge. They moved like men who had come to watch a fire and found better sport: unhurried, spreading wide, and one of them had a pistol held low against his thigh.

"Hale," Sable said. "Ambrose."

"I see them." Hale did not step behind Sable. He stepped in front. "The alley behind the cooperage—Maren will have taken the stair. Go left at the arch. Don't stop for anything, don't stop for me."

"Ambrose—"

"Sterling." For the first time since Sable had known him, the surveyor smiled, and it was a young man's smile, from before the Governor's service, before the falsified charts, from some summer when measuring coastlines had still seemed an honest trade. "I reported Corvane. I have owed a death in this city for ten years. Let it go to the right account."

The shot came without warning and without ceremony, the way shots do.

Hale took it in the chest, a little left of center, and the impact turned him half around, and he caught himself against the ropewalk post with the dignity of a man refusing to fall before he had finished speaking.

"Left at the arch," he said again, and slid down the post, and sat, and was done.

Sable ran. Behind them the second shot cracked against brick, and boots hit the cobbles, but the crowd had become a mob of gawkers and rescuers between hunter and hunted, and Sable went left at the arch, and down, and down, into the hollow dark of the drowned quarter where the tide talked loudly enough to hide everything.

* * *

In Hale's coat, when they took the body to the mortuary house, the men appointed to search it found nothing of value: three shillings, a pencil stub, a folded chart of a coastline drawn with loving precision. They did not search the lining, because men who burn print shops do not think to check seams.

Sewn into the left breast, wrapped in oilcloth, were Corvane's original survey notes—the true soundings of the drowned quarter, the depths at which the village roofs still stood, the measurements Vane had ordered altered and Hale had ordered preserved. And folded among them, in Hale's cramped hand, a single sheet:

*To whoever finds this. I was the instrument. Here are the readings I falsified, and here, overleaf, the ones I kept true. I cannot give back what I erased. I leave it written instead. —A. Hale, Surveyor.*

The atlas's truth had been carried all night in a dying man's coat, over his heart, and had not burned.

* * *

The cellar under the drowned quarter was cold and smelled of kelp and old stone. Maren had a shuttered lantern lit low, and by its seam of light Sable found them: Maren on an overturned skiff, Elowen awake now and solemn-eyed in her lap, watching her mother's face with the gravity of a child who understands more than language.

For a long moment no one spoke. Then Elowen reached out both arms toward Sable, and Sable crossed the cellar and knelt and held her, held her so tightly she protested, and felt the shaking start in their own chest and travel outward until it owned everything.

"Quill?" Maren asked.

"Gone. The shop with him."

"And Hale?"

Sable could not answer immediately. Elowen patted their cheek with one small hand, patting, patting, the way children comfort what they cannot comprehend.

"He stayed," Sable said at last. "He paid what he owed."

Maren nodded slowly, and looked at the lantern, and Sable saw her jaw set in the lamplight—that specific hardening, grief converting itself to load-bearing stone.

"So we carry them," she said. "Both of them. Quill bought the plates time. Hale bought yours. That's coin, Sable. That's not nothing. That's the richest we've ever been."

Sable drew back enough to look at her, at the baby, at the salt-crusted arch above them where a village's last standing wall still held up the sky.

"The magistrates' session is in four days," Sable said. "The cathedral crypt. Hale's names. Corvane's atlas. Quill's plates—six of nine, wrapped in felt, if the coal hatch held the fire off them, and I think it did, because Quill thought of everything except himself."

"Then we dig them out," Maren said.

"We dig them out. And we finish what all three of them died buying us the time to do."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone and street, the bells of Vellamare were still ringing the alarm—and beneath the alarm, faint but relentless, the tide went on reading its own patient map of everything the Governor had drowned.
