## 1. The Weight of Paper

Zephyrine Coldharbour had learned early that a life could be measured in what it could carry.

Their mother had taught them this, though not in words. In the winter Zephyrine turned nine — the winter the first republic fell, the one nobody in Vellmere spoke of anymore because it had been replaced by the second, which was now dying in its turn — their mother had stood in a doorway holding a single carpetbag while soldiers searched the house, and everything she owned in the world had fit inside it: two dresses, a photograph, a sewing kit, and a loaf of black bread wrapped in a linen cloth that Zephyrine would later learn was not linen at all but a map, printed on fabric, of a border that no longer existed.

Everything else had been left behind. Everything else had been taken. And her daughter — Zephyrine refused, even now, to settle on son or daughter; the clerks could not decide either, and the ambiguity had proven useful across two collapsing governments — her child had watched from the stairs and understood, in the way children understand the things no one will say aloud, that the world divided itself cleanly into two kinds of objects: those you could run with, and those that ran you down.

Zephyrine was thirty-one now and lived by the first kind.

The flat above the chandler's shop on Rue des Salines was spare as a held breath. A bed. A chair. A kettle. A window that looked out over the rooftops toward the river and, beyond the river, the low gray line of hills that everyone in Vellmere called *the other side* without ever saying what was on it. Under the floorboard beneath the bed frame, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the typewriter case — a good case, leather over ash wood, brass corners gone soft-green with age, containing a Corona Three folding portable that had crossed four borders in twenty years and never once been asked to type anything incriminating, because its real cargo had never been the machine at all.

And hanging on the back of the chair, because a coat on an empty hook looked like nothing and a coat in a trunk looked like evidence, hung the coat.

It was a plain coat. Wool, dark gray, mended at the cuffs with thread chosen to disappear. Anyone who saw Zephyrine in it saw a courier of the Diplomatic Service, fourth class, unremarkable, the sort of person whose papers were checked quickly because there was clearly nothing behind them. The tailoring was deliberately dull. The lining was deliberately dull. The lining was also where the codes lived.

Sixty-two of them, sewn in as patches — small squares of silk, each embroidered in thread the color of the lining itself, worked so fine that a man's fingers would pass over them and feel nothing but cloth. Each square was a cipher key. Together they were, though Zephyrine had never assembled them and never wanted to, the whole of the republic's remaining diplomatic correspondence capability west of the capital. The capital had fallen in October. The ministry's archives had burned — or so the radio said before the radio went silent — and whether the keys were compromised no living person outside this room knew, which meant they were either worthless or the most dangerous sixty-two squares of silk in the province, and there was no way to find out which except to keep carrying them.

Zephyrine had sewn in the last one themselves, three weeks ago, by candlelight, with their mother's old needle, hands steady, thinking the entire time: *this is just work. This is only stitching. This is what I do.* The lie of ordinariness was a skill like any other. It improved with practice. Some nights Zephyrine feared how much they had improved.

* * *

Morning came gray and slow, the way winter mornings came in Vellmere, rising out of the river mist like something surfacing from deep water. Zephyrine dressed by feel, checked the floorboard, checked the coat, ate half a loaf standing at the window. Below, the town was doing what Vellmere always did in the hour after first light: pretending.

The baker fired his ovens. The fishwives set out their trays of river pike already going soft at the gills, because the barges hadn't come up from the delta in nine days and everyone knew it and no one said it. Old Hessler swept his step with the particular violence of a man sweeping away something that wasn't dirt. At the corner of the market square, the notice board still held the government's last proclamation — RECRUITMENT: DEFENSE OF THE PROVINCE, printed on good paper in good ink, curling at the corners now — and pinned beside it, smaller, cheaper, newer, a second notice in a different hand entirely: a list of regulations issued by the Provisional Military Administration of the Northern Territories, effective upon arrival.

*Upon arrival.* The phrase did the work of an army all by itself. Everyone in Vellmere read it every day, and everyone in Vellmere walked past it, and the town went on weighing fish and firing ovens and sweeping steps with the desperate, meticulous energy of people keeping a house clean for guests they hoped would die on the road.

Zephyrine pulled on the coat and went out to work.

Officially, Zephyrine Coldharbour was the clerk-correspondent for the Office of Records and Registry, sub-office Vellmere, population 4,100, function: maintenance of civil documentation. Unofficially — in the way that everything official in the republic had become unofficial, since officially the republic still existed and officially therefore nothing needed hiding — Zephyrine was the western courier. Twice a month for six years, Zephyrine had carried sealed dispatches along the river road to way-stations, farmhouses, and once, memorably, a chicken coop whose third coop from the left contained a drop-box instead of hens. Six years of riding the same forty kilometers of road until it wore into the body like a groove worn into a stair. Six years of being no one, carrying nothing, seeing nothing.

It had even, mostly, been true.

* * *

The Office of Records occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, and it smelled the way all such offices smelled: dust, ink, cold iron, the faint sweet rot of paper left too long in river air. Zephyrine arrived at eight, lit the stove, unlocked the registry with the two keys that hung under their collar, and began the morning's fiction of work.

There was little enough of it left. The birth registers were current through September. After September, the births thinned out like the last leaves on a branch — not because fewer children were born but because fewer families bothered to report them, or dared to. The death registers told the truer story: October's pages filled fast, and November's faster, and the causes listed grew terse. *Fever. Fever. Wounds. Fever. Causes unknown.* On the twelfth of November some clerk with more honesty than sense had simply written *the war*, and the supervising registrar — old Madame Kessel, who had signed off on fifty years of bureaucratic lies and chose this of all moments for precision — had let it stand.

Zephyrine copied names. That was most of the work now, copying names from one book to another, updating rolls, striking through the departed. Somewhere in the middle rows of the archive, under lock, in the cabinet that required both Zephyrine's key and Madame Kessel's, sat the ledger.

Zephyrine tried not to look at the cabinet, and looked at it anyway, the way the tongue returns to a loose tooth.

Everyone in the service knew the ledger existed. Fewer than a dozen people knew what was in it. It had come west from the capital in the spring, in a diplomatic satchel chained to a courier's wrist, and it had been logged into the Vellmere sub-office with a classification stamp Zephyrine had never seen before or since: a closed compass rose, inked in violet. *Instruments of the Provisional Government and Persons Material to Its Continuance.* That was all anyone was told. A ledger of persons material to continuance. For six months it had sat in its cabinet growing heavier in the imagination, and Zephyrine had carried dispatches past it twice a week and never once been allowed to open it, and had long ago stopped resenting this, because in the courier's trade ignorance was armor. You cannot be made to tell what you do not know.

Lately, though, Zephyrine had begun to wonder whose armor it was.

Madame Kessel arrived at nine, shaking river damp off her umbrella, her face carrying the particular grayness it had carried since October. She was seventy and had survived three governments by the simple method of making herself indispensable to records rather than regimes, and she looked now, Zephyrine thought, like a woman doing arithmetic she did not like.

"Coldharbour." She set down her umbrella. "Nothing on the wire?"

"Nothing since Thursday."

"Nothing since Thursday," Kessel repeated, and hung her coat with exaggerated care. That was the whole conversation. It was, Zephyrine reflected, more conversation than they'd had about the war all month. The wire to the east had gone quiet in increments — first garbled, then delayed, then repeating yesterday's messages as though nothing were wrong, then silent — and the silence had spread outward from the capital like ice across a pond, and here they all were, standing on it, listening to it creak.

At eleven, Zephyrine took the morning's thin stack of correspondence to be franked, and that was when the boy came.

He was maybe fifteen, one of the harbor kids, cap pulled low, and he walked into the office the way harbor kids never walked into offices — straight-backed, eyes forward, like someone rehearsing. He set a sealed dispatch on the counter. Oilskin, corded, wax stamped. He said the words he'd clearly been given: "From the sector relay. For the officer administering records."

"That's Madame Kessel."

"She said—" and here the boy's rehearsal broke, just slightly, his voice dropping, "—she said give it to the courier. You're the courier."

Zephyrine looked at the seal before touching the envelope, because looking was habit and habit was survival. It was the seal of the Ministry's western directorate — genuine, or an extremely good forgery, though nobody left had the resources to forge it. Beneath the ministry arms, pressed small into the wax, sat the closed compass rose.

Violet ink. The ledger's mark.

Zephyrine signed the receipt. The boy left at a pace just short of running. Through the window Zephyrine watched him go down toward the water, shoulders hunched now, a kid again, and felt the envelope sitting on the counter like a stone placed on a table between two people about to negotiate.

Kessel came out of the archive, looked at the dispatch, and did not touch it.

"Well," she said. "Open it or don't. But if you do, Coldharbour, sign the register. If it's what I think it is, I don't want my name anywhere near your name."

"You haven't read it."

"I don't need to read it." Her eyes went, briefly, inevitably, to the locked cabinet, then away, out the window, toward the river road. "I've been reading this government's handwriting for fifty years. This is the handwriting it uses at the end."

* * *

Zephyrine did not open it until evening, until the office was shuttered and the stove banked and they were back in the flat with the door barred and the lamp turned low. Old habit: never break a seal in a room with windows facing the street. Older habit, newer fear: perhaps there were no safe rooms anymore, only rooms that hadn't been searched yet.

They cut the cord. They broke the violet wax. The paper inside was ministry stock, the good rag paper, and the hand was the western directorate's chief secretary — a hand Zephyrine recognized from six years of routing slips, neat and upright, a hand that never wasted a stroke. It wasted none now.

*By direction of the Minister-in-Exile Functions, western directorate, and pursuant to emergency instruments of continuity:*

*COURIER COLDHARBOUR, Z., fourth class, is hereby named principal bearer for DIPLOMATIC MISSION HALVEN, with full authority and custody as enumerated. Bearer will present at the Halven Consulate at the earliest practicable date with all instruments in bearer's charge.*

*Bearer will note: courier ANSELM, P., previously designated principal bearer, failed to report at way-station FERME-DU-PONT on the ninth instant and has not communicated since. His instruments are presumed intact and in need of recovery or replacement. The directorate does not use the word 'presumed' lightly. Bear well.*

*By hand. By night. Burn this.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The chief secretary's hand was the signature, and the hand did not tremble, which meant either that the man writing it did not know how bad things were or that he knew exactly, and had decided that steadiness was the last thing he had left to give.

Zephyrine read it three times. Then, as instructed, they held the page to the lamp and watched the good rag paper catch and curl and go to ash in the basin, and stood there while the light of their orders died, thinking about one word in the middle of all the formal language.

Anselm.

Old Anselm, senior courier, who had taught Zephyrine the routes six years ago, who drank his coffee bitter and his brandy bitterer, who had once spent an entire border delay teaching Zephyrine dice games with borrowed coins and had won every one and bought the drinks anyway. Anselm, who was sixty if he was a day, who had carried for the first republic too, who joked that he'd survive every government in the country out of pure spite and bad knees.

*Failed to report.* *Has not communicated.* *Presumed.*

The directorate did not use the word *presumed* lightly.

Zephyrine stood in the cold flat with the ash cooling in the basin and did the thing couriers do, the thing that was the whole profession distilled: they set the fear down on one side of the scale, and on the other side they set the job, and they waited to see which weighed more. It was a trick, of course. It had always been a trick. The fear always weighed more; you simply got good at carrying it anyway, the way you got good at carrying a case whose real cargo wasn't the typewriter.

Outside, the mist was coming up off the river, swallowing the far bank, erasing the hills, the road, the other side. Somewhere out in all that gray, Anselm had stopped moving, and whatever he'd been carrying had stopped with him.

Zephyrine turned down the lamp. Tomorrow, they told themselves, they would go and see Kessel. Tomorrow they would begin. Tonight it was enough to stand at the window of a quiet room in a town still pretending, coat on its hook with its sixty-two secrets sleeping in the lining, and believe — one more night — that this was just another delivery.

Down toward the bridge, very late, a truck engine sounded. Then another. Not the farmers' trucks, which rattled and coughed. These engines ran smooth and heavy and many-tired, coming from the east, and they did not stop at the bridge.

They kept coming.
