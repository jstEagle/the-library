## 2. Orders from a Dying Hand

The dispatch arrived on a Tuesday, which Zephyrine would remember later, because Tuesdays were the day they cleaned the shop windows and the world had no business interrupting glass-polishing with history.

It came by a boy of perhaps eleven, breathless from the hill road, his cap too big and his boots too small, carrying an oilcloth packet sealed with wax the color of dried blood. He did not know what he carried. He only knew the man at the relay station had pressed a coin into his palm and said, *Vellmere, the stationer's, ask for the one who mends books.* The boy repeated this like a catechism, proud of his errand, and Zephyrine tipped him another coin to buy the whole thing silence — not that the boy needed it. In Vellmere, children were taught early that packages were nobody's business, the same way they were taught to cross themselves before the shrine and to look at their shoes when soldiers passed.

Zephyrine took the packet inside, turned the sign on the door to *closed for inventory*, and stood in the back room among the ledgers and paste pots and the smell of hide glue, holding the thing at arm's length as if it might go off.

The seal was correct. That was the first terrible thing. The second was the date stamp: three days old. Dispatches from the capital used to arrive in one. Three days meant the relays were breaking down, or being searched, or both. A republic's mail was like its pulse; you could read its health in how fast the blood moved. This blood was sluggish. Clotting.

They broke the seal with a bone folder, the way they'd been taught — never a knife, knives tear — and unfolded a single sheet typed on the ministry's old Adler, the one with the worn *e* that always printed faint.

*To the bearer. Anselm Voss has failed to report at Halven. Nine days overdue. You will proceed as courier-designate under Article Twelve. Cargo as previously arranged. The Consulate must receive confirmation of credentials before the fifteenth. Burn this after reading.*

There was no signature. There hadn't been signatures for months, only the seal, because names could be extracted and seals could only be broken. Zephyrine read the page twice, then held it over the stove and watched the corners curl brown, then black, then let the last unburned scrap — the corner with the faint, dying *e* — go down into the ash like a moth.

Article Twelve. They knew the article the way a sailor knows the knot that saves him and the knot that hangs him. It was the emergency provision, the one that converted a courier from a functionary into an instrument: no explanations required, no records kept, deniability all the way down. Every republic wrote such articles hoping never to use them, the way every family keeps a funeral suit in the back of the wardrobe. The suit was out now. The suit was being worn daily.

And Anselm. Nine days silent.

Zephyrine sat down on the stool by the window and looked out at Vellmere doing its Tuesday business. Frau Heller shaking her rugs. The cobbler arguing with his son about a last. Two army lorries parked outside the gendarmerie, empty for now, but parked the way a sentence is begun. The town looked exactly as it had looked yesterday and the day before, and this was the lie Zephyrine had been living inside for a year now: that routine was a wall, when it was only a curtain, and curtains are for rooms people intend to stay in.

Anselm Voss had taught them the road. That was the trouble. Four years ago, when Zephyrine was new and frightened and too honest for the work, Anselm had walked them the length of the border corridor, pointing out which innkeepers could be trusted and which merely pretended well enough to be useful, which priests took confessions seriously and which took notes. He'd shown them how to sew documents into a coat lining so the stitches followed the garment's natural seams, invisible even to a tailor's eye. He'd taught them the rule that governed everything: *You are not brave. You are boring. Boring men cross borders; interesting ones get searched.*

He was fifty-one years old, careful as a watchmaker, and he had gone quiet between the second checkpoint and the river, in country he knew better than his own kitchen.

Zephyrine did the arithmetic they always did and hated themselves for doing. Nine days. If Anselm had been delayed — roads closed, papers questioned, weather — he would have found a way to send word. He had signal arrangements nested inside signal arrangements; he could make a chalk mark on a church door mean *delayed, proceeding* and a different mark mean *turn back*. No marks had come through the network in either direction. Which left the other arithmetic, the one with no remainder: either he was taken, or he was dead, or he had run. Zephyrine did not believe he had run. Men who ran sent no word either, but men who ran also emptied accounts first, and Anselm's account in Vellmere still held his deposit against winter lodgings. A man planning to vanish does not prepay for a room he won't need.

So: taken or dead. And if taken, then somewhere in an army interrogation cell, a man who knew the route, the contacts, the cargo schedule, and the contents of every coat lining he'd ever stitched was answering questions with whatever he had left. Zephyrine tried not to imagine it and imagined it anyway, the way you cannot help touching a bruise.

The cargo. *As previously arranged.* The phrase was a courtesy and a cruelty. Nobody had ever told Zephyrine what they carried. That was doctrine — a courier who doesn't know can't confess — but after four years the not-knowing had developed a weight of its own, a phantom limb of information. They knew the shape of it: flat, rigid, book-sized, wrapped in oilcloth and packed beneath the false bottom of the typewriter case, balanced so the case still felt right when lifted. They knew it went to the Halven Consulate, the neutral mission upriver, where foreign eyes could see what domestic eyes were forbidden to. They knew it mattered enough that the ministry had spent real money on couriers rather than trusting the post. What it *said*, whether it was codes or correspondence or something worse, Zephyrine had never been permitted to learn, and had learned instead to carry ignorance the way they carried everything else: close to the body, evenly distributed, so it wouldn't show.

They went upstairs and took the typewriter case down from the top of the wardrobe. The typewriter itself was real — a battered portable, bought legitimately years ago, its ribbon half-spent — because the best hiding places are furnished. A hollow case with nothing in it invites suspicion; a case with a working machine and a sheaf of carbon paper and a tin of spare ribbons invites nothing at all. Zephyrine lifted the carriage, checked the false bottom's fit, and found the oilcloth parcel where it had waited for eleven months, patient as a seed.

Eleven months. They had carried it to three towns and back without ever being asked to deliver it, and each time the orders had been the same: hold, wait, be ready. The republic had been keeping its secrets in motion, like a man moving money ahead of creditors. Now the orders said *proceed*, and proceed meant the waiting was over, and the waiting being over meant there was nothing left to wait *for*.

Zephyrine set the case on the bed and began, without deciding to, to pack.

This was the ritual that steadied them: the sorting of the portable life. Codes into the coat lining — the diplomatic cipher book, thin as a missal, its stitches redone twice yearly so the thread would look aged. The second code, the field cipher, into the heel of the left boot, wrapped in waxed silk. Money distributed: some in the belt, some in the hatband, a little visible in the breast pocket, because a traveler with no money is as suspicious as one with too much. Papers — the forged commercial pass identifying them as a stationery supplier's agent, which had the double virtue of explaining the typewriter and being dull enough to put a border clerk to sleep. The photograph went into the shirt pocket, over the heart, face inward: a woman and two children on a balcony in a city Zephyrine could barely remember, taken before the wars, before the flight, before everything that made inheritance a matter of what fits in a case.

Their mother's photograph. Their mother, who had fled across this same border twenty-two years ago with a sewing kit and a language she'd spend the rest of her life losing her accent in, and who had died believing — or at least saying — that the republic her daughter's... that her child served was worth the serving. Zephyrine had inherited her caution, her stitching skills, and her habit of measuring every room by its exits. It was, they supposed, an inheritance like any other: you didn't choose it, and you caught yourself wearing it.

* * *

In the evening they walked down to the harbor, because the harbor was where the town went to think, and because Ivo Brandt was usually there when the light went bad, drinking cheap brandy and correcting the sea.

Ivo sat on the bollard nearest the fish sheds, a map folded in his coat pocket that he took out sometimes just to fold again. Once, he had drawn maps for the army — good maps, brilliant ones, the kind that win campaigns — until the campaign he'd mapped turned into something he couldn't sign his name to, and he'd said so, loudly, in a room full of officers, and been broken to civilian life with a speed that impressed even the civilians. Now he drank and drew charts nobody paid for and knew more about the border country than any man alive, including, Zephyrine suspected, the men currently occupying it.

"Your boy came by the office today," Ivo said, without looking up. "Asking after you. Little fellow, big cap."

"Did he now."

"Mm." Ivo turned his glass by its base, a quarter turn, precise even drunk. "Roads east of the pass are closed. Has been since Thursday. Official reason is snow." He finally looked at Zephyrine, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who reads terrain for a living and had learned to read faces the same way. "Snow doesn't close roads. Snow closes *reports about* roads. Somebody doesn't want traffic counted."

"You should write for the newspaper."

"The newspaper prints what the garrison allows." He drained the glass and set it down with the exactness of a man placing a unit on a map. "Whatever you're not going to tell me you're not doing, do it soon. Windows are closing. They always close faster than the maps say."

Zephyrine almost told him then. The words got as far as the teeth. But Article Twelve cut both ways — no explanations given, none asked — and besides, Ivo was watched; disgraced men are always watched, it's the cheapest surveillance there is, the watchers knowing the watched have nothing left to lose except company. So they said only: "If a friend needed to know which roads aren't closed, hypothetically—"

"Hypothetically," Ivo said, standing, brushing off his coat with two efficient strokes, "a friend would tell them that the river road is open but suicidal, the forest track is open and slow, and the third option—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes, some old professional pride surfacing through the brandy like a drowned body coming up, "—the third option doesn't exist yet. But it could. For a price." He touched his cap, an ironic little salute, and walked off along the quay with the rolling, deliberate gait of a man navigating by instruments only he could see.

Zephyrine stood a long time watching the water go dark. *For a price.* Everything had a price now. The republic itself was in arrears.

* * *

That night they wrote the acceptance. Doctrine required it to be bland past the point of parody — a commercial letter, order confirmation, shipping terms — and Zephyrine composed it with the typewriter that was also a vessel, the keys striking home with their small faithful clatter:

*Messrs. Halven & Co., Importers. Gentlemen: Re your inquiry of the 3rd inst. We confirm our representative will call upon you before the 15th to complete formalities regarding the consignment previously discussed. All documentation travels with our representative. We trust the usual courtesies will be extended. Yours faithfully.*

They read it over. Somewhere in a consulate office upriver, a tired man would decode this into *courier incoming, cargo intact, deadline firm*, and would understand that the *usual courtesies* meant asylum, stamps, archive, sanctuary — the whole liturgy of neutrality performed over a parcel of paper. And somewhere else, possibly, a man who was not tired but alert would intercept a copy and decode nothing, because there was nothing to decode, because it was a letter about stationery, because boring men cross borders.

Boring men crossed borders. Interesting ones got searched. And dead ones, Zephyrine thought, sealing the letter, stopped reporting altogether, and the republic that sent them did not stop to mourn, only to designate.

They addressed the envelope, propped it in the outgoing tray for morning collection, and lay down dressed, in the manner of soldiers and couriers and other people whose sleep is a loan rather than an asset. From the street came the sound of the lorries starting up again — engines turning over in the cold, one, then two, then a third — and headlights sweeping the ceiling like searchlights rehearsing.

Just another delivery, Zephyrine told themselves, the way they had told themselves before every crossing for four years. Just paper. Just roads. Just a man named Anselm who was probably fine, probably delayed, probably sitting in some farmhouse right now complaining about the coffee.

The lie settled over them like the coat they'd wear tomorrow, familiar, well-stitched, and not warm enough.

Outside, the third lorry's engine caught, and the convoy pulled out toward the eastern pass — toward the checkpoints, the river, the road Anselm had not come back from — and Zephyrine lay in the dark listening to it go, understanding with a clarity that felt like cold water that the delivery had already begun, and that they were not the sender.
