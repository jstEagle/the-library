## 3. Requisition

The trucks came at dawn, when the light was still the color of dishwater and the frost held its grip on the windowpanes of Vellmere.

Zephyrine heard them before they saw them — a low grinding on the north road, too heavy for carts, too many for farmers — and stood at their window in the grey half-dark with one hand pressed flat against the cold glass, counting engines by ear. Four. Then a fifth, lagging, its transmission complaining. They had learned this from their mother, or from whoever had taught their mother: count first, understand after. Numbers could be carried away quietly inside the head where no one searched.

By full light the market square was occupied.

They came out with the rest of the town, because not coming out would have been worse, and stood in loose knots along the shopfronts while soldiers in field-grey unwound wire from spools and staked it across the square's western mouth, closing the road to the pass. The soldiers were young. That was the first thing Zephyrine noticed and could not stop noticing — young men with red hands and chapped lips, breathing steam, handling their rifles the way farm boys handle tools they haven't yet broken. One of them glanced at the crowd and looked away quickly, as if the crowd were something he'd been told not to touch.

An officer climbed onto the fountain's rim. He was older, precise, his collar clean in a way that seemed almost obscene against the morning. He unrolled a printed notice and read it in a flat, carrying voice that didn't trouble itself with warmth: Vellmere was hereby placed under the administration of the Army of the Provisional Authority. Curfew from dusk to dawn. All arms to be surrendered within twenty-four hours. All public records, registers, ledgers, correspondence, and documents of every kind belonging to the former municipal administration were to be delivered to the requisition officers stationed at the customs house by noon.

*All records*, he said, and Zephyrine felt the words go through the crowd like a current through wet rope.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. In Vellmere, people had gotten good at stillness; it was a skill the border had been teaching them for generations, and the last year had been the final course.

* * *

Zephyrine knew what lived in the customs house, because everyone in Vellmere knew, though knowing had a different weight depending on who you were.

The town ledger. Not the tax rolls, not the granary book — those were furniture, harmless. The other ledger. The one kept for eleven years in the clerk's back office behind a door that was always locked, the one that recorded, in a careful looping hand, every favor done for the republic that used to be: who sheltered the survey teams, who sold grain below price to the garrison at Halven Ford, whose barn hid the radio operator through two winters, who signed the loyalty attestations, who drove couriers east in the false bottoms of cider carts. Eleven years of names. Three hundred and forty-one entries, give or take, each one a family, each family a street, each street the length of Vellmere itself.

Zephyrine's own name was not in it. Their work had never passed through the clerk's office; it traveled in coat linings and sealed dispatches and was known to perhaps four living people. But their mother's name was there — entered twice, in '31 and again in '34 — and half the faces in the crowd pressing around them now belonged in that book the way some men belong to drink: helplessly, permanently, with consequences already accrued.

At nine o'clock the requisition officers began.

There were three of them, set up at trestle tables under the arcade of the old wool exchange, and they worked with the terrible calm of men doing arithmetic. A captain with spectacles received the documents as they came. Beside him sat a civilian — not army, something else, someone with a satchel and soft gloves — who examined each item, made an entry in a small black notebook, and sorted the papers into canvas pouches. The pouches were stamped. The stamps came down with small, decisive sounds, like knuckles on a door.

Zephyrine watched from the shadow of the bakery awning and understood, with the slow horror of watching water rise around your own doorstep, exactly what they were seeing. This was not looting. Looting is chaos. This was *collection*. Every name in that ledger was being lifted out of Vellmere's keeping and folded into a satchel, and once it crossed into that satchel it stopped being history and became evidence, and evidence has only one direction to travel — toward a list, toward a summons, toward a truck at dawn like this one but smaller, and quieter, and going somewhere worse.

Old Marta Brenner went up to the table with her hands shaking so badly she dropped her market basket, and the eggs inside broke, and nobody laughed, and the soft-gloved civilian waited without expression until she'd gathered herself enough to surrender the parish register she'd been holding against her chest like a grandchild.

"Thank you for your cooperation," the captain said. He said it to every person. It was the worst thing about him.

* * *

Through the morning the tables filled. Deed books. School registers. The minutes of the town council going back thirty years, hauled out by the council clerk himself, Herr Vasch, who walked the stack over with his spine straight and his face gray, and who paused at the table just long enough to say, very quietly, "Some of those are duplicates," which was either courage or suicide or both, and the soft-gloved man wrote something down, and Zephyrine did not think it was *duplicates exist.*

The army wanted everything, and everything was given, because Vellmere had four hundred souls and the column in the square had, by Zephyrine's count, more than two hundred men, and numbers were numbers even here, even now. Resistance was a word from posters in another country. What existed in Vellmere was delay, misdirection, the small treasons of the weak: a register misplaced, a page stuck to the one beneath it, a cellar wall with a cavity. Zephyrine watched these small treasons happen all around them all morning and took a fierce, ashamed comfort in each one, the way you take comfort in a candle during a flood.

Then, at half past ten, the soldiers went to the customs house.

Zephyrine saw them come out with the ledger before they understood what they were seeing — a soldier on each side, carrying it flat between them like a stretcher casualty, wrapped now in oilcloth against the damp. Behind them came the clerk, Aldous Renn, who had kept the book for eleven years, who had locked its door every night, who was walking beside it with his hat in his hands saying something — pleading something — and the lieutenant escorting them was not listening, was in fact walking slightly faster, the way you walk past a beggar you've already decided not to pay.

At the table, the oilcloth came off. The soft-gloved civilian opened the ledger and turned pages slowly, checking something against his black notebook, and Zephyrine, watching his face, saw the moment his expression changed — not much, a settling, the look of a man confirming that a delivery has arrived complete — and knew that whatever he was cross-referencing, he had known the contents already. Someone had copied this book before. Someone had sold it. The seizure was theater; the sentence had been written months ago in an office none of them would ever see.

Renn was still standing at the table when they closed the ledger. "My name is in it," Zephyrine heard him say. His voice had gone thin and high. "I kept it. Whatever's in there, I'm the one who—"

"Yes," the captain said, not unkindly, which was somehow crueler than the alternative, and wrote Renn's name on a slip of paper, and slid the slip into a separate envelope, and the envelope into his breast pocket, over his heart, where orders go.

* * *

By noon the trestle tables were empty, the pouches loaded into a staff car, and the town stood around in the bright cold afternoon like a house after a death, everyone moving carefully, speaking low, not looking at the places where things used to be.

Zephyrine did not go home. They walked the long way, past the print shop on Tanner's Row, past Odile Ferrant's darkened windows, telling themselves they were only passing, that there was no reason to stop, that stopping was the kind of act that got noticed — and stopped anyway, and stood in the lee of the doorway with their collar up, and knocked twice, softly.

The door opened a hand's width. Odile's face appeared in the gap, ink-stained even at the edge of sight, eyes going past Zephyrine's shoulder to the empty street and back.

"You shouldn't be out walking," she said.

"No one should be out walking." Zephyrine slipped inside. The shop smelled of lead and oil and cold, and the presses stood silent under their sheets, and Odile bolted the door behind them with hands that were steady in the way of hands that have decided to be steady.

"They took the ledger," Zephyrine said. There seemed no point building up to it. "Renn too. And the parish register, the council minutes, everything. It's in a staff car heading west right now."

Odile said nothing for a long moment. She crossed to the window and stood with her back to Zephyrine, looking out at nothing, a small woman gone broad-shouldered with years of lifting type, and Zephyrine watched her shoulders and understood that she was not frozen; she was calculating. Printers calculate. It is what a lifetime at the case teaches you: how much fits on a page, how long a run takes, how much paper is left in the drawer.

"There were copies," Zephyrine said. "Of the ledger. Weren't there? Renn was careful."

"There was a press copy. One." She turned around. Her face was terrible and calm. "Locked in the cellar with the type. Which they will find, if they search me, in an hour. If I move it tonight, in a week." She looked at her own hands as though consulting them. "Eleven years of names, and by Friday that book will be sitting in some staff officer's office being indexed, and after indexing comes lists, and after lists comes lorries. I've seen it before, child. You have too. You're just younger about admitting it."

Zephyrine thought of Anselm, somewhere east of the second checkpoint, silent these nineteen days. Thought of the dispatch sewn into their own coat lining, weighing almost nothing, weighing everything. The lie of ordinariness they had put on yesterday felt thinner than ever now, a garment the morning had already worn through.

"Why are you telling me this?" they asked.

Odile looked at them for a long time — looked, Zephyrine realized, at their coat, at the way they stood, at the particular guardedness of a person carrying cargo — and something in her expression shifted, the way type shifts under the fingers just before it locks into the chase.

"Because you knock like a courier," she said, "and I set type for the founding documents of a country that lasted eleven years, and I am not going to stand in my own shop while they carry three hundred families away in a satchel." She pulled the sheet off the nearest press as she said it, briskly, like a woman deciding to bake bread. "Come downstairs. Both of us have work, and mine starts tonight."

Down the cellar stairs, into the smell of paper and damp stone, Zephyrine followed her — and it was there, in the yellow light of a single bulb, that Odile Ferrant lifted the loose flagstone behind the type cabinet, drew out the press copy of the town ledger wrapped in waxed cloth, and put it into Zephyrine's hands without asking their name.

It weighed less than a loaf of bread.

That was the thought Zephyrine carried up the stairs a quarter hour later, out the back door, into the freezing blue dusk of Vellmere's first occupied day: *it weighs less than a loaf.* Three hundred and forty-one lives, bound in board and thread, and lighter than bread. Somewhere west, a staff car was carrying the original toward indexing and fire. Somewhere east, a road ran through checkpoints toward a consulate that might or might not still open its doors. And somewhere between, a courier named Anselm had stopped answering the road, and a courier named Zephyrine now stood in a dark alley holding the only copy of a book the army believed it owned.

Behind them, faint through the walls of the print shop, they heard the clatter of the press begin to turn.
