## 4. The Widow's Back Room

The print shop sat at the crooked end of Rue des Plombs, where the street narrowed as if the buildings on either side were leaning in to share a secret. Zephyrine had passed its window a hundred times without stopping: the faded gold lettering on the glass — FERRANT & FILS, above it a smaller line, *and daughter*, scraped away by some earlier economy — the trays of lead type visible through the frost-furred pane, the smell of ink and solvent that reached even the street, sharp as a struck match. It was the kind of shop that had survived the republic by being too small to matter, and now, Zephyrine hoped, might survive the army for the same reason.

They checked the street twice before knocking. The trucks had come at dawn and had not left; their engines idled in the market square all morning like animals waiting to be fed. Officers in grey-green moved through the town with clipboards, posting notices that curled at the corners in the cold. Zephyrine had read one from across the square — REQUISITION OF RECORDS, BY ORDER OF THE OCCUPATION ADMINISTRATION — and had felt the words land somewhere beneath their ribs, where fear kept its permanent lodgings.

The dispatch tube was inside their coat, against their chest, wrapped in oilcloth. It had been there since dawn. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

Zephyrine knocked.

For a long moment there was no answer, and they were preparing to knock again when a bolt slid back and the door opened the width of a forearm. One eye regarded them through the gap — dark, quick, set in a face the colour of old paper, lined the way type gets worn at its highest points.

"The shop is closed," the woman said.

"I need printing done," Zephyrine said. "Small work."

"We're closed for that too."

"Odile Ferrant?" They watched the eye change. "I was told — if I ever needed honest work done quietly, this was the house for it."

The eye did not blink. "Who told you?"

"A man named Anselm."

Something moved behind the eye then, something that was not surprise exactly but recognition of what surprise would cost. The door opened fully. "Inside. Quickly."

* * *

The shop smelled of metal and oil and, beneath both, of years. Type cabinets lined the walls, their shallow drawers half-open like mouths mid-word. A proofing press sat under sheets in the corner, and above it, hanging from a nail that had probably held calendars and ration posters in its time, hung a framed page — dense with columns, headed by an engraving of a broken chain.

Zephyrine stood dripping snow onto the floorboards and understood, with a slow cold settling, whose house they had walked into. Everyone in Vellmere knew the Ferrant name. Every schoolchild had recited the words that Odile Ferrant had locked into lines of type when she was younger than Zephyrine was now — the founding declaration, four thousand characters of it, each one placed by hand. They had set the republic in lead because paper burns but cast metal endures. That was what her husband used to say, anyway, before the fever took him and the *fils* became just a memory in the shop's signage.

"You knew Anselm," Zephyrine said.

"I knew his handwriting." Odile bolted the door behind them, drew a blind over the window, and lit a lamp turned low. "He sent me work sometimes. Circulars. Nothing worth remembering." She turned and looked at Zephyrine properly, taking in the coat, the travel stains, the way they stood with their shoulders drawn in around their chest like someone carrying breakables. "And you are the person he told me about, which means you carry something he thought I'd shelter. So let's not perform. Show me."

"I don't know what you—"

"Child." Her voice wasn't unkind. It was simply too tired for theatre. "I have been in this trade forty years. I can tell a person hiding papers the way I can tell a forged plate — by the weight they put on their feet. Sit down by the stove before you faint on my floor, and show me what's in your coat."

Zephyrine sat. The stove in the back room gave off a thin iron heat that made the lamplight tremble. Their hands, when they brought them to the coat's inner seam, were not quite steady. They worked the stitches they themselves had sewn two nights ago — coarse thread, chosen to look repaired rather than new — and drew out the oilcloth packet, and unwrapped it on Odile Ferrant's worktable between a composing stick and a tray of twelve-point bodoni.

The dispatch was a single sheet inside a brass tube, sealed at both ends with wax stamped by the chancellery's courier seal: a compass rose within a broken circle.

Odile went very still.

She did not touch it at first. She bent close, the way she must have bent over proofs all her life, reading not the words but the impression itself — depth of bite, sharpness of edge, the tiny flaw at the rose's southern point that only someone who had handled hundreds of these seals would ever have noticed.

"This is genuine," she said quietly. "This is the last diplomatic seal of a government that stopped existing on Tuesday." She straightened up and looked at Zephyrine with an expression Zephyrine could not read — grief and fury and, underneath both, something almost like respect. "You're the courier."

"I'm the courier," Zephyrine said, and heard their own voice say it as though reporting the weather.

"To where? The consulate?"

"Halven. Across the river." Zephyrine swallowed. "Anselm was supposed to take it. He's gone silent on the road. No word in nine days."

Odile pulled out a stool and sat down across the table, and for a while she said nothing. Her hands, which had been restless since the door opened, went flat and still on the wood, and Zephyrine saw that they were a printer's hands — short-fingered, scarred at the knuckles where lead and brass had bitten her for decades, nails cut blunt.

"They posted the requisition notice this morning," she said at last. "All records. All registries. Ledgers, rolls, correspondence. Anything with a name written in it." She met Zephyrine's eyes. "Do you understand what that means? The town ledger has every soul in Vellmere who signed anything for the republic. Petitions. Relief funds. Shelter lists during the border purges. My name is in that book eleven times. Yours may be too."

"It is," Zephyrine said. "Twice."

"Then you came to the right widow, or the worst one, depending on how the winter goes." She rose, went to the type cabinet, and stood with her hand resting on a drawer as if it were the shoulder of an old friend. "Anselm trusted me because in thirty years I never once printed a lie, and never once repeated a truth I was paid to keep quiet. Both halves matter. Anyone can do one."

"I'm not asking you to print anything."

"No," Odile said. "You're not asking me for anything at all, which is why you're standing there looking like a man asking for something. Sit still."

She disappeared into the back of the shop, and Zephyrine heard a hatch open, a ladder scrape, the smell of cold earth rising. When she returned she was rubbing her hands together against the cellar chill.

"There's a room down there. My husband built it during the purges, when we hid three editors for five weeks. Stone walls, earth floor, a vent through the coal chute. The soldiers requisitioned the registry office and the granary and Father Aubry's records, but nobody requisitions a printer's cellar because nobody thinks a printer owns anything but ink." She looked at Zephyrine steadily. "You'll sleep there tonight. You'll keep the dispatch on your body, not in my house — whatever you carry stays against your skin, so that if they take either of us, they only get one of us and none of the cargo. In the morning we'll see about the road. Now — have you eaten?"

Zephyrine opened their mouth to refuse, out of habit, out of the deep courier's discipline of owing nothing to anyone — and found that the refusal wouldn't come. It had been thirty hours since the trucks were sighted on the north road, and longer than that since they had last eaten anything warm.

"Yes," they heard themself say.

* * *

She fed them onion soup with bread fried in drippings, and while they ate she worked, because Odile Ferrant was incapable of sitting idle at her own table. She sorted type by feel, sliding letters into her composing stick with a speed that needed no light, and talked in the unhurried way of people who have decided to be honest and can afford the luxury.

"My husband always said the republic would die twice," she said. "Once when the armies came, and once when people stopped believing it had been real. He worried about the second more." She snapped a quoin tight. "I set the founding declaration when I was nineteen. I set it twice, actually — once for the official record and once for myself, a private copy, because I wanted to hold the thing in my hands after everyone else had gone home to bed. Four thousand characters. I could set it today from memory, in my sleep, upside down." She glanced at Zephyrine. "That's what a printer does with a country. We keep it in pieces, in boxes. Any fool can burn paper. Nobody's ever burned lead."

"Why help me?" Zephyrine asked. It came out blunter than intended. "You don't know me. Anselm vouched for me and Anselm is—" They stopped.

"Gone silent," Odile finished softly. "Yes." She set the stick down. "Because forty years ago I was nineteen years old and a government didn't exist yet, and a woman I never met carried the draft of its founding papers through three checkpoints sewn into her petticoats so that I would have something true to set in type. She wasn't a soldier. She wasn't important. She was just a woman who happened to be going the right direction." She shrugged, a small economical gesture. "Countries aren't made by generals, child. They're made by couriers. And someday somebody will come to your door needing you to be going the right direction, and you'll understand."

Zephyrine looked down at the soup, at the dispatch lying on the table with its compass rose seal, and felt the shape of something being handed to them that was older and heavier than any single delivery.

"The soldiers will search shops," they said. "Random inspections. It's what the manuals recommend."

"The manual writers have never met a printer's cellar." Odile allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "But you're right to worry. Which is why tomorrow you leave, first light, before the patrols settle into their rhythms. And why tonight—" she nodded toward the hatch "—you go down there now, before the street lamps come on."

* * *

The cellar room was low-ceilinged and dry, smelling of coal and paper and something faintly medicinal — the ghost of some long-ago convalescence. A cot, a blanket, a candle stub. Through the vent came the sound of the street, muffled: boots, a truck changing gear, the occupation learning the geography of Vellmere.

Zephyrine lay on the cot with the dispatch against their chest where it had lived all day, and listened, and did not sleep. Above them, through the floorboards, came the small precise sounds of Odile working late — the slide of the stone, the tap of type being distributed, once, very softly, a few bars of something sung under the breath, a tune Zephyrine did not know and suspected was older than the republic.

They thought about Anselm, who had eaten dinner at their table six weeks ago and complained mildly about the weather on the north road. They thought about the ledger in the registry office, in a clerk's satchel now, or stacked in a truck, every name in Vellmere riding toward whatever the army did with names. They thought: *I should have gone straight through. I should not have stopped. Stopping is how couriers die.*

And underneath that thought, quieter, stubborn, like type worn into a page: *Stopping is also how countries survive.*

Near midnight, the floorboard sounds stopped. Then footsteps crossed the ceiling, and the hatch lifted, and Odile's voice came down the ladder, pitched low and very even.

"Don't come up. There's a patrol on our street, checking doors. Stay exactly where you are, and if anyone comes down those steps who isn't me—" a pause, and Zephyrine heard her take a breath "—there's a loose stone behind the coal chute. Your husband's size, the space fits a person. Use it."

She began to draw the hatch closed, then stopped.

"Zephyrine," she said — and Zephyrine realized they had never told her their name. "Anselm described you too. In case you ever came. He said: *the quiet one, the careful one, the one who measures everything.*" The hatch lowered to a crack, and her eyes appeared in the gap, dark in the lamplight from above. "Measure carefully in the morning. Because I've had word tonight, from a neighbour — they've set up the first checkpoint already, at the toll bridge on the north road. And they're not checking cargo."

Another breath.

"They're checking names."

The hatch closed. The bolt slid home. Above Zephyrine's head, calm as Sunday, Odile Ferrant went back to setting type.
