## 5. Anselm's Ghost

The smuggler's name was Perrin, and he drank like a man trying to drown something that could swim.

Zephyrine found him where Odile said they would: the back room of the Salt Cellar tavern, past the kitchen where two women gutted herring by lamplight, through a door that stuck until you lifted it on its hinges. The room smelled of tallow and wet wool and the particular staleness of men who had stopped caring what their rooms smelled like. Perrin sat alone at a table scarred with knife-marks and candle-wax, a bottle of plum brandy at his elbow, a deck of cards laid out in a patience that had clearly been abandoned hours ago.

He looked up when Zephyrine entered, and his eyes did the quick arithmetic Zephyrine had learned to expect in Vellmere since the trucks came — *what are you worth, what will you cost me, can I afford to know you*.

"You're the one Ferrant sent." His voice was gravel poured slow. "Sit. Or don't. The chair's broken anyway."

Zephyrine sat. The chair wobbled but held, which felt like an omen of some kind, though Zephyrine had never been able to read omens and suspected they were mostly weather with ambitions.

"I need to know about the road east," Zephyrine said. "Past Vellmere. The checkpoints."

"Everyone needs to know about the road east." Perrin turned the bottle a quarter-turn on the table, a small ritual, a man giving his hands something honest to do. "Nobody likes the answer."

"I'm not everyone."

"No." He studied them with the flat professional attention of a man appraising cargo. "You've got the look. The courier look. Hands quiet, eyes doing inventory. Anselm had it too."

The name landed in Zephyrine's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They had prepared for it — had told themselves all morning, walking the long way through Vellmere's crooked lanes, that they would hear it eventually, from someone, in some form. Preparation, Zephyrine knew, was mostly a story people told themselves so their faces would hold still when the moment came.

Their face held still.

"You knew him," Zephyrine said.

"Knew him? I carried him twice." Perrin laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the shape of laughter worn hollow, like a glove left out all winter. "Twice. That's how good he was. Nobody crosses the second checkpoint twice unless they're very good or very stupid, and Anselm was neither. He was careful the way water is careful. It finds every crack."

*Was.* The word sat between them now, undeniable, and Zephyrine understood that whatever came next would be built on top of it.

"When?" Zephyrine asked. It was the wrong question first, and they knew it even as they said it. The right question was *how*, or *where*, or *did he talk* — but grief is clumsy, and it reaches for time before it reaches for truth.

"Eleven days ago. Maybe twelve. The nights run together now." Perrin poured himself a finger of brandy, considered it, poured another, and pushed neither glass toward Zephyrine. A host who had stopped pretending. "You want the whole thing or the short version?"

"The whole thing."

He nodded slowly, as if Zephyrine had confirmed something about them — that they were the kind who wanted the whole thing, who believed the whole thing was survivable if you held it properly. He took a drink. Set the glass down with exaggerated care, aligning it with the table's edge, buying himself the seconds.

"There's a woman in Harrow Fen," he began. "Runs fish up the river road, runs other things back down. She saw it. Not close — close enough. The second checkpoint sits where the river narrows, before the ice bridge. The army built a timber platform over the bank, floodlights, the whole theater of it. They'd been stopping every traveler for three days. Turning most of them back. Detaining some." He paused. "You understand what detaining means now. Everyone does."

Zephyrine nodded. In Vellmere they had watched the detained walk out to the trucks with their collars straightened and their faces arranged into expressions of cooperation, and none of them had come back, and the town had learned to say nothing about this in the same way it had learned to say nothing about the republic falling — a silence practiced until it became a muscle.

"Anselm came through at dusk," Perrin went on. "My friend says he looked calm. Carrying a typewriter case — did you know that? Same as couriers always do. The army has orders about typewriter cases. Did you know that too?"

Zephyrine's hands, folded on the table, went very still. Outside the door, one of the women gutting herring said something low and laughing, and the ordinary sound of it made the room feel farther away, as though Zephyrine were hearing it through river ice.

"They opened the case on the platform," Perrin said. "Under the lights. And inside — well. You know what was inside. He argued it was just a machine, papers for his firm, invoices, nothing. He was good at arguing. But the officer in charge — a captain, my friend thinks, or someone wearing a captain's coat — he didn't care what the papers said. He cared that the case had a false bottom. And a false bottom means a courier, and a courier means—" He spread his hands, an ugly little flower blooming. "Orders are orders. There's a standing directive. Anyone carrying sealed diplomatic material through occupied territory is treated as a spy under field regulations. No tribunal. No transfer. The sentence is carried out at the point of capture."

"And they carried it out," Zephyrine said. Their voice sounded strange to them. Level. Almost bored. Somewhere beneath the levelness, something was screaming, but it screamed in a language Zephyrine had spent years refusing to learn, and so it stayed below the surface, a current under ice.

"On the platform. At dusk, so the whole queue of travelers could watch. That's not incidental, by the way. That's design. They want the roads to carry the news faster than any telegraph." Perrin drained his glass. "And the case, the ledger, everything — they burned it right there. In a brazier they'd brought for the purpose. My friend says the paper caught fast. All those names going up in one breath."

Zephyrine thought: *he burned alone.* Thought: *the names burned with him, and the army believes that was the end of them.* Thought, with a cold clarity that arrived uninvited and unwelcome: *they believe it because they have never understood that a list is not paper.*

Aloud, they said only: "Did he give them anything? Names?"

Perrin looked at them for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Zephyrine's face seemed to decide something, because he leaned forward, elbows on the table, and lowered his voice to the register men use for things that could get a room full of strangers killed.

"He gave them nothing. My friend was close enough to hear the last part. He stood there while they read him the regulation, and he said — she remembers it exactly, she's told it four times and it comes out the same every time — he said, 'You can burn the book. The book isn't the names.' Then they shot him." Perrin sat back. "So. No. He gave them nothing. But here's what you actually came to ask, and I'll give it to you straight because Ferrant vouched and her vouching is worth more than my brandy."

He tapped the table once, hard, like a judge's gavel in miniature.

"They know a courier is coming. Not might be. Is. The directive at the checkpoints isn't general anymore — my friend heard the officers talking. There's a description circulating. Height, build, the typewriter case, the route. They pulled the manifests from the old foreign ministry before the ministry finished burning, and they know what the last dispatch pouches contained, and they know the republic's final mission was to the Halven Consulate. Which means they know where you're going. Which means the third checkpoint — the one at the consulate gate itself — isn't a checkpoint anymore. It's a net."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a floor tilts when a ship changes course. Zephyrine gripped the table's edge and kept their face where it was.

"They burned Anselm's ledger," Zephyrine said slowly, working it through aloud because saying things aloud was how they tested whether they were thinking or merely panicking. "They believe the evidence is destroyed. But they still expect a courier. Why? If the book is ash, why guard the consulate gate?"

"Because they're not sure." Perrin shrugged, and the shrug contained multitudes: the army's paranoia, its bureaucratic terror of its own superiors, its inability to believe that any single copy of anything was ever truly singular. "Armies don't burn things and then relax. They burn things and then double the guards, in case fire missed something. In case there were two books. In case the courier memorized the names — and a courier who memorized the names is worse than a courier with a book, because you can't search a memory at a checkpoint. Well." He smiled without warmth. "You can. They do. But it takes longer."

*You can't search a memory,* Zephyrine thought, and then thought, immediately after, with a sensation like stepping off a stair that wasn't there: *but they will try.*

The silence stretched. One of the herring women laughed again; somewhere above, floorboards creaked under the tavern's evening weight. Zephyrine became aware that they had not breathed properly since the word *platform*, and let the breath out slowly, and found that the breath shook more than they wanted it to.

All their life, they had survived by being small. Unremarkable. The coat with the codes sewn into its lining was deliberately shabby; the face was deliberately forgettable; the gait was deliberately unhurried, the walk of a person with nowhere important to be. Invisibility was not a talent Zephyrine had cultivated. It was an inheritance — passed down from whoever had fled before them, from the photograph of the family they barely remembered, from every generation that had learned the same lesson written in different scripts: *be nothing, and they will pass you by.*

Anselm had been invisible too. Careful the way water is careful. Twice across the second checkpoint. And the army had put him under floodlights and shot him at dusk so that a queue of strangers would carry the news down every road like sparks.

Invisibility, Zephyrine understood now, sitting in a smuggler's back room smelling of tallow and defeat, was not safety. It was merely the delay before being seen. The army did not need to find you. It needed only to make the world so narrow that finding you was inevitable — a funnel of checkpoints, a description on every officer's tongue, a standing order that converted your existence itself into a capital crime. You could be as gray as weather, and the weather would still catch you at the river crossing, because the river crossing was the only way through, and they owned the river.

The illusion collapsed quietly, the way ice gives way — not with a crack you hear, but with a sudden absence of support beneath you.

"So," Zephyrine said. "Every road east is a throat, and they're holding the knife at each one."

"That's a dramatic way to put it." Perrin reached for the bottle, found it light, and set it down with disgust. "But yes. The first checkpoint you might bluff — it's staffed by clerks, clerks are lazy, lazy is survivable. The second is where Anselm died, and they'll be watching it like hawks now, expecting you to avoid it, which means the avoiding routes are watched harder than the road itself. And the third—"

"Is the consulate gate."

"Is the consulate gate." He looked at Zephyrine with something that might, in a better-lit life, have been pity. "Ferrant didn't tell you to turn back?"

"She offered me her cellar."

"A cellar's not the worst fate in this country right now." He almost smiled. "But you're not going to take it. I can see it from here. You courier types — you've got religion, even if you don't pray. The book is the sacrament and the road is the church and you'll walk into the floodlights same as Anselm did, because the alternative feels like drowning."

Zephyrine said nothing, because he was right, and because admitting it aloud would hand him a piece of them they couldn't afford to lose.

"What I don't understand," Zephyrine said instead, "is how they knew the contents. The dispatch pouches were sealed. The manifests—"

"Were filed. In triplicate. By a government that trusted paperwork more than it trusted God." Perrin's laugh was brief and bitter. "The new regime didn't conquer the republic, friend. It inherited it. Every clerk, every file, every manifest — the machinery didn't stop, it just changed whose name was on the letterhead. Somewhere in a requisitioned ministry office, a man who used to work for your side now works for theirs, and he reads the same files, and he follows the same procedures, and one of those procedures was flagging the last diplomatic pouches bound for Halven." He tapped his temple. "They don't need spies. They have archives."

*The ledger in the clerk's satchel,* Zephyrine thought. *The town's names, confiscated at dawn, sorted by a man at a desk. The same desk, probably. The same man.* The army was not a horde. It was a filing system with guns, and Zephyrine had been imagining they could hide from it the way one hides from a storm. But you cannot hide from an archive. An archive remembers where everything is.

"Last question," Zephyrine said. "If you were carrying what I'm carrying — hypothetically — how would you cross?"

Perrin stared at them. Then he laughed, really laughed this time, a short harsh bark that made the candle flame jump. "Hypothetically. Of course. Everything in this country is hypothetical now." He turned the empty bottle a quarter-turn, completing the ritual. "I wouldn't. That's my honest answer, and it's free. But if I were young and stupid and religious about a book—" He held up fingers, counting. "I wouldn't go alone. Alone, you're a description. With company, you're a crowd, and crowds confuse lists. I'd want someone who knows the checkpoints from the inside — not rumor, not fish-wives' tales, but the actual ground, the sight lines, the rotation of the guards, which officers take bribes and which take trophies. Someone who drew the maps, maybe. If such a man existed."

Zephyrine went very still. "Such a man exists."

"Does he." Perrin's eyebrows rose with theatrical innocence. "There was a cartographer once. Army survey corps. Drew half the border maps the checkpoints are sitting on now. Brandt — Ivo Brandt. Defected two years back, or was thrown out, depends which story you buy. Lives over on Tanner's Lane, drinks at the Broken Compass, hates everybody equally. He knows where every eye is posted between here and the consulate gate, because he's the one who decided where to post them." He leaned back. "He also charges like a wounded bull and trusts nobody, including the people who pay him. Especially them."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because Anselm was my passenger twice, and I liked him, and I didn't lift a finger when he walked toward that platform, because lifting a finger gets fingers cut off." Perrin's voice dropped, and for one moment the drunkard's mask slipped and something older and wearier showed underneath. "Consider this the finger. Consider us square. Now get out of my room before I change my mind and sell your description to the provost's office, because the price is getting better every day, and I'm getting poorer every day, and I'm only human, whatever that's worth."

Zephyrine stood. The broken chair scraped. At the door, hand on the warped wood, they paused.

"Perrin. The woman who saw it. Your friend in Harrow Fen. Anselm's last words — *the book isn't the names*. Are you certain?"

"Certain as anyone is." He was already reaching for the cards, resuming his abandoned patience, dealing solitaire to an audience of candle smoke. "Why?"

"Because if it's true," Zephyrine said, "then they killed him for nothing. And if they killed him for nothing, then everything they think they won at that checkpoint is worthless — and they don't know it yet."

Perrin looked up from the cards. For a moment neither of them moved, two people holding opposite ends of the same terrible arithmetic.

"Well," he said finally. "Let's hope you're better at staying alive than he was. Tanner's Lane. Ask for the mapmaker. And Zephyrine—" He used their name for the first time, though Zephyrine had never given it. "Whatever he charges you, pay it. Some prices look steep until you see the alternative."

Outside, Vellmere lay under its first honest snow, the flakes coming down thick and silent, erasing the truck ruts in the square, softening the edges of the requisition notices on the customs house wall. Beautiful, in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. Zephyrine pulled their collar up against the cold and against the sewn-in codes pressing flat against their ribs, and turned toward Tanner's Lane, and the snow began at once to fill their footprints behind them, as if the town itself were practicing the army's trick: making it seem as though no one had ever passed this way at all.
