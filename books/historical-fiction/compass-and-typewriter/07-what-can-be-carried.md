## 7. What Can Be Carried

The cellar of Odile Ferrant's print shop smelled of ink and cold stone and, beneath both, of apples rotting quietly in a barrel no one had emptied since autumn. Zephyrine sat on an upturned crate with everything they owned in the world arranged on the floor before them, and the arrangement itself was a kind of confession.

It did not look like much. It never had. But tonight, under the single bulb Odile had run down on a fraying cord, the smallness of it felt deliberate — as if Zephyrine had spent thirty years practicing for exactly this moment, when a life would have to fit inside a case designed to hold forty pounds of iron and keys.

They began, because beginning was better than thinking.

* * *

The typewriter case came first, because it was the mission and the mission was the only thing that mattered now — that was the story, and Zephyrine told it to themselves the way you tell a child there is nothing under the bed. They opened it and lifted out the machine, a Remington with a chipped *R* and a ribbon gone grey at the edges, and set it aside on the crate. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged where the carriage arm would rest, lay the ledger.

They still had not read it. That was not modesty; it was discipline, or perhaps fear wearing discipline's coat. The book named every soul in Vellmere who had signed anything, paid anything, sheltered anyone in service of the republic that was dying two hundred kilometers to the north without so much as a gasp. Anselm had carried it. Anselm was dead for it. Zephyrine carried it now, and the fastest way to die for a thing, they had learned, was to know too much of it too soon. Names were heavy. You could drown in names. Better to carry them sealed than to open the book and find your neighbors' faces between the lines.

So: ledger, wrapped, back into the case. Codes next — four of them now, sewn into the linings of two coats and the waistband of the spare trousers, each a strip of rice-paper covered in figures fine as insect legs. Zephyrine pressed a thumb against the seam of the grey coat and felt the paper's faint ridges through the wool, like a pulse. Somewhere in the capital, some clerk had stitched these into a courier's clothing and called it security. The republic's last act of diplomacy, hidden in its own laundry.

Then the rest of it. The remainder. The everything-else.

* * *

There was a tin of tea, half full, bought in Vellmere three weeks ago when three weeks ago had still felt like a country with a future. Zephyrine weighed it in their hand. Tea was weight. Tea was comfort, and comfort was weight, and Ivo Brandt had said last night over his third glass of something colorless: *You'll want to travel like a rumor, not like a refugee. A rumor carries nothing.* He had said it with the contempt of a man who had once moved whole armies across paper and now could not move himself off Odile's kitchen chair.

Zephyrine kept the tea. Some rebellions are small.

A folding knife, bone-handled, the blade worn thin from sharpening. Kept. A spool of black thread and a needle — kept, because seams opened and seams closed, and the codes lived in seams. A bar of soap. A stub of pencil. A folded map of the northern provinces that Ivo had dismissed with a snort — *I drew that one, and I lied in three places out of spite* — but which Zephyrine kept anyway, because even a liar's map tells you where the liar thought you'd go.

And then, at the bottom of the canvas satchel, wrapped in a handkerchief that had once been white, the photograph.

Zephyrine held it a long time before looking at it properly. This was ritual. Every few months, in some new room, in some borrowed light, they unwrapped it and looked and rewrapped it, and the ceremony took less than a minute and cost more than anything else they owned.

Four people stood in front of a house with a crooked porch. A woman with her hair pinned up carelessly, one hand on the shoulder of a girl of maybe ten. A man in shirtsleeves squinting into the sun he should have been facing. And a boy at the edge of the frame, half-turned away, caught mid-motion as if the camera had interrupted him leaving.

Zephyrine knew — because Odile had asked, gently, one night over soup, and Zephyrine had answered because silence would have been ruder — that the woman was their mother, and the man their father, and the girl a sister named Marta whom Zephyrine could not remember ever speaking to, though they must have spoken a thousand times. The boy at the edge was themselves, aged six, already walking out of the frame.

The family had fled east in the first winter of the war. Zephyrine did not remember the fleeing. They remembered only fragments afterward: a train station, a stranger's hands lifting them onto a truck, the smell of straw and engine oil, and then years of other people's kitchens. Their parents had sent them ahead with a cousin of a cousin of a friend, the way you throw a child over a wall before climbing it yourself, and the wall had grown taller every year until no letter could cross it, and then the letters stopped, and then the country the letters would have come from changed its name twice.

Everything Zephyrine owned had come to them the same way. The satchel — left by a courier who hadn't returned for it. The knife — a gift from a border farmer who'd said *keep it, I'm going somewhere knives won't help*. Even the photograph had arrived inside someone else's letter, mailed by a stranger who wrote only: *Thought you should have this. Forgive me if I am wrong about the address.*

Inheritance. That was the word for it, apparently. Other people inherited houses and land and silver. Zephyrine had inherited exits. Every object in their possession had been carried by someone running, and handed off at a door, a station, a ditch — passed along like a bucket in a fire line, and now the fire had reached Vellmere and the bucket had come around to Zephyrine's hands again, heavier than water, holding names instead.

They almost put the photograph back without deciding anything. That was the old habit: keep everything, decide nothing, let the weight accumulate until some border guard made the decision for you. But Ivo's voice was in the room now, or the memory of it — *travel like a rumor* — and rumors cannot afford sentiment.

Zephyrine looked at the family on the crooked porch. The mother's face was blurred where the print had been creased and refolded too many times, decades of pockets and palms wearing her features down to a suggestion. Soon, if the folding continued, she would be nobody at all. Just weather-stain. Just paper.

*If I leave you here,* Zephyrine thought, *and the army burns this street, you burn with it. If I take you and they search me, they find a photograph, and a photograph is a story, and stories get people shot.*

Neither argument decided it. What decided it was simpler and worse: the photograph was the only thing Zephyrine owned that they had chosen. Everything else had been handed to them by the fleeing. This — they had kept. Through eleven towns and nine winters, through searches and sickness and one long hungry spring, they had kept it, and a person who has inherited nothing but exits is entitled to one object that is not an exit.

Into the case it went, flat against the ledger, handkerchief and all. Grief, filed under practicality. The bulb hummed. Somewhere above, Odile's treadle creaked in slow rhythm, the widow working late the way she always worked late, setting type for orders she no longer believed in because her hands needed the motion the way other people's needed prayer.

* * *

What remained on the floor was the life that didn't fit.

A second shirt, decent wool, good for funerals and interviews. Zephyrine touched the collar, remembered buying it in Halven city two years ago for a delivery that required looking respectable, and put it in the discard pile with a firmness that surprised them. Funerals could be attended in what you were wearing. There would be funerals enough.

Books: three. A grammar of the Halven tongue, essential, kept. A novel with a cracked spine whose plot Zephyrine had loved and could no longer recall. A slim volume of poems, water-damaged, that had belonged to — they searched memory and found nothing — someone. Someone had given it to them, or left it behind, and Zephyrine had carried it through at least five towns without reading past the first page. That was inheritance too: carrying the unread gifts of the vanished, honoring debts whose terms you'd never known.

They kept the grammar. They kept the novel, because a border is easier to cross with something to read at midnight, and terror is lighter when divided into chapters. The poems went to the discard pile, and Zephyrine's throat tightened doing it, which was absurd, which was grief performing arithmetic, and they let the tightness pass through and out like a draft under a door.

A tin cup. Discard — Odile had cups. A length of rope. Keep; rope was the most honest tool ever invented. Matches, a candle end, a small brass compass whose needle wobbled drunkenly north-northwest and had to be forgiven rather than trusted. Keep, keep, keep. The compass had also been inherited — found in the lining of the satchel, as if the previous owner had hidden it from himself — and Zephyrine had long suspected it pointed true north only when it felt like it, which made it, they supposed, a fitting heirloom for a family of fugitives.

Boots. Two pairs, one nearly dead. Wear the living pair; abandon the corpse. Socks, mended and mended again. Keep all. Cold kills more couriers than bullets — Ivo had said that too, in one of his rare moments of usefulness, before retreating back into the bottle like a man into a bunker.

Last, the money: sixty-one crowns in mixed notes, soft as cloth from handling, plus a gold ring that had been sewn into the satchel's strap by whoever packed it last. Emergency money. The kind you sold only when selling it was the last act available to you. Zephyrine redistributed the notes — some in a boot, some in the case's false bottom beneath the typewriter's feet, some in the breast pocket where a man might reasonably keep a wallet and no reason to be shot for it. Never carry your life in one pocket. Another inherited lesson, teacher unknown.

When it was done, the case held: a typewriter, a sealed ledger, a photograph, four code-strips distributed through two coats hanging on Odile's cellar nail, sixty-one crowns in three locations, matches, candle, rope, knife, thread, soap, pencil, tea, a lying map, and a compass that lied too. One life, packed for departure. It fit with room to spare, and the room to spare was the part that hurt — all that empty space where a different life might have been, a life with furniture in it, with books read past page one, with a sister remembered well enough to mourn specifically.

Zephyrine closed the case. The latches went *snick, snick*, a sound like a small door locking twice.

* * *

Odile found them an hour later, asleep sitting up against the crate, and did not wake them. She stood at the bottom of the stairs with a lamp, an old woman in a shawl looking at a young courier curled around a typewriter case the way children curl around the things they cannot explain wanting, and she thought about the founding documents she had set in type forty years ago — the ones with all those fine words about permanence — and how the republic's last testament had ended up sewn into a stranger's coat lining, traveling south in the dark.

She put a blanket over Zephyrine without waking them, and went back up to her press.

Zephyrine dreamed of the crooked porch. In the dream the boy at the edge of the frame turned fully around and walked back into the photograph, and the mother's blurred face sharpened just enough to say something — one word, maybe a name — and then the whole picture folded along its old creases and went dark, and Zephyrine woke in the cellar with the blanket sliding off and the lamp burning low and the taste of a word in their mouth they couldn't pronounce.

Dawn was two hours off. Ivo had said they'd leave at dawn, before the garrison's patrols settled into their morning boredom, before the frost turned to mud on the river road. First checkpoint by noon, he'd said, if the passes held. *My passes will hold,* he'd said. *It's the people holding them I worry about.*

Zephyrine rose, stiff, and checked everything once more — the ritual of the sorted life, performed now not to choose but to reassure. Ledger: sealed. Codes: sewn. Photograph: flat against the book, mother against names, the two inheritances pressed together like hands.

At the top of the stairs, the print shop waited, and beyond its fogged windows, Vellmere waited, gray under a sky the color of dishwater, soldiers' breath already steaming at the corner posts. Somewhere north, a road ran toward a frozen river and a checkpoint where a courier named Anselm had stopped being a rumor and become a warning.

Zephyrine picked up the case. It weighed forty pounds, give or take a ledger, give or take a life.

The latch of the shop door was cold under their hand, and outside, the first gray light found the road — and standing at the mouth of the lane, stamping his boots against the cold, waiting with the impatience of a man who despised waiting, was Ivo Brandt, sober for the first time in days, and watching the checkpoint road with an expression Zephyrine had not seen on him before.

Fear, recognized and refused.

"Change of plan," Ivo said, when the door opened. "We're not going alone."
