## 8. First Checkpoint: The Toll of Names

Zephyrine had expected Ivo to mean a guide, or a bribe, or at most a second set of hands. What he meant was a funeral.

"There's a grain convoy leaving the mill road at seven," he said, jerking his chin toward the gray horizon. "Farmers from the eastern parishes, hauling their requisition quota to the army depot past Kessel. The soldiers wave them through like clockwork. We walk in among the carts."

"And the farmers? Do they know they're carrying us?"

"They know three more people are walking to market." Ivo's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "In winter, everyone going anywhere is going to market. Nobody asks which market."

Three more people. Zephyrine counted heads in the lane as the light came up: Ivo; a stooped woman in a black shawl who introduced herself only as Marta and then never spoke again; and a young man with windburned cheeks and a canvas coat too heavy for the season, who said his name was Pell and who would not stop looking at the typewriter case.

"That's an expensive thing to carry to market," Pell said.

"It's my trade," Zephyrine said. "Typist. Letters, invoices, whatever's paid for."

"Who pays for typing on the consulate road?"

"Men with money and no penmanship." They let the silence do the rest of the work. It was a lesson Anselm had taught them without ever meaning to: the best lie is a boring one, delivered by a boring person, and then left alone.

The mill road ran white and rutted between frozen ditches, and the grain convoy was exactly what Ivo had promised — six carts, four men, two boys, and a smell of chaff and horse that clung to everything. The farmers took one look at the strangers falling in beside them and arranged their faces into the particular blankness of people who have survived many governments. One of them, an old man with a whip curled in his fist, muttered, "Keep behind the third cart. The sergeant counts from the front."

"Why?" Zephyrine asked.

"Because he does," the old man said, and cracked the whip, and that was all the answer there was.

* * *

Ivo counted from the front too, but he counted differently. He counted the sandbag emplacement on the rise — two machine guns, one traversing toward the road, one toward the fields, which meant the fields were considered the likelier avenue of trouble, which meant the army had learned something since his day, and he hated not knowing what. He counted the wire strung along the ditch. He counted the clerk's tent set well back from the barrier, its stovepipe smoking, because checkpoints were never decided by rifles; rifles only collected the bodies. Checkpoints were decided by men with lists.

He had drawn this one. That was the joke of it, the joke he carried under his ribs like a stone: somewhere in the army's archive sat a map in his own hand, inked in his own style, with the sight lines and the drainage culverts and the little annotation in the margin — *clerk's position should remain out of earshot of vehicle inspection* — that some captain had approved without reading. He had been good at his work. That was the whole of his disgrace. Good cartographers made good roads for armies, and when Ivo Brandt finally understood whose feet those roads were built to carry, his maps were already marching down them without him.

"You'll want to be dull," he told Zephyrine, low, as the barrier came into view. "Not clever. Clever is a smell. Soldiers are trained to notice smells."

"I'm not clever," Zephyrine said. "I'm a typist going to market."

"And I'm a surveyor on contract to the depot command." He handed them the pass without looking at it. He had forged it two nights ago with a steadiness that had surprised him; his hands hadn't been steady around a bottle in years, but around a pen they remembered everything. "Marta is my sister. Pell is her son."

Zephyrine glanced back at the young man in the heavy coat. "Is he?"

"He is today. Today is all a forgery has to cover."

The sergeant at the barrier had a red nose and the patience of a man who had stood in this cold every morning since the trucks came, and would stand in it every morning until someone told him to stop. He waved the first carts through with barely a glance. Then his eye found the pedestrians, and the list came out of his greatcoat.

"Names."

Ivo gave them. His voice had changed — flatter, more bored, the accent shifted half a province eastward. Zephyrine marveled at it even while hating the necessity. Everyone was someone else now. Perhaps that was what occupation meant: a whole country issued new names and no time to break them in.

"Surname Brandt." The sergeant ran a cracked thumbnail down the column. "There's a Brandt on here."

Ivo did not move. Zephyrine watched him not move — total stillness, the stillness of a man whose heart was doing violent things behind a face of wood — and understood suddenly how much courage it costs to look stupid rather than look guilty.

"Surveyor," Ivo said. "Contracted out of Kessel. If your list wants me, it wants me at the depot, because that's where I'm due."

The sergeant looked at him for a long moment, then turned the list around and jabbed a line. *Brandt, F. — laborer.* "That's not you?"

"Do I look like a laborer?" Ivo spread his hands — ink-stained, soft-palmed, ruined for work. "My eyes went bad drawing lines. They gave me a pension and my wife gave me nothing. Now I draw lines for whoever pays."

Something moved behind the sergeant's face — not suspicion exactly, but the fatigue of a man who knew that half the people crossing his barrier were lying and could not afford to check each lie against the cost of being wrong twice a day. He grunted. He waved at the clerk's tent. "Papers go to Voss. He stamps, you go."

* * *

The clerk's tent smelled of coal smoke and cheap varnish, and Zephyrine felt the smell land on them like weather, because they knew that varnish. Odile bought it by the tin. Odile thinned it, tinted it, sealed her forgery-inks with it — the deep violet-black she called *republic blue*, mixed originally, so the story went, for the founding documents themselves, and kept alive ever after in the same unwashed stone jar like something domesticated but not tame.

Voss the clerk was younger than his posture. He took the passes one at a time, held each to the lamp, stamped, squared the stack, took the next. Zephyrine rehearsed boredom: invoices, penmanship, a bad marriage, cold feet. Boredom, boredom. The case strap cut into their shoulder. Forty pounds, give or take.

Then Voss stopped squaring the stack.

He was holding Marta's pass — Marta, who had not spoken since the lane, who stood now at the tent flap with her black shawl pulled down over her brow like a woman attending her own funeral. And Zephyrine saw it happen in the small muscles of the clerk's face: the tiny contraction at the corner of his eye, the almost imperceptible tilt of the head, a man listening not to words but to ink.

"This printing," Voss said. "Where was it done?"

Ivo answered before the pause could grow teeth. "Kessel stationery shop. Where else does anyone get papers done?"

"We had documents last month," Voss said slowly, still looking at the pass, "from a man out of Vellmere. Same weight of stock. Same—" He tilted it toward the lamp again, and Zephyrine watched the light come through the paper and knew, with sick certainty, what he was seeing: the violet-black, the particular bite of Odile's press, the seal rendered with a fidelity no honest printer would dare. "Same shade. It's a distinctive shade."

"Government issue," Ivo said. "It's meant to be distinctive. That's rather the point of seals, isn't it? If my sister's travel pass looks official, thank God for it. Would you prefer we traveled on something less convincing?"

It was a gamble of exactly the kind Ivo had warned against — clever, a smell — and Zephyrine felt the tent go thin around them, the canvas walls no thicker than the moment. Outside, the sergeant shouted something about carts. A horse stamped. The stove ticked.

And then Marta spoke, for the first and only time.

"Young man," she said, in a voice like a drawer opening, "my husband carried papers like these through two wars and a revolution, and if you have a complaint about the shade, you may write to him at the cemetery in Dolen. Stamp the things. My son is waiting in the cold, and he has weak lungs, and I have buried enough of my family without adding you to the list of causes."

Silence. Voss looked at her. Something in the old woman's certainty — the sheer administrative outrage of her — reached the clerk where argument could not. He had perhaps a mother. He had perhaps a list of his own dead. He stamped the passes. He pushed them across the table.

"Move along," he said. "And tell your stationer his ink is memorable. That's not always a compliment these days."

* * *

They were through. Twenty paces past the barrier, fifty, the carts creaking ahead, the machine guns receding into the white morning — and Zephyrine discovered that passage had a taste, faintly metallic, like a coin held in the mouth. Guilt, they thought. This is what guilt tastes like. Not the grand kind. The small kind, the daily kind, the kind that walks beside you wearing your coat.

Because Pell had not come through.

It happened at the barrier itself, in the space of thirty seconds while the tent drama swallowed everyone's attention: the sergeant with his list, working down the column of pedestrians, had come to the young man in the heavy canvas coat and found a name he liked better than most. *Pell, A. — Kessel parish.* Or a name near it. Zephyrine saw the exchange only in fragments, turning at the sound: the sergeant's finger on the paper, Pell's face going carefully blank — the same blankness the farmers wore, but younger, unpracticed, cracking at the edges — and then two soldiers materializing from the emplacement with the unhurried menace of men for whom detention was simply another chore between breakfast and lunch.

"Just questions," the sergeant was saying, in the tone of a man who had said it many times and knew precisely what it was worth. "Step over to the wire, sir. Won't keep you but an hour."

Pell looked down the road. Zephyrine watched him look — watched his eyes find Marta's back, find Ivo's, find Zephyrine's, and hold there a half-second too long. He knew them. However he'd fallen in with Ivo's plan, whatever heavy cargo rode in the lining of that absurd winter coat, he knew who they were and what would happen to him if the soldiers' questions got thorough. And he said nothing. He lifted one hand, not quite a wave, and let himself be walked to the wire fence at the roadside, and stood there among the frozen weeds with his hands in his pockets like a man waiting for a bus that might never come.

"Keep walking," Ivo murmured. His voice was level. His knuckles, on his stick, were white.

"He knew us," Zephyrine said.

"He knew *you*. He volunteered for this route yesterday; he knew what the list might say. Everyone on this road gambles. Some dice come up wrong." But he didn't look back either, and neither of them believed him, and the carts rolled on, and the distance to the barrier grew with the horrible ease of all distances that cannot be re-crossed.

At the top of the rise Zephyrine finally allowed one glance back. The checkpoint had resumed its rhythm: carts, stamps, breath steaming. Pell still stood at the wire. Two soldiers were going through his coat pockets with methodical fingers, and even at this distance Zephyrine saw the moment one of them found the lining — saw the soldier's hand stop, press flat against the canvas side of the coat, and stay there, and then the soldier's head came up and turned toward the clerk's tent, and Pell's shoulders dropped by a fraction, the way a man's shoulders drop when he finally stops pretending.

"What was he carrying?" Zephyrine asked.

"How would I know?"

"Because you don't collect strangers, Ivo. You collect assets. What was in his coat?"

Ivo said nothing for a long time. Ahead, the depot road forked, and the grain convoy groaned rightward toward the army gates, and their parting with the farmers approached with the dumb punctuality of everything else that morning. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flatness of a confession he'd been saving.

"A book. Part of one. Pages sewn into the lining — his own idea, or someone's. Names, he said. More names." He looked at Zephyrine, and for once there was no bitterness in his face, only the exhausted clarity of a mapmaker reading terrain he can no longer change. "I thought it was ballast. I thought if anything went wrong, it should go wrong around something worthless. I was wrong about which of us was carrying the valuable cargo."

Behind them, small in the white morning, the soldiers were walking Pell toward the clerk's tent. Toward Voss, with his lamp and his memory for shades of ink. Toward a list that already knew too much and would soon know more.

Zephyrine tightened the strap on the typewriter case and did not stop walking, because stopping was impossible and grief was a luxury scheduled for later, and yet their feet had grown heavier with every step, as if the detained man's coat had somehow been draped, unseen, across their own shoulders.

"One book or all of them," Zephyrine said quietly, to no one, to the road.

Ahead, past the fork, past the frozen ditches, the second checkpoint waited somewhere beyond the river — and now the army would have a name, a face, and a description of everyone who had passed through with them.
