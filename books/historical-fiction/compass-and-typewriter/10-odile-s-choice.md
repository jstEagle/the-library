## 10. Odile's Choice

The army took the town records on a Tuesday, and Odile Ferrant counted the trucks from her upstairs window the way she had once counted lines of type — by habit, by eye, by the old discipline of measuring the world in columns. Six trucks. Two officers in grey coats with the new eagle stamped on their collars. A clerk with a satchel that would not have held a hymnal, let alone the history of Vellmere, and yet he carried the whole of it away: the births, the debts, the grain accounts, the ledger with its careful columns of names — names of people who had signed petitions, sheltered deserters, sold bread to the republic's postmen, voted in elections that no longer existed.

She watched them load the strongbox from the town hall onto a truck bed and thought, with a printer's cold clarity: *paper burns.*

That was all. No grief yet, no fear. Just the professional assessment. Paper burns. Ink fades. Memory dies with the rememberer. She had spent forty years fighting all three, and she was not going to stop because a clerk with a small satchel had won the argument for now.

She went downstairs and opened the shop.

* * *

The print shop smelled the way it had always smelled — oil, ink, cold iron, and the ghost of solvent — and Odile drew the breath of it like a woman coming home from a long journey. She had not run a full press since her husband died, six years now, but the machines were hers and had been her husband's before her, and his father's before that. The Ferrants had printed the republic's founding proclamation in this room. Her husband, then a young man with ink to his elbows, had pulled the first proof while the delegates argued in the capital about whether to sign. He had printed it anyway. *Set it as though it were true,* his father had told him, *and it will become true.* That had been the family creed for three generations, and Odile had never found reason to amend it.

The army had not come for the shop. They had come for records, for names, for the machinery of the old government — but they did not understand printing. That was the mercy of conquerors: they always underestimated the trades. They understood guns and grain and ledgers, but they did not understand that a press was a machine for making a thing true, or at least for making it undeniable, and that the plates were worth more than the book.

She had pulled no plates from the town ledger. She had never seen it whole. But she did not need to.

She had set the names herself.

It had been her small, quiet treason for years — the widow's tithe. When the town clerk came in to print the annual register, the notices, the proclamations, Odile had kept a standing forme in the back cabinet: a locked chase of type holding every name she had ever seen cross the clerk's desk with the republic's seal beside it. Petition signatories. The women who had sewn armbands. The men who had driven the mail coaches through the pass in the last hard winter. She had set them in six-point type, dense as scripture, and locked them in the cabinet the way other widows locked away wedding silver. She had told herself it was sentiment. She had told herself it was habit. She had told herself a hundred small lies, and now, standing in her own shop with the trucks' engine noise still hanging in the cold air, she admitted what it had always been: preparation. A printer keeps her formes. A printer knows that the edition is not the work. The work is the type, and the type can always be reset.

She unlocked the cabinet and carried the forme to the press.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon with the shutters drawn and a single lamp burning, and the work was the old work, the true work, and her hands remembered everything. Composing stick. Quoins. The satisfying resistance of type locked tight in the chase, each letter shouldering its neighbor, a whole town's worth of defiance standing shoulder to shoulder in a steel frame. She had been afraid all day — she was not a fool; she had watched the soldiers requisition the grain stores and the post office and old Marchand's horses, and she knew the arithmetic of occupation — but the fear went somewhere strange when she worked. It went into her hands and came out as precision. Every letter she set was a letter she had set ten thousand times, and the type did not care that she was afraid. The type only cared that she was accurate.

By dusk the forme was ready: two pages, closely set, one hundred and forty names, each with a line beneath it — what they had done, in the plainest words she could manage. *Gave shelter. Signed the petition of March. Drove the mail. Fed the garrison's deserters. Voted.* No adjectives. Odile had spent her life around words and had learned their treachery; she wanted none of hers between these names and the men who would come looking. Just the names and the facts, set tight, ready to be multiplied.

One hundred copies. She had thought about the number while the lamp burned. A hundred was more than the army could burn. A hundred was enough that no clerk's satchel could gather them all back in. A hundred was a hundred hearths, a hundred hands, a hundred chances that somewhere, in some town down the line of the new roads, a page would surface years from now in a Bible or a floorboard or a child's schoolbook, and someone would read it and know that the people in the ledger had not been erased, only murdered, and that someone had refused to let the murder be tidy.

She inked the press and pulled the first proof, and the sound the machine made was the sound of her whole life: the heavy breath of the platen, the hiss of the roller, the small wet kiss of ink finding paper. She held the proof to the lamp and read her own handiwork, and there in the middle of the third column, set in the same six-point type as all the others, was her own name.

*Odile Ferrant. Printed the republic's founding proclamation. Set the town's registers.*

She had put herself in years ago, when the forme was young and the risk had seemed theoretical. She considered taking herself out now. She stood with the composing stick in her hand for a long moment, considering it, and then she locked the forme back up with herself still in it. A list with the printer's name missing was a list with a hole in it, and holes were how lies got in.

She printed all night.

* * *

The hundred copies came off the press grey and damp in the early morning, and she laid them out on the drying racks like fish, like laundry, like something harvested. Her back ached. Her wrists ached. She was sixty-one years old and she had pulled a hundred impressions in one night, and she would pay for it for a week, and she found she was smiling at the racks in the lamplight, at the hundred identical pages with their hundred and forty names, and she thought: *my husband would have pulled two hundred.*

Then she got practical, because defiance that is all fire and no craft is just a faster way to die.

She had planned the scattering the way she had once planned a difficult job: backward from the finished piece. The pages could not simply be thrown into the street — the wind would deliver them to the first patrol, and the army would burn them and call it done. They had to be *placed*. Placed in things that were searched rarely and thoroughly: the false bottoms of flour barrels. The lining of a church cushion. The dry well behind the tannery, wrapped in oilcloth, where things could wait a decade. Inside the covers of hymnals, where no soldier in history had ever looked, because soldiers did not believe God kept records.

She wrapped twenty pages in oilcloth for the well. Twenty went into a flour barrel she would deliver to the convent herself — a widow selling flour to the sisters was the most invisible creature in any occupied town. Twenty she sewed, that same morning, into the linings of coats at the secondhand dealer's, buying the coats outright with the last of her husband's silver and reselling them back at a loss, so that the pages would walk out of town on strangers' shoulders, distributed by chance into whatever future was coming.

And thirty she gave to people. That was the hardest part and the most necessary. She could not do it alone — a hundred pages carried by one woman was one arrest away from nothing. So she went, in the grey of that morning, to four houses. She did not explain. She did not ask for courage. She said only: *This is a page of names. If the army comes for you someday, and someone asks whether you were ever loyal to the old government, this page proves it. Keep it where you keep your important papers.* And she watched each of them take it — the baker's daughter, the schoolteacher, the old ferryman, the boy who swept the town hall — and she watched them understand, one after another, that they were being handed not a document but a duty, and each of them took it anyway, and Odile walked home through the occupied streets of Vellmere feeling something she had not felt since the trucks came.

She felt useful. She felt like the town's printer again, in the full old sense: the person whose job was to make sure that what happened, happened in more than one copy.

* * *

They came for her on the fourth day.

She had expected the third, or the fifth; occupations kept no schedule but their own. It was mid-morning, and she was in the shop setting a funeral notice for the ferryman's brother — real work, paid work, the cover of an ordinary life — when the bell over the door rang and two soldiers came in, followed by the clerk with the satchel. She knew the clerk now. Everyone in Vellmere knew the clerk. He had the face of a man who had discovered that his small talents were exactly the size of the moment, and he looked around her shop the way a man looks around a room he intends to own.

"Madame Ferrant." He did not consult a paper. He knew her name the way she knew typefaces. "The commandant requires an inventory of your business. All plates, all formes, all type. The army is consolidating the printing trade."

*Consolidating.* She set down her composing stick carefully, so as not to throw it at him. "I am a widow with one press and a drawer of jobbing type. What will the army consolidate?"

"That is not for me to say." But his eyes had already gone to the back cabinet, and Odile felt the cold move through her, slow and total, like water filling a boot.

The cabinet. The forme was in the cabinet — no. She had broken the forme down the morning after printing. The type was distributed back into the cases, one hundred and forty names dissolved into forty thousand anonymous letters, indistinguishable from every wedding notice and grain price she had ever set. That had been the craft of it, the last discipline: leave no forme, leave nothing locked, leave only letters.

But the *plates*. She had cut a stereotype plate of the two pages — one plate, lead and antimony, cast from the forme before she broke it down — because a hundred copies had seemed to her like a beginning and not an end, and a plate could print a thousand more. The plate was supposed to have gone into the well with the oilcloth bundle. She had meant to take it the night of the printing. She had meant to, and then her back had failed her on the stairs, and she had wrapped it in a rag and put it — she searched her memory with rising horror — in the flour bin. Under the flour. In the shop. Four feet from where she stood.

The clerk was walking toward the back cabinet.

Odile did not think. Later, she would try to remember thinking, and would find only the body's own knowledge, the printer's reflex: *the job is not done until the forme is distributed and the plates are stored.* The plate was not stored. Therefore the job was not done. Therefore she was still mid-job, and a printer does not stop mid-job.

"Mind the floor," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The ink never fully comes up. Your boots will carry it into the commandant's office, and he'll ask where you've been."

The clerk paused — vanity, the universal solvent — and looked down at the floor, and in that one-second pause Odile turned to the flour bin, plunged both hands through the cool white weight of it, and closed her fingers on the rag-wrapped plate.

There was no time to run. The back door opened onto the yard and the yard onto the street and the street had soldiers on it; she had watched them for four days; she knew the street. There was no time for the well, which was a hundred meters off and behind a locked gate. There was only the bin, and the plate in her hands, and the clerk turning back around.

So she did the only thing a printer could do. She opened the bin's false bottom — the bin had one; every piece of furniture Odile owned had one; it was a family affliction — and she dropped the plate in, and poured the flour back over it, and closed the lid, and turned around with white hands held up like a woman showing she was unarmed, and said, "You'll want the type cases. The lead is in the melting pot. I'll show you, if the army intends to be thorough."

The clerk looked at her flour-dusted hands. He looked at the bin. He looked at her face, and whatever he found there — the plain, tired, unremarkable face of a widow of sixty-one — was apparently not worth a second glance, because he said, "The commandant will want you to open everything yourself," and turned to the cabinet, and Odile stood in her own shop with her heart going like a press out of register, and understood that she had perhaps a day. Perhaps an hour. The army was consolidating the trade. They would come back with a wagon for the press itself, and when they moved a press they emptied everything around it, and a false bottom in a flour bin is only a secret until someone carries the bin.

They inventoried her for two hours. They took an inventory of type, of paper stock, of ink — and Odile answered every question with the flat precision of a tradeswoman, because accuracy was her armor, and the more useful she was, the less they looked at her. She showed them the melting pot and the jobbing drawer and the cabinet with its empty chases, and she watched the clerk write it all down in a satchel-sized book, and she thought: *he is writing down the death of my trade in a book smaller than a hymnal, and he thinks the book is the point.*

At the door, the younger soldier paused. He was maybe nineteen, and he had looked, the whole two hours, at the wall above the press, where a framed proof of the founding proclamation still hung — her husband's first pull, the one with the ink too heavy in the upper left corner, the flaw that made it priceless to her. The boy had read it. She had watched his lips move.

"Madame," he said, at the door, very quietly, in the accent of the north country, "why do you keep that? It's forbidden now."

Odile looked at him. She was holding the door. Beyond him the street was grey and patrolled and the trucks were parked by the town hall where the records had gone, and somewhere down that road a courier named Zephyrine — the girl with the typewriter case who had left a sealed dispatch in her cellar two nights ago, who had gone toward the checkpoints with the last of the republic in a luggage case — was either alive or dead, and Odile would probably never know which.

"Because I set it," she said. "And what a printer sets, she stands behind."

The boy looked at her a moment longer, and then he went, and the bell rang, and the shop was quiet.

* * *

She worked until dark, and then she worked by the lamp with the shutters drawn, and what she worked at was the plate.

She could not leave it in the bin. The wagon was coming — the clerk had said within the week, and clerks kept schedules the way armies kept schedules, which is to say without mercy. She could not carry it to the well; she had tried the yard door twice that day and both times there had been a patrol at the corner. She could not sew it into a coat; it weighed as much as a hymnal and had corners like a knife.

So she did the thing she should have done from the start, the thing her husband would have done without hesitating, and the knowledge of it made her laugh out loud in the empty shop, a short, hard, astonished sound.

She would print again. Tonight. One more run — not a hundred, more, as many as the paper stock allowed, three hundred if the paper held — and she would send the pages out tomorrow morning by every channel she had left, and then, when the wagon came for the press, there would be no plate to find, because a plate that has done its work is only lead, and lead goes back in the melting pot. She would melt it herself. She would melt her own name back into metal and pour it into the type cases as new sorts, and the letters that had spelled *Odile Ferrant* would go on to spell grocery bills and funeral notices and, someday, if the world turned, something free.

The work is not the plate. The work is the type. And the type is not the letters. The work is what the letters say, and what they say is *we were here, and it was true, and it was set as though it were true, and therefore it was.*

She inked the press. She pulled the first sheet of the last run and held it to the lamp, and the lamp flickered — and not from any draft. From the yard, through the shutter slats, she saw the light of lanterns, many lanterns, moving toward her gate, and heard boots, many boots, and a voice she recognized as the commandant's saying something about the printing trade and the hour of consolidation being moved up.

They had come a week early. They had come *now*.

Odile Ferrant looked at the press, at the three hundred pages still unprinted, at the plate in the bin, at the lamp, and she did the arithmetic of a lifetime in the space of a breath. She could hide the plate and lose the run. She could run the pages and lose the plate. She could do neither and be only a widow taken in the night, and the town would never know what she had almost finished.

She took off her apron, folded it over the chairback — a printer leaves her station tidy — and went to open the door before they broke it, because there was one thing left she could control, and it was the manner of it.

"Come in, gentlemen," she said to the lanterns. "Mind the ink on the floor."

And behind her, in the flour bin, under four kilos of white silence, the plate waited with her name on it — and so did the last unprinted run, and so did the cellar key, and somewhere down the dark road toward the checkpoints, a courier with a typewriter case had no way of knowing that the woman who had hidden the dispatch was about to pay the whole bill.
