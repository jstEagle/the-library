## 13. Ice and Gunfire

The river announced itself before we saw it — a long pale seam in the dark, the sound of it gone entirely. Frozen rivers make no noise, and that silence is worse than any sound. A living river talks to you all night; a dead one just waits.

We came down through the alder scrub on our bellies the last hundred meters, Ivo ahead of me, his coat dragging snow behind him like a shed skin. He'd made us wait three hours in the treeline while he watched the far bank with a pair of cracked binoculars he kept wrapped in a wool sock.

"Patrol crosses at midnight and again at four," he'd said, without turning around. "The commandant runs them like clockwork because clockwork is what he trusts. We go at two-forty. The ice will hold if we stay off the channel mark." He tapped a spot on the map he'd drawn from memory, an X in blue pencil that meant *here the water still moves beneath*. "And we do not stop. Whatever happens, we do not stop."

I had asked him then, quietly, what *whatever happens* was supposed to mean, and he'd looked at me with those bloodshot cartographer's eyes and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

Now it was two-forty by the cheap watch I'd bought in Vellmere with money I no longer remembered earning, and the moon had gone behind cloud, and the river lay below us like a road someone had poured out of milk.

Ivo checked my straps one last time. The typewriter case rode against my back under the pack frame, and beneath my coat, flat against my ribs, the ledger rode inside its oilcloth. Two books. One of them worth my life to every man on that bank, and the other one — the ledger — worth more than my life to everyone who couldn't be here to carry it themselves.

"The channel mark," Ivo whispered for the third time. "A line of darker ice, maybe a hand's width wide, running crooked down the middle. You'll feel it more than see it. Cold comes up through it. Step over, don't step on."

"And you?"

"I'm right behind you." He smiled, or something near enough to a smile in the dark. "I've crossed this river forty times on paper. Tonight I get to do it in person. Every mapmaker should be so lucky."

* * *

The first ten meters were easy. The ice sang faintly under our boots — a high glassy note, like a finger run round a wet rim — but it held. The cold came up through the soles and into the bones of my feet, and I counted my steps because counting was the only thing keeping the terror in its cage. Eleven. Twelve. The far bank was a black mass of pine, maybe two hundred meters. It might as well have been two hundred miles.

At thirty meters I felt it: the channel mark, a seam of colder air rising through a crack in the ice, thin as a hairline fracture in porcelain. I stepped over it the way Ivo had told me, one long stride, and heard him do the same behind me, the crunch of his boot landing a half-beat after mine. We were a machine, the two of us, for exactly as long as machines last.

Forty meters. Fifty. My breath came out in rags. Somewhere behind us, upstream, a dog barked once and stopped, and both of us froze mid-stride like children playing a game where the penalty for losing is a firing squad.

Nothing followed the bark. We moved again.

It was at eighty meters — I know because I was counting, I never stopped counting — that the searchlight came on.

It bloomed from the far bank downstream, a white flower opening in the dark, and swept upriver and found us the way a hand finds a coin on a table. The light hit me full in the face and erased everything: the pines, the bank, Ivo, my own hands. There was only white, and the ice under my feet, and a voice amplified through a megaphone cracking across the valley:

"*HALT. STAND WHERE YOU ARE.*"

I did not halt. I am not proud of what happened next, and I will not pretend I thought it through, because thinking had left me somewhere around meter fifty. I ran.

"Off the light line!" Ivo's voice, sharp as a blade coming out of a sheath. "Zephyrine — *diagonal*, off the light line!"

I cut left, blind, the world nothing but glare and the stuttering geometry of my own shadow thrown huge across the ice. Behind me Ivo's boots hammered a different rhythm, going the other way, and I understood — not slowly, not with any dignity, but with the animal speed of understanding — that he was making himself the louder target.

A shot cracked. Then another. The ice beside me jumped, a small dark star punched through the surface, and a thread of black water welled up out of it and began to spread.

"*HALT OR WE FIRE ON THE WOMAN—*" the megaphone began, and got the rest wrong, because I am not a woman, and there was no time to correct them, and some absurd clerkish corner of my brain noted that even now, even here, they could not get the paperwork right.

Then Ivo fired back.

He had a pistol — I'd known that, an army sidearm he'd kept through disgrace and drink and defection, cleaned every night in Odile's cellar with the tenderness other men spend on photographs of their mothers. Six rounds in it. He emptied three of them toward the searchlight in fast, disciplined pairs, the way soldiers are taught, and the light swung away from me and onto him, and in that swung moment of darkness I ran harder than I have ever run in my life.

The ice took my weight and gave it back. The crack-star I'd made was somewhere behind me now, or beside me, I couldn't tell; the whole river seemed to groan underfoot, a vast tectonic complaint, the sound of a frozen thing being asked too many questions at once. Ahead the pines swam closer, black on blacker. Behind me: Ivo's pistol, two more shots, then one, then the flat metallic click of an empty gun, and then his voice, clear across the ice, carrying the way voices do over frozen water:

"*KEEP GOING! DON'T YOU DARE LOOK BACK!*"

I looked back.

I want that recorded, wherever this record ends up. Everyone in these stories is brave and clean and never looks back. I looked back. One stride, half a second, the typewriter case swinging heavy against my spine.

Ivo stood on the ice in the searchlight with his empty pistol raised like a duelist, a small stooped figure in a bad coat, and he was not running. He was standing between the light and me, arms out, and even at that distance, even across eighty meters of killing ground, I saw him turn his head and find me in the dark — find me, the way he'd found every route and river and checkpoint on paper, precise to the last degree — and he nodded.

Just once. A cartographer confirming a position. *You are here.*

Then the light filled with muzzle flashes, and I turned, and I ran, and the shooting started in earnest.

* * *

They say terror makes time slow down. That is a lie told by people who have never been shot at. Terror makes time disappear. There was no sequence anymore, only fragments with gaps between them where whole minutes used to be:

— The treeline arriving against my chest, branches whipping my face, one cutting my cheekbone open with a bright cold line I wouldn't feel until later.

— Falling, going down hard on my knees in the snow, and the case slamming forward against my back, and my hand flying up instinctively to cover it, to shield it, my body choosing the book over my own skull without consulting me.

— Bullets in the trees above me, clipping pine, the needles raining down soft as ash.

— The river behind me, still firing. Not continuously — in bursts, in pauses, in the rhythm of men who have orders about prisoners. That rhythm was the worst part. Men who intend to kill you shoot steadily. Men who intend to take you alive shoot in bursts, and wait, and listen for you to give up.

I did not give up. I climbed. The bank rose steeply through the pines and I climbed it on hands and knees with the case strapped to me like a child, like a twin, and somewhere in the climbing the shooting behind me changed character — fewer bursts, longer silences, and then, once, a single shot with a different sound, flatter, closer to the water, and then nothing at all.

Nothing is not a sound. Nothing is the absence of every sound, and it went on for a long time.

I reached the top of the bank and lay in the snow among the roots of a fallen pine, and my body shook so violently that the branches above me shed their snow in small white avalanches, and I put my mittened hand over my own mouth because I understood, dimly, that the noise I was making was not crying and not breathing but something between the two that could get me killed.

Two hundred meters of open ice. Eighty of them under fire. Ivo had been maybe sixty meters behind me when the light found us.

Do the arithmetic. They teach you to do it whether you want to or not. Sixty meters, in the open, under a searchlight, with an empty gun. What are the chances. Name the number. Say it plainly, the way Ivo would have said it, plotting a route with no good options: *the probability is low. The probability is very low.*

But not zero. Nothing is ever zero. That is the cruelty of arithmetic — it never lets you close the account. It leaves the figure open, a wound in the page, and you bleed into it all night.

* * *

I don't know how long I lay under the fallen pine. An hour. Three. Long enough for the sweat to freeze in my collar and turn my shirt into a suit of thin armor. The searchlight on the far bank swept the river twice more, methodical, patient, and then died, and the patrol's voices carried faintly across the valley — orders, boots, the creak of a truck. Once I heard a man laugh. I will hear that laugh for the rest of my life, whatever length of life I am granted, and I will never know what was funny.

When the far bank had been dark and quiet for what felt like a season, I moved. Not toward anything — Ivo's map was burned into me, the route past the third checkpoint drawn in blue pencil lines I could trace with my eyes shut — but away, away from the river, deeper into the pines, following the contour of the land the way water follows it downhill. Ivo had drilled it into me in Odile's cellar, tapping the map: *"If it goes wrong, don't think. Follow the terrain. Terrain doesn't lie. People lie, maps can lie, terrain never lies."*

So I followed the terrain, and the terrain took me down a frozen streambed and along a deer trail and through a stand of birches so dense the moon, when it finally came out, fell through them in coins.

At some point I stopped shaking. At some point I noticed I was bleeding from the cheek and licked the cut and tasted iron and salt and kept walking. At some point — this is the part I keep returning to, the part I cannot file anywhere — I realized I was still counting. Steps. I had never stopped. Four thousand and something. Ivo's voice in my head saying *we do not stop*, and my feet obeying a dead man's order, or a living man's, I didn't know which, and the not-knowing riding on my shoulders heavier than the case.

Near dawn I found the ruined mill Ivo had marked — a collapsed gristmill in a fold of the hills, its wheel long since rotted to a black skeleton, its roof half-open to the sky like a broken ribcage. He'd called it a fallback point. Fallback points are places you plan to arrive at together.

I went in. I built no fire. I sat down against the stone wall with the typewriter case in my lap and my arms around it, and I waited for my hands to stop trembling enough to work the buckles.

They never fully did. But they worked.

* * *

The oilcloth came away from the ledger with a sound like a held breath released. In the grey pre-dawn light that fell through the broken roof, I opened it for the first time.

I had carried it for months sewn into coat linings and hidden under floorboards and strapped against my body through checkpoints and rivers, and I had never once been permitted to read it. Courier's discipline. *You carry the cargo; you don't open the crates.* Anselm's rule, passed down the chain of hands that had passed the book to me.

Anselm, who was executed at the second checkpoint with his half of the cargo burned into ash. Anselm, whose ghost had walked beside me since the day I learned his name.

I turned the first page.

Names. Columns of names, in a clerk's careful hand — dates, towns, acts. *Gave shelter. Passed letters. Printed notices. Hid a deserter. Paid a fine that saved a family.* Page after page after page, the quiet heroism of ordinary people recorded in the dry language of evidence, each entry a life that had bent toward the republic when bending cost something. I read until the light strengthened. I read names from towns I'd passed through without knowing what slept beneath their floors. And there, deep in the middle of the book, in entries dated within the last year:

*Ferrant, Odile — printer, Vellmere. Printed and distributed prohibited documents.*

*Brandt, Ivo — cartographer, formerly of the Army Survey Office. Provided maps and routes to persons assisting evasion of military authority.*

And then, further down the same page, in fresher ink, an entry added recently, the handwriting slightly different:

*Courier unknown — name withheld. Carries diplomatic codes and this ledger toward the Halven Consulate. Status: active.*

My own name, withheld. My own life, entered in the book I was carrying, described in the third person by someone who believed in what I was doing enough to write me into the record of it.

I closed the ledger and pressed it flat against my chest with both arms, the way you hold a thing you are afraid will be taken, and outside the broken roof the sky went from grey to the color of old pearl, and the morning came up cold and clean over the hills, and somewhere south of me, across a frozen river, a man was either dead or alive and I could not go back to learn which.

The arithmetic said one thing. The book in my arms said another. Both of them were right.

I repacked the case. I tightened the straps. Two days' walk remained to the Halven Consulate, and the third checkpoint waited at its very gate, staffed by officers hunting a courier who was, at this moment, sitting in a ruin reading her own name in a book of the condemned.

*Active,* the ledger said. *Status: active.*

I stood up, and I started walking.
