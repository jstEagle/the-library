## 14. Alone with the Case

The ruin I had fled to was no place to stay. Its walls were open to the road on two sides, and the road was where the patrol would come from, sooner or later, asking its questions with rifles. So I walked north through the failing light until the trees thickened and the ground dropped away toward water I could hear before I could see — a river running under ice, muttering to itself like an old man who had been left out of every important conversation.

The mill stood above the race on the far side of a collapsed footbridge. I crossed on the fallen beams, arms out, the typewriter case swinging its small dead weight against my hip, and let myself into what remained of the grinding floor.

Whoever had milled here had left in a hurry or been made to leave; sacks split open years ago still slumped against the wall, their contents long since eaten by mice and weather, so that the floor lay under a grey drift of flour-turned-to-stone. The wheel was gone but the shaft remained, and the great iron gearing sat half-sunk in the dark like the ribcage of something that had once moved. There was one corner, behind the gearing, where the roof still held and the wind could not find me. I put my back against the cold stone and my hands on the case, and I did not open it right away.

I listened instead. That is a courier's habit — you learn to spend the first minutes of any room listening for what is wrong with it. Water under ice. Wind in the broken louvres. A shutter somewhere tapping its nail like a slow telegraph. Nothing else. No dogs. No boots. The army did not know yet where I was, which meant the army did not know yet where I was not, either, and both of those facts were worth more to me tonight than fire.

There would be no fire. I got out the codes instead — the real ones, sewn into my coat lining, the diplomatic ciphers I had carried so long they had worn a smooth path against my ribs — and I checked them by touch in the dark, the way you check the pulse of someone sleeping. Then, because there was nothing left between me and morning except myself, I opened the typewriter case.

* * *

The machine sat in its bed of grey felt, keys dull under the grit of the road, exactly the honest, useless typewriter it appeared to be. I lifted it out. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, bound in the plain brown boards of an inventory book, lay Anselm's ledger — the ledger, the one he had died for at the second checkpoint, the one the smuggler had sworn had burned with him, because a lie told to a stranger is sometimes the last kindness available.

It had not burned. It had been carried, by a man who drew fire on a frozen river so that I could run with it, and now it was here, in a ruined mill, in my hands, and Ivo Brandt was somewhere behind me on the ice with his fate unwritten.

I untied the oilcloth the way you unwrap something that might be wounded.

The first pages were columns. Names, dates of arrest, dates of transfer, disposition. A clerk's hand, neat and unimpassioned, the handwriting of someone paid by the page. I understood within a few lines what I was holding: not the town ledger the army had seized at Vellmere, but its twin, its shadow — the regime's own accounting of everyone who had served it, sheltered it, printed for it, spied for it, believed in it. A list of assets. And against each name, in the final column, a word like a verdict handed down before any trial:

*Retired. Retired. Compromised. Liquidated. Active.*

Anselm had not stolen a record of heroes. He had stolen the machinery's own parts list, and the machinery wanted its parts back.

I turned pages. The candle I did not have would have shown me nothing worse than the dark showed me already; I read by the grey light through the louvres, and when that failed I read by feel, tracing the ink with a fingertip like a blind woman taking dictation from the dead.

I found Odile Ferrant two-thirds through, under *Printers and Allied Trades*. *Ferrant, O., Vellmere. Set type, founding documents, year one. Status:* — and the clerk's pen had hesitated there, or so I imagined, because the word written over the original entry was newer, wetter, in a different hand: *Active.* She had been active the way a lamp is active, the way a press is active, quietly, in her cellar, for twenty years. Somewhere in Vellmere tonight her cellar held my dispatch, and Odile herself held whatever the soldiers decided to hold her for.

I found Ivo Brandt under *Survey Corps, seconded*. *Brandt, I., Captain (reduced), cartographer. Drew checkpoint surveys, sectors one through three. Defected [date]. Status: Liquidated — pending.* Pending. The clerk had not caught up with him yet. On the frozen river, in the dark, with patrol lanterns swinging across the ice, Ivo Brandt was still officially a matter pending, and I sat in the flour-dust of a dead mill and understood that this was the most anyone I loved could hope for tonight: to be pending.

Then I found my own name.

*Coldharbour, Z. Courier, diplomatic service. Inherited route from Coldharbour, M. (see deceased). Carries ciphers and archive materials. Status: Active.*

My mother's name was in the book too, cross-referenced, marked *deceased*, and beneath it, in smaller script, a note I had never been allowed to read because I had never been allowed to read anything: *Coldharbour, M. — executed en route. Cargo recovered in part. Recommend surveillance of issue.*

Issue. That was me. Twenty-two years of measuring my life in portable things — codes sewn into linings, documents folded to the width of a cigarette, a whole self packed down small enough to cross borders unnoticed — and here was the explanation, in a stranger's handwriting, older than my grief: I had been watched since before I could walk. My invisibility had never been mine. It had been issued to me, along with the coat, along with the route, along with the silence everyone in Vellmere mistook for modesty. The republic I had served without ever seeing had served itself by making me a corridor, and corridors are not supposed to read what passes through them.

I closed the ledger. I sat with my hands flat on its cover, feeling it breathe, if books can be said to breathe, and outside the wind worked at the louvres and the river muttered under its lid of ice, and somewhere south of me a man might have been dying for this book for the second time in a week.

I opened it again.

Because that was the thing, the thing I could not stop doing, the thing I would do all night whether I chose to or not: I knew these names. Not all of them — but enough. Here was the schoolmaster at Brenne Cross who had given me soup once, in a kitchen that smelled of chalk, and asked no questions, and I had thought him merely kind. *Compromised.* Here was the ferryman who took no money in winter. *Liquidated.* Here, in a column of innkeepers, the woman at the Halfway House whose beds I had slept in four times over six years, whose hands smelled of lye, who had never once looked at my case longer than politeness required. *Retired.* Retired. As if she had been permitted to resign. As if the word were a mercy instead of a euphemism with teeth.

Every name was a room I had stood in. Every disposition was a door closing behind me, months or years after I had passed through it believing myself unseen. I had built my whole life on the arithmetic of smallness — carry little, want little, be seen by no one — and the ledger laid that arithmetic open on its page and showed me the sum: I had never been invisible. I had been tolerated. And tolerance, in the economy this book kept, was simply a slower line in the same column.

Somewhere past the middle hours of the night, I stopped reading forward and began reading differently — reading the way Odile must have set type, letter by letter, giving each name its own body of ink. I said them. Not aloud, not quite; I said them in the place behind my teeth where you keep prayers you don't believe in. The schoolmaster. The ferrywoman. The woman at the Halfway House. Odile Ferrant, active. Ivo Brandt, pending. Anselm — I found him too, near the front, entered in a hurried hand, disposition already filled in, dated three weeks past. They had recorded his execution in the same book he died carrying, filed his death inside the very cargo they killed him for, and somehow that — the bureaucratic tidiness of it, the way his name sat in their neat columns as though he had merely changed departments — somehow that was the cruellest page of all, and I pressed my thumb flat over it until the ink threatened to smear, and then I stopped, because smearing was theirs. Erasure was theirs. Everything I had, everything this night had left me, consisted of not doing what they did.

* * *

Towards dawn I heard the ice on the river crack once, far off, a sound like a rifle's cousin, and I was on my feet with the case strapped before my mind had finished deciding. But it was only the river, talking in its sleep. Only the river. I stood in the grey half-light among the ruined gearing and made myself think the sentence through, because terror is a currency and I refused to keep spending it on sounds.

Only the river. Which meant not yet the soldiers. Which meant Ivo might still be — pending. Might still be lying flat somewhere on the south bank with a bullet through him, or swimming, or walking north along the towpath with his collar up and his maps in his head, that precise drunk mind plotting routes even now, even bleeding. I had heard shots. I had counted them — three, spaced, deliberate, the rhythm of aimed fire rather than a volley into darkness — and some stubborn clerk in my own chest kept filing that fact under evidence. Three aimed shots miss more often than they hit. Ivo would have appreciated the statistics. Ivo, who priced his treason like a tradesman and drank like a man paying off a debt, had drawn a patrol off me with the calm of someone laying a ruler along a border, and I would not — I could not — write him into the ledger's column while the column itself said *pending*.

So I gave him the dawn. I sat back down and I did the work that was mine to do, which was this: I memorized.

If the case were taken from me tomorrow at the consulate gate — if the third checkpoint did what checkpoints do — then the book would die in a clerk's satchel like the town ledger of Vellmere, and the only copy left in the world would be whatever lived behind my eyes. So I read the pages again, slowly, and this time I carried them inward. Names in rows of ten. Initials. Towns. Dispositions. I built the columns in my head the way Ivo built maps, hatched and bordered, and whenever my attention slid off the page toward the river, toward the south, toward the sound that was only ever the ice, I dragged it back and set another row of names into memory like type into a chase, locked tight, ready to print.

By the time the light through the louvres turned from grey to the colour of dishwater, I knew the book. Not perfectly — a courier knows better than to promise perfection — but well enough that burning it would cost them a fire and cost me nothing I could not rebuild, name by name, on any paper in the world, on the consulate's typewriter if it came to that. Odile would have understood that. Odile, who scattered a hundred copies across the countryside before they came for her, who had turned a cellar into a mint of witness. They could burn the original. They could burn the plates. They could not burn the setting.

I ate the heel of bread from my pack, drank snow-melt from the race, and repacked the case: typewriter on top, ledger beneath, oilcloth bound tight, straps drawn hard. The codes stayed where they lived, against my ribs. My inheritance, all of it portable, all of it carried by hands before mine — my mother's route, Anselm's book, Ivo's map folded in my inner pocket, Odile's ink drying on forged passes that had gotten us through the first checkpoint and would have to get me past the third.

At the broken footbridge I paused, the way you pause at a door you're not sure you'll see again from this side. Behind me the mill held its corner of dark and dust and the shape of where I had sat all night, and ahead of me the road ran north through bare trees toward the Halven Consulate and the gate where officers were hunting a courier with a typewriter case.

Two days' walk. One checkpoint. One book of the condemned, riding under the keys of a machine that had never written anything but letters home.

I settled the strap on my shoulder, took the bank path north, and walked — and did not look back at the river, or the ice, or the south, where a man's status was still, against all sense, against all arithmetic, *pending*.
