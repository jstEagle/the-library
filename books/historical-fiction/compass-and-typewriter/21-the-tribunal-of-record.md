## 21. The Tribunal of Record

They gave me three days to prepare my testimony, and in those three days I learned that a consulate prepares for justice the way a family prepares for a funeral: with linens, seating charts, and quiet arguments about who sits where.

The hearing room had once been a ballroom — the Halven Consulate was built in the years when republics still believed in themselves, and its proportions said so. Now the parquet floor was scuffed by crates of evacuated files, and the crystal chandeliers had been dimmed to discourage attention from aircraft above. A long table had been set at the north end for the tribunal of record: five observers from five neutral nations, each with their own flag on a small stand and their own reasons for being present that they would not admit aloud. Below them, two chairs for testimony. To one side, a bench for the army's delegation — six officers in new wool, arriving under a flag that six months ago had been an insult to say.

I sat with my back to the tall windows because Consul Verhoeven insisted the light would be better there, and I understood what he did not say: they wanted my face lit for the record, but they also wanted no sniper's angle on me from across the square. A courier learns to read rooms for exits. This one had four, and I counted them while clerks arranged water carafes and papers with the nervous precision of people who knew that whatever happened here could not be undone.

The ledger sat on the witness table between us and the army delegation like a guest neither side wished to acknowledge. It had been rebound in gray archive cloth, stamped with the consulate's seal, catalogued as *Deposit 114*. A dead book made safe. Except it wasn't dead. Every name inside it was still breathing somewhere, or had stopped breathing recently enough that breath was the whole argument.

Verhoeven came to me before the session opened. He looked as though he had slept in his collar, which he probably had.

"The army delegation will challenge the document's provenance," he said. "They'll say the ledger is forged. They'll say you are a fiction. They may be polite about it, or they may not. There is no third version."

"I know."

"They will also ask who else handled the book. The chain of custody. They want names." He paused. "Give them none that aren't already gone."

I thought of Odile's hands setting type in the dark of her cellar. Of Ivo's maps drawn in pencil that could be erased, because he understood better than any of us that everything could be erased. "Most of them already are," I said.

* * *

The tribunal convened at ten o'clock, and for the first hour it was all procedure, which is how institutions bury courage when they're frightened of it. The observers read their mandates into the record. The senior observer, a silver-browed woman from a country whose name I had only ever seen on shipping manifests, stated plainly that this tribunal had power to record and recommend and nothing more — no arrests, no restitution, no verdicts. Justice, she might have said, in its most diluted form: the truth spoken into a stenographic machine before witnesses who were obligated only to listen.

But listening, I would learn that day, is not nothing.

When I took the chair, the army delegation's counsel — a civilian lawyer with a careful face, the kind of man regimes keep for exactly this weather — began within a minute.

"You state that you carried diplomatic codes for the former government of Vellmere province."

"I state that I carried papers I was given, and I carried them where I was told."

"And among those papers, a ledger." He gestured without touching it, as though it might stain him. "A handwritten register of names. Can you authenticate this document?"

"I can tell you where it came from. Whether it is authentic is what your own requisition clerk proved when he sealed it in his satchel at dawn with the rest of the town records."

Murmurs from the observers' table. The counsel adjusted his cuffs.

"Then let us speak of provenance. You will confirm the individuals through whose hands this document passed en route to this consulate."

And there it was, earlier than I expected. The question that was itself an arrest warrant waiting for ink.

I had rehearsed this. I had rehearsed it walking the consulate grounds at night, saying the names to the dark, testing each one like a coin bitten to see if it was real. And I had discovered, in that rehearsal, that I could not do what Verhoeven asked. Not because I was afraid. Because silence was the thing we had all been fighting.

"I can confirm them," I said. "All of them."

"Zephyrine—" Verhoeven, low, from behind me.

"The previous courier was named Anselm Vey. He was executed at the second checkpoint on the river road in the first week of winter, and the ledger he carried was burned with his body, which is why a second copy existed, which is why I was sent. The second copy was hidden by Odile Ferrant, printer, of Vellmere, who set type for the founding documents of the old republic and who printed one hundred copies of these names before she was arrested. She was executed in Vellmere market square. Her printed pages surfaced in three towns afterward and are circulating still — possibly in your own garrison, Counselor; your clerks would know. The route past the checkpoints was plotted by Ivo Brandt, formerly cartographer of the Third Survey Corps, who drew the checkpoint maps you are now using and who drew a different map for me. He drew fire on the river ice so that I could cross carrying this book. His body was never found, and I am glad to report, as of yesterday, that there is a reason for that."

The room had gone very still. Even the stenographer's hands had slowed.

I put my hand flat on the gray cloth cover of the ledger.

"There are four hundred and twelve names in this book. I have read every one. I did not read them when I carried them — I believed then that my task was delivery, and delivery requires only obedience. But obedience is what your machinery runs on, Counselor, and I have seen where it goes. So now I will read the names aloud, and you may object to each one if you like, and the objection will be entered in the record beside the name, and both will outlive everyone in this room."

* * *

I began at nine minutes past eleven.

The first forty names went easily, because they were strangers to me: farmers, ferrymen, a schoolteacher from Dossenval, a woman listed only as *the widow at Kiln Bridge*. My voice held. The army counsel objected to the fourth name, then the ninth, then stopped objecting because each objection was being recorded directly beneath the name it tried to erase, and I watched him understand, with visible discomfort, that his objections were becoming part of the evidence.

At name sixty-one I faltered, because it was Odile Ferrant's, and I saw her cellar again — the smell of ink and coal, her hands steady on the press while the trucks rolled into Vellmere, her saying, without being asked, *the books go on living after the men who print them, that's the whole trick of the trade.* I drank water. The silver-browed observer said quietly, "Continue," and something in the way she said it told me she understood exactly what this cost, and exactly why it had to cost it.

At one hundred and thirty I heard a sound from the clerks' bench — the small scrape of a chair, deliberately muffled.

I kept reading. Two hundred. Two hundred and fifty. The army delegation grew restless; one officer left, then another, and the counsel consulted in whispers with the colonel who remained. Outside, snow had begun again, soft against the tall windows, erasing the square the way this regime erased towns — gently, thoroughly, believing no one wrote things down.

Three hundred and seven was Ivo Brandt. I read his rank, his defection, his maps, the ice, the gunfire I had heard behind me while I ran. My voice cracked on the word *gunfire*, and I let it crack. Let the record hold the crack. A clean voice would have lied about him.

Four hundred and twelve was my own name. *Zephyrine Coldharbour, courier, Vellmere.* I read myself into the book I had carried, and closed it.

Then, from the clerks' bench at the back of the room, a voice said: "The checkpoint ledgers corroborate."

Every head turned.

He stood among the consular clerks in a borrowed gray coat, spectacles he didn't need, a clerk's green visor pushed up on his forehead — and underneath the disguise, unmistakable to anyone who had ever watched him bend over a map, Ivo Brandt, alive, thinner, a scar along his jaw that had not been there on the river ice. The remaining army officers came to their feet at once, and the colonel's voice cut the air: "That man is a deserter of the Third Corps. This consulate is harboring—"

"This consulate," the silver-browed observer said, with the mildness of a glacier, "is recording. Sit down, Colonel, or leave."

The colonel looked at his counsel. The counsel looked at the stenographer's machine, at the flags, at the five observers who would carry today's transcript to five capitals. Then he stood, gathered his papers with terrible composure, and said, "My government does not recognize the jurisdiction of this proceeding," and marched for the doors, the last officer trailing him like the tail of a kite going down.

The doors slammed. Snow fell. And Ivo Brandt walked forward through the sudden hush and laid three folded sheets of paper on the witness table beside the ledger.

"The commandant at the river crossing keeps his own list," he said, to the tribunal, in the flat precise voice of a man reading coordinates. "Not for justice — for leverage. These are copies I made before I defected. Names taken at the first, second, and third checkpoints. Dates. Dispositions." He paused, and for one moment his eyes found mine, and what passed between us was not apology — he had told me his price and paid it in full on the ice — but recognition, the way two survivors of the same wreck recognize each other on shore. "Dispositions," he repeated. "That is the word the army uses. I trained some of the men who wrote these entries. I taught them to draw the rivers. I can name the hands behind every line."

"Your name appears in the deposit ledger," the silver-browed observer said. "Entry three hundred and seven. Do you corroborate the circumstances of its inclusion?"

"I corroborate them," Ivo said. "I plotted the route knowing what it would cost. I consider the fee settled."

He sat down beside me, in the second witness chair, and his hand was steady on the table, and only the pulse in his throat said otherwise.

* * *

The session ran until dark. Transcripts were certified. The observers signed page after page, and the stenographic machine swallowed everything — the names, the objections, the slammed door, the desertion of the army delegation entered into the record as its own kind of testimony. Imperfect, all of it. No arrests would follow from today, no restitution, perhaps not even acknowledgment for years. Five governments would file five transcripts and act on them slowly, or partially, or cynically. Justice outside the law is a seed, not a building, and seeds do not keep you warm.

But when the room emptied, the silver-browed observer stopped at our chairs.

"For the record," she said, "I have sat on four tribunals in twelve years. This is the first at which every name was read to the end." She looked at the ledger, then at me. "It matters more than you think. Records have long memories and longer reach. Regimes fall faster than archives burn."

After she left, Ivo and I sat alone in the ballroom among the stacked crates and dimmed chandeliers, two people the book had carried as much as they had carried it.

"You hid among the clerks for three days," I said. "In this building. While I walked the grounds deciding whether to testify."

"You decided. I only survived. Different skills." He took off the useless spectacles and folded them. "I reached the far bank. Barely. A miller's family pulled me out of the reeds and kept me through the worst of it. By the time I could walk, Odile's pages were everywhere, and the army was hunting a ghost with my description. Coming here seemed the only place a ghost could become a man again — in front of witnesses."

"Odile is dead."

"Yes." He looked at the ledger. "And in that book, and in a hundred printed copies, and now in five national archives, she isn't. That is the entire art, Zephyrine. Everything else is just carrying."

Outside, the snow had stopped. Through the tall windows the consulate square lay white and empty, and beyond it the road south toward Vellmere, and beyond that the spring, which was coming whether anyone testified or not.

Verhoeven appeared in the doorway, and his face had the particular weariness of a man about to complicate a victory.

"Coldharbour," he said. "There is a refugee at the service gate. Arrived tonight from the river country. She says she has news — and a packet — and she is asking for the courier by name."

I stood. The ledger lay closed on the table between us, four hundred and twelve names strong, and I found I was already measuring the packet's weight before I had even seen it, the way I measured everything now, the way I always had: in what can be carried.
