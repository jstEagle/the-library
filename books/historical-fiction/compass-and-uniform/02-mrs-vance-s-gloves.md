## 2. Mrs. Vance's Gloves

The car started badly on October mornings, as if it too felt the season closing in. Mara turned the crank until his shoulder ached, and when the engine finally caught he stood a moment in the exhaust-warm air and let the sound of it fill the street. The Hupmobile sat outside the Vance house like something borrowed from another century — which, in a town where the bridge closed every winter and the ferry ran on the moods of the river, it very nearly was.

He wiped his hands on the rag he kept folded in his breast pocket, checked his watch against the church bell, and went up to the door.

Mrs. Vance was never late. She came down the steps with her coat already buttoned, her hat pinned at the exact angle that said *I am a serious woman and you will not enjoy disagreeing with me*, and her gloves going on one finger at a time, in the order she did everything: deliberately, completely, without waste.

"Mill district first," she said. "Then the hall by eleven."

"Yes, ma'am."

He opened the door for her and she took his arm to step up — a formality they had never once discussed and never once skipped — and settled herself on the seat with her satchel of notes on her lap. Mara went around to the driver's side and let himself into the front, where he belonged, where no one looked, and pulled the lever that put them into motion.

The fog had come off the river overnight and lay in the streets like something poured. It thinned as they climbed away from the water and thickened again as they descended into the mill quarter, and all the while Edith Vance talked above him through the little folding glass, rehearsing.

"'You are told that the vote is a responsibility too grave for women,'" she said, in the voice she used for halls and not for cars. "'I ask you: who among the men of this town exercised great responsibility when they voted themselves shorter hours for their own clubs and longer hours for your own wives?'"

A pause. The rustle of papers.

"No. Too clever. They'll hear contempt." Papers again. "'You are told—'"

Mara drove. He knew this speech already, or most of it; he knew most of her speeches already, having heard them assembled piece by piece from the passenger seat over ten years, the way a man assembling a quilt comes to know every square. He could have recited the opening of the 1909 address at the courthouse steps. He sometimes did, quietly, when alone in the garage, just to feel how the sentences were built — not out of vanity, but because it seemed important that somebody who wasn't paid to admire her should know exactly how good she was.

The mill streets received the car the way they always did: with stillness.

It was not hostility, precisely. It was colder than hostility, more patient. Men coming off night shift stood at the corner by the Crane Number Two gate with their lunch pails hanging from their hands, and watched the Hupmobile pass without moving their heads, only their eyes. Women at the wash lines paused with wet sheets half-lifted. Children did not wave. In the better streets across town, children waved at motorcars the way children do, palms open, delighted. Here the children learned early what a motorcar cost and who paid for it, and so they simply watched, and their watching had weight.

"'"—and I say to the mill owners of this town, in the words of the prophet: you have weighed your scales and found them wanting."'"

*Wanting.* The word hung back there in the fog behind the glass.

Mara slowed for a cart horse being led out of the tannery lane, and in the slowing he caught the reflection in the shop window they were passing: the car, and beyond it, faintly, Mrs. Vance's hat, and beyond both, printed backwards in silvered glass, two mill men standing outside the Company store, watching. One of them said something to the other. He couldn't hear it. He didn't need to. There were only so many things a man could say about a lady in a fine coat in a fine car telling her fine friends that the mills were wicked.

He wondered — not for the first time, but with a new discomfort, the way you might notice a stone in your shoe after miles of walking — whether she heard those unspoken remarks too. Whether she chose not to.

"'Gloves,'" Edith said suddenly, mid-sentence, to no one. "Mara. Do I have my gloves?"

He glanced back. They lay in her lap, where she had been worrying them under the papers.

"You're wearing them, ma'am."

"Am I." She looked down as if confirming a stranger's report. Then, dryly: "Ten years and you've never once let me leave them in a café. What would I do without you?"

"The speeches would go on, ma'am."

"Don't be modest, it doesn't suit you." More paper. "'You are told—'"

She wore grey kid gloves, stitched finely, kept clean by a woman whose hands did not go near the machines that made them. Mara knew the price of gloves now in a way he hadn't before this job. He knew what a pair cost against a week's wage at the Number Two, because he drove past the Number Two twice a week and had learned, without meaning to, to do sums. That was the trouble with driving someone everywhere for ten years. You accumulated arithmetic you never asked for.

* * *

They stopped at the general post office on Cutter Street because there was always mail, and because Mrs. Vance liked to walk the last stretch to the hall — *they should see me arrive on foot, Mara, it's a whole sentence in itself* — and so he left the car idling at the curb and went in for the bundle.

There were eleven letters. He carried them out on his palm, squared, and handed them across to her, and she sorted through them standing right there on the sidewalk, because she read everything immediately, always, as if the world might have changed its mind since morning.

"Bills. Bills. The Hall committee — finally. This is from—" She stopped. Her thumb stayed on the envelope a moment longer than it needed to.

Mara looked down the street, at nothing.

"This is from the city," she said, which meant nothing and covered everything. She slid it into her coat pocket rather than the satchel. "Well. Come along at three, then, and we'll want the long way round by the north road."

"Yes, ma'am."

But she did not move toward the hall at once. She stood there holding eleven letters and looking at the fog rolling off the river two streets over, and something crossed her face that Mara had seen perhaps four times in ten years — a flicker, quickly mastered, of a woman who was tired all the way down. Then it was gone, filed away somewhere behind the hat pin, and she was Mrs. Vance again, upright, certain, a monument in motion.

"Three o'clock," she repeated. "And Mara — if anyone asks where I was this morning, I was with you. All morning. Which is true, so it costs nothing to say."

"Yes, ma'am."

She went up the street on foot, and the people on the sidewalk parted around her the way water parts around a prow, some nodding, some not, and Mara got back in the car and sat with his hands on the wheel and did not drive anywhere for a full minute.

Because he had seen the envelope. He saw every envelope; it was his profession, though his profession was officially *driving*. Cream stock, heavy, city postmark, and the hand that had addressed it — he'd carried enough of them — belonged to a law office, not a friend. And he had seen her face when she touched it, and her face had not said *money*. Money she handled briskly, bills especially, with the cheerful ruthlessness of any working organizer. Her face had said something more like *again*, or *already*, or *how long can this continue*.

He should not speculate. That was the rule he had made for himself in the first year, when he'd realized the letters on his lap were not parcels but lives: *read everything, understand what must be understood, repeat nothing, wonder as little as possible.* Wondering led to opinions. Opinions led to hesitations. And a courier who hesitated was worse than useless; he was dangerous.

He put the car in gear and drove the loop to be visibly *with her, all morning*, and told himself the cold feeling in his stomach was only hunger.

* * *

At three o'clock she came out of the hall early, which she never did, and her mouth was set in the line that meant a decision had been made rather than avoided, and she gave him an address he did not expect: the Commercial House, where traveling men stayed and where, therefore, nobody local would be seen.

Inside, while she was gone — forty minutes, by his watch, sitting in the car in a street where the lamps came on early — he did not open her satchel, because it remained in her hands. But when she returned she was carrying the cream envelope openly, and in the elevator-less lobby doorway she stopped and said, "Read me back tomorrow. Nine o'clock, the depot road, then the Harrow farm. And Mara."

He waited.

"Tonight there'll be a letter on the hall table ready to go to Miss Brandt in the east. It's sealed. See it onto the evening boat, will you? It matters."

"I'll see it myself, ma'am."

"Good." She looked at the envelope in her gloved hand, turning it once. Then she said, in a voice lighter than her eyes, "Anonymous generosity. The most restful kind, don't you find? No gratitude required. No questions asked."

"Very restful, ma'am."

"Mm." She almost smiled. "That's what I thought, once."

She got in, closed the door, and did not speak again until the mill streets slid past the windows, and the watchers came out of their doorways as they always did, silent, patient, keeping their own accounts. Mara watched them in the mirror and thought: *she takes their money's money and drives through their streets telling them the scales are wanting, and she knows, and I know she knows, and neither of us has ever said the word aloud.*

Except he didn't know. Not truly. He had suspicions shaped like knowledge, which is a different animal and much harder to house.

That evening, the letter to Miss Brandt sat on the hall table where she'd promised, sealed, weighted under the letter knife. Mara picked it up. It was thin — one page. He turned it over once in his hands under the hall lamp.

In the beginning, he had read the letters out of necessity disguised as duty: a courier who cannot vouch for a packet is a fool, and he refused to be a fool. Then it had become ritual, then craft, then simply the shape of his nights — steam over the kettle, the letter knife, the lamp turned low, the reading, the resealing so carefully that even the fold remembered its crease. Ten years of other people's words passing through his hands like water through a lock. He knew which correspondents were owed flattery and which were owed truth. He knew which silences protected which people, and he kept a ledger of them in his head more careful than any account book: *this silence shields the Hall committee; this one spares her sister; this one keeps old Mr. Pruen from learning his son marched.*

He had never yet found a silence that protected himself. He supposed that was what made him good at this. A man with nothing visible to lose makes an excellent keeper of secrets.

He held the thin letter to Miss Brandt over the kettle's breath and worked the flap loose, and read:

*Miss Brandt — Your proposal reaches me at a busy hour, so I will be brief. Yes: come for the winter session. The workshop needs new hands and the movement needs new blood, though between ourselves it mostly needs both at once. Bring whatever you have assembled regarding our finances — I would rather see it here, in my own house, than have it circulating elsewhere. We will discuss what to do with it when you arrive.*

*You ask whether the rumors about our funding trouble me. Everything troubles me. That is the condition of the work. Come before the bridge closes — after that, the river decides who belongs to us.*

*E.V.*

Mara stood a long time in the small kitchen with the letter open and the kettle ticking as it cooled.

*Regarding our finances.* *What to do with it.* *The rumors.*

He had spent ten years perfecting the art of not wondering, and here was a twenty-year-old stranger's name on an envelope, arriving before the bridge closed, carrying papers about money — and Edith Vance wanted those papers brought *inside her own walls*, which was either honesty or containment, and he found he could not tell which from where he stood, and that frightened him more than any single secret he had ever kept.

Because if it was containment — if the polish and the gloves and the prophet's words about scales were all built over a hole in the ground filled with somebody's tainted money — then every mile he had driven her, every seal he had smoothed flat, every silence he had catalogued so lovingly in his head, had been a stone laid carefully on top of that hole. Not hiding it. Keeping the grass trimmed over it.

He resealed the letter with the cake of wax he kept for the purpose, pressed his ring into it evenly, and set out for the evening boat through streets gone blue with dusk. The fog was coming up off the river again, swallowing the lamplight one by one. Somewhere east, beyond the ice that would soon close the crossing, a girl named Tilly Brandt was packing a satchel full of answers.

And Mara Okafor, who knew everything and admitted nothing, walked beside the black water carrying one thin letter, and for the first time in ten years he was afraid of what he carried — not because of what it said, but because of what it was going to make him look at.
