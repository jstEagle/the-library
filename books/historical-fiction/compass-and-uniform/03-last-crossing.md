## 3. Last Crossing

The bridge closed on the first of December, or it was supposed to. This year the river had other ideas.

Mara felt it before he saw it — a change in the quality of the cold that came off the water, something mineral and final in it, the way iron smells before snow. He was loading the motorcar at half past six in the dark, wrapping Mrs. Vance's correspondence in oilcloth against the damp, when old Hobb called up from the landing with his lantern swinging.

"She's going out early," Hobb shouted across the yard, jerking his chin toward the river. "Ice is forming at the narrows already. Bridge'll shut by noon if the committee has any sense, which they mostly don't."

"By noon," Mara repeated, because Hobb expected an answer, and because repeating things back to people had been, for ten years, the safest way to speak.

The bridge — the only bridge for forty miles either direction — was an iron lattice affair, black with coal smoke, that crossed the Verren where the river widened and slowed. Every winter the town closed it: the planking iced over, the expansion joints seized, and once, before Mara's time, a cart horse had gone through a rotten span with the cart still hitched. So each autumn the committee bolted the gates, and each spring they opened them again, and between those two days the town of Verrenholm became an island. Mail went by ferry until the ice road formed, then by foot across the ice itself, and even that was a gamble the postmaster refused to take after mid-January. Whatever you had not said by the time the river locked, you would not say until thaw.

Mara had lived through nine closed seasons. He had never minded them before. It occurred to him, buttoning his driving gloves in the grey light, that this might be because he had nothing anyone could take from him by keeping him here.

* * *

Mrs. Vance came down at seven sharp, as she always did on a last-crossing day, dressed in the dark green wool that made her look, from a distance, like something official — a magistrate, a school inspector, a woman whom obstacles ought to apologize to. She carried her writing case under one arm and a list of errands in the other hand, and she handed him the list without greeting, which was not rudeness but economy; Edith Vance did not waste words on mornings, she saved them all for afternoons.

"The bank first, Mara, then the chemist, then the letters must be on the noon boat if there is a noon boat, and if there isn't—" She paused, looking past him at the river, where the fog was lifting in rags. "If there isn't, we hold them until they can go. Nothing in that bundle will spoil."

Nothing in that bundle would spoil. He almost smiled. Fourteen letters, sealed and addressed, and he knew every one of them the way a priest knows his penitents' sins — not because he wanted to but because wanting had stopped being part of it years ago. Tonight, or tomorrow night, he would steam each seal soft over the lamp, read what Mrs. Vance had told the world beyond the river, commit its silences to memory, and reseal it so perfectly that even she could not tell. Then it would travel, and her words would be hers again, and Mara Okafor would go back to being a man whose name appeared on no document anywhere in Verrenholm except, perhaps, a wage ledger he had never been shown.

"Is the car warm?" she asked, climbing in.

"I ran the engine while you finished your tea, ma'am."

"Good." She settled her gloves — the kid leather ones, the good pair — in her lap. "Then let's see the last of the open water before it goes."

* * *

They drove the long way along the mill road because she asked him to, and Mara understood why without her saying it. On last-crossing days she liked to look at the town she claimed she was going to change. The mills stood on both banks, five storeys of brick with windows the color of dirty ice, and their chimneys were already working, pushing smoke into a sky the same shade, so that it was impossible to say where the town ended and its weather began. The hands were coming in off the morning shift, women mostly, shawled, their faces turned down against the wind off the water. As the car passed — it always drew eyes, a motorcar in a town where most people walked — some of those faces lifted.

Mara watched them in the mirror the way he watched everything: sideways, cataloguing, forgetting nothing.

He knew most of their names. That was the strange arithmetic of his position. The women who stared at Edith Vance's car with expressions ranging from indifference to something colder than indifference — he knew their rent troubles from letters, knew whose husband had been blacklisted after the strike of '09, knew which of them sent money home to mothers in the next county and how much of it, because Mrs. Vance's correspondence with the relief committees itemized such things. He knew them and they did not know him, and there were days — more of them lately — when he could not decide whether this made him the richest man in Verrenholm or the poorest.

A girl of perhaps sixteen stood at the mill gate and did not turn away as the car passed. She looked straight into the window, straight at Edith Vance, and her expression held no hatred at all, which was somehow worse. It held assessment. *So this is what she looks like,* the face said. *So this is what it costs.*

Edith saw her too. Mara caught it in the glass: a small tightening at the corner of his employer's mouth, quickly mastered.

"They're watching us," Edith said lightly. "Let them watch. Half of organizing is being seen."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You think I'm wrong."

It was not a question, and it was dangerous ground, and ten years of practice told him exactly how to walk it. "I think you're seen, ma'am. Whether that's the same thing—"

"—is a question for philosophers, and we haven't time for philosophers before luncheon." But she smiled a little, and he heard in it something he had learned to listen for over a decade of reading her mail: the public voice, polished and deployed. There was another voice underneath it, the one that appeared in certain letters to certain correspondents — shorter sentences, rougher timber, occasionally despairing — and he wondered, as he had wondered more often that autumn, why she kept the second voice locked in paper where no one was supposed to read it and let the first one do all the living.

Because no one reads her letters, he thought, and then corrected himself, because someone did. Because he did. Because he was the only person alive who knew both voices, and he had built his whole quiet life inside that knowledge like a man living in a wall.

* * *

At the landing, Hobb was right: the ferry committee was already out on the water in the punt, knocking at the new ice with boathooks, listening to it. Mara left the engine running and carried the bundle down to the boat himself. Fourteen letters. He counted them twice, once with his eyes and once with his fingers, because the fingers did not lie.

Hobb took the bundle and squinted at the sky, then at the water, then at Mara, in descending order of trust. "Noon boat's the last," he said. "After that, anything crossing goes on foot over the ice, and I wouldn't put my own boot on it till it's a hand's thickness, which it won't be for a week yet. So whatever else needs saying this year, say it by noon."

"By noon," Mara said.

"You're a parrot today, boy." Hobb heaved the bundle aboard. "You know what my father used to say about this river? He said the freeze don't close the door. It just asks everybody one question: who's staying, and who was only passing through?" He grinned, gap-toothed, sixty years on that water in his face. "Which are you?"

Mara looked at the far shore — a low grey line of fields and the spire of the Farbank chapel, half dissolved in river fog — and found, to his surprise, that he did not have an answer ready. Nine winters he had stood on this landing and the answer had been easy: *passing through*, always, a man in transit even when standing still, belonging to the road and to the errands and to no one.

"I stay," he said finally, because it was true, and Hobb laughed and pushed off.

* * *

The rest of the morning was errands. The bank, where the teller greeted Mrs. Vance with too much warmth and Mara stood by the door counting the seconds of the handshake. The chemist. The ironmonger, where Edith bought a new hinge for the workshop's back gate and Mara carried it, feeling its cold weight, thinking of the workshop — the typesetting room with its four compositors' stands, the little press named *Perseverance* that three women had learned to run and two more were learning, the smell of ink and lead and lamp oil that clung to his coat all winter and which he had come, without ever deciding to, to love.

The workshop was the reason. He had known it for years, though he had never once permitted the thought to finish itself in words. Whatever else the money was, whatever compromise it fed on, the workshop was real: real type set by real women who now earned a wage that did not come from the mills, real pamphlets that went out under the movement's seal, real girls from the spinning floor who came in after shifts with ink to the wrists and something new in their backs, in the way they stood. You could burn the argument down, tear the speeches apart, prove every hypocrisy ten times over — the workshop would still be standing there in the morning, and the girls would still be setting type in it.

That was the ledger he kept in place of a conscience. Sins on one side; type sticks on the other.

He wondered — driving Mrs. Vance home through the thinning fog, her silence companionable, the river visible now between buildings, white along its edges like a page curling — whether Edith kept a similar ledger. Whether she lay awake in the green room at the top of the house doing the same arithmetic: *the money is filthy, therefore; but the press runs, therefore; but the rhetoric is moderated, therefore.* Her letters suggested she did. One letter especially, from nearly two years back, to a correspondent in the city whose name he had promised his memory to forget, containing a sentence he had never managed to forget at all: *I have made a bargain with a devil of the ordinary commercial kind, and I find that devils keep their side of bargains better than saints do.*

He had resealed that letter with particular care. Some letters deserved to arrive perfect.

* * *

At noon they stood at the landing again, this time among a dozen others, watching the ferry make its last crossing. The whole town had a habit of turning out for it, though nobody organized the turnout; it was simply understood that the closing of the river was an event, like an execution or a wedding, something that marked you as having survived another year.

The fog had burned off entirely and the light was hard and clear, the kind of winter light that showed everything at double distance. Mara could see the ice sheeting out from both banks now, thin as window glass, advancing on the middle channel where the dark water still moved. The ferry came across low in the water with its last cargo: two crates of lamp oil, a coffin, mail sacks, and a family — a man, a woman, a child of four or so — leaving for the city before the roads shut. The child waved at the crowd on the landing and nobody waved back except Edith Vance, who raised one gloved hand solemnly, as if seeing off troops, and the child waved harder.

"They're going somewhere warmer," said a woman beside Mara, to no one.

"They're going somewhere," her neighbor answered. "That's the main thing."

The ferry tied up. Hobb and his boys unloaded it, and then, in front of everyone, Hobb walked to the chain that held the landing gate and began winding it shut. The crowd watched in silence. Somewhere behind Mara a man coughed. The chain rattled through the windlass, link by link, and the sound of it went through Mara strangely — not like a gate closing but like a lock clicking, like something being sealed, like wax pressed down over a sentence that could not now be unsaid.

Until thaw. Five months, perhaps six. Whatever was unfinished in Verrenholm would stay unfinished. Whoever owed anything — money, apology, truth — would carry it through the winter like a stone in a coat pocket.

The crowd dispersed slowly, in twos and threes, the way crowds do when the thing they came for turns out to be smaller than the feeling it produced. Edith lingered, looking at the water, and Mara stood three paces behind her, in the exact position he had occupied for ten years: near enough to hear, far enough to vanish.

"Well," she said at last. "That's that."

"That's that, ma'am."

"No post now until the ice road bears. No word from the city, no instructions, no interference." Something in her voice loosened as she said it, and he heard — he was almost sure he heard — relief. "For five months, Mara, this town belongs to itself."

"For five months, ma'am."

She turned from the railing and for a moment her eyes passed over him — not through him, as they usually did, but actually over him, taking in the coat, the cap, the face — and he had the vertiginous sensation of being briefly visible, a candle flame flickering up in a dark room before settling again.

"Come along," she said. "Cold out here."

They walked up from the landing and Mara did not look back at the river, because he did not need to. He could feel it happening behind him — the ice advancing from both shores, meeting in the middle, stitching the gap closed stitch by stitch in the failing light. By morning the Verren would be a white road to nowhere, and the town would be a closed envelope, and everything in it — every secret, every debt, every letter unsent and truth unspoken — would be held fast until spring.

And the extraordinary thing, the thing he carried home through the blue dusk like contraband, was that some part of him was glad. For nine winters he had feared the closing the way other men feared illness. Now, walking the frozen streets with his hands in his pockets, he examined the gladness carefully, the way he examined seals, and understood it at last: for five months no one could leave. Not the guilty, not the curious, not the women with assessing eyes at the mill gates, not Crane's money moving upriver on the noon boat, not Edith Vance retreating to the city to be admired. Everyone would have to stay and be a neighbor to their own consequences.

Everyone, including him. And if everyone was trapped, then no one was watching the door — and a man who had spent ten years standing near doors, holding coats, carrying messages out and silence back, could almost believe that a closed season was the closest thing to freedom he would ever be offered.

He let himself into the kitchen of the Vance house, hung his coat, lit the lamp, and set the day's remaining correspondence on the table — three letters that had missed the noon boat, held over till thaw.

Or so he thought. He was reaching for the sealing wax out of pure habit, his hands moving through the ritual on their own, when he heard footsteps on the back path — quick, uneven, unfamiliar — and a fist struck the door, twice, hard, in a knock that belonged to no one in Verrenholm.

Through the frosted glass, small and distorted, he could see a figure. A young woman, hatless, her skirts crusted to the knee with ice, a satchel strapped across her chest — and behind her, faint in the last light, the white expanse of the river, showing the broken trail of someone who had crossed it on foot.
