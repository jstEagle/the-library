## 7. Ice on the Windows

By the third week of January, the frost had climbed so high up the workshop windows that the world outside came to Tilly Brandt in fragments — the frozen river a white smear at the top of the glass, the mill chimneys reduced to gray pillars rising out of ice ferns and feathered frost-flowers. She had taken to working with her back to the window because looking through it too long made her feel as though she were inside a sealed jar, and the jar was beautiful, and the beauty was the worst part.

The workshop smelled of ink and coal oil and wet wool. Six women worked the hand presses and the type cases in shifts, their breath visible in the cold corners of the room where the stove's reach failed. They were good women, quick and careful: Berthe Hagen, who could set a column of eight-point faster than any man Tilly had apprenticed under; Sadie Murch, whose knuckles were scarred from the mill years before she'd come to the press; the Kessler sisters, Anna and Ruth, who argued in German and English simultaneously and always about the same thing, which was whether accuracy or speed mattered more. Tilly loved them with an immediacy that frightened her. She had been in this town eleven days. Eleven days, and she already knew she would fight for this place.

That was precisely why she couldn't stop cataloguing.

She kept the catalogue in her head mostly, and on loose sheets hidden inside the false bottom of her satchel when she couldn't help writing things down. *Donation received, October — no donor named. Donation received, November — "a friend of the cause." Wages paid to organizers traveling south — from what fund? The workshop's rent is current, the paper stock never runs short, and Mrs. Vance's gloves are new.* That last one felt like a betrayal even to think. Edith Vance owned exactly three pairs of gloves that Tilly had seen, and all three were fine, kid leather, unmarked by work, and every one of them said something Tilly didn't want to hear.

*Money comes from somewhere. Money always comes from somewhere.*

Her father had been a cooper in Brandt's Hollow until the barrel trade died and took his lungs with it. She had learned arithmetic counting staves, learned injustice watching the lumber company buy the town council for less than the cost of one season's shipping. At nineteen she'd walked out of the Hollow with two dresses and a head full of speeches, and for four years the movement had been pure to her — pure as new snow, before anyone walked across it. Then she'd started reading ledgers. Snow was the first thing to go.

"Miss Brandt." Berthe's voice cut across the clatter. "You've been staring at that galley for ten minutes and you haven't touched it."

"I'm composing."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Composing has your fingers moving." But Berthe said it gently, and slid a cup of chicory toward her across the stone sill of the wash-up sink, and went back to her case.

Tilly picked up the composing stick. In the tray before her lay the week's copy — a piece on wage boards, another on the mill girls' lunch-hour organizing, safe material, clean material. Her eyes went, against her will, to the far end of the room.

Mara Okafor was mending the second press.

He did everything quietly, this driver. He swept the walk before anyone else woke. He hauled coal without being asked. When Edith Vance spoke, he stood near the door with his cap in his hands like a man perpetually about to leave, and when the letters came — and letters came daily, bundled, sealed with wax, tied in twine — it was Mara who carried them, Mara who logged them in a neat hand, Mara who knew the post schedule better than the postmaster did. Tilly had watched him for eleven days the way she watched everyone, and what unsettled her was not what she saw but how little there was to see. Most people leaked. Most people had a face they wore when they thought no one looked, and a different one underneath, and if you sat still long enough the seam between the two showed. Mara had no seam. Or — and this was the thought that kept her awake in the narrow cot above the typesetting room — Mara's seam was sewn so finely and so long ago that only someone who had spent her whole life looking for seams would ever find it.

She had found one. On Tuesday.

Edith had dictated a letter while Tilly copied shorthand in the corner, and midway through, a name had come up — a committee appointment, nothing — and Tilly had glanced up and caught the driver's face. Not a flinch. Nothing so legible. It was more that for half a second his stillness had *cost him something*, the way a held note costs a singer. As though the name had landed somewhere in him and he'd chosen, consciously, deliberately, not to let it echo out.

Nobody chose not to echo over a committee appointment.

Now she set type she wasn't reading and listened to the room around him. The women talked while they worked; they always talked, and the stove made a low approving noise beneath their voices, and lately — Tilly had noticed this with the sharp mixed feeling of a prospector seeing color in the pan — their talk had drifted toward wages, toward hours, toward the mills.

"Twelve hours in the spinning room," Sadie was saying, ink to her elbow, "and they dock you for the ten minutes the doffing takes past the bell. My sister lost a quarter of her pay one month to the clock. A *quarter*, and her man works the card room besides."

"They say the Crane dividend was seven percent this year," Anna Kessler said, not looking up from her case. "My cousin keeps books for the commission house. Seven percent, in a year the girls went out for three weeks and got nothing but blacklisted."

"Crane." The name dropped into the room the way a coin drops into a well — you heard it fall and fall and never heard it land. Ruth Kessler shushed her sister out of habit rather than conviction, and no one spoke for a moment, and in that moment Tilly turned her head just enough to see Mara's hands on the press frame.

They hadn't stopped moving. That was the thing. A guilty man stops; an innocent man doesn't care. Mara Okafor's hands kept turning the wrench with the same steady pressure, but his shoulders had drawn in by a degree, a tailor's adjustment, invisible unless you were watching for cloth pulling wrong.

He'd heard. He'd heard, and he already knew whatever the name meant here, and the knowing hurt him.

Tilly bent over her galley and felt the strange double pulse of hope and dread that had become her constant companion since the night she'd crossed the ice road. Hope, because if the driver knew — if the driver had known for years, maybe — then she was not alone in the dark with her ledger pages and her suspicions. Dread, because a man who had kept such knowledge quiet for years would not thank a stranger for digging it up. And dread again, deeper, colder: because if the driver knew and had stayed, then perhaps the knowledge, once dug up, would not be the weapon she imagined. Perhaps it would be worse than a weapon. Perhaps it would be a truth that broke the very hands that needed to hold it.

* * *

She cornered him that evening, obliquely, the way you approach a horse that might kick.

The workshop emptied by six. Edith had been called to the Harrow house — the widow Harrow, wealthy, influential, one of the movement's local pillars — and had gone dressed in the good coat and the second-best gloves, leaving behind the smell of violet water and instructions about tomorrow's mail. The women went home through streets already blue with dusk. And Mara stayed, as he always stayed, banking the stove, wiping the rollers, covering the type cases with their wooden lids, putting the room to bed like a man closing a church.

Tilly stayed too. She claimed the sorting table and the day's correspondence, ostensibly to log it, and she let the silence stretch until it was just the two of them and the ticking of the cooling stove.

"The ice road's holding," she said. "I crossed it nine days ago and I'd swear it's thicker now than then. The ferryman told me the river does that sometimes — freezes mean early, then fights itself all season."

"It does that." He didn't look up from the roller he was cleaning. His voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man who had decided decades ago that no sentence of his would ever need to hurry.

"Ferryman says a woman crossed from the far shore last winter, alone, carrying a satchel, and lived to tell it. Says the river takes who it takes and spares who it spares, and there's no reasoning with it."

"There isn't." The roller squeaked under the naphtha cloth.

"She carried documents, that woman. Papers that could have changed things on both shores, if she'd found the right person to give them to." Tilly kept her voice light, curious, a woman making conversation about rivers. "The ferryman said the trouble was never the crossing. The trouble was that once you're here, you have to decide who you are."

Mara set the roller in its cradle. For a moment he stood with his back to her, a lean dark shape against the frost-laced window, and the last coal-light caught the edge of him. Then he turned, and his face was kind, and open, and gave away absolutely nothing, and Tilly thought: *God, but you're good. You're so good it must have cost you everything to get this good.*

"The river doesn't ask who you are," he said mildly. "It asks how much you weigh. Everything after that is arithmetic." He gathered his cap and scarf from the peg. "Mrs. Vance will want the Harrow letter answered tonight. It's on the desk. I've left it unsealed — she said to tell you."

He was gone before she could shape the next question, and the door closed behind him with the soft finality of a book shutting, and Tilly sat alone in the cooling room, furious and admiring in equal measure.

*Left it unsealed.* Of course he had. Everything he did was correct. Everything he did was service. Ten days she'd circled him and he had given her nothing but competence and courtesy, a surface like river ice — load-bearing, featureless, and cold all the way down.

Well. She had crossed ice before.

She went to the desk. The Harrow letter lay squared to the blotter's edge, unsealed, its neat lines asking the widow Harrow for the use of her hall for a February meeting. Tilly read it twice, logged it, and then, from the inner pocket of her jacket, she took out the folded page she had copied three nights ago by candlelight in the cot room upstairs, her breath frosting, her hand steadier than her heart.

It was a ledger page — copied true, every figure, every date, from the financial summary Edith Vance kept locked in the cedar box in her room. Tilly had picked no lock; the lock was a formality, a gesture, and the box's key lived in the drawer beneath it, and Edith Vance was a woman who had grown used to being surrounded by people who would never look. That was her error. That was perhaps her tragedy. People always looked.

The page showed disbursements. It showed, in Edith's own compact figures, quarterly sums received into the general fund — sums that dwarfed every subscription and bazaar penny the women scraped together — each marked only with a small initial in the margin, the same initial every time: *A.C.*

Aldous Crane. The mills' owner. The man whose spinning rooms ran twelve-hour shifts and docked the doffing minutes and paid dividends of seven percent in the very year the mill girls struck and starved.

Tilly read the copied page one more time in the empty workshop, and her father's ghost stood at her shoulder smelling of sawdust, and she asked herself the question she had been refusing to ask since the night she'd arrived: *What do you do if the money that prints your truths comes from the men your truths condemn?*

Then she folded the page, and she thought about Mara Okafor's hands on the press frame, drawing in by a single degree at the sound of the Crane name, and she made her decision the way she made most decisions — quickly, and trusting her feet to find out later why her head had been right.

She crossed to the mending bench where he worked. She lifted the tin lid of the parts box — the shallow tray of screws, cotter pins, washers, and worn quions that Mara sorted and tended with the tenderness other men spent on tobacco or children. She laid the copied ledger page flat in the bottom of the tray, beneath the reach of casual sight but squarely in the path of any hand that went looking for a spare screw, which was to say: in the path of *his* hand, and no other. Then she closed the lid, banked the stove, put on her coat, and stepped out into the dark.

The cold hit her like a hand. Above the street the sky was clear for once, hard black and glittering, and the river lay along the town's edge like a drawn blade. Somewhere out on that white plain the ferryman's hut showed one yellow light, and beyond it the far shore was only a rumor.

Let him find it, she thought. Let him know that somebody else knows. Two people carrying the same weight walk slower than one, but they can carry it farther — or they can drop it on each other. She honestly didn't know which this was going to be. She only knew she could not spend the whole winter circling him in polite code while the presses printed other people's truths on other people's money.

Behind her, in the dark workshop, the parts box sat shut and patient, and the frost climbed the windows, and the river fought itself all season long.

She was halfway up the hill to the boarding house when she heard footsteps behind her, quick and light on the packed snow, and turned expecting Berthe or one of the Kesslers — but the figure coming up the street through the lamplight wore a heavy coat she didn't recognize, and a hat pulled low, and walking beside it, pale and composed and freshly returned from the widow Harrow's, was Edith Vance herself — and the man between them, touching his hat brim, was neither of their drivers.

"Miss Brandt," Edith called pleasantly, from twenty feet off, in a voice pitched to carry in the cold. "How fortunate. There's someone here I've wanted you to meet. He says he's come about the workshop — and about the *future* of our funding."

The stranger raised his head, and smiled, and Tilly's hand closed involuntarily around the empty pocket where the original of the ledger page would have been, if she hadn't already hidden it where only Mara would look.
