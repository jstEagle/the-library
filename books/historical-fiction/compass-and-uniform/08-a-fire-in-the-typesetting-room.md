## 8. A Fire in the Typesetting Room

The cold that winter came down like a verdict. It sealed the windows of the workshop with ferns of frost, turned the ink to syrup unless we kept the pots near the stove, and made the walk from the carriage house to the pressroom a crossing of one's own small personal Antarctica. I had lived through eleven winters in this town and could not remember one that bit like this one, though I suppose every winter believes itself exceptional while it is happening.

I was in the storeroom when I smelled it.

You must understand what that smell meant to me before it meant anything else. Ten years of carrying letters teaches a man the language of paper — the dry sweetness of new stock, the sour breath of old rag, the tallow-and-ash ghost of a candle held too close. I knew the smell of scorching paper the way a priest knows the smell of incense: not as information but as vocation. My hands were moving before my mind had finished the sentence.

I came through the doorway into the typesetting room and the world had gone orange.

It was not yet a fire, properly speaking. It was a fire's first argument with the world — flames no higher than my knee, climbing the skirt of the Albion press where someone had draped a drying cloth over the frame, licking up the oiled rags in the corner bin, painting the lead type in the cases with light that made each letter gleam like something molten. The smoke was already at the ceiling, thick and yellowish, curling along the beams toward the loft where we stored three reams of paper and every pamphlet we had printed since November.

Tilly was asleep in the chair by the composing stone. She had been proofing the wage-articles until two in the morning and had fallen asleep sitting upright, pencil still in her hand, the way she did everything — stubbornly, half-consciously, as if even her body refused to concede that work could stop. The firelight found her face and made her look very young.

"Tilly!" My voice cracked on the second syllable. "Tilly, out!"

She woke the way soldiers wake — all at once, no softness in it — and then she saw what I saw, and for one heartbeat neither of us moved. I have thought about that heartbeat many times since. In it lived everything we had not yet said to each other: the ledger page she'd left for me, the questions I'd deflected, the truth circling us both like a hawk that hadn't chosen which of us to strike.

Then we moved.

She went for the sand buckets; I went for the fire. This was not bravery. I want to be honest, in this account, about the difference between courage and reflex. I beat at the flames with the heavy wool blanket Tilly threw me, and the heat came through the weave and seared the backs of my hands, and I thought, with a clarity that still shames me, *the letters are in the loft* — not *Tilly*, not *the women*, but the letters, the satchel of Edith's correspondence I kept locked in the trunk upstairs because ten years of habit does not die in one winter. A man's instincts tell him exactly who he is. Mine told me I was still, at the root of myself, a courier.

The fire died under the blanket and the sand. What remained was smoke, and steam hissing off the wet press, and the smell — oh, the smell of burnt oil and scorched cotton hanging in the room where we set words, as if the room itself had been caught saying something it shouldn't.

Tilly stood in the doorway with an empty bucket in both hands, breathing hard, watching the black ruin of the drying cloth drip off the press frame onto the floor.

"Well," she said.

* * *

By seven o'clock there were nine of us in the workshop kitchen, and the whole town knew.

Word travels fast in a closed place. When the bridge shuts and the ice road forms, the town becomes a single organism with one nervous system, and the news of a fire in the suffragists' workshop traveled along it the way pain travels along a nerve — instantaneously, and to every part at once. Mrs. Hargrove arrived with her sleeves already rolled and a look that dared anyone to ask why a woman who worked twelve hours at the looms had come to fight a fire at dawn. Behind her came the Pell sisters, and Ruth Ellison with her hands still blue from the mill yard, and old Marta Kessel, who was sixty and had walked a mile on ice to get there. They came without being sent for. That fact sat in the room with us all morning, heavier than the smoke.

Edith arrived last, and Edith arrived dressed.

I do not mean that unkindly, and I want to record it fairly, because what happened in that kitchen taught me something about her that took me another four chapters of my life to fully understand. She came in her good coat with her collar straight and her hat pinned at the correct angle, and she stood in the doorway of the burned typesetting room with her gloved hands folded, surveying the damage the way a general surveys a field — not with grief but with assessment. Only I, who had read a thousand of her letters, saw the tremor in her left hand where it gripped the glove.

"Mill men," she said.

It was not a question, so no one answered it.

"Look at this." She crossed to the corner bin and lifted the charred rags with two fingers, dropping them back as though they were evidence in a trial she was already conducting in her head. "Oiled waste, left against the frame. A lamp knocked or carried. The cloth laid over the press so the flame would have something to climb." She turned, and her voice took on the register I had heard through a hundred closed doors — the speechifying voice, the voice built for halls. "This was set. This was done to us deliberately, by people who cannot bear what we are building here."

"It was done by carelessness, more likely," said Marta Kessel, mildly, from the doorway, and the temperature of the room dropped several degrees. "We've all left rags in bins, Mrs. Vance. Every shop in this town smells of burnt oil come February."

"Every shop in this town isn't printing articles about Crane wages," Edith returned, and I heard the edge beneath the edge — the thing she wasn't saying, which was that she knew better than any of them what the Crane name touched, because she had been touching it for years.

I kept my eyes on the floorboards and said nothing, which is the oldest skill I own.

But inside me something was moving, some animal turning over in its den. Because Edith was wrong in one particular, and right in another, and the combination frightened me more than either would alone. She was wrong that it required malice — the fire could have been simple winter negligence, and half the town's shops had suffered worse from stoves and lamps. But she was right that someone had been asking after the workshop. Right that a stranger had stood at the bar of the Anchor & Chain three nights before the bridge closed, buying rounds and asking easy, smiling questions — *that's the ladies' print shop, is it? How many girls work there? Do they keep the place lit of a night?* — and I knew, because I had been in the Anchor & Chain that night doing what I always did, which was listening while pretending to drink.

I knew the man's face. I had seen it before, once, ten years ago, coming down the steps of the Crane house on the hill with his hat in his hand and an envelope under his arm — the envelope that became the donation that became the compromise that became everything. He had aged. His smile had not.

His name was Prosser. And he had been stranded here, same as the rest of us, when the ice closed the river — a Crane agent sealed into a town full of the very women his employer paid Edith to moderate, with nothing to do all winter but watch the workshop and wait for instructions that could no longer reach him.

Or nothing to do but act on instructions he had received before the closing. The thought arrived cold and complete, like a nail driven flush.

* * *

We salvaged what we could through the morning. The press itself survived — the Albion was built of iron and indifference and would outlive most of us — but the frisket was scorched, the cloth destroyed, and the smoke had gotten into the paper stock upstairs, forty sheets gone grey at the edges and smelling like a dead hearth. Tilly carried the ruined paper out to the yard stack, sheet by sheet, and her face as she did it was the face of a woman counting casualties.

I followed her out. The cold hit like water.

"You smelled it before I woke," she said, without preamble. She never wasted time arriving at things. "How?"

"I know the smell of burning paper." I kept my voice level. "Occupational hazard."

She looked at me then — that long, level, cataloguing look she had, the one that made me feel like a document spread open under her lamp. "You keep saying things like that. Occupational hazard. Ten years of driving. You say them like a man reciting lines he's tired of."

"Maybe I am tired."

"Of the lines, or of the play?" She dumped the last armful of spoiled sheets and dusted her palms. "Mara. Whoever set that fire — if someone set it — they wanted to interrupt the wage articles. Which means the wage articles matter to somebody with something to lose. Which means we're close to something." She paused, and her voice dropped, gentler and therefore more dangerous. "And you already know what we're close to, don't you. You've known longer than I have."

Down in the yard the snow lay unbroken except for our tracks, two lines of dark prints side by side, and I thought about how easy it would be to lie to her, and how I had spent ten years lying by omission to everyone, and how the lies had begun to feel less like protection and more like sediment — layer upon layer burying whatever the original shape of me had been.

"I know that nobody burns down a press over wages alone," I said finally. "Wages are a symptom. If someone wants the presses stopped, it's because the presses are close to naming the disease."

"And the disease has a name."

"The disease," I said, "has money."

I walked back inside before she could ask the third question, because I could feel it forming behind her teeth like weather.

* * *

That evening Edith summoned me to the parlor of the house on Chapel Street where she lodged, and I went, as I had gone a thousand evenings, carrying my cap in my hand like a man with no interior life whatsoever.

She stood at the window looking out at the frozen river, which lay white and featureless under the moon, a road that led nowhere, a bridge to nothing. Without turning she said, "You were first into the room today."

"Tilly was sleeping in the chair. She might have—"

"Yes. You saved her. Everyone has told me. That's not what I'm asking about." Now she turned, and the lamplight did its usual work on her face, carving the authority deeper, softening nothing. "I'm asking how you knew to come running. Most men would have stood in that doorway deciding whether a fire was their business. You moved like the fire was yours."

"The workshop is my business. I drive for it. I carry for it."

"You carry." Something flickered across her face — not suspicion; Edith had known about my reading for only a few days then, and the knowledge was still raw between us, still finding its shape. But the word *carry* had changed weight in that room since the confrontation, and we both felt it. She let it lie. Then, instead, she said: "Prosser was seen in town before the closing. You knew that."

It was not a question either, and it stopped my breath. Because I had not told her. I had told no one. I had filed the sight of him in the Anchor & Chain away in the vast archive of things Mara Okafor knows and says nothing about, and here it was, surfacing in Edith's mouth.

"How—" I began.

"I have eyes too," she said quietly. "You are not the only one in this town who watches. He asked after the workshop's hours. He asked whether the women printed at night. And now, three weeks later, a fire starts in the typesetting room from rags that were, I am quite certain, placed rather than dropped." She came away from the window. "If it was him, he acted on standing orders, because nothing has reached this town since the ice. Which means Aldous Crane decided, before the river shut, that our press had become inconvenient."

There it was, spoken aloud at last, in her parlor, by her mouth: *Crane*. Not *our benefactor*, not *our friend in the city*, not any of the circumlocutions her letters had used for a decade. Just the name, bare and terrible, sitting on the carpet between us like something exhumed.

"Mrs. Vance," I said carefully, "if Crane wanted the workshop silenced, why would he bother with a small fire? He funds it. One withdrawal of support would close us by spring."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide — watched the calculation move behind her eyes like ice shifting on the river — whether to answer honestly. This, I understood later, was the loneliest part of being Edith Vance: the constant arithmetic of what could be safely said, even to the man who had read every word she'd ever written.

"Because," she said, "a withdrawal can be explained as prudence, and a fire can be blamed on mill thugs, and a blamed fire fills halls for a month. He doesn't want to close us, Mara. He wants to manage us. He wants us radical enough to sell papers and docile enough to leave his mills alone, and he wants everyone — his friends, my enemies, the whole comfortable architecture of this town — to believe the movement and the money are strangers to each other." Her voice thinned. "A fire serves that better than a ledger ever could."

"And you'll use it that way?" I heard my own voice go flat and strange. "Blame the mills publicly for a fire you can't prove?"

"The town will blame them regardless."

"That isn't the same as saying it."

"No," she said softly. "It isn't. And you may put that in your archive, courier. There's a difference between what is true and what is useful, and I have spent fifteen years walking the line between them, and tonight I am too tired to pretend the line isn't there."

I left her parlor with my head ringing and stood awhile in the street, under a sky hard and starless with frost, looking down toward the workshop where one window still glowed — Tilly, working late again, guarding the press by the simple expedient of refusing to leave it.

Fear had been in me all day, a low continuous hum like the stove flue drawing. But somewhere between Edith's window and Tilly's lamplit glass, the fear changed its chemistry. It hardened. I felt it happen physically — a setting, a curing, like ink drying into permanence. Because I understood, standing in that street, what the fire had actually done, and it was not what Prosser or his absent master intended.

Before the fire, the workshop had been a building where women printed pamphlets. After it, it was a thing that nine women had run toward at dawn without being asked — a thing Marta Kessel had walked a mile on ice to defend, a thing Tilly would not leave alone at night, a thing whose charred press frame the women had touched with their fingertips the way pilgrims touch the feet of saints.

You cannot frighten people out of something they have already decided belongs to them. That is the lesson every mill owner eventually learns, and pays dearly for learning.

I turned toward the workshop. Tilly saw me coming and opened the door before I knocked, and behind her the damaged press stood in its corner, scorched and scarred and utterly unrepentant.

"We're setting the next issue tomorrow," she said. "All of it. The wage tables, the Crane comparisons, everything we've been circling." She searched my face. "Unless you have a reason we shouldn't. One reason. Said plainly, this time."

The wind came down the frozen river and shook the shutter behind me, and somewhere out on the white miles of ice, in a boarding house in this sealed and stranded town, a man named Prosser was waiting to see what we would do — and reporting, in whatever channel remained open to him, to a man on the far shore whose money sat in our account like a splinter working toward the heart.

"No," I said. "Set it all."

And I stepped inside, and pulled the door shut behind me, and stood with my back against it — the invisible man, guarding the door of a building that was no longer just a building, while somewhere below the ice the river ran dark and patient, keeping its own account of everything we did next.
