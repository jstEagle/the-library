## 10. Tilly's Proof

The ledger page lay flat under Tilly's palm the way a caught bird lies still — not because it wanted to, but because it had nowhere left to go.

She had been at it for six weeks. Six weeks of borrowed lamplight and borrowed minutes, of copying figures into a notebook she kept sewn into the lining of her coat, of learning the workshop's rhythms well enough to know when the typesetting room emptied, when Mrs. Hargreaves locked the front but not the side door, when Mara drove Mrs. Vance to the far villages and when he stayed. She had come to this town on the ice road with a satchel of documents and a certainty, and the certainty had only grown teeth.

Now the teeth were out.

She spread the last of it across the worktable after hours: three sheets of onionskin copies of correspondence, the copied ledger page from her confrontation with Mara, and the affidavit — signed, witnessed, dated — from Mr. Pell, the retired shipping clerk who had handled Crane Mill's private disbursements for eleven years and whose conscience had finally cost him his silence in a two-hour conversation conducted in whispers in the back of the chandlery while his wife waited outside with the horse.

*Received of A. Crane, Esq., the sum of four hundred pounds, disbursed per instruction to E. Vance, suffrage organization, quarterly.*

Four hundred pounds. Quarterly. For years.

Tilly read it again. She had read it perhaps forty times. Each time the numbers refused to become anything other than what they were. That was the cruelty of proof: it didn't argue with you. It just sat there, being true.

She thought of Edith Vance on the platform at the harvest meeting, her gloved hands describing arcs in the air, her voice like a bell rung in clean water — *and we say to the millowners, we say to the men who grow fat on the bent backs of your mothers and daughters: your day is done.* Tilly had stood in the back of that room with her heart going like a drum and believed every word. She had crossed a frozen river to be near that voice.

And the voice was bought. Not corrupted, maybe — that was the part that kept her up at night — not wicked, not even, God help her, entirely wrong. Bought the way you buy a roof. The way you buy bread for people who have none. Edith Vance had taken Aldous Crane's money and used it to keep the lights on in a workshop full of women who could now set type faster than any print shop in the county, and Tilly could construct that defense in her sleep, had constructed it, had lain awake constructing it night after night while the wind tested the windows.

But a movement that cannot name its own money is not free. A woman who says *your day is done* to the man who pays her rent is reading from his script whether she knows it or not. Tilly had watched it happen without watching it happen: the speeches that stopped short of naming Crane Mill specifically; the strike fund appeal that never mentioned the lockout at Number Two; the way *the millowners* became an abstraction, a weather system, something no one's actual face attached to.

She gathered the papers, squared their edges, and tied them with string. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and found it strange, because inside she was not steady at all. Inside she was twenty-three years old and standing at the edge of something with no railing.

There was one more thing to do, and she had been avoiding it for three days.

---

She found him in the stable at dusk, mending a harness strap by the light of a storm lantern. Of course he was in the stable. He lived in the margins of other people's business the way mice lived in the walls of this town — everywhere, unseen, surviving on what fell from other tables. She had spent weeks trying to decide what he was: servant, spy, ghost, fool. She had watched him carry Mrs. Vance's letters like a man carrying eggs across ice, and she had watched his eyes when he thought no one was looking, and she had decided he was none of those things, or all of them, which amounted to the same trouble.

He looked up when she came in. Didn't startle. She'd have bet money he never startled; a man who startled didn't survive being invisible.

"Miss Brandt." He went back to the strap. "Mrs. Vance won't need the car until Thursday."

"It's not about the car."

"Mm." He pulled the awl through the leather. The lantern threw his shadow huge against the plank wall, bigger than him, which struck her as the whole story of Mara Okafor in a single image.

She sat down on the feed trunk across from him and put the string-tied packet on her knees where he couldn't help but see it.

"I know you know," she said.

The awl paused for half a second — half a second, then resumed. But she'd seen it. Ten years of practice and she'd still seen it.

"The cold makes the leather brittle," he said. "You have to work slower in winter."

"Cranemoney," Tilly said. "That's what my mother called it, when a factory man gave a subscription to the chapel roof so nobody would ask where the coal came from. Four hundred pounds a quarter, Mr. Okafor. From Aldous Crane. To Mrs. Vance. I have the disbursement records, I have the correspondence routing, and I have Mr. Pell's affidavit, sworn before a justice on the far shore before the ice closed." She leaned forward. "I have enough."

Mara set down the awl. He did it carefully, the way he did everything, as though even tools deserved gentleness, and looked at her for a long moment with those dark, quiet eyes that gave back exactly nothing except, tonight, a kind of exhaustion so old it had fossilized into calm.

"Pell," he said. "The shipping clerk."

"You know the name."

"I know everyone's name." He almost smiled. It was the saddest almost-smile she had ever seen. "That's most of what I do. Names, dates, amounts. Which silences keep which people safe." He wiped his hands on a rag, slowly. "How long have you known?"

"Six weeks. How long have *you* known?"

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, the river boomed under the ice — that deep shifting groan the river made on cold nights, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and both of them glanced toward the sound without meaning to. Everyone in town had learned to glance. By February you stopped hearing it; you only heard it when you were already afraid.

"Ten years," Mara said.

The number went through her like a nail. "Ten years. You've read her letters for ten years—"

"That's not what I said."

"But it's true, isn't it? You're her courier. And couriers who know everyone's name—" She stopped herself, recalculated. He would not be driven. She had learned that much. You could not drive Mara Okafor anywhere; you could only stand somewhere true and wait to see if he walked over. "Ten years, then. You've known for ten years that the woman who stands on platforms and damns the mills is paid by the mills, and you've said nothing. Driven her. Carried her letters. Sealed them with your own hands."

"Yes."

The word had no excuse in it, no defense, no apology. Just the fact, laid down between them like the strap he was mending.

"Why?" It came out harder than she intended. "You live here. These women feed you. Half the typesetters in that workshop can't afford coal and you've known the reason why their own movement won't name the man who owns the reason—"

"Because if I'd spoken in year one, or year three, or year seven," Mara said quietly, "the workshop closes. The press goes to auction. Mrs. Hargreaves is back in the mill boarding house at nine shillings a week, and the girls who learned to read composition off her are back on the spinning floors, and the movement becomes a pamphlet nobody prints." He folded the rag in quarters, then eighths, precise as bookkeeping. "I chose the workshop over the truth. Every morning for ten years I chose it again. That's not a defense, Miss Brandt. It's just the shape of my life."

"And it ends now." Tilly untied the string and laid the packet on the harness bench between them. "Because it can't go on being both. That's what I came here to tell you, and I'll tell you plainly, because I think you're owed plainness after all these years of sealed envelopes. The bridge is closed. Nobody's coming to help us and nobody's leaving. In spring, Crane's people will come over that bridge, and if this isn't published by then, it never will be — he'll have had a season to close every hole, buy every witness, retire poor Mr. Pell to someplace quiet. This winter is the only door we'll ever get. So I'm publishing. On the workshop press, with the workshop women, before thaw."

"He'll burn it."

"Then he'll burn it while the whole town watches, and the town will know why."

Mara looked at the packet for a long time. His face did the thing faces do when a man looks at his own funeral arrangements: nothing moved, and everything worked underneath.

"There's a version of this," he said at last, "where you take that boat of papers to the far shore in spring and give it to a London paper, and it's news for a fortnight, and then it's a scandal, and then it's a footnote, and the workshop closes anyway, only slower."

"And there's a version where we print it ourselves, on our own masthead, and it can't be bought, buried, or footnoted, because it belongs to the women who set the type." She stood. Her legs felt like someone else's legs. "I didn't come to debate you, Mr. Okafor. I came because there's a page missing from my file, and it's you."

He raised his head slightly.

"The courier who read every letter. Who knew the money's name for ten years. Who carried the compromise in his pocket every day it was being denied on every platform in the county." She heard her own voice shake and let it. "If I publish without you, the story has a villain-shaped hole where a man should be, and the first thing Crane's lawyers will find is that hole, and they will widen it until it swallows Edith Vance entire and leaves the machinery that bought her standing. If you stand with me — if your name is on it, if you testify to what you carried and when — then it isn't an accusation anymore. It's a confession, witnessed. It's the truth with its hand on the Bible." She took a breath. "Help me publish. Or I do it alone, and when the reckoning comes — and it will come, Mr. Okafor, it's coming over that ice right now whether we like it or not — I will name you, and Mrs. Vance will fall, and you'll fall with her, and the difference between the two versions is only whether you were brave in daylight or dragged out of the dark."

The lantern flame bowed once, straightened.

For a moment she thought he would answer with anger — she had rehearsed for anger, braced for it like weather. Instead he reached out and touched the string she had untied, drawing it slowly through his fingers, and she understood with a lurch that she was watching ten years pass under his hands: every envelope, every secret, every morning he had chosen the roof over the truth, all of it running out through his fingers like the string, and nothing he could do to stop it.

"Do you know what my name sounds like?" he said, so quietly she had to lean in. "Out loud? I don't. Not fully. I've been 'the driver' so long I half believe it." He looked up at her, and his eyes weren't empty anymore; they were full of something she couldn't name and didn't want to look away from. "You're asking me to walk out of a wall I built around myself, brick by brick, for ten years, onto ground where everyone can see me."

"Yes."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow I put the question to the women of the workshop. All of it. Every figure, every name, including yours." She held his gaze. "I won't lie for anyone's safety. Not hers. Not yours. Not mine. That's the whole point."

Mara nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something rather than threatened him. Then he rose, taller than she remembered him being, and took up the storm lantern, and the stable filled and emptied with moving light.

"Then I need one night," he said. "There's a letter I've been carrying for ten years too. Mine. And if my name goes on that masthead, I want the sentence under it to be in my own words."

He walked past her toward the door, then stopped, his back to her, the lantern trembling very slightly in his hand — the only tremble he permitted himself.

"Miss Brandt. When you put it to the women — because you will, you should — tell them the truth costs the truth-tellers too. Tell them I said that. Tell them I'm ready to pay it."

He pushed open the stable door and stepped out into the dark, and the wind off the river came through the gap like a hand, smelling of iron and deep cold, and far out on the ice the river groaned again — longer this time, lower, a sound less like something turning over in its sleep and more like something beginning to wake.

Behind her on the harness bench, the packet of papers lay open under the dying lantern-light, and Tilly realized her hands, steady all evening, were shaking now — not from fear, she told herself, not only from fear — because tomorrow the question went to the women, and whatever they answered, the town that had slept behind its closed bridge would wake by spring into a world with nowhere left to hide.
