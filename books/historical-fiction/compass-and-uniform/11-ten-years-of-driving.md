## 11. Ten Years of Driving

The river at night was a different country.

Mara had lived beside it for ten years and had never once walked out onto its frozen surface until now. There was a superstition in town about the ice — that it kept its own accounts, that anyone who crossed outside the marked road was borrowing against a ledger the river would one day collect — but tonight he wanted no road, no markers, nothing made by other people's hands. He wanted to stand on something that owed him nothing.

He stepped off the bank below the mill race, where the fog came up thin even in winter, and the ice took his weight with a sound like a held breath.

Above him, the town glowed along the ridge: the workshop's windows still lit, where Tilly was working late with two of the typesetters; Edith's house with its single lamp burning in the study, which meant she was awake, which meant she was writing letters he would not read until morning, or would not read at all, if Tilly's ultimatum did what ultimatums do. The bridge stood dark and barred at its far end, a black lattice against the stars, closed now for six weeks and closed for three more months at least. Behind him, the far shore was a rumor.

He walked toward the middle of the river because the middle is where you go when you want to be equidistant from every decision.

The cold found the gaps in his coat and settled in like a tenant. He let it. He had spent ten years being comfortable and invisible, and invisibility turned out to be the more expensive condition.

* * *

His name was Mara Okafor. He rarely heard it spoken anymore. In the town they called him *Mrs. Vance's man*, delivered with varying temperatures of respect; in the movement they called him *the driver*; Edith herself called him *Mara* only when she needed something urgently, and *Mr. Okafor* when she was angry, and nothing at all when she was thinking, which was most of the time. His name had become a tool stored at the bottom of someone else's drawer.

But there had been a time when strangers said it aloud.

He could still hear the first occasion. He was nineteen, new off the boat train from the coast, standing in the booking office of the Grand Terminus with his cardboard case and his father's secondhand overcoat, and the clerk had looked at his papers and said, "Okafor. Mara Okafor," reading it back slowly, tasting the syllables like a man sampling an unfamiliar wine, and then — this was the part he kept — the clerk had smiled and said, "Well then, Mr. Okafor. Where are we sending you?"

*Where are we sending you.* As if Mara were the subject of the sentence and the world merely its object. As if he were a man with destinations rather than errands.

He had been sent to the Vance household as a driver on the recommendation of a cousin of a woman his mother had known, and in those first weeks he had been so grateful for the position that he had made himself small deliberately, methodically, the way a tailor takes in a seam. He learned to stand at attention by the motorcar while conversations happened around him. He learned the servant's grammar: the lowered eyes, the half-step-back, the art of being furniture with excellent posture. And when Edith had discovered — how, he never knew, though he suspected she simply watched him watch things — that he had a fine memory and a careful hand, she had added the letters to his duties without ceremony. *You'll take these to the post before the bridge closes. Mind the seals.*

*Mind the seals.* That was all the instruction he ever received, and all the permission he ever required.

The first letter he opened was an accident of steam. He had carried the bundle through a wet fog from the ferry landing, and the envelope gum had softened, and Mrs. Hargrove's letter to her sister peeled open in his hands like a flower deciding to bloom early. He should have pressed it shut. Instead he stood under the gas lamp outside the post office and read four lines about a sick nephew and a loan of eight pounds, and he sealed it again with a borrowed stick of wax he bought that same hour, and he told himself: *never again.*

Never again lasted eleven days.

* * *

The ice creaked beneath him as he walked, long groaning syllables, the river talking in its sleep. Out here there was no wind to speak of, only the vast pressure of the frozen water adjusting itself, and the sky enormous and indifferent above the smoke haze of the mills. He stopped where the current ran deepest — you could tell by the darker grain of the ice, like the grain in walnut — and he looked down, absurdly, as if he might see through to the moving water, the living river going about its business under a lid.

Ten years. He tried to hold the number honestly. Not ten years of driving — that was the cover story, the respectable surface. Ten years of reading. Perhaps four thousand letters, conservatively, at thirty-some a month, season after season. He had read love letters and suicide notes, treasury reports and threats, a bishop's confession, a factory girl's plea for help getting away from a foreman whose name Mara still knew and had never spoken. He had read them all by lamplight with the wax stick warm in his hand, and he had resealed them and delivered them, and somewhere in the fourth or fifth year he had stopped telling himself it was accident or duty and admitted the plain truth: he read because the letters were where the world kept its real sentences, and he was a man starving for real sentences.

And he had altered some.

That was the wound he could not stop touching tonight. He counted them the way a drunk counts dry days — seven, he believed, though he could no longer swear to seven and not nine. Small alterations. A date moved. A name softened from *Crane* to *our friend in the north*. Once — the worst one — a paragraph lifted entire from a letter from Adele Kessler to her sister in the city, a paragraph in which Adele said she had seen Aldous Crane's private secretary leaving the Vance house at dusk and *did not know what to make of it*, and Mara had cut it because he understood exactly what to make of it, and because Adele Kessler had three children and a printing trade and a life that would end if Crane's people learned what she'd noticed. He had protected her. He told himself he had protected her. He had taken her observation out of the world the way you take a splinter out of a foot, and the foot had walked on, and Mara had carried the splinter in his own palm for six years until it became part of the hand.

Seven letters, altered. Four thousand, read. One man, erased.

Out on the ice the cold had passed through his coat and his wool and his skin and was now addressing the bone directly, and he welcomed it, because grief needs a body to live in and he had been trying to evict his for a decade.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic he could not make come right.

If he had published the truth about Crane in year five, the workshop would have died in its infancy. That was not speculation; Crane's money was the workshop's heating coal and its type and its rent, and everyone in the movement knew the money flowed but nobody asked the well where it drew from. He had seen the women arrive — mill girls with ink-stained fingers learning to set type, learning that their opinions had weight in lead — and he had watched them begin to speak in meetings, hesitantly at first, then with the gathering confidence of people discovering their own lungs. To expose Crane then would have been to snatch the air from those lungs. So he had chosen. He chose the workshop over the truth, the living thing over the honest one, and he had paid for the choice in the only currency he possessed, which was himself.

Or — here was the second column of the ledger — if he had been an honest man in year five, he would have gone to Edith privately and said, *I have read your mail for two years and I know where the money comes from and we must decide together what to do,* and whatever shame that confession cost him, the movement would have had a decade to find clean money, or to survive without any, or to fail honestly instead of succeeding corruptly. Instead he had played Providence with other people's lives from the servant's side of a motorcar, deciding which truths got to exist, and called it loyalty.

Both columns were true. That was the unbearable part. He had done harm and good with the same hands, in the same nights, by the same lamplight, and no future honesty could retroactively unmix them.

Tilly's face rose in his memory: young, sharp, sitting across from him in the workshop kitchen three days ago with her copied ledger page between them like a card laid down. *You knew,* she hadn't quite said, but her eyes had said it, and then yesterday the plainer version: help me publish, or I do it alone and take you both down. She had meant it as a threat. It had landed, in some locked room of him, as an offer of rescue.

Because — and here was the thing he had only begun to admit since she arrived — being the keeper of secrets had curdled somewhere along the way from privilege into prison. He knew everything and could say nothing. He was the most informed man in the town and the least consulted. He had watched Edith moderate her rhetoric year by year, soften the attacks on the mills, redirect the organizers away from the wage question, and he had watched the mill girls in the workshop absorb each redirection without knowing why their own cause kept swerving from their own grievances, and he had said nothing, and said nothing, and said nothing, until the silence was so heavy he had to carry it in his posture, shoulders bent slightly forward like a man walking uphill forever.

The fog was coming up off the open water downstream, ghosting along the ice. Somewhere far off, a crack rang out like a rifle shot — the ice settling, growing, doing its slow work — and he stood very still until the echo died among the bridge pilings.

* * *

He thought about the man he might have been.

It was a useless exercise and he performed it anyway, walking again now, south along the invisible channel, his boots ringing faintly on the frozen river. In the other life — the one where he had never taken up the wax stick — he would perhaps have been a printer. He had the temperament for it: the patience, the exactness, the love of sentences that say precisely what they mean. He might have worked in some shop in the city, setting other people's words by day and writing his own by night, and his name would have been on the union roll and the lodging-house register and eventually, maybe, on the spine of a book. Strangers would have said *Okafor* and meant a person, not a service.

Instead he had become the world's most literate ghost. Every skill he had, he exercised in secret: his memory used for cataloguing other people's silences, his penmanship for forging seals, his judgment for deciding, alone in the dark, which realities deserved to circulate. He had taken the noblest instrument a person can have — the ability to speak truly — and buried it under ten years of professional discretion.

And for what? For safety. Everyone safe, everything quiet, the workshop warm, Edith honored, the movement funded, the town asleep. He had kept the whole fragile arrangement running like a man keeping plates spinning, and he had mistaken the spinning for purpose, the exhaustion for virtue.

The anger, when it finally arrived, arrived quietly, the way ice forms — no drama, just a slow solidifying across everything. He stopped walking and stood in the middle of the frozen river at midnight and was angry: at Edith, who had built a cathedral on Crane's money and let others kneel in it; at Crane, who bought movements the way he bought timber, by the ton; at himself, most thoroughly, for being the mortar in all of it. And underneath the anger, softer and more frightening, was grief — genuine grief, the kind you feel at a funeral — for Mara Okafor, nineteen years old, standing in a booking office, being asked where he'd like to be sent. That man had died by degrees, a little each time Mara reseal an envelope, and tonight was the first night Mara had allowed himself to mourn him properly.

He took off his glove. His bare hand was clumsy in the cold, but he managed, with numb fingers, to pull his notebook from his breast pocket and a pencil stub, and he wrote by feel more than sight, leaning the pad against his knee:

*I, Mara Okafor, have read every letter placed in my care for ten years. I altered seven of them. I knew the source of the money. I am done choosing other people's silence for them.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then he put the notebook away, and the act of writing them stayed in his hand like heat.

It was not yet a decision. A decision would require him to walk back to the shore and choose a door — Tilly's ultimatum, or Edith's lamp, or the third thing forming in him now, the thing that had no shape yet except that it began with *I* and ended with a signature. But something had shifted in the deep water under the ice, some current finding a new channel, and he could feel the change in the way the river felt its thaw months before the ice showed a crack.

He turned back toward town. The workshop windows still glowed on the ridge, and as he watched, a shadow crossed behind the glass — Tilly, or one of the women, still working at this hour. Working on what? On the pamphlet, if she was already past asking. On the proof that would burn down the comfortable world he had spent ten years fireproofing.

The ice spoke again under his boots, longer this time, almost conversational, and Mara Okafor walked toward the shore and the lit windows, and for the first time in ten years he was not deciding what other people should be allowed to know. He was deciding what he would.

At the foot of the river stairs, where the marked ice road began and the town's jurisdiction resumed, he saw a figure waiting with a lantern — small, hooded, unmistakably Tilly — and she raised the light when she recognized him, and called across the frozen shallows:

"Mara. We need to talk. Tonight."
