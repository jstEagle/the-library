## 12. What Edith Knew

The ledger page had lived for three days in the drawer of Edith Vance's writing desk, beneath the false bottom she'd had installed years ago for documents she could neither burn nor keep in the open. She went to it each morning the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth — not to learn anything, but to confirm that the ache was still there, still hers, still shaped exactly as she remembered it.

On the fourth morning the page was gone.

She stood with the drawer open and the winter light falling grey across the empty wood, and she did not at first feel fear. What she felt was something closer to relief, and that frightened her more than anything else could have. Ten years of carrying the thing alone, and some part of her had been waiting — *hoping* — for someone to take it out of her hands.

She counted what else should be there. The Crane correspondence: present. The affidavit from the foreman's widow, folded into thirds: present. The copies Tilly Brandt had made, the ones Edith had found tucked into her own valise like a confession left on a pillow — those were gone. All of them. Taken sometime between last night's lamp and this morning's frost, taken by someone who knew precisely where Edith kept the things she could not look at.

There were only three people in the house who might have known about the false bottom. One of them was Edith herself. One of them had arrived across the ice three weeks ago with sharp eyes and sharper arithmetic. And one of them had been driving her car, carrying her letters, standing just behind her shoulder, for ten years.

She put on her gloves. She did not button her coat properly; her hands were not steady enough for buttons, and she would not let Mara see unsteady hands.

He was in the carriage house, which is where he always was at that hour, going over the motor with a rag and a wrench though the car would not move again until spring. The workshop needed no driving and neither did she; the whole town was walkable end to end in twenty minutes, and yet she had kept him on, kept the salary flowing, because — she understood it now, standing in the doorway of the carriage house with the cold pouring past her — because he was the last person who had known her before the compromise, and letting him go would have been a kind of admission.

"Mara," she said.

He looked up from the engine. In the lamplight his face did its usual work: the pleasant blankness, the deference worn smooth by a decade of practice. But she had spent a decade reading faces from podiums — finding the skeptic in the third row, the hostile man by the door, the woman who needed one more sentence before she could believe — and she saw, for the first time in ten years, that his face was not blank at all. It was guarded. There is nothing blank about a wall.

"Mrs. Vance." He set down the wrench. "Cold out here."

"It is." She came forward until the lamplight reached her too, until they stood in the same small circle of warmth the lantern threw against the dark. "Tilly Brandt made copies of certain documents. A ledger page. You've seen them."

It was not a question, and she watched him decide not to pretend otherwise. His jaw moved once, the way a man's does when he swallows something that wants to come up as words.

"They're missing," he said.

"They are. And you know where they've gone."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind worked at the loose board over the carriage-house window, the same note over and over, patient as water. Edith realized she was holding her breath and made herself release it.

"I do," Mara said.

"Then tell me."

"Mrs. Vance—" He stopped. Started again, and this time his voice had lost its servant's smoothness entirely, and what was underneath it was quieter and much older. "You should sit down."

"I will not sit down in my own carriage house like a woman receiving news."

"No," he said. "I don't suppose you will." He wiped his hands on the rag with terrible thoroughness, buying seconds, and she understood suddenly that this was the moment he had rehearsed for ten years without ever believing he would reach it. "Then I'll say it standing. I've read every letter. Every one. For ten years. Before I sealed them, before I carried them, before anyone else in the world saw what you wrote — I read it. By lamplight, usually. I know what you said to Mrs. Pankhurst's people about the workshop's finances. I know what you told your sister in March of ought-nine when Mother was dying and you couldn't leave. I know which donations you refused and which you didn't, and I know why." He looked up then, straight at her, and his eyes were not deferential at all; they were exhausted. "I know about Aldous Crane, ma'am. I've known since the first year."

* * *

Later she could never reconstruct what she had done with her body during the minutes that followed. She was standing; then she was sitting on the crate by the workbench without having decided to sit; her gloves were off, though she had no memory of removing them. The wind kept working its board. Mara stayed where he was, half a room away, giving her the distance one gives a struck animal.

"You opened my correspondence," she said finally. Her voice sounded strange to her — flat, procedural, a lawyer's voice. "For ten years. That's a prison term, Mara. That's — do you understand what that is?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Don't *ma'am* me. Not now." She pressed her palms flat against her knees. "Every word I ever wrote. Every doubt. Everything I put in letters because there was no one in this town I could say it to aloud. You were — you were the one person I thought was simply furniture. Reliable furniture. And the whole time—"

"The whole time I was the only one listening," he said. It was not defiance. That was the unbearable thing about it. He said it the way a man reports weather, or a wound. "Yes."

"Why?" It came out cracked. "Why would you—"

"Because you wrote to people about me." He said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Early on. Your cousin in the city, about whether a driver who couldn't read could be trusted with the accounts. Whether I seemed clever enough to be dangerous. You decided I wasn't, and after that I decided you should know what it costs to be underestimated. Only—" he paused, and rubbed the rag between his hands, "—only it stopped being about that almost immediately. After the first year. After Crane."

The name sat in the air between them like ice in a glass.

"You knew about Crane," Edith said slowly, "for nine years. And you said nothing. To me, to anyone."

"What would I have said?" For the first time something rose in his voice — not anger, exactly, but the pressure of a decade held down. "That the great Edith Vance takes money from the family that owns the mills she marches against? To whom? The town closes its bridge and holds its breath for five months, and you want the driver to be the one to break it? They'd have closed the workshop by spring. Forty women learning a trade, a press running, a paper going out — I chose that. Every year, I chose it again. I read your letters and I kept the silences in the right places and I told myself it was protection." He laughed, short and awful. "Maybe it was. Maybe it was cowardice wearing protection's coat. I've had ten years to wonder which."

Edith looked at him — really looked, the way she had not permitted herself in years, because looking closely at people invited questions she could not answer — and saw that he was fifty feet away and also closer to her than any living soul. He knew her letters the way a priest knows confessions. He had carried her doubts in his pockets. He had sealed them carefully with his own breath on the envelope glue and delivered her best self to the world while keeping her worst self locked in his head, and he had never once, in ten years, used it against her.

Betrayal, she thought. This is betrayal. So why does it feel like being *found*?

"There are copies missing," she said. Her voice was steadier now. Something had broken loose in her and the breaking had a strange steadiness to it, the calm after the dam goes. "Tilly's copies. You took them."

"I took them," he agreed. "She'll have noticed by now. She notices everything."

"What do you mean to do with them?"

"That depends," Mara said, "on the answer to a question I've wanted to ask you for nine years."

"Ask it, then. I think we've passed the point where either of us is owed privacy."

He came a step closer, into the full light, and she saw that his hands were shaking. Good, she thought, absurdly. Let us both shake. Let this be hard for both of us.

"Do you remember," he said, "the letter you wrote in October of ought-four? To your sister? You were at the mill gates all day, and the Crane boy — Aldous — sent his man with the first check, and you sat in the kitchen at midnight and wrote that you felt like a woman who had sold the river to buy a boat. Do you remember?"

She did remember. She remembered every word, because it was the truest thing she had written that decade and she had addressed it to the one person safe enough to receive it. Or so she had believed.

"I remember," she whispered.

"I've carried that letter for nine years," said Mara. "Carried it the way you carry the ledger. So here is my question, and I need you to answer it like I'm nobody, like I'm the paper itself." He drew a breath. "*Should you have taken the money?*"

And there it was. The question she had never dared ask anyone, never dared ask herself except at three in the morning with the wind at the windows — the question underneath every speech, every moderated resolution, every careful phrase she had chosen so as not to alarm the donors. She had built a career on not asking it.

She found that when it was finally asked — by her driver, in her carriage house, with the wind worrying its single note outside — the answer did not come as thunder. It came quietly, the way truth does when it has waited too long.

"No," Edith said. And then, because the mask was already on the floor between them and there was no dignity left to protect: "I tell myself yes. I tell myself forty women learned typesetting because of it, that the press ran, that the paper went out, that half-measures feed people while purity starves them. Some nights I even believe it. But Mara — I didn't take the money *for* the women. I took it because I was afraid of becoming small. Because a movement needs a leader and I wanted to be the leader more than I wanted to be right." She looked down at her bare hands, hands that had shaken thousands of hands, that had signed the compromise and the speeches both. "The workshop would have found another way. Slower. Hungrier. Honest. I chose fast and comfortable and I dressed it up as strategy, and I've known it was a lie since roughly the second week."

Mara made no move toward her. He only nodded slowly, twice, as though checking items off a list he had carried longer than she had.

"Tilly thinks the truth would free the movement," he said. "That if Crane's money is named — named by us, first, on our own terms — the women in that workshop stop being an extension of your bargain and start being their own thing. She may be right. She's usually right, which is the most irritating thing about her." He hesitated. "There's more. The copies I took — I didn't take them to destroy them."

"No?"

"I took them because tomorrow night, or the night after, someone has to decide what gets printed. And I've decided it shouldn't be you, and it shouldn't be me, and it shouldn't even be Tilly." His eyes met hers, and for a moment she saw the boy he must have been before invisibility taught him stillness. "It should be the women whose names are on the press. They're voting, Mrs. Vance. Tomorrow. On whether the truth comes out over your name or without it — or at all."

Edith sat very still. Through the window the river showed white between the warehouses, sealed and silent, holding whatever was given to it. Ten years of secrets, and now the decision was walking out of her hands entirely, into a room of typesetters and compositors who had never been asked to decide anything.

"And if they vote to publish with my name in it," she asked, "what would you have me do?"

Mara picked up his wrench, his ritual, his armor, and turned back to the engine that would not run until spring.

"Ma'am," he said gently, "I'd have you do the thing you've never done in ten years of letters."

"Which is?"

"Tell the truth out loud, to everyone, first — before the pamphlet can do it for you." He glanced back at her. "Because I promise you this. Tilly's proof won't stay in that drawer, and Crane's man hasn't left town, and when it breaks, it will break however *it* wants. The only choice left in any of this is whether you're standing behind the truth or under it when it lands."

She rose. She picked up her gloves and did not put them on. At the door she paused, the cold coming through the gap like a hand on her throat, and heard herself say the thing she had walked in knowing and not admitting:

"The copies weren't the only thing missing from that drawer, Mara."

He turned. "What else?"

"My resignation isn't written yet," said Edith Vance. "But it will be by morning — one way or the other. The question is whether I write it as a confession or a eulogy, and I find—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—I find I no longer trust myself to choose alone."

Behind her, in the carriage house, the lantern guttered once in the wind, and somewhere across the frozen river, in a lit window above the typesetting room, the women were already gathering.
