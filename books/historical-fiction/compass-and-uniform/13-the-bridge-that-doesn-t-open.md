## 13. The Bridge That Doesn't Open

The river had begun to lie.

Mara heard it first, before he saw it — a sound beneath the ice like a great door groaning on its hinges, low and patient, the sound of something that had held all winter deciding whether to keep holding. He stood at the foot of Landing Street with his collar up and his breath tearing away in rags, and he watched the ferryman, old Sorrel Hake, drive his iron bar down into the white plain of the river and listen to what came back.

It was not the answer Mara wanted.

"You hear that?" Sorrel didn't turn. He was a broad, weather-cured man with a beard gone the color of dirty wool, and he had worked this crossing for forty years, longer than Mara had been alive in any way that counted. "That's her talking. That's her saying she's thin."

"I need to cross," Mara said. "Tonight. Or first light."

"Everybody needs to cross." Sorrel drove the bar down again, leaned on it. Somewhere under their feet, thirty feet of black water moved against the underside of the world. "Mill men need to cross. Post needs to cross. That gentleman staying at the Marlowe House — *he* needs to cross, asks me every morning with a smile I don't care for. River doesn't care what anybody needs. She cares how thick she is, and she is getting thinner by the hour. Warm spell come up from the south two nights ago. You didn't feel it? Wind off the water like a wet cloth?"

Mara had felt it. He had lain awake in his room above the livery listening to the eaves drip, drip, drip, each drop a small betrayal, winter loosening its grip three weeks early. For ten years he had thought of the closed bridge as a wall, and walls could be hated because they were simple. Now he understood the season differently. It was not a wall. It was a lid, and lids lift.

"I have to reach people on the far shore," he said. "There are friends there. People who can help us if—" He stopped. If the workshop burned. If Crane's agent made good on whatever he was making good on. If the pamphlet the women had voted to publish needed hands beyond this town to carry it when spring came. He did not know anymore which of these he meant; perhaps all of them.

Sorrel finally turned and looked at him, and his eyes were kind in the tired way of men who have pulled bodies from rivers. "Okafor. You drive for Mrs. Vance. You've driven her through every kind of weather this town can manufacture. So you know what a road looks like when it's lying to you." He pointed with the iron bar at the ice road, at the staked line of brush that marked the crossing path where the current ran slower and the freeze went deepest. "Look at the color."

Mara looked. The ice near the far channel, which should have been the white of old snow, had gone the gray-green of bottle glass. And along the middle reach, where last week sledges had crossed singing, there was a seam of open water no wider than a hand — a black thread stitched through the white, breathing fog.

"When did that open?"

"This morning. By tomorrow it'll be a boat's width. By the day after, nobody crosses, and I mean nobody, until she freezes again or she breaks. And she isn't going to freeze again. You can smell spring under the fog if you're honest about your nose." Sorrel pulled the bar out of the ice and shouldered it. "So no. I won't take you out. Not you, not the mail, not God himself if he showed up asking polite. Last man I let talk me onto thin ice is still down there wearing his horse."

The fog lay along the river in long slow banks, and the far shore — that low dark line of trees and the smoke of towns where allies waited, where letters could be posted beyond the reach of this sealed valley — had dissolved entirely. Mara realized he could not have said which direction it was except by memory. The world had shrunk to the width of a voice carried over water.

"There has to be another way," he said. "Downstream? The shallows at Bend Rock—"

"Bend Rock is rapids under three inches of candle-ice. You'd last four minutes." Sorrel's voice gentled. "I know what's building over there in town. Word walks on ice even when men can't. You think I don't know who's been asking about the ferry schedule and writing things in a little book?" He meant Crane's agent; everyone meant Crane's agent now; the man had become a kind of weather. "You want my advice? Stop looking across the river. Whatever's coming, it's coming from inside this town, and whatever answers it has to answer with, they're inside too. There's no help coming over that ice. There never was going to be. There's just what's here."

He turned back toward his hut on the shore, its stovepipe bleeding thin smoke into the fog, and called over his shoulder: "And tell those women at the workshop — the ice takes its account same as everybody. Keep their lamps trimmed."

Mara stood a long while after he was gone. The river spoke beneath him, that low door-hinge groan, and for the first time all winter he did not feel the claustrophobia of entrapment but something colder and clearer, like a hand laid flat on the back of his neck. No rescue. No letters out, no letters in, no train beyond the hills, no authority beyond the fog. The whole apparatus of the outside world — the courts, the party committees, the distant editors who might have shielded them, the far-shore friends whose names he knew only from ten years of other people's correspondence — all of it might as well have been on the moon.

Whatever happened next would happen here, among these streets, these mills, these women with ink-stained fingers, this failing ice.

He turned up toward the town.

* * *

The Marlowe House stood at the corner of Mill and Fourth, a hotel gone slightly to seed since the mills had eaten the better class of guest, and its lobby windows glowed amber through the fog like the eyes of something watching. Mara did not go in. He had learned his trade in doorways and lobbies, in the art of being furniture, and so he simply stood at the hitching rail across the street, pretending to see to nothing, and let the man come out to him.

Crane's agent came out because agents always come out; they cannot bear an audience they cannot see.

His name was Pell, and he had arrived in November with good boots and a bad smile, and had missed the bridge closing by the same caprice of weather that had delivered Tilly across it. He was stranded here like everyone else, and it had made him something worse than dangerous — it had made him impatient. Impatience, Mara had learned, was the quality that turned money into violence.

"Mr. Okafor." Pell came down the hotel steps and stood in the street, hands in the pockets of a coat that cost more than the workshop's press. Fog beaded on his hat brim. "Still playing lamppost, I see. You're very good at it. Ten years, isn't it? A man could develop quite an attachment to a view."

Mara said nothing. The old reflexes were strong — silence, deference, the slight bow of the head that made him smaller. But he noticed, with a sensation like a bone setting, that the reflexes were weaker than they had been a month ago. Since the vote in the typesetting room. Since the night he set his own confession in lead.

"The thing about ferries," Pell went on pleasantly, "is that schedules change. Ice breaks early, ice breaks late. A flexible man might get word across the river by April. Might get names into the papers over there. Might get certain accounts settled favorably." He smiled. "A flexible man might also find that buildings are flammable in any season. Terrible fire code, this town. As your Mrs. Vance already knows — or does she? Hard to keep track of what she knows."

"The fire marshal ruled the press fire an accident with wire insulation," Mara said evenly.

"The fire marshal rules what makes sense for a town that lives on Crane payroll." Pell took a step closer, and lowered his voice to the register men use when they believe themselves generous. "Here's the shape of it. The pamphlet doesn't print. The ledger goes back in a drawer. Mrs. Vance gets a graceful season and a quiet retirement somewhere south, which is more than she deserves. And you — you keep driving somebody, anywhere, invisible as ever. Everybody keeps exactly what they had. That's all I'm offering. Continuity."

"And if we don't?"

Pell looked past him, up the hill, toward where the workshop's windows glowed above the rooftops — lamplight in the typesetting room, late, the silhouettes of women moving behind frosted glass like figures in a lantern slide. His face did something almost wistful.

"Then in April, when the ice goes out and the boats run and the trains move, a great many things arrive in this town at once," he said. "Lawyers. Inspectors. Men from the insurance company with questions about fires. Money moves fast in spring, Mr. Okafor. Faster than rivers." He turned back. "But I'll tell you a secret, since you're the one who reads everyone's mail." He let that sit between them like a blade laid flat on a table, and Mara felt his own face refuse to react, ten years of practice holding, barely. "Spring is far away. Winter isn't done with anyone yet. Accidents don't need bridges."

He touched his hat brim and walked back into the amber glow of the Marlowe House, and the door closed behind him with the softness of a well-oiled threat.

Mara stood in the street. The fog pressed close. Somewhere downhill the river groaned again, longer this time, and he heard — or imagined he heard — a crack travel the length of the valley, sharp as a rifle, followed by the small collapsing whisper of ice settling into a new opinion of itself.

*Accidents don't need bridges.*

He walked up the hill fast, past the shuttered mill gates, past the saloon where mill men drank and lately had grown quiet when the workshop women passed — a different quiet than before, less contempt, more consideration, the quiet of men reading pamphlets by stove light. The exposé wasn't printed yet. But the grievances in it — the wage cuts, the speed-ups, the girl's hand mangled in the carding room last autumn and written off as carelessness — those grievances were already loose in the town like sparks ahead of a fire, and everyone knew who'd struck the flint.

The workshop's door was unlocked, because it had been unlocked since the vote; they had decided a press with something to hide bolted its doors, and a press with nothing to hide did not.

Inside, the warmth hit him like a wall — coal stove, glue pot, the smell of ink and hot metal, and six women bent over the stone setting the final pages by lamplight. Tilly looked up first. She read his face the way she read everything, fast and without mercy.

"No crossing," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Not tonight. Not this week. Maybe not till the break-up." Mara unwound his scarf, and his hands, he noticed, were steady — steadier than they had any right to be. "Sorrel says the river's done carrying anyone. We're sealed. Completely. There's no letter going out, no ally coming in, no committee, no lawyer, no newspaper from the city. Until the thaw, it's us."

Silence around the type case. One of the older women, Mrs. Adeyemi from the spinning floor, set down her composing stick with the deliberateness of a woman laying down a tool she means to pick up again.

"Then it's us," she said, as if confirming an appointment.

"He came to see you," Tilly said to Mara. "Pell. I could tell from across the room. What did he offer?"

"Continuity. Everything stays as it was. The pamphlet dies quietly, Edith retires gracefully, and I stay invisible." Mara heard his own voice say the next part plainly, without decoration, the way he had decided to say everything now. "And if we refuse — lawyers and inspectors and accidents in the spring. And accidents sooner, if he's feeling efficient."

Nobody gasped. He had expected fear — he had spent ten years expecting fear on behalf of other people, curating it, sealing it in envelopes. Instead the women looked at one another in the lamplight with an expression he recognized only slowly, because he had never been permitted to wear it himself: the look of people taking a measurement, not of the danger, but of themselves.

"We knew this was the price when we voted," said Nell Brandt — no relation to Tilly, a compositor with forearms like cable. "Sealed town, angry money, no witnesses coming. We voted anyway."

"We voted to publish," Mrs. Adeyemi corrected gently. "Nobody voted on burning."

"Then let's vote on that too," Tilly said. "Tomorrow. Tonight, we finish the forms."

But Mara was shaking his head, and the strangest feeling was rising in him — not dread, though dread was there, but something underneath dread, load-bearing. "No," he said. "No more votes on whether we're afraid. We already answered that. From tonight we do one thing at a time and we do it in the open, the way we said. Finish the pages. Print Thursday. And meanwhile—" he looked around at their faces, ink-smudged and resolute, at Tilly watching him with something that had softened, in recent weeks, into respect "—meanwhile nobody walks home alone. Nobody. Not to the boarding houses, not to the mill rows. In pairs, lamps lit, the long way if the long way is busier. Pell doesn't need a bridge to burn a building. He needs us scattered."

Mrs. Adeyemi picked her composing stick back up. "Then it's a good thing," she said, "that we stopped being scattered some time ago."

They bent to the stone. Mara took the place they kept for him at the far case — his place, they had made it his place sometime in February without remarking on it, and he had crossed the room to it every night since with the sensation of a man walking into his own name. He began setting lines. *The undersigned, having served ten years as courier...*

Outside, the fog thickened until the window gave back only their own lamplight, and below it all, faint but ceaseless now, the river worked at its seams — groaning, cracking, keeping its account — as if the whole sealed valley were one enormous pressure waiting to be released, and everyone in the workshop, typing steadily through the night, were the words being forced up through it.

Near midnight, footsteps on the stair — quick, uneven, half-running. Mara's body knew trouble before his mind did; he was between the door and the press before the knock came.

The door opened on Edith Vance.

She stood in the doorway with snow still on her shoulders and her breath ragged from the climb, and in her hands, held out before her like evidence at a trial, was a bundle of letters tied in the old blue ribbon he knew better than his own heartbeat — the ribbon he had retied ten thousand times in lamplight. Her face was pale and very calm, the calm of someone who has passed through fear into decision on the walk over.

"Mara," she said. "We need to talk before Thursday. About what happens when they read my name aloud."

Behind him, the type fell silent, and every woman in the room turned to listen.
