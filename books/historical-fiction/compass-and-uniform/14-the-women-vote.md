## 14. The Women Vote

Tilly had learned one thing in three winters of organizing, and she had learned it the hard way, standing on a soapbox in a mill town not unlike this one while a man twice her size explained patiently that the ladies inside would decide nothing because he, the foreman, would decide for them: *if you want to know what people will risk, don't ask their leaders. Ask at the washline.*

Edith Vance would decide to publish. Tilly was nearly certain of it — certain enough that she had stopped being able to sleep for the certainty. Edith would decide it the way she decided everything, with her chin lifted and her gloves smoothed and some noble phrase about sacrifice, and she would decide it *alone*, and the decision would be wrong, or right, but either way it would belong to her and not to the forty women who fed the press its paper.

And Mara — Mara would do what Mara always did. He would hold the door. He would carry the satchel. He would stand in exactly the right place to be useful and invisible, and if you asked him afterward whether he had chosen anything, he would look at you with those careful grey eyes and say, *I drove where I was asked to drive.*

No. She was done asking leaders.

* * *

She caught the women where they could not be caught by anyone else: at the end of the day shift, when the typesetting room smelled of ink and cold iron and the stove had been fed for the night, when hands were still black with lead and voices were loose with the particular giddiness of women who had worked twelve hours and were about to walk home through the dark together.

"Ten minutes," Tilly said, standing on the composing stone so she could see all their faces. "I'm asking for ten minutes and your silence after."

Josie Hark, the forewoman of the case room, folded her arms. Josie was thirty-four, missing two fingers on her left hand from a carding machine she'd escaped six years ago, and she had the habit of looking at Tilly the way a mason looks at a wall built by someone else — measuring, not unkindly, how much would hold. "Ten minutes of what?"

"Of the question nobody's put to you."

The room settled. Tilly counted them without meaning to anymore — she'd been counting women in rooms since she was nineteen. Thirty-one tonight. The Hendricks sisters near the window. Old Mrs. Prewitt, who set type faster than girls half her age and claimed her eyes were better too and was lying. Ruth Ellery, who had come to the workshop two years ago with a bruise she never explained and now ran the bindery like a small navy. The Fenn girl, sixteen, who still flinched at loud noises from the press.

"I've spent six weeks going through ledgers," Tilly said. "You know that. You've watched me doing it and most of you have politely not asked why. I'm going to ask why no longer." She laid it out flat, no ornament: the Crane money, eleven years of it, routed through a shipping concern and a false name; the letters between Edith's solicitor and Crane's agent; the quiet softening of the movement's language in exactly the seasons the payments arrived; the fire in the typesetting room and the man seen asking questions at the ferry before the ice closed the river. She did not name Mara. She did not name what she suspected about Mara. Some debts you keep in your own pocket until you know what they're worth.

When she finished, the silence had a texture to it, like felt.

"So," Josie said at last. "Mrs. Vance has been eating out of the Cranes' hand."

"Mrs. Vance took money that came from the men she stands up and condemns," Tilly said carefully, "and the question of what she knew, and when, and what she traded for it — that's part of what the pamphlet would say. All of it. Her bargain and his name in the same column."

"And if we print it?" That was Ruth Ellery, arms crossed, chin down. "Crane's man is already here. He's stuck here same as us till thaw, but stuck men get desperate. We print it, we're not a workshop anymore. We're a target with a roof."

"We're already a target," Tilly said. "The fire wasn't the stove. You all know it wasn't the stove."

Nobody argued. They had all privately known it, the way households privately know things.

"Here's what I've decided not to decide," Tilly said, and she heard her own voice go steady in the way it only went steady when she was most frightened. "This isn't Mrs. Vance's choice. It isn't mine. It isn't Mara Okafor's, though God knows he carries more of this place than any of us credit him for. It's yours. This press runs because you run it. Your names aren't on anything yet — that's been someone else's decision too, keeping you nameless, keeping you safe, and I'm telling you I think safe and nameless have started costing more than they pay. So we vote. Publish the truth, all of it, under our own masthead — the Women's Cooperative Press, our names or no names, but ours. Or we stay quiet, keep setting wedding announcements and wage receipts till the ice goes out, and hand what I found back to the two people who've been circling it for weeks and let them manage it. Either way, majority rules, and nobody gets to be sacrificed by somebody else's conscience. Not even for the cause."

"The cause," said old Mrs. Prewitt, dry as a stamp, "has a great many people willing to be sacrificed in it, and remarkably few willing to ask the sacrificed first."

There was laughter — short, surprised, real.

"How do we vote?" asked the Fenn girl, and then blushed at having spoken.

"Paper," Josie said immediately. "No hands. No looking at each other's hands." She was already pulling slips of the scrap paper from the cutter's bin. "Two boxes. Ink pot for publish. Chalk mark for quiet. Fold them small. Nobody watches anybody."

They voted the way they did everything in that room: efficiently, in the smell of ink, shoulders touching.

Tilly stood apart while they folded. She watched Mara, without seeming to, where he sat in the corner with the delivery ledger open on his knee, pretending to check figures he surely knew by heart. He had not spoken. When she had come in and announced her ten minutes, he had risen halfway out of his chair — and then, catching whatever was in her face, sat back down and made himself into furniture. She had meant to exclude him and discovered, watching him fold himself smaller, that excluding him was a kind of cruelty, and that he received it like something he'd ordered. Later, she thought. There would be time later to tell him: *you are not furniture, and I am sorry, and also I don't trust you yet, and both of those things can be true.*

Edith was not there. Edith was across town drafting what Tilly knew would be a measured, courageous, entirely solitary statement about the future of the movement. Tilly had left before reading it. She was tired of reading things first.

* * *

Josie and Ruth counted. They counted twice, then a third time because Josie said the first two counts didn't agree and Ruth said they agreed fine and Josie said *we count again*, which told Tilly everything about how close it was before anyone said a number aloud.

Seventeen to fourteen.

For a moment nobody moved. Seventeen to fourteen was not a mandate. Seventeen to fourteen was a room split almost exactly down its own middle, four women who would walk home tonight afraid of what they'd chosen, and seventeen who had chosen it anyway, and Tilly understood suddenly that this narrow, ugly, honest margin was worth more than any unanimous resolution she'd ever drafted, because unanimity was usually just fear wearing its Sunday clothes.

"That's it, then," Ruth said quietly. She looked at her chalk-marked slip, the one vote against her own pamphlet, and squared it with the others. "We publish."

"On our masthead," Josie said. "Not hers." She said it without venom, which somehow made it heavier. "Whatever Mrs. Vance decides to do — denounce us, claim us, stand up tomorrow and make a speech of it — the imprint says *Women's Cooperative Press of Harrow's Crossing*. Let history wonder who she was to us. Let it read the thing itself."

"The Fenn girl can cut the masthead block," Ruth said. "She's got the steadiest hand."

"I do," said the Fenn girl, pink and blazing.

Then the room did the thing Tilly would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would try and fail to describe to organizers in other towns in other years: it simply began to work. Josie started assigning pages — Crane's shipping concern to Ruth, who had a cousin in the freight office; the ledger facsimiles to Mrs. Prewitt, whose memory of every rate the mills had paid for job work over fifteen years was itself a kind of evidence; the affidavit from the washerwoman who'd carried Crane's letters to be safekeeping, to Tilly. No speeches. No resolutions. Thirty-one women turned into a newspaper the way water turns into ice: all at once, and because the temperature demanded it.

Mara closed his ledger. He stood, and for once he did not make himself small on his way to the door; he crossed the room to Tilly and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that only she could hear.

"Seventeen to fourteen," he said.

"You were listening."

"I listen. It's what I do." Something moved behind the grey of his eyes — she would think, later, that it was the first unguarded thing she'd ever seen in his face. "You should know that if it had gone the other way, I'd have helped you publish alone. I decided that walking the river last week. I just — hadn't found the door into it."

"The door was never yours to find," Tilly said, but she said it gently. "It was theirs."

"Yes," he said, and it sounded like relief and grief sharing a coat. "I'll drive wherever you need, starting tomorrow. There are eleven houses on the ice road that take in strangers. I know every one."

He went out into the dark with his collar up, and Tilly stood a moment longer among the cases and the smell of lead, listening to the workshop breathe.

Fourteen against. She would not forget them either. Two of the fourteen came to her before they left — one to say she'd vote against it again but would set type for it if asked, and one, the youngest Hendricks sister, who gripped Tilly's wrist hard and said, "My father works for Crane. When it prints, I need to not be home that evening." Tilly wrote down an address on the ice road and put it in the girl's palm and closed the fingers over it, and understood that this, too, was what publishing cost, paid in units no ledger recorded.

* * *

She walked to Edith's lodging house at nine, through streets where the snow had frozen into gray glass and every lamp wore a halo of ice fog. She rehearsed sentences and discarded them. *Mrs. Vance, we have voted.* *Mrs. Vance, you are no longer the decider.* None of them were survivable as spoken, so she settled on the plainest.

Edith opened the door herself, still dressed, letters spread on the table behind her, and Tilly saw at once that she already knew — of course she knew; thirty-one women cannot keep a secret, and Edith Vance had ears in this town the way rivers have tributaries.

"Well," Edith said. There were lines around her mouth that hadn't been there in autumn. "Come in, Miss Brandt. I imagine you've come to tell me my movement has outvoted me."

"Not your movement," Tilly said, stepping over the threshold into the lamplight. "That's rather the point."

Something crossed Edith's face — anger, and beneath the anger, unmistakably, relief, arriving like a thief at its own trial. She poured tea without asking, and her hand was not steady, and she said:

"Then you'd better tell me what my name looks like in your columns. Because I've spent eleven years wondering, and I find I'd rather read it than sleep."

Behind them, through the frost-furred window, the lights of the workshop burned past midnight for the first time all winter.
