## 15. Setting the Type

The workshop smelled of coal oil and cold iron when Tilly barred the door behind us at half past nine, but the cold was the least of what we were shutting out. She turned the key in the lock of the typesetting room — a room that had never before been locked, because no one had ever thought there was anything inside worth stealing or defending — and then she stood with her back against the door for a moment, breathing hard, as if she had run all the way from the river though I knew she had only come up from the dormitory stairs.

"Nobody comes in," she said. "Not Mrs. Vance, not anyone, until we say."

I looked around at the women already gathered there. Hester Kline, who set type faster than any man the *Courier* had ever employed and said so at least weekly. The Brontë sisters — not sisters at all, but called that because they'd arrived on the same mill shift nine years ago and refused ever to be separated — Ruth and Ada, hands stained permanently grey with ink. Old Marta, who had worked a composing stick since before any of us could read her own name in print, and who now sat at the stone like a judge taking her bench. Eleven women in all, and me.

They had voted three hours ago. I had stood at the back of the workshop hall with my cap in my hands and watched them raise their hands one by one — seven to four — and I had felt something break loose in my chest that I could not yet name. It was not joy. Joy is light. This was heavy, like lead itself, like a form locked in the chase, ready to be printed and impossible afterward to unprint.

"All right," Tilly said, and pulled the manuscript from her satchel. "From the top."

* * *

We began, as all honest things should begin, with the money.

Tilly had copied out the ledger pages in her small, upright hand — Aldous Crane's disbursements across eleven years, routed through a shipping concern in the capital and laundered through two names neither of which belonged to him — and it was Hester who set the first line. She read aloud as she worked, because Marta insisted on it: *"The committee which has presented itself to this town as the Women's Franchise League has received, since 1902, the sum of four thousand and sixty pounds from Mr. Aldous Crane of Crane Textiles."*

"Colder," Marta said. "Your ems are too tight. Money should sit loose on the page. Let the reader see the spaces between the figures. That's where the shame lives."

Hester adjusted her spacing without argument, which told me how seriously they were all taking this, because Hester never adjusted anything without argument.

I took my place at the second stone, where the correspondence was to be set — Edith's letters, or the relevant portions of them, the ones in which she acknowledged the source of the funds and weighed it against the cost of refusing. I had carried those letters. I had sealed those envelopes with my own breath over the flap, ten years of breath, and here was the paper laid open under the lamp, and here was I, setting it into lead.

My hands did not shake. I noticed that. I had expected them to shake and they did not, and somehow their steadiness frightened me more than trembling would have.

Ruth Brontë worked beside me, setting the affidavit of the witness — a former clerk in Crane's shipping office named Prewitt, whom Tilly had found through the train-side network of organizers that ran, invisible as frost, from one closed river town to the next. Ruth glanced at my letters more than once. Not at the type — at my handwriting, the fair copy I had made for the compositors to work from.

"You write a very careful hand," she said.

"I've had practice being careful."

She didn't smile. None of us smiled much that night. There was a quality to the work — I can describe it no better than that — a quality of held breath. Every galley we filled was a step further from the shore of the life we had all been living, and everyone of us knew it, and everyone of us kept setting type.

* * *

Past midnight the stove burned low and Ada fed it without being asked, and the light in the room went orange and moved along the ranks of type cases like firelight on armor. Somewhere outside, the wind came down the river valley and pressed against the windows, and the ice answered it with that long groaning sound it makes in deep winter, the sound of a thing too large to be comfortable in its own bed.

Marta stopped work and listened to it, her composing stick resting on the stone.

"My husband used to say the ice talks to itself at night," she said. "Keeping its courage up."

"Does it need courage?" Ada asked.

"Everything frozen needs courage," Marta said. "It knows it has to break someday. That's the whole bargain of being ice. You hold, and you know holding ends, and you hold anyway." She went back to her stick. "Now stop gossiping and set your lines."

It was after that, in the smallest hours, that Tilly came to stand at my shoulder.

"The confession goes in tonight," she said quietly. "If you're going to write it, it goes in tonight. We lock the forme at dawn."

I had known it was coming. Since the vote, I had known. Tilly had not asked me twice; she had made her ultimatum on the ice road days ago and I had given my answer by walking into this room tonight, and we both understood that the answer included this last piece. But understanding a thing and sitting down before a blank sheet of paper to do it are different countries entirely, with no bridge between them.

I took the paper. I took the pen. The lamp hissed.

* * *

Here is what I wrote, or began to write, four times, tearing each attempt into pieces small enough to swallow:

*My name is Mara Okafor, and for ten years I was Mrs. Edith Vance's driver and courier.*

Ten years. Ten winters of this town, ten closings of the bridge, ten springs of watching the far shore come back as if the world beyond it were a rumor that kept proving itself true. Ten years of reading other people's words by lamplight and sealing away whatever I found there. I had learned every silence the way a ferryman learns currents — where it was safe to cross, where the water ran thin over rocks, where a man could drown quietly without anyone hearing the splash.

I knew about Aldous Crane before Tilly Brandt was old enough to vote. I knew that the money that paid the workshop's rent and kept the press turning and bought coal for this stove came from the same family whose mills swallowed twelve hours a day out of the lives of the women now setting type around me. I knew, and I chose the press over the truth, and I told myself every winter that the choice was mercy — that a running workshop with a hidden wound was better than an honest corpse. Maybe it was. I have stopped believing that I was the person entitled to make that calculation.

The women at the cases worked on. Hester's fingers moved among the type like a pianist's. Ruth read Prewitt's affidavit aloud in a low voice, checking it word by word against the lead, and her voice never wavered even at the ugly parts — the parts where Crane's man described, in clerk's language, the arrangement by which the League would moderate its rhetoric about mill conditions in exchange for continued funding. *Moderate its rhetoric.* Four words, set in lead now, that meant: *be quiet about the lint in your lungs, the locked doors, the wage docked for sitting down.*

Ada caught me looking at them and misread it entirely, or else read it perfectly.

"Set your sentence, courier," she said gently. "We'll carry you. That's what a family does."

So I wrote it. All of it. That I had read every letter. That I had known of the bargain since my third year in Edith Vance's service. That I had altered nothing — I want that on the record, I wrote, I sealed what she wrote and delivered what she sealed — but that knowing and saying nothing is its own kind of alteration, that a town is edited by every silence kept inside it. That when the bridge closed last autumn I had a decade of truth folded in my coat and I chose, once more, to keep it folded, until the women of this workshop took the choice out of my hands, which was the first kindness anyone had done me in years.

My pen scratched. The stove ticked. The ice groaned its long patience into the dark.

When I finished, my hand was steady still, but my eyes were not, and I sat for a moment letting the lamp blur, grieving — I recognized it at last — grieving the man I might have been if I had spoken in year three instead of year ten. A man with a shorter history and a lighter coat. A man whose name strangers would have known without needing a pamphlet to learn it.

Tilly took the pages from me without ceremony, which was the greatest ceremony available. She read them standing, by the lamp, her face giving back the orange light and nothing else. Then she sat at the case herself and began setting my confession in eight-point Caslon, the body face we had chosen for the exposé, and I watched my ten years go into lead letter by letter, backwards, mirror-wise, the way all truths must be set before they can be read.

"That's the thing about type," Marta said, watching me watch. Her voice was soft, for her, which meant it barely reached the far wall. "You have to set it wrong to make it read right. Everything important is backwards first. Remember that."

* * *

Dawn was still an hour off when the forme was full and locked — four pages, tied and planed, heavier than any of us could lift alone, so we lifted it together, six pairs of hands on the iron, and carried it to the bed of the press that had survived the fire with scorched paint and a bent feed-board but a heart still true.

That was when someone knocked at the outer door.

Three knocks. Even. Not frantic, not the knock of the agent's hired men, which we had all begun to dread in our separate cells of fear. A patient knock. A familiar one.

Everyone looked at everyone. The bar was across the door and Tilly's hand went to it and paused there, asking the room.

"It's for me, I expect," I said, though I didn't believe it, and I don't think anyone else believed it either. Nothing in that knock belonged to me.

"Nobody comes in," Tilly repeated, but she didn't move her hand, and the knock came again, and beneath the door, under the gap where the lamplight spilled into the corridor, I saw a shadow settle itself — unhurried, straight-backed, gloved hands folded — the shadow of a woman who intended to wait exactly as long as waiting required.

The press stood loaded. My confession lay locked in the forme, backwards and final. And Edith Vance, who had not been invited, who had been published over and named within, stood on the other side of four inches of oak in the blue hour before dawn, and knocked a third time.

"We hear you, Mrs. Vance," Marta called out, in no hurry at all. "But the press doesn't run till sunrise, and what runs in it isn't ours alone to undo. So you'll forgive us if we take another minute."

The shadow didn't move. The stove ticked. Tilly looked at me, and I looked at the forme, and somewhere below us the river shifted its weight and spoke.
