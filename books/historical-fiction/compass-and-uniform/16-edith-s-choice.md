## 16. Edith's Choice

The proof sheet lay on her desk like something dead that still had a pulse.

Edith Vance had read ten thousand pages in her life — parliamentary reports, hostile editorials, letters from women whose husbands had put them out in the snow for attending a meeting. She had learned to read the way a soldier crosses open ground: quickly, low to the facts, expecting fire. But she could not make herself turn past the first page of this.

*THE CRANE MONEY: AN ACCOUNTING*, said the masthead. No name beneath it but the workshop's. Not hers. They had published over her, without her, and the absence of her name was worse than an accusation would have been. It was an amputation. Ten years of speeches, of cold station platforms and colder drawing rooms, of standing upright while men laughed into their cuffs — and here it was, set in lead, the whole architecture of her life laid bare by other hands, and her own hands were not even on it.

She turned the page.

There was Aldous Crane's ledger, copied line for line. There was the letter she had written him in the spring of 1906, when the workshop's rent came due and the press was three weeks from being sold for scrap — *Dear Mr. Crane, I write not as an advocate but as a practical woman…* She remembered writing it. She remembered the rain on the window of the post office, and how her hand had not shaken until after the envelope was sealed. And there, further down, was the sentence she had composed herself, in her own mind, a hundred times over nine winters and never once spoken aloud:

*Mrs. Vance accepted funds from mill ownership while publicly campaigning against mill conditions.*

True. Every word. She had built a career on the difference between what was true and what was sayable, and now the two had been collapsed into a single line of type, and there was nowhere left to stand.

She heard the press running downstairs. That was the thing she could not get past — the press was still running, printing more copies, the women working in shifts through the afternoon as though this were any other pamphlet, as though the words were not eating her alive one line at a time. The rhythm of it came up through the floorboards like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

A knock. She knew the interval of it — two soft raps, a pause, Mara's fist never quite committing to the third.

"Come in."

He stood in the doorway with his coat still on, holding his cap, looking — for the first time in ten years — as though he did not know where to put himself in her room. Behind him, in the corridor, Tilly Brandt watched with her arms folded, and did not pretend otherwise.

"You've seen it," Mara said.

"I've seen it." Edith kept her voice level. She had kept her voice level through hecklers and magistrates and one memorable evening in 1909 when a man had thrown a herring at her from a balcony. "I'd ask who authorized this, but I suspect the answer is a room full of people I taught to authorize themselves."

"Yes," Mara said.

It was not a deflection or an apology. It was simply agreement, offered plainly, and she found she had no reply prepared for plainness. She had spent a decade preparing replies. No one had ever just agreed with her before.

"There will be a meeting tonight," he said. "In the typesetting room. The whole town's been told."

"The whole town." She looked at the proof sheet again. "And what do they expect me to do there?"

"I don't know," Mara said. "But you should decide before you arrive. Whatever you decide."

He put his cap back on and left, and Tilly went with him, and Edith sat alone with the dead pulsing thing on her desk and listened to the press below her window counting out her ruin in copies of four hundred per hour.

* * *

She had been twenty-nine when she understood what compromise cost, though at the time she had called it arithmetic.

The workshop had been dying. Not metaphorically — actually dying, the way institutions die, which is quietly and by invoice. Rent overdue twice. The type founders wanting payment upfront. Eleven women learning a trade that would vanish if the door closed, and Edith lying awake doing sums that always ended in the same place: *the press goes to auction, the women go back to the mills, and you will have taught them just enough to know exactly what they lost.*

Crane had come to her himself. She had expected a lawyer, a go-between, an envelope slid across a table. Instead Aldous Crane — silver-haired, courteous, smelling faintly of coal smoke and good wool — had sat in her cramped office among the pamphlets condemning his family's mills and said, "Mrs. Vance, I'd like to keep your workshop open."

"Why?"

"Because a movement that argues is manageable. A movement that starves is dangerous. Starving people don't argue; they burn things." He had smiled then, and it had been, horribly, a kind smile. "You want the truth? Your rhetoric costs me nothing. Your silence would cost me everything. So I'll pay for the first, and we'll both pretend the second isn't part of the price."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in six weeks I'll buy this building at auction and turn it into a storeroom, and eleven women will walk back through my father's gates, and neither of us will have clean hands either." He had stood, straightened his coat. "You're not choosing between clean and dirty, Mrs. Vance. You're choosing which dirt you can live inside."

She had taken the money. She had told herself it was for the women, and some nights that was even true. Other nights she knew it was also for the platform, the crowds, the particular electricity of five hundred faces going quiet when she stepped forward — and those nights were rarer only because she rationed them so carefully.

The moderation had come in increments so small each one seemed reasonable. A speech softened here. A mill owner's name removed from a list there. An endorsement withheld, once, from a strike she privately believed in. Each concession bought another year of the workshop; every year the concessions grew heavier, like a coat sewn onto the body one stitch at a time until you could no longer tell where the tailoring ended and the skin began.

And through all of it, there was Mara. Driving her. Carrying her letters. Sealing them and delivering them and saying almost nothing, ever, in ten years. She had thought, in her vanity, that his silence was loyalty. Now, reading the proof sheet with its careful, damning accuracy — dates right, amounts right, even the rain on the post office window rendered somehow between the lines — she understood that his silence had been knowledge. He had known. Perhaps he had always known. And he had driven her anyway, every morning, through streets full of people whose wages her bargain helped hold down.

What did one call that? Treachery? Devotion? She suspected it was the same substance wearing different coats, and that she was the last person alive qualified to distinguish them.

* * *

At four o'clock she did the thing she had been avoiding all day. She went downstairs.

The typesetting room smelled of ink and hot metal and the tallow candles the women burned because the gaslight was bad near the composing stones. It was crowded — compositors, bindery girls, two of the older folders with flour still on their aprons from the noon bread, Tilly at the center of it with a galley proof in each hand like a surgeon holding instruments. The talk stopped when Edith appeared. It stopped completely, the way weather stops.

She had faced hostile rooms before. This was not hostile. Hostility she knew how to answer; hostility was a form of engagement. This was something else — forty women watching her the way one watches a verdict being written.

"Mrs. Vance." Tilly's chin came up. "We didn't expect you down here."

"I imagine not." Edith walked to the center of the room because she refused to speak from a doorway like a visitor in her own building. "How many copies?"

"Six hundred printed. More by dark. Mara's arranging distribution along the ice road starting tomorrow — the towns on the far shore, the mill villages upriver."

"So it cannot be recalled."

"No," Tilly said. "It can't."

Edith nodded slowly. Part of her — the part that had managed campaigns for fifteen years — noted, with clinical disgust at itself, that the distribution plan was excellent.

"Then let me ask the question you're too honest to enjoy," she said. "Do you want me to denounce this? Because I can. Tomorrow I stand on the steps and call it forgery and factionalism, and half this town believes me, and the workshop splits down the middle, and by spring there are two movements in this valley tearing at each other while Crane reads the reports and smiles. Or—" she paused, and found the pause was real, not rhetorical, "—or you want the other thing. And I need to hear someone say it to my face."

Silence. Then one of the old folder women — Berta, who had worked the mills thirty years before her hands gave out — spoke without raising her hand, the way she said whatever she thought whenever she thought it.

"We don't want you to denounce it, Mrs. Vance. We want you to mean it the other way."

"The other way."

"To stand up tonight and say it's true," Berta said. "All of it. Out loud. With your name attached, since ours wouldn't fit on the masthead."

"That would end my career."

"Yes," Berta said. "Probably."

"It would hand Crane the greatest victory of his life."

"He's had victories every year of my life," Berta said. "I'd like to see him lose one, even an ugly one."

Edith looked around the room. Young faces and old ones, ink-stained fingers, a girl of perhaps seventeen gripping a composing stick like a weapon. None of them looked away from her. That was new. Crowds always looked at her; rooms always watched her; but these women were not watching. They were *waiting* — the way one waits for weather to break, or a debt to be called in.

They had already decided. The vote had happened, she later learned, days ago, without her. What they wanted now was not permission. It was witness.

"I need the afternoon," she said.

Tilly studied her for a long moment. "You have it. The meeting's at seven."

* * *

She walked to the river alone, which was foolish, and she knew it was foolish, and she went anyway. The town was sealed in its white silence; the bridge stood closed and furred with frost, and the river beneath ran black between shelves of ice. Crane's agent was somewhere in town — stranded like everyone else, Mara had reported, pacing the hotel parlor like a fox in a box — but even he would not follow a woman onto the ice road at dusk.

She stood at the bank where the ferryman tied up and looked across at the far shore, invisible now behind fog and frozen spray, and understood with sudden clarity that she had spent fifteen years speaking to that far shore — to parliament, to the newspapers, to history, to the great gray audience of the future — and had never once, in all that time, said anything true to the forty people in the room upstairs.

That was the compromise, she saw now. Not the money. The money had merely priced it. The true compromise was this: she had made herself legible to everyone and known to no one. Five hundred audiences had heard her convictions. Not one living soul had heard her doubts — except, perhaps, a driver who read her mail, and whom she had never once asked a direct question in ten years, because asking might invite an answer.

Well. She had questions now, and no time left to be delicate with them.

*The movement*, she thought, standing on the ice-scoured bank while the light failed, *was never clean. It was never going to be clean.* The suffrage cause had been funded by temperance money and inheritance money and Crane money; its marches had been protected sometimes by police who beat strikers in other streets; its heroes had mortgages on their souls just like ordinary households. The myth of purity was the enemy — hers most of all, because she had embodied it, traded on it, polished it nightly like brass. And a myth of purity could be punctured with a single ledger page. But honesty — honesty could take the wound and keep walking.

*No movement is clean,* she thought, testing the sentence in her mind the way she tested all her sentences, turning it, weighing it, hearing it in the acoustics of a hall. *Only honest or dishonest.*

Yes. That was the speech. That was the whole of it, and everything else was scaffolding.

She turned back toward town, toward the lamplight of the workshop, and her step — she noticed it with something like amusement — had changed. For fifteen years she had walked to platforms the way one walks to a scaffold or a coronation, gathering herself, armoring up. Tonight she was walking the way she had walked at nineteen, to her first meeting, knowing nothing, certain of only one thing: that the truth was worth the discomfort of saying it. Everything since had been, in one way or another, forgetting that.

She stopped once, at the corner by the shuttered mill offices, and looked up at the dark windows of Crane & Sons. Somewhere in that building was a ledger that matched the one downstairs line for line. Somewhere in this town was a man who believed he had purchased her silence permanently, and who was about to learn, along with everyone else, exactly what his purchase had bought and what it had failed to buy.

"Mr. Crane," she said aloud, to nobody, to the frost, to the sealed season, "you bought my moderation. You never bought my conscience. I'd simply misplaced it."

Her breath hung in the air a moment and dispersed, and she laughed — short, unlovely, relieved — the first laugh she could remember in years that had no audience in it whatsoever.

* * *

At seven o'clock the typesetting room could not have held another body. People stood in the corridor and on the stairs; word had gone out through the mill rows and the laundries and the shop counters, and the town had come — mill workers with coal dust worked so deep into their knuckles it would outlive them, shopgirls, the ferryman himself planted at the back with his arms crossed like a man attending a funeral he intended to heckle.

Tilly stood her at the front, beside the composing stone, and did not introduce her. There was no program. That was the point, Edith realized. No chairwoman, no platform, no minutes. Just the room.

She looked out at them and found, to her astonishment, that she was not afraid. Terrified, rather — but terror of a different species than fear, the kind that sharpens rather than freezes. She took out the paper she had written her remarks on, looked at it, and put it back in her pocket unread. If she spoke from notes, it would be a performance, and performance was precisely what she had come to bury.

"My name is Edith Vance," she said, and heard, in the sound of her own voice saying it, how long it had been since the name had referred to an actual person. "For nine years I have taken money from Aldous Crane to fund this workshop, and in exchange I softened what I said about his mills. Everything in the pamphlet is true. I have read it. I could not have set it down more accurately myself."

The room made a sound — not a gasp, nothing so theatrical; a low collective exhale, like a house settling.

"You came here tonight expecting either a defense or a resignation," she went on. "You deserve better than both. So let me give you the truth instead, which is cheaper and harder to counterfeit." She told them. All of it — the dying workshop, Crane's visit, the exact terms, the incremental silences, the strike she had failed to endorse in 1911 because the timing was inconvenient to her funding. She watched their faces as she laid her fifteen years on the composing stone like type to be sorted, and she did not flinch, because flinching was a luxury of people with something left to hide.

"I thought I was protecting this workshop," she said. "Some nights I even was. But I was also protecting a story about myself, and the story was a lie, and lies in a leader are a tax paid by everyone under her. You paid it. These women paid it in moderated demands and delayed strikes and a movement that argued politely with men who were not being polite to them." Her voice held; she was surprised and grateful. "So here is what I have learned, at the price of everything I built, and I give it to you freely because you have earned it whether you want it or not: no movement is clean. Ours was never clean. Yours will not be clean. There is no clean. There is only honest or dishonest, and the dishonest kind fails eventually no matter how well it marches. Choose honest. Even when it costs you a leader." She looked down at her own hands, ink-flecked now from the proof sheet, and smiled — small and real. "Especially then."

For a moment the room was absolutely silent.

Then Berta began to clap — not applause, exactly; three slow strikes of her palms, deliberate as a gavel — and the room took it up, and it rolled through the typesetting room and up the stairwell and out into the street where the mill workers' wives waiting in the cold heard it and did not know yet what it meant, only that something had broken open somewhere and the sound was coming toward them.

Edith stood through it with her spine straight because her spine was the last institution she had any authority over, and when it quieted, Tilly leaned close.

"Crane's man is outside," she murmured. "He's been listening at the window. And Mrs. Vance — he didn't come alone."

Edith turned toward the frosted glass of the street-facing windows, where shadows now stood against the lamplight, three of them, motionless, patient, and she felt the old campaigner's instinct rise in her — the mapping of exits, the counting of friends — and she set it aside, deliberately, like putting down a tool she would not need anymore.

"Well then," she said to Tilly, and to the room, and to whatever the night had brought to her door. "Let's not keep them waiting in the cold. It would be the first discourtesy I've shown anyone in nine years that wasn't already in print."
