## 17. The Agent at the Door

He came on foot, which was the first wrong thing about him. No one came to the workshop on foot in February unless they had no horse, no sleigh, and no intention of being followed — or unless they wanted to be seen. Mr. Pell had always wanted to be seen.

Mara watched him come up the river road from the typesetting room window, a dark figure moving with the deliberate patience of a man who has never once hurried for anyone. The fog that morning lay low and white over the ice, and Pell seemed to condense out of it, gaining substance by degrees: the grey wool coat, the fur collar, the bowler he never removed even indoors, even in Crane's own offices, as though his head were a piece of business correspondence that must not be opened.

"He's crossing the yard," Mara said.

Tilly looked up from the galley of type she'd been locking into the chase, her fingers black to the second knuckle. Behind her, three of the women — Ruthie Osei, the Kessler sisters, old Marta who had set type since before any of them were born — went still in the way workers go still, not with fear but with the coiled attention of people deciding whether this is a problem they will be permitted to solve themselves.

"Does he know?" Tilly asked. "About the printing?"

"The story went out on the ice road four nights ago," Mara said. "A hundred copies. Maybe more, if the ferryman sold his discretion cheaply, which he did." He did not say *I carried them*. He did not need to. Everyone in the room knew whose hands had packed the satchel, whose boots had walked the frozen channel in the dark, whose breath had hung in clouds over the ice while he handed bundles to strangers with lanterns. "Pell is stranded here same as the rest of us. Whatever he knows, he learned it inside these streets."

"Then he learned it from someone in these streets," Marta said, "and that someone will answer to us later." She said it without heat, the way one notes a chore for Thursday.

Below, the knock came. Not a knock, really — a series of measured raps, spaced like punctuation.

* * *

They had talked about this, in the week since the pamphlet went out. Not formally; Edith had argued against any rehearsal at all, saying it would make them look guilty of something besides the truth. But Tilly had made them walk through it anyway, around the stove after hours, because Tilly believed — Mara had come to understand this about her — that courage unpracticed was only luck wearing a better coat.

"If he offers money," Tilly had said, "we refuse it plainly. If he threatens, we say nothing we'll regret. And nobody stands alone in front of him. Nobody."

Mara had agreed to all of it. He had known, even then, that he would break every part of it.

Now he stood at the back of the front room while Edith went to the door — because it was her building, her name, her ruin, and she insisted on opening her own doors — and he watched Pell step inside trailing cold air and the smell of cigar smoke that even the fog couldn't wash off him.

"Mrs. Vance." Pell removed his hat, which somehow made him worse. Bare-headed, with his thin hair combed flat, he looked like an accountant who had wandered into a church. "I wonder if I might have a few minutes. In private, if your — staff—" his eyes moved across the room, counting them, pricing them, "—don't mind waiting."

"They aren't staff," Edith said. "They're the publishers. Anything you have to say to me, you may say to the masthead."

Something flickered across Pell's face — not anger. Disappointment, almost gentle, the look of a man watching a door close that he had expected to walk through. "The masthead," he repeated. "Yes. I've read it. It's very well done, the pamphlet. The composition especially. Whoever set that type knew their trade." His gaze drifted, unhurried, past Tilly, past the press with its scorched frame from the fire, past the windows fogged with breath and steam, and settled — Mara felt it settle, like a hand on the back of his neck — somewhere near the back of the room. Not quite on him. Near him. Pell was too good to look directly at what he wanted.

"I've come with two propositions," Pell said. "I'll state them both, so there's no misunderstanding later about what was offered. First: Mr. Crane will buy the press. The whole apparatus — type, stock, paper contracts, the lease on this building. Generously. More than generously. The women currently employed would be offered positions at the *Gazette*, at full wages, which I'm told is more than they earn here."

"We earn exactly what we print we earn," Ruthie said. "It's on page two."

"And second," Pell continued, as though she hadn't spoken, "if the sale doesn't interest anyone, Mr. Crane asks me to convey his understanding that a building full of paper and lamp oil, in a town where the fire brigade answers slowly in winter, is a building living on borrowed time. He would be very sorry if anything happened to it. He says so sincerely." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man reporting weather. "Those are the propositions. I'm authorized to wait for an answer until sundown."

The room was quiet. Outside, the wind moved along the eaves, and somewhere down toward the water the ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like a door in an enormous house being tested.

Edith stepped forward. Mara had spent ten years learning every register of her voice: the platform voice, the parlor voice, the voice she used on donors and the softer one she used when she thought no one heard. What she used now was none of them. It was flat and plain and it belonged to no drawing room anywhere.

"You may tell Aldous Crane that the press is not for sale, that neither are we, and that if he burns this building he will be burning the evidence of his own bargain, which we have thoughtfully distributed beyond his reach. You may tell him that too."

"I will tell him exactly that," Pell said, and bowed slightly, and turned for the door.

And Mara found that his feet had already decided.

* * *

Later — that evening, and for years afterward — he would try to reconstruct the moment and find it had no edges. There was no decision he could point to, no argument won against himself. There was only the fact that Pell was walking toward the door, and the door was going to open, and the fog outside was going to take him back the way it had produced him, and everything Mara had done on the ice road four nights ago would remain half a thing, a truth published by no one, authored by shadows, deniable by everyone including the man who had carried it.

Ten years of invisibility. Ten years of sealed envelopes and memorized silences, of standing behind chairs and beside cars and just outside lamplight, of being the man people described afterward as *the driver*. He had told himself it was service. He had told himself it was protection — of Edith, of the workshop, of the women who needed the work more than they needed the truth. Some of that had even been true. But underneath it, in the place where a man keeps the account he doesn't open in daylight, there had been something else too: the comfort of it. The safety. Invisibility was a uniform, and he had worn it so long he had forgotten it could be taken off.

Tilly had said once, in the typesetting room, ink to her knuckles: *You know what I can't figure out about you? You've read everyone's mail for ten years. You know everybody's story. And nobody in this town knows yours.*

She hadn't meant it as a wound. She had meant it as a question. He had deflected it, the way he deflected everything, with deference and a closed face. But it had stayed in him like a splinter working toward the surface, and now, as Pell's hand rose toward the latch, the splinter broke through.

"No," Mara said.

His own voice startled him. It came out lower than he intended, rougher, a voice with gravel in it, a voice that had not been asked to carry weight in a decade and had gone soft in the middle from disuse.

Pell stopped. Turned. And this time he looked directly at him, and Mara understood that whatever game of near-looking the man had been playing, it was over now. Pell knew exactly who he was looking at. Had probably known since he walked in.

"Mr.—?" Pell said, leaving the name open like a trap.

"Okafor," Mara said. "Mara Okafor."

Saying it aloud was a physical act, like setting down a load he had forgotten he was carrying. His shoulders actually dropped. Somewhere behind him he heard Tilly's breath catch, and Edith go very still, and Marta's chair creak as the old woman leaned forward.

"For ten years," Mara said, "I have driven Mrs. Vance's car. For ten years I have carried her correspondence. And for ten years, before sealing every letter, I read it." He let that stand in the room. Let it land on Edith, who had learned it eleven days ago and had not yet finished absorbing it; let it land on Tilly, who had suspected and been right; let it land on the women, who were hearing it for the first time and would weigh it however they weighed it, tonight or tomorrow, and he would accept the verdict either way. "I read the letters from Crane. I read the ones where the donations arrived without names attached. I read the ones where the rhetoric got softer every time the money got larger. I knew. I have known since the first winter."

"Mara," Edith began quietly.

"No." He didn't raise his voice. He raised nothing except his eyes, and he kept them on Pell. "I'm not confessing to this room. This room already knows what I am. I'm telling *you*." He crossed the floor then — six steps, seven, until he stood closer to Pell than he had ever stood to any agent of the Cranes, close enough to see the powder of frost melting on the man's wool shoulders, close enough that Pell had to tilt his chin up slightly, which he clearly resented. "So you can stop looking around my face for the man who set that type. You're looking at him. I set it. Line by line. Including the line about the courier."

Pell's expression did not change, which was itself information. Men who could be rattled changed expressions. Pell simply waited, the way he waited for trains and settlements and deaths, and then he said, mildly: "That's very dramatic. It changes nothing. A printer is a printer. We'll find the press whether or not it has a name attached."

"That's the other thing you should carry back to Mr. Crane," Mara said. "You can't burn what's already gone. Four nights ago, before your man even reached the ferry office, a hundred copies of that pamphlet crossed the ice road. They went to Halvorsen's farm, to the fishing camps at the north channel, to the mill workers' boarding houses on the far shore — yes, *your* mill workers, the ones whose wages are printed on page two, the ones who now know why their petitions died in committee every year for a decade. They went out in satchels and bread sacks and coat linings. By now they've been copied by hand, read aloud in kitchens, passed from house to house. There is no bundle left to seize, Mr. Pell. There is no press whose destruction undoes it. The story isn't in this building anymore." He paused, and found that the next sentence was the easiest one he had ever said, and that frightened him more than anything else that morning. "It's in a hundred hands across the ice road. And hands don't burn."

The silence that followed had texture. Mara could feel the women behind him — not as an audience but as a weight at his back, a wall he had spent ten years standing outside of and was now standing inside of. Ruthie had folded her arms. The Kessler sisters had risen from their stools without appearing to decide to. Even Edith had not moved to soften what he'd said, had not stepped in to manage it, and he understood that this, too, was new: she was letting him speak as herself spoke, and discovering, perhaps, that the movement survived it.

Pell put his hat back on. It was, Mara thought, the gesture of a man closing a ledger.

"A hundred copies," Pell said. "In a county of forty thousand souls. You mistake paper for power, Mr. Okafor. Paper is ash. It has always been ash. Mr. Crane has outlasted editors, ministers, judges, and two governors. He will outlast a hundred copies on an ice road." He adjusted his gloves, finger by finger. "But I appreciate the performance. It's rare to see a servant find his voice. Rarer still to see it matter."

"There's one more thing," Mara said.

Pell waited.

"The ferryman," Mara said. "You'll want to cross back to the Crane offices as soon as the ice allows, or send word ahead of yourself. You should know that Joss Merriam took twenty dollars four nights ago and carried three bundles himself, and that he has been telling anyone who buys him a drink exactly what was in them. Your employer's name is on the ice road too, Mr. Pell. Every mile of it. When the thaw comes, you won't be carrying news to Crane." He held the man's eyes. "You'll be carrying it home."

For the first time, something moved behind Pell's face — small, quickly mastered, but real. The look of a man doing arithmetic he had not planned on. Then it was gone, smoothed away under the professional blankness, and he inclined his head a half-inch, the smallest possible concession to having been struck.

"Sundown," he reminded them, gesturing vaguely at the offer of purchase, as though the offer were a train still standing at the platform. "If anyone wishes to reconsider."

No one reconsidered. The door opened; the fog leaned in, grey and patient; and Pell went out into it, and the fog took him, and the yard was empty again except for his footprints filling slowly with meltwater.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a moment. The stove ticked. Downriver, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Then Marta said, "Well," in the tone of a woman resuming a chore interrupted, and went back to her case, and the room exhaled, and the presses' ordinary life resumed around him — Tilly checking the locked chase, Ruthie trimming paper, the small industry of people who had decided, collectively and without discussion, that a threat received before noon was no reason to lose an afternoon.

Edith came to stand beside him at the window. For a while she said nothing, watching the footprints in the yard dissolve.

"Ten years," she said finally. "You said it aloud. To him. To all of us."

"Yes."

"Why today? Why him?" She wasn't accusing. She sounded genuinely puzzled, a scholar confronting a datum that didn't fit.

Mara watched the last footprint fill with water and vanish, the yard erasing the evidence that anyone had ever stood there. "Because he called me a servant," he said. "And I realized I was the only person left in this town who still believed it."

Edith looked at him then — really looked, the way she had perhaps never looked at him, the way one looks at a person rather than a fixture — and whatever she saw made her nod slowly, once, and turn back to the room.

It was Tilly who broke the mood, arriving at his elbow with ink still on her hands and an expression caught between triumph and alarm. "That was either the bravest thing I've seen since the bridge closed," she said, "or the most reckless, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Neither can I," Mara admitted.

"Well, whichever it was, it's about to cost us." She nodded toward the window, toward the road, where the fog was already producing new shapes — figures this time, several of them, moving fast, coming up from the direction of the ferry landing. Mill men, by their coats and their rolling gait. Six of them. Eight. And at their head, unmistakable even at distance, big-shouldered and grim, Joss Merriam himself, the ferryman, walking toward the workshop at a pace that had nothing casual in it.

"They're coming from the landing," Tilly said quietly. "Mara — what happened at the landing?"

Mara watched them come, and felt the ice beneath the whole town shift by a fraction, the way it does just before it speaks.
