## 18. What the Ice Takes

Tilly had imagined fire before. Every organizer did, sooner or later — you learned to sleep with your shoes by the bed and the important papers in one satchel, and in that satchel's pocket, folded small, a mental list of what you would carry out of a burning building and what you would let go. She had rehearsed it in three towns already: the press stays, the type stays, I stay. The rest is ash waiting for its match.

She had not imagined it would come in daylight.

The men from the landing came up Water Street at an unhurried walk, five of them, and that was what frightened her first — the patience of them. Men who meant only mischief ran. These men moved like men on wages, spaced out along the frozen ruts, coats buttoned, one carrying a lantern even though the sky was wide and gray and full of afternoon. The sixth man walked apart, behind, gloved hands folded like a deacon's. She knew his face now. Crane's agent — Mr. Pell, though nobody had introduced him. He had been seen in town twice before the bridge closed, and both times he had been smiling at something no one else could see.

"They're going for the coal chute," Mara said beside her. He was standing very still at the workshop window, and Tilly saw that his stillness was not calm. It was a man holding a door shut inside himself. "It's unlocked. It's always unlocked."

"Then we lock it," Tilly said, and was already moving.

* * *

What she would remember afterward — this was the strange part, the part she turned over for years — was how ordinary the hour felt until it didn't. The typesetting room smelled of lead and lamp oil and the coffee someone had burned at dawn while setting the last pages. The women were there because they had refused to go home since the pamphlet went into the ice road in a hundred mittened hands: Hettie Crane — no relation, she said grimly, none whatsoever — at the stone; the Brandt cousins pulling proofs; old Mrs. Sallow who had set type for thirty years under men who never learned her name, her fingers moving over the cases blind, faster than sight. The press stood in the corner where the fire had scorched it in December, patched and painted over, and Tilly had come to think of that scar as the workshop's signature.

She got to the coal chute door with Mara two steps behind and threw the bar across it, and the wood of the frame was so old and swollen with damp that the bar seated with a sound like a knuckle cracking, loud in the quiet street.

Outside, the men stopped.

For a long moment nobody moved on either side of the door. Tilly could hear the river. Everyone in this town could always hear the river — under the ice it went on talking to itself all winter, a low mutter, patient as arithmetic — but today it seemed louder, or perhaps she was simply listening harder than she ever had.

Then Pell's voice came through the wood, mild as milk. "Miss Brandt. I'd hoped to find you. We have business."

"You have nothing here," Tilly said. "Your employer's money is named on page two. His method is named on page one. There's no bargain left to buy."

"Everything's a bargain," Pell said pleasantly. "Prices just change. That press is worth something to whoever holds it. Right now it's worth more standing than burned. Come spring that changes. Wood, oil, a long dry February —" A pause, and she could hear him choosing the words like a man selecting tools. "Mr. Crane is a fair man, Miss Brandt. He pays what things cost."

"And what does this cost?" Hettie had come up behind them, sleeves rolled, ink to the elbows.

"Nothing," said Pell, "if the press stops."

Tilly heard it then — the whole architecture of the man's world in one word. Nothing if you stop. Something if you burn. He had crossed the ice with hired men and a price list, and the price list was the whole of his imagination. He could not conceive of a room full of people for whom stopping was not for sale.

"No," she said.

A silence. Then, without heat, almost gently: "Bar that door well, then."

Boots crunched away over the frozen ruts. Through the seam of the shuttered window Tilly watched them fan out around the building with the unhurried competence of men who had done this work in other towns, for other mills — one to the back, two toward the oil store, two staying visible at the front where anyone watching would see them and think: five men, and us, and winter, and no help coming from anywhere because there was nowhere for help to come from. The bridge was shut. The ferry was beached. The telegraph office had gone dark in January when the line came down past Miller's Bend and stayed down. The town was a sealed jar, and Pell had brought his match inside it weeks ago and had simply been waiting for the right hour.

"He'll wait for dark," Mara said quietly. He was looking at the street, but Tilly saw his eyes move the way they always moved — cataloguing, memorizing, filing everything into the vast silent archive of himself. "He won't risk witnesses in daylight unless he has to. Dark comes at four-thirty now. That gives us —" he glanced at the window, at the iron-colored light — "two hours."

"Two hours for what?" asked Mrs. Sallow, not looking up from her case. "There's nowhere to run the press to. There's nowhere to run ourselves to. We're on an island of ice, girl. You can't outrun a match on an island."

"No," Tilly said slowly. And then something shifted in her, the way the light shifts when a cloud moves off the sun, and she heard herself say a thing she had been circling for weeks without knowing it: "But we're not the only ones on the island."

Mara turned to look at her.

"The pamphlet," she said. "We didn't print it for ourselves. We printed it for everyone Crane's ledger touched — and the ledger touched half this town. Wages held flat eleven years. The company scrip. The deaths in the carding room that got written up as carelessness." She was speaking fast now, the way she spoke when the shape of a thing finally stood up in front of her. "Pell thinks he's come to burn a printing shop. He hasn't. He's come to burn the only place in this town that ever printed the truth about the mills, and every soul on Water Street knows it. Let him find out what that costs."

"It costs them their jobs," Mara said. "Come thaw, the mills open again. Crane remembers faces."

"Yes," Tilly said. "And they know that better than we do. So ask them. Don't decide for them." She looked around the room, at the ink-stained women, at Mara with his decade of deciding-for-others written invisibly all over him. "That's the whole disease, isn't it? Deciding for people what they can bear to know. Ask."

* * *

They went out in pairs, and Tilly went with Mara because she did not trust him not to go alone and stand in front of some danger as a penance, which was the one thing about him that still made her want to shake him.

Water Street at dusk in deep February was a corridor of blue snow and yellow windows. They knocked at the mill houses first — the rows of identical brick boxes below the hill, where the smoke of the mills would rise again in April and everything would resume, unless it didn't. Tilly had done this work before, the knocking, the cold porches, the careful speeches, and she had her opening ready. But at the first door it wasn't needed.

The woman who opened it — broad-shouldered, forty maybe, a shawl over a factory apron she hadn't yet taken off — looked at the pamphlet in Tilly's mitten and said, "You're the ones that named the carding room." Not a question. "My brother's in it. Page three."

"We need bodies at the workshop tonight," Tilly said. "There are men coming with oil."

The woman looked past them both, down the street, at the other lit windows, and Tilly watched her do the arithmetic — job against brother, winter against spring, fear against the particular fury of a person who has just read her family's grief printed plainly by strangers and found it true. Then she stepped back inside, and Tilly's heart began its slow descent, and then the woman came out again with her coat already on and called across the street, in a voice built for carrying over looms:

"Nell! Get your boots! They're trying to burn the print shop!"

Doors opened. That was the thing Tilly could not have predicted and never forgot: how many doors simply opened. Not instantly everywhere — there were windows that stayed dark, houses where the shade came down at the sight of them, a man who took the pamphlet and shook his head and closed the door gently as if shutting a coffin. But enough. Mill workers in their heavy coats. Boys too young for the mill, sent anyway by mothers who stood in their own doorways holding lamps. The ferryman, gray-bearded, who had refused Mara at the ice only days ago and now came up the street with an axe over his shoulder and a coil of rope and no explanation offered or needed. Even the saloon emptied by degrees, men drifting out with their collars up, and Tilly understood that whatever was printed on page one, the grievances had been living in these people far longer than any pamphlet, and all the paper had done was give them a place to put what they already knew.

By full dark there were forty of them in the yard behind the workshop, and more coming, and Pell's men — Tilly saw them regroup at the corner by the oil store, saw the hesitation enter their formation — had discovered that the sealed jar contained more than they had bargained for.

* * *

The fight, such as it was, lasted less time than the waiting.

Pell's men made one attempt at the back of the building, where the yard was darkest, and found the yard full of mill workers who knew every inch of that ground the way Pell's men knew nothing — knew where the fence leaned, where the drainpipe gave handholds, where the ground fell away sharp to the coal sheds. Nobody died. Two of the hired men went down hard and stayed down, groaning theatrically; the rest withdrew to the street, and then Pell tried words, standing in the lamplight with his gloves folded, offering money by name to men whose names he had clearly researched, and Tilly heard Hettie laugh at him from the crowd, a short harsh sound like tearing cloth, and heard the crowd take up the laugh until it became something with teeth in it. That was when the hired men began looking at each other. Men on wages understand one argument fluently, and it was being conducted above their heads in a language they suddenly couldn't afford.

Pell stopped smiling.

Tilly watched him take the measure of the street — the lamps in the windows, the axes, the rope, the women standing in the yard's open gate with their arms linked, Mrs. Sallow among them, seventy years old and immovable as the type she had set — and Tilly watched him arrive, visibly, at the fact that the price of this thing had changed while he stood there, and no longer favored his employer.

"This isn't finished," Pell said.

"Yes, it is," said the ferryman, and spat.

They went down toward the landing, all six, walking fast now, the patience gone out of them, and the crowd did not pursue — did not need to. The whole town followed at a distance instead, a loose procession of lamps moving down to the shore, and Tilly walked in it with her heart going like a trip-hammer, and beside her Mara walked like a man in a dream, and she realized he was watching the crowd, not Pell. Watching the lamps. Counting them.

"I thought it would just be us," he said. His voice was strange. "Ten years I've been — I thought the letters were mine to guard because nobody else would carry them. I thought the silence was load-bearing. Take it out and everything falls." He stopped in the snow and looked at the procession strung out behind them down the hill, forty lamps, sixty, a constellation walking. "It wasn't load-bearing. It was just heavy."

"No," Tilly said. "It was just quiet. There's a difference."

* * *

At the landing, the ice told the ending.

February had been working at the river all month — the fishermen said the ice was rotten past the channel, black and honeycombed, holding near the shores and treacherous in the middle where the current ran deepest. Pell must have known it and gambled on it holding; it was the only road left to him, and pride is a poor surveyor. The crowd on the bank watched his party string out across the river in the dark, single file, lanterns swinging, and the river talked under them the whole way, that low muttering arithmetic, adding up.

Halfway across, the ice spoke louder.

Tilly heard it from the shore as a sound she had no name for — not a crack exactly, more a long groaning exhale, the sound of something immense shifting its weight — and then a white seam shot out across the black surface, quick as a struck match drawn backward, and one of the lanterns swung wildly and went out. Shouting, thin across the water. The file of men froze where they stood, spread out, arms wide, the oldest trick there was, and the river considered them.

It let them keep going.

Slowly, lantern by lantern, the little procession crawled the rest of the way to the far shore and pulled itself up the bank and vanished into the dark of the far woods, and nobody on the Water Street side cheered, because there was nothing to cheer that the river hadn't already decided. But nobody moved to leave, either. They stood on the bank in the cold and watched the ice, and Tilly understood that they were watching it the way you watch an adversary who has just shown you his strength — respectfully, and with new information. The bridge would not open soon. The mills would not open soon. Whatever Crane sent next would have to come the same way they had all come: across the failing ice, at the river's pleasure, in the river's time.

"The river keeps its own account," the ferryman said beside her, coiling his rope. He nodded once at the black water talking under the white. "Always has. Men think winter closes the book. Winter only pauses the reading."

* * *

They walked back up to the workshop together through the falling dark, and the crowd came with them, and nobody suggested dispersing. Someone had brought bread; someone else had brought a jug; the yard behind the print shop filled with mill workers eating in the cold, and the lamps were hung on the fence posts, and Mrs. Sallow sat down inside at her case again because she said her hands got stupid without the type, and the sound of her stick sorting lead came out through the doorway like a heartbeat under the voices outside.

Hettie found Tilly by the press and gripped her arm hard enough to bruise. "You set my wages on page two," she said.

"Yes."

"My husband said don't come out tonight. My husband works the crane gang." Her eyes were wet and furious. "I came anyway. You want to know why? Because eleven years somebody's been deciding what we could bear to know, and tonight I decided." She let go, and steadied, and looked older and younger at once. "Set the next one soon. We'll read it."

Later, when the yard had thinned to diehards and the lamps were burning low, Tilly found Mara standing at the unshuttered window, looking out at the town. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, sifting down through the lamplight over Water Street, over the mill rows with their windows still glowing — more windows lit than she had ever seen at this hour, as if half the town had decided, tonight, not to sleep yet.

"You were never alone," she said. "You just never rang the bell."

He didn't answer for a moment. In the glass his reflection and the lit street overlapped, the invisible man printed faintly over the town he had spent ten years watching through windows.

"There's mail coming with the thaw," he said finally. "Spring mail. Answers to the pamphlet — condemnation, certainly, and worse than condemnation. And every letter will have to be read, and logged, and answered." He turned from the window, and Tilly saw something in his face she had never seen there in all the winter: the look of a man who has put down a weight and found his arms haven't forgotten how to reach for things. "I'm going to need help with the archiving."

Tilly laughed — she couldn't help it — and outside in the yard, Mrs. Sallow's stick clicked on, sorting the lead, letter by letter, into words.

Then Mara stiffened, his eyes going past her to the door, where a knock had sounded — three blows, deliberate, unfamiliar. Through the frosted pane stood the silhouette of someone small, hooded, snow-crusted, someone who had clearly just come off the ice road from the far shore, where no one was supposed to be able to cross at all.
