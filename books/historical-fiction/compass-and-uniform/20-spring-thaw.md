## 20. Spring Thaw

The river spoke first.

Tilly heard it before dawn on the third of March, a sound like a door groaning on rusted hinges, long and low, running under the floorboards of the workshop where she had fallen asleep at the proof table again. She came up out of her chair with her heart already moving before her mind caught up, and stood a moment in the dark with ink on her fingers and the smell of cold lead and lamp oil around her, listening.

The ice was breaking.

She had grown up inland, among fields that only froze hard enough to skate on, and so she had never heard a river let go of its winter. It was not one sound but many — a cracking like rifle fire somewhere downriver, then a grinding, a shudder that traveled through the pilings of the workshop's old foundation and up through the soles of her boots, then silence, then the deep groan again. The whole frozen surface of the Verre was waking after four months of holding still, and it did not wake gently.

She dressed in the dark, not because she needed to hide from anyone anymore but because the habit of quiet mornings had gotten into her blood this winter, and went out into the blue hour with her coat over her nightdress. The air smelled different. That was the thing no one had warned her about. All winter the town had breathed its own closed breath — coal smoke and tallow and the sourness of too many bodies indoors — and now, all at once, there was water in it, open water, the smell of mud and movement and everything downstream.

The bridge stood gray against the paling sky, its boards dark with frost-melt, its far end lost in a haze that was not fog but distance returning to the world. For four months that far end might as well have been painted on the horizon. Now Tilly looked at it and understood, with a lurch that was half joy and half vertigo, that it led somewhere again. There were trains on the other side. Newspapers. Aldous Crane's lawyers, probably, and Crane's money moving like water toward whatever cracks it could find. The world they had defied in the sealed months of winter was coming back across the ice to collect, or to congratulate, or both.

But first, the river.

She walked down to the bank where the ferryman kept his boat hauled up above the waterline, and found half the town already there, or so it seemed: mill workers in their caps, women from the laundry with shawls over their heads, children hanging off the mooring posts. No one spoke much. They stood in the flat early light and watched the Verre come apart.

The ice did not melt so much as surrender in pieces. Great plates of it, gray-white and rotten at the edges, lifted and tilted and slid under one another with sounds like furniture being dragged across an enormous floor. The current showed itself between them, black and muscular, and where the plates ground together they reared up and shattered into ridges of rubble that caught the first sun and burned white. A whole floe the size of the workshop floor turned slowly in midstream, carrying a fence post upright on its back like a flagstaff.

"Early this year," said the ferryman beside her, unasked. He was a squat man who had refused Mara passage in the worst weeks, when the ice was failing, and Tilly had not forgiven him for the refusal even while understanding it. "Last spring we waited till nearly April."

"Does it mean anything?" she asked. "An early thaw?"

He shrugged, eyes on the water. "Means the river doesn't care what any of us planned. Never has." Then, grudgingly, because perhaps the winter had changed him too: "Means the mail will come soon. First boat upstream carries the post. Always has."

The mail. Tilly felt the word go through her like a fishhook. Four months ago they had set type through the night — Mara's steady hands, the women working in shifts, the press clacking until dawn — and printed five hundred copies of the exposé on paper they had bought with pooled wages. *The Crane Money: An Account.* Five hundred copies, carried out across the ice road in satchels and coat linings and bread baskets by women who had voted, narrowly, tremblingly, to tell the truth on their own masthead. Some of those copies had gone out on foot before the last safe crossing; some had gone with travelers who took them as far as the rail junction and mailed them onward, one stamp at a time, so that no single hand could be blamed for the whole flood.

And now the river was opening, and the answer to all of it was coming back the same way.

She looked down the bank and saw Mara standing apart from the others, as he always stood, hands in the pockets of his driver's greatcoat, watching the ice run. Even at this distance she could read the set of his shoulders. He had signed his name six weeks ago — his full name, written plainly on the cooperative's charter in front of everyone — and he was still learning how to carry it, the way a man learns to carry a weight he chose rather than a weight he was handed. She went and stood beside him. He didn't startle. He never startled; that was ten years of training that would take another ten to wear off.

"The ferryman says the first boat carries the post," she said.

"I heard." His breath smoked. "I've been thinking I should sort it. Like before." He said *before* the way other people said a name of the dead.

"No," Tilly said. "Together. Out loud. That was the whole point."

He looked at her then, and something in his face eased, the way it always did when someone reminded him that the old rules were dead — that he had helped kill them. "Together," he agreed. "Out loud."

Out on the river, a floe struck the near piling of the bridge with a boom like a struck drum, and the crowd murmured, and the ice rode on downstream toward towns that had not yet heard what the Verre town had done all winter.

* * *

The first boat came two days later, riding high and empty except for the pilot, two deckhands, and a mail sack that made the deckhands curse as they hauled it up the landing stairs.

It was not a sack. It was sacks — seven of them, canvas-brown and strapped, and the pilot handed over a manifest with the number of items written in a figure that Tilly made him repeat twice. Nine hundred and forty letters and parcels addressed to the Cooperative Printing Workshop, Verre Landing, and its members by name. In four months, from a town that the country had mostly forgotten existed, they had become a destination.

They laid the mail out on the long typesetting tables, and by unwritten agreement the whole workshop stopped its presses for the afternoon to face it, because this was work that belonged to everyone. Mara stood at the head table with a letter knife, and Tilly beside him, and the women ranged down the benches — Hester and Agnes from the typesetting room, young Brida who had carried forty copies across the ice road sewn into her petticoat, old Mrs. Callow who had worked the mills for thirty-one years and had a paragraph of her own in the exposé about the carding-room wages of 1906. Edith Vance sat at the far end.

That was its own strangeness, Tilly thought, watching her. Six weeks since Edith had stood in front of the assembled town and owned the bargain aloud — *I took Crane's money because I believed the workshop mattered more than my pride, and I was wrong to believe I could spend your trust like capital* — and every day since, Edith had come to the workshop not as its founder, not as the famous Mrs. Vance whose name drew crowds in eleven cities, but as a woman with a chair at the end of the table. She had asked for no better seat. She answered the door sometimes. She had learned to sweep, badly, and accepted correction from Brida without flinching, which Tilly counted as a rarer form of courage than any speech she had ever given.

"Open them," said Mrs. Callow, when the room had gone quiet. "Let's hear what the world thinks of us."

Mara cut the first envelope. His hands, which could reseal a letter so that no one living could tell, moved carefully, as if each flap were something breakable. He cleared his throat.

"*To the traitors and slanderers of the Verre workshop,*" he read. "*You have bitten the hand that fed you and shown yourselves enemies of decent order. Your leader is finished and so are you—*" He stopped. Looked up. "That one's short."

"Burn it," said someone from the back, and half the room laughed, but Tilly shook her head.

"Weigh it," she said. "Every letter counts, good or bad. We should know what we're owed."

So they made two piles, and the piles told a story all afternoon. There were condemnations — dozens of them, some printed form letters from men's clubs and citizens' leagues whose names sounded like furniture, some scrawled in furious hands, a few genuinely frightening in their precision about where people lived. Those Mara set aside without comment, and Tilly saw him do it gently, the old courier instinct: protect first, inform later. But the other pile grew faster.

There were letters from women Tilly had never met — a hatmaker in a city three days east writing that her girls had read the exposé aloud in the workroom and demanded their books be opened to them the same week. A seamstress who enclosed a money order for two dollars and eleven cents, *all I can spare till quarter-day*. A schoolteacher who wrote, in a careful hand, that she had cut the pamphlet's wage tables out and pinned them to her classroom wall, and that her students — boys as well as girls — had copied them into their notebooks like scripture.

And there were the reprints. That was the word arriving over and over like a drumbeat: *reprint, reprint, reprint*. A print shop in Harrow Junction had reset the whole text and run two thousand copies under its own imprint, adding only a line beneath the masthead: *Set in solidarity with the Verre cooperative.* A league of laundry workers had excerpted the wage tables in their own bulletin. Someone — unsigned, from a port town downriver — reported that the exposé was being read out in shipyard canteens by whoever could read loudest, and that men who had laughed at suffragists all their lives had gone quiet at the sentence about the carding rooms, because their mothers had worked in carding rooms, and no one had ever put the numbers on paper before.

"They're printing our words," Brida said, wonder in her voice, "in other people's shops."

"On other people's presses," Agnes corrected, "with other people's ink," and the room laughed, and Tilly felt the laugh move through her chest and lodge there, warm, because that was the thing none of the condemnation could touch: you could burn a workshop, but you could not burn a sentence once five hundred strangers had memorized it.

Mara opened one letter and went very still.

Tilly watched him read it twice, watched the color leave and return to his face, and held out her hand without asking. He gave it over. The hand was steady but slow, a man handing over evidence.

It was written on heavy cream stock, the kind that announced its own expense, and it was signed *A. Crane*.

Tilly read it aloud, because they had agreed — out loud, together — and because she would not let a letter from that man arrive like a serpent into their house and hide under the floorboards. "*Madam and gentlemen,*" she read. "*I have read your production with interest. You paint me as a man who buys silence. I write to offer a demonstration that I buy nothing of the kind. I intend to fund a competing print works in your town, staffed at double your wages, open to all comers regardless of sex or politics. Let the market judge which press the town prefers. I remain, etc., etc.*"

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, a gull — the first gull of spring, riding the open water — cried once and was gone.

"He's going to build a press," Brida said finally, "to prove he doesn't buy things."

"There's a kind of sense in that," Mrs. Callow said dryly, "if you don't look at it straight on."

Edith rose from her chair at the end of the table. She had gone gray when Tilly read the signature — Tilly had seen it, everyone had seen it — but she stood now with her back straight, and when she spoke her voice was the old platform voice, only quieter, meant for a room of forty instead of a hall of four thousand.

"He means it," she said. "This is not a threat; it is an argument, and it is better made than his usual arguments. He will pay double wages, and he will hire whomever he pleases, and in six months' time he will point at his full benches and our empty ones and say the market has spoken." She looked down the length of the tables, at the faces turned up toward her, and Tilly saw her take a breath like a woman stepping off a ledge. "Which is why I have something to say this morning, and I would rather say it here than anywhere. May I?"

"It's your chair too," said Mrs. Callow.

Edith folded her hands. Tilly noticed they were not gloved. All winter she had worn gloves everywhere, armor against the cold and the stares; today her bare hands were chapped at the knuckles like any working woman's, and she let them be seen.

"I have spent eleven years raising money for this cause," Edith said, "and I have been very good at it, and part of being good at it was never asking too closely where it came from. When I did ask — and I did ask, late, and alone, and hoping for a comfortable answer — I made a bargain instead of a report, and I called the bargain necessity. This winter you all learned what it cost. So I am done raising money the old way. From today, the Vance Fund — what remains of it, and it is considerable, and it is clean if nothing else about me is — goes to the cooperative. Not to me. Not through me. To the cooperative, chartered, governed by its own members, with its books open to anyone who asks, including Mr. Crane, including the newspapers, including the devil. I will sign it over this week. And I will step back."

The room did not gasp. Tilly had expected something — protest, applause, Hester's sharp cry — but instead there was a silence of a different texture entirely, the silence of forty people deciding whether to believe a thing. It was Brida who broke it, because Brida was nineteen and had not yet learned to soften questions.

"Step back to where?"

Edith smiled, and it reached her eyes, which Tilly had not often seen. "To the road," she said. "There are eleven cities where they know my name and not one of them knows yours. Somebody has to go stand on platforms and say: the pamphlet you've been passing around was written by women with ink on their hands, and the press that printed it belongs to them, and if you want to help the cause, send your dollars to Verre Landing and not to me. I can fill halls. I have always been able to fill halls. What I cannot do anymore is stand between you and your own voices, and God knows I have tried, and it nearly ruined everything." She looked at Mara, then at Tilly, then back down the benches. "The movement survives the winter without me. I find I am prouder of that than of anything I ever built with my own name on it."

Mrs. Callow snorted, but her eyes were wet. "Eleven cities," she said. "Mind you mention the carding rooms first. Thirty-one years, and I'll not be footnote to nobody's speech."

"You'll be the second sentence," Edith promised, and the room laughed, and the vote — though no one called it a vote — passed in the laughter, the way the best ones do.

* * *

Evening came late now; the light held past six. After the others had gone home, Tilly stayed to lock up, and found Mara in the sorting room still at it, working through the last of the second sack by lamplight, reading each letter under his breath before setting it on its pile. Some habits, she thought, were not prisons but instruments. He had spent ten years reading other people's words in secret and calling it sin; now he read strangers' words aloud in lamplight and it was simply work, honest work, the archive of a season that had changed more lives than any of them yet knew.

She sat down across from him and pulled a letter from the open pile — one of the support pile, thick, forwarded twice, its envelope soft with handling. Inside was a newspaper clipping and a covering note. She read the note first: *You do not know us. We are nine women who work the canning sheds at Port Maren. We read your account aloud over three nights. We enclose the clipping because it shows what we did next.*

The clipping was from a Port Maren weekly, dated a fortnight past. SHED WORKERS PRESENT WAGE DEMANDS; OWNERS REFUSE COMMENT. Beneath it, in the covering note's careful hand: *We are not printers. We have no press. But we have a list of what we are owed, and we got the idea of lists from you. Tell the woman who used to be Mrs. Vance that her fall was the first stone of our bridge.*

Tilly read it twice. Then she read it aloud, slowly, because the room was empty and the words deserved air, and because she wanted to hear whether they sounded real outside her own skull. They did. More than real. They sounded like weather — like something that had started here and was now falling on places she would never see.

Mara listened with his elbows on the table and his chin in his hands. When she finished he said, quietly, "The first stone of our bridge." He turned his lamplight toward the window, where the dark river ran loud and free beyond the glass, hauling its broken winter downstream to the sea. "Ten years I carried other people's letters and thought the words belonged to the people who paid for the paper. It never once occurred to me that words, once loose, belong to whoever needs them."

"That's because you were a courier," Tilly said. "Couriers think in routes. It's a map problem. You'll think in floods eventually. Give yourself a year."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he sorted three more letters onto the piles, and his hands slowed, and he said, in the voice of a man laying a card face-up, "There's something else in the sack. It came for you. I've been turning it over for an hour trying to decide whether tonight was the night."

"Whose hand?"

"That's the thing." He drew a final envelope from the bottom of the sack — heavy stock, forwarded twice like the cannery letter, franked at the rail junction — and laid it on the table between them, face up. "I don't know it. But it's a woman's hand, and it's addressed to you by name, and Tilly—" he paused, choosing, and she braced herself, because he had earned the right to choose his words now and always chose them well — "it's addressed to *Tilly Brandt, care of the person who reads this first*. Not the workshop. The person."

Tilly looked at the envelope a long moment. The lamp guttered and steadied. Beyond the window the river went on talking to itself in its new spring voice, carrying the winter away piece by piece to towns downstream that had no idea what was coming to them on the current.

She picked it up. The paper inside was thick. More than one page. Something small and hard shifted within the folds, with the faint metallic whisper of a key.

"Well," she said, and slit it open.
