## 1. The Weight of Water

The tide came in twice a day, and twice a day it reminded everyone on Sainte-Marée-de-Bas exactly who was in charge.

Zephyrine Joubert had learned this lesson before they had learned their letters. The island sat a half-mile off the coast like a stubborn old woman refusing to move inland, connected to the mainland by a spit of sand and shell that appeared at low water and vanished beneath three feet of green-brown sea at high. The company called it the Tide Road. The islanders called it the Causeway when they were feeling grand and the Sand Spit when they were not, and the children called it nothing at all because they were too busy racing the water across it, shrieking, pockets full of stolen plums, betting each other they could beat the sea home.

Zephyrine had never once lost that race. This was not boasting. It was simply a fact of their body, the way some people had good singing voices or lucky hands. Zephyrine had been built for water the way a heron was built for shallows — long-limbed, narrow-shouldered, with lungs that seemed to hold more air than any doctor could account for. Their father, Émile Joubert, had been the finest pearl diver the Meridian Trading Company ever contracted, and he liked to say that Zeph had been diving before they were born, which was technically true and which he repeated so often that the whole dockside quarter could recite it along with him by the time Zeph turned ten.

"Born in the ninth month," Papa would announce to anyone new, holding up his spread fingers, "and my wife dove the oyster beds until the seventh. So you see, the child had already held its breath for two months before it ever breathed at all."

"And that," Mama would add dryly, from wherever she was mending nets or counting pearls or pretending not to listen, "is why the child has never once won an argument. It learned early that holding your breath gets you nothing."

This morning, the first proper morning of the diving season, Zephyrine stood at the edge of the company dock watching the boats go out, and thought about weight.

Everything on Sainte-Marée had a weight that changed. The causeway weighed nothing at dawn and everything by noon. A pearl the size of a peppercorn could weigh more than a house if it meant the difference between a debt paid and a debt called in. Water itself was the worst of all — light as nothing when you floated on it, heavy as sin when you carried it in your chest, down where the oysters grew in the dark.

"You're doing your thinking face," said Odile Marchand, arriving at Zeph's elbow with two cups of chicory coffee and the expression of someone who had decided, unilaterally, that one of them was for Zeph.

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You have a thinking face. Your brow does a thing." Odile demonstrated by furrowing her own broad, sun-darkened forehead into an expression of tremendous philosophical anguish. "Like a crab that's heard some bad news."

"That is not what I look like."

"It is exactly what you look like." She pressed a cup into Zeph's hands. It was too hot and too bitter and made the way only Odile's grandmother made it, which was to say badly, with great love. "Drink. You'll want your wits today. First dive of the season, and Delacroix is coming down to count us like chickens."

Zeph drank. The coffee scalded all the way down and settled in the stomach like a small angry coal, which was its purpose. Across the harbor, the company factor's white house caught the early sun and threw it back, blinding, the way it always did — as though the building itself were showing off. Factor Delacroix's house had glass windows. Real ones, imported, six panes each. Half the island had never looked through a window that wasn't a hole with a shutter.

"He counts us every year," Zeph said.

"He *inspects* us every year. There's a difference. Counting is what you do to things you might lose." Odile blew on her coffee. "Inspecting is what you do to things you own."

It was a distinction without much comfort in it, but that was Odile all over. She had a gift for saying the true thing in the way that made it worse, and then laughing, so that you couldn't quite be angry at her. She was seventeen, a year older than Zeph, broad-shouldered from hauling nets, with a gap in her teeth she refused to let the dentist in Port-Rouge fix because, she claimed, it whistled when she whistled and confused the gulls.

Below them, the divers were loading the boats. There were eleven licensed divers on Sainte-Marée-de-Bas, and every one of them worked for the Meridian Trading Company, because there was nowhere else to work, because the company owned the beds, and the boats, and the dock, and the factor's house with its six-pane windows, and — though no document said so in words plain enough to hang a man with — the divers themselves. Not slavery. Never that. The company had contracts, and the contracts had debts, and the debts had interest, and the interest had a way of growing like mangrove roots through the generations until a family found itself standing on ground that was entirely root and no soil at all.

The Jouberts owed the company four hundred and twelve francs. Zeph knew the number the way they knew their own heartbeat. Papa had borrowed against the season when Mama had the fever, seven years ago, and the number had gone down and crept back up and gone down again, patient as the tide. Zeph's own contract — indentured apprentice, diver's line, term of twelve years — was folded in oilcloth under a floorboard at home. They had read it so many times they could recite it, including the clause about *extension of term for damages, losses, or delinquency*, which Factor Delacroix could interpret however he liked, and generally did.

"There's Papa." Odile nodded at the second boat, where Émile Joubert was stowing weights with the unhurried economy of a man who had done ten thousand dives and intended to do ten thousand more. He was forty-one and grey at the temples and still moved through water better than men half his age. He caught Zeph looking and raised one hand — not a wave, exactly, just an acknowledgment: *I see you, you're late, get down here.*

"I should go."

"You should." Odile drained her coffee and grimaced at it fondly. "Dive well. Come up breathing."

"That's the whole job, isn't it."

"The whole job," she agreed, "and the hard part."

* * *

The first dive of the season was half work and half ceremony, the way most things on Sainte-Marée were. Before the boats went out past the breakwater, old Madame Toussaint came down to the dock in her Sunday shawl — it was Tuesday, but the shawl did not care — carrying a bottle of rum and a face like a storm front. She poured the rum into the water in a thin silver thread and spoke the names of the drowned.

There were not many names. That was the strange mercy of pearl diving; it killed slowly, mostly, taking the hearing first, then the strength in the chest, then the rest. But it took some fast, and those were the ones Madame Toussaint named. Three names this year. She had been naming them for fifty years and the list grew longer by inches, the way a coral head grows, which is to say imperceptibly and then all at once, when you look away and look back.

Zeph mouthed the third name along with her. Tomas Liorc. He'd gone down on a cold morning four years ago and come up wrong, blood in his ears, and died on the dock with his boots still on. Zeph had been thirteen. They remembered mostly the boots — his mother hadn't been able to bear unlacing them, so they'd buried him laced, and Zeph had thought about those boots for weeks afterward, underwater somewhere in their own head, still tied, still waiting to be walked ashore.

Then the ceremony was over, and the ceremony became work, as ceremonies do.

By midmorning Zeph was in the water, and the water was the same as it always was, which is to say it was home in a way that land had never quite managed to be. Down below the light went green, then blue-green, then a blue so deep it felt less like a color than like a decision. The oyster beds lay in rows the company had marked with stakes, though the oysters themselves ignored the stakes and grew where they pleased, because oysters had never signed anything.

Zeph worked the rope, dropped the weighted stone, and slid down it into the cold. Forty feet. Fifty. The pressure closed around the chest like a hand — gentle at first, then less gentle, then insistent, the sea reminding them again, *I am heavier than you, I am heavier than everything.* At the bottom the world went quiet except for the heartbeat, huge and slow, a drum in a cathedral. Fingers found shell. Shell came free. Into the net bag, over and over, until the lungs began their argument and Zeph kicked for the surface and broke into the daylight gasping, alive, richer by twenty shells and poorer by one breath.

Papa hauled the bag aboard. "Good cluster," he said, turning a shell in his brown fingers. "Company'll be pleased."

"Will they?"

"They're never pleased. But they'll be pleased-adjacent." He grinned, quick and crooked, the grin Zeph had inherited along with the lungs. Then the grin faded, and he glanced toward shore, toward the white house on the hill, and lowered his voice even though the nearest other boat was thirty yards off. "Your mama talked to me last night."

"About what?"

"About you. About after this season." He turned the oyster shell over once more, then set it down carefully, the way he handled anything that might hold something valuable inside it. "She thinks you should ask Delacroix about buying out the last of the contract. Cash, at season's end. We've got the pearl money put by, near enough."

Zeph treaded water beside the boat and felt the phrase settle over them like the pressure of the deep. *Buying out.* Freedom, priced. It was the dream every diver's child carried and almost none of them reached — the contract burned, the name struck from the company ledger, the whole Atlantic suddenly open in every direction.

"We don't have near enough," Zeph said. "We have *some*. There's a difference. Odile taught me the word for it."

"Near enough, with a good season. And there's talk—" Papa stopped himself. He was bad at stopping himself; it was one of his defining features. "There's talk the company's bringing in new terms this year. Delacroix has letters from the head office. If we wait, the buy-out price may—"

"Papa." Zeph caught the gunwale. "What kind of new terms?"

He didn't answer right away, and in that silence Zeph heard the thing they would spend the rest of the season learning to hear: the sound of a man deciding how much truth a boat can carry.

"Nothing settled," he said finally. "Talk. And talk on Sainte-Marée is worth about as much as an empty shell." He clapped the gunwale twice, briskly, the conversation declared closed by executive order. "Up the ladder. We've got beds to strip before the tide turns."

* * *

They came in with the afternoon light going gold, eleven boats heavy with shell, and the whole dock quarter turned out to watch the unload — not because the unload was interesting, but because the dock quarter turned out for everything. News traveled on Sainte-Marée the way water traveled through limestone: invisibly, inevitably, finding every crack. By the time the last basket was weighed, everyone knew three things.

First: the season's opening take was good. Second: Madame Toussaint's grandson had gotten engaged to a girl from Port-Rouge, which was either wonderful or suspicious depending on whom you asked, since Port-Rouge girls were widely suspected of having ideas. Third: the mail packet had come in on the morning tide, ahead of schedule, and it had brought a passenger.

That was the one that made the dock go quiet in patches, quiet spreading like oil on water.

Passengers were rare. The packet brought post, supplies, company clerks, and once a year an inspector who counted everyone like chickens. But a *passenger* — a person with no business listed, no crate assigned, no reason — that was an event. And this passenger had come ashore at the company stairs, been met by Delacroix's clerk, and installed in the guest room of the white house, and nobody had seen her since except Baptiste the fish-boy, who swore she was a woman, tall, with a captain's coat and a sword at her hip, and who was widely disregarded because Baptiste also swore he'd once seen a mermaid and described it in detail that matched his cousin's new wife exactly.

Zeph heard all of this from Odile, who heard it from three separate sources while ostensibly helping stack baskets.

"A captain's coat," Zeph said.

"A *captain's coat*, Zeph. On our island. Where the largest vessel is Papa's boat, which we have all agreed to call a boat and not a bathtub out of politeness."

"Maybe she's the inspector."

"Inspectors wear grey and look constipated. This woman has a *sword*." Odile leaned close, dropping her voice to the register reserved for genuinely important intelligence, such as engagements and prices. "And here's the part. Marta at the company store says the clerk registered her in the book. Wrote the name down himself. And Marta saw the page."

"And?"

Odile paused, savoring it, because Odile believed information, like rum, should be poured slowly.

"*Voss,*" she said. "Captain Ilsa Voss. Out of Corviere, on the mainland. And Zeph — Marta says the clerk wrote it twice. Once in the visitors' column." Her eyes went bright. "And once in a column Marta's never seen used before. A column headed *Persons of Interest to the Crown*."

The dock noise went on around them — baskets thumping, scales clanking, somebody's rooster making its case for importance — but for Zeph the sound pulled back, distant, like the surface seen from forty feet down.

Voss. The name landed somewhere deep and specific, in a place Zeph kept locked and visited rarely: a summer afternoon four years ago, sunlight on the causeway, a tall woman in a captain's coat kneeling in the sand to speak to a thirteen-year-old at eye level, laughing at something, pressing a coin into their hand and saying, *One day I may need a favor, little fish, and you strike me as someone who pays their debts.*

A promise made in sunlight. Zeph had nearly forgotten it. Now it surfaced all at once, whole, like a body coming up from the deep.

"Zeph?" Odile was frowning at them. "You've gone pale. Well. Paler."

"I have to go home," Zeph said. "There's — I have to check something."

They left Odile mid-sentence and took the long way, along the tideline, where the evening water slid in and out over the sand with its endless patience, its endless weight. Behind them the white house on the hill caught the last of the sun and threw it back across the harbor, blinding, and in one of its six-pane windows a lamp was being lit — a small square of gold, burning in a room where a stranger with a sword now slept.

Zeph walked faster. Under the floorboard at home, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a contract, a coin, and a memory — and the tide, as it always did, was already beginning to turn.
