## 2. A Promise Made in Sunlight

The floorboard came up the way it always did — reluctantly, with a groan of old nails, as though the house itself disapproved of what Zeph kept beneath it.

Zeph knelt on the packed-earth floor of their mother's old room and pried at the board's edge with a fingernail, then with the flat of a knife, then with the particular combination of patience and profanity that all hidden things eventually demand. The board gave. Cool air rose from the gap, smelling of salt and oiled cloth and two years of waiting.

The oilcloth bundle was exactly where they'd left it. Zeph drew it out into the lamplight and unwrapped it slowly, the way you'd unwrap something sleeping.

Inside lay three things.

A coin: silver, worn nearly smooth, stamped with a ship on one side and a woman holding scales on the other. Not worth a week's rations, honestly. Worth everything, in every way that mattered.

A contract: Zeph's indenture papers, the Meridian Company's seal pressed fat and purple into the bottom corner like a waxen thumbprint. *Zephyrine Joubert, aged nine years, term of service twenty-one years, compensation to the family as agreed.* The word *compensation* had always struck Zeph as obscene. There was no compensation. There had only ever been subtraction.

And a memory — which is to say, nothing physical at all, except that when Zeph lifted the coin and turned it in the light, the memory came with it, whole and sunlit, the way it always did.

* * *

It had been three summers ago, in the long gold stretch between pearl season and storm season, when the harbor filled with ships that had nowhere urgent to be and the island briefly believed itself the center of the world.

Captain Ilsa Voss had come ashore with her sleeves rolled and her boots unpolished, which on most captains would have meant ruin, but on Ilsa Voss meant comfort. She commanded the *Kindly Meridian* — no relation to the company, she liked to insist, "and if they think otherwise they can take it up with my cannon" — a tramping merchantman that smelled of tar and cinnamon and carried, that year, forty barrels of salt pork, a consortium of very quiet passengers, and one twelve-year-old son who wished passionately to be anywhere else.

The boy's name was Anselm, and he was, by his own account, drowning.

"I'm going to be eaten by a crab," Anselm announced to the entire beach, "and no one will care."

Zeph, sixteen and waist-deep in the shallows with a net over one shoulder, considered this. "What kind of crab?"

"What kind—" Anselm stared at them. "Does it matter?"

"Of course it matters." Zeph waded closer. "A spider crab can barely open an oyster. You'd die of boredom first. A coconut robber, now, that's different. They can crack a whole coconut. They could absolutely manage a boy."

Anselm looked at the water, then back at Zeph, visibly recalculating his relationship with the sea. "You're joking."

"I'm mostly joking."

"That's worse!"

Zeph laughed, and something about the laugh — or perhaps simply the fact that Zeph was the first person on the island who had spoken to him as anything other than *the captain's boy* — cracked whatever shell Anselm had arrived in. By afternoon they were racing crabs across the wet sand. By evening he had been formally introduced to Odile, who took one look at him and declared that anyone that pale needed feeding immediately, and by the end of the week Anselm Voss had appointed himself an honorary resident of the tidal island, a title no one had offered and no one dared revoke.

His mother came ashore less often. Captain Voss was not a woman who wasted daylight, and there was always cargo to argue over, manifests to dispute, and the factor Delacroix to charm — which she did, effortlessly and with visible contempt, in the way of someone who finds a game easy and refuses to pretend otherwise.

Zeph met her properly on the fifth day, at dusk, on the seawall.

"You're the Joubert child," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Zeph."

"Zeph." She said it like she was testing a knot. "My son talks about you. He says you know where the deep pearls are."

"He says that because I told him that. To get him to stop talking about his Latin tutor."

That earned a short bark of a laugh, quickly bitten off, as though laughter were something she rationed. She was younger than Zeph had expected — thirty-five, maybe, with sun-scoured cheeks and gray coming in early at her temples, and hands that told a longer story than her face did. Rope callus. A scar across the back of the right hand, old and white. She looked out at the harbor, where her ship rode the last light, and then down at Zeph, and her expression did something complicated.

"The Meridian Company holds your paper," she said.

"Yes."

"How many years left?"

"Fourteen."

She nodded slowly. Fourteen years. Zeph watched her do the arithmetic — watch her arrive, without being told, at the number Zeph tried never to say aloud: fourteen more years meant Zeph would be thirty, and their mother would have been dead eleven years by then, and the whole bright span between here and there belonged, hour by hour, to the purple seal on a piece of paper under a floorboard.

"It's honest work," Zeph said, because it was the thing you said.

"It's honest work," Captain Voss agreed, in the tone of someone agreeing that a cage was, technically, shelter. Then she crouched, so their eyes were level, which no adult had bothered to do since Zeph's mother died. "Let me tell you something about honest work, Zeph. The sea doesn't care whose paper you carry. When you're under the water, you belong to yourself or you don't come up. Remember that. Whatever else they own — and they'll tell you they own a great deal — the breath in your lungs is yours. Spend it like it's yours."

Zeph didn't fully understand her then. They understood that she meant well, the way a compass means well; the direction of the meaning was clear even if the country it pointed toward was not.

"All right," Zeph said.

"Good." She stood, joints cracking like rigging in a squall. "Now. My son tells me you know where the good mussels are, and he has informed me — loudly, at breakfast — that ship's biscuit is an abomination. Show us where the mussels are, and I'll consider your debt to this island paid."

"They're not really in debt," Zeph said. "I just didn't want him talking about the Latin tutor again."

"A wise policy," said Captain Voss gravely. "Mussels, then. For the republic."

* * *

The mussels became a ritual. The *Kindly Meridian* came three times that summer, and each time, Anselm came ashore like a cork released from a bottle, and Zeph showed him rock pools and rope knots and how to read the tide tables upside down, and Anselm taught Zeph chess, badly, and three words of High Cantorian, uselessly, and how to lose gracefully, which he himself never managed.

And Captain Voss sat on the seawall with a mug of Odile's terrible coffee and talked to Zeph the way she probably talked to no one — not as a diver, not as company property, but as a person whose opinion on matters of tide and weather and human nature might be worth having.

It was on the third visit that she asked the question.

They were on the seawall again, watching Anselm attempt to teach Odile's youngest to sail a coracle that was manifestly unsuited to sailing, and Captain Voss said, without preamble:

"If you weren't indentured. What would you do?"

No one had ever asked Zeph that. Not once, in sixteen years. The question sat in Zeph's chest like swallowed sunlight.

"Dive," Zeph said, after a while. "But free. My own boat, my own grounds. Sell to whoever pays best. Or — I don't know. Chart the reefs. Everyone charts the shipping lanes, nobody charts the living part of the sea, the coral, where things breed. Someone should map it before it's all dragged. Like making an atlas," they added, embarrassed by the size of the thought, "but for underwater."

Captain Voss was quiet for a long moment.

"An atlas of the drowned world," she said.

"It's stupid—"

"It is not stupid." And she said it with such force that Zeph looked up, startled, and found her looking at them with an expression that was almost fierce. "Do you know how many people I've sailed with who had a thought like that in them? A real one? Most people's dreams fit in a coat pocket, Zeph. Yours needs a shelf." She drained her coffee, grimaced at it — Odile's coffee deserved grimacing at — and set the mug down with a decisive clack. "So. Here is what will happen. In four years, when my charter runs out and I take my money and retire to somewhere with worse weather and better wine, I will need a chartmaker. Someone to sound my waters, keep my hulls off the rocks, and make beautiful maps of ugly coastlines. It pays poorly at first and the food is terrible and I am, by all reliable accounts, a tyrant."

Zeph's heart was doing something unseemly. "But my paper—"

"Your paper has fourteen years on it. Mine ends in four. Which means," she said, tapping the seawall with one scarred finger, "that in four years I will be able to buy out an indenture outright, with change left over, if I choose to. And I am telling you now, in front of the sea and the mussels and my idiot son, that I choose to. If you'll have me."

The harbor lights were coming on. Somewhere behind them, Anselm had fallen in the water and was pretending he hadn't. The world was ordinary and golden and entirely intact.

"Why?" Zeph asked. It came out smaller than intended. "Why would you spend that much on me?"

Captain Voss looked at them for a moment, and something in her face softened into something older and sadder.

"Because somebody bought me once," she said quietly. "When I was younger than you, and worth nothing on any market, somebody spent money they couldn't afford on me, and never explained why, and never let me thank them properly. So." She stuck out her scarred right hand. "Consider this the explanation. Do we have an agreement, chartmaker?"

Zeph took the hand. Her grip was like an anchor chain and twice as warm.

"We have an agreement."

"Then shake on it properly, because I don't make promises twice." And then, softer, with the ghost of a smile: "Fourteen years goes fast, Zeph. Faster than you think. The tide turns whether you watch it or not."

* * *

That had been three years ago.

Three years, eleven months remaining, and the *Kindly Meridian* had not been seen since the spring.

Zeph sat back on their heels in the lamplight, the coin warm in their palm, and made themselves count the facts the way you count breath before a deep dive.

Fact: Ilsa Voss keeps her word. She had shaken on it in front of the sea.

Fact: the war had reached the shipping lanes in the autumn. The *Kindly Meridian* ran the northern route last anyone heard.

Fact: today a strange captain with a sword had taken the white house on the hill, and the factor Delacroix was dead on his own floor, and the seasonal bridge would close within days, sealing the island until spring.

Fact: Zeph had heard the name *Voss* spoken exactly once today, in the harbor office, by a clerk who didn't know Zeph was behind him — spoken low, to the new commander, followed by the words *enemy of the crown*, followed by the words *and the son, if he's on the island.*

Zeph closed their hand around the coin until its edges bit.

Outside, the tide was turning. Down at the mangroves, in the place where the water went thin and the roots went thick, there was a half-rotted skiff that everyone had agreed for years was worthless — everyone except Zeph, who had been patching its seams in secret since midsummer, against a day they hadn't let themselves name.

The lamp guttered. Zeph wrapped the contract back in its oilcloth, put the coin in their pocket where it lay against their hip like a small hot coal, and pressed the floorboard down.

Fourteen years went fast, she'd said.

Somebody, apparently, had decided they'd go faster still — and Zeph had four days, a broken boat, and a promise to keep before the bridge closed and the sea itself became a locked door.
