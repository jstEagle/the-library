## 3. The Bridge Closes

The tide road to Meridian Cay did not believe in schedules. It believed in the moon, and the moon had opinions, and for eleven months of the year the road revealed itself twice a day like a shy actor who had to be coaxed from behind a curtain of water. Zephyrine Joubert had walked it since before they could walk properly — first strapped to their mother's back while Odile balanced a basket of oysters, later carrying baskets themselves, and now, at seventeen, walking it with the easy arrogance of someone who knew every sandbar by its temperament.

The twelfth month was different. In Storm Season, the road stayed under.

Every year on the first of the Sowing Winds, the people of the cay gathered at the western spit to watch the sea close its door. It was not a ceremony anyone had planned. It had simply become one, the way things do when they happen often enough: old men grumbling, children racing the last dry stretch of sand, someone always bringing fried dough that everyone pretended not to want and then ate all of.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of the crowd with a fried dough in each hand and watched the water come up the road the way it always did — not rushing, but patient, the way a debt collector is patient. The tide didn't chase you off the causeway. It simply arrived, inch by inch, until staying became a thing only fools and poets considered.

"There she goes," said Pépin, beside them, mouth full. He was fourteen, broad as a crab pot, and had been Zephyrine's best friend since the day he'd fallen off the diving raft and Zephyrine had fished him out mostly out of curiosity about what noise he'd make. "Another year on the rock."

"Another year of your company," Zephyrine said. "The sea is cruel."

"I'm a delight."

"You're a barnacle. You've attached yourself to me and there's no scraping you off."

Pépin considered this seriously. "Barnacles get free rides and don't have to work. I'd like that arrangement formalized."

Down the beach, the last of the children shrieked and scrambled up the spit as the water took the road. Someone's grandmother clapped. Somebody else's dog barked at the ocean, which ignored him with the dignity of an institution. And that was the whole of it: the door closed, the island sealed, and six hundred souls settled in for the season with their salted fish and their grudges and their small, necessary kindnesses.

Zephyrine felt the closing in their chest the way they always did — half grief, half relief. No ships now but the company's cutter, which came when it pleased or didn't come at all. No letters. No new faces. Just the cay, and everyone on it, floating alone in a gray sea until spring.

They finished the second fried dough and thought, as they had every day for three weeks, of Captain Ilsa Voss, and the promise made in sunlight, and the boy asleep in the mangroves with a bullet hole through his shoulder.

* * *

The trouble with promises is that they keep no calendar. This one had been made in high summer, back when the tide road ran dry and ships came weekly and the word *enemy* still belonged safely to old wars in far-off books.

Ilsa Voss had captained the *Cormorant*, a supply brig out of the northern ports, and she had come to the cay four seasons running with flour and iron nails and stories that made the whole harbor stay up too late. She'd taught Zephyrine to read a compass properly, not the company's way — which was to say, badly, on purpose, so divers couldn't find their own way to good beds — but truly, needle and heart together. She paid her crew fairly. She once threw a customs officer into the harbor for spitting near Odile, and Odile, who forgave almost nothing, had forgiven her instantly.

And she had a son. Tomas. Quiet boy, quick hands, seasick in any weather calmer than a monsoon, which everyone agreed was the funniest thing about him. He used to sit on the company dock drawing maps of islands that didn't exist, and he gave them names, and Zephyrine had spent one entire golden week helping him chart the archipelago of Nowhere.

Then the war came, the way war does — sideways, in paperwork first. The Crown declared the northern ports hostile. The Meridian Trading Company, whose loyalty was to whoever signed its ledgers, hoisted new colors overnight. And the *Cormorant* made one last run, not for cargo but for her son, because Ilsa Voss was many things but never once a woman who left her child on enemy ground.

She hadn't made it out clean. There had been shooting at the breakwater — Zephyrine had heard it from the drying sheds, distant and flat, like someone clapping very slowly. By nightfall Tomas was in the mangroves with lead in his shoulder, and his mother stood in Zephyrine's doorway with rain coming off her in sheets, saying the words Zephyrine would carry all season:

*I can't take him out yet — they're watching every hull. But when the road closes, they'll stop looking. Nobody leaves in Storm Season; nobody checks. You promised me safe passage once the water rose, Zeph. I'm asking you to keep it.*

Zephyrine had promised. That was the whole of the problem. They had promised in peacetime, when promises were cheap as fried dough, and now the water had risen, and the promise weighed more than the island.

* * *

Three days after the closing, Zephyrine went out at low water to check on the boy, telling their mother they were gathering conch.

Odile Joubert missed nothing, but she had raised her child on a policy of strategic blindness — see only what you can survive seeing — and so she only said, "Conch are east. You're going west," and went back to mending nets, and let them go.

Tomas Voss lay where they'd left him, in the hollow between the mangrove roots where the mosquitoes were fat and the air smelled of rot and green life. He'd built himself a shelter of palm fronds that leaned hopefully to one side, the architectural opinion of a boy who was dying and stubborn about it. His color was bad. The wound had been sewn with sail thread by Zephyrine's own shaking hands, and it had held, but the flesh around it was hot and angry red.

"You're late," Tomas said. His voice was dry paper. "I marked the days on a root. We should discuss my accommodations. The neighbors are crabs, and they're terrible gossips."

"They're also better company than you."

"Lies." He tried to grin and it cost him; Zephyrine saw the wince he tried to hide and pretended not to. That was the etiquette of wounds: you both agreed the hurt wasn't happening. "Any news?"

"The road's closed. Company posted the notice. No traffic till spring." Zephyrine crouched, unwrapped the water skin, held it to his lips. "Which cuts two ways. Nobody hunts you. Nobody helps you either."

"Ilsa will come back."

"In Storm Season? On that sea?" Zephyrine kept their voice light, which was a skill, learned young, from a mother who could joke during a hurricane. Inside, they were doing arithmetic that never came out even. Even if Ilsa braved the storms, the company's cutter patrolled the deep channel. Even if she slipped past it, she couldn't land — the breakwater guns were manned year-round now. And even if she landed, the boy couldn't walk to a boat.

There was no version of this where Zephyrine did less than everything.

"Eat," they said, digging conch out of the basket. "Chew slowly. If you die of something stupid after I've kept you alive this long, I'll be very annoyed, and I'll tell your mother, and she'll throw me in the harbor too."

"She wouldn't. She likes you." Tomas accepted the meat and chewed, grimacing. "It needs lemon."

"It needs a kitchen, a cook, and a kingdom. Eat your rubber and be grateful."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and Zephyrine sat with him while it passed, watching the channel through the mangroves out of habit. Empty gray water. Somewhere beyond it, a captain with no country, counting waves.

* * *

That evening the cay gathered in the company square for the Ration Reading, which was the closest thing Storm Season had to theater.

Factor Delacroix stood on the company steps with the great ledger open on the lectern before him, reading out allotments in a voice like a man announcing his own funeral arrangements. Delacroix was sixty, thin as a mast, with white hair combed with mathematical precision and a habit of pausing before each name as though consulting the ledger's soul rather than its columns. Everyone hated the Ration Readings. Everyone attended anyway. On an island, grievance is entertainment.

"—Joubert, Odile: rice, eight measures; oil, one; salt fish, six. Diver's rate."

A murmur went through the crowd. Diver's rate was diver's rate, fair enough — but the murmur was because the measure before had been short. Everyone had heard it. Delacroix heard it too, and lifted one pale hand.

"The company reminds the community," he said, "that Storm Season stores are finite. Adjustments have been made equitably across all accounts."

"Equitable," muttered Pépin, next to Zephyrine, with the venom of a boy who'd been given five measures instead of seven. "My father hauled rope for this company thirty years and equitable just shrunk our dinner."

"Shh."

"I'm shushing. Loudly. Internally."

Delacroix continued down the list, name by name, and Zephyrine watched him and thought, not for the first time, that the factor looked worse than usual — gray around the mouth, hands unsteady on the great book. Rumor said the company had ordered the winter stores doubled against the war and then halved again by some clerk in a distant office, and that Delacroix had to stand here every season and read out the consequences of other people's arithmetic. Zephyrine almost pitied him. Almost. Pity for factors was a luxury, like fresh fruit.

Then Delacroix reached the bottom of the page, closed the ledger with a soft, final sound like a lid on a coffin, and added, in a voice pitched lower than usual:

"One further notice. By order of the company and the Crown's warrant: the island is hereby under seal. Any vessel approaching these waters without company colors will be fired upon. Any person found aiding persons traveling under false pretense—" and here his eyes crossed the crowd without resting anywhere, though Zephyrine felt the words go past them like a blade passed close enough to part hair, "—will answer to company law. There is no appeal until spring. Good evening."

He stepped down. The lanterns guttered. Six hundred people walked home through the dark doing quiet math of their own.

* * *

Zephyrine didn't sleep that night, which was fine; sleep was for people whose futures fit inside one season.

Near dawn they climbed to the widow's walk above the drying sheds, where the whole cay showed itself at once: the crooked streets, the company warehouse with its new flag, the mangrove shore hiding a boy with a hole in him, and the great gray sea, doorless now for months. Somewhere out there was a boat that needed finding, or building, or borrowing. Somewhere out there was a plan that didn't exist yet.

Their mother came up the ladder without asking permission, because it was her roof and her child, and stood at the rail looking at the same gray nothing.

"West conch beds," Odile said. "Interesting diving grounds this time of year."

"Mama—"

"I raised you on stories about your father," Odile said, to the horizon. "You know what I never told you? That the last thing he said to me was a lie. Said he'd skip the deep bed that day, weather turning. Went anyway. Deep beds pay double, and we owed the company that quarter." Her jaw worked. "So understand me, Zeph. When I say mind your own business, it is not because I am blind. It is because I know exactly what the sea charges for other people's business, and I have already paid once, in full, and I do not buy twice."

Zephyrine looked at her — at the net-scarred hands, the face weathered into something like the cliffs, beautiful in the way of things that refuse to fall down.

"I already promised," they said quietly.

"I know." Odile sighed, long and slow, a tide going out. "You're your father's child after all. Loyal past reason." She turned from the rail. "There's old Bastien's boat under the south tarp. Hull's sound. Don't tell me why you asked, because then I'd know, and knowing is expensive."

She went down the ladder. Zephyrine stood alone on the walk as the sun came up the color of brass, and below, across the roofs, the company bell rang the hour — and then, oddly, rang it again. A mistake, probably. The bell-ringer was new.

Probably a mistake.

By noon it was not a mistake, and the whole island knew: Factor Delacroix had not appeared for morning inventory. His office was locked from the inside. And little Marguerite Sennet, sent up with his tea, had come down the warehouse stairs white as bone-dock foam, saying nothing, holding her empty tray with both hands, walking as carefully as a girl carrying water across a tilting deck.

The company militia was already forming up in the square, bayonets catching the brass-colored sun.
