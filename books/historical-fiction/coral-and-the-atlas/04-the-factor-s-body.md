## 4. The Factor's Body

The morning the factor was found, the island smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet — that green, metallic promise hanging over the mangroves — and Zephyrine Joubert was elbow-deep in a basket of oysters, arguing with their grandmother about the proper way to shuck.

"You're butchering them," Odile said, not looking up from her net-mending. She could mend a net, thread a needle, and insult three generations of a family without ever raising her eyes from her work. It was a gift.

"I'm *opening* them."

"You're committing violence. There's a difference. A good shuck is a conversation. You ask the oyster, and it agrees."

"The oyster is dead, Grand-mère."

"All the more reason to be polite."

Zephyrine was about to explain — for perhaps the four-hundredth time — that politeness was a luxury for people who weren't rationing their breakfast, when the church bell began to ring. Not the measured nine strokes that marked the company's morning accounting. This was fast. Frantic. The kind of ringing that made your hands stop moving on their own.

Odile's needle paused mid-stitch.

"That's Père Anselme," she said. "And he only rings like that for two things. A drowning, or a death on land."

The oyster knife slipped from Zephyrine's fingers and clattered against the basket rim. They both knew there was a third thing the bell had rung for, once, long ago — but nobody on Brise-Sèche liked to say the word *shipwreck* out loud, as if the sea might take it as an invitation.

They went with the crowd. Everyone went with the crowd; that was the nature of the island. Two hundred souls packed onto a crescent of coral and sand no bigger than a rich man's garden, connected to the mainland by a causeway the tide swallowed twice a day. When something happened on Brise-Sèche, it happened to everyone, and everyone wanted to see it first so they could be the one telling it correctly at the fish tables that evening.

The crowd was thickest at the foot of the company house, where the factor's residence stood — the largest building on the island, whitewashed twice a year, with shutters the blue of deep water and a veranda where Factor Gaspard Delacroix took his coffee every morning at seven, visible and punctual as the sun.

He was not on the veranda now.

He was at the bottom of the sea-stairs.

* * *

Zephyrine saw him from the edge of the crowd, over the shoulders of the fisherwomen and the company clerks and old Tomas the netmaker, who had climbed onto an upturned dinghy to see better and was narrating to anyone within earshot in the confident tones of a man who had seen nothing at all.

"Slipped, you see. Slipped on the wet stones. Old men and sea-stairs, it's the oldest story—"

"Factor Delacroix is fifty-one," said Madame Roux the baker, "and he danced at my daughter's wedding until the musicians went home."

"He danced *badly*," Tomas conceded, "but he danced."

Zephyrine pushed through to the front, Odile's grip on their wrist the only thing that kept them from getting closer. The sea-stairs descended from the company house's private landing in twelve broad stone steps, worn smooth by a century of boots, and at the bottom of them, folded against the lowest step with his head resting against the barnacled stone as though listening for something in the water, lay Gaspard Delacroix.

He was still in his nightclothes. That was the detail Zephyrine's mind seized on and would not release — the factor, who rose at six and was dressed by seven, who believed that a man in his dressing gown was a man already halfway to ruin, lying in the grey dawn in his nightshirt with his bare feet white as the belly of a fish. One shoe was missing. The other lay three steps up, upright, absurdly neat.

There was blood. Not a great deal of it, but enough — a dark thread in the water pooling around the lowest step, thinning as the tide crept in, as though the sea were patiently trying to tidy it away.

Père Anselme knelt beside the body, his cassock soaked to the knee, one hand resting on the factor's chest. He looked up at the crowd, and Zephyrine had known the old priest their whole life — had watched him baptize half the island's children and bury the other half's grandparents — and had never once seen him look afraid.

He looked afraid now.

"Someone fetch the company's men," he said quietly. "And someone else fetch a sheet."

* * *

The company's men came, but slowly, in the way that everything on Brise-Sèche happened slowly except the tide. There were four of them — the private militia that Meridian Trading kept on the island ostensibly to protect the pearl stores from pirates, though everyone knew the pearls were far more likely to be stolen from inside than outside. They wore grey coats with brass buttons, and they were led by a man named Sergeant Brant, who had arrived two years earlier from the mainland and had never once been seen to smile, which the islanders respected, in a way, as a form of honesty.

Brant stood at the top of the sea-stairs and looked down at the body for a long time. The crowd watched him watch. It was, Zephyrine thought later, like watching a man read a letter he had been expecting.

"Nobody touches anything," Brant said at last. "Nobody leaves the island."

A murmur went through the crowd — not of surprise, exactly. More the sound of a door being quietly closed.

"The bridge closes tonight regardless," someone called out. "Tide's already turning."

"Then it closes early." Brant's eyes moved across the faces below him, unhurried, cataloguing. "Nobody leaves the island," he said again, "until the company says otherwise."

Zephyrine felt Odile's hand tighten on their wrist, and understood, in the way one understands weather, that the world had just changed shape.

* * *

They held the questioning in the company warehouse, because the warehouse was the only building big enough, and because Brant apparently believed that the smell of brine and pearl-shell put people in a cooperative mood. The islanders filed in by household. Zephyrine came with Odile, and found themselves seated on a crate of rope between the Roux family and old Tomas, who had finally gotten his story straight and was whispering it to anyone who would listen: he had seen a light at the company house around midnight, he said. A lantern, moving in an upstairs window. Moving *quickly*.

"Quickly how?" Zephyrine whispered.

"Quickly like someone carrying it in a hurry."

"Thank you, Tomas. That's very precise."

"I'm a precise man."

"You're a man who saw a light."

"Which is more than you saw."

This was true, and Zephyrine shut up about it.

Sergeant Brant stood at the front beside a clerk with a ledger, and asked his questions in the flat voice of a man counting coins. Who had seen the factor yesterday evening? Who had quarreled with him? Who owed him money?

The last question produced a silence so complete that Zephyrine could hear the tide turning outside, the soft gargle of the channel beginning to fill.

Because everyone owed him money. That was the thing about Factor Delacroix that nobody had said aloud while he lived, and which his death now made suddenly, dangerously sayable: he had been the island's lender, its bookkeeper, its benevolent-seeming master, and half of Brise-Sèche carried his marks against their names. The Rouxs owed for flour during the bad season. Tomas owed for net-twine. Odile — Zephyrine glanced sideways — Odile's debts went back further than Zephyrine's memory, into the years when their mother was still diving, and were the reason Zephyrine wore the company's brass indenture tag on a cord around their neck like a small, cold medal.

"Nobody?" Brant said, when the silence had gone on long enough to curdle. "Interesting. A man who lends to an entire island and is owed by no one."

"He was generous," said Madame Roux, with the rigid dignity of a woman saying something she did not believe for the record.

"He was," Brant agreed, and Zephyrine could not tell if the sergeant was mocking her or the word itself.

The questioning went on through the morning. Zephyrine was asked what they had been doing the previous night, and answered truthfully: mending dive baskets, eating a supper of rice and the last of the salted fish, sleeping. Odile was asked the same and gave the same answer, though she added, with a look at Brant that would have curdled milk at ten paces, that she was seventy-three years old, weighed less than the factor's boots, and had not climbed a stair without complaint since the last coronation of anyone.

Brant wrote it all down. He was, Zephyrine noticed, very thorough with the writing and very sparing with the looking. He asked about the factor's habits, his visitors, his correspondence. He asked whether the factor had received any letters from the mainland recently.

And here something happened that Zephyrine would turn over in their mind for many nights afterward. Père Anselme — seated near the front, his hands folded, his face composed — said, "He received letters constantly. He was the company's man. Letters were his bread."

"Yes," Brant said. "But any letters he was expecting. From anyone in particular."

The priest was quiet a moment. "He did not confide in me, Sergeant. He was not a confiding man."

"No," Brant said. "He was not."

* * *

They were released near noon, and the island did what an island does with a death: it cooked. Not out of callousness — out of necessity, and out of a kind of stubborn ceremony. A death was to be marked with food, and the fact that the dead man was the factor, the man who set the rations, the man everyone owed, did not change the rule. It only made the cooking more interesting.

By late afternoon the fish tables near the church were crowded, and the talk was of exactly the kind Zephyrine had predicted at dawn. Madame Roux had brought fried bread. Tomas had brought his lantern story, now improved with a second light, seen by his wife, who had been asleep at the time. Children ran between the tables stealing crumbs. The tide had come fully in over the causeway, and somewhere out in the grey water the mainland sat invisible, suddenly as distant as the moon.

Zephyrine sat with Odile and a plate of fried bread, watching the crowd.

"You're doing your thinking face," Odile said. "Put it away before someone sees."

"I'm allowed to think."

"You're allowed to shuck. Thinking is for people with no debts." But she said it without teeth, and Zephyrine knew she was rattled too. She was tearing her bread into smaller and smaller pieces and not eating any of them, which for Odile was the equivalent of screaming.

"The shoe bothers me," Zephyrine said quietly.

"What shoe?"

"The factor's shoe. It was three steps up. Upright. Neat." Zephyrine turned the image over again, the way they turned shells in the sun, looking for the flaw. "If a man slips on wet stones, Grand-mère, he falls. He doesn't stop partway down to take off one shoe and stand it up."

"Maybe it came off in the fall."

"Upright? On a stair? Facing downhill?"

Odile was quiet. Then, very quietly, in the voice she used for things that were true and unwelcome: "Then it didn't come off in the fall. It was put there."

"By whom?"

"By someone who wanted it to look like a slip." She finally ate a piece of bread, as if to seal the conversation. "And now you're going to stop thinking about it, because the people who arrange shoes on stairs are not people a pearl-diver's child investigates. You dive. You bring up shells. You keep your head under the water where it's safe."

"The water isn't safe, Grand-mère. That's rather its defining feature."

"The water is honest," Odile said. "That's what I mean. It only drowns you. It doesn't drown you and then stand your shoe up to make it look like you wanted to drown."

Zephyrine laughed despite everything — a short, startled sound — and Odile almost smiled, which on her face was an event like an eclipse, rare and not to be looked at directly.

It was then, across the crowded tables, that Zephyrine saw the other thing. The thing that turned the day from a dark story into a dark story with teeth.

A boy was slipping along the edge of the gathering — sixteen or so, tall, dark-haired, moving with the particular careful lightness of someone trying not to be seen by anyone specific. He was not from the island. Zephyrine knew every face on Brise-Sèche the way they knew every rock in the diving grounds, and this face was new. It was bruised along one side. It kept its eyes down. And it was heading, with the unerring instinct of the hunted, toward the tangle of mangroves and old hulls at the island's northern edge, where the wrecks went to be forgotten.

Zephyrine knew that walk. More than that — Zephyrine knew that *plan*, because it had been described to them, in detail, on a sunlit afternoon two years ago, by a woman with a captain's voice and a laugh like rigging in the wind, who had held Zephyrine's hand across a table in the Meridian guesthouse and made them promise something that had seemed, at the time, like the easiest promise in the world.

*If anything ever happens to me,* Captain Ilsa Voss had said, *you get my Emil off whatever rock he's standing on. Whatever it costs. Promise me, little fish.*

*I promise,* Zephyrine had said, sixteen years old and immortal, certain the promise would never come due, the way one is certain of everything at sixteen.

The boy — Emil, it had to be Emil, older and hurt and terrified — glanced up once as he slipped past the tables. His eyes found Zephyrine's for half a heartbeat, and in them was a question, and a plea, and the particular desperation of someone who has run out of everywhere to run.

Then he was gone into the mangroves.

"Grand-mère," Zephyrine said, standing, heart going like a drum in a storm, "I have to check the dive baskets."

"At this hour?"

"The tide," Zephyrine said, which was the island's universal excuse, its *open sesame*, its answer to everything, and Odile — who had raised a diver's child and knew every current of their heart — looked at them for one long, knowing moment.

"Zephyrine," she said. "The factor's body is barely cold, the company's dogs are sniffing every doorstep, and you have the face of someone about to do something that will be in the story they tell about you after you're dead."

Zephyrine kissed the top of her head. "I'm just checking the baskets."

And they went — past the tables, past the church, past Sergeant Brant standing at the warehouse door with his ledger and his unsmiling face, watching the crowd with the patience of a man who already knew more than he was saying — toward the mangroves, and the wrecks, and the boy in the shadows, while behind them the tide crept up the causeway and closed the island like a fist.
