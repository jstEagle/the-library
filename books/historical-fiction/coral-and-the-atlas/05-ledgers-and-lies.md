## 5. Ledgers and Lies

The mangroves swallowed them the way they swallowed everything — quietly, root by root, until the world above the water seemed like a story someone else had told.

Zephyrine went first, barefoot, testing each tangle of roots before putting weight on it, because the mud here had opinions and none of them were kind. Behind them came the boy.

He was not really a boy. He was perhaps nineteen, which at the moment looked ancient and also unbearably young, the way drowning men look young. His name was Tomas, though he hadn't offered it; his mother's letter had. *My son. My Tomas. Whatever they say he is, he is only my son.* Captain Ilsa Voss had written that in a hand that shook, on paper Zephyrine still carried folded against their ribs, and every time the boy stumbled behind them Zephyrine felt the paper press there like a small accusing heart.

"Here," Zephyrine said, ducking under an arch of roots into the hollow of the old wreck — the *Meridian's own* *Cormorant*, dead thirty years and rotten as old fruit, its ribs opened to the sky like something that had died mid-laugh. In the hollow, wrapped in sailcloth, were the things that mattered: a waterskin, half a loaf gone hard, a pot of Odile's fish stew with cloth tied over it, and the long dive-knife with the bone handle that had been Zephyrine's mother's.

Tomas lowered himself down with a sound he tried not to make. The wound in his side had been sewn with sailmaker's thread by lamplight three nights ago, before the factor died, when everything was merely dangerous instead of fatal. It was weeping again. Zephyrine could smell it under the salt and rot — a sweet wrongness, like fruit going over.

"Let me see."

"It's fine."

"Everything is fine," Zephyrine said. "The weather is fine. The food is fine. The island is full of fine things." They peeled back the bandage and did not let their face do anything, which took practice. The stitches held, but the flesh around them was hot and striped with red. "Fine," they repeated. "But it wants cleaning, and it wants salt, and salt means the shallows, and the shallows mean being seen."

"Then don't clean it."

"And have you die of rot in my mangroves? Your mother would climb out of the sea itself to scold me, and she'd be right to." Zephyrine tore a strip from the hem of their shirt, wet it from the waterskin, and worked as gently as they could while Tomas bit down on a length of rope and stared at the canopy of leaves. Above them a heron shifted and complained, one gray resident of the island's smallest and most honest nation, which recognized no crowns and traded in nothing but fish.

When it was done, Tomas lay breathing hard and Zephyrine sat back against the hull and let the tide-sound fill the silence.

"They're saying," Tomas said at last, carefully, "that your factor was murdered."

"They're saying a great many things. That's what islands are for."

"Sergeant Brant went door to door this morning. Mama said he would." A pause. "She said he counts things when he thinks. People. Boats. Lies."

Zephyrine thought of Brant standing at the warehouse door with his ledger and his unsmiling face, patient as driftwood. "Then we should be very careful what we leave lying around for him to count."

* * *

The walk back across the island took longer than the walk out, because the island had changed shape overnight.

It was a strange thing, Zephyrine thought, how a place could be one thing for seventeen years and another thing in a single morning. Yesterday the paths between the drying racks had been full of gossip — who was courting whom, whose nets had come up empty, whether old Père Anselme had actually seen a sea serpent or merely the bottom of a rum bottle. Today the same paths were quiet in a new way, the way a house is quiet when someone in it has just died. Doors stood open a suspicious few inches. Conversations stopped when Zephyrine passed and resumed in a lower key, like tide coming back into a hole.

At the cistern, Madame Okonkwo was ladling out the morning ration with a company slate in one hand and a look on her face that could have curdled cream at forty paces.

"You're late," she said.

"The tide was late. I waited for it."

"The tide waits for no one, child, and neither does the company. Two measures today. Everyone's two measures." She dipped the tin cup with exaggerated care, as if the rice inside might be listening. "And if you see your grandmother, tell her that 'I forgot' is not a reason, 'my knees' is not a reason, and if she comes to this line tomorrow with that face, I'll give her one measure and a sermon."

"I'll tell her. She'll be delighted. She collects sermons."

Madame Okonkwo snorted despite herself, then leaned closer over the barrel, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "They searched the Perrin house this morning. The factor's own house, mind, and Brant himself turning over the man's linens like a washerwoman." Her eyes flicked up, quick and bright. "You know what he took out of it? Papers. Armfuls of papers. Not the silver, not the strongbox — the strongbox he left sitting there open and empty-eyed, and papers he carried off like they weighed more than lead."

"Maybe the factor was killed for his paperwork," Zephyrine said lightly. "Some people are killed for less."

"Some people," Madame Okonkwo said, straightening, "should learn to be boring, and fast." And she waved the next person up the line as if nothing at all had passed between them, because on this island that was how you survived: everything important was said sideways, in the space between two ordinary sentences, where even a careful man with a ledger couldn't write it down.

* * *

Grandmother Odile received the report of the boy's wound the way she received all news: sitting in her doorway shelling beans as though the beans had personally wronged her and would be made to answer for it.

"Hot and striped, you said."

"Hot and striped."

"Hm." A bean hit the bowl. Another. "There's yarrow under the north wall of the churchyard, if a person knew where to look, which you don't, so it's fortunate I know where to look and can tell you, being that the churchyard is the one place on this island where Brant's soldiers won't dig around, God and the dead having more rights here than the living." Another bean. "And honey. There's honey in the church stores, which Father Lucien guards like it's the crown jewels, which between you and me it may as well be, since the crown has taken everything else."

"Odile." Zephyrine sat down on the step beside her. "Why do you help me with this? You've never once asked me whose side the boy is on."

The old woman's hands kept moving, brown and knuckly and unhurried as the tide.

"When I was younger than you," she said, "there was a war, and both sides landed here at different times, and both sides bought our fish and ate our bread and told us we were on the winning side. Do you know what I learned?" She held up a bean, inspected it against the light. "There's no such thing as whose side a boy is on. There's only whose mother is crying for him. Everything else is paperwork." She dropped the bean in the bowl. "And I've never once trusted paperwork."

Zephyrine thought of the paper folded against their own ribs and said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

"One more thing." Odile's voice changed, the humor going out of it like a cloud over water. "Delacroix came asking after you yesterday evening. After the body was found."

"Delacroix? What business does the pilot have with me?"

"That's what I wondered. He wanted to know if you'd been out to the wrecks lately. Wanted to know it in that soft voice of his, the one he uses on the channel markers." She looked at Zephyrene then, directly, and her eyes were the flat gray of a sea before weather. "Whatever you've got hidden in those mangroves, child, someone else already knows the mangroves are worth looking in. Delacroix knows every channel and every wreck on this coast better than the fish do. If he's asking, he isn't wondering. He's confirming."

* * *

Zephyrine found Delacroix exactly where Delacroix always was at low water: at the pilot's hut at the island's eastern point, mending a chart, drinking coffee he had somehow obtained despite the rationing, and radiating the mild, well-oiled harmlessness of a man who had spent forty years making himself useful to whoever was currently in charge.

"Zephyrine Joubert!" he said warmly, without looking up. "Come in, come in. Mind the ink. Sit. Do you take sugar? Don't answer, we haven't any."

"I heard you were asking after me."

"Were you now." He finished the coastline he was inking, blew on it gently, and only then raised his eyes, which were pale and friendly and missed absolutely nothing. "Yes, I suppose I was. You know how it is — a man dies, the bridge closes, and suddenly everyone remembers everything they've ever seen and wonders if it matters. I remembered that you dive the northern wrecks. I wondered if you'd seen anything. Boats. Lights. Strangers."

"Strangers," Zephyrine repeated.

"Mm. The company is very interested in strangers just now." Delacroix set down his pen and smiled, and it was a genuinely kind smile, which somehow made it worse. "You know, I piloted the first ship Captain Voss ever brought through the shoals. Before the war, when she was just a clever woman with a clever ship and a very loud laugh. I liked her. I liked her the way you like weather — from a distance, with respect." He picked up his coffee. "So when I hear a rumor that a stranger was seen near the wrecks, a young man, limping — I think to myself, Delacroix, old man, you must be getting senile. Because if such a boy existed, and if certain people found him, it would be a tragedy. And I hate tragedies. Bad for trade."

The room was very quiet except for the tick of the drying chart and the sea below the point.

"What is it you want, Delacroix?"

"Want?" He laughed, genuinely delighted. "Nothing, nothing. Well — one thing. When this is all over, whatever happens, whichever flag ends up flying over this ridiculous rock—" he waved his cup at the window, the harbor, the closed causeway, the whole sealed world— "remember that I asked you nothing, saw nothing, and am a foolish old man who talks too much about weather." His smile thinned by a degree, the first honest thing he'd shown. "Because the company's ledger, child, is not the only book on this island. Every pilot keeps a logbook of his own. Channels, depths, sandbars. And other things. Who boarded where. What cargo never got declared. Which factors took their percentage off the top of the grain ration during the famine year and let us eat seaweed." He turned back to his chart, conversation apparently concluded. "Brant is counting things, you know. He counted all day yesterday. But a ledger only tells you what a man wrote down. It never tells you what he left out."

* * *

Zephyrine carried the yarrow home through the blue end of the day, mind churning like the channel in a crosswind. Delacroix knew. Had known, probably, from the first night. Was choosing — for now — to be a foolish old man who talked about weather. Which meant the boy's survival depended on the goodwill of a man whose entire profession was knowing exactly how much water lay under a keel, and selling passage over it.

And Delacroix had mentioned the famine year. Off the top of the grain. The factor's strongbox, open and empty-eyed.

They were so busy turning this over that they nearly walked into Sergeant Brant.

He was standing at the corner of the warehouse where the lantern didn't reach, and he did not apologize, because Brant did not occupy space so much as commandeer it. In his hands was the factor's ledger — Zephyrine recognized the tooled leather cover, the gilt company crest worn to a smudge — and he was reading it by the light of a hand-lamp held by a bored-looking soldier behind him.

"Zephyrine Joubert," Brant said. Not a question. He consulted some inner page. "Diver. Indentured, age six. Mother deceased, father unrecorded. Wages credited against debt of eleven pounds, four shillings, current balance—" his eyes flicked up— "considerable."

"That's me. Famous for it."

"Your grandmother draws a widow's ration she hasn't strictly qualified for since the second year of the war. Nobody's corrected the entry." He said it the way he'd read the numbers, flat and factual, but something in it wasn't a threat. It was almost — curiosity. "Funny thing about ledgers, Joubert. Everyone on this island is in one. And everyone believes the ledger is the truth about them." He closed the factor's book with a soft clap of leather. "This one says the factor shipped out four hundred barrels of grain last spring. The harbor master's tally says five hundred and sixty. One hundred and sixty barrels walked off this island without ever existing, according to the company." He watched Zephyrine with those patient, unsmiling eyes. "Dead men keep poor records. Living ones keep worse. Good night."

He walked on toward the factor's house, lamp bobbing, and Zephyrine stood in the dark holding a fistful of churchyard yarrow like a bouquet for a funeral, understanding two things at once and liking neither.

Sergeant Brant was not hunting a spy.

Not yet. He was hunting arithmetic — and somewhere between four hundred barrels and five hundred and sixty, the factor had made an enemy who could count.

Which meant the murderer wasn't, perhaps, an enemy of the crown at all.

It meant the murderer might be sitting at the company's own table, keeping the company's own books — and the next entry in that ledger, unless Zephyrine moved faster than a closing tide, would be written in Tomas Voss's blood.
