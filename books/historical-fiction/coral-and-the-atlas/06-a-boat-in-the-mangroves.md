## 6. A Boat in the Mangroves

The mangroves on the eastern lip of Sainte-Marne-des-Flots grew in the manner of old women gossiping — hunched, tangled, and full of secrets they had no intention of sharing. Zephyrine had known this since childhood. They had spent whole summers of their eighth and ninth years wedged into those roots, listening to the crabs tick across the mud and inventing names for every heron that stalked the shallows. There had been one heron, tall and humorless, whom they had called Monsieur Delacroix even before there was a real Monsieur Delacroix to compare him to. The comparison, in retrospect, had been unkind to the heron.

Now, three days after the factor's body had been carried up from the customs house on a door wrenched off its hinges, Zephyrine crouched in the same mud, fourteen years older and considerably wetter, looking at a boat.

It was not a good boat. It was, in fairness, barely a boat at all — more an argument between planks that had been settled with tar and prayer. But it was nine feet long, it floated when Zephyrine pushed it, and most importantly it belonged to no one the Meridian Trading Company had thought to question.

"You're going to sink," said Odile from behind a root.

"I'm going to *sail*," Zephyrine corrected, "with tremendous dignity."

"You're going to sink with tremendous dignity, which is somehow worse."

Odile Fontenot had followed them out of the village at a distance that fooled absolutely no one, least of all the two of them. She was nineteen, broad-shouldered from hauling nets, and possessed of the particular talent — common among the island's fisher families — of being able to deliver bad news while chewing something. She was chewing now.

"The militia searched the north shore this morning," she said. "Brant's men. Four of them, poking through every skiff with bayonets like they were shucking oysters. Old Tomas lost his knife when they went through his dory. He's very put out about it."

"They didn't come here?"

"East shore smells." Odile shrugged. "Company men don't like smelling things they don't get paid for."

Zephyrine let out a breath and sat back on their heels, mud squelching obligingly beneath them. The tide was going out, exposing the pale ribs of the mangrove roots, and the light came slantwise through the canopy in coins of gold. Somewhere above, a heron — a different heron; this one they had named Captain, for reasons lost to time — regarded them both with the weary patience of a creature who had seen everything and approved of almost none of it.

Three days since the bridge closed. Three days since the factor had been found with his skull broken and his ledgers missing pages, and the island had turned from a village into a held breath. The company's cutter patrolled the channel now, its gun crew lounging on deck with the studied boredom of men who hoped someone would be foolish enough to give them a reason. Rations had been halved at the commissary. Every boat on the north beach had been counted twice and marked once.

And somewhere on this island, bleeding slowly and hiding badly, was Anton Voss.

* * *

Zephyrine had found him by accident, two nights past, and the finding had rearranged their understanding of everything.

They had gone out at low water to check the pearl lines — because the oysters did not care about murdered factors, and neither did hunger — and had heard breathing in the sea cave below the old salt house. Not the breathing of the wind, which whistled, or the seals, which grumbled. Human breathing. Wet, labored human breathing, the kind that came from a man trying very hard not to make any sound at all.

They had nearly turned around. That was the honest truth of it, and Zephyrine had made peace with the shame: they had stood in the dark with their lantern guttering and thought, *walk away, walk away, you owe him nothing, you were only ever a child.*

But then they remembered sunlight.

They remembered the summer Ilsa Voss's ship had lain at anchor for six weeks waiting on cargo, and the captain had come ashore every third day with fruit she'd hoarded like a dragon and stories she gave away like water. They remembered sitting on the breakwater, eleven years old, salt-crusted and hollow-legged, while Voss taught them knots with names like songs — the monkey's fist, the sheepshank, the carrick bend — and told them about ports so far away the ships that reached them came home with different crews and the same stories twice-told.

And they remembered the last afternoon, when the cargo had finally cleared and Voss had pressed a small brass compass into their hands, closing their fingers around it like a priest giving absolution.

"When you're ready," she'd said, "come find me. Any port under my flag, ask for Ilsa Voss, and tell her the tide child kept the heading. She'll know what it means. I'll know what it means." She'd grinned then, gap-toothed and sunburnt. "Everyone owes somebody a debt, little diver. Better to choose yours than have it chosen for you."

So Zephyrine had gone into the cave, and found Anton Voss curled against the stone with a bullet wound in his side wrapped in strips of his own shirt, feverish, half his mother's face staring out of his own — the same crooked jaw, the same stubborn set of the brow — and twenty years old if he was a day, shaking like a boy.

He hadn't recognized them at first. He'd tried to fight, which would have been funny if it weren't so sad — a dying man swinging at a ghost with fists like wet paper — and then he'd heard their name and started to cry, quietly, furiously, as though crying were another thing he couldn't afford.

"She said you'd come," he'd whispered. "She said — she made me promise, if anything happened, that if I could reach anyone on this rock, it was you. I told her nobody survives a promise made to a stranger's kid. She said—" He'd laughed, wet and cracked. "She said the tide child keeps the heading."

Zephyrine had not slept since.

* * *

"You've got the look," Odile observed now, pulling Zephyrine back to the mangroves and the terrible boat. "The look you get when you're about to do something stupid and want company in the blame."

"There is no stupid thing."

"Zeph."

"There is a small logistical matter."

"*Zeph.*"

Zephyrine looked at the boat. Odile looked at Zephyrine. The heron looked at everyone and found them all wanting.

"It's for fishing," Zephyrine said.

"Mm."

"Night fishing. For mullet. Which are famously abundant on the east shore."

"Famously," Odile agreed, "abundant nowhere near here. Whose mullet am I fishing for, Zeph? Because I've watched you lie to the factor's clerk about your catch weights for six years and you were never once this bad at it."

That was true, and it stung a little. Lying to the company had always felt like a private art form — a small daily tax refund claimed against a lifetime of indenture. You learned to keep your voice level and your numbers plausible and your eyes somewhere over the clerk's shoulder. But those lies had been about fish. This lie had a person inside it, a person who was currently bleeding into a sea cave, and apparently the difference between the two was that one made your hands shake and the other didn't.

"I need a night on the water," Zephyrine said carefully, "and I need you not to ask where."

Odile chewed. Odile swallowed. Odile looked out at the channel where the cutter sat like a knife laid across the throat of the bay.

"Is it about the soldier?"

The word landed like a dropped anchor. Zephyrine kept their face still, which was itself an answer, and Odile nodded slowly, the way she nodded at weather coming in.

"Half the village knows," she said. "Which means all of it does. Marta saw smoke from the salt house. The Delacros' boy found blood on the tideline. And Brant's men dragged the tavern keeper out of his own kitchen yesterday because somebody said somebody said the Voss woman's son was seen at the well. They broke his fingers, Zeph. The tavern keeper. For repeating a rumor." She paused. "Well. For repeating it badly. He named three people who definitely weren't involved, which even Brant's idiots eventually worked out."

"So it's not safe."

"Nothing on this island is safe. The bridge is shut, the food's short, and there's a man with a rifle list walking around calling himself law." Odile crouched beside the boat and ran her thumb along the gunwale, frowning professionally. "This thing will take a wave like a drunk takes stairs. Slowly, painfully, and with a strong chance of ending face-down."

"It just has to take *one* wave. One crossing. Out to the channel, past the patrol line—"

"Past the cutter."

"—at slack tide, when the watch changes and they cluster on the lee side away from the spray. Papa used to run pearls through worse gaps than that."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then she stood, dusted mud from her knees with great ceremony, and said, "I'm coming."

"You are absolutely not."

"He can't row. He'll be lucky to sit up straight. One hand on a tiller, one diver in the water if the boat swamps — because it *will* swamp, Zeph, I've met this boat — that's two people minimum, and that's assuming the sea behaves, which the sea has never once done for either of us." She ticked the points off on her fingers like items on a market list. "Also, I owe your mother a debt, and she's dead, so you inherit it. Also also, I like Ilsa Voss. She taught me the sheepshank too, and she never once asked why my father drank. Also also also—"

"Stop listing."

"—if you go alone you will drown, and I refuse to be the person who knew and let it happen, because then I'd have to live with it, and living with things is my least favorite activity."

Zephyrine wanted to argue. They assembled the arguments carefully — the danger to Odile, the extra mouth Brant might count missing, the simple mathematics of a smaller risk being better than a larger one — and then looked at their friend standing hip-shot in the mud with her arms crossed, and understood that none of the arguments would survive contact with her.

"One condition," Zephyrine said. "You don't learn the details. If Brant takes the island apart, you know nothing. You helped a friend go night fishing. You suspected nothing, because you're not very bright."

"That last part's going in my eulogy."

"Say it anyway."

Odile spat in her palm and held it out. Zephyrine clasped it. The bargain was struck the way all real bargains on the island were struck — in mud, with spit, witnessed only by a heron.

* * *

The problem, as always, was the tide road.

Sainte-Marne-des-Flots was two islands pretending to be one: the high town on the rock, where the company kept its warehouses and its factor's empty house and its militia barracks, and the low town on the sandspit, where everyone else lived at the mercy of the sea's moods. Twice a day the tidal flats opened a causeway between them, and twice a day the sea took it back, and everyone organized their entire existence around this fact with the cheerful fatalism of people who had never been offered an alternative.

For generations the bridge closing had meant nothing worse than delayed mail. Now it meant the island was sealed — no boats out without company writ, no traffic between shores without militia pass, no news except what crawled in on the tide. The seasonal bridge closing had always been a rumor of winter. This year it had teeth.

Which meant moving Anton at all required moving him invisibly, across ground that Brant's patrols crossed twice nightly, in a body that could barely stand.

"We wait for storm season," Odile said that evening, on the salt-house steps, watching the first thunderheads pile up over the western water like furniture stacked by a careless god. "The weather breaks the patrol routine. Nobody stands watch in rain like that. We take him out in the worst blow of the year and let God sort the paperwork."

"He won't last until storm season proper. That wound's going bad — you can smell it starting." Zephyrine said it flatly because saying it any other way wasn't survivable. "We have four days. Maybe five."

"Four days."

"Five if we're lucky and he's stubborn."

"He's Ilsa Voss's son. He'll be stubborn enough to embarrass us both." Odile pulled her knees up. "Then we do it in four. In weather. At night. With a boat that floats mostly out of spite." She glanced sideways. "Any other good news?"

"One thing." Zephyrine reached into their shirt and drew out the brass compass, turning it in the last light. The needle swung, settled, pointed faithfully north. "Voss's compass. She gave it to me years ago. I keep thinking—" They stopped, embarrassed.

"Thinking what?"

"That she chose me for this before I existed to her as anything but a kid on a breakwater. That she handed me the debt with the gift. And I keep wondering whether she knew — whether she already knew her son was company trouble, even then. Whether the whole kindness was a net cast forward." They shook their head. "It doesn't matter. A promise is a promise regardless of why it was asked. That's the whole point of promises. Otherwise they're just weather forecasts."

Odile considered this with the seriousness she reserved for few other subjects.

"My grandmother says the sea keeps accounts," she offered. "Everything owed comes back around. Maybe that's what this is. Something owed coming around." She yawned enormously. "Or maybe it's just two idiots and a bad boat. The sea probably doesn't distinguish."

Down in the village, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, which rang sweet and unhurried, but the commissary bell, hard and fast, the sound of the company wanting attention. Lamplight bloomed along the north shore. Figures moved. Somewhere, a dog began barking with the frantic rhythm of an animal that had seen something it shouldn't.

Odile and Zephyrine stood together.

"That's the second mustering tonight," Odile murmured. "They're searching again. Closer in, by the sound of it."

The commissary bell kept ringing, and beneath it, faintly, came another sound — boots on the tideline, many boots, coming up the east shore toward the mangroves where a terrible boat sat floating in plain view, and a lantern bobbed in the dark like a slow, patient heartbeat.

"They're not stopping," Zephyrine whispered.

"No," Odile said. "They're really not."
