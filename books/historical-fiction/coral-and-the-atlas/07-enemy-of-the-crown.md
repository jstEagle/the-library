## 7. Enemy of the Crown

The proclamation went up on the company house door at low tide, nailed there by a militia man whose hammer-work suggested he had more enthusiasm than skill. By the time Zephyrine saw it, half the island's divers had already gathered beneath it in that particular silence of people reading something they did not like.

*By order of the Crown and the Meridian Trading Company,* it began, which was an interesting way of putting things, since everyone on the island knew the Crown's name was mostly decorative — the way a figurehead decorates a ship without steering it.

*Whereas one Ilsa Voss, formerly master of the brig* Andromeda, *has been declared an enemy of the Crown for crimes against the realm, including but not limited to: piracy, sedition, conspiracy with foreign powers, and the theft of Company property —*

"Theft," said old Marguerite Chen, spitting into the sand with the accuracy of a woman who had been diving since before the seawall was built. "That woman paid for every rope on her ship twice over."

"— her person, vessels, and effects are forfeit. Any who shelter her, aid her, trade with her, or convey intelligence to her shall be judged accessory to treason and punished accordingly."

And then, because documents of this kind always saved the worst for last, in smaller letters as though shame could be made typographic:

*Her son, Emil Voss, is to be detained for questioning upon discovery.*

Zephyrine read that line four times. Around them the crowd was already dissolving back into its business, the way crowds do when a thing becomes too dangerous to look at directly — heads turning away all at once, like a school of fish changing direction. Only Marguerite stayed, and she looked sideways at Zephyrine with eyes gone pale as old shell.

"You've gone white as bleached coral, child," she said. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing wrong. You breathe like your mother did the year the red tide came." Marguerite picked up her basket of tools and settled it on her hip. "Whatever you're about to do — and you are about to do something, I've known you since you were small enough to fit in a clam bucket — do it quietly."

She shuffled off before Zephyrine could decide whether to lie or thank her.

* * *

The walk from the company house to Odile's cookshop took eleven minutes if you dawdled, seven if you were late for supper, and four if you were trying very hard not to look like you were hurrying. Zephyrine managed it in five by stopping once to examine a fence post with great scholarly interest.

Odile was stirring a pot of what the island optimistically called stew — meaning: whatever the ration barrels had yielded, boiled until it surrendered — and she took one look at Zephyrine's face and reached for a second bowl without asking.

"Sit," she said. "Eat. Then tell me why you look like a woman carrying a secret the size of a whale."

"How would you know that look? Have you carried many whale-sized secrets?"

"Two," said Odile placidly. "One drowned me and one I outlived. Sit down."

Zephyrine sat. The stew was mostly salt fish and mostly salt, but hot, and heat helps. They ate in silence until Odile had served two more customers — a pair of militiamen who took their bowls, muttered thanks, and left coins they didn't have, which meant the company was paying them in credit against wages they'd likely never collect.

When the door swung shut behind them, Odile lowered herself onto the opposite bench and folded her arms.

"Emil Voss," she said.

Zephyrine nearly dropped the spoon. "How—"

"Child. The proclamation says the son is to be detained *upon discovery*. Which means he has not yet been discovered. Which means somebody on this island knows where he is, and is now walking around looking exactly the way you are walking around." She tapped the table. "I run the only cookshop on a tidal island. People talk to me the way water talks to a drain. Everything comes through eventually."

"Does anyone else know?"

"That someone knows? Yes, half the island suspects it. Who it is?" Odile shrugged. "No. And we will keep it that way." She paused, and something gentler moved across her broad face. "You promised the captain, didn't you. In peacetime. Before any of this."

It wasn't really a question, but Zephyrine answered it anyway. "She pulled me out of the channel when I was fourteen. Cramp in the current off the spit. Any other captain would have kept sailing — cargo schedules, you understand. She turned the* Andromeda *into the wind, came down herself, dove for me, wrapped me in sailcloth, and then lectured me the entire afternoon about diving alone." Zephyrine smiled despite everything. "She said, 'You owe me nothing, little fish. But someday I may ask you for something, and I expect you'll consider it.' That was the promise. Not even a real promise. A maybe."

"And now it's a yes."

"Now it's a yes. Except—" Zephyrine set the spoon down carefully, because hands that shake spill things. "Except the maybe has teeth now. Sheltering him is treason. Feeding him is treason. *Looking at him wrong* might be treason. And the bridge is closed, so there is nowhere for him to go, and the militia has doubled its watches, and Brant—" The name stuck a little. "Brant is offering rewards for information. Company scrip. Enough to buy out an indenture."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Outside, the tide was turning; you could hear it change the way you can hear weather coming, a soft deep shifting under the pilings.

"Then let me tell you about my two secrets," Odile said at last. "The first one, I kept for money. A man paid me to say I hadn't seen a boat leave the north shore, and I hadn't seen it, so it seemed easy money. But a secret kept for money isn't yours anymore — the money owns it, and the man who paid owns the money, and soon enough the man owns you. That secret drowned me. Cost me my husband, in the end, though that's another story and a longer one."

"And the second?"

"The second, I kept for love, and I outlived it, and it never weighed anything at all. That's the whole difference, Zephyrine. It isn't whether you keep a secret or tell one. It's who you keep it *for*." She stood, gathered bowls, and paused at the stove with her back turned. "Come back tomorrow night. After last watch. Bring nothing, carry nothing, know nothing. But come hungry, because whatever happens, no one conspires well on an empty stomach."

* * *

The mangroves on the island's western fringe grew in a tangle so dense that even the militia avoided them, partly from tactics and partly from mosquitoes. Zephyrine knew the paths through them the way other children knew scripture — learned from their mother, who had learned from hers, going back to whenever the first Joubert had decided that a place nobody else wanted was exactly the place to keep things.

Which is how, three nights after the proclamation, Zephyrine came to be crouched in the roots of a red mangrove, watching a nineteen-year-old boy attempt to sleep standing up against a tree trunk while arguing about soup.

"There was no soup," Emil Voss insisted, in the fierce whisper of someone defending his honor. "There was the *idea* of soup. There was hot water that had once been near a fish."

"Then your mother lied to me," Zephyrine whispered back, "because she told me her son complained about food the entire voyage south and she'd hoped the tropics would cure him."

"My mother exaggerates."

"Your mother dove off a moving ship to save a drowning stranger."

"...She does exaggerate."

He was thinner than the boy Zephyrine remembered from the summer the* Andromeda *last called — taller too, and hollowed under the eyes, and there was a bandage around his ribs that had been white once and was now the color of surrender. The wound beneath it was a musket ball's work, or near enough, taken somewhere between here and wherever the* Andromeda *had gone to pieces. He'd walked, crawled, and been carried the last stretch by a fisherman named Tomas who told no one except his wife, who told no one except Odile, who told no one except Zephyrine, which was either a very short chain of trust or a very long one depending on how much faith you had in drains.

"Let me see the bandage," Zephyrine said.

"It's fine."

"Let me see it anyway."

He unbuttoned his shirt with the exaggerated patience of the young, muttering, and Zephyrine peeled back the wrapping. The flesh was angry but not rotten — no red streaks crawling toward his heart, which was the sign their mother had taught them to fear above all others. Zephyrine rewrapped it with the clean cloth brought in a waterproofed oilskin roll, along with a jar of Odile's honey-and-salt wash and, crucially, actual bread.

Emil held the bread for a moment like it might be a trick.

"Eat it," Zephyrine said. "Before the mosquitoes eat us both."

He ate it. Slowly, at first, then not slowly at all, and Zephyrine looked away to give him the dignity of pretending not to notice.

"They'll hang you," Emil said finally, with his mouth half full. "You know that. If they find me here, they won't just take me. Anyone who touched this gets tried as accessory. You, the fisherman, the cook woman. All of you."

"Odile says being accessory to something is still better than being accessory to nothing. She says it gives shape to a life."

"Your cook woman sounds unhinged."

"She is. Gloriously. She's also the only reason you've eaten in four days." Zephyrine settled back against the mangrove roots, and the humor drained out of their voice like water out of a hull. "Emil. We need to talk about what comes next, because 'next' is arriving faster than I'd like. The proclamation names you. Detained for questioning — but nobody detains anyone for questioning anymore, we've all seen how the company questions. And the bridge closes day after tomorrow for the spring repairs, which means after that, this island is a locked box until storm season, and Brant's men are already searching the boats."

"Then get me to a boat."

"Every boat is being inspected. Every hull, every hold, every net bundle." Zephyrine picked up a fallen mangrove leaf and tore it neatly in half, then in quarters, watching the pieces drift onto the black water. "So we don't put you in a boat."

"Then how—"

"We put you in the water."

Emil stared. "You're joking."

"I'm a pearl diver's child. I've held my breath since before I could spell. There's a gap in the inspection line at the eastern channel — thirty feet of dark water between the pilings where the watch lanterns don't reach, because the current there eats light and boats alike. At slack tide, it's swimmable. Barely. With help." Zephyrine looked up at him. "On the far side, moored in the mangrove cut past the point, there's the* Petrel*. Small. Ugly. Smells like fish, which is honest of it. Two days' sail south to the free ports if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then you'll wish you'd been hanged, briefly, and then you'll be fine, because you'll have me aboard, and I intend not to sink."

"*Aboard?*" Emil's voice cracked upward. "You're coming?"

"You can't sail. You can barely stand. You're injured, you're a landlubber's landlubber, and the last time you were on open water you complained about soup. Yes, I'm coming." Zephyrine said it lightly, but the truth underneath it was heavier: that the promise had grown legs and was dragging them both toward the horizon. "Besides. My mother always said a Joubert belongs in the water. She just never specified *which* water."

For a moment Emil said nothing, and in the dark his face did something complicated — gratitude and guilt wrestling for the same ground. Then he laughed, a short exhausted sound, and leaned his head back against the mangrove trunk.

"My mother was right about you," he said. "She said, 'Find the little fish if anything ever goes truly wrong. Everyone else will weigh the risk. The little fish will weigh only you.'"

"Your mother," Zephyrine said, "sounds like a dangerous woman."

"She is. That's rather the problem. They've declared her an enemy of the crown, and I don't even know if she's alive."

The tide shifted beneath them — that soft deep turning again — and somewhere out beyond the mangroves, faint as a thought, came the sound of a patrol boat's oars. Both of them went still. The oars passed. The night resumed.

"One more thing," Zephyrine murmured, when the water's silence returned. "The night the factor died. Where were you?"

"Why?"

"Because Brant is asking everyone the same question, and he's asking with manacles. If you're going to be interrogated, better it happen here, by a friend, over bread."

Emil was quiet long enough that the mosquitoes got ambitious. "In the water," he said at last. "Trying to swim the channel the night I landed. Half-drowned, feverish, bleeding. I heard shouting from the factor's house across the water and I couldn't have told you what it was. That's my answer, and it's the truth, and it convicts me of nothing except bad swimming."

"Good," said Zephyrine, and stood, brushing mud from their knees. "Rest. Eat. Tomorrow night, Odile feeds us. The night after, we swim."

They had taken perhaps ten steps toward the path when the thought arrived — cold, sudden, complete, the way the worst thoughts arrive — and stopped them dead among the roots.

The factor had died the same night Emil came ashore. Had died within shouting distance of the water. Had died, according to every rumor on the island, *knowing something*, because Brant had sealed the ledgers and turned the factor's office inside out hunting papers that were never found.

And the only person who had been in the water that night, close enough to hear shouting, close enough to see—

Was currently leaning against a mangrove tree, eating stolen bread.

Zephyrine turned slowly. Emil was watching them already, bread forgotten in his hand, his expression unreadable in the dark.

"What did you hear," Zephyrine asked quietly, "the night Delacroix died?"
