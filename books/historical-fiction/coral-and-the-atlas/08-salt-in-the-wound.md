## 8. Salt in the Wound

The wound had opinions.

Zephyrine discovered this on the second morning of hiding Voss's son in the drying loft above the pearl sheds, because the boy would not stop sharing them. He lay on a bed of sacking and dried kelp with his shoulder wrapped in strips of an old sailcloth shirt, and every time Zephyrine changed the dressing he offered a running commentary, as though the pus and the angry red weals were a performance he had personally arranged for their benefit.

"That one," he said, pointing with his good arm at a streak of inflammation crawling toward his collarbone, "is new since yesterday. I've named it. I'm calling it Delacroix, because it's also spreading lies about me."

"You shouldn't joke," Zephyrine said, and dipped the cloth in the basin of boiled seawater.

"I shouldn't *bleed*, either, but here we are." He grinned, then winced when the grin pulled at the torn flesh near his jaw. His name was Anselme, though so far he had answered to *boy*, *fool*, and — from Odile, who had climbed the ladder once and immediately regretted it — "that great limbed catastrophe." He was nineteen, maybe twenty, with his mother's square jaw and none of her caution. The musket ball had gone through the meat of his shoulder two nights before the factor died, and he had walked four miles on it before collapsing in the mangroves where Zephyrine had promised, in another life, a sunlit afternoon, to help him if ever he needed helping.

Zephyrine wrung out the cloth. Salt water dripped back into the basin. "Hold still."

"Hold still," Anselme repeated, mock-solemn. "You sound like my mother. She used to say that when she was pulling splinters out of me on the *Vireo*. Hold still, Anselme. Courage, Anselme. Stop screaming, Anselme, the whole harbor can hear you."

"Did you stop?"

"No. But I learned to sing instead, which frightened everyone more."

Despite themselves, Zephyrine smiled. It was difficult not to. There was something absurd and rather magnificent about the boy — feverish, half-starved, hunted by a private militia that had already shot at him once, and yet lying there in the kelp like a critic reviewing a disappointing play.

They pressed the cloth to the wound and Anselme hissed through his teeth, but he did not move. That was how Zephyrine learned the difference between his jokes and his mother: the jokes were noise, but underneath them he was still as a stone, enduring exactly as much as was asked of him and not one flinch more.

"It's not poisoning yet," Zephyrine said, examining the edges of the tear. "But it wants cleaning properly, twice a day, and it wants honey if we can get any."

"Honey." Anselme said the word the way other men said *salvation*. "There's honey on this rock?"

"There's bees behind the chapel. Old Père Anselme—" they stopped. "Your namesake, I suppose. He kept hives. Nobody's touched them since he went to the mainland last spring."

"Then tonight," Anselme said grandly, "you shall rob a church for me. I am becoming an excellent influence on your moral character."

"You were already the worst thing that ever happened to my moral character. You arrived in my loft and made me a thief and a smuggler inside of three days."

"And how does it feel?"

Zephyrine thought about it honestly, because the boy deserved honesty even if everything else on the island had stopped deserving anything. "Like diving," they said at last. "Cold at first. Then you stop noticing."

* * *

The loft smelled of kelp, tar, and the sweet rot of oyster shells, and it was, all things considered, a magnificent place to hide a man nobody could know existed.

It sat above the company's own drying sheds, which was the joke of it — the militia marched past twice a day on their rounds, boots ringing on the boardwalk close enough that Zephyrine could count the rivets in their gaiters through the floorboards, and never once looked up. Why would they? The lofts held nothing but shell and salt and the smell of money not yet quite made. Sergeant Brant himself had stood beneath it on the first morning, calling up, "Anyone up there?" and Zephyrine had leaned over the ladder-hole, face composed into the flat boredom of the indentured, and said, "Only shells, sir," and Brant had grunted and moved on, because Brant was a man who believed what was convenient.

Anselme had lain beneath a mound of discarded shell then, breathing through his mouth, with a knife — Zephyrine's knife — in his good hand. They did not mention this afterward, either of them. Some things were better left in the dark like that, unexamined.

Odile came at midday, when the tide took the workers to the western beds. She came by way of the roof, which she claimed was easier than the ladder, which was a lie, which she told so that she could complain about the ladder anyway. She brought bread stolen in increments too small for anyone to miss, a jar of rendered fat, and news, delivered while she rewrapped the dressing with the brisk, unsentimental hands of someone who had bandaged drowning victims and found them less trouble.

"The militia searched the eastern quarter yesterday," she said. "Door to door. Brant says he's looking for the factor's killer, but he searched the Voss woman's old lodgings first, and he searched them badly — turned everything and took nothing. You don't hunt a murderer that way. You hunt a spy that way."

"He thinks Anselme's still on the island."

"He *knows* it," Odile said. "He's just too proud to say so without a body. And there's talk the cutter's been signaled. Not the supply boat — the navy cutter. Somebody on the mainland has decided this island is interesting again." She tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels, studying Anselme the way she studied weather. "You're fevered."

"I'm always fevered. It's part of my charm."

"Your charm is going to rot off your shoulder if we don't get that honey." She looked at Zephyrine. "And if we don't get you off this island before the cutter comes, no amount of charm will matter. A navy surgeon will look at you for exactly as long as it takes to decide you're worth questioning, and after that you'll wish you'd been shot cleanly."

"Odile," Anselme said, with his most winning smile, which was very good indeed, "has anyone ever told you that you have the comforting manner of a funeral?"

"Frequently. By people who then needed me." She gathered her jar and her remaining bread. "Tonight. Honey, clean water, and I'll bring word about the mangrove boat. Zeph, walk me down."

* * *

On the ladder, out of Anselme's hearing, Odile stopped and gripped Zephyrine's wrist.

"How long can you keep doing this?" she asked quietly.

"As long as it takes."

"That's not an answer. That's a song lyric." Her grip tightened. "Listen to me. You're sleeping in the shed below that loft so the guards see a diver coming and going, yes? And by day you're in the beds with the others, and by night you're boiling water and playing surgeon, and somewhere in between you're supposed to be finding a seaworthy boat, a window in the patrols, and a tide that agrees with all of it. You cannot do all of it. Nobody can do all of it."

"I did promise."

"I know what you promised." Odile's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than her scolding. "I was there. Under that same sun. I helped carry her rum, if you remember, and I liked her, Zeph, insofar as I like anyone. But a promise made to Ilsa Voss doesn't require you to drown yourself keeping it. She'd be the first to say so."

"She'd be the first to say so," Zephyrine agreed. "That's why it's mine and not hers."

Odile let go of their wrist. For a moment she only looked at them — at the shadows under their eyes, at the salt cracked into their knuckles — and whatever she saw made her exhale through her nose, slow and defeated.

"The bees," she said finally. "Père Anselme's hives. There's a veil and gloves hanging in the chapel vestry, and the smoker is in the sacristy cabinet, bottom shelf, under the altar wine nobody drinks. If Brant catches you robbing a church, tell him you were moved by piety. He'll believe it. He's never once set foot inside except to check the rafters for snipers, and God has clearly given up on him." She swung down onto the boardwalk. "Twice a day on that dressing. And Zeph — if it goes bad, if the arm goes black —"

"It won't."

"If it does," she said, "you'll tell him the truth before I have to. Promise me that one too."

She didn't wait for the answer. She never did.

* * *

Night fell the way it always did during the closed season — fast, total, and smelling of rain that hadn't committed yet. Zephyrine went to the chapel at moonrise.

Robbing a church, it turned out, was easier than robbing a hive. The chapel stood at the island's high point, small and whitewashed and leaning slightly eastward as though listening for ships. Inside it smelled of candle wax and dust and, faintly, of the sea, because on this island everything eventually smelled of the sea; even God, Zephyrine thought, must keep a salt-stained handkerchief here.

The vestry yielded the veil. The sacristy cabinet yielded the smoker, and — tucked behind it, which felt like the chapel itself taking a bribe — a stub of beeswax candle that Zephyrine pocketed on principle, because wax sealed wounds too, or so the old divers swore.

The hives stood in a row behind the apse, pale boxes in the dark, humming faintly like sleeping machinery. Zephyrine had watched Père Anselme work them a hundred times as a child, sitting cross-legged in the grass while the old priest narrated everything in his murmuring way: *Smoke first, patience second, greed never.* It occurred to Zephyrine now, crouched in the grass with the smoker puffing in their hands, that they had inherited an education of entirely the wrong kind — everything useful they knew, they knew from promises and pilfering and priests.

The bees, drugged with smoke, parted around their hands like a congregation making way. The comb came free in heavy golden slabs, dripping down their wrists, and Zephyrine laughed — actually laughed, the first time in days — standing in a dead priest's bee yard at midnight with stolen honey running down both arms, feeling for one absurd moment like the richest person on the island.

Which was, of course, when the lantern flared.

"Don't move," said Sergeant Brant.

* * *

He stood at the corner of the chapel with his lantern raised and his carbine slung — slung, Zephyrine noted with the strange clarity of terror, not leveled. Behind him, half in shadow, a militiaman whose name Zephyrine didn't know held the lantern lower and looked, frankly, embarrassed.

Brant advanced two paces and took in the scene: the smoker, the veil pushed up, the honey-slick arms, the open hive. His expression traveled a long road — suspicion, confusion, dawning disbelief — and arrived somewhere near disgust.

"You're *robbing a church*."

"I'm—"

"With *bees*. At *midnight*." He said it as though each fact made the next less survivable. "Explain yourself."

Every story Zephyrine had prepared dissolved. What remained was the truth wearing its best clothes.

"Père Anselme kept these hives," they said. "He went to the mainland in spring and nobody's tended them since. The comb was going to spoil. Honey spoils, Sergeant, same as anything. My grandmother taught me smoke-work before she taught me to dive, and I thought —" They shrugged, honey dripping off their elbow for emphasis. "It seemed wrong to let it go to waste. Seemed wrong, with him gone, to let everything of his go to waste."

Silence. A bee, drunk and bewildered, landed on Zephyrine's forearm and stayed there.

Brant stared at them. The militiaman behind him stared at the bees. Somewhere below, the sea worked patiently at the rocks, indifferent as ever to the dramas of the dry land.

"You'll surrender the honey to the stores," Brant said at last, stiffly. "Ration regulations."

"Of course, Sergeant."

"And the equipment goes back where you found it."

"Yes, Sergeant."

He turned, then paused, and over his shoulder added, in a tone that might in a different man have been almost wistful: "He baptized me, you know. The padre. I didn't cry, apparently. My mother was mortified." Then he straightened, remembered himself, and barked, "Home! And if I find you up here again I'll have you on charges!" — and crunched away down the path, lantern swinging, leaving Zephyrine alone in the dark with the bees and a heart hammering loud enough to sting.

The bee on their forearm crawled a slow circle and lifted away.

"Well," Zephyrine said to it, shakily. "One of us should get the honey home."

* * *

Anselme ate a fingerful of honeycomb directly from Zephyrine's cupped hands, eyes closed, making sounds of such disgraceful gratitude that Zephyrine glanced nervously at the floorboards.

"You don't understand," he mumbled. "Three days of bread and fat. Three days. And now —" he gestured vaguely with the comb, scattering wax flecks, "— *ambrosia*. Robbed from a church. You are the finest criminal of our age and I intend to name my first boat after you."

"Your first boat is going to be a rowboat, and it's going to sink."

"Then I'll name the wreck after you. It's the thought that counts." He sobered as Zephyrine cleaned the wound and packed it with the honey, and for a while there was only the hiss of breath and the small busy sounds of the loft. When the new dressing was tied, he flexed the arm experimentally, and something in his face eased — the first easing Zephyrine had seen there in days.

"Better?" they asked.

"Worse," he admitted cheerfully. "Hurts more now that it's clean. Odile says that means it's working."

"Odile says many things."

"She says the navy cutter's coming." He said it lightly, watching Zephyrine's face rather than their hands. "I heard her through the boards. So. We're running out of island, aren't we?"

Zephyrine sat back. Below them the sheds ticked and settled; beyond the shutter-vent, the lighthouse turned its blind eye across black water. "The tide road opens in nine days. Maybe eight, if the moon runs early."

"Nine days of honey and prayers." Anselme leaned his head back against the kelp and looked up at the rafters, where the swallows used to nest before the sheds were built. "My mother used to say the sea keeps what you give it and returns what you need. I used to think that was just a thing mothers said." He turned his head, and his grin came back — crooked, fever-bright, entirely his mother's jaw. "But you've heard from Odile about the boat, haven't you? You've got a face like a woman deciding whether to tell her sister the wedding's off."

Zephyrine hesitated. Then, because the boy deserved honesty: "Odile found a boat in the mangroves. But there's a problem. It's not ours."

"Whose is it?"

"It belongs," Zephyrine said slowly, "to somebody who came ashore the night the factor died. And this morning, Odile found fresh footprints going into those mangroves — and only one set coming out."

The grin faded from Anselme's face, and in the dark of the loft, with the honey still sweet between them, the island seemed suddenly much smaller than it had at sunset.

"Somebody," Anselme said quietly, "knows about the boat."
