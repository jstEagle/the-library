## 9. The Informer

The ration line moved the way all suffering moves on Meridian Cay: slowly, loudly, and with a great deal of commentary from Madame Fournier.

"Half a measure of rice," she was announcing to no one and everyone, her voice carrying over the heads of forty damp islanders. "Half a measure! When I was a girl we gave *pilgrims* half a measure, and they hadn't even earned it."

"You've never given anyone anything for free in your life," said old Bastien behind her, not unkindly.

"I gave my husband thirty years," Madame Fournier shot back. "Ask him how he liked it."

Zephyrine stood three places back, hood up against the drizzle, and let the familiar rhythm of complaint wash over them. It had been four days since the factor's body was found in the drying shed, four days since Lieutenant Brant's militia had nailed the island shut like a crate of spoiled cargo, and already the people of Meridian Cay had metabolized catastrophe into something they could argue about in queues. That was the island way. Grief was a luxury; grumbling was free.

The storehouse door opened and Odile emerged with the ledger under her arm and an expression that could have curdled milk at fifty paces.

"Next," she called.

Odile had run the company kitchen for longer than Zephyrine had been alive, and she doled out rice the way she dispensed wisdom: in exact measures, with a look that dared you to ask for more. She was the closest thing Zephyrine had to family on this rock, which meant she was also the person most likely to notice what Zephyrine had been doing at night.

"Name," said Odile now, not looking up.

"Joubert. Full share, plus the child's supplement for Tante Marguerite's household."

Odile's eyes came up slowly. In them, Zephyrine saw the question arrive before it was asked — saw it land like a gull on a rail, settle, cock its head. *Where were you Tuesday night. Why is there mud on your hem that doesn't match this island's mud. Who is bleeding in the mangroves.*

"A full share," Odile repeated. "You'll want salt too, then. Diving so much lately, in weather no sane person dives in."

"You know me. Can't keep out of the water."

"Mm." Odile weighed the rice with two fingers, added a thumb-width more than the measure, and swept it into Zephyrine's tin before anyone could see. Then, quietly, under the creak of the queue shuffling forward: "Someone's been asking after you. Not Brant's men. One of us."

The tin went heavy in Zephyrine's hands, though it held barely two pounds of rice.

"Asking what?"

"Asking where you go. When you're seen. Whether you've been seen at the north spit." Odile handed the next woman her ration without breaking stride, her mouth still bent into its usual disapproving line, as though she and Zephyrine were discussing nothing more scandalous than the price of lamp oil. "I told them you dive where the pearls are and sleep where you're told, like everyone else. But Zeph." Her eyes flicked up once, sharp as a knife coming out of a sheath. "Whatever you're carrying — and don't tell me you aren't, I changed your swaddling clothes, I know what your guilt looks like, it looks exactly like your father's — whatever it is, carry it faster."

She turned to the next in line. "Bastien. Half measure, and no, you cannot have sugar, sugar is for the sick and the dying and you are merely old."

* * *

Zephyrine spent the afternoon doing what Zephyrine always did when the world became too complicated: working the shallows with a basket and a short blade, prying chitons off the breakwater rocks as if scraping shellfish could put the universe back in order. The rain had thinned to a fine sea-mist. Out past the reef, the water lay the color of tarnished pewter, and beyond it, somewhere past the horizon, the war was presumably continuing without them, politely, at a distance.

*One of us.* The words kept surfacing like a body that wouldn't stay down.

There were sixty-three souls on Meridian Cay when the bridge closed. Sixty-two now, if you didn't count the factor, and nobody did anymore except the ledger. Zephyrine turned them over one by one while the knife clicked against stone. Madame Fournier, who would inform on her own shadow for the entertainment value. Bastien, who couldn't climb the north spit's hill without resting twice and knew it. The Leclair brothers, who'd sell their grandmother for rope and their grandfather for sailcloth. Père Anselme, who prayed aloud for peace and privately, Zephyrine suspected, missed the excitement of other people's wars.

And Brant's militia — six men billeted in the company barracks, eating the company's salt beef, following the company's orders. They didn't count as *us*. But they counted.

It wasn't only the informer, either. It was the arithmetic underneath. Emil Voss was mending badly in the sea cave at the north spit, feverish by turns, hungry always, and every day he stayed was a day the odds grew teeth. Captain Voss's ship would come back for him — she had promised, in the coded grammar of coastal signals, that a boat would appear on the third dark of the moon — but the third dark of the moon was nine days off, and the informer might talk tomorrow.

Zephyrine scraped a chiton loose, misjudged, and took a strip of knuckle skin with it. They sucked the wound and considered, not for the first time, that their mother had drowned in water calmer than this, and that she would have laughed herself sick at the idea of her child dying on *land*, of all places, over politics.

* * *

Evening brought the militia's rounds, as it always did, and with them Corporal Reyes, who was nineteen, seasick, and constitutionally incapable of menace.

"Curfew check," Reyes announced through the door of Tante Marguerite's house, in the tone of a boy apologizing to a dog he has been sent to bathe.

Tante Marguerite, who was eighty-one and deaf as a boulder, looked up from her netting. "WHAT?"

"CURFEW," Reyes shouted.

"IT'S ABOUT TIME SOMEBODY DID SOMETHING ABOUT THE CURFEW," she agreed warmly. "COME IN, BOY, YOU'RE LETTING THE COLD IN."

What followed was twenty minutes of Marguerite force-feeding a soldier of the occupying militia cassava cake and interrogating him about his mother, his boots, and whether he was eating enough, while Reyes sat pinned in the best chair radiating the helpless misery of a young man who had enlisted to be a hero and instead been adopted by an elderly woman on a tidal island. Zephyrine, in the corner, mended netting and watched the intelligence operation unfold. Marguerite extracted more from that boy in one evening than Brant's whole garrison had learned about the island in a season. It was, Zephyrine thought, the only war being conducted properly on Meridian Cay.

"He says," Marguerite reported afterward, when the door had closed on Reyes and his half-dozen stolen cakes, "that the lieutenant's in a temper. Says somebody's filed a report. A formal one." She worked her needle through the netting, eyes narrowed. "Says Brant's been ordered to search the north end. Caves."

The bottom dropped out of the room, or Zephyrine's stomach did; the sensations were similar.

"When?" they managed.

"Tomorrow, low tide. Which gives you—" Marguerite glanced at the window, at the grey light dying over the channel "—about eleven hours, if you count like a dishonest woman, which I do." She tied off her thread and looked up, and for a moment the comedy drained out of her face entirely and left something older underneath, something that had buried a husband and two sons and kept making bread regardless. "I don't know what you've got hidden up there, child. I don't want to know. But I taught your mother to swim in that channel, and she taught you, and neither of you ever brought anything home that needed hiding unless it was going to die without you." She handed Zephyrine the finished net. A fine net, strong knots, newly weighted along one edge. "For the chitons," she said. "The big ones. Off the north spit, where nobody fishes this time of year."

Zephyrine understood. You couldn't move a wounded man down a cliff path in daylight. But you could lower him to a boat at night, if you had a net that would bear a man's weight, and you could be found holding that net with an innocent explanation and a basket of shellfish.

"Tante—"

"Eat your cake," said Marguerite, "and stop thanking people. It attracts attention."

* * *

The walk to the north spit took two hours in the dark, most of it bent double against wind that came off the channel with the specific intention of humiliating Zephyrine personally. Twice they heard footsteps and flattened into gorse. Both times it was goats. The goats of Meridian Cay, having observed the collapse of lawful order with equine indifference, had simply resumed their ancient program of eating everything not actively defended, and they regarded Zephyrine's covert operations with frank contempt.

The sea cave opened onto the channel at its mouth and ran back forty feet into the cliff, dry at low tide, a black throat of dripping stone. Zephyrine whistled twice, low — the pearl-divers' signal, *safe water below* — and heard the whistle answered, thin and cracked, from inside.

Emil Voss was seventeen, and he had his mother's jaw and none of her certainty. He lay propped against a folded sail in the deepest dry pocket of the cave, his bandaged shoulder shining with sweat in the light of a shuttered lamp, and when Zephyrine's face appeared he tried, with heartbreaking diligence, to sit up like a soldier.

"Don't," said Zephyrine. "Please. I've climbed enough hills tonight for both of us."

"You shouldn't come," Emil said. His voice was roughened by fever. "If they catch you—"

"If they catch me, I'll say I'm poaching crabs, which will be believable because I am extremely good at it." Zephyrine set down the pack: bread, a flask of Marguerite's fish broth, clean linen, the little paper packet of willow-bark powder bought from Odile with three pearls that had cost years of lung-burning bottom time. "How's the shoulder?"

"It hurts."

"Good. Hurting means it's alive. My mother used to say that."

"She sounds like she'd have gotten on with mine." Emil smiled despite himself, then hissed as he shifted. While Zephyrine changed the dressing — the wound was ugly but no longer angry, the redness retreating like a tide with somewhere better to be — he talked, the way the young and frightened talk, filling the dark with small facts. His sister's letters. The schoolmaster in Corviere who'd taught him charts. How he'd come to the island in the first place, a courier's errand gone wrong, a bullet from a nervous militiaman on the quay, and Captain Voss's face when they'd dragged him aboard — no, that part he never told, that part lived behind his eyes where Zephyrine left it alone.

But tonight he stopped mid-sentence and said, abruptly: "They know I'm here. Don't they."

Zephyrine kept their hands steady on the knot. "Why would you say that?"

"Because you came early. And because you brought broth, and you only bring broth when you think I'm about to need strength for something." Seventeen, and sharp as broken glass. His mother's son after all. "There's a search coming, isn't there."

"Tomorrow. Low tide. North caves." Zephyrine tied off the bandage. "So we leave tonight. All of it happens tonight now."

"There's no boat until—"

"I know when the boat is. We don't need a boat tonight. We need to get you out of this cave and somewhere Brant's men won't look, and I have one place like that on this island, and it's terrible, and you're going to hate it."

"What is it?"

"The old pearling camp on Sable Islet. Half a mile across the channel, no beach worth landing on, and the whole floor crawls at high tide." Zephyrine began packing away the old dressings. "My father used it as a staging cache. There's a stone smokehouse above the tideline with a false floor. You can lie flat in it and hear the water go by underneath. Very restful."

Emil stared at them. "You're going to row me across open water, at night, with a militia searching the coast, to hide in a *tomb*."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"How else would I say it?"

"Fondly," said Zephyrine. "Fondly is the traditional way."

* * *

They almost made it.

The descent from the cave to the waterline went well, absurdly well, Emil's arm over Zephyrine's shoulder, the weighted net rigged as a sling, the skiff waiting where Zephyrine had cached it under sacking three nights ago. The channel lay flat as poured pewter, the current slack between tides, the far shore of Sable Islet a low black tooth against the stars. Zephyrine got Emil aboard, got the oars shipped without a sound, got fifteen yards offshore before the lantern came around the point.

It came slow, swinging, held by a figure walking the tideline with the unhurried confidence of someone who knows exactly what he's looking at. Behind Zephyrine, Emil's breath went shallow and quick.

"Scoot down," Zephyrine breathed. "Flat. Now."

Then they shipped oars, picked up the basket, and began, with tremendous deliberation, to fish.

Not to fish, precisely — to tend lines. There were lines, set that morning, honest lines with honest hooks, and Zephyrine hauled them in by lanternless moonlight with the unhurried competence of a person whose entire soul is occupied by shellfish. The lantern drew closer. Ten yards. Five.

"Late for fishing, Joubert."

Corporal Reyes. Of course. Alone, off-book, his rifle slung and his breath fogging — a boy taking the cold watch because nobody senior wanted it.

"Fish don't keep curfew," Zephyrine said. "I've complained to the factor about it repeatedly."

Something crossed Reyes's face at the word *factor* — a flinch, quickly mastered — and Zephyrine, whose life currently depended on noticing things, noticed it.

"You should head in," Reyes said, not moving. "Lieutenant's orders. No boats on the water after dark."

"This is a coracle of domestic commerce," said Zephyrine. "Also I'm nearly done. Also, you look freezing. Take the broth flask. Marguerite made it, and she counts the cakes you take, so don't think you can skip dinner."

That earned half a smile, there in the dark, before Reyes mastered himself again. He stood a moment longer, looking at the water, at the skiff, at Zephyrine's face — and Zephyrine understood with cold clarity that this was the moment, that whatever Reyes decided in the next five seconds would decide everything, and that the corporal knew it too.

"Pull your lines," Reyes said finally. "Go home. And Joubert — " he hesitated, and his voice dropped, and what he said next was so quiet the channel nearly took it: "Whoever wrote the report on you. It wasn't a soldier. We don't write reports like that. It came in written, anonymous, to Brant's desk. Somebody on this island knows how to use company stationery."

He turned and walked back along the tideline, lantern swinging, and did not look back.

Zephyrine pulled for Sable Islet with arms made of lead and a mind made of ice. They got Emil into the smokehouse, got the false floor settled over him, got the broth flask and the willow-bark passed down into the dark — Emil gripping their wrist once, hard, and saying only, "Come back," and Zephyrine saying only, "Obviously" — and then they rowed home alone under the failing stars, turning one fact over and over like a pearl with a flaw in it.

An anonymous report. Company stationery. Someone literate, someone with access, someone with a reason to destroy a pearl-diver's child rather than go to Brant directly.

Sixty-two souls on Meridian Cay. And as Zephyrine stepped onto the shingle at the landing and saw, by the light of the storehouse window, a figure standing in the shadows beside the ration ledgers — a figure holding a lantern up deliberately, so Zephyrine would know they'd been seen and expected — the list of suspects got suddenly, terribly short.

"Zephyrine," said the figure pleasantly. "Walk with me. I think we should discuss what you've been diving for."
