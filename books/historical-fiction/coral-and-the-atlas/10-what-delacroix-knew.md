## 10. What Delacroix Knew

The factor's house sat at the crown of the island like a hat worn by someone who thought very highly of hats. It was two stories of pale timber with shutters painted the blue of expensive ink, and it had been sealed since they carried Delacroix out of it feet-first. Now, in the gray hour before dawn, Zephyrine stood in the alley behind it, listening to Odile pick the lock of the pantry door with a fish bone.

"You learned that from your grandmother," Zephyrine whispered.

"I learned patience from my grandmother," Odile said. "I learned this from the cook she worked for, who was a thief and a saint in roughly equal measure." The lock gave a small, apologetic click. "There. And if anyone asks, we are here because we heard a noise."

"What noise?"

"A suspicious noise."

"Odile, that's worse. That's so much worse than no noise."

"Shh." She pushed the door open on hinges that had been oiled recently and well — the factor had been a man who took care of his comforts — and slipped inside, pulling Zephyrine after her.

The pantry smelled of cloves and candle wax and something underneath both, something that did not belong in any pantry: the sweetish, mineral smell of the sea at low tide, of wet rope, of the mangrove shallows where things hid when the water left them. Zephyrine knew that smell the way other children knew the smell of their mothers' kitchens.

"He kept diving weights in here," Zephyrine said, surprised. "Look. A whole crate of them."

"Or gunpowder wrapped in oilcloth," Odile said darkly, but when she pried at the crate lid, it was only lead. Weight belts, dive lines, a set of face-glasses of a make Zephyrine had never seen — glass ground thick and round, rimmed in brass, made for eyes that wanted to stay open underwater. Delacroix had owned diving equipment. The factor who had spent nine years on Meridian Rock counting pearls into ledgers had owned the tools of the trade he was supposed only to buy.

"Why would he have these?" Zephyrine asked.

"Same reason anyone has anything they shouldn't," Odile said. "Someone paid him, or someone frightened him, or both, and usually both come together like barnacles on a hull." She lit the shuttered lantern she'd brought and held it up, and the little room leapt into being around them: sacks of rice stamped with the Company's meridian-and-star, barrels, jars of pickled limes, and against the far wall, a desk that had no business being in a pantry.

It was a small desk, but it was locked, and it was bolted to the floor.

"Well," Odile said. "Well, well."

Zephyrine crouched beside it while Odile worked the fish bone into the keyhole again — she had graduated to the desk without discussion, the way she graduated to most things — and tried to think about Delacroix as a man instead of a body. That was harder than it should have been. In life, the factor had been mostly a voice from the weighing room, calling numbers in a flat tone that made even beauty sound like arithmetic. *Two hundred and eleven carats, lot forty.* He had thin hands and a habit of touching his collarbone when he lied, which meant he touched it often. He had once given Zephyrine a whole dried fig for finding his dropped spectacles, and had watched them eat it with an expression of such naked anxiety that Zephyrine had understood, even at eight years old, that kindness cost this man something and he was never sure he could afford it.

Now he was a shape under sacking in the weighing room, guarded day and night by men of the Company's own militia, and everyone on the island said the same thing in different ways: *the spy killed him, the spy from the cutter, Voss's boy.* Everyone except Zephyrine, who had seen Luc Voss the night the factor died — grey-faced, fevered, barely able to stand upright without leaning on a wall — and knew that a man could not strangle anyone while he was fighting simply to keep breathing.

Which meant somebody else had killed Delacroix. And whoever it was had wanted, very much, for everyone to believe otherwise.

"There," Odile breathed. The desk drawer slid open.

Inside lay a ledger — not one of the great bound volumes in the counting-house, but a slim private book with a marbled cover — a bundle of letters tied with string, and a small tin box that rattled.

* * *

They carried everything to Odile's room above the cooperage, because Odile's room had a door that locked from the inside and a landlady who was deaf in one ear and loyal in the other. By lantern light, with the rain starting against the shutters in the soft persistent way of the season, Zephyrine opened the ledger while Odile read the letters.

The ledger was not written in numbers.

That was the first strange thing. Nine years Zephyrine had lived under the Company's arithmetic; they could read a tally sheet faster than a prayer. But this book was words, in Delacroix's cramped, careful hand — the hand of a man who wrote as though afraid the paper would argue — and the words were dated, and the dates went back further than Zephyrine had been alive.

*Received per the *Cormorant*, out of Meridian, cargo entered as salt beef. Not salt beef.*

*Weight discrepancy, spring survey. Entered loss: four hundredweight. Actual loss, by my count at the waterline: nothing. Nothing lost. Something gained and not entered. I am told to enter it as storm damage. There has been no storm.*

*Asked Brant's predecessor what lies beneath the north shoal. Was told to weigh pearls and not questions. I have decided to do both.*

"Zephyrine." Odile's voice had changed. She was holding one of the letters out flat, the way you hold something that might still be alive. "Read this one."

The letter was old, the ink gone brown, but the seal was intact and unmistakable: the meridian-and-star of the Meridian Trading Company, pressed deep and confident. It was signed at the bottom with a name Zephyrine knew from the manifests, from the ration chits, from the brass plate outside the office that smelled of pipe smoke and authority.

*Factor Delacroix — Your discretion regarding the north shoal operations continues to be valued. You will continue to enter all recoveries as weather losses. The salvage crews' indenture terms are to be renewed as drafted. Death and desertion reduce our obligations admirably; see that the rolls reflect this. You will say nothing to anyone, including especially those whose deaths you record. A man who knows what he knows must be a man who says nothing. This is what his wages purchase.*

— *for the Director, Meridian House*

Zephyrine read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times the words kept sliding off, refusing to mean what they plainly said.

The north shoal. Every diver on the island knew the north shoal the way sailors know a reef that has taken ships: as a place of bad water and bad luck, where three divers had drowned in Zephyrine's grandfather's time and two more in their father's, where the current ran strange and the company posted a boat at the edge of it during season, ostensibly to watch for squalls. The company called it a hazard. The islanders called it the Hungry Ground, half in jest, the way people joke about the things that have actually eaten their neighbors.

But there were no hazards on the north shoal. There was a wreck — an old one, older than the Company itself, from the look of the ledger's earliest pages — and the Company had spent forty years quietly picking it clean and writing off every recovery as storm damage so that the treasure of some drowned king or drowned republic never touched a tax book, a shareholder's report, or a customs officer's honest eye.

And the salvage crews. The indentured divers sent out to the Hungry Ground season after season. *Death and desertion reduce our obligations admirably.* An indentured diver who died cost the Company nothing. An indentured diver who survived a season on the north shoal might talk.

"They weren't accidents," Zephyrine said. Their own voice sounded far away. "Any of them. My mother's cousin Tomas. The Baptiste brothers. They weren't drowned by bad luck. They were sent to a wreck nobody was meant to survive, over and over, because dead divers don't draw wages and living ones ask questions."

Odile had gone very still. She was a broad, calm woman who cooped barrels for a living and laughed easily, and Zephyrine had seen her frightened exactly once before, the night the bridge closed. Now she was frightened again, but underneath the fear her jaw was set in a way Zephyrine recognized: it was the face she made before doing something irreversible.

"My uncle Baptiste," she said quietly. "Both of them. My grandmother went to the Company office with a petition. She walked all the way down the tide road with her good shawl on." She turned the letter over in her hands. "They gave her a sack of rice and told her the sea takes what the sea is owed."

"The sea wasn't owed anything. The Company was owed silence, and it collected." Zephyrine looked down at the ledger, at the neat entries marching through the years, and felt something open up inside them that was not quite grief and not quite anger but had the weight of both. All those years of standing on the tidal flats watching the Company boats head north. All those seasons of holding breath until stars burst, learning the trade that fed them, and the trade had been feeding a graveyard.

And then, because Zephyrine's mind had always worked sideways, another thought surfaced, cold and bright as a pearl coming up out of the dark water.

"Luc," they said. "Odile. Why does Brant want Luc Voss hanged so badly?"

Odile frowned. "Because he's a spy. Because there's a war on somewhere and the Company has picked its side."

"No — why does *Brant* want him hanged. Before anyone from the mainland even arrives. Before any trial worth the word. He's been ready to hang that boy since the hour the factor died, and the bridge hasn't opened, and nobody's ordered him to. He wants it finished before anyone can ask questions." Zephyrine spread the ledger open on the table, the old letter beside it. "Delacroix kept records. Forty years of them. If Ilsa Voss's husband was a salvage captain — if her family's ship went down working the north shoal — if her son came here following any of it—"

"—then Delacroix isn't just a murder anymore," Odile finished slowly. "He's a witness who got murdered. Or a blackmailer. Or a man who finally grew a conscience and started keeping receipts." She tapped the last page of the ledger, the final entry, dated six days before his death. "Look at this."

The last entry was short. Zephyrine read it aloud, though their voice caught partway through:

*"He came to me tonight, the young one. Voss's boy. He showed me papers — his mother's papers. Charts of the north shoal in her hand, and a manifest from before the war. He knows what his father died doing. He wants the truth and I am too old and too frightened to give it to him, but I have decided, God forgive me, that someone will have it even if it kills me, which it may. Everything is copied. Everything is hidden where the water keeps its own counsel. If I die, let them dig in the place where the drowned ledger sleeps."*

The rain picked up against the shutters. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog barked twice at nothing and gave it up.

"The drowned ledger," Odile repeated. "Where the water keeps its own counsel." She looked at Zephyrine, and her expression was almost apologetic. "You're the diver."

"I'm the diver," Zephyrine agreed. Their heart was going hard and quick, but underneath the fear was something else, something warm and stubborn that had been growing since the night Captain Voss had looked at them across a sunlit deck and asked for a promise. Luc Voss had come to this island hunting his father's truth and it had very nearly killed him. Ilsa Voss was out there somewhere, declared an enemy of the crown, waiting to hear whether her son lived. And here, in a dead man's marbled notebook, was the thing that could save all three of them — because a boy hanged for murder was convenient, but a boy carrying proof of forty years of the Company's drownings was dangerous, and Brant knew the difference better than anyone.

"It'll be in the water somewhere close," Zephyrine said, thinking aloud. "He wouldn't trust it ashore — the militia searches every house twice a week now. Somewhere he could reach it himself without being seen. The mangroves, maybe, or—" They stopped. The smell. The pantry had smelled of wet rope and mangrove shallows. Of course. He'd kept his diving gear in the pantry because he used it. Regularly. From somewhere near his own house.

"Zephyrine?" Odile said.

"The channel pool," Zephyrine said slowly. "Behind the factor's garden, where the tide comes in under the rocks. There's a cave mouth under the north lip — I found it when I was ten, diving for lost coins. Locals won't go near it; they say it's a devil's kitchen." They almost smiled, and the smile tasted strange. "Delacroix put his spectacles in the weighing-room lost-and-found box himself once rather than admit where he'd dropped them. He was a man who hid things in plain sight and then worried about it for years. A devil's kitchen under his own garden is exactly where a man like that would hide the thing that scared him most."

Outside, boots on cobblestones. Two pairs, unhurried, passing. The militia walked the town in the small hours now, and everyone had learned to stop talking when the boots went by, and to start again only when they couldn't hear their own heart.

When the boots faded, Odile spoke, very quietly. "You can't dive it alone. The cave mouth at slack tide, in storm season, with the current running wrong — even your mother wouldn't try it alone."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I'll need you on the line. And I'll need daylight, and I'll need Brant's patrol to be looking somewhere else." They gathered the ledger and the letters back into their bundle, wrapping them in oilcloth, and their hands were steady now, which surprised them. Fear was still there — fear was a permanent resident lately, like the damp — but purpose had moved in alongside it and the two of them shared the room uneasily, like old married people.

"And Zephyrine." Odile caught their sleeve at the door. Her face in the lantern light was grave. "If the drowned ledger says what I think it says — if it names names, if it shows what happened to my uncles and your cousin and every diver the Company ever fed to the Hungry Ground — then it's worth more than Luc Voss's life. It's worth more than ours. Brant won't let it see daylight. Whatever happens tomorrow, remember that the most dangerous man on this island isn't the one with the gallows."

"It's the one who needs the hanging," Zephyrine said.

It was fully light when Zephyrine climbed the path home along the ridge, the oilcloth bundle strapped tight against their chest beneath their coat, the eastern sky the color of a pearl fresh out of the shell. Below, in the weighing room, a squad of militiamen was changing the guard over Delacroix's body, and at their head stood Brant himself, tall and dry in the drizzle, giving orders.

And Brant, without turning his head — without any reason at all to look up at the ridge path at that exact moment — looked up.

His gaze found Zephyrine across forty yards of morning air and held there, patient and unreadable, for the length of one long breath. Then he turned back to his men as if nothing had happened, and Zephyrine stood frozen on the path, understanding with sudden terrible clarity that the race for the drowned ledger had already begun — and that Brant had just marked the starting line on their back.
