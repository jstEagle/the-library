## 11. Brant's Bargain

The militia kept its headquarters in the old customs house, which everyone on Meridian still called the Salt House because the walls wept brine in the wet season and no amount of lime or paint had ever persuaded them otherwise. Zephyrine had passed it a thousand times without thinking twice — a squat stone building with a bell tower that hadn't rung since the year of the great storm, its windows fogged with salt-frost so the whole place looked like it was peering out at the world through cataracts.

Now they stood outside it at dawn, hands shaking not from cold but from what they were about to do, and thought: *I am going to walk into the wolf's den and ask the wolf for a favor.*

"You could still turn around," Odile murmured beside them. She had insisted on coming, over every argument Zephyrine had mustered, and had won by the simple expedient of refusing to lose. Her gray braid was pinned up tight, her good shawl around her shoulders, and she carried a basket of dried fish as though she were merely an old woman delivering provisions to soldiers, which was exactly the sort of lie Odile wore best.

"And leave you standing here looking innocent? You've never managed it once in your life."

"I'm very good at it," Odile said. "You just never notice because you're always watching my hands."

Zephyrine almost smiled. Almost. Then the door of the Salt House opened and a militiaman looked out at them, young and bored and armed with a musket he held the way a farmer holds a rake, and the smile died where it stood.

"We're here to see Captain Brant," Zephyrine said.

"About what?"

"About the murder," Zephyrine said, and watched the word land like a fish dropped on a deck. "Tell him Zephyrine Joubert knows who killed Factor Delacroix."

* * *

They waited an hour in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old rope, on benches worn smooth by generations of smugglers, debtors, and men waiting to be hanged. Odile hummed under her breath — one of the diving songs, the slow ones, the ones meant to keep a diver's heart even in deep water — and Zephyrine understood it for what it was: a rope thrown across dark water.

Then the inner door opened and Sergeant Brant came through it, and Zephyrine revised everything they'd assumed about him in the space of a single breath.

They had expected the company's militia captain to be like the factor's clerks: soft-handed, sharp-eyed, a man made entirely of arithmetic. Instead Brant was broad and weathered, with a face like driftwood — bleached, cracked, but solid all the way through — and a limp he didn't try to hide. He dismissed the guard with a jerk of his head, sat down across from them heavily, and looked at Zephyrine for a long moment before speaking.

"Joubert. The diver." He said it not as a question. "You pulled bodies out of the channel after the *Cormorant* went down. Six years ago. You were fourteen."

"Yes, sir."

"I was there when they brought the third one in." His voice was flat, but something moved behind it. "My brother was on that ship."

Zephyrine hadn't known that. The *Cormorant* had been a company grain ship, lost with eleven hands in a squall, and the island had mourned in its usual way — quietly, quickly, and then back to work. "I'm sorry," they said, and meant it, and watched something in Brant's face decide something.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." He leaned forward. "You sent word you know who killed Delacroix. That's either true, or you're the bravest fool on this island, and I've met most of the candidates."

"It's true. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"I know who killed him," Zephyrine said carefully, "and I know why, and I know what he found out right before he died. But I'll tell you only if we make a bargain first."

Brant's eyebrows rose slowly, like two caterpillars encountering an unexpected leaf. "A bargain. With me."

"With the company. Through you."

"Child, I arrest people for a living. Bargaining is just arresting with better manners."

Odile snorted. It was entirely involuntary — Zephyrine saw her clap a hand over her mouth a half-second too late — and Brant turned to look at her with the weary expression of a man who has spent his career being laughed at by grandmothers.

"You find something amusing, madam?"

"I find it amusing that you think this child came here to bargain," Odile said, lowering her hand. "They came here because they're out of options, and they're hoping you are too. And from the look of your boots, captain, you haven't slept in three days, your men have searched half the island twice and found nothing, and the company office in Port Meridian is sending someone to replace you if you don't produce a murderer soon. So yes. I find it funny. Forgive an old woman her humor."

There was a long silence. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the bell tower, making the dead bell hum faintly, like a sleeper muttering.

"My boots," Brant said at last, "are standard issue."

"They're left-footed wrong and resoled twice," Odile said. "I resoled them myself, three years ago, for a man named Perrin who said he had a cousin in the militia. You'd be the cousin, then."

Brant stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. "God help me. The whole island is related." He rubbed his face with both hands. "All right. Talk. What's your bargain?"

Zephyrine took a breath. This was the part they had rehearsed all night, lying awake in the loft while the rain worked at the roof, turning the words over until they were smooth as sea glass. Every version of the plan had come back to this room, to this chair, to this one impossible roll of the dice: trusting the company's own soldier.

"The killer is a man named Anselme Roche. He works the drying sheds. Delacroix discovered that Roche had been skimming pearl weight off the company's ledgers for years — small amounts, hidden well — and instead of reporting him, Delacroix was blackmailing him. Roche killed him to stop the bleeding. I can prove the blackmail. There are letters."

"Letters." Brant's eyes narrowed. "Where?"

"That's the second part of the bargain." Zephyrine held his gaze, heart hammering, voice steady by sheer force of will. "I give you the letters and Roche. In exchange, the company releases my indenture. Full papers. Signed before witnesses."

Brant sat back. Outside, a gull screamed once and fell silent, as though embarrassed by the noise.

"You want me to buy a confession with a piece of paper."

"I want you to buy a murderer with a piece of paper. Cheap, captain. Cheaper than a trial, cheaper than sending to Port Meridian for investigators, cheaper than another week of this island starving while your men kick over fishing nets. You get your killer, the company gets its letters, and I stop being property. Everyone walks away richer except Roche, and he started it."

It was a good speech. Zephyrine knew it was a good speech because they had practiced it into the floorboards until Odile threatened to smother them with a pillow. But Brant didn't answer right away. He got up, limped to the salt-fogged window, and stood looking out at the harbor, where the tide was pulling back from the flats and exposing the long brown spine of the tidal road.

"Do you know what I did before the company gave me this uniform?" he said, without turning. "Twenty-two years in the crown navy. Gunner's mate, then bosun. I shelled ports for men in offices like this one. When my service ended, they offered me a pension and a pat on the head, and the company offered me a wage, and I took the wage, because a man has to eat." He turned around. "I'm telling you this so you understand I'm not sentimental about the company. But I'll tell you something else. There's a boy in my cells right now. Sixteen years old. They caught him with a company signal flag in his kit, and the order from Port Meridian says anyone aiding the traitor Voss is to be treated as a spy, and spies hang." His jaw tightened. "He cries at night. He thinks I can't hear him."

Zephyrine's blood went cold and hot at once. Tomas. It had to be Tomas — Ilsa Voss's son, caught somewhere between the safehouse and the mangroves, captured with the one thing that would damn him beyond appeal. All their careful plans, the boat, the tides, the promise — and the sea had already taken him.

"When?" Their voice cracked on the word.

"Two nights ago. Near the north channel." Brant studied them. "And here's what interests me, Joubert. A wounded boy, alone, in a marsh, carrying a signal flag he has no earthly use for — unless someone was going to signal a boat. Someone local. Someone who knew the channels." He let that sit in the air between them like smoke. "So here's *my* bargain. You give me Roche and the letters. I give you your indenture papers. And I lose the paperwork on one sixteen-year-old prisoner, who will escape custody tonight through a window I inexplicably failed to bar, and vanish forever."

Zephyrine couldn't speak. Beside them, Odile had gone very still, her hands folded on her basket, her eyes bright and wet.

"Why?" Zephyrine finally managed. "Why would you—"

"Because I've read the orders from Port Meridian," Brant said quietly. "All of them. Including the ones Delacroix received before he died — the ones Roche apparently never saw, sitting in the factor's desk, asking him to falsify tonnage reports on the grain shipments. Eleven men drowned off the *Cormorant*, Joubert, because she was overloaded past her marks and the reports said otherwise. My brother wasn't lost to a squall. He was lost to a ledger." He picked up his hat from the bench. "I can't hang the company. It's too big and I'm too small. But I can hang Roche, free a boy who never chose any of this, and sleep one night without hearing crying through the wall. That's my bargain. Take it or leave it."

* * *

They walked home along the tide road with the water returning around their ankles, warm as bathwater, the flats steaming in the late-morning sun. Odile carried the basket of fish, which they had never delivered and never intended to. Neither of them spoke for a long time.

"He knew," Zephyrine said at last. "About Tomas. About the plan. Maybe not all of it, but enough. He's been watching us."

"Everyone's been watching everyone," Odile said. "That's what happens when you seal an island. We turn our eyes inward and find out what we look like." She shifted the basket to her other hip. "I don't trust him."

"I know."

"But I believe him. Those aren't the same thing, and at your age you should learn the difference early." She stopped walking, and the water swirled around her calves, and she looked at Zephyrine with an expression they had seen maybe five times in their life — the expression she wore when she was about to say something she'd been carrying a long way. "Your mother trusted a company man once. That's how you exist. He signed her indenture transfer and put her on a boat and she landed here and met your father, and it was the luckiest worst day of her life. Company men aren't wolves, Zeph. They're dogs. Some bite, some herd, some just want to be told they're good. The trick is learning which is which before you put your hand out."

"And Brant?"

"Brant is old and tired and wants to go home." Odile resumed walking. "Which makes him the most dangerous kind of honest there is. Honest men with nothing left to lose will do anything for a clean conscience."

The rest of the day was work, because the island did not pause for conspiracies. There were oysters to rack, nets to mend, the ration line to stand in — smaller again, the rice cut with more sand than ever, and old Père Anselme's shed conspicuously shuttered, its owner nowhere to be seen. Zephyrine mended netting with fingers that trembled and listened to the dock talk flow around them: the militia searching the mangroves again, the factor's office sealed, the rumor that a company cutter would arrive with the spring tide to carry off "the traitor's accomplices," whoever those might be. The word *accomplices* sat in Zephyrine's chest like a swallowed hook.

At dusk, a note appeared under the door of their house, written in a clerk's careful hand:

*Roche will be taken tomorrow at the evening ration line. Have the letters ready at the Salt House by noon. Bring nothing else. Tell no one. After the arrest, come to the cells — the east window is loose. — B.*

Zephyrine read it twice, fed it to the cookfire, and watched the paper curl and blacken and rise.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow Anselme Roche would pay for Delacroix, and Tomas would slip out an unbarred window into the dark, and Zephyrine would have to get a wounded, frightened boy across an island full of soldiers to a boat hidden in the mangroves — with the whole garrison about to be roused by an arrest, and a company cutter coming with the spring tide, and every eye on the island turned inward, hunting.

Odile appeared at their shoulder with two cups of chicory coffee, strong enough to strip barnacles, and pressed one into their hands.

"Well?" she said.

"Tomorrow," Zephyrine said. "Everything happens tomorrow."

"Everything always does." She touched her cup to theirs. "Get some sleep, little fish. Tomorrow you swim."

But sleep did not come easily, and when it finally did, it came full of water — and in the last hour before dawn, Zephyrine woke suddenly to a sound they knew better than their own heartbeat: the deep, far-off groan of the bridge bell at the mainland crossing, ringing out over the flats.

Ringing early.

The tide road was opening ahead of schedule — and with it, whatever was coming across.
