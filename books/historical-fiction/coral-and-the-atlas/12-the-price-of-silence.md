## 12. The Price of Silence

The bell rang eleven times before it stopped, which was wrong in every way a bell could be wrong. It rang too early for the tide. It rang too long for a signal. And it rang with the flat, urgent voice of a thing that had been waiting all night for permission to speak.

Zephyrine stood at the window of the loft above the drying sheds, still half in the grip of the dream — water, always water, and something gleaming far below that was either a pearl or a face — and watched the flats come up out of the dark. The ebb had barely begun. The tide road should have been a rumor until midmorning. Instead, by the first grey light, there were figures already on it, six of them, walking the causeway in that deliberate, spread-out way soldiers walked when they wanted you to count them.

Behind them came a seventh figure who did not walk like a soldier at all. He walked like a man crossing a ballroom floor that someone had unfortunately flooded.

"The mainland sent a *gentleman*," Zephyrine said to the empty room, and the empty room said nothing, which was more courtesy than Zephyrine felt like giving anyone before breakfast.

* * *

By the time the party reached the gate, half the island had found reasons to be outdoors. There is no spectacle a tidal island loves better than an arrival, except perhaps a departure, and since nobody had left Coral's Edge in nine days except feet-first, the arrival won by acclamation.

Zephyrine wedged into the crowd near the fish stalls, where Odile was already installed behind her counter like a general at a ridge, arms folded, weighing the newcomers with the same flat expression she used on mackerel.

"That one," she said quietly, without moving her lips, "has never been rained on in his life."

The gentleman in question wore grey wool, of all things, wool, in this heat — and carried a leather case chained to his wrist the way other men carried a prayer book. He looked at the fish stalls, the drying racks, the crowd, and his gaze kept sliding off everything as though none of it could possibly be relevant to him.

"Militia came back with a *clerk*," Odile muttered. "You know what that means."

"That somebody upstairs wants paper instead of blood."

"Somebody upstairs always wants both. They just send the paper first so the blood has somewhere to be written down." She flipped a mackerel on her rack with unnecessary force. "Go on. You've got gulls circling you, little fish, and I don't mean the feathered kind."

She was right. At the edge of the crowd, Sergeant Mabry — the youngest of the militia, the one who still said *thank you* when handed bread — was scanning faces with the pained look of a man following instructions he didn't enjoy. His eyes found Zephyrine and stopped.

"Diver," he said. "Captain wants you. Warehouse. When the bell rings the hour."

"And if I'm diving?"

"Then he wants you wetter."

Zephyrine smiled the way you smile at weather, and Mabry, to his credit, almost smiled back before remembering which side of the ration ledger he stood on.

* * *

The warehouse smelled of tar, salt meat, and the particular institutional loneliness of buildings that exist only to contain value. Factor Delacroix's desk still stood in its place, scrubbed clean now, everything squared away by hands that wanted very much for the past week to have never happened. Behind it sat two men.

Brant she knew. Captain of the Meridian militia, a broad, patient man with a face like a cliff that had thought about eroding and decided against it. He looked tired in the specific way of a man who had walked a tide road at four in the morning because somebody important told him to.

The other man was the wool coat. He did not rise.

"Zephyrine Joubert," Brant said. "This is Master Corvin, assessor for the Meridian Trading Company, home office."

"Assessor," Zephyrine said. "Of what?"

"Of losses." Corvin opened the leather case without hurry. He had small, clean hands, and he handled paper the way Zephyrine's mother had handled pearls — by never letting them touch anything they might mark him. "The company has suffered several categories of loss this season, Joubert. A factor. A cargo manifest. Certain... reputational assets. My task is to determine what can be recovered and what must be written off."

"And I'm what, exactly? An asset or a loss?"

For the first time, Corvin looked directly at them, and Zephyrine understood why he'd worn wool: some men needed armor even in summer. There was nothing cruel in his face. That was the unsettling part. It was simply a face that had reduced cruelty to arithmetic.

"That depends," Corvin said, "on your memory."

He slid a single sheet across the desk. It was a deposition — Zephyrine recognized the format from the indenture papers, the same lovingly dense legal script — and it began, *On the night of the ninth, I did witness...*

"I don't remember the ninth," Zephyrine said carefully.

"You do," Brant said quietly. "Everyone remembers the ninth. The lamps went out along the quay for half an hour. Nobody's forgotten it. The question is what you saw while they were out."

Zephyrine read the deposition. It was very thorough. It placed them on the north quay — correct. It noted their known acquaintance with Captain Ilsa Voss — correct, and public record, from peacetime, from sunnier years. And then it arrived, in careful clauses, at the part where Zephyrine Joubert witnessed a wounded stranger lurking near the factor's office on the night Delacroix died.

It was a lie shaped exactly like the truth, which was, Zephyrine had to admit, craftsmanship.

"And if I sign this," Zephyrine said, "what happens to the man you're describing?"

"He'll be tried," Corvin said. "Properly. A tribunal, evidence, the full dignity of the law."

"On this island?"

Corvin paused a fraction too long, and Brant looked at the wall.

"There's a cutter coming," Corvin said instead. "Crown charter, within the fortnight. It will carry the tribunal, a magistrate, and my report. Sign this, and your testimony becomes part of an orderly process rather than a..." he considered, "...complication."

"And my indenture?"

"Assessed," Corvin said, "as recoverable." He drew a second paper from the case, and there it was — Zephyrine's own contract, the one signed by a mother's thumbprint and a company seal eleven years ago, with a neat diagonal line drawn across it in red ink and a space below for a signature. "The company is prepared to consider your obligation discharged. Full papers. Free passage off the island when the cutter arrives. A settlement against wages owed — and you are owed wages, Joubert, I checked, everyone here is owed wages, it's practically the company's religion."

The number written below the red line was more money than Zephyrine had ever seen assembled in one place, including the year the pearl beds gave that famous triple bloom and the whole island got drunk on cane spirits and hope.

Freedom. Papers. Enough coin to buy a boat, a berth on the mainland, a life with no company seal on any page of it.

All for one signature on a lie that would put a wounded man in front of a tribunal that would almost certainly hang him.

"You killed Delacroix yourselves," Zephyrine said. It wasn't a question, quite. "Or he knew something about the manifests, the short weights, the ghost cargoes, and someone decided he was a write-off. And now you need a body for the body, and the body you picked swims for a living and owes you his whole life."

The silence that followed had texture. Brant studied the wall as if it owed him money. Corvin folded his hands.

"What I need," Corvin said, "is order. Islands like this run on debts, Joubert. Every debt needs a debtor, or the ledger stops meaning anything. Give me a debtor, and this island goes back to being boring, and believe me—" he glanced, just once, at the tar-stained beams, the salt-rotted crates, the entire waterlogged world around him, "—I want this island to be boring more than you can possibly imagine."

"The bell will ring the hour soon," Brant said, gently, to the wall.

"I'll want to consult my memory," Zephyrine said. "It's a diver's memory. It keeps strange hours."

Corvin nodded, unsurprised, unbothered, a man who had budgeted for refusal as a line item. "Until the evening bell," he said, and returned to his papers, dismissing them the way a tide dismisses a footprint.

At the door, Brant caught Zephyrine's arm — lightly, the way you stop a child from stepping off a jetty.

"The offer's real," he said, low. "Papers and all. I wouldn't have brought him here otherwise." A pause. "And the alternative, if you're caught helping that woman's son — there's no red line through *that* contract. Understand me?"

"I understand," Zephyrine said. "You understand I can't swim in a straight line. Never could."

Brant let go. Something in his cliff-face shifted, almost imperceptibly — and Zephyrine would spend the rest of the day deciding whether it had been pity or warning, and would fail.

* * *

Odile closed the smokehouse early, which she had done exactly twice in living memory: once for a funeral, and once for a wedding she disapproved of. This counted as both.

"They want you to drown quietly," she said, feeding the fire slivers of green wood until the smoke ran sweet and thick, "and hand them the body of your word as payment. That's the whole trick of it, little fish. Silence isn't free. Silence is the most expensive thing on this island, and they're asking you to buy it with someone else's neck."

"They offered wages owed. Eleven years of wages."

"Hm. And how much do you reckon they stole first?" She jabbed the fire. "A company doesn't pay what it owes. It pays what it fears. Which means you should be asking what you know that frightens them — because it isn't your testimony, child. Testimony from an indentured diver is worth less than their ink. They're buying your *silence*, which means somewhere there's a thing you might say that costs them more than gold."

"The ledgers," Zephyrine said slowly. "Delacroix's real ledgers."

"Which are either ash by now," Odile said, "or sitting at the bottom of some channel wearing chains, because that's where honest paper goes on this island." She looked up, and her eyes were shrewd and kind at once, which was Odile's most dangerous combination. "You're going to say no to them. I can see it loading in your face like a crossbow."

"I promised the captain. In sunlight, in peacetime, when promises were easy. You don't get to renegotiate a promise just because the weather changed."

"No," Odile agreed. "You don't." She turned a fish. "But you *do* get to be afraid about it. Refusing to be afraid is how people die stupid. Be afraid, and die clever."

* * *

The mangroves received Zephyrine the way they always had — ankle-deep in warm water, roots arching like the ribs of some enormous patient beast, the air thick enough to chew. Captain Voss heard them coming before she saw them; she always did. She stepped out from behind a root wall with a boathook held like a spear, saw who it was, and lowered it by degrees.

"Well?" she said. "You've got the face of bad news and the walk of good news, and I've never known how to read that combination."

"A mainland clerk offered to buy me today. Papers, wages, freedom. Price was your son's neck."

Voss sat down on a root, slowly, the way people sit when something heavy has been set down inside them.

"And Emil's answer to that," Zephyrine continued, "is going to be to do something heroic and idiotic, so we move tonight instead of tomorrow. Tide's wrong, moon's wrong, everything's wrong, but the cutter comes within the fortnight and the bridge is open *now*, and wrong tides have carried smarter smugglers than me."

From deeper in the green shade came a rustle, then a voice younger than expected and steadier than it had any right to be.

"I heard all of it," said Emil Voss, emerging with a crutch under one arm and his mother's jaw on his face. "And I'm not letting anyone hang for me. Least of all someone who's already spent."

"Sit down," Voss said.

"I am sitting. Technically. Three points of contact."

"Emil—"

"If they sign that paper," he said to Zephyrine, ignoring her entirely, "they're free. Actually free. You'd trade that for me? A stranger with a hole in his shoulder and terrible sea legs?"

"Not a stranger," Zephyrine said. "Your mother fished me out of the channel when I was nine and a rip current was doing its level best, and she never once mentioned it after, which on this island means it was a serious favor. Debts like that don't expire. And nobody hangs in my place. Not while I can still hold my breath longer than anyone else in the company books."

Emil opened his mouth.

"Don't argue with the diver," Voss said, and there was such warmth under the gruffness that even Emil shut up. "Tonight, then. Dusk bell. We'll need—"

The words stopped in her mouth.

Because through the tangle of roots, faint and far and utterly wrong, came the sound of the bridge bell again. Ringing the evening hour. Hours early. And beneath it, thinner, unmistakable, the creak of timber and chain — the sound of the tide-road gate swinging wide.

At dusk. With the light dying. With nobody meant to cross until morning.

Voss and Zephyrine looked at each other across the darkening water, and Zephyrine thought, with a calm that surprised them: *someone told them where we are.*

"Someone moved our crossing up," Voss breathed. "And I'd wager my ship it wasn't us who told them."
