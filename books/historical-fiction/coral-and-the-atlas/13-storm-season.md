## 13. Storm Season

The first storm of the season arrived the way old debts do — announced for weeks, denied until the last possible moment, and then suddenly everywhere at once.

Zephyrine felt it coming in their teeth three days before the sky turned. That was a diver's inheritance, along with the burst eardrum scar and the permanent taste of brine: a low ache in the jaw when the pressure dropped, as though the sea were already speaking through them, impatient to be heard. Their mother had called it *the sea clearing its throat*. She'd said it cheerfully, right up until the season she didn't come back up from the deep beds.

By the time the company's bell began its slow, ugly clanging — the signal that all work on the water was forbidden and all workers were to report for indoor labor — Zephyrine was already at the shuttered window of the drying shed, watching the horizon go the color of a bruise.

"You look like your grandmother," said Odile, who had appeared beside them with the silent, unnerving efficiency she brought to everything, including grief. "She used to stand like that. Like she was listening to something rude."

"She usually was."

Odile snorted. It was the first sound resembling laughter either of them had made in days, and they both pretended not to notice.

* * *

The island in storm season was a different country. The tidal road that connected Meridian Rock to the mainland had drowned weeks ago, but now even the memory of it seemed absurd — the channel between them churned brown and white, throwing up things it had no business having: half a fishing skiff, a drowned goat, once, memorably, an entire wardrobe with all its drawers still in it, which Odile had claimed with the unembarrassed pragmatism of the truly poor and which now stood in her mother's kitchen holding salt fish.

The company, naturally, treated the weather as an opportunity.

"Indoor labor," Factor's clerk Pellier announced to the assembled divers and shore workers, standing on a crate so that his voice could carry over the wind, which was already testing its range like a singer before a performance. "Net mending. Shell sorting. Ledger copying. Anyone found outside the compound walls after the evening bell will be considered a deserter of contracted obligations."

"A deserter," muttered Baptiste, the oldest diver on the island, whose hands shook now unless he held them around a warm cup. "In a hurricane. He wants us to know he'd charge us rent on the lightning."

"He would," said Zephyrine quietly. "At interest."

Baptiste laughed, then coughed, then laughed again anyway, because at seventy-one you learned that laughing through a cough was one of the few freedoms left that couldn't be invoiced.

* * *

The compound during storms was worse than the weather. Two hundred people crowded into sheds built for sixty, the air thick with wet wool, tar, and the particular sourness of fear that has been rationed too long. The murdered factor's absence hung over everything; his replacement, the company militia captain named Roux, walked the aisles of net-menders with his boots ringing on the boards, and everyone bent lower over their work when he passed, the way grass bends before it is stepped on.

Zephyrine mended nets. It was, they had discovered, the perfect occupation for someone with a secret: it required the hands and only the hands. The eyes could go anywhere. And so while their fingers worked the twine in its patient, ancient rhythm, their eyes counted.

They counted the militiamen — eleven visible, which meant perhaps fourteen total, given the ones who'd be sleeping off rum. They counted the boats hauled above the tideline — six worth stealing, two worth dying in. They counted the sacks in the ration store, and did the arithmetic that everyone on the island was doing silently and no one dared say aloud: at current allocation, the food ran out nine days before the bridge could plausibly reopen, even if the storms relented early.

And they counted, every time Captain Roux passed, the way his eyes snagged on the door of the old cooperage — the one building on the compound that had been locked since the murder, the one where, if Odile's information was right, the wounded son of Captain Ilsa Voss lay hidden under canvas and coil rope, feverish, getting thinner by the day.

Nine days of food. One boy who needed more than he was rationed. One promise, made years ago in sunlight, that was starting to feel less like a debt of honor and more like a stone in the chest.

* * *

That evening, in the corner of the women's shed that Odile had annexed through sheer force of personality, the three of them held what Odile called a meeting and what was really just two people pretending to mend nets near a third person who was pretending to sleep.

"He ate today," Odile reported, in the low, flat voice she used for anything that mattered. "Half the biscuit. Kept down most of it."

"The fever?"

"Down. Not gone. He asked—" She stopped. Picked at the twine. Started again. "He asked if his mother knew we were coming. I told him yes."

It wasn't quite a lie. Captain Voss knew *that* they were coming; whether she knew *when*, or whether any message could reach her across a drowned sea in storm season, was a question none of them could answer. The cutter that had declared her an enemy of the crown had also, conveniently, cut the island off from every honest post. News traveled now only by rumor, and rumor traveled only as far as the militia allowed.

"My grandmother," Zephyrine said, working a knot, "used to say a promise is a boat. You build it in good weather, and then one day there's a storm, and you find out whether you built a boat or a story about a boat."

"Your grandmother sounds exhausting."

"She was. She was also never wrong, which is worse."

Odile looked at them across the lamplight. In the flicker, her face was young again — younger than her years, which the company had spent freely, the way it spent everything. "There's no boat, Zeph. There's a leaky dory under mangrove roots and a boy who can't walk to it without being carried. And between here and there are fourteen men with muskets and a captain who's decided that shooting a spy will make him look decisive to whatever auditor eventually crawls out here."

"I know."

"And the food runs out in nine days, and hungry men do desperate arithmetic."

"I know."

"And yet you've got that face. The listening-to-something-rude face."

Zephyrine tied off the knot and held the mended stretch of net up to the light, checking the mesh. Through it, the lamp flame broke into small bright pieces, like stars seen through water.

"I'm thinking about Delacroix's ledger," they said.

Odile went very still. Everyone on the island went very still at the mention of the ledger — the factor's private book, missing since the night of his murder, the object the militia had torn three sheds apart looking for, the thing Captain Roux wanted badly enough that he'd doubled the reward for information and halved the rations to pay for it.

"What about it?"

"I'm thinking that a man like Delacroix — a man who skimmed, who kept two sets of numbers, who was frightened enough of somebody to write things down — wouldn't keep his real ledger somewhere dry and sensible. Dry and sensible is where they search." Zephyrine lowered the net. "I'm thinking about where *I'd* put it, if I were a coward who trusted nothing on land."

Odile stared at them. Then, slowly, her eyes went to the window, to the black churn of the channel beyond it, and understanding moved across her face like weather.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you absolute eel."

* * *

The storm peaked two nights later, and it was in the teeth of it — in the howling, in the rain that came sideways and stung like flung gravel, in the dark so complete that the world existed only as sound — that Zephyrine finally understood why the sea cleared its throat before it spoke.

They had gone out for the boy's fever. Willow bark was gone; the company medic hoarded his quinine like a dragon; but the old remedies grew wild along the windward rocks, and Roux's curfew applied to people, and Zephyrine had decided, with the calm madness of the desperate, that a person crouched low against a rock in a hurricane was arguably not *outside* so much as *part of the landscape*.

They got the bark. They nearly lost an ear to a flying sheet of corrugated iron, which would have been symmetrical. And it was on the way back, pressed into the lee of the sea wall with the wind screaming overhead like a choir of the cheated, that they saw it.

Lightning lit the channel for one white instant — and in that instant, Zephyrine saw the wreck of the old pearl boat *Cormorant*, Delacroix's own vessel, broken on the reef shelf since before anyone could remember, its hull split open to the sea like a ribcage.

And they saw, wedged in the crook of that ribcage, above the high-tide line, inside the hull where only a diver could reach —

A shape. A box-shape. Wrapped in oilcloth.

Gone again in the next darkness, but seen. *Seen.*

Delacroix hadn't trusted the land. Delacroix had grown up on this rock same as everyone, had known what every child of divers knows: that the sea keeps what it's given better than any strongroom ever has. Nobody searches the drowned place. Nobody swims in storm season. Nobody dives a wreck on a reef shelf in a gale except a fool or—

Well. Except a diver's child with a promise to keep and nothing left to lose but an indenture they were already planning to spend.

Zephyrine stood in the roaring dark with the willow bark inside their shirt and their heart going like the company bell, and they started, very quietly, to laugh.

* * *

The next morning the storm had blown itself down to a sulk — heavy gray skies, a tired sea still slapping at the rocks like something insulted — and the island emerged to count its damages. Three roofs gone. The pier's end splintered. One of the militia boats stove in against the seawall, which improved the odds considerably and which everyone agreed, in the safety of their thoughts, was the storm's finest act of rebellion.

Roux stood on the pier surveying it all with the expression of a man doing sums that kept coming out against him. Zephyrine passed him at the required respectful distance, head bowed, willow bark long since delivered, face arranged into the pleasant blankness that indenture taught faster than any trade.

"Joubert," Roux said.

Their stomach dropped, but their feet kept moving, turning, bowing. "Captain."

"You dive. Before your contract was suspended for the season, you dove the deep beds."

"Since I was eight, Captain."

Something moved behind his eyes — the arithmetic of a man realizing that the tool he needed might already be standing in front of him, free of charge. "When the water settles, I'll have work for divers. Salvage. There's wreckage along the reef shelf from the blow, and cargo in it that belongs to the Company." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who believed smiling was a technique rather than a feeling. "You'll be well compensated."

"Of course, Captain," said Zephyrine, who was thinking: *the reef shelf. The Cormorant. He's going to send me directly to it, wearing his leash, diving his salvage, with his men watching from the boat.*

And also thinking: *he doesn't know which wreck. He doesn't know what's in it. And he has just handed me a reason to be in the water that no one will question.*

"Good," said Roux, dismissing them, and turned back to his ruined pier.

Zephyrine walked away across the compound with their heart hammering and their face smooth as a shell, and found Odile waiting by the water barrels, arms crossed, reading their expression the way she read everything — completely, and without mercy.

"Well?" she said.

Zephyrine glanced back once, at the gray sea, at the reef shelf hidden beneath it, at the box wrapped in oilcloth sitting in a dead man's boat like a heart in a ribcage.

"Get the boy ready to move," they said. "The tide road isn't the only way off this island. And in three days, I'm going diving."
