## 14. The Drowned Ledger

The tide gave back its dead slowly, the way a miser counts out coins.

Zephyrine had learned that from their father, back when their father still dove and still spoke. He'd say it with his mouth full of salt water, surfacing with a grin, holding up whatever the morning had surrendered — a spoon, a boot, once an entire church bell from a wreck so old nobody remembered the parish it had belonged to. *The sea keeps nothing forever,* he'd say. *It just likes to make you wait.*

Zephyrine thought of him now, treading water in the grey-green murk off Gull Rock, with a length of rope tied around their waist and the other end knotted to a mangrove root that Odile had tested three times with her whole body weight. Below them, maybe four fathoms down where the light went from amber to tea to nothing, lay the wreck of the *Cormorant* — the company sloop that had "accidentally" burned and slipped her moorings two years ago, taking with her, if Delacroix's dying hints were true, a strongbox full of ledgers that could hang half the men on this island.

Half the men, and every one of them currently carrying a militia musket.

"Anything?" Odile's voice came thin and warped through the water. She was stretched flat on the rocks above, one hand on the rope, her grey braid dripping, looking for all the world like a grandmother who had merely stopped to rest on her way to market and not like a woman aiding and abetting treason before breakfast.

Zephyrine surfaced long enough to spit and answer. "The hull's collapsed. I can see into the hold, but it's silted up to my chin down there. Give me ten more minutes and my whole life story."

"You've told me your whole life story twice already this week. It needs an editor."

"It needs a *different ending*," Zephyrine said, and went back under.

The cold closed over them like a hand. Down here the world was simple: pressure, breath, the burn in the chest that every diver's child learned to treat as a conversation rather than an enemy. *Not yet,* Zephyrine told the burn. *Not yet.* Their father's voice, or their own — after enough years underwater the two had merged.

The *Cormorant*'s hold gaped like a broken jaw. Zephyrine wedged themselves through the gap where the hull had folded, feet first, and felt the silt swallow them to the waist. Their fingers walked along the rotten decking, counting planks, counting the paces Delacroix had described in that last fevered hour before the fever took him properly — *third rib from the mast step, under the false floor, iron box, green with rot, you'll know it by the lock, brass, shaped like a scallop shell, my father's joke, God rest the vain old fool.*

The mast step was still there, a stub of blackened timber like a broken tooth. Zephyrine counted ribs. One. Two. Three. Their fingers found the seam of a false floor — a plank that sounded hollow when rapped, and did, and Zephyrine's heart did something acrobatic that was very bad for breath conservation.

They dug. The silt came away in cold clouds. And there it was: a corner of iron, furred with rust, and set into its face a brass plate green as pondwater, worked into the shape of a scallop shell.

*You'll know it by the lock.* Delacroix, you ridiculous dead man. You vain, brilliant, frightened dead man.

The box weighed as much as a sleeping child. Zephyrine hugged it to their chest, kicked for the light, and tried not to think about the fact that the rope around their waist was also, technically, the rope around the evidence that would get them hanged if anyone on this island saw them carrying it.

* *

Odile had a fire going in her kitchen when Zephyrine came dripping through the back door, and the smell of her fish stew could probably have ended the war on its own, if either side had been sensible enough to surrender to it.

"Put it in the bread oven," she said, without turning around. "And wring yourself out first. You're dripping on my floor like a guilty thing."

"I *am* a guilty thing."

"Guilty things drip more politely. Here." She pressed a towel into their hands and a bowl of stew into them a moment later, in the efficient way she had of making charity feel like a chore. Zephyrine ate standing up, shivering, while Odile knelt at the bread oven and drew out the strongbox, turning it in the firelight.

The lock was fused. Two years of salt water had welded it shut as thoroughly as any crown seal.

"I could smash it," Zephyrine said.

"You could smash it and bend everything inside. Iron box, paper inside — you know what salt water does to paper, even boxed paper?"

"Turns it into soup."

"Turns it into *memory*," Odile corrected. "Which is worse. No. We dry it slow, and we open it patient. My mother had a trick for rusted locks — vinegar and time. We have vinegar." She paused. "Time we have less of than we'd like."

Zephyrine set down the bowl. Outside, somewhere across the dark water, the militia's evening drum went round the island — two beats, pause, two beats. The sound they'd started making after the murder, the sound that meant *curfew, stay in your holes, we are watching.* It had gotten so Zephyrine's heart flinched at it the way it once flinched at thunder.

"Odile. Can I say something cowardly?"

"You usually do. It's part of your charm."

"When we open this box — if Delacroix was right, if those ledgers say what he thought they said — then there's no going back. Not for me. Not for you. Not for the boy in your cellar." They meant Tomas Voss, Captain Ilsa Voss's son, who had spent eleven days now in the damp dark under Odile's floorboards, healing from a militia bullet and complaining about the soup. "The company doesn't forgive. The company doesn't even *forget well*. It keeps accounts."

Odile was quiet for a moment. She turned the strongbox over in her lap, and the firelight caught the little brass scallop shell, and something in her face went soft and far away.

"My husband kept accounts too," she said. "Not wicked ones. Just the ordinary kind — who owed him a day's labor, who owed him a chicken. When he died I found his book, and do you know what the last entry was?" She smiled, and it wobbled slightly at the edges. "*Odile — forgiven, everything.* He'd written it the week before. He never told me. He just wanted it *written down* somewhere, in his own hand, so it would count."

"That's the most romantic thing I've ever heard and I'm going to pretend it wasn't, because I'll cry, and crying is bad for my diving."

"Your father cried at everything. Weddings, funerals, good weather."

"My father was *underwater* most of the time. Nobody could prove it."

Odile laughed — a real one, brief and bright — and then her face settled into the gravity that had been living in it for two weeks now. "Zephyrine. Listen to me. Whatever is in this box, you were going to be in this fight regardless. You promised a drowning woman's son your help back when the sun was shining and it cost you nothing. That's the only kind of promise worth anything, and you made it. The box doesn't change that. The box only tells you *who else* has been drowning you all along."

Zephyrine looked at the strongbox. The scallop shell gleamed, smug as a saint.

"Vinegar and time," they said.

"Vinegar and time."

* * *

Vinegar and time, it turned out, meant three days of Odile dabbing the lock like a nurse tending a wound, while Zephyrine went about the business of being an innocent indentured pearl diver, which was the finest acting performance of their life and had no audience, which was the worst of it.

The ration line on the second morning was where Brant found them.

He didn't grab their arm. Brant never grabbed; he simply appeared beside you, large and unhurried as weather, and suddenly you were talking to him whether you'd agreed to or not. He was the militia sergeant now — the *company's* militia, though half its men were island-born and hated the company the way you hate a tooth that pays for your house.

"Rations are short again," Brant said, conversationally, as the clerk ahead of them counted out rice with the enthusiasm of a man burying treasure.

"The sea's short. The sea's been short all season."

"The sea's been *short*," Brant agreed, "ever since the *Cormorant* burned. Funny thing, that. Ship full of company records, catches fire at anchor, slips her lines, sinks in the one spot on the whole coast where nothing can be salvaged. And now the factor's dead, and the factor's replacement keeps asking me about that ship." He looked at Zephyrine then, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had grown up hauling nets and had learned exactly what a net feels like from the inside. "Funny thing, Zeph."

"Is it?" Zephyrine's voice came out steady. Somewhere inside, a small person was screaming and running in circles. "I wouldn't know. I was eleven."

"You were eleven," Brant said. "And now you're not." He took his ration — short, like everyone's — and paused. "There's a man on the militia payroll. Been on it for years. Informs on his neighbors. You'd know him if I said the name, and I'm not going to say the name, because I want to see what you do with your face when you next see him." He shouldered his ration bag. "Be careful with your face, Zeph. It's a good face. It'd be a shame if it told the wrong person the right thing."

He walked off. Zephyrine stood in the ration line with their pulse hammering in their ears and their portion of rice in their hands, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like drowning: *he knows. He doesn't know what, but he knows that something, and he's decided — God help me — he's decided to be on my side of it, or at least on the side of watching me.*

Or he was playing the longest game on the island. With Brant, it was genuinely impossible to say.

* * *

The lock surrendered on the third night, with a crack like a knuckle, and the smell that came out of the strongbox was the smell of two drowned years — salt and iron and old paper gone soft as skin.

Odile had cleared her kitchen table and covered it with cloth. She'd also, Zephyrine noticed, brought up every candle in the house, and had sent Tomas down into the cellar with strict instructions to listen at the floor and thump twice if anyone came to the door. From below, faintly, came the sound of a wounded young man trying to make himself comfortable on a dirt floor. He thumped once, experimentally. Odile ignored it.

The ledgers came out wrapped in oilcloth that Delacroix's father had, apparently, been wise enough to line with wax. The paper inside was swollen and stained, the ink bloomed grey at the edges — but the writing had been done in that iron-gall ink that company clerks used, the ink that outlasts everything except conscience, and page after page of it was legible.

Zephyrine read. Odile read. The candles burned.

It was worse than Delacroix had thought.

The *Cormorant*'s ledgers recorded two years of the Meridian Trading Company's island business, and the island business, it turned out, had three sets of numbers: the numbers reported to the crown, the numbers reported to the company's own head office, and the numbers that were true. The true numbers told a story that Zephyrine read twice because the first time their eyes kept sliding off it, refusing.

The pearl beds. Their father's pearl beds. The company had been recording takes at a fraction of the harvest — and the difference, year over year, boatload over boatload, had gone into the factor's private accounts, and from there into names that made Odile's breath stop when she read them: names of magistrates on the mainland, names of company directors, a name that was, Zephyrine was almost certain, the crown's own harbor commissioner.

"They've been stealing from the crown," Odile whispered, "and from their own company, and from every diver on this island, and the factor was going to sell the proof to the highest bidder, and *that's* why he's dead."

"And the divers," Zephyrine said. Their voice sounded strange to them, flat and far. "Look at the indenture rolls. Look at the death column."

The death column. Divers who'd gone down and not come up — recorded not as losses to be mourned or even investigated, but as *assets recovered*, the company reclaiming the cost of their gear against their families' debts. Zephyrine ran a finger down the names. Half of them they'd known. Grown up with their children. One of them was their father.

There it was, in iron-gall ink: *Joubert, A. — drowned, gear recovered, debt of 14 crowns 6 shillings transferred to estate.*

Fourteen crowns and six shillings. That was what their father's life had weighed, on the company's scale. That was the debt Zephyrine's indenture had been built to pay, and here was the proof that the debt itself was a fiction — that the harvest had been stolen, the books cooked, and a generation of divers sent down into the dark to fill a ledger that was never anything but a lie.

"Zephyrine." Odile's hand was on their arm. Zephyrine hadn't noticed their own breathing change, but Odile had, the way she always did. "Breathe, child. Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like your father dove. Come away from the table a moment."

"I'm *fine*." And then, because it was Odile, and lying to her was like lying to the tide: "Fourteen crowns and six shillings, Odile. He kept my father's gear. He charged my mother for it. And my mother *paid*."

"I know."

"She paid it in *pearls*."

"I know." Odile closed the ledger gently, as if it were something sleeping. "So now you know what Delacroix knew, and why somebody killed him for it. And now, Zeph, you have to decide — tonight, before you sleep — what you're going to do. Because there are two kinds of people who find a thing like this. The kind who use it to save themselves, and the kind who use it to save everyone, and the second kind have a much shorter life expectancy but considerably better funerals."

Zephyrine looked at the ledger. At the little brass scallop shell, lying open on the table like a hand unclenched at last.

Outside, the curfew drum went round: two beats, pause, two beats. And underneath it, fainter, from the direction of the militia post by the bridge, a sound that didn't belong — voices, several, raised, and then a whistle, the long falling whistle the militia used for *alarm*, and then boots, many boots, on the tide road.

Odile was already moving, snuffing candles. "Cellar," she said. "Now. Take the ledgers."

Zephyrine gathered the oilcloth bundle to their chest in the dark, heart slamming. From below came two sharp thumps — Tomas, listening, warning — and then a third thump, and a fourth, fast and frantic, a rhythm that meant something none of them had agreed on and all of them understood.

Someone was at Odile's door. And they weren't knocking the way neighbors knock.
