## 15. Odile's Debt

The cellar stairs were steeper than sin and smelled of salt pork, tallow, and years of other people's secrets. Zephyrine went down them blind, one hand on the oilcloth bundle, the other on Odile's shoulder, which was narrower than they had always imagined it. Above them, the knocking stopped. That was worse than the knocking.

"Under the shelves," Odile breathed. "There's a gap behind the pickle crock. Don't breathe on the jars — Brant counts them."

Zephyrine wedged themselves into the dark between the shelving and the stone, pulling their knees up until they were roughly the size and shape of a frightened barrel. The ledgers dug into their ribs. Through the floorboards overhead came muffled sounds: the scrape of the bar being drawn back, Odile's voice pitched to a pleasant, unhurried drawl that Zephyrine had never once heard her use.

"Why, Sergeant. At this hour?"

"Company business, Madame Feral."

*Brant,* Zephyrine thought, and their stomach folded itself into a shape usually reserved for deep-water diving.

The footsteps crossed the ceiling directly above them. Dust sifted down through the boards and settled, fine as flour, on Zephyrine's knuckles. They counted their own heartbeats because it was that or panic, and they had learned young that a diver who panicked used up air she needed for other things.

"I'm surprised you found my shop in the dark," Odile was saying overhead. "Most men need daylight to navigate my clutter."

"Most men aren't paid to find things." A pause. A chair creaking under a weight it didn't like. "You had a visitor this evening, Madame."

"Half the island visits me. I sell thread. It's not a popular vice, but it's mine."

"A young visitor."

Another pause, longer. In the dark below, Zephyrine pressed the bundle tighter and thought, with absurd clarity, of Captain Voss laughing in the sun three years ago, her son Anton chasing gulls down the tideline, everything still legal, everything still easy.

"My nephew comes Tuesdays," Odile said. "For his shirts."

"It's Thursday."

"Then he'll be mortified when he realizes. He's never been good with days. Or shirts."

Above, someone laughed — not Brant; one of the others, a younger voice. Then Brant's voice again, flat as slate: "Open your back room, please."

"The back room is where I sleep, Sergeant, and where I keep my unmentionables. If your sergeant's dignity requires an inventory of my unmentionables, you may tell the factor—" She stopped herself, neatly, like a seamstress trimming a thread. "You may write to the factor."

Silence. Long enough that Zephyrine's lungs began to ache from breathing in sips.

"The factor," Brant said at last, "is dead. Perhaps you hadn't heard. Things have changed, Madame Feral. I'd get used to it quickly, if I were you."

Chairs scraped. Boots crossed the ceiling and receded. The bar slid home. And then — nothing. A long, ringing nothing, the whole house holding its breath.

Odile's voice came down through the floor, very quiet. "It's all right. Come up slowly. Mind the ninth step, it screams."

* * *

Upstairs, Odile relit a single candle and set it behind the blue glass lamp she kept for late customers, so the light read as commerce rather than confession. Up close, Zephyrine could see that her hands were not entirely steady, though her face was composed as ever — that broad, weathered, unimpressable face that had watched over the island's comings and goings since before Zephyrine could walk.

"You knew him," Zephyrine said. "Brant. By name."

"Everyone knows Brant by name. That's how he likes it." Odile lowered herself into her chair with a small grunt. "Sit down, child, before you fall down. You've gone the color of old candle wax."

Zephyrine sat, clutching the ledgers. "He was looking for me."

"He was looking for *a* young person. Whether it's you depends on what else he knows, and what else he's been told." Odile's eyes went to the bundle in Zephyrine's arms. "And whether those books are what Delacroix's boy thinks they are."

"They're the factor's private ledgers. There are pages missing from the company copies. These fill the gaps." Zephyrine hesitated. "Ration shortfalls. Shell weights falsified. Divers marked dead who shipped out alive on the company's own boats. And payments, Odile. Payments to somebody initials-only, going back six years."

The candle flame leaned in the draft, righted itself. Odile looked at it rather than at Zephyrine.

"Six years," she repeated.

"Odile." Zephyrine leaned forward. "I've known you my whole life. You taught me to read off your order slips. You fed me when the ration boat was late, which was always, which you said was the company's fault and I believed you. If there's something I should know before I carry these books across the island in the dark—"

"There is," Odile said. "And you're not going to like it any more than I like telling it, but you've earned the truth twice over now. Once for carrying those books, and once for asking plainly." She folded her hands on the counter, an old habit, as if preparing to measure out cloth. "Do you remember my husband?"

Zephyrine blinked. "You had a husband?"

"I did. Armand. Died the year you were six, or so everyone on this island believes." Her mouth curved, without much humor in it. "He drowned. Officially. Drowned men are very convenient, in the Meridian's accounting. They don't ask for wages, they don't write letters, and their families collect a burial fee that costs less than replacing a dory."

Zephyrine sat very still. Somewhere outside, the tide was turning against the pilings, that patient grinding sound the island made in its sleep.

"Armand was the factor's clerk before Delacroix," Odile said. "Before the war got close enough to smell. He kept the true books — the ones you're hugging like a life vest, child, mind the mildew — and one autumn he found the same thing you've found. The shell weights. The divers sold south. He wrote to the magistrates on the mainland. Three letters." She held up three fingers, then closed them slowly into a fist. "The third one never arrived. Instead the factor's men came for him at low tide, and Armand went into the water off the north point, and I was told he'd been drinking, and I was paid thirty silver and asked to sign a paper saying I accepted the account."

"And you signed it."

"I signed it." Odile's voice didn't waver, but something behind it did, some long-held structural thing settling under strain. "I had a daughter, Zephyrine. Marguerite. She was nine. Signing was the only thing standing between her and being an indenture's orphan with no claim on anyone. So I signed, and I took the money, and I hated myself efficiently and completely for about four years, and then I started doing sums instead."

"What kind of sums?"

"The useful kind." Odile reached beneath the counter and brought up a key, worn smooth, hanging from a string around her neck that Zephyrine had always taken for a keepsake. "Every crate of thread that's come through this shop in six years has been a little heavier than its manifest claimed. Every shipment a little slower to leave. Every diver the company listed as lost — I've written to their families on the mainland, under a trading name, quietly, over years. Some of them are alive, Zephyrine. Shipped to pearl camps down the coast with new names and no papers, making quotas for men who never paid the crown a sou of duty on them. Armand's ledgers prove it. That's why the factor kept them locked and that's why Brant wants them burned, and that's why you are currently sitting in the most dangerous chair on this island."

Zephyrine looked down at the oilcloth bundle. It weighed no more than a gull's egg and no less than a drowning.

"Why didn't you tell me? When Tomas brought me here — why not say all this?"

"Because knowing things gets people killed, and I wanted one more hour of you being only a scared kid running an errand." Odile smiled then, really smiled, tired around the edges. "And because I owed you. Not just your mother — you. Who do you think sent the anonymous coin every midwinter, the year your ration chit 'failed'? Who do you think told Captain Voss, three years back, which cove was safe for a shore leave she wasn't permitted? Armand's debt doesn't die with him. I've been paying it in installments my whole widowhood, and tonight it seems the balance has finally come due."

* * *

They worked after that, because grief and fear are both improved by occupation. Odile cleared the bottom of her thread chest and lined it with oilskin while Zephyrine wrapped the ledgers in the last of the spare sailcloth. They spoke in the low, quick shorthand of two people who have suddenly become conspirators and are surprised to find the fit natural.

"Tomas can't move tonight," Odile said. "Brant's patrols will walk the tide road till dawn, looking spooked and important. But the mangrove channel at the west end — the one your friend the captain's boy is hidden in — the militia won't row it in the dark. Too many roots, too many snags. They're soldiers, not fishermen."

"So we go at first light. Before the fog lifts."

"Before the fog lifts," Odile agreed. "You take the chest on the cart as far as the salt sheds, like you're hauling supplies for me, which no one will question because you've done it a hundred times. Then the mangroves. Then the boat."

"And then?" Zephyrine tied the last knot and looked up. "Anton's wounded, Odile. Badly. And even if we clear the channel, even if we reach the rendezvous — the *Petrel* can't come inside the bar while the company's watch battery sits on the headland. Captain Voss said she'd signal. But she also said—" They faltered. "She said if the battery fired on her, she couldn't stop to pick us up. Orders."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she rose, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and took down the blue glass lamp.

"Your Captain Voss," she said, "made me a promise once too. The night before she sailed south, she sat in that same chair you're sitting in and drank my terrible coffee and told me that if anything ever happened to her, I should look after her boy the way I'd want someone to look after mine." She set the lamp down gently. "I failed her son once already, in a manner of speaking. I let the world that killed Armand grow up around Anton without lifting a finger, because lifting fingers is how widows drown. So tomorrow I'm done signing papers and paying installments."

"Odile, you can't come with us—"

"I'm not coming with you." She turned the lamp so its blue light fell across the map she'd scratched years ago into the underside of the counter lid — channels, depths, a little x at the north point that Zephyrine had never dared ask about. "I'm doing something better. Tomorrow morning, when Brant parades his men on the quay, I am going to walk into the company office and present a bill for six years of short-weighted thread, in front of witnesses, loud as church bells. A stubborn old shopkeeper disputing an invoice is the most boring thing in creation. Every eye on this island will be on the spectacle — including the eyes you need elsewhere."

Zephyrine stared at her. "That's insane."

"That's *thread*, child. Never underestimate thread. It holds things together, and it unravels." Odile blew out the candle, leaving only the blue glow, and for a moment her face in it looked younger and harder and very much like a woman who had once loved a clerk with dangerous handwriting. "Get some sleep in the back room. First light, the cart, the mangroves, the boat. And Zephyrine—"

"Yes?"

"When you see Ilsa Voss again, tell her the debt is paid." A pause. "Tell her it was never hers."

Zephyrine lay awake most of the night anyway, listening to the tide and the distant boots of the patrol, one hand resting on the chest of books like a hand on a sleeping thing.

Which is why they heard the whistle — three notes, low, repeated — come thin across the water from the direction of the mangroves, well before dawn.

Tomas's rhythm. But faster. And meant for them.
