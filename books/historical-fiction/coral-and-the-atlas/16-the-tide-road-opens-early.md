## 16. The Tide Road Opens Early

The tide had always been the island's honestest citizen. It came when the almanac said it would, left when it said it would, and never once, in all of Zephyrine's nineteen years, had it told a lie or borrowed a boat and failed to return it. The divers used to say you could set your whole soul by the tide, if you couldn't afford a clock.

Which was why, when Odile shook Zephyrine awake in the grey hour before dawn with the words *"The road is open, child, and it should not be,"* Zephyrine felt the words land somewhere beneath the ribs, in that cold drawer where the body keeps its worst expectations.

"What do you mean, open?" Zephyrine whispered, already pulling on boots. One never woke this quietly in a house with a wounded man upstairs unless one intended to keep being quiet about it. "The spring tide isn't for three days. You told me so yourself. You made me write it on my hand."

"I did." Odile stood at the window, a small iron-grey woman silhouetted against a sky the color of unpolished silver. She had been a diver for fifty years and her spine had opinions about soft beds and earlier hours both. "And look at my hand." She held it up, palm out, as if presenting evidence to a court. It was steady. It was always steady. That was the terrifying thing about Odile's hands. "I wrote nothing on mine because I don't need to. And yet I can hear the flats from here."

Zephyrine listened. At first there was only the wind worrying at the shutters and the distant complaint of gulls. Then, beneath it, a long low hiss, like the island breathing out through its teeth.

The sound of water withdrawing across the sandbar. The sound of the tide road — the causeway of packed wet sand that surfaced only twice a month at the lowest springs, the secret way off the island that no company boat watched because no company man believed it existed.

Three days early.

"That's not possible," Zephyrine said, lacing the second boot wrong and not caring.

"The sea has stopped asking our permission about things lately," Odile said. "The factor turns up dead, the bridge closes, the moon comes up over the mangroves looking like an unpaid debt. Why should the tide behave?" She turned from the window, and in the dark her eyes were bright and hard as the pearls she'd spent a lifetime prying from oysters. "Get the boy. Get the papers. Meet me at the north slip before the light is good enough to see by. We are leaving now, or we are explaining ourselves to Sergeant Hale's muskets later."

* * *

Upstairs, by the light of a shuttered lantern, Ansel Voss was already awake. Of course he was. He had been shot through the shoulder six weeks ago and had spent every hour since lying in Odile's spare room listening to the house settle, the militia patrol past, and his own heart conduct its stubborn, inconvenient business. Sleeping, he once told Zephyrine, had become a negotiation he kept losing.

He was sitting up with his back against the wall, gray-faced but combed, wearing Odile's late husband's coat, which hung off him like sailcloth off a spar. His mother's letter was folded in his breast pocket. He touched it the way other men touched talismans.

"It's time," Zephyrine said.

"I heard the tide." Ansel swung his legs off the cot, and the effort cost him something — Zephyrine saw him pay it, silently, the way his mother must have taught him, the way sailors pay for everything: without complaint and in advance. "Is it supposed to be open?"

"No."

"Ah." He looked at the ceiling for a moment. "In the navy we had a word for situations like this."

"We're not in the navy."

"We had a word anyway." He reached for his crutch, thought better of it, and took Zephyrine's offered arm instead. "It was several words, actually, most of them unprintable. But the gist was *go now and apologize to God later.*"

Zephyrine laughed despite everything — despite the fear sitting in their chest like swallowed seawater, despite the ledger hidden in the flour barrel downstairs with Delacroix's true accounts copied out in Zephyrine's own careful diver's hand, despite Brant's offer still hanging over everything like weather. Laughing felt like defiance. It was the only kind they had in stock.

They went down the stairs together, slowly, one step and then a rest, while Ansel narrated his own progress in a low, dry voice ("Step four. Historians will call this era my stair period") until Odile hissed at them both from the doorway and the humor folded itself away like a chart no longer needed.

At the door, Zephyrine paused. The flour barrel stood in the corner of the kitchen, ordinary as bread. Inside it lay the drowned ledger's twin — proof of what the Meridian Company had done: the smuggled arms, the false weights, the debt-slavery written down in sums so neat they looked almost innocent. Three days, Zephyrine had promised themselves. Three days until the spring tide, three days to decide whether to take the ledger or leave it, whether to buy Ansel's passage with silence or spend everyone's safety on the truth.

Now the sea had voted. Now there was no three days.

Zephyrine lifted the ledger out, brushed off the flour, and wrapped it in oilcloth.

Odile watched from the doorway and said nothing, which from Odile was a paragraph.

"You think I'm a fool," Zephyrine said.

"I think," Odile said, "that your mother once dove the deep banks alone for a pearl she didn't need, because it was owed to someone dead, and I said the same thing to her, and she did it anyway, and here you are." She adjusted the bundle of food and dry clothes under her arm. "Fools run in that family like tide in a channel. Take the ledger. Tell the truth badly if you must. But come back alive, because I am too old to raise another one of you and mean it this time."

* * *

The north slip was a broken-toothed jetty the company had abandoned years ago as useless, which meant it was the only jetty nobody watched. Beyond it, the world had rearranged itself. Where last night there had been black water and the moaning of channel currents, this morning lay a vast pale plain — the flats, exposed and steaming faintly in the first light, stretching nearly a mile to the far shore where the mangroves began and, beyond them, the fishing village of Petit-Sable where a boat waited.

Or where a boat had waited. That was the problem with miracles. They came with fine print.

Odile was already there, talking in a fierce undertone to old Baptiste, who owned the flattest-bottomed skiff on the island and the most pessimistic mouth. He was gesturing at the sky.

"Three days early," he was saying as they arrived. "In sixty years I have seen the tide run early twice, and both times there was weather coming that the almanac hadn't heard about. The sea empties itself before a storm like a thief emptying a house. You want to cross a mile of mud with a wounded man and a stolen ledger while the sky is deciding whether to drown us all?"

"Yes," said Zephyrine.

Baptiste looked at Ansel. Ansel looked back with the particular polite steadiness of a man who had been shot once and found it educational.

"He'll do," Baptiste grunted, which Zephyrine understood was the warmest thing he had ever said about anyone.

They started across as the light came up gold and thin. The tide road was strange going — firm sand ribbed like a shell, pools of trapped sea flashing mirrors, tiny crabs boiling away from their footsteps in nervous silver crowds. Ansel managed the first quarter mile with gritted teeth and Zephyrine's shoulder, then the wound won an argument, and they rigged the skiff's oar as a litter-pole between two walkers and half-carried, half-dragged him, all of them sinking to the ankle in the wetter stretches.

"So," Ansel said, between breaths, because apparently nearly fainting did not qualify as a reason to stop talking, "the ledger. My mother will want to know what's in it. When we reach her ship."

"Arms," Zephyrine said. "The company sold arms to both sides of the coastal war for three years. Delacroix recorded it in cipher and reported honest cargo. There are men in the capital whose signatures are in the margins. That's why he had to die — not because he knew too much, but because he'd begun writing it down properly, and proper writing gets read."

"And you intend to be read."

"I intend," Zephyrine said, "to trade my indenture for testimony. That was the bargain Brant offered me — my freedom for my silence. So I'm going to offer somebody better the same price for the opposite."

Ahead, Odile snorted. "You hear this child? Negotiating with the whole world before breakfast."

"It runs in the family," Ansel said mildly.

They were two-thirds across, close enough to see the mangroves individually, to smell the land — and then Baptiste, walking point, stopped so abruptly that Zephyrine walked into him.

He pointed back the way they had come.

On the far shore, tiny with distance but unmistakable, figures were spilling out of the company warehouse district onto the flats. Lanterns still burning against the dawn. A dozen men, maybe more, spreading out along the waterline. And above them, on the headland, a signal flag climbing a pole in jerks.

"They've found the slip," Odile said flatly. "Someone talked."

Nobody said the name. Everybody thought it.

"They can't cross," Baptiste said, but his voice had gone up half an octave. "Look at them. They don't know the road. See — that one's already in the soft ground to his knees. They'll lose their boots, maybe a man or two to the channels, but—" He squinted. "There's a rider on the headland. Someone who knows to follow the shell-ribbed sand. Someone local."

The wind chose that moment to arrive — not arriving gradually as wind preferred to do, but all at once, a long shove from the southeast that flattened the pool-mirrors and sent the crabs diving and made the mangroves ahead hiss like a crowd before bad news.

Baptiste looked at the sky. Then he did something Zephyrine had never seen him do. He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile.

"Well," he said. "There's your reason the tide ran early. Storm's coming down the coast like a bill collector. We have perhaps an hour. Maybe less." He turned. "And those fools behind us have chosen to spend theirs walking into it on open mud."

Ansel, white-lipped, gripped Zephyrine's arm. "Then we go now and they drown or don't, yes?"

But Zephyrine was looking back at the figures on the flats — at the little group floundering in the soft ground, at the rider picking his way surely along the ridged sand, closing the distance with the unhurried confidence of a man following a map he'd known all his life — and feeling something worse than fear. Feeling recognition.

Because Zephyrine knew how that rider sat a horse. Knew the set of those shoulders. Had spent entire childhood summers trailing after them, learning where the oysters hid.

"Mireille," Zephyrine said quietly.

The informer. The friend. The person who had shared every dive, every meal, every secret since they were both small enough to fit inside the same washtub — and who, sometime in these six sealed weeks, had decided that survival bought with silence was worth whatever it cost everyone else.

The wind shoved again, harder, and out over the sea the horizon had begun, very softly, to darken.

"She's not militia," Zephyrine said. "She's alone out front. Which means she wants to reach us before they do." Zephyrine shifted the oilcloth bundle higher under one arm and turned to face the mile of narrowing sand between them and everything that was coming. "Keep moving. All of you. If it's a fight she wants, she'll have to catch me first — and I have been holding my breath longer than anyone on this island."

Behind them the signal flag snapped in the rising wind. Ahead the mangroves bent like bowing courtiers. And between, on the gleaming tide road, a single rider came on at a walk, patient as the sea.
