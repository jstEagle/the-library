## 17. Everything Owed

The morning after Zephyrine gave their testimony, the whole island seemed to hold its breath, and then to let it out all at once, badly, like an old man climbing stairs.

Word traveled the way everything traveled on Meridian Cay: sideways, through kitchen windows and laundry lines and the particular language of women slapping wet cloth against stone. By the time the sun cleared the mangroves, every soul on the island knew three things. First, that Zephyrine Joubert had stood in the factor's own parlor and read aloud from a ledger that was supposed to be at the bottom of the lagoon. Second, that the ledger proved the Meridian Trading Company had been running guns under pearl weights for six years. Third, that Zephyrine had traded their indenture for the right to stand there and say so.

What they did not know — what only four people on the island knew — was that Voss's son was still alive, still feverish, still hidden in the salt-house loft with a knife wound that had begun, at last, to look less like an accusation and more like an ordinary wound.

Zephyrine sat on the steps of Odile's house with a bowl of congee they had no appetite for, watching the tide road.

It was open. That was the miracle and the terror of it. The bridge of sand and coral that connected the Cay to the mainland should have stayed drowned another nine days, but the storm season had come early and strange this year, pushing water around like a child rearranging furniture, and the road had surfaced three mornings ago — bare, glistening, walkable. Which meant anyone could leave. Which meant anyone could arrive. Which meant Brant's cutter, anchored sullen in the deep channel, could send men ashore whenever it pleased.

"You're not eating," Odile said from the doorway. She was seventy-three years old and had hands like driftwood, and she had raised Zephyrine on fish guts and proverbs and never once told them a comforting lie.

"I'm eating."

"You're performing eating. There's a difference. Your mother used to do the same thing when she was frightened." Odile came down the steps with the slow ceremony of a woman who had decided long ago that rushing was for other people. She settled beside Zephyrine and looked at the tide road too. "Brant sent a boy around at dawn."

Zephyrine's stomach tightened. "What did he want?"

"To remind us that testimony given under duress is worth very little in a court. To remind us that the company has lawyers who will arrive on the next packet boat. And to ask, very politely, whether we had seen any strangers on the island." She smiled without warmth. "I told him the only strangers I'd seen were his soldiers, and that I'd be happy to see them go."

"Odile—"

"Hush. I'm seventy-three. I've buried two husbands, one hurricane, and every debt I ever owed except one." She turned and looked at Zephyrine properly then, and her eyes were the same gray as the morning water. "You know which one I mean."

"The boat," Zephyrine said quietly.

"The boat. The boat I owe to Ilsa Voss, may God keep her wherever the crown has chased her to. The boat I swore I'd have ready if her boy ever needed to leave this island." Odile picked a bit of loose fiber off her sleeve. "You went behind my back, child."

"I—"

"Don't apologize. It was well done, going behind my back. If I'd known, my face would have known, and my face is famous all over this island." A flicker of pride crossed the old woman's expression, quick as a fish. "But you should know that everything you did — the ledger, the dive, the testimony, all of it — you did because you were paying my debt for me. And no Joubert has ever let another Joubert pay alone. So now we are even, you and I, and we finish this together."

Zephyrine looked down at the congee, at their own reflection trembling in it, and found their throat had gone tight. They had spent seventeen years being owned — paper ownership, ink ownership, the kind of ownership that calls itself employment — and in all that time they had never once been *owed* anything by anyone. Being owed to was a strange sensation. It made them feel taller than they were.

"There's something else," Zephyrine said. "The ledger. When I testified, I only read the gun pages."

Odile waited. She was good at waiting. She'd had practice.

"There are other pages. Debts. Real ones — money the company owes the divers. Bonus payments they recorded and never paid. Wages withheld. My father's name is in it, Odile. He died owed eleven pounds, four shillings, and they wrote it down like it was a fact about the weather."

The old woman was silent a long moment. Then she said, softly, "Everything owed," as if tasting the phrase, and shook her head. "That's a dangerous book, child. More dangerous than the guns. Guns only kill men. Ledgers can make whole islands remember what they're angry about."

"Isn't that what we want?"

"That depends entirely on how much anger an island can carry before it capsizes." Odile rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Come. Bring the bowl. You'll think better with your hands busy."

* * *

They worked through the morning in the salt-house, ostensibly repairing nets, actually holding a war council conducted entirely in the grammar of fishing. Mireille came first, the factor's former housekeeper, a small round woman whose grief for the murdered Delacroix had curdled, over the past weeks, into something harder and stranger — into the discovery that the man she had served for twenty years had kept two sets of books and two sets of sins, and she had dusted around both without seeing either.

"The packet boat comes Thursday," Mireille said, knotting a net with furious precision. "Company lawyers. Company investigators. Possibly crown men, if Brant has written ahead, and Brant writes ahead about everything, including his breakfast."

"So we have two days," Zephyrine said.

"Two days in which the boy cannot walk to the boat on his own legs, the boat itself needs its seams sealed, and half the island wants to march on the company warehouse with pitchforks because someone — and I won't speculate who — has been reading certain pages aloud at the well."

"I didn't read them aloud."

"You showed them to Baptiste, and Baptiste has the discretion of a church bell." Mireille sighed. "Eleven men came to me this morning asking whether it was true the company owes them wages from '41. What do I tell them?"

"The truth."

"The truth is that they're owed money they will probably never see, by a company that will call the book a forgery, and that the last time this island remembered what it was angry about, the militia burned three boats and called it an accident." Mireille's hands stopped. For a moment she looked very tired and very old and very much like the conscience of the whole cay. "But yes. The truth. Tell them the truth. My mother always said a lie is just a debt you take out against yourself."

From the loft above came a soft thump, then another — the agreed signal, one knock for *fine*, two for *need*, three for *danger*. Two knocks. Zephyrine climbed the ladder with a cup of water and found Emil Voss sitting up against the wall, color back in his face, hair plastered to his forehead, looking — and this was somehow the most alarming development yet — bored.

"If I lie here one more day," he announced, "I will begin talking to the rats, and I regret to inform you the rats started it."

"You shouldn't be sitting up."

"I shouldn't be alive, according to a surgeon I never met, and yet here I am, exceeding expectations daily." He took the water and drank it all. He had his mother's way of drinking — direct, unhesitating, as though the world might change its mind about offering. "Someone was arguing outside earlier. Loud voices."

"Baptiste being loud about wages."

"Wages?"

"The company owed the divers money. Years of it. It's in the ledger."

Emil lowered the cup. Something moved across his face — recognition, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a young man realizing that his narrow escape had become everyone else's wide revolution. "My mother used to say," he offered, "that the sea keeps honest accounts even when men don't. That's why she liked sailing better than trading." He paused. "She asked about you, you know. In her letters. Before the letters stopped being wise to send."

"What did she say?"

"She said you were the only person on that whole island who dove like you weren't trying to get it over with." He smiled, small and crooked. "High praise. From her it was practically poetry."

Zephyrine felt their ears go warm and descended the ladder before their face could file a report.

* * *

In the afternoon, Brant himself came to the salt-house.

He came alone, which was its own kind of message — the commander of forty militiamen walking unarmed into a shed full of nets, hat in hand like a man calling on a neighbor. He was younger than his rank suggested, with the careful pleasantness of someone who had learned early that charm was cheaper than force and almost as effective.

"Master Joubert." He glanced around the salt-house with frank curiosity. "Cozy."

"It's a salt-house."

"It is. Though I hear you've been keeping more than salt in it." His eyes drifted upward, toward the loft, with the delicacy of a man pretending he wasn't looking where he was looking. "I'll come to the point, since I know how diver folk prize plainness. Your testimony yesterday has caused difficulties. Not for you — for the company, certainly, but also for me, professionally. There will be an inquiry. Men will come from the mainland with titles and briefcases and a great deal of expensive paper. And when they do, they will ask me a question I would very much like to answer correctly."

"Which question?"

"*Where is the son of Ilsa Voss.*" He said it lightly, conversationally, the way one mentions rain. "Because here is my difficulty, Master Joubert. If I find him, I am required to detain him as an enemy asset, and the procedure for enemy assets is not gentle, and I have read enough of the ledger you produced to suspect that this island's account with my employer is already overdrawn in ways I was never told about. But if I *fail* to find him — if, through some tragic inefficiency, he slipped away before I could act — well. Then I simply never found him, and the inquiry closes, and everyone gets to go home." He put his hat back on. "I can be inefficient, Master Joubert. I have been inefficient my entire career; it is how I survived it. But I cannot be inefficient on behalf of someone I don't trust. Do you understand the difference?"

Zephyrine understood. It was Brant's Bargain again, the same shape it had always worn: help offered with a hook in it. "And what do you want for your inefficiency?"

"The rest of the ledger. The pages you didn't read. I'm told they concern debts." His pleasantness thinned, just slightly, showing something underneath that might once have been an actual principle. "There are men on that mainland payroll who have been skimming from dead divers' accounts for a decade, and if the inquiry comes, they will burn this island to cover themselves. I'd rather have the evidence first. Call it insurance. Call it spite. Call it whatever you like, so long as you call it soon."

He left the way he'd come, unhurried, nodding to Odile on the path with genuine courtesy, and Zephyrine stood among the nets feeling as though they'd shaken hands with a rope — useful, and liable to tighten.

* * *

Evening came gold and low. Zephyrine walked out to the boat.

Odile's boat was a small sloop called *Mercy*, which Odile claimed was named for her second husband's ship and which everyone knew was named for her first husband's dog. She lay in the mangroves where the channel narrowed, half-hidden, her hull freshly tarred, her little sail folded like a sleeping bird. Zephyrine checked the seams again anyway, ran a palm along the wood, whispered the old diver's blessing to her keel — *hold, hold, hold* — because it couldn't hurt.

Emil came limping down the mangrove root-path as the light failed, moving better than he had any right to, with Odile behind him carrying bread and smoked fish wrapped in oilcloth.

"She'll hold?" he asked, looking at the boat.

"She'll hold."

"And the tide road — it stays open?"

"Until the water turns. Three days, maybe four. After that, nothing crosses until spring." Zephyrine hesitated. "There's something you should know about tomorrow. Brant came."

Emil listened without interrupting while Zephyrine laid it out — the offer, the price, the thin blade of principle hidden inside the commander's pleasantness. When it was done, Emil was quiet for a long moment, watching the water slide black and silver between the roots.

"My mother trusted you," he said finally. "Out of everyone she met on this island in peacetime, she trusted exactly one person, and she wrote to me saying so in a letter I still have memorized. So here's what I think. I think you give Brant the ledger pages. All of them. Because a man like that doesn't switch sides — but he does switch ships, and right now the company's ship is taking on water, and he knows it." He smiled, and for a heartbeat he looked so much like his mother that Zephyrine's chest ached. "And I think tomorrow night, when the tide is right, you put me in that boat and point me at the mainland and don't look back until I'm gone. Everything owed, Zephyrine. That's what my mother called promises like ours. Not favors. Debts. And the sea keeps honest accounts."

Behind him, Odile snorted softly in the dusk. "*Everything* owed," she repeated, and there was something in her voice Zephyrine had never heard before — not the proverb voice, not the scolding voice, but something almost like awe. "Child. Come here. Look at the channel."

Zephyrine looked. The tide was turning early — hours early — and the water in the channel was doing something wrong, pulling backward against itself, the way it did only before the great storms, the way old divers called *the sea settling its books*.

"The road's going to close faster than we thought," Odile said quietly. "One night, maybe two. Not three." She fixed Zephyrine with a look that had steered generations safely through worse nights than this. "So it's tomorrow. Tomorrow night, everything gets paid at once — the boy, the ledger, the whole bloody account. And you'll need to decide, before then, what *you* are still owed, Zephyrine Joubert, because I promise you the sea will ask."

Far out in the deep channel, past the mangroves, a lantern blinked twice from the anchored cutter — and then, after a moment, a third time.

Three blinks.

*Need.*

Or perhaps: *ready*. Or perhaps — and Zephyrine's blood went cold as deep water — *I know*.
