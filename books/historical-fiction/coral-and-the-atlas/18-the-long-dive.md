## 18. The Long Dive

The tide turned at four bells, and Zephyrine felt it before anyone told them — a loosening in the chest, the way the water under the pier stopped pushing and began, almost shyly, to pull.

"You don't have to do this," Odile said for the ninth time. She sat on the edge of the drying racks with her arms crossed, which was how she always sat when she had already decided someone was going to do a foolish thing and wanted credit for having objected. "The ledger is at the bottom of a channel. The bottom of *a channel*, Zephyrine. It has been down there since before the bridge closed. It is happy there. Leave it be."

"It's not happy anywhere," Zephyrine said, checking the knots on their weight belt. "It's a ledger."

"That is exactly my point."

They were alone in the boatyard, or as alone as the island ever allowed — the mangroves hid them from the harbor, but sound carried strangely over water, and every so often a militiaman's boot would scuff along the far seawall like a cough in church. Above them, gray clouds had begun assembling themselves into something with opinions. Storm season was early this year, or late, depending on whether you asked the fishermen or the almanac, and both were shouting about it in the tavern from dawn until the militia shut the tavern.

Emil Voss lay in the skiff under an oilcloth, his bandaged shoulder rising and falling with the careful breathing of a man who had learned that groaning costs extra. He had insisted on coming.

"You didn't have to come," Zephyrine said.

"And miss this?" Emil's voice was thin but intact. "My mother smuggled cannon through three blockades. If I lie in a boat while a pearl diver's child does all the work, she'll know. Mothers know. It's their one supernatural power."

"Her other power is throwing things," Odile said.

"She comes by it honestly."

Zephyrine laughed despite themselves, and the laugh did what laughs do — it made the thing they were about to do briefly smaller. They had spent the night piecing together what Brant's bargain meant, what the drowned ledger might prove, and the arithmetic of it kept waking them: testimony against the company in exchange for their indenture, but only if the proof existed, and the proof was currently being read by worms at forty feet, if it existed at all, if Delacroix had truly weighted it and dropped it rather than burned it. A great many ifs, arranged in a row like stepping-stones across a very deep place.

Delacroix himself had sworn it was true. That was the strangest part — the old informer, caught between his fear of the company and his fear of what the company would do to him once there was nothing left to sell, had wept onto the table in the sail loft and said, *I put a rock on it. God forgive me, I put a rock on it like it was a cat.* Nobody had known quite what to say to that.

"He sank it in the channel because he couldn't reach deep water," Zephyrine said now, mostly to hear the plan aloud one last time. "Past the third marker buoy, where the current runs. Two hundred paces, maybe less. The paper will be mush by now unless—" 

"Unless," Odile agreed darkly. Unless was doing a lot of work in this plan and everyone knew it.

"Unless the company's strongbox leather held. Factor Perrin shipped documents in those sealed skins. I've recovered two of them off wrecks. Wax-stoppered, tarred at the seam." Zephyrine pulled their cap on. "If Delacroix stole it already sealed, it might have lasted the month."

"*Might,*" said Odile, with relish.

"Might is my favorite word. It means not yet no."

They waded out to the end of the slip, where the channel water went from tea-colored to sudden green-black, cold even in summer, colder now with the storm stacking up over the outer shoals. Zephyrine stood waist-deep a moment, letting the sea take their weight, and did what their mother had taught them before she taught them letters: they listened. The tide road was closed — the sandbar that connected the island to the mainland lay under six feet of gray water, restless, muttering. But beneath the muttering was the older voice, the patient one, and it said what it always said: *come down, come down, I keep everything.*

Their mother would have made them wait for slack water. Their mother was three years drowned herself, and Zephyrine had decided, in the fierce private way of children who refuse funerals, that she could be consulted anyway.

"Slack won't come till after dark," they said aloud. "And after dark there are patrols."

"So go now, against the current, like a mule," Odile said. "Wonderful. I'll tell your eulogy. I've been practicing. It's mostly complaints."

"Make sure you mention I was right."

"I'll mention you were *wet.*"

* * *

The first twenty feet were easy, almost kind. Zephyrine slid down the channel wall hand over hand along the old pilings, feeling the water close over them like a door, the world above going from noise to rumor to nothing. Down here the storm didn't exist. The murder didn't exist. There was only the green half-light, the cold pressing in like an enormous attentive hand, and their own breath, loud in their ears, the truest clock they owned.

At thirty feet the light turned the color of old bottle glass. At forty, the bottom came up to meet them: silt, shell hash, the ribs of some scuttled dinghy wearing a coat of algae like an old soldier gone to seed. Zephyrine knelt in it, boots sinking an inch, and let their eyes adjust, moving their head slowly, the way you read a dark room.

Nothing. Silt, shells, silt, a very indignant crab, more silt.

*Two hundred paces past the third buoy,* Delacroix had said, and then corrected himself twice, because Delacroix's memory of anything he'd done wrong was conveniently fogged. *Or perhaps a hundred and fifty. Or near the buoy itself. I was frightened. It was dark.*

"You are a terrible man," Zephyrine said into their own mask, the words coming out as a stream of bubbles, "and when I get up there I am going to say so at length."

They swam. Against the current it was like swimming through wet wool with opinions. The channel floor shelved down, forty feet becoming forty-five, then fifty, the pressure settling onto their chest like a sleeping cat grown enormous. Twice they found things that weren't the ledger: a company crate stamped MERIDIAN, empty, its contents long since stolen or drowned; and a child's shoe, small and whole and impossible, sitting upright in the silt as though waiting. Zephyrine looked at the shoe longer than they meant to. The sea keeps everything, their mother used to say, but she'd never finished the thought, and Zephyrine had never dared ask what the sea thought it was keeping it all *for.*

On the third pass along the deep bend, they saw the rock.

It wasn't subtle. Delacroix had been frightened and it showed: a stone the size of a bread oven, wrapped in net line, sitting in a shallow crater it had dug for itself on impact. And lashed beneath it, tar-seamed, wax-stoppered, furred with a month of soft green growth —

The strongbox.

Zephyrine's heart did something complicated. Their lungs reminded them, politely but firmly, of the time.

The knot was a company knot — they recognized it from the cargo docks, the double hitch the stevedores used because it tightened under strain and mocked amateurs. But Zephyrine had been untying company knots since before they could write their own name, usually while the company wasn't looking, and their fingers knew the trick the way birds know south. Pull the standing part, ease the bite, worry the elbow loose—

The knife would have been faster. The knife would also have cut the strap holding the box closed, and the box needed to stay closed, absolutely, or a month of dark water would finish in ten seconds what the company hadn't managed yet. So: fingers. Cold, stupid, wonderful fingers, working blind while their chest began to knock on the inside of their ribs like a neighbor complaining about noise.

The knot gave.

Zephyrine hugged the box to their chest — it was heavier than hope, lighter than fear — kicked for the pilings, and hauled themselves up the shadowed ladder of old timber toward the thin silver ceiling of the world, kicking hard enough to make the cold climb feel like flying, which was a lie, but a useful one.

The light got wider. Got warmer. Got—

* * *

They broke the surface gasping behind the mangrove roots, and Odile's hands were under their armpits before they'd finished their first breath, hauling them over the gunwale of the skiff with a strength that had surprised people for sixty years.

"Breathe, you ridiculous eel. Breathe. Emil, count her breaths, it's what your mother would do."

"My mother would throw something," Emil said, "but I don't want to waste her arm strength on you twice in one week."

Zephyrine lay against the thwarts and breathed and let the sky spin pleasantly overhead. The clouds had organized. Somewhere beyond the shoals, thunder cleared its throat and pretended it hadn't.

"Well?" Odile's face appeared above them, upside down, ferociously hopeful. "Well?"

Zephyrine lifted the strongbox just above the level of the boat, dripping, green-furred, sealed.

Odile made a sound Zephyrine had never heard her make before, somewhere between a sob and the noise she made at good news about pastry.

"Don't celebrate yet." Zephyrine sat up, working blood back into their dead-white fingers. "We don't know what's inside. We don't know if the seal held. One crack of salt water in the wrong seam and it's soup."

"Then open it."

"Not here." Zephyrine nodded toward the seawall, where — right on schedule — a patrol had appeared, two militiamen strolling with the elaborate casualness of men pretending not to search for something. "And not with witnesses. If this is what I think it is, the next people who see it opened should be people we've chosen. Brant. The harbormaster. Whoever else we're willing to bet the island on."

"The sail loft," Emil said quietly. "Tonight. My mother can stand watch, such as she is." He grinned without much humor. Ilsa Voss was still limping from the fence at the factor's yard, and had spent the intervening days radiating fury at approximately the temperature of a forge.

"Tonight," Zephyrine agreed.

They got the skiff moving, sail up, running for the sheltered side of the boatyard with the strongbox wrapped in Odile's spare shawl like the world's most dangerous infant. The wind had freshened; the whole island smelled of iron and incoming rain. As they rounded the mangrove point, Zephyrine looked back once at the channel — flat, innocent, keeping its other secrets — and felt the strange lightness that came after long dives, as if the body only gradually remembered it was allowed to be heavy again.

"You did well," Odile said gruffly, not looking at them, which from Odile was nearly an embrace.

"I did well," Zephyrine agreed. "Say it again."

"Don't ruin it."

* * *

But the island, as Zephyrine should have known by now, never let you carry good news unwatched.

They reached the sail loft as the first fat drops came down, and found the door standing open, which it should not have been, and inside, lit by one shielded lamp, sat Captain Ilsa Voss with a pistol across her knees and an expression that stopped Zephyrine in the doorway like a rope across a stair.

"Shut it," she said. "Lock it. Now."

Zephyrine shut it. In the corner, the sail loft's boy — Tibeau, twelve years old, all elbows and loyalty — was pressed back among the canvas rolls, white-faced.

"Madame Voss?"

"There was a man at the window not ten minutes past." Her voice was low and even, the voice of a woman measuring distances in her head. "Watching the loft. Then watching *you* — he watched your skiff come round the point. He saw you carrying something wrapped."

Odile set the shawl-bundled strongbox down very gently, as if it might overhear. "Who? Which man?"

"Big fellow. Company coat. Walks on the outside of his heels." Ilsa's eyes never left the door. "The one they call Quist."

Everyone in the room arrived at the same thought at the same moment, and the silence made it audible.

Quist. Brant's man. The one Brant had promised, hand on heart, knew nothing and reported to no one but him.

"He was too far to see details," Ilsa said. "Maybe he saw cloth. Maybe he saw a bundle. Maybe he saw nothing but a diver coming home and got curious." She paused, and the lamp guttered as the storm leaned against the roof. "And maybe he's already on his way to tell Brant that the evidence you're all betting our lives on just came up out of the channel — and that Brant's bargaining position is about to change."

Zephyrine looked down at the strongbox, sea-dark and sealed, holding whatever it held — absolution or ruin, they wouldn't know for hours yet — and understood with perfect clarity that the dive had been the easy part.

Outside, the rain arrived all at once, like a door slammed, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on the dock. Running.
