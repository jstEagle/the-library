## 19. What the Sea Keeps

Zephyrine came up out of the water with the ledger wrapped against their chest and the whole grey sky roaring in their ears, and for a long moment they did not know which way was up, only that they had chosen correctly, because breathing was happening, and breathing meant up.

The storm had passed in the night but left the lagoon churned to milk, all its familiar colors stirred away, so that surfacing felt like being born into a country made entirely of fog. Zephyrine trod water, hugged the oilcloth bundle tighter, and coughed up half of the Mangrove Cut.

"You're alive," said a voice from above, "or you're a very ambitious piece of driftwood."

Odile crouched at the edge of the skiff with a boathook in one hand and an expression in the other that could have curdled cream at forty paces. She was seventy-one years old, missing two fingers on her left hand to a moray eel and one tooth to a company clerk, and she had been pulling drowning people out of this lagoon since before Zephyrine's mother was a notion.

"I'm alive," Zephyrine rasped.

"Prove it. Say something clever."

"The eel is still down there."

"Which eel?"

"The one I promised you."

Odile snorted, which on her face was the equivalent of weeping with joy, and hauled them aboard by the collar with the effortless cruelty of a woman who had spent five decades hauling nets. Zephyrine collapsed in the bilge, shivering, and pressed the bundle to their chest like a second heart.

"Is it?" Odile asked. Her voice went quiet in the way it did when she spoke of serious matters — tides, debts, funerals.

"It's the ledger." Zephyrine's teeth were chattering too hard for dignity. "Delacroix's real one. Not the pretty copy in his office. The one he kept wet."

They both looked down into the green murk where the factor's strongbox had sat for eleven days among the drowned pilings of the old warehouse, furred already with weed, guarded by a crab with opinions. Eleven days since Delacroix had floated face-down between the bridge pylons with his ledgers burning holes in the company's story. The militia had dragged the shallows for papers. They had never thought to drag the Cut, because everyone knew the Cut was suicide in storm season — riptides, submerged fencing, water cold enough to stop a heart between beats.

Everyone except a pearl diver's child, who had been holding their breath past the point of good sense since the age of six.

"He kept it underwater," Odile said, shaking her head as she shipped the oars. "A man drowns in salt water and he hides his sins in salt water. There's a joke in there the sea wrote first."

* * *

Getting back to the mangroves took an hour of rowing that felt like three of rowing uphill through soup. The storm swell was still working itself out of the lagoon in long lazy shoulders, and twice the skiff shipped enough water that Zephyrine had to bail with their cupped hands because the actual bailing cup had gone overboard in the dive, along with, apparently, the last of Zephyrine's luck.

Ansel was where they had left him: wedged into the hollow of the biggest red mangrove on the seaward fringe, half in the boat and half out of it, one arm bound in a sling made from Zephyrine's spare shirt and his color somewhere between paper and candle wax. He was nineteen, Captain Ilsa Voss's son, with her square jaw and none of her certainty. The musket ball had gone through his shoulder nine days ago in the scuffle at the customs shed — *spy*, said the proclamation nailed to the bridge house, though Zephyrine had seen him that day and he had mostly been trying not to be shot, which hardly seemed a hanging offense anywhere but here.

He opened his eyes when the skiff bumped the roots.

"You found it," he said.

"We found it." Zephyrine climbed in beside him, dripping, and began unwrapping the oilcloth with fingers that refused, on principle, to work properly. "Say nothing else until we've dried it."

The ledger inside had fared better than any paper had a right to, thanks to the factor's tin box and a double wrapping of waxed canvas that suggested Delacroix had known exactly what might happen to him. Still, the edges were soft as bread, the ink blooming brown at every seam. Zephyrine laid the pages open across the thwarts in the dappled light, and Odile stood over them turning each one with a bone folder she produced from somewhere in her endless pockets, and for a while nobody spoke.

It was worse than they'd hoped. That was the strange part. Zephyrine had imagined smuggling, short weights, the quiet starvation of indentured families' ration accounts. All of that was there, page after patient page in the factor's cramped hand. But there were also names. Ships' names, dates, ports. Guns listed as cotton. Cotton listed as guns. And running down the margins, initials — *R.B.* over and over, initialing each entry like a man signing his own confession in installments.

"Brant," Odile said at last. She said it the way you'd spit a fishbone.

"Captain Brant," Zephyrine agreed. "Commander of the garrison. Keeper of the island's peace." They touched the initials gently, as if they might go off. "And the man who found the body. And the man who declared Ansel a spy within four hours of the factor being dead."

"A busy morning for him," Odile said dryly.

"He needed the spy," Zephyrine said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it made it realer than thinking it. "Because Delacroix was going to sail on the next company packet with this ledger under his arm. To testify. Brant couldn't let the ledger go, so he killed the man — or had him killed — and then he needed someone to hang it on before anyone asked why the factor died facedown in the water with his strongbox empty. A foreigner's son. A stranger. Someone whose mother is now an enemy of the crown, so no one will even ask questions."

Ansel had gone very still. "My mother," he said carefully, "is not an enemy of anything. My mother hauls cargo for people who pay her, and last year one of those people lost a war."

"That's all it takes," Odile said. "It's always been all it takes."

* * *

They dried the pages by lamplight that evening in Odile's stilt-house, hung like laundry along a line strung above the stove, and the little room filled with the smell of old ink and brine and fish stew, which was, in Zephyrine's opinion, the exact smell of home.

Ansel slept propped in the corner chair, feverish but breathing evenly, his bandage changed. In sleep he looked twelve years old. Zephyrine found this unbearably annoying, because it was much easier to risk one's life for a cause than for a boy who looked twelve and thanked people too earnestly.

"He asked me today," Zephyrine said quietly, over the stew pot, "why I'm doing this. The whole thing. The promise, the hiding, the dive." They poked at the fire. "I didn't have a good answer. I said I promised his mother. Which is true. But it isn't the whole truth, is it."

Odile ladled stew without looking up. "What's the whole truth?"

"I've spent my whole life on this island being told history happens somewhere else. On ships. Over the horizon. We just hold the pearls and count the dead when the ships come back short-handed." Zephyrine turned the pages nearest the stove, careful of the heat. "Delacroix used to say that. *History is a rumor carried by ships.* He said it laughing. And then he decided to become the rumor himself, and they put him in the water for it." A pause. "I don't want to spend my whole life holding pearls."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "You know what the sea does with things?"

"Buries them."

"Keeps them." She pointed her spoon at the window, toward the black lagoon. "Everything goes in eventually — anchors, promises, men, ledgers, my second-best hat — and the sea keeps them. Doesn't use them, doesn't lose them. Just keeps. And then some fool dives down and brings one thing back up, and suddenly everything the sea kept quiet has to be dealt with." She looked at Zephyrine over the pot, and her eyes were very old and not unkind. "That's you, girl. You're what the sea gave back. So now everybody's got to deal with you."

"Boy," said Ansel, from the corner, awake after all, voice thick with fever. "Or — sorry. I don't actually know how you say it here."

"However you like," said Zephyrine. "The island hasn't settled on a word and neither have I."

"Good," said Ansel solemnly. "Then I'll say *the diver*. You're the diver. You went into water I wouldn't send a dog into and came back with the thing that's going to save my life." His fever-bright eyes were terribly sincere. "I'll call you the diver for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be."

"Don't make jokes about how long it turns out to be," Zephyrine said sharply, and then, because the sharpness surprised them both, added more softly, "eat your stew."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, got argued out over the next two nights, in whispers, between tides.

The tide road — the sandbar causeway that joined the island to the mainland at low water, passable maybe six hours a day in fair season — had been closed since the murder, gated and picketed, the company militia checking every soul who crossed. But the storm season was ending. Chapter sixteen of the island's unwritten almanac was already writing itself: the tides were running lower, cleaner, earlier. Within days the road would stand open for a stretch the pickets couldn't hope to seal, and every smuggler, debt-runner, and lovesick ferryman on the coast knew it.

Ansel couldn't walk the road. He could barely stand. He would have to go by water, out through the mangroves at night, around the point, and meet — someone — beyond the bar. Odile knew someone. Odile knew everyone, which was either a comfort or a terror depending on the hour.

"And the ledger?" Zephyrine asked, on the second night. "Once he's safe. What then? Do we give it to the crown authorities at Port Meridian? Sell it back to the company? Burn it?"

"No," said Odile.

"No to which?"

"All of it." She was mending a net, which she did while thinking, the way other people paced. "You give it to the crown, the crown buries it — half those ports are run by men with the same initials in their margins. You sell it to the company, you've sold your testimony and your soul for company scrip. You burn it, and Ansel dies anyway, because once he's gone they'll need a new story and they'll write yours into it." Her needle paused. "No. Ledgers don't work unless they're read by the right people at the wrong time. What we need is an audience."

"Of whom?"

"Of everyone," said Odile placidly, "at once."

Before Zephyrine could unpack that, a sound stopped them both — the low, carrying note of the garrison horn, blown not once but three times, the signal that meant *all hands, all streets, close your shutters.*

Then boots on the boardwalk below the stilts. Lantern light swinging up through the floor gaps. And a voice Zephyrine knew — flat, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a list — Captain Brant's:

"Odile Marchand. Open up. We're looking for stolen property."

* * *

There was nowhere to hide a ledger in a house the size of Odile's kitchen, and Brant's men searched it like men who intended to find something. Zephyrine lay flat on the roof planks in the rain gutter with their heart attempting to exit through their ears, listening to drawers pulled out and set down hard, listening to Odile answer questions in monosyllables of magnificent contempt, listening — worst of all — to Ansel's slow, careful breathing in the hidden space behind the water barrel, praying the fever didn't make him cough.

They didn't find it, because it wasn't there. It was in a tarred parcel weighted with a stone at the bottom of the rain cistern, which Zephyrine had moved it to an hour before on a pure animal instinct they couldn't explain and didn't argue with.

Afterward, boots receding, Odile climbing up to help them in, both of them sitting on the floorboards shaking slightly and pretending not to.

"He knows," Zephyrine whispered. "Not where it is. But he knows someone has it. That's why the search — he's out of patience."

"Mm." Odile poured them both a finger of rum, medicinal. "Then we're done waiting for a convenient date. Tide road opens early tomorrow. Dawn tide, lowest of the month. Ansel goes out through the Cut tonight, meets Père Toussaint's boat beyond the bar—"

"And the ledger?"

Odile smiled then, and it was not entirely a nice smile.

"The ledger," she said, "goes to the audience. Every soul on this island gathers at the bridge house for the ration distribution at dawn — Brant reads the names himself, likes the theater of it. Tomorrow he'll be reading them to the largest crowd we've had in weeks." She tapped the cistern hatch with her boot. "You get up on that platform, diver, and you tell them what the sea kept."

Zephyrine stared at her. "They'll shoot me."

"They'll shoot you slower if you wait," said Odile, "because he's already decided to. Best do it while everyone's watching."

From the corner, in the dark, came Ansel's voice — steadier than it had been in days, almost amused: "For what it's worth, *diver*, my mother always says the best time to jump is when the water looks worst."

Outside, far off across the black lagoon, a light appeared where no light should be — low on the water, past the point, moving without hurry.

Odile saw it too, and her face changed.

"That's no fishing boat," she said quietly. "That's a navy cutter, and it's coming in ahead of the tide."
