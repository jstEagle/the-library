## 20. The Cutter Arrives

The morning the cutter came, the whole island turned out on the shingle as if it were a festival. That was the strange thing about catastrophe, Zephyrine thought — after enough days of it, people began to dress for it.

They stood in their best ragged clothes, the pearl divers and the salt-boilers and the fishwives, squinting out at the gray line where the sea ended and the sky gave up pretending to be different. Odile had come with her hair pinned up and a ribbon in it that Zephyrine recognized as having once been a flour sack. Old Maa Bassam had brought a stool. A stool! As though she intended to watch the end of the world seated comfortably.

"She's late," Maa Bassam said, by way of greeting.

"Cutters keep their own time," Zephyrine said.

"So do I," said Maa Bassam, "and mine says she's late."

The tide road was open — it had opened three days early, the sea having apparently decided that all this business of secrets and drowned ledgers was beneath it — and the company's men had spent those three days doing nothing at all about it. Brant's militia stood at the far end of the shingle in their gray coats, muskets held in that particular way that said *we have been told to look imposing and we are not sure how*. Behind them, the warehouse where Emil Voss lay hidden looked exactly like every other building on the island, which was the entire point of warehouses, Zephyrine had always felt.

Beside them, not touching but near enough to feel, stood Ilsa Voss. She had washed and bound her hair and borrowed a coat from Odile's dead husband's trunk, and she looked less like a woman declared an enemy of the crown than like a woman who had declared the crown an enemy of hers and won the argument. Her hands, though. Her hands would not stay still. They found each other, parted, found each other again.

"He'll be frightened," she said, for perhaps the fortieth time since dawn.

"He'll be fine," Zephyrine said. "He's your son."

"That's what frightens me."

Zephyrine laughed despite everything, and Ilsa almost smiled, and between them they managed something that a stranger might have mistaken for calm.

The plan was simple, which meant it was either very good or already dead. Brant, whose bargains had a way of unraveling even as one shook hands over them, had agreed — in exchange for the recovered ledger pages, Delacroix's confession, and Zephyrine's sworn testimony against the Meridian Trading Company itself — to deliver Emil Voss to the crown cutter not as a spy to be shot but as a material witness to be deposed. It was a thin distinction. Zephyrine knew how thin. Thin distinctions were what kept the sea out of boats; you did not sneer at them.

"You understand," Brant had said the night before, in the back room of the counting house, his voice low and his eyes everywhere else, "that if this goes wrong, I was never part of it."

"You've never been part of anything," Zephyrine had agreed pleasantly. "You're famously uninvolved. There are songs about it."

Brant had not appreciated that. Brant did not appreciate most things. But he had signed the paper, because Brant above all people understood which way the wind was shifting, and the wind had been shifting toward Meridian for a week now, smelling of ledgers and rope.

* * *

The cutter came up out of the haze at mid-morning, and it was nothing like anyone expected.

They had all imagined a warship — guns, marines, a captain with a face like a closed door. What arrived instead was a trim little vessel with patched sails and a figurehead missing one arm, and when she dropped anchor in the roads her crew went about their work singing, badly, a song about a girl who married a fish. The pennant at her masthead was the crown's, right enough. The rest of her looked like something assembled from spare parts and stubbornness.

"That," said Odile slowly, "is the *Petite Vérité*."

"It can't be," said someone behind them.

"It is. My cousin served on her during the blockade. They pulled her out of reserve for this. She's a survey vessel, Zeph. She measures coastlines." Odile's voice carried a wonder that Zephyrine had not heard from her since before the bridge closed. "They didn't send a gunship. They sent a ship full of clerks."

"A ship full of clerks," Zephyrine repeated, and felt something loosen in their chest that they had not realized was wound tight.

Because that was the point, wasn't it. That had been the whole gamble, the whole terrible wager of the last weeks — that the way to fight the Meridian Trading Company was not with muskets but with numbers, not with heroes but with honest arithmetic. Delacroix had died for it, or near enough. The drowned ledger had come up out of the mangrove mud still legible, its ink preserved by the same cold salt water that had kept it secret, and every page of it was a small door opening onto the company's crimes: the false weights, the sold rations, the divers' deaths written down as *equipment losses*, and at the bottom of it all, in Delacroix's careful hand, the record of what he himself had done and could no longer bear.

A gunship could have burned the warehouse. Clerks could read.

The longboat came ashore through the surf, and out of it stepped, first, a small round woman in a naval coat too big for her, carrying an umbrella against a sun that was not shining, and second, a stack of ledgers so tall it required a sailor to walk behind and steady them.

"Monsieur Joubert?" the small woman called, before her boots had even fully left the boat. "Or — forgive me — Joubert of no fixed honorific? I was told to ask for Joubert."

"I'm Zephyrine Joubert."

"Splendid." She planted the umbrella in the shingle like a flag and extended a hand. "Commodore's Adjutant Perrine Duval, Crown Commission of Inquiry into the Affairs of the Meridian Trading Company, which is a very long title that mostly means I count things until people confess. You'll be my favorite person on this island, Monsieur Joubert, because I'm told you brought me numbers."

"I brought you a ledger."

"I *love* ledgers." She said it the way other people said *I love my children*. Then her gaze moved past Zephyrine, sharp as a gull's, and settled on the gray coats at the end of the beach, and on the warehouse beyond, and the warmth in her face banked itself down to coals. "And I'm told there's a young man here the company would very much like me not to meet."

"There is," said Ilsa Voss, stepping forward. "I'm his mother. I'm also, according to your government, an enemy of the crown."

Duval looked at her for a long moment. Then she took off her hat, which seemed to be her version of removing gloves.

"Captain Voss. Yes. There is a warrant out for you." She tucked the hat under her arm. "There is also, as of six days ago, a superseding order from the Admiralty instructing that any persons able to give evidence touching the affairs of the Meridian Trading Company are to be taken into the protection of this commission, warrants notwithstanding, and I will tell you honestly, Captain, that I have read both documents several times and the second one is much more fun."

"And my son?"

"Your son," said Duval, "is the single most valuable piece of paper on this island, and I intend to treat him accordingly. Which is to say: fed, warmed, deposed gently, and never once alone with anyone wearing a gray coat." She raised her voice slightly, so that it carried down the beach to the militia, who had begun, very quietly, to shift. "That last part was a public announcement. I find announcements work better than warnings. Warnings invite discussion."

* * *

They brought Emil out at noon.

Zephyrine had half expected him to be carried, but he walked — leaning on Odile's arm and on a boat hook repurposed as a cane, pale as the inside of a shell but upright, and when he saw his mother across the shingle he stopped walking entirely, just stood there, and Ilsa crossed the distance between them at a pace that started as dignity and ended as a dead run.

She caught him hard enough that the boat hook clattered away, and he made a sound that was half laugh and half something else, and then the two of them were just holding each other on the wet stones while the whole island watched and pretended very loudly not to.

Zephyrine looked away, out at the cutter riding at anchor, and let them have it.

Odile leaned close. "You did that," she said.

"The commodore did that. And Delacroix, in his way. And Brant, God help us, because he does arithmetic faster than he does conscience."

"You did that," Odile repeated, with the immovable patience of a woman who had known Zephyrine since both of them were small enough to fit in a clam basket together, and Zephyrine had no answer for that, so they watched the sea instead.

Emil reached out and found Zephyrine's shoulder, gripping it with fingers still thin from fever. "They told me you'd come back for me," he said. "In the dark, in that warehouse. Every night I told myself: Zeph promised. Zeph doesn't drown promises."

"I nearly did," Zephyrine said. "Drown one, I mean. There was a stretch where the promise and I were both underwater."

"Well." Emil's grin was his mother's grin, worn thinner. "You came up. That's the whole trade, isn't it? Divers don't find pearls by staying dry."

Duval cleared her throat with the delicacy of a woman interrupting a coronation.

"If the family reunion could survive being moved onto dry, warm, defensible ground," she said, "my clerks have set up in the customs house, and there is soup. I want to be clear about the soup. It is bad soup. Navy soup. But there is a great deal of it, and it is hot, and I am told the alternative on this island has been rationed to the point of philosophy." She paused, and her eyes flicked once more toward the gray coats, who were withdrawing now, unhurried, in the manner of men who wanted very much to be seen leaving. "One thing more, Monsieur Joubert. Before we sail, I'll need your deposition. All of it. Everything you know about the company, the factor's death, the ledger, and — " she consulted a paper — "one Mister Brant, formerly of the company's private militia, presently styled 'Acting Magistrate,' a title I intend to enjoy dismantling."

"He made a bargain," Zephyrine said carefully. "Testimony for cooperation. I gave my word it would hold."

"Mm." Duval folded the paper away. "Then your word holds. I'm not in the business of breaking other people's promises, only of making liars account for their own. He'll answer for what he did. He'll be credited for what he helped undo. Both things can be true; it's called justice, and it's terribly unpopular." She smiled, quick and crooked. "Now. Soup?"

* * *

The customs house smelled of mildew and old tea, and it had never in its life hosted so many important people, and it wore the occasion like a coat two sizes too big. Sailors hauled ledgers in stacks. Clerks — actual clerks, an entire migrating flock of them — established themselves along the long table with their nibs and blotting paper, and the sound they made, that soft continuous scratching, was the most hopeful noise Zephyrine had heard in months.

Emil slept in a real bed, under a real blanket, with his mother sitting beside him reading a salvaged shipping almanac as though it were scripture. Twice Zephyrine passed the door and twice Ilsa looked up, and the second time she mouthed something across the dim room that Zephyrine chose to interpret as *thank you*, though it might have been *he snores*, which was also true.

It was evening when Duval found Zephyrine on the customs house steps, watching the tide come in over the road.

"You should eat," Duval said, handing over a bowl without waiting for agreement. She sat down with the sigh of a small woman who had been standing importantly all day. For a while neither of them spoke. The water crept up the causeway stones, patient as everything the sea did.

"When I was a girl," Duval said eventually, "on the mainland, my father kept a chandlery, and the Meridian factor used to come in and buy rope at prices no honest rope costs, and my father would smile and sell it to him, and afterward he'd sit in our kitchen looking like a man chewing a lemon. I asked him why, once. Why smile at a man you despise. And he said: *because the sea is wide, petite, and my ledger is narrow.*" She stirred her soup. "I have spent thirty years making my ledger wider than his sea. And today I sailed into a harbor and met a pearl diver's child who got there first."

"I didn't get anywhere," Zephyrine said. "I got pushed. By the bridge closing. By the murder. By a promise I made in sunlight to a woman who scared me."

"Yes, well." Duval shrugged. "Most of history is people getting pushed and deciding which direction to fall. You fell forward. Not everyone manages it." She finished her soup and set the bowl down. "Your deposition, then. First thing tomorrow, before the tide turns. I want you sworn and recorded and safe aboard before the company's own people arrive."

Zephyrine turned. "Their people are coming?"

Duval looked out at the darkening water, and for the first time since her longboat touched the shingle, the little adjutant looked tired.

"Oh, they're coming, Monsieur Joubert. A brig out of the company's southern station, flagged as a mail packet, carrying — if my information is right — a director's representative and rather more armed men than the post usually requires. She was sighted this afternoon, forty miles south, making good speed." She rose, brushed off her coat, retrieved her ridiculous umbrella. "So tomorrow we will have a race, you and I. Your testimony against their arrival. Ink against muscle. The oldest race there is."

She paused at the top of the steps, silhouetted against the lamplight spilling from inside.

"Sleep well, Joubert. Tomorrow we find out whether the truth can outrun a brig."

Out past the breakwater, the tide road vanished under black water, and somewhere in the dark to the south, sails were already bending toward them.
