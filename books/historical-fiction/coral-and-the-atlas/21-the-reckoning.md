## 21. The Reckoning

The cutter *Resolute* had come at dawn, riding the morning tide into the harbor with her guns run out and her pennant snapping like a whip being cracked somewhere far away. By noon, the factor's great hall — the same hall where Delacroix had once counted pearls by lamplight, licking his thumb before each page — had been transformed into a courtroom. A long table for the commissioners. A smaller one for Brant, who sat behind it with two lawyers and the posture of a man who had rehearsed being wronged.

Zephyrine stood at the witness rail in their good shirt, the one Odile had boiled clean the night before, and tried not to look at their own hands. They kept seeing them anyway, in memory: prying open the drowned ledger, salt-wrinkled fingers on waterlogged paper, the company's sins coming apart in their grip like wet bread.

The commissioner was a narrow man named Halloran, sent from the mainland with a clerk, a soldier, and the particular expression of someone who had been promised this island was simple and had discovered within four hours that it was not.

"State your name for the record," Halloran said.

"Zephyrine Joubert. Pearl diver, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company since I was six years old."

"You understand," Halloran said, "that by testifying against the Company, your indenture — which is, I'm told, eleven years from fulfillment — may be forfeit entirely."

"I understand it better than you do," Zephyrine said. "I've been counting those years in my teeth."

Someone in the gallery laughed — quickly stifled, but real. Odile, probably. Odile laughed at funerals; it was how she prayed.

Halloran let the smile pass over him without landing. "Then tell the commission what you found."

* * *

Zephyrine told it plainly, because they had learned — late, and mostly from drowning — that plainness was the only thing salt water couldn't ruin. They told about the ledger hidden beneath the floor of Delacroix's storeroom. About the pages recording shipments that never existed, insurance claims on cargoes deliberately scuttled, divers' deaths entered as weather losses so the Company could collect on men it had sent down with rotten air lines.

"The third entry from the end," Zephyrine said. "Dated the spring before last. *One diver lost, gear failure, compensation declined.* That was Tomas Aguilar. His gear didn't fail. I patched it myself two days before, and there was nothing wrong with it. But he'd filed a grievance about his share, and Mr. Brant's policy was that grievances cost more settled than sunk."

Brant's lawyer rose. "Objection. The witness is offering speculation—"

"The ledger is in your evidence chest," Zephyrine said. "In Delacroix's hand. He wrote *declined* himself, and underlined it twice. He liked underlining things he was proud of."

The lawyer sat down slowly, the way a man sits when the chair has been pulled out from under him once already.

Halloran consulted his clerk's copy. "You're telling this commission that the Company's factor — the murdered man whose death occasioned these proceedings — was himself keeping records of the Company's crimes?"

"Yes, sir."

"And why would he do that?"

"Insurance," Zephyrine said, and heard the whole hall understand it at once, the way a wave understands a seawall. "Delacroix didn't keep the ledger because he had a conscience. He kept it because a man who knows where all the bodies are buried sleeps easier knowing nobody knows where *he's* buried. It was his life insurance. And somebody decided to collect early."

Every head in the hall turned — not to Brant, but slightly past him, to the space where a man might have been standing if he were standing anywhere at all. Brant himself did not turn. He watched Zephyrine with an expression of almost paternal disappointment, as though Zephyrine were a promising apprentice who had chosen, inexplicably, to burn down the shop.

* * *

They had arrested Brant three days after the tide road opened — which was to say, three days after everything: the storm, the boat in the mangroves, Emil Voss carried bleeding across the sandbar with Odile's shawl knotted under his arms, and Captain Ilsa Voss walking out of the surf herself, half-drowned and entirely unrepentant, to put a pistol on the factor's table and say, *Take me to whoever is in charge. I have been declared dead by two governments and I intend to disappoint them both.*

That part hadn't gone in the official account. Zephyrine suspected it never would.

But the arrest had been quiet, almost administrative. Soldiers came to Brant's quarters while he was eating breakfast. He finished the breakfast first. That was Brant all over — he believed that composure was a form of ownership, that if he refused to be flustered then reality itself would have to file an apology.

Now, in the hall, he rose to speak in his own defense, and Zephyrine understood why he'd waited. This was the performance. He had dressed for it — not his factor's coat but a plain merchant's jacket, the costume of a humble servant of trade.

"Commissioner," Brant said. "I will not waste the commission's time denying the ledger. It is genuine. Delacroix wrote it, and Delacroix was — we know now — a blackmailer, a thief, and a murderer." He paused precisely, letting each word settle like silt. "What I deny is the leap this child asks you to make. A ledger exists; therefore I killed its author? The witness is an indentured diver with no education in accounts, no standing in law, and — if we are honest — a documented history of consorting with persons declared enemies of the Crown. Captain Voss sits in your brig at this moment. Her son was smuggled off this island under cover of the very storm this witness now profits from describing. Ask yourself, Commissioner: who taught this child to tell stories?"

The hall went still. Zephyrine felt the old weight — the one they had carried since childhood, the weight of being small and brown-skinned and contractually owned, the weight that said *you are a tool that talks back*. For a moment it pressed exactly as it always had.

Then Zephyrine thought of Tomas Aguilar's mother, who had been told her son died of weather. Of Odile, who had spent nine years pretending not to notice what the ration ledgers skimmed off the top because noticing was dangerous and she had children. Of Ilsa Voss, sitting in a cell right now, who had made a promise-keeper out of a pearl diver's kid because she'd seen, back in peacetime, something worth trusting in a ten-year-old holding a sea urchin wrong.

"I wasn't taught to tell stories," Zephyrine said. "I was taught to hold my breath and bring up what's valuable. Same skill set, turns out." They turned to Halloran. "And Commissioner — ask Captain Voss about the *Meridian Star*. Ask her why a ship carrying Company pearls also carried dispatches for a foreign navy, and why Delacroix's ledger records payment for 'passage fees' on dates matching her runs. The Company wasn't just insuring wrecks it caused. It was selling information through them. Captain Voss knew because she was the courier — until she refused, and became an enemy of the Crown overnight, and the Company offered to help the Crown find her. Ask her. She's been waiting three days to be asked."

Halloran looked at Brant for a long moment. Then he said, mildly, in the tone of a man laying down a winning card he has been holding out of courtesy: "We asked her yesterday, Mr. Brant. At length."

Brant's face did not move. But his lawyer began gathering papers that had not needed gathering, and Zephyrine, watching, understood that some men drown quietly, without any water involved.

* * *

The session broke at dusk. Verdicts, Halloran announced, would wait for the depositions crossing on tomorrow's tide — the Company's mainland partners were sending advocates, the Crown was sending an auditor, and everyone, it seemed, was sending paperwork. Justice, Zephyrine reflected, moved at the speed of correspondence.

Outside, the light was doing the thing it did here in the last hour, turning the harbor to hammered copper, gulls stitching the sky shut behind the headland. Odile fell into step beside them on the path down toward the divers' quarter, and for a while neither spoke, because some silences are conversation.

"He called you a child," Odile said finally.

"I am a child."

"You're twenty-two."

"To him I'm a child. To the ledger I've got eleven years left. Funny how those add up differently depending on who's doing the arithmetic."

Odile snorted. Then, quieter: "My mother used to say the sea keeps every promise eventually. Even the bad ones. Especially those." She bumped their shoulder with hers. "You kept yours. The boy's on a boat to his auntie in Port Maren with a clean bill of health and my second-best scarf. Whatever happens tomorrow — that already happened. Nobody can unkeep a promise."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "They can only charge you for it."

"That's the spirit."

They laughed, both of them, the tired kind, and below them the tide came in over the flats the way it always had, indifferent and punctual, erasing and rewriting the shore in the same motion.

* * *

Zephyrine was summoned again that night — not to the hall but to the small room behind it where Halloran had established himself among teetering deposition boxes, with the soldier posted outside and the clerk asleep at his desk with his cheek on a form.

Halloran poured two cups of tea that tasted, both of them, faintly of the tin they'd traveled in. He pushed one across.

"Tomorrow, when the advocates arrive, they will offer you things," he said. "The Company will offer money. The Crown may offer freedom with conditions attached — there are always conditions; I've never met a freedom without them." He studied Zephyrine over the rim of his cup. "So before they fill your ears, answer me one question honestly. Not for the record. For me."

"All right."

"Why? You had a way off this island. The boy was smuggled, the captain was someone else's problem, the storm covered everything. You could have dived your quota and kept your head down for eleven years and died an old woman— an old person—" He waved vaguely. "Free, eventually, in the technical sense. Instead you handed me a ledger that ends your indenture and possibly begins someone else's imprisonment. Why?"

Zephyrine looked into the tea for a while. Outside, the water slapped the pilings, patient as bookkeeping.

"When I was small," they said, "my father told me the trick to diving is that you can't fight the bottom. You go down, you take what's true, and you come up. The drowning happens when people try to stay down. When they decide the bottom is where they live." They looked up. "Survival bought with silence is just drowning slow. Somebody told me that once, or I figured it out in the water — I honestly don't remember anymore which. But it's true, Commissioner. I've tested it."

Halloran nodded slowly, and for a moment the narrow bureaucratic face looked briefly, humanly tired. "For what it's worth — and I'm told it isn't worth much until tomorrow's tide — the auditor arriving on the morning boat carries warrants signed in advance. If your testimony holds, and it will, the Company's charter on this island is finished. Everything changes."

"That's what frightens me," Zephyrine said. "Everything changing. I'd gotten rather good at everything staying terrible."

Halloran almost smiled. Then a knock came at the door — the soldier, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he'd been told to deliver gently and had decided, on reflection, not to.

"Commissioner. Sorry. The prisoner — Brant. His cell's empty. Guard says he asked for paper to draft a confession, proper and full, and the guard gave it to him because orders were to treat him civil till sentencing. Twenty minutes later the lamp had guttered and the window bar—" He stopped, swallowed. "There's a boat missing from the Company dock, sir. The *Kestrel*. And the tide's already turned."

Halloran set down his cup with a small, precise click, like a full stop.

Zephyrine was already moving, grabbing their patched diving coat off the hook, and found they were — of all the ridiculous things — smiling.

"Commissioner," they said, "how do you feel about a night dive?"
