## 1. The Weight of Paper

The mail boat was called the *Perpetua*, which struck me as a cruel joke the river had been telling itself for forty years. She was oak below the waterline and optimism above it, and she moved through the December fog the way an old man crosses a courtyard he's slipped on before: carefully, resentfully, with frequent pauses to reconsider.

I stood at her rail with my hands in my pockets and my breath doing its small performance in the cold, and I counted what I had left. One satchel. One delivery. One town at the end of the map where the republic's long arm had never bothered to reach, or had reached once and found nothing worth taking.

"You're staring at that bag like it owes you money," said the pilot, whose name was Brann and who had told me so twice, unprompted, both times as though conferring a gift.

"It's heavy," I said.

"Paper's always heavy." He spat over the rail with the economy of a man who'd been fined for it elsewhere. "You a clerk?"

"Something like that."

"Clerks always say 'something like that.' Priests too." He adjusted the wheel a degree, a gesture of tremendous authority accomplishing nothing, since the channel was straighter than a sermon. "What's in it, if it's not rude to ask?"

"Diplomatic dispatches," I said, because it was true, and because truth is the best disguise when nobody expects you to use it.

Brann nodded as if this confirmed something unpleasant about me and left me to my fog.

Here is what was in the satchel, since we are being honest with each other and there is no one aboard to hear us: eleven sealed packets in three colors of wax, addressed to consulates and trading houses in towns I would not name to a priest on his deathbed; a bill of lading for machinery that did not exist, bound for a factory that had burned down in the year I still thought of as *recently*; and, sewn into the false bottom beneath all of it, a set of ciphers keyed to a network I knew for a fact had stopped answering its mail seven years ago.

Fifteen years I had carried things for the republic. First for the customs service, legitimately — manifests, tax schedules, the tedious blood of any bureaucracy. Then for the men behind the customs service, less legitimately. Then, after the purges, for whoever still paid in coin and didn't ask whether I flinched at loud noises. The satchel changed hands and owners the way a bad coin does, and I stayed with it, because a courier who stays with his bag lives longer than a courier who develops opinions.

But a man can only carry weight for so long before he starts to notice his own shoulders. Somewhere on the downriver boats that autumn, watching the reeds give way to hills and the hills to the flat gray country around Coral's Bridge, I had made the decision I should have made a decade earlier: this was the last run. Deliver the packets, collect the final payment, and then become the kind of man who owns a chair and sits in it. I had even chosen the town. That was the vanity in it — I hadn't just picked a place to retire, I had picked *this* place, as if retiring were a thing you could do romantically.

* * *

Coral's Bridge announces itself the way shy people enter rooms: by apology. First a church spire, thin as a needle in the fog. Then the smell — woodsmoke and river mud and, underneath, the faint sweetness of the pickle works that employed half the eastern shore. Then the bridge itself, which gives the town its name and its character and, I would come to understand, its fate: a long stone span with eleven arches, built by some empire that no longer existed, connecting the two halves of a town that had never agreed on much except that the bridge should stay.

We tied up at the lower quay, where a boy of perhaps twelve took the mail sacks with the gravity of a field marshal and a hand out for coins with the shamelessness of one. The *Perpetua* would take on pickled herring and coal and be gone by evening tide. I watched her settle against the pilings and felt the peculiar grief of watching your last exit prepare to leave without you.

"Room at the Ferryman," Brann offered behind me. "If they'll have you."

"They'll have anyone."

"That's what I heard about the town," he said, and turned back to his wheel, delighted with himself.

The Ferryman's Rest stood where the quay met the north road, and it was exactly the sort of inn I needed: old enough to have seen everything and forgotten most of it, kept by a widow named Marta Gusev who looked at new arrivals the way a farmer looks at weather. I took a room under the name Ansel Brandt, which was neither clever nor original, but then, neither am I, and originality in a false name is a confession in itself. I paid for a week and asked, casually, whether the customs house was still keeping regular hours.

"Regular as bells," she said. "Old Vane keeps them."

I put my coin on the counter slowly, so the trembling would look like age rather than memory. "Vane," I said. "Still?"

"Fifteen years, near enough. Quiet man. Good with his figures." She narrowed her eyes at me in a way that suggested she catalogued every guest against some private ledger of suspicion and had just made an entry. "You know him?"

"I knew a Vane once. In the capital. Probably a cousin."

"Probably," said Marta Gusev, in the tone of a woman filing my lie under a heading I didn't want to read.

* * *

Let me tell you about Elias Vane, since I am apparently telling you everything now, and honesty, once started, is difficult to stop mid-stream.

Elias Vane ran the northern relay of the network for nine years, and he ran it the way a good customs clerk runs a customs office: precisely, invisibly, and with paperwork for everything. While other station-masters drank and boasted and got themselves shot, Vane filed his secrets in triplicate and hid his fugitives inside legitimate cargo the way other men hide a knife in a loaf. He never once raised his voice. He never once told me his daughter's name, though I gathered there was one, from the way he'd go quiet whenever children passed his window. There were silences in that man the size of cathedrals, and I walked through them twice a month for nine years carrying his dispatches, and I never once thought to ask what it cost him to keep them.

Then came the last winter of the old network, and the last mission, and whatever happened in that alley behind the chandlery in Kessel — and afterward there was no more Elias Vane, only a customs clerk in a river town who might have been a cousin, or might have been nobody, and a courier who stopped asking questions because the answers had gotten heavy.

I told myself he'd retired. That's the mercy we do ourselves: we call it retirement.

* * *

I spent two days being a tourist in a town too small for tourism. I walked the bridge end to end and back, counting arches like a man with nothing better to do, which was precisely what I was. I bought gloves. I ate herring prepared four different ways and disliked three of them. I learned that the town council met on Tuesdays, that the ferry to the south bank ran "when it ran," and that everyone, without coordination, referred to the coming holiday with a small satisfaction — New Year's, the amnesty, the one day the government's calendar and the people's overlapped.

"The amnesty," the ferryman told me, poling us across the brown water. "Best thing this republic ever did, and it did it by accident. Everything before the war — pardoned. Everything before, wiped clean. My brother came home because of it. Sat in a cell six years for printing pamphlets, and on amnesty night he walked out and ordered a steak."

"That's a good ending," I said.

"It's a good *steak*," he said. "The ending's still being written. But yes."

On the third morning I presented myself at the customs house, satchel on my shoulder, heart conducting its usual pre-delivery argument with my stomach. The building sat on the quay between the pickle works and a chandler's, squat and gray and utterly unremarkable, which is the highest compliment architecture can pay to secrecy. A brass plaque read OFFICE OF THE CUSTOMS — E. VANE, CLERK, and beneath the lettering someone had polished the brass so often that the name shone brighter than the title, as if the metal understood what mattered.

I knocked.

"One moment," said a voice from within, and I want you to understand what happened to me in that pause. Fifteen years of discipline, and the sound of a woman's voice in Elias Vane's office undid me entirely. Because it wasn't a wife's voice or a maid's voice. It was young and careful and carried, underneath its courtesy, the same cathedral-sized silences I remembered from the man himself, as if silence were an heirloom passed along with the furniture.

The door opened.

She was perhaps thirty, with her father's exact eyebrows — I would have known them anywhere, those two dark strokes of permanent mild judgment — and none of his ability to hide, or perhaps all of it, worn so long it had become her face instead of a mask. She wore a high-collared dress buttoned to the throat in December fashion and January defiance, and she looked at me the way Marta Gusev had, the way everyone in a border town learns to look: sorting me, filing me, deciding what I was before I could finish being it.

"Mr. Brandt," I said. "Ansel Brandt. I have papers for the customs office."

"Papers," she repeated.

"Diplomatic dispatches. Consular seals." I held up the satchel like a fisherman displaying a modest catch. "Routine."

"Nothing that comes off the river is routine," she said, but she stepped back and opened the door, and I went into the warm dimness of the office, past the great ledger desk, past the filing cabinets with their brass handles worn silver, past a wall of drawers each labeled in a small precise hand — *Manifests 1898–1901, Manifests 1902–1904* — and I thought, absurdly, *he labeled them, of course he labeled them, he would have labeled his own coffin.*

"I'm Coral Vane," she said. "The customs clerk. You'll want to leave the bag for inspection."

"Vane." I let the surprise show, and it required no acting whatsoever. "I knew a Vane in the capital. Customs also. Perhaps a relation?"

"My father. He's dead." She said it the way you report weather — accurate, complete, closed. "Nine years now. I hold the appointment since."

"I'm sorry."

"Everyone is. It was a long time ago." She held out her hand for the satchel, and I saw her fingers: ink-stained at the second knuckle of the right hand, calloused in the pattern of a woman who wrote figures all day, every day, for years. Her father's hands, younger. "Your seals will need verifying against the consulate register. That takes until tomorrow. You can collect your receipt at noon."

"Of course." I unslung the bag and gave it to her, and I want to record, for whatever court eventually reads this account, that handing over that satchel felt like handing over a kidney. I had carried it through checkpoints and revolutions and one memorable flood, and now it sat on the desk of a dead man's daughter while she copied my false name into a real ledger with her dead man's handwriting, and I smiled at her and discussed the weather.

"You chose a quiet town to deliver to," she said, not looking up. "Nothing ever happens here."

"That's why I chose it," I said, and heard too late how it sounded.

Her pen paused — one heartbeat, no more — and resumed. "For your deliveries, Mr. Brandt?"

"For my retirement," I said, recovering. "I'm done after this. Going to find a chair and sit in it."

"A chair," she said. "That's the whole ambition?"

"You'd be surprised how expensive chairs are. The sitting-down kind of expensive."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth that on another woman's face might have been a smile, and she stamped my receipt — *Received, E.V. in absentia, per standing instruction* — and I did not ask what standing instruction, because couriers who ask questions develop opinions, and couriers with opinions develop graves.

* * *

That evening I sat in the Ferryman's window with a glass of the local plum brandy, which tasted the way its name promised and nothing like plums, and I allowed myself, for the first time in fifteen years, to plan forward instead of merely planning away. A chair. I would buy an actual chair. A good one, walnut, with arms. I would learn the names of the town council and complain about the ferry schedule. I would grow a beard or stop shaving, one of the two, and I would become a fixed and harmless feature of Coral's Bridge, like the bridge itself, bearing weight without asking what it carried.

Across the quay, the customs house showed one lit window, and behind it, I imagined, the daughter of Elias Vane was inspecting my dispatches with her father's patience, finding in them nothing but the ordinary corruption of a dying republic, and stamping them through.

The fog came up the river in the evening the way it always did, thickening around the lampposts, softening the bridge until its arches looked like a row of closed eyes. Somewhere upstream, I later learned, a gunboat with its running lights doused was making steady way downriver toward us, carrying Colonel Ferran and four hundred men and the paperwork of requisition. But that evening I knew none of it. That evening the town was warm and smelled of smoke and brine, the amnesty was nineteen days away, and I sat in a window with bad brandy and a good plan, believing for one last hour that a man could simply set his burdens down.

The customs house window went dark. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

I finished my drink and went up to bed, and if I slept at all, I dreamed — this I remember — of a drawer that would not close, no matter how many times I pushed it, no matter how gently, no matter how hard.
