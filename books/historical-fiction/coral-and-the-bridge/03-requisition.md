## 3. Requisition

I had planned to sleep until the light came up over the water and then drink one honest cup of coffee before deciding what to do with the rest of my life. The republic had other appointments for me.

The guns woke me. Not firing — just arriving. There is a particular sound an army makes when it takes a town without resistance, a sound like a very large piece of furniture being moved through a very small house: boots on frost-stiffened boards, wagon iron on cobblestones, the creak of leather and canvas and authority. I lay in my room above the ferry tavern and listened to Coral's Bridge change hands before the sun had cleared the marshes, and I thought, with the peculiar calm that has served me badly all my life, *so that is where they sent them.*

I dressed in the dark out of fifteen years of habit. Dark trousers, grey coat, nothing that catches lamplight. A courier learns to dress like furniture, and apparently I had never stopped being furniture, because when I stepped to the window no one looked up at me at all.

They came along the river road in column, then fanned out at the fish market with a discipline that would have been beautiful if it had been aimed at anyone else. I counted by habit — two hundred foot, maybe more still crossing the ridge behind; a machine-gun section unlimbering beside the customs wharf with the tenderness of men watering horses; cavalry going north toward the toll road. And over the roofs, from the direction of the town square, the first flag went up the records-office pole, hauled by two soldiers who did it quickly and professionally, the way you'd hang washing before rain.

Colonel Ferran's men. I knew the shoulder patch from the old days — the black bridge on yellow, which I had always thought an odd thing to be proud of. Bridges are for crossing, not for owning. But Ferran had never been a man for irony. He was a man for lists.

* * *

Downstairs the tavern keeper, a widow named Mrs. Havel who charged me extra for hot water and apologized for it every morning, was standing in her doorway with her apron wrung in both hands like a neck.

"They're putting paper on the doors," she said.

"Paper?"

"Seals. Yellow paper and wax. They sealed the records office, they sealed the harbor master's shed, they sealed old Pimm's warehouse and nobody even knows what Pimm kept in there." She turned to me, and her face asked the question her manners wouldn't: *what have you brought to my house?*

Nothing, I wanted to say. I am finished. I am a man delivering his last parcel, after which I intend to grow vegetables and become boring. Fifteen years I had told myself some version of this, and here is what I will say about self-deception: it keeps beautifully in a cold climate.

"I'll go and look," I said, which was true as far as it went, and put on my coat.

The street had filled with the particular crowd that forms at any disaster — half spectators, half inventory. Soldiers were going door to door with requisition orders, and I watched the ritual repeat itself down the whole length of the river road: knock, paper, argument, surrender. At the saddler's a lieutenant read aloud in the flat voice men use for documents, and the saddler stood holding his hat while his apprentices' names were written into a book. Billets. The army required beds, stables, kitchen access, and, the order noted with bureaucratic delicacy, "the cooperation of householders." Cooperation. In my experience that word has done more damage than any shell.

And then I saw the seals themselves.

A sergeant was closing up the harbor master's shed as I passed — a squat brick building that hadn't held anything worth sealing since the last war, unless you count rope. He brushed the doorframe with a size, pressed a strip of heavy yellow paper across the seam, and set his stamp into red wax with a little twist of the wrist, a motion I recognized the way you recognize a walk on a dark street.

I stopped walking. That was a mistake, so I made it worse by stopping completely, in the middle of the street, in front of everyone, and staring at a wax seal like a man who has seen a ghost in a government building.

Because I knew those codes.

The seal bore three lines of cipher marks around the garrison stamp, and I could read them the way you read your own name — not because I had ever seen this seal before, but because the cipher was mine. Ours. The old network's route-code, the system my mentor Vane had built and I had carried, the marks that said *this package authenticated, this courier sanctioned, this route open.* Somebody had taken a dead underground railroad's private language and stamped it on the doors of the republic's army, and every mark meant something to me that it plainly meant nothing to the sergeant twisting his wrist over the wax.

The top line, in our old grammar, would have read: *contents verified.* The middle line: *no passage.* And the third —

The third line was a date code. New Year's Eve. Midnight.

I want to explain what that felt like, but the closest I can come is this: imagine spending fifteen years burying a language, and then waking to find it painted on the side of the gun pointed at your town. It meant someone in Ferran's command had the old files. Not copies in an archive — someone had the *working keys*, the living cipher, the thing that should have died on the night everything else died. Which meant either a miracle or a hunt, and I have never once in my life been the beneficiary of a miracle.

"You there," the sergeant said. "Lost something?"

"My balance," I said. "Age."

He looked me over the way sergeants do — shoes, coat, hands, eyes — and found nothing worth the trouble, because I had spent a career being nothing worth the trouble. He twisted another stamp into the wax and moved on down the row of sheds, sealing empty buildings against crimes they did not contain, and I walked back toward the customs house feeling the satchel under my arm weigh what it had always weighed and also considerably more.

* * *

Here is what was in the satchel, since we have arrived at the point where honesty costs nothing yet: diplomatic dispatches for the consulate downriver, sealed in the capital's cipher, which I was contracted to deliver and had intended to deliver, after which I intended to retire on a small sum and a large silence. Ordinary work. My last work. I had chosen Coral's Bridge because it was off every map that mattered, because it had sat out the fighting with its hat pulled down, because its customs clerk had once been the best friend of the only man I ever obeyed — and because I wanted, childishly, to see what was left of the house where I had learned everything I knew.

That was yesterday. Yesterday it was a good plan. Today the army had put my childhood alphabet on the doors of the town jail.

At the customs house I found the soldiers already there.

There were four of them on the steps, and a fifth chalking numbers on the lintels — billet numbers, quartering marks, the arithmetic of occupation. The customs house was coming off the list of buildings and onto the list of homes, which is a worse fate than it sounds. Buildings only get searched. Homes get *inhabited*, and their inhabitants get studied, slowly, over bread and soup, by young men whose job is to notice things.

A corporal with a clipboard stopped me at the bottom of the steps. "Resident?"

"Guest. Of Miss Vane."

"Then you can carry your own trunk somewhere else by noon. Officers upstairs. Civilians lodge south of the mill canal, per the order posted at the market." He said it without heat, the way you recite a bus schedule, and I understood that the warmth would come later, if it came at all, from men further up the clipboard.

"The customs records," I said. "Are they sealed too?"

He gave me the look civilians get for asking about records. "Everything's sealed. Everything gets inventoried. Move along."

Everything gets inventoried. I moved along. And as I went I passed the window of the ground-floor office where Coral Vane had spent her life dusting ledgers she never opened, and I thought about her father — Elias Vane, customs clerk, the most careful man I ever met, a man who taught me ciphers inside conversations about tariffs because he trusted paper less than he trusted people and people not at all. I thought: *if they inventory this building, they will inventory it thoroughly, and Elias Vane was never thorough by accident.*

I did not yet know about the drawer. Let me be clear about that, because later, when everything came apart, people wanted to know what I knew and when. I knew nothing except a shape of dread: the specific feeling of a courier who realizes the cargo he should have been watching has been sitting under someone else's roof for fifteen years, patient as a seed.

* * *

By noon the town had been digested. The school became a signal station. The church stayed a church, which surprised me until I saw the chaplain's face and understood it had been converted too, just more quietly. Soldiers slept on porches waiting for their billets to be assigned, eating from tins, and the women of Coral's Bridge moved among them with the tight pleasant faces of people performing hospitality at gunpoint. Mrs. Havel got a lieutenant and two men. The saddler lost his apprentices to the stables. And the north road, the river barge landing, and the bridge each acquired a checkpoint by mid-afternoon, as if the army had looked at our little town and decided the important thing about it was leaving it.

I watched the bridge checkpoint take shape from the window of the ferry tavern, and I did not like what I saw. The other posts would be staffed by conscripts, bored boys checking crates. The bridge got something else. The man directing the sandbags and the inspection table was a sergeant with a face like a closed ledger — still, unhurried, reading everything. He positioned his men the way a chess player positions pawns he actually intends to use, and when a cart came through he didn't wave it past; he walked around it twice. I had known maybe six soldiers like that in my life, and every one of them had cost somebody dearly.

I learned later his name was Rask. That afternoon he was only a problem shaped like a man, standing between me and the only exit I could not replace.

Because that was the other arithmetic I was doing, the one underneath the troop counts: two hundred dispatches in my satchel that suddenly mattered far less than the fact that my satchel existed at all. If Ferran's command had the old route-ciphers, then the old couriers were a category of person again — hunted, priced, filed. And there was exactly one courier left alive who had carried for Elias Vane, and he was drinking bad coffee in a tavern full of soldiers, trying to remember how a retired man walks.

You walk slower. That's the answer. You walk slower, and you keep your hands visible, and you let your face go pleasantly empty. I had fifteen years of practice at pleasantly empty. I finished the coffee, which really was terrible — Mrs. Havel charged extra for hot water and delivered on none of her promises — and I went out to be usefully invisible.

* * *

The requisition order was posted at the market by evening, and I read it twice because the second reading was worse.

It was fair-minded, in the way that a knife is fair-minded: it cuts everywhere equally. The town was declared a provisional military district. Movement restricted. Assembly prohibited. Records of all civil offices placed under military seal pending inventory — and here was the sentence that mattered, buried in clause eleven like a wire under snow: *all seals to remain until final disposition of records, scheduled following the close of the current amnesty period.*

Final disposition. I had spent thirty years learning to read official prose, and "final disposition" has only ever meant one of two things: locked away forever, or burned. Given a colonel with Ferran's reputation and a republic collapsing under its own generals, I could tell you which way to bet, and I would not bet on locked away.

Amnesty period. The word reached out of the poster and took me by the collar. There was an amnesty running — had been for months, one of the new government's gestures, pre-war offenses forgiven if nobody made noise about them. It expired, if I remembered the gazette correctly, at midnight on New Year's Eve. Three weeks away. After that, everything pre-war became *closed* — not resolved, closed, the difference between a wound and a sealed room.

Someone in the capital had decided that the cleanest way to end the story of the dissidents and the people who sheltered them was to burn the paperwork, and Colonel Ferran had been sent to a quiet river town to inventory exactly that paperwork, using exactly the cipher that the dissidents' own courier had built, on exactly a clock that ran out with the year.

I stood in the market square in the cold with my hands in my pockets, doing sums. Two hundred families, though I couldn't have told you then how I knew the number, or why that number and not fifty or five thousand — I felt it the way you feel weather. Two hundred families who had done a dangerous kindness once, recorded somewhere in a town whose records were now sealed with my own dead language, awaiting a final disposition scheduled for the hour the calendar forgave everybody.

There was a version of me — I met him there in the square, shook his hand, almost liked him — who said: *not your cargo. Your cargo is in the satchel. Deliver the satchel, cross at dusk, be a vegetable farmer by spring. You carried enough. Everyone carries until they don't; you don't owe the dead a second war.*

And there was the other version, who said nothing at all, and simply looked at the customs house windows, where lamplight was coming up, and at the shadow that moved behind them — Coral Vane, who would be serving her father's dinner hour alone as she had for fifteen years, dusting a museum of respectable lies, in a building the army was about to inventory down to its nails.

Elias Vane had been thorough by accident of never. His daughter had been raised in that thoroughness like a plant raised against a wall, bent by it without knowing why. If there was anything in that house — and there was, I realized, there must have been, because a man does not build a cipher into his daughter's silences for nothing — then the machinery I once served was three weeks from feeding it into a stove.

The bugle sounded the garrison call from the schoolhouse roof, thin and wrong in the river air. Lamplighters were going along the streets, and behind them, at intervals, the yellow seals glowed faintly on the doors, *contents verified, no passage,* in an alphabet only two living people could read.

One of them was across the square, lighting her lamp in a house full of soldiers.

I crossed the square to go and lie to her.
