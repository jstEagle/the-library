## 4. What Father Kept

The soldiers had been in my house for six hours and they had already ruined two things I could name: the good tea (Lieutenant Osta's men had found the tin and pronounced it "foreign," which apparently meant confiscatable), and my certainty that my father had been exactly who I said he was.

The first loss was trivial. The second was about to get worse.

Requisition meant paperwork — this much I knew from history, which in our republic was mostly a list of things that had been taken from people by men holding clipboards. If Colonel Ferran's army wanted the customs house, they would want the deeds, the manifests, the shipping registers. Proof of ownership. The state liked to steal properly, with documentation. So before Osta came back with his questions, I went into Father's study to find every paper that proved the Vane family was boring.

I want it noted that I did not go looking for secrets. Fifteen years of deliberate ignorance is an investment, and I had no intention of cashing it out on a Tuesday morning because some lieutenant had a feeling. I went looking for tax records. There is a particular dignity in tax records.

The study smelled the way it always had: lamp oil, dust, and the ghost of pipe smoke that no amount of scrubbing had evicted. His chair still sat behind his desk at its permanent angle toward the window, because for fifteen years I had cleaned around it rather than move it, which tells you most of what you need to know about how I grieved. The ledgers lined the shelves in their neat rows, customs ledgers, forty of them, each one labeled in his small square hand: *Tonnage, 1893. Tonnage, 1894.* I had dusted every spine twice a year since he died. I had never opened one.

That is not entirely true. I had opened one, once, at nineteen, to a page of cargo figures so aggressively dull that I took it as a personal vindication. *See?* I had told myself, closing it. *Flour. Rope. Tar.* My father, who vanished on overnight trips to the marshes two or three times a month, who received letters in a hand nobody in town recognized, who taught me to memorize routes through the reeds as a game when I was eight — my father dealt in flour, rope, and tar. The game, I explained to myself, was a game. Children need games. Some fathers teach chess.

You can build a whole life out of explanations like that. I know, because I did. It stands up better than you'd think, provided no one ever checks the foundations, and no one had checked mine in fifteen years. Until Ansel Sandoval walked off the mail boat with my father's eyes in someone else's face, and said my name like a man confirming an address, and something in the floorboards of my certainty had gone soft.

But that was yesterday, and yesterday I was still a woman whose father had been a customs clerk.

* * *

The requisition papers were where papers go to die: the bottom drawer of the desk, under a nest of ribbon and dead pens. Deed to the house. Deed to the wharf share. A lease agreement with the ferry company dated 1901, signed by a man named Brac, whom I remembered as a smell — pipe smoke and river mud, someone who used to lift me onto the bollards. I set them aside, all present, all boring, all proof of a life so unremarkable that even an army should struggle to make anything of it.

It was while I was putting them back that my knuckles hit the back of the drawer, and the back of the drawer clicked.

I sat very still for a moment, the way you do when a house makes a sound at night. Then I did it again, deliberately. Knock. Click. A sound like a latch giving way somewhere behind a wall of wood.

Every child learns the architecture of hiding places eventually — the loose brick, the space under the stair. What I had never learned was my father's, because I had genuinely believed there wasn't one. He was a customs clerk. Customs clerks do not have false drawers. False drawers are for smugglers and spies and other people who appear in novels my godmother used to lend me and then take back quickly.

The panel slid inward, tilted, and came free into my hands.

Behind it was a cavity the length of my forearm, lined in old velvet gone grey with age, and inside it lay three things: a pistol wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of letters tied in string, and a ledger.

Not a customs ledger. It matched the others in size, which I suppose was the point, but the cover was unmarked, and it was heavier than it looked, the way certain books are heavier than they look, as if the paper has absorbed something from what passed through it.

I want to tell you I hesitated. I did not hesitate. Whatever I had spent fifteen years building out of flour and rope and tar, my hands went straight past it, straight past the pistol, to the book, and opened it, and the first thing I saw was a name I knew.

*Marta Cillik, ferryman's widow, East Row. Sheltered: 14–19 March 1896. Two men. Moved north.*

Marta Cillik. Marta, who had taught me to gut a fish on her dock and told me my braids were the finest on the river. Marta, who my father visited every month without fail, whom I had assumed — I remember assuming this — was a romantic interest, and had felt quietly pleased about, because a widowed friend seemed a gentle sort of secret for a lonely man to keep.

I turned the page.

*The Odry family, tanners' lane. Sheltered: one woman, pregnant, 8 days. Child born in the cellar. Moved south with the coal run.*

*Father Ansek, parish of St. Ved. Hid documents in the church loft. Do not use again after April.*

*Jonas Pell. Courier. Reliable. Lost December 1897 — believed taken at Hrast ford. Wife knows nothing.*

On and on. Two hundred names — I counted later, when counting seemed like a thing a person could do; that night I simply turned pages until the lamp guttered and my hands stopped being mine. Dates, houses, routes, code marks in the margins, little symbols I didn't recognize and one, near the end, that I did: a small looping figure that appeared beside dozens of entries, and that I had seen before, drawn in the steam on a kitchen window when I was ten, by a guest of my father's who laughed when I asked what it meant.

Ansel Sandoval's mark. I understood that with a lurch that arrived in my stomach several seconds before it arrived in my head. The loop was next to entries spanning years. The loop was next to entries describing deliveries of *people*.

* * *

Here is what grief is supposed to do, according to everyone who writes about it: soften with time. Mellow into something bearable, a stone worn smooth in the pocket. Mine had done the opposite. It had hardened into a shape, a comfortable shape, a customs-clerk-shaped hollow that I fit myself into every morning, and now here was a book shaped nothing like it, and the stone in my pocket had sprouted edges.

Betrayal is not the opposite of grief. It is grief that has been lied to.

Because that was the word my mind kept reaching for, absurdly, unforgivably, standing over a ledger full of rescued human beings: *betrayed*. He had betrayed me. He had stood in this room and taught me to copy manifest figures in a fair hand and sent me to the shop for tobacco and asked about my lessons, and all the while the walls of my childhood were lined with other people's desperation, and I had dusted around it twice a year.

He had let me believe he was small. Worse: he had trained me to defend the belief. I thought of all the times neighbors had hinted — old Mrs. Voss with her raised eyebrow about Father's "night fishing" — and how I had shut each hint down with a smile and a fact, *he keeps the tonnage accounts, you know how the port office is,* and how Mrs. Voss would nod, and look at me with something I had filed away as pity and now recognized, with the ledger open in front of me, as respect. She had known. Half the town had known. I had been the only person in Coral's Bridge keeping my father's innocence alive, single-handedly, against all evidence, like a woman watering a dead plant for fifteen years.

And underneath the betrayal, quieter and far worse, a question with teeth: *why hide it from me?*

The obvious answer flattered neither of us. To protect me — yes, certainly, if the army had ever come asking, ignorance was armor, and he was right to forge it. But there was another answer sitting in the margins, in those looping courier marks, in the notation beside one entry that made my breath stop:

*C.V. ran the east cut alone, age 12. Steady hands. Never asks.*

C.V. Coral Vane. Age twelve. The east cut — the reed channel I had been taught as a *game*, paddling a flat-bottomed skiff through fog while Father timed me from the bank and called out turns. *Steady hands. Never asks.* He had written it down the way he wrote down tonnage. A performance review. I had been staff.

I put the book down on the desk, carefully, because my hands were shaking and the desk was the last solid thing in the world. And then I picked it up again, because putting it down felt like agreeing with everything it implied, and I read the entry four times, and somewhere between the third and fourth reading the betrayal curdled over into something else — not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but a terrible vertiginous pride. *Steady hands.* He had been proud of me. He had hidden a revolution inside our ordinary life and hidden me inside the revolution, and neither hiding had been kind, but both had been careful, and care was — I had to admit it, sitting in his chair for the first time in fifteen years — care was a language my father had spoken fluently and never once translated.

*Never asks.* Well. I was asking now.

* * *

The letters came next. There were only nine, and most were in cipher, rows of figures that meant nothing to me. But the top one was plain hand, undated, unsigned, and short, and I read it standing up because sitting felt suddenly too trusting.

*If you are reading this, the drawer has failed or you have grown clever. Both are possible. Forgive the concealment; it was for your safety and also, I confess, for mine — I did not think I could keep lying to your face, and so I kept you from the face-to-face altogether. The names in the book are owed more than silence. If the state ever falls and amends are offered, see they are paid. If the state comes for the book first, burn it and live. That is not cowardice. It is arithmetic.*

Burn it and live.

My father, it turned out, had anticipated me down to the hour: the army at the door, the records sealed, the clock running. Burn it and live. He had left me instructions the way he'd left me the house — practical, unsentimental, and wrong, or right, depending on what a person decided a name was worth.

I looked at the pistol in its oilcloth. I looked at the ledger, two hundred lives in square handwriting. I looked at the door, beyond which Lieutenant Osta was presumably at this moment drafting his questions about the Vane family customs business, and I realized I was sitting in an unlocked room with the single most dangerous object in Coral's Bridge on my knees, and that I had perhaps twenty minutes of privacy left in my own home.

Fifteen years of chosen ignorance, and here was the bill. I had imagined, insofar as I imagined anything, that if my father's ghost ever spoke to me it would say something comforting. Instead it said: *arithmetic.* And I heard myself answer it out loud, in an empty study, like a madwoman, or like his daughter:

"No."

Not burn it. Not yet, anyway. Because Marta Cillik had gutted fish with me on her dock, and the Odrys had a child born in a cellar who would be seventeen now, if the child lived, and Jonas Pell had a wife who knew nothing, and every one of those square entries was a debt my father had assigned to whoever grew clever enough to find the drawer. *See they are paid.* He hadn't written *if convenient.* He hadn't written *burn it and live, that is not cowardice, it is arithmetic,* without knowing — he must have known — that I would find the arithmetic wanting.

Or he hoped I wouldn't find it at all. Either way, the choice was mine now, and it sat in my lap weighing more than any book should.

Downstairs, boots on the porch boards. Osta's voice, asking one of his men where the mistress of the house had got to. The handle of the study door turning as politely as a knock.

I closed the ledger, slid it into the bread basket under the day's cloth — the one domestic sleight of hand every woman in this town could do asleep, and God forgive me, the first lie of my new life was baked goods — and smoothed my apron, and arranged my face into the expression I had practiced for fifteen years: pleasant, vacant, the face of a customs clerk's daughter who has never asked about anything.

The door opened. Lieutenant Osta stood in it, holding my father's file, and smiled the smile of a man who has already begun counting discrepancies.

"There you are, Miss Vane," he said. "I wonder — might we talk about your father's business?"
