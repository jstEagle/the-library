## 7. Billeted

They arrived at four in the afternoon, six men and a lieutenant, and the lieutenant did me the courtesy of knocking before he informed me that my house was no longer entirely mine.

"Requisition," Lieutenant Osta said, holding out a printed order as though it were a visiting card. "The customs house is municipal property suited to command purposes. You'll retain the upper floor."

"The customs house has been a private residence for fifteen years," I said. "It was deeded to my father on his retirement."

"Then the requisition corrects an oversight." He stepped past me into the hall without waiting to be asked, the way men do when they have already decided the answer to every question they intend not to ask. He was younger than I expected — thirty perhaps, with the careful neatness of a man who irons his own collar — and he had the eyes of an accountant. I have since learned that this is precisely what he was. The army had many men for shooting and very few for arithmetic, and arithmetic, done patiently, kills more thoroughly than shooting ever does.

Six soldiers came in behind him carrying their own bedding, which I will say for them showed a certain consideration, and began the business of making my father's house into a garrison. They stacked rifles against the hat stand. One of them put his boot on the hall bench to tie his lace, and I watched the mud from the parade ground spread into the grain of wood my father had varnished every autumn, and I thought: *you are dusting around them now. That is the shape of your life. Dust around the boots of strangers.*

"Cooking arrangements?" Osta asked, and it took me a moment to understand that he meant he intended to eat here.

"There's a cookshop by the fish market."

"I'm sure there is. But we'll be taking meals in, Mrs.—"

"Miss," I said. "Vane. Coral Vane."

"Miss Vane, then. Six men eat a great deal, and cookshops attract talk." He smiled at that, a small dry smile, as if talk were a form of weather he found tedious. "You'll be compensated at the standard rate."

I said yes, because there was no other word in my mouth, and because — I confess this freely, since I am done pretending my life was ever anything but performance — some cold practical chamber of my heart had already begun calculating how many loaves six soldiers consume per week, and whether the cellar held enough salt pork, and where in that calculation one hides a ledger.

But that last item was new thinking, only three days old, and I had not yet learned to stop my ears against it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being occupied: it is mostly housework.

The first two days I cooked, and scrubbed, and moved my own furniture to suit other people's convenience, and answered questions. The questions were the work. The cooking was only the stage set.

They were not cruel men, my billets, most of them. There was a boy called Pellin who was nineteen and homesick and wept once, quietly, over a letter from his mother, and pretended he hadn't, and I gave him extra bread the next morning without comment, and he knew I'd heard and was grateful in the way of dogs who have been kicked. There was a big slow man named Dreck who fixed the kitchen pump when it seized, unasked, and looked embarrassed by my thanks. The sergeant of the detail, a gray, competent woman called Harsch, kept the others off the second floor and away from me with a vigilance I initially mistook for kindness.

It was not kindness. It was discipline, which is kindness's strict cousin, and Sergeant Harsch understood something I took another day to understand myself: that Lieutenant Osta did not want his men fraternizing because fraternizing produces conversation, and conversation produces information flowing in the wrong direction. He wanted the house quiet. He wanted *me* quiet. Not silenced — nothing so crude. He wanted me managed, like a figure in a diorama: Customs Clerk's Daughter, Serves Dinner, Says Nothing of Interest.

So I performed her. God, how I performed her.

I made myself dull the way other women make themselves beautiful — deliberately, with craft. I let my voice flatten when I answered questions about my father. *He was a customs clerk. He processed river cargo for twenty-two years. He died of his heart eight years ago, in the winter, in that chair.* I invented small tedious details because I had discovered, in those first days, that the truth is suspiciously smooth and lies need texture: he argued with the harbor master about mooring fees; he kept canaries until the noise annoyed him; he voted, when voting still meant anything, for whichever candidate promised dredging. Every fabrication was a brick in a wall, and I laid them so fast and so smoothly that within three days I had built myself a father who never existed, a mild gray man made entirely of mortar, and I caught myself almost mourning him because he was easier to love than the real one.

That frightened me more than the rifles in my hallway. I want to be honest about that. It was not the soldiers' presence that frightened me — soldiers are merely weather, and weather passes. What frightened me was how good I had gotten, in seventy-two hours, at the thing I had been practicing all my life without knowing it was a skill. Fifteen years of chosen ignorance, and it turned out ignorance was only the rehearsal. Now was the performance.

And then there was Ansel, sleeping in my father's old boathouse across the garden with a twisted ankle and a satchel of dead dispatches and my father's ledger open under a shuttered lamp, decoding marginal marks I couldn't read, and every time I carried water across the yard I felt the whole architecture of my respectability balanced on his bad ankle like a plate on a pole.

"You're doing well," he told me on the third evening, in the garden, in the flat courier voice he used when anyone might be listening. "Harsch reported the household as compliant. Osta wrote it down himself."

"How do you know what she reported?"

"Because I know what a report looks like when it's being written." He nodded toward the study window, where Osta sat every night with the lamp burning late. "She brings him paper. He writes. She stands at ease while he does it. Then she takes the pages back and burns them herself — she doesn't trust the stove down here."

"Why doesn't she trust the stove?"

"Because sergeants know that lieutenants file everything, and sergeants protect their men from files." He said it lightly, but I saw him filing it too, somewhere behind his eyes, in whatever ledger couriers keep instead of hearts. Then: "Coral. He's going to ask about your father soon. Not casually. Formally. Be ready."

"He's been asking about my father since he walked in."

"No. He's been asking about the *house*. Those are different investigations." And he limped back toward the boathouse before I could ask the obvious question, which was: different *how*, exactly, and which one should terrify me more?

* * *

The dinner happened on the fourth evening, and I record it here because it deserves recording — because it was, I think, the moment the war stopped being something happening outside my windows and became a guest at my table.

Osta dined in state that night. Not with the men — he had Harsch lay the table in the study, my father's table, the one with the burn mark from a lamp accident in 1896 that Father always claimed was a memento of his wildest youth. Osta sat at it with his collar undone exactly one button, which I understood to be his version of informality, and requested — requested, mind you, in the tone of a man who has discovered that politeness extracts more than orders — that I join him.

"The men tell me you cook better than the mess," he said. "I thought I should verify the intelligence personally."

"I follow recipes, Lieutenant. Intelligence rarely survives translation into stew."

He laughed at that. It was a real laugh, brief and surprisingly warm, and I hated it, because warmth from an auditor is worse than coldness. Coldness you can brace against.

He ate well and talked little for the first course, and I performed Miss Vane of the flattened voice, passing bread, refilling his glass at intervals calculated to seem natural. It was during the second course — river fish, the last of the season's catch — that he set down his fork in the manner of a man clearing space on a desk, and said:

"Your father must have seen a great deal, in that office. Twenty-two years of manifests."

"River cargo is not riveting reading, Lieutenant."

"Riveting, no. But comprehensive." He produced, from inside his jacket — he had brought it to dinner, folded, the way other men carry love letters — a sheet of paper, and unfolded it beside his plate as though it were a napkin. "I've been reviewing the port records for the years before the war. Interesting reading, actually. Traffic through this town dropped forty percent between 1893 and 1895, then recovered completely after 1898. Do you know why?"

"Dredging," I said. "The channel silted up. My father complained about it constantly. Then the government funded the dredge and the traffic came back."

"That is exactly what the official record says." He smiled at me, pleasantly, and turned the paper around so I could see it, and it was indeed the port record, columns of tonnage in a clerk's careful hand — my father's hand, and my stomach went cold even as my face did nothing at all. "Remarkable recovery, though. The dredge accounts for maybe half of it, according to the engineer's reports. The rest just reappears. Cargo that stopped declaring itself in 1894 starts declaring itself again in 1898, same shippers, same routes." He tapped a column. "As if someone spent four years looking the other way, and then decided to stop."

"Perhaps the new harbor master was stricter."

"Perhaps." He folded the paper away. "Your father retired in '96, I see. Left the service right in the middle of the lean years. Convenient timing."

"My father had a weak heart and a small pension. Timing wasn't a luxury he enjoyed."

"No," Osta agreed mildly. "I suppose it wasn't." And he finished his fish and complimented the cook — meaning me, meaning the performance, and I could not tell whether he was applauding or grading — and asked me, as an afterthought at the door, whether the house had always been so well built.

"It's stood eighty years, Lieutenant."

"They did build things to last, then." He glanced up the staircase, toward the second floor, toward the study where the false-backed drawer had been empty for three days and where I had spent half of each night lying awake inventing explanations for its emptiness that would survive a patient man. "Good evening, Miss Vane. Thank you for dinner."

I washed dishes for an hour afterward and did not think about anything, which is a skill, and then I went up and stood in the study doorway — Harsch's men slept elsewhere, but the lamp was Osta's and it burned late — and I looked at my father's desk, at the drawers he had lined with cedar against damp, at the innocent respectable surface of it all, and I understood what Ansel had meant.

Osta wasn't hunting a courier. Not yet. He was auditing a town. He had pulled the port records the way a doctor pulls a chart, and he had found a symptom — four silent years, a recovery with no cause — and he was tracing it backward through every document this office ever produced, and the office had been my father, and my father was dead, which left the documents, and the daughter, and the house.

He was building a case out of tonnage figures. Two hundred families were going to be convicted by arithmetic.

I should have felt terror. What I actually felt, standing in that doorway with dishwater still cooling on my hands, was something hotter and less useful — anger, pure and ridiculous, that this neat young man with his folded papers thought he could reconstruct my father from freight manifests. He got the silences wrong. He got everything about the silences wrong. You cannot audit a silence, Lieutenant; I grew up inside one; I know its dimensions better than any record you'll ever pull.

Then the anger cooled, as anger does, and left the fear underneath, and the fear said: *he will request the personnel files from the capital. Clerks leave trails too. Service records, transfer orders, commendations — paper generates paper, and Father generated twenty-two years of it.*

I didn't know then that Ansel was already afraid of exactly that. I didn't know that Sergeant Rask at the bridge was already weighing an old debt against an old wanted circular. I knew none of the machinery moving around me. I knew only that at dawn I crossed the garden with the morning water, and Ansel met me at the boathouse door, and I said, before he could speak:

"He's pulling my father's files. From the capital. I don't know how I know — I just know how he eats. Like a man reading while he chews."

Ansel went very still. Then he looked past me at the house, at the study window where the lamp had burned past midnight again, and he said, quietly:

"Then we've run out of days. We move tonight."
