## 8. The Old Cipher

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that old men do badly and cannot stop doing: the sum of what they were against the remainder of what they are. I was doing it that night in the customs house cellar, by candlelight, with Coral Vane's father's ledger open on a barrel head, and I can report that the numbers did not favor me.

I had told her I would look at the marginal marks. That was two hours ago. She had gone upstairs to feed Lieutenant Osta's men their supper — an act of hospitality so weaponized it deserved its own chapter in a manual on siege warfare — and I had promised to have something worth hearing when she came back down.

What I had, so far, was a headache.

The ledger itself was honest work: two hundred names in Elias Vane's small, upright hand, dates, addresses, the quiet bookkeeping of sedition. But it was the margins that mattered. Faint strokes of graphite in the outer gutters, sometimes dots, sometimes short verticals, occasionally a symbol like a shepherd's crook. To a customs inspector they would read as tally marks, a clerk counting bales or barrels. I knew better. I had invented half of them.

Not personally — I want to be clear about that. I am not so vain as to claim credit for my own ruin. The cipher predated me. It came out of the river towns in the years before the war, a system built by smugglers who had needed to move brandy past excise men and later agreed, in the way that practical people do, that men were easier cargo than brandy and paid better besides. Elias had inherited it. Then he had handed it to me along with a satchel, a false name, and the cheerful assurance that a courier's life expectancy was "longer than you'd think."

Elias, as I have had occasion to note before in the privacy of my own skull, lied about several things.

* * *

Let me explain the cipher, because understanding it took me most of the night and you deserve to suffer through at least some of the process.

It worked on threes. Every entry in the ledger that marked a safe house carried three marginal features: a number of dots giving the day of the week the house could receive; a number of verticals giving the hour, counted from dusk; and a crook, turned left or right, indicating whether the drop went toward the capital or away from it. There was more — a rotation scheme keyed to the season, so that a dot-count in summer meant something different than the same count in winter — but that was the spine of it. Simple enough to teach a ferryman in ten minutes. Obscure enough that Ferran's intelligence people, in fifteen years of trying, never cracked a network that was mostly dead anyway.

Dead, I say. I assumed dead. You assume your own funeral arrangements are settled; it saves time.

So there I sat, translating. The first dozen entries were old friends and old ghosts. *Mirela Kostin, weaver, three dots, four strokes, crook right.* Which meant Thursdays, four hours after dark, outbound toward the marshes. I had drunk tea in Mirela Kostin's kitchen in another lifetime, back when my knees worked without commentary. She would be seventy now, if she lived. Her name sat in the ledger between a schoolteacher and a fisherman, three ordinary people who had committed the extraordinary crime of not asking questions when strangers needed beds.

That was the thing about Elias's ledger that no army report would ever capture. It wasn't a list of revolutionaries. It was a list of kindnesses, itemized. Two hundred entries, each one a person who had looked at danger and offered it soup.

I got through forty pages before I found myself.

* * *

You have to understand how it happens. You're reading names, letting them wash over you the way you let rain wash over a coat — sorry for each one, responsible for none of them individually because the collective weight would crush you flat. Your eyes do the work and your heart stays home. And then your own name comes up out of the page like a hand closing on your wrist.

*Sandoval, A. Courier. Northern circuit.*

Underneath, in Elias's tidy script, a date. The date I first walked into this town, twenty-two years old and full of the particular arrogance of young men who believe secrecy is a kind of courage. And underneath that, a notation I recognized because I had watched him write it, or ones like it, a hundred times:

*Reliable. Ask nothing. Keep.*

Keep. As in keep him. As in, this one is worth the risk of keeping.

I sat with the candle guttering and read my own name four times, and I will confess something I have confessed to no one: my first feeling was not horror. It was pride. A hot, shameful little flare of it — *reliable, keep* — the way a dog feels when the master's hand comes down. Fifteen years of telling myself I had escaped the work, retired from it, become a mere carrier of diplomatic paper for a republic I'd stopped believing in, and one line of graphite in a margin showed me the truth, which is that I had never stopped being proud of exactly the thing I claimed to regret.

The second feeling was cold, and it has stayed with me longer.

Because if my name was in the ledger, then so were the others'. Doret. The Kessler brothers. The woman called only Marjan who ran the western drops and whose face I never learned because that was the rule and I obeyed the rules, ask nothing, keep. If Ferran's men ever opened this book — and they would, they were already in the records office with their seals and their catalogues — every name in it became testimony, and testimony in Colonel Ferran's army had exactly one use.

And there, wedged in the margin beside Marjan's entry, was something that turned the cold into something worse.

A schedule.

I nearly missed it. It was rendered in the seasonal key — winter rotation, third cycle — and I had to rebuild the rotation table in my head from twenty-year-old memory, counting on my fingers like a schoolboy, before the pattern resolved. Three drop points. Dates running forward from the ledger's last year, not backward into it. And the final date in the sequence —

New Year's Eve.

* * *

Here is where I should explain what a dead-drop schedule was doing in a ledger that hadn't been touched in fifteen years, and I will, but first let me pour us both the dignity of admitting how long I sat there refusing to understand it.

The schedule wasn't old. That was the whole problem. The ink — pencil, actually, the same soft graphite as the rest of the margins — was fresh. Not fifteen years fresh. Not even a year fresh, if my eye still meant anything, and my eye had been the thing that kept me alive through two wars' worth of paper. Someone had added these entries recently. Someone who knew the cipher, knew the rotation, and knew the drop points well enough to update them.

Which meant the network was not dead. It was dormant. Sleeping like a seed in frozen ground, waiting for exactly the kind of thaw that a collapsing republic, desperate to close its ugly books before New Year's, might accidentally provide.

Or — and here the wry voice I keep for company in my own head cleared its throat — it meant someone had reconstructed the schedule recently for less charitable purposes. Baited it. Left it for a courier to find, the way you leave grain at the edge of a field and sit in the hedge with a gun.

Both readings were available. Both fit the facts. I have learned, at considerable expense, that when two readings fit the facts equally well, the correct response is neither panic nor relief but preparation for both.

I copied the schedule onto a scrap of paper, burned the scrap in the candle flame, and watched the ash float down into the barrel like gray snow. Then I put the ledger back in its wrapping of oilcloth and sat listening to the house above me: boots on the floorboards, the lieutenant's men laughing at some joke that surely wasn't funny, Coral's voice moving through it all like a blade wrapped in linen. Even muffled, even performing hospitality, she cut.

* * *

She came down after midnight. I heard the kitchen door bar slide home above me — she barred it every night now, a habit three days old and already permanent — and then her steps on the stairs, careful, because the fourth step had a voice and she had learned its name the first evening.

"Well?" she said. No preamble. She had brought the candle from upstairs and the two flames leaned toward each other across the dark like conspirators, which I suppose they were.

"I found the cipher," I said. "It's ours. Elias's. The margins are a complete operational record — receiving nights, hours, directions. Anyone who knows the key can reconstruct the entire northern circuit from this book."

"Can Ferran's people know the key?"

"Not quickly. Maybe not at all." I hesitated, and she caught it. Fifteen years of silence trains a person to hear hesitation the way sailors hear weather.

"But."

"But there's more in the margins than the cipher. There's a drop schedule, Coral. Three dead-drop points on the marsh route, dated forward. The last one is New Year's Eve." I held up a hand before she could speak. "And before you ask whether that's luck — no. I don't believe in that much luck arranged in one direction. Either someone kept the network alive all these years and is signaling that the routes still run, or someone rebuilt the schedule to flush out whatever couriers are left. Us, presumably, being whatever couriers are left."

She set her candle on the barrel head. In its light her face did something complicated — hope and suspicion wrestling, and beneath both, the thing I was beginning to recognize in her, the hunger of a woman who had spent fifteen years starving on ignorance and had just been shown the pantry door.

"Someone," she said slowly, "kept Father's routes alive. For fifteen years. Without using them?"

"Routes don't need using. They need maintaining. A stake driven into a marsh bank doesn't rot if someone replaces it. A hollow fence post doesn't fill in if someone clears it." I heard my own voice change as I said it, take on a texture I didn't like and couldn't refuse. "It takes a certain kind of person. Patient. Local. Someone who was here the whole time and never left."

"Like whom?"

"Your godmother, possibly. The ferryman. Half the names in this ledger live within a mile of us." I rubbed my eyes. "Or nobody. Or a soldier with a good memory for ciphers and a bad sense of humor."

"And your name is in it too." She said it quietly. Not a question. She had been sitting with the ledger long enough to have gone looking, and I felt no resentment about that. I'd have done the same.

"My name is in it. Marked reliable." I managed the wry tone, mostly. "Apparently I fooled everyone, including me."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, but the place a smile keeps its coat. "Father thought well of you."

"Your father thought well of a great many people he should have warned instead." I heard the bitterness land between us and didn't try to recall it. There would be time for confessions later, or there wouldn't; either way it wouldn't be tonight, with soldiers sleeping over our heads and a schedule in the margins pointing at New Year's Eve like a compass needle. "Coral. Listen to me. This changes things. If those drops are real — even one of them real — then the ledger doesn't have to cross the bridge. It doesn't have to pass a checkpoint at all. There's a way out through the marshes that predates every uniform in this town."

"The marshes are frozen."

"The marshes are *watched*, is what they are, and freezing makes them quieter. I'll go out tomorrow and see what condition the route's in. If even one drop point is maintained, we can stage the ledger north in pieces, a few pages a night, until there's nothing left in this house worth killing anyone over."

I said it and immediately heard the flaw, the way you hear a wrong note in your own singing. She heard it too.

"In pieces," she repeated. "You want to tear it apart."

"Pages travel better than books."

"It's not a book." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went very level. "It's two hundred people, Ansel. My father wrote them down whole. You don't carry a person across a marsh in pieces."

I wanted to argue. I want that on the record, since the record seems determined to make me honest: I wanted to argue, because arguing was easier than admitting that she was right, and easier still than examining why a man who had spent fifteen years carrying other people's secrets unopened should suddenly find himself unable to bear the sight of his own name in one.

"Tomorrow," I said instead. "Let me walk the route first. Then we'll decide what travels whole and what doesn't."

She picked up her candle. At the stairs she paused, her back to me, and said, without turning around:

"You looked strange just now, reading it. When you found your name. Like a man meeting himself coming the other way." A beat. "I used to wonder what my father saw in you. I'm starting to think he saw someone he was hoping you'd become. Whether you ever do is between you and the margin."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with the ashes of the schedule and the memory of a word in Elias Vane's handwriting.

*Keep.*

* * *

I did not sleep much, which was fine; sleep and I had been on reduced terms for years. Toward the coldest hour I climbed the cellar stairs and let myself out the kitchen door into the yard, where the frost had furred the fence rails white and the river ran black and slow beyond the town, carrying ice toward the sea like a long rumor.

The bridge stood against the stars, and on it, small with distance, the ember of Sergeant Rask's lamp. He was there every night now. He had been there the evening I arrived, though I hadn't known then what it meant, a hunter posted at the one door out of town. I watched his light and he, perhaps, watched the town, and between us lay the whole arithmetic of the matter: a man who owed me, a man hunting me, and a debt that could be paid in either direction depending on which way the wind blew on the night it came due.

And behind me, under the loose floorboard in the cellar, two hundred names waited in oilcloth, one of them mine.

Three drop points on the marsh route. New Year's Eve written in fresh graphite. Tomorrow I would go out before dawn and walk the frozen ground and learn which kind of trap the world had laid for me — the kind that saves, or the kind that catches.

I went back inside and barred the kitchen door, and was nearly asleep when the bells started.

Not church bells. Army bells — the harsh, doubled ringing that garrisons use when they want a whole town awake and afraid at once. Through the wall I heard boots, and Osta's voice giving orders, and then, distorted by cold and distance, a herald reading a proclamation from the courthouse steps in the flat voice men use for laws.

I caught only the end of it, the part that mattered, the part that reached every house in Coral's Bridge like a hand through a window:

"—all cases predating the armistice shall be closed permanently at midnight, New Year's Eve. All records destroyed. All testimony void. After that hour, no name shall be answerable for any debt of the old years, and no proof of any such debt shall be received."

No proof received. No proof existing. Burn the ledgers, burn the witnesses' standing, burn the past — and call it mercy.

I lay in the dark doing the only arithmetic left. Nine days.
