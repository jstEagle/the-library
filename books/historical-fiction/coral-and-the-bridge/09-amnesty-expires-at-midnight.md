## 9. Amnesty Expires at Midnight

The proclamation went up on the customs house door the morning after the barge inspection schedules were posted, and I will say this for Colonel Ferran: he understood theater. He could have read the announcement from his headquarters like any other order. Instead he had it read aloud from the bridge itself, at noon, by a lieutenant with a good voice and a bad conscience — Osta, as it turned out, though I didn't know his name yet — while the whole town was politely encouraged to attend.

Politely encouraged, in the Colonel's vocabulary, meant that the soldiers stopped being polite about it around eleven-thirty.

I stood at the back of the crowd with my collar up and my satchel under my coat, which is to say I stood exactly where a man carrying stolen diplomatic dispatches and harboring knowledge of a two-hundred-name ledger should never stand: in public, near authority, within reading distance of the gibbet they'd started building by the tollhouse. But Coral was somewhere in that crowd, and I wanted to see her face when she heard it, because I already knew what the paper said. I'd known since dawn, when a dragoon nailed it to the door with four blows of a hammer that sounded, in the frozen quiet, like someone knocking on a coffin.

*By order of Colonel Emilio Ferran, Military Governor of the River District:* the broadsheet began, in the ornate official hand that always reminded me of a priest trying to sell you something, *all legal proceedings predating the Reconciliation are hereby declared closed as of midnight, December the thirty-first.*

Closed. A tidy word. Closed the way you close a grave.

*All records pertaining to such proceedings shall be destroyed by fire. All names entered in said records shall be struck from civil memory. No testimony, written or spoken, relating to pre-Reconciliation matters shall hereafter be admissible before any tribunal, commission, or court of the restored Republic.*

Restored. That word was doing more work than any word I'd ever seen. The republic hadn't been restored; it had been embalmed and propped up at its own funeral, and now the funeral was getting a fireworks display.

Osta read it out clearly, twice, the way you'd read a menu to a man who couldn't afford anything on it. Then Ferran himself came out onto the bridge — the middle of it, right over the water, where the ice floes ground against the pilings like the river trying to chew its way free — and said perhaps forty words. I remember most of them.

"The past is a debt," he said. "On New Year's Eve, the Republic forgives it. Attend the celebration. Bring your children."

Then he smiled, the small satisfied smile of a man who has just solved a problem involving two hundred families and one match, and went back to whatever generals do indoors in December.

The crowd did what crowds do when a colonel smiles at them: they clapped, carefully, the way you clap with numb hands. And then they dispersed, quickly, each person suddenly very interested in being home before dark, and I watched the town's collective face do something I recognized from fifteen years of watching towns learn about armies. It wasn't fear exactly. Fear is hot. This was the cold arithmetic people do behind their eyes — *who among us is in those records?* — and I could see the question traveling from shopkeeper to ferryman to fishwife like smoke finding drafts.

Coral found me at the corner of the chandlery, or let me find her, which amounted to the same thing with her. She had her marketing basket with her, two onions in it, purchased purely for verisimilitude. She was better at this than I was, and it appalled me.

"You've read it," she said. Not a question.

"I helped draft the prototype." I kept my voice under the wind. "Three years before the war. Different province, same language. We called it the Closure Act. The idea was that a nation can't heal while it keeps picking at wounds."

"And do nations heal?"

"Nations don't have skin," I said. "People do. And people scar."

She looked back toward the bridge, where the soldiers were already taking down the reading platform but leaving the gibbet, which apparently was not considered temporary. Her breath came out in a thin white ribbon and tore away.

"Two hundred names," she said quietly. "And in nine days, it becomes illegal to say any of them."

"Worse than illegal. Pointless. That's the genius of it." I heard the bitterness come out of my mouth and didn't try to call it back. "Ferran doesn't need to hunt anyone. He just needs to wait. After midnight on the thirty-first, there's no proof of anything. The ledger stops being evidence and becomes kindling with ambitions. Every arrest warrant expires. Every testimony evaporates. A woman could stand in the courthouse square and shout her husband's name until spring, and legally, nothing would have happened to him. He'd never have existed."

"And the man who carried the messages?"

"Also never existed," I said. "Which, I confess, has been my ambition for some time. I simply planned to achieve it without legislation."

That earned me half a laugh, which I counted as a victory, because Coral Vane's laughter had been rationed since 1896 and I'd learned not to expect surplus.

* * *

We walked home separately, three minutes apart, the way you do, and I spent those three minutes doing sums I didn't like.

Here is what nobody tells you about being a courier: you spend years learning to think in deadlines. The dispatch must reach General Maro before he moves north. The pardon must reach Inspector Vellac before the morning session. Time is never weather, for a courier; it's terrain, and every hour is either a road or a wall. Fifteen years of retirement had rusted that machinery, but it still turned, and it turned now with a horrible smoothness.

Nine days. Nine days until the ledger became paper. And the paper knew it — that was the thing I couldn't shake, sitting that evening in Coral's kitchen with the false-backed drawer open in front of me and the ledger lying between us like a patient we couldn't diagnose. Two hundred names in her father's upright clerk's hand, dates, places, the quiet architecture of a network that had moved dissidents out of the country while the customs house stamped tar on their trail. Elias Vane had built all this under the army's nose and then died on a marsh road keeping it running, and I had run, and now his book sat on a kitchen table with an expiration date.

"It can't stay here," I said, for perhaps the sixth time that week. I want it noted that I was right every time, and that being right has never once made me useful.

"Then it goes across the bridge," Coral said, for perhaps the sixth time.

"To whom? The foreign consuls are down to two in this district — the Dutch trade attaché and the Sardinian consul, who is widely believed to be a wine merchant and possibly actually a wine merchant. Neither of them can do anything with a list of names."

"They can keep it," she said. "That's the whole point, Ansel. Not to use it. To keep it. So that someday, when somebody asks what happened to the Havel family, or the Ostrovs, or old Mistress Karel who taught piano to half this town, somebody can open a book and say: here. Here is what happened. They sheltered people. They were real. It happened."

I looked at her. There is a version of this story where I tell you I saw her father in her face just then, and it would be true, but not in the way stories usually mean it. What I saw was the same thing I'd seen in Elias Vane in 1894, when he took a stranger's papers out of my hand and said, *these people will be dead by Thursday unless someone carries this.* The same absolute refusal to accept that a fact, once known, could be put back down.

"Elias talked like that," I said.

"He got it from somewhere," said Coral, who had decided, somewhere in the last week, to stop pretending her father fell off a turnip wagon into conspiracy.

* * *

The trouble with a deadline made of law rather than logistics is that you can't outrun it, bribe it, or lose it in a crowd. A general's timetable bends. Midnight on the thirty-first does not. Somewhere in the capital, presumably, clerks were already preparing the burning — I imagined the records office in the district seat, crates being stacked in a yard, kerosene requisitioned, an officer assigned to watch the fire and sign a form confirming that the past was now, officially, over. There would be a form. There was always a form. I had signed enough of them.

And the cruelty of it — I kept coming back to this, turning it over like a coin with only one face — the cruelty was the *amnesty*. That was the word on the broadsheet. Amnesty. Forgiveness. As if the crime had been committed by the hunted. As if two hundred families needed forgiving for the offense of having been sheltered by a customs clerk, and the proper penance was oblivion. I have served institutions my whole life, first the Republic's foreign service, then its shadow, then nobody, and I can report with professional authority that the worst thing institutions do is not cruelty. Cruelty at least admits the victim exists. The worst thing is erasure dressed as mercy. Burn the records and call it healing. Strike the names and call it peace. Put a ribbon on the mass grave and hold a parade.

Ferran announced there would be a parade, too. New Year's Eve, dusk, the bridge illuminated with lanterns, one ceremonial crossing open to inspected traffic, fireworks over the water at midnight — *at which hour,* the supplementary notice said, in smaller type, *the closure of all pre-Reconciliation matters takes full effect.* They had scheduled it. Fireworks at the stroke of the pen. Somebody in the Colonel's staff had a sense of composition, and I hoped whoever it was choked on it.

* * *

Coral spent the next day doing something that frightened me more than the proclamation had: she was calm. She baked bread for the soldiers billeted upstairs — Lieutenant Osta and three men who ate like a slow disaster — and she carried the loaf up with the serenity of a woman whose conscience contains no ledgers whatsoever, and she came back down and sat across from me and said, "We'll need the coal shipment."

"We will not need the coal shipment. The coal shipment goes through the barge inspection at Miller's Reach, where Sergeant Brandt's men break open one crate in five with an axe and their consciences, and—"

"Not the coal shipment," she said patiently, in the tone of a schoolteacher who has decided not to fail you yet. "Something *like* it. Something boring. Ansel, listen to me. You've been planning this like a courier. Dispatches, routes, speed. But this isn't dispatches. It's a book. Books don't need to be fast. They need to be uninteresting."

I opened my mouth to explain the seventeen ways a river checkpoint could make a book interesting, and closed it again, because through the ceiling came the sound of Lieutenant Osta's boots crossing the floor above, stopping directly overhead, and staying there. Listening. Or just standing. With Osta you couldn't tell, and I suspected that was the point of him.

Coral and I looked at each other across the table, and neither of us said another word for a full minute, and in that minute I understood something about the house I'd been sleeping in. It had been built for this. The silences in it weren't empty. They were load-bearing.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was standard, so I did what I do on such nights: I inventoried. Old habit. Satchel contents, exits, debts, lies outstanding. The inventory went badly, because everything kept resolving into the same column.

Item: nine days. Item: one ledger, two hundred names, currently worth less every day it sits in a drawer, and worth precisely nothing after midnight on the thirty-first. Item: three ways out of town — the north road, where Corporal Deng's checkpoint had grown from two sentries to six and acquired a mirror-on-a-pole for checking wagon undersides, which showed either impressive initiative or a nervous superior; the river, where the barge inspections had gone from cursory to thorough after someone in headquarters read a manual; and the bridge. The bridge was Ferran's showpiece, lantern-hung, sergeant-posted, and closed to everything except the army's own traffic and the occasional inspected cart, and the sergeant he'd chosen for it was named Ivo Rask, and I had recognized the name off the requisition list the way you recognize a scar in a mirror.

Rask. Fifteen years ago I'd carried a pardon north through snow with a bullet graze on my shoulder and delivered it ninety minutes before a firing squad's breakfast, and the man it saved was Rask's brother, though Rask didn't know I knew, and I had spent fifteen years hoping never to stand in front of a man whose family I'd saved and whose duty required shooting me. The Republic has a sense of humor. It's just a very bad one, and it never explains the joke.

Item, final: one retired courier, fifty-three years old, bad knee, worse conscience, carrying a satchel of dispatches he'd stopped believing in somewhere around the second war and a dead man's book he'd started believing in entirely too much.

Outside, the river grumbled under its ice. Somewhere north, in the district records yard, the crates were stacking up. Somewhere in this house, two floors up, Lieutenant Osta was presumably asleep, or awake, and there was no meaningful difference in the danger.

Nine days. I lay in the dark and did the only mathematics that mattered. After midnight on the thirty-first, the ledger would be paper. Before it, it had to be history — carried out, handed over, kept alive somewhere beyond the reach of matches. And between here and there stood three checkpoints, one suspicious lieutenant sleeping above my head, and a sergeant at the bridge who owed me his brother and his oath to the same government.

In nine days the Republic would forgive the past.

I intended to be rude enough to refuse.

It was somewhere around three in the morning, somewhere between that resolution and actual sleep, that I heard Osta's boots cross the floor above again — unhurried, deliberate, stopping at the top of the stairs — and then begin, slowly, to descend.
