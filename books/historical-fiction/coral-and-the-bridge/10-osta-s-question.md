## 10. Osta's Question

Lieutenant Osta sent for me at ten in the morning, which I took as a courtesy. Men who intend to arrest you do not send for you at a civilized hour; they come at dawn with mud on their boots and their buttons undone. Ten o'clock meant he wanted something from me first. It meant he thought I had something to give.

I dressed carefully for it — the grey wool dress, the plain collar, my mother's brooch pinned just so. Not mourning, exactly. Respectability. A woman who dresses like an accusation invites one; a woman who dresses like a widow of good standing gets offered tea before she gets offered handcuffs. Fifteen years of dusting my father's ledgers and pouring sherry for the customs inspector had taught me that much: authority is mostly costume, on both sides of the desk.

The requisitioned parlor of the Meridian Hotel had been arranged into an office overnight. Someone had carried in a desk too fine for a lieutenant — mahogany, brass corners, probably looted from the records office along with everything else Ferran's men hadn't burned yet. Osta sat behind it with a single lamp lit against the grey morning, though the window was right there, and a cup of coffee he wasn't drinking.

"Coral Vane," he said, without looking up from a folder. "Daughter of Edvard Vane, customs clerk, deceased fifteen years this March."

"You have my condolences backwards," I said. "But yes."

He looked up then. He was younger than his rank suggested — thirty perhaps, with the kind of face that would stay boyish until it suddenly didn't, and eyes that did all the aging in advance. He gestured to the chair across from him. I sat. He did not offer me coffee. Of course he didn't; the coffee was a prop, not a hospitality.

"Your father held the customs post at Coral's Bridge for twenty-two years," Osta said. "Impressive tenure. In that time, by the office's own tallies, he processed—" he turned a page, delicately, like a man turning the page of a hymnal— "eleven thousand four hundred and six shipments. Do you know what strikes me about that number, Miss Vane?"

"That you counted?"

A flicker. Not quite a smile. "That it is *small*. A river town, a bridge crossing, a barge route south — and your father cleared fewer shipments per year than a landlocked depot half this size. Either Coral's Bridge was the sleepiest crossing in the republic, or someone was not writing everything down."

"The war emptied the river," I said. "You know that better than I. Half those years there was nothing on the water but reeds and rumor."

"Half," Osta agreed. "Which leaves the other half." He drew three papers from the folder and laid them side by side on the desk, squaring their edges with two fingers, a small fussy gesture that I would later understand was his version of a priest arranging the elements. "I've been reading your father's files. Forgive me — I know he's dead, and I know it's indelicate. But dead men keep excellent books, and yours kept *very* interesting ones."

*Yours.* As if the ledgers had passed to me like the china. Perhaps they had. That was rather the point of the morning.

"Item one." He tapped the leftmost paper. "In eleven years of records, your father logged exactly forty-one seizures. Forty-one, out of eleven thousand shipments. The regional average is one seizure per hundred. Your father's rate is one per two hundred and seventy-eight. Either he was miraculously bad at finding contraband, or miraculously uninterested."

"He was a careful man," I said. "He preferred talking to searching. Searching costs hours; a word in the right ear costs nothing."

"A man after my own heart," said Osta, and I understood that he was being pleasant the way a cat is pleasant to a bird it has decided not to eat *yet*. "Item two." Tap. "His duty roster. Customs men rotate postings every five years — regulation. Your father applied for and received six consecutive waivers. Six. Waivers require a sponsor in the capital. Who sponsors a minor customs clerk in a river town for eighteen years, Miss Vane?"

"I wouldn't know. I was a child, and then I was a daughter, and daughters are not told about sponsors."

"No," he said softly. "They're told about silences. I've noticed this town runs on them." He let that sit between us like a third person at the table. Then, tap. "Item three. This is my favorite."

He turned the last paper around so I could read it. A shipping manifest, dated the autumn before the war ended — the autumn before my father died. My father's own hand at the bottom, the small precise script I knew better than my own name. And beside it, in the column where the cargo should be itemized: *household goods, assorted, 14 crates.*

"Fourteen crates of household goods," Osta said, "on a barge bound downriver, consigned to a widow in the capital whose house, according to the municipal rolls, contained exactly one room. Fourteen crates, Miss Vane. What does a one-room widow do with fourteen crates of household goods?"

"Burns them for warmth, if the winter was cold enough."

This time he did smile, briefly, and it was worse than the not-smiling. "You're quick. I'd hoped you would be. The dull ones take all day and confess to things they didn't do, and the clever ones take all day and confess to things they did, and neither sort is any use to me." He gathered the papers back into the folder with that same liturgical neatness. "So let us skip to the part where we are honest with each other, because I have very little time and you have somewhat less."

My hands were in my lap. I made sure of it, because I wanted them somewhere I could feel them, and because a trembling hand on a table is a confession written in a language every soldier reads fluently. Terror, I was discovering, is not the absence of poise. It is the fuel of it. Every nerve I had was singing, and I simply chose to conduct the choir instead of joining it.

"Lieutenant," I said, "if you believe my father smuggled something during the war, I can't help you. He was a clerk. He came home at six, he ate at seven, he read until nine, and he died doing none of those things, which was the only irregularity of his life. Whatever discrepancies you're finding in fifteen-year-old paper, you'll find them in any office in this country that survived the war. Books burn badly and get rewritten worse."

"Mm," said Osta. He rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at the bridge — our bridge, gray in the drizzle, with Sergeant Rask's men posted at either end like bookends holding up nothing. "Do you know what I admire about this town? Everyone keeps saying *the war*, as though it were weather. *The war took the barges. The war emptied the ledgers. The war killed your father.* But wars don't kill clerks, Miss Vane. People do. And people don't waive a clerk's rotation for eighteen years out of affection." He turned back. "Someone in the capital found your father useful. I intend to find out who, and what he was used *for*, and whether the arrangement survived him."

"And you think I know."

"I think," Osta said, returning to the desk but not sitting, leaning instead with both hands flat on the mahogany, "that you have spent fifteen years performing the role of a customs clerk's daughter so convincingly that even you have started to believe it. I think you dust that office the way other women tend graves. And I think that when Colonel Ferran sealed the records office three days ago, you were the only person in this town who looked *afraid* rather than merely annoyed." He straightened. "Fear is data, Miss Vane. It's the most honest thing anyone's given me since I arrived."

"I was afraid," I agreed, "because soldiers were going through my father's things. You'd be afraid too. It isn't guilt, Lieutenant. It's property."

"It's both," he said pleasantly. "It's usually both."

* * *

He let me go at noon, which surprised me, and then he didn't let me go at all, which didn't. Two soldiers walked me home through streets gone quiet in that particular way towns go quiet when everyone is listening through their own walls. At the customs house door, Private Henn — the young one, the one who blushed when I caught him staring at the portrait of my father — actually tipped his cap, and I hated how much comfort I took from the gesture of a boy holding a rifle in my hallway.

Lunch was served at one. I cooked it myself, because a woman who cooks her own meals under occupation is a woman with nothing to hide, and also because I did not trust Henn near my stove. Ansel ate in silence across from me, and I narrated the interview for him in the low, level voice I use for reporting weather, while inside me the whole performance sat like swallowed glass.

Osta had laid out his three cards, and I had played mine, and the terrible thing — the thing I could not say aloud at my own table with soldiers' ears in the walls — was that I had *enjoyed* it. Not the fear. The craft. When he asked about the fourteen crates, some muscle in me I had never trained answered for me before I could think, smooth and dry as oiled leather. *Burns them for warmth.* Where had that come from? Who taught me to lie with that particular economy?

And then I knew, sitting over cold soup, and the knowing went through me like cold water through cloth.

My father had. Every silence in that house had been a lesson. Every question I'd learned not to ask, every year I'd learned not to wonder why we never visited his colleagues in the capital, every Christmas when the gifts arrived without cards from people whose names he never spoke — I had been drilled for fifteen years by a man who never once raised his voice or gave an order. He had built me the way he built his false-backed drawer: patiently, invisibly, against a day he hoped would never come. I had spent my adulthood resenting him for the silences. It turned out the silences were the inheritance. The ledgers were only the deed.

"You played it well," Ansel murmured, so low the words barely crossed the table.

"I lied," I said, equally low. "Fluently. To an officer of the army, with soldiers in my kitchen, and the worst part is that it *worked*, and the second-worst part is that I want to do it again tomorrow." I set down my spoon. "Do you understand what that means? I spent fifteen years being honest about a lie. Now I'm lying about the truth and I'm *good* at it. My father would be proud. That's the part I can't forgive."

Ansel looked at me for a long moment. There was something in his face I couldn't name — recognition, maybe, or its cousin, grief. "He'd be proud," he said finally. "And he'd tell you the trick is not to enjoy it. The moment you enjoy it, you start believing your own lies, and then you're no use to anyone, least of all yourself."

"And which are you? Useful, or enjoying it?"

"Ask me again after New Year's Eve," he said, and went back to his soup, and I let him, because the private was refilling the water jug and some sentences are only safe in a language the walls don't speak.

* * *

Osta came himself at dusk. No escort — just the lieutenant, hatless, rain in his hair, standing on my step with a folder under his arm like a neighbor come to borrow sugar. Henn hovered behind him, miserable.

"Forgive the hour," Osta said. "I find evening interviews go better. People's defenses are tired. So are mine, for what it's worth."

"Then we'll both be inefficient," I said, and stepped aside, because refusing entry to a billeting officer is a right I did not have and he knew I did not have, and we both preferred the fiction that I was choosing.

He did not sit in the parlor. He walked it. He looked at the portrait of my father — the official one, the respectable one, painted the year I turned nine — and he looked at the display case of customs seals and ceremonial keys, and he looked at the shelf of ledgers, spines uncracked, dusted weekly, and I watched him read the room the way I'd watched him read his files: thoroughly, and with growing enjoyment.

"Your father's study," he said. "May I?"

"It's been searched twice already."

"By soldiers," he said. "Soldiers search. I *read*." 

He went in ahead of me, uninvited past the threshold, and stood in the middle of the room where my father's desk still faced the window, and for a moment he simply breathed, and I understood that this was theater too — his, this time. Everything with Osta was an interview. The walking was questions. The pausing was questions. The man had turned rudeness into a science and called it method.

"Ferran wants this town closed by New Year's Eve," he said conversationally, to the window. "Records burned, cases closed, amnesty expired. Clean slate. Very tidy. I'm sure you've heard."

"I hear fireworks are planned."

"Fireworks, yes." He turned. "Here is my difficulty, Miss Vane. I am a thorough man serving an impatient colonel. Ferran wants the past *gone*; I want it *understood*. We make an uneasy pair. Because I keep finding these—" he produced a paper from his folder, and my stomach dropped before I saw it was only the manifest again, the fourteen crates, my father's hand— "and each one is nothing. Each one, alone, is a sleepy clerk and a slow river. But there are dozens of them, and dozens of nothings begin to look like a something, and a something in a town with a bridge and a marsh and a consulate barge twice a month begins to look like a *route*." He set the paper on my father's desk, precisely squared. "So I am going to write to the capital tonight and request the full personnel file of Edvard Vane. His sponsors. His waivers. His commendations, if any. And when it comes — post takes four days, five with the roads as they are — I will know exactly what kind of man kept your books."

"Then you'll have your answer and leave us in peace."

"Oh, I expect the answer will be *interesting*," Osta said. "Interesting answers rarely bring peace. They bring more questions." He collected his hat from the chair where he hadn't sat. At the door he paused, and for one unguarded instant the boyishness fell away entirely and I saw the thing underneath, which was not cruelty — cruelty would have been simpler — but appetite. Pure, patient, scholarly appetite. "One more thing, Miss Vane. The courier who used to run this river — the one the old circulars call the Wren. Fifteen years, and no name, no face, no grave. If a man like that existed, someone in this town fed him, hid him, paid him. Someone's father, perhaps." He settled the hat on his head. "When my file arrives, I'll know whether to ask you about your father's business." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Or about his *friends*."

He left. Henn lingered a half-second too long, and I saw the boy's eyes flick toward the study door and away, and I filed that away too — everyone was filing things away now, the whole town had become a drawer of false backs.

I waited until the boots faded on the lane. Then I went to the study, and I took the fourteen-crate manifest off the desk, and I held my father's handwriting in the lamplight for a long time.

Five days, Osta had said. Five days until the capital's answer came, and the amnesty died with the year, and every road out of this town funneled through one inspected crossing at dusk. I put the paper down with a steady hand — steady, always steady now, the lie living in my muscles whether I willed it or not — and went to find Ansel, because whatever we were going to do, we had five days to do it, and I had just learned the most dangerous thing about myself:

I was no longer afraid of lying.

I was afraid of how much I liked it.
