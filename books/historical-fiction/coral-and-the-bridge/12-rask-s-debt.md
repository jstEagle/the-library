## 12. Rask's Debt

The trouble with a long memory is that it keeps long accounts. I had spent fifteen years trying to forget that I kept one at all.

Sergeant Ivo Rask. That was the name on my posting orders, on my pay book, on the requisition manifests stacked on my desk in the customs house annex like slates waiting to be written on. A clean name, as army names go. It had never been attached to anything glorious and never to anything shameful, which is the most a man of my talents can hope for. I arrested smugglers. I inspected barges. I stood at the bridge in the cold and looked at faces and forgot them by supper. Fifteen years of forgetting, done professionally.

And then, on the second Tuesday of the occupation, Ansel Sandoval walked past my checkpoint with his collar up and his gait carefully ordinary, and fifteen years of forgetting came due all at once, with interest.

* * *

You should understand something about wanted circulars: they lie. Not in what they say — the description was accurate enough, down to the scar through the left eyebrow, which I could see even from twenty paces in bad light — but in what they can't say. A circular gives you a face. It cannot give you a walk, a way of tilting the head when pretending to read a posted notice, a particular patience in the hands. The capital's artists are competent. They drew me a face, and for fifteen years I checked faces against it, hundreds of faces, ferry men and priests and one unfortunate taxidermist from upriver who bore an uncanny resemblance and bore a grudge ever after. None of them were him. Then a courier in a plain coat crossed my bridge in a town small enough that no stranger had any honest business in it, and I knew him the way you know a song from three notes.

I let him pass. I want that recorded somewhere, though there is nowhere to record it. I looked at the face from the circular and I let it walk off my bridge into the snow, and I went back to stamping manifests, and my hands did not shake, because Ivo Rask's hands do not shake, whatever else may be said of them.

What I did not expect — what no reasonable man would have expected — was that Lieutenant Osta would come knocking at my door three days later holding a telegram like a man holding a live coal he intended to hand to someone else.

* * *

"The capital," Osta said, "is being cooperative."

He had come to the annex at dusk, when the clerks had gone and the stove had burned down to a sullen orange. He did not sit. Osta never sat if standing made him taller, and he was a man who needed every centimeter of advantage he could manufacture. He laid the telegram on my desk and smoothed it flat with two fingers, the way a dealer lays out a card he already knows will win.

"Cooperative," I said. It seemed safe.

"I've requested the pre-war courier files. The Sandoval network." He said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease you were proud of diagnosing. "Ferran believes — and I concur — that the man who ran those dispatches may have returned to the republic. This town has a history, Sergeant. Your bridge sits on top of it."

"My bridge sits on top of a river," I said.

For a moment I thought he would smile. He didn't. That was worse. "The files arrive within the week. There's a circular among them, an original, from the year '96. You'll review it. You'll review all of it. If any face in this town corresponds to any face in those files—" He tapped the telegram once, softly, like a knock on a coffin lid. "—you'll bring it to me before you bring it to anyone. Including the colonel. Do you understand the order of operations, Sergeant?"

"I understand you want my paperwork first."

"I want your eyes first," Osta said. "Paperwork is what happens afterward." And he took his coal and went out into the dark, leaving me alone with a stove and fifteen years of arithmetic.

* * *

Here is the sum, since I have clearly reached the age where a man does his sums whether he wants to or not.

In the winter of '96, my brother Tomas was nineteen and stupid in the specific way that gets boys hanged. He carried pamphlets. Not weapons, not codes, not anything that mattered — pamphlets, printed on a hand press in a cellar, arguing that generals should not run countries, a position history has since treated generously and Tomas treated recklessly, distributing them in garrison towns, of all the cursed places. He was caught outside Vellmar with forty copies in his coat. Forty copies. Enough pamphlets to hang a boy twice over.

The trial was going to be brief. The trials were always brief then. My mother wrote to me — I was already in uniform, three years in, already practiced at looking at faces and forgetting them — and her letter said only: *he is your brother.* As if I might have forgotten. As if a man forgets the person who taught him to swim by pushing him off the ferry landing.

I did what soldiers do, which is write up the chain of command, and the chain of command did what chains do, which is nothing, link after link after link. And then, because I had nothing left, I did the thing a soldier should not do. I wrote to a name my cousin the printer let fall once over brandy. A courier. No address, no description. Just a name and a prayer, and I sent it into the dark the way you throw a coin into a well, without expecting water.

Ten days before the trial, a pardon arrived at the Vellmar garrison. Proper seals, proper signatures, the whole architecture of legitimacy — and no record, ever after, of who had signed it or why. The clerks shrugged. The commandant, who had better instincts than he was given credit for, burned his correspondence and released my brother. Tomas came home thin and frightened and alive, and eventually stupid again in safer ways, and today he runs a bakery two provinces away and names his loaves after his children. That is what the courier bought him. That is what Ansel Sandoval bought him, though I did not know his name then; the circular came later, in the files they gave us during the purges, and I matched the story to the name and kept both locked where I keep things.

A debt like that doesn't gather dust. It gathers interest. Every year Tomas baked bread, the debt grew. Every year my brother's children learned letters, the debt grew. And now the man himself was walking past my checkpoint with his collar up, and the lieutenant wanted the original circular on my desk within the week, and the whole account was coming due at once, in a currency I wasn't sure I had.

* * *

I found him at the lockhouse.

Not by cleverness. By subtraction. A stranger in Coral's Bridge must sleep somewhere, eat somewhere, be someone, and the town was small enough that subtraction did the work of a dozen informants. The Vane customs house had soldiers billeted in it, so not there. The inn had Osta's clerk drinking in it every evening with the persistence of a leaky roof, so not there. Which left the lockhouse on the marsh road, rented in the name of a shipping agent who did not exist, and I knew this because checking nonexistent shipping agents against registries is the sort of work I am actually good at.

I came at dusk, alone, unarmed, which in my trade is either suicide or diplomacy depending on how it goes. The door opened on a chain, and one eye, and the scar above the eyebrow doing its documented work in the lamplight.

"Sergeant," Ansel Sandoval said. He didn't sound afraid. He sounded tired, the way a man sounds when the knock finally comes and he's grateful to stop waiting for it.

"You know who I am."

"You're the sergeant at the bridge. You let me cross." The chain stayed on. "I've been wondering why."

"So have I," I said, "every night since. May I come in, or shall we hold our conversation through a gap wide enough for a knife?"

He thought about the knife comment longer than a comfortable host would have. Then the chain came off, and I stepped into a room that smelled of lamp oil and cold tea, and he sat down across from me with the table between us like a sensible man, and we looked at each other across it the way two debts look at each other.

"Winter of '96," I said. "Vellmar garrison. A boy named Tomas Rask, forty pamphlets in his coat, trial set for the tenth of December." I let that sit. "Someone arranged a pardon. The signature was never traced. I've spent fifteen years half-hoping it never would be, in case the truth was smaller than the mystery."

"And now?"

"Now the truth walked onto my bridge wearing its own face from a wanted circular, and I find the mystery has gotten larger, not smaller." I leaned forward. "Because here is what I cannot make fit, courier. The man in the files was a legend. Eleven years of routes, eleven years of dispatches, and the army never once caught him, never once turned him, never once even confirmed his handwriting. Men like that don't come back. Men like that retire to some coastal town and drink themselves respectable. So why does Ansel Sandoval return, in winter, to the one town in the republic that still smells of him?"

"That," he said quietly, "is a dangerous question."

"Everything worth asking is. Answer it anyway."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh me — courier against sergeant, ledger against ledger — and I understood suddenly, in my stomach, that he was deciding what I was. Not what I knew. What I *was*. And that the answer mattered to him in ways I couldn't yet count.

"There's a woman here," he said finally. "The daughter of the man who trained me. And there's a thing in this town that belongs to two hundred families, and the army means to burn it at midnight on New Year's Eve, and I mean to see that it isn't burned." He spread his empty hands. "That's the whole of it, Sergeant. No conspiracy. No network risen from the dead. One old courier and one impossible errand."

"A thing that belongs to two hundred families." I turned that over. Names, then. A ledger. I had requisitioned enough records offices to know what Ferran's men did with ledgers, and it began and ended with fire. "And Osta has requested the original files from the capital. Courier routes, safe houses, the '96 circular with your face on it. Within the week, Sergeant Rask — myself included — will be studying everything the republic ever knew about you." I stood, because sitting felt too much like being comfortable, and comfort was a thing I'd forfeited on my own bridge. "When those files arrive, I won't be able to pretend I don't recognize you. Recognition becomes duty. Duty becomes paper. Paper becomes a firing squad, and I've seen the arithmetic of firing squads, courier, and it never comes out in the prisoner's favor."

"So this is the arrest," he said. Almost mild. As if I'd told him it might rain.

"This is the opposite of an arrest, and God help me for saying so." I got my hat from where I'd dropped it inside the door. "Two days. The files take four days from the capital if the line is clear, five if it snows, and Osta's telegraph clerk drinks, so call it five. In two days I will come back here, or you will come to me, and you will tell me one thing. Not where the ledger is. Not who's carrying it. Just this: that the man who saved my brother was worth saving. That he spent the years between then and now being the thing the pamphlets said men could be, instead of the thing the generals proved they were. Because I have served the republic for eighteen years, courier, and I no longer know what I serve, and I need — do you understand? I *need* — to believe that once, in the winter of '96, my uniform did one clean thing."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the marsh wind came off the ice with a sound like tearing linen, and somewhere toward the bridge a patrol boot crunched and faded.

"And if I'm not?" he asked. "If I'm just a tired man running one last errand?"

"Then in two days I do my duty," I said, "and I arrest you, and I hand Osta everything, and I write to my brother and tell him the pardon came from a coward who got lucky." I put on my hat. "But I don't think you're going to tell me that. I watched you cross my bridge. A man running a mere errand walks faster and looks guiltier. You walked like a man carrying something heavier than paper — like a man who's already decided how the errand ends and is only negotiating with the hour."

At the door I stopped, because there was one more thing, and debts demand their full enumeration.

"New Year's Eve," I said, without turning around. "Ferran closes the bridge at noon. One inspected crossing at dusk, and it will be a trap baited so obviously that only desperate men will step in it. Which tells me desperate men are exactly who it's built for." Now I turned. He hadn't moved from the table. His face was doing something disciplined and expensive. "I'm telling you this as a warning, which makes me a traitor twice over — once for '96 and once tonight. So spend me well, courier. Two days. And when you decide how this ends, remember there's a sergeant at the third checkpoint who owes you a brother, and who would very much like, before all accounts are closed, to know which side of history his debt landed on."

The wind took my words sideways off the lockhouse porch. Behind the lamplit window, Ansel Sandoval sat very still, the way men sit when a clock they'd been ignoring has begun to audibly tick.

I walked back along the marsh road in the dark, and the snow started around the second bend, soft and indifferent, covering my tracks behind me the way it covers everything, and I thought: five days until the files, two days until my answer, twelve days until midnight on New Year's Eve, and somewhere in that narrowing arithmetic, a town of silences was about to get loud.

I only hoped that when it did, I would recognize the sound.
