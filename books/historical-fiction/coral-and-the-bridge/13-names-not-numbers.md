## 13. Names, Not Numbers

Ansel slept in the coal cellar with the ledger under his head like a pillow he didn't trust, and I lay awake in my own bed listening to Lieutenant Osta's boots cross the floor above me — four steps to the window, four steps back, the metronome of a man who could not sleep either and wanted everyone to know it.

I had told myself for fifteen years that my father was a customs clerk. I had said it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones, and I had built a whole life on top of them the way you build a house on a riverbank, telling yourself the water is far away.

The ledger sat one floor below me, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was not far away at all.

I got up past two in the morning, when Osta's boots finally stopped, and went down to the cellar with a candle stub and the stubbornness that was apparently the only thing my father had ever left me outright.

* * *

Ansel woke the way soldiers wake — all at once, hand already moving — and then saw it was me and made himself relax by visible effort, which I found oddly touching.

"You should be asleep," he said.

"You should have told me years ago."

"I only learned what was in the house three days ago."

"You knew what my father *was* fifteen years ago."

He had no answer for that, which was fair, because there wasn't one. He sat up against the coal bins, gray-faced, ankle propped on a crate, and watched me set the candle down between us like a lamp on an examination table.

"Then read it," he said quietly. "If you're going to carry it, you should know what it weighs."

I unwrapped the oilcloth. The ledger was exactly what it appeared to be from the outside — a customs book, columns for tonnage and duty, dates going back twenty years. My father's handwriting marched down the margins in his small, upright script, the same script that had written my name on my schoolbooks and *flour, sugar, tea* on the household lists. That was the obscenity of it. It was the same handwriting. There had never been two men.

Ansel turned the pages for me at first, showing me where to look. The entries were innocent alone. *Barque Meridian, 40 tons, salt fish, duty paid.* But in the margin beside certain entries ran small marks — a slanted stroke, a circle, sometimes a number — and beside those marks, folded into the columns like weeds in a wheat field, were names.

Not numbers. Names. Full names, home streets, dates of arrival and departure, and in some cases a single word of disposition: *passed*, *held*, *gone north*.

I started at the beginning because I am my father's daughter and I do things in order even when order is a form of self-harm.

The first name stopped my finger before I'd read a full line.

*Marta Ellery.*

My godmother. Marta Ellery, who had taught me to whistle through a blade of grass on the ferry landing, who sent a ham every Christmas for eleven years after my mother died because she understood that grief is mostly a problem of meals. Her entry read: *M. Ellery — harbored V. Kessler & son, 6 nights, Oct. Disposition: passed south. Cost her the back room all winter.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the words kept sliding off, refusing to mean what they plainly said. Marta Ellery had hidden people. In the back room. The back room where I had been told the damp ruined everything and we must never store linens there. I had napped in that room as a child, on a pallet Marta laid out herself, while somewhere below or behind or around us the machinery of my father's other life turned over.

"Coral," Ansel said softly.

"Don't." I turned the page.

*Joren Hale, ferryman.* Joren, who let me steer the ferry across on Sundays when the current was lazy, who called me "the admiral" and saluted with the pole. His entries ran down three separate years. He hadn't just ferried cargo. He had ferried people — twelve listed by name, and after each, the same notation in my father's hand: *no charge. Refuses payment. Says the river owes him.*

"The river owes him," I repeated aloud, and heard my voice crack on the second word, and hated myself for it, and kept reading.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about denial: it isn't ignorance. Denial is a full-time job with excellent benefits, and I had held the position for fifteen years without missing a shift. Every oddity in my childhood I had processed and filed. Father's late meetings — customs business, tariffs don't keep banker's hours. The strangers who stayed a night and left before dawn — commercial travelers, tired men. The year the back room smelled of boiled linen and iodine — Marta's rheumatism cure, which involved neither.

I had not been blind. I had been *employed*. And now, sitting on a coal crate at three in the morning with a candle guttering, I was being handed my termination papers, itemized.

Because here was the thing, the thing that burned worse than betrayal: the names were kind. If the ledger had held strangers, criminals, fanatics — if my father had been running guns or printing treason — I could have filed that too, filed it under *father was someone else, father lied to everyone equally*. But the names were my schoolteacher. Old Mistress Pava, who taught me penmanship and once kept me after class not for punishment but because she'd noticed I hadn't eaten. Her entry: *Pava, E. — sheltered the Aldous girls, 9 days. Taught them letters while in hiding so they would lose no schooling. Requested nothing.*

She had taught fugitive children their letters in her own kitchen while the republic hunted them, and requested nothing, and I had spent my girlhood learning penmanship from her and never once asked why a woman of her quality lived above a chandlery and took in mending.

Page after page. The doctor who wrote false fever certificates — Dr. Ilan, who lanced my ear infection when I was nine and told me jokes the whole time so I wouldn't cry. The widow Brenner whose attic held eleven people over two winters. The constable — *the constable* — who logged false patrol routes so the searches went upriver instead of down. Two hundred and six names in total, and I counted, because counting was something to do with my hands while the rest of me came apart.

And threaded through all of it, appearing and reappearing like a stitch through cloth, was my father himself. Not as a clerk. As the hub. Every route came through Coral's Bridge; every route came through him. He was the reason the town had escaped the war for so long — not luck, not obscurity, but a man at a desk performing dullness so convincingly that even his own daughter believed the performance.

"He was good," Ansel said. He had been silent for an hour, letting me read, which I suspect cost him more than talking would have. "At this. The best I ever carried for."

"How long did you know him?"

"Eleven years. From the time I was nineteen." He looked at the candle rather than at me. "He trained me. Routes, ciphers, how to walk through a checkpoint like a bored man. Everything I know that kept me alive came out of this house."

"And you never told me."

"He forbade it. Said you were the one clean thing in his life. Said—" Ansel stopped.

"Said what?"

"Said that if the day ever came when you had to know, it would mean he'd failed. That the whole point of the work was that you'd get to grow up ordinary." He turned a page gently, as if the paper might bruise. "He failed, then. Or the world did. Depends on how you keep accounts."

I kept accounts the way my father had taught me, without knowing he was teaching me: precisely, and without mercy.

* * *

Toward dawn I reached the last pages, and there I found the thing I will not repeat to anyone, though I suppose I am repeating it now, to you, whoever you are, since these chapters have to go somewhere.

There was an entry dated the week before my father died. My father died in the flood season, officially — drowned inspecting a mooring, swept off the landing, a clerk's banal end. The ledger's final entry said otherwise. It listed a party of seven being moved north, and beside it, in my father's hand: *Ferran's men watching the north road. I'll take this run myself. A. carries the dispatches south — too valuable to risk both.*

A. Ansel. My father had split the cargo — put the dangerous, hunted human cargo on himself and sent the boy courier away with papers. He had chosen the harder crossing for himself, deliberately, and sent Ansel south, and Ansel had come back to find the river high and the man gone, and had carried that arithmetic for fifteen years without anyone to check his sums.

I closed the ledger.

"Well," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone older, someone who had recently read two hundred and six names. "You didn't abandon him. He ordered you out. You've spent half your life apologizing for obeying him."

"It's more complicated—"

"It's never more complicated. That's the lie complicated people tell." I stood, and my knees complained, and I ignored them. "He chose. He always chose. He chose to run the network, he chose to hide it from me, he chose to take that last party north, and he chose to send you away so you'd live. The only person in this whole ledger who gets any credit for consistency is him."

Ansel looked up at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — something between grief and awe, as if I'd grown a second head and the second head was my father's.

"What are you saying?" he asked.

I picked up the ledger and held it against my chest, and felt its weight honestly for the first time. Not the weight of paper. The weight of two hundred and six people who had been kind to a fatherless girl, and had done it at the cost of their safety, and had never once asked her to notice.

"I'm saying I've read it cover to cover, and I know every name in it now, and I am not going to forget a single one. So listen to me, Ansel Sandoval, and listen carefully, because I have spent fifteen years practicing silence and I'm finished with the position." I set the ledger down on the coal crate between us, flat, like a contract. "If your plans fail — if the barge fails, if the marshes fail, if they catch you at that bridge — I will carry this myself. Across the water, into the consulate, into whatever comes after. I don't need your routes and I don't need your permission. I grew up in a smuggler's station and didn't know it, which means I can pass through any checkpoint in this country, because I've been passing through my whole life."

The candle had nearly drowned itself. Upstairs, faintly, Osta's boots began again — four steps, four steps back.

Ansel studied me for a long moment. "Your father used to say almost exactly that," he said finally. "Word for word, near enough. Right before he'd do something reckless."

"Then you already know how this family negotiates."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, because through the cellar's high window, gray with first light, we both saw it: smoke rising from the direction of the river yards, and beneath it, the sound of the army's steam winches waking up — and the low horn of a coal barge taking on cargo at the downstream dock, bound for the river inspection, bound for the second checkpoint.

Ansel was on his feet before his bad ankle agreed to it, swearing softly.

"That's the Meridian's coal shipment," he said. "It wasn't supposed to load until Thursday."

Down at the docks, someone was loading early, and neither of us had told them to.
