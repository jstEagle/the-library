## 14. The Barge Gambit

The Meridian's captain was a man named Hollis, who had run coal downriver since before the war and had run other things downriver since after it, which was the more profitable trade by exactly the margin that kept him willing to do it.

Ansel found him at the downstream dock with his coat open to the cold and his hands buried in his armpits, watching four stevedores shovel black mountains into his hold as though someone had lit a fire under them, which in a sense someone had — the fire being the news that Colonel Ferran's requisition officers would be auditing every dock manifest by Friday.

"Nobody told you to load early," Ansel said, by way of greeting.

Hollis didn't look at him. "Nobody had to. Word comes down the river faster than the river does. Ferran's men are going to start weighing cargo. A man loads early so he's gone before they start weighing."

"And if I told you there was one more item for your hold? Small. Sealed. Wrapped in oilcloth."

"Then I'd tell you my hold is full of coal, friend, and coal is honest. It weighs what it weighs and it burns what it burns." Hollis finally turned, and his face did something complicated — recognition, calculation, and a kind of tired resignation, like a man discovering the bill he'd been avoiding had been slipped into his pocket years ago. "But I'd also say that some debts don't stay sunk. You're Sandoval."

"You have the advantage."

"I have the disadvantage, is what I have." He scratched his jaw. "Fifteen years ago you moved a crate through my father's dock at Wrenford. My father said you paid double and asked nothing. That kind of customer is either an angel or a fever, and I've never met an angel who could swim."

The stevedores' shovels rang against the hold plates. Somewhere upstream, an army bugle was murdering the morning.

"One bundle," Ansel said. "Beneath the coal line, aft, where the inspection lamp doesn't reach. It rides to Consul Verre at Marsh Point. If the Meridian is searched—"

"If the Meridian is searched, then whatever's wrapped in oilcloth is just coal dust and bad luck, because I never saw it, you never handed it to me, and my father never mentioned your name on his deathbed, which he didn't." Hollis looked out at the water, at the gray sheet of it, at the checkpoint barge anchored midstream with its lantern already burning in daylight, because army lanterns burn out of fear, not necessity. "Midnight loading. She casts off before dawn. Don't come to the dock — docks are watched now, everything's watched now, this whole town has become one long look."

He held out his hand, palm up, not for money but for the bundle, and Ansel understood that the gesture itself was the transaction: trust performed openly, in daylight, where anyone could see it and therefore no one would believe it.

Ansel walked back through the yards with his ankle complaining in a rhythm he'd begun to think of as conversational. The plan had a shape now, and the shape was simple, and simplicity, he knew from long experience, was either salvation or the last thing a trap says before it closes. Coal went downriver every week. Coal was dull. Coal was the one cargo even a suspicious sergeant couldn't make interesting. Two hundred names, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping under six tons of the most boring commodity in the republic, bound for a consul who owed favors to no one and therefore to everyone equally.

It might actually work, he thought, and immediately made a note to stop thinking things like that. In his experience, hope was the sound a plan made just before something broke.

* * *

I served dinner to four soldiers that evening, and I want it noted that the bread was good.

This is what my life had become: measuring my days in the quality of what I set before men I despised, because hatred, like everything else in this house, had to be kept behind the wainscoting. Lieutenant Osta sat at the head of my father's table — he had promoted himself to the head within two days of billeting, without discussion, the way water finds the low place — and he ate neatly and praised the bread and watched me over the rim of his cup with the patient attention of a man doing a jigsaw in his head.

"Mrs. Vane," he said. He knew perfectly well I wasn't married. He used the title the way a card sharp uses a marked deck — courteously, and to my disadvantage. "The Meridian loads tonight."

"It loads every winter," I said. "Coal doesn't keep well above ground."

"No," he agreed. "Things do best when they're kept below." He buttered his bread. One of the privates laughed at something and strangled it when Osta glanced over. "You'll forgive the observation, but this town has always moved more freight than its customs records show. Your father's ledgers were very tidy. Tidiness is its own kind of evidence, wouldn't you say? Real business is messy. Only fiction balances."

My father had balanced his books to the half-penny for thirty years, and I had grown up believing that was virtue. I now understood it had been camouflage, and that I had been raised inside the disguise without ever being fitted for a costume myself.

"I wouldn't know about fiction," I said, and passed him the butter, and hated how steady my hand was. It had been three weeks since the drawer, three weeks since the ledger, and lying had gone from terror to technique to something almost like habit, which frightened me more than any patrol. Every silence in this house had apparently been a rehearsal, and I had been on stage my whole life without knowing the play had started.

"The Meridian will be inspected," Osta said pleasantly, to no one in particular, to the bread, to the candle. "Standard procedure. Randomized. I've ordered cargo breaks on anything leaving before dawn — ships that sail in the dark are either fleeing something or carrying something, and I find it's rarely fear."

He looked at me when he said it. Not accusingly. Worse — expectantly, the way a man sets a plate and waits to see who sits down at it.

I wished him good night with all the warmth of a locked door, went upstairs, and did not sleep. Below the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth beneath my winter coats in the cedar chest, lay the ledger, waiting for midnight and a coal barge, and I lay awake listening to the wind off the marshes and thinking: he knows. Not about the ledger specifically. But he knows something is moving, because suspicion is the only cargo he truly believes in.

At half past eleven I carried the bundle downstairs in a market basket with turnips on top of it, because my mother's rule had always been that the best hiding place for one lie is inside a bigger, more boring one, though she'd meant it about brandy.

* * *

The transfer took four minutes and cost Ansel approximately ten years of his life.

Coral walked the dock path with her basket while he stood at the coal yard fence pretending to check a mooring line that belonged to someone else's boat, and Hollis's deckhand took the basket into the hold, and somewhere in the middle of it all the world was briefly ordinary — a woman buying turnips, a man fussing with rope, a ship taking on fuel. Then the Meridian's lines came off, and the barge slid out into the current with six tons of coal and two hundred families' worth of proof riding quietly in its belly, and Ansel let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since 1896.

She cast off clean. She cleared the outer moorings. She made the bend at the old tannery, and Ansel stood at the fence in the dark and let himself think the forbidden sentence — it worked — and that was, of course, precisely when the army lantern on the checkpoint barge swung around and a voice with a speaking trumpet began explaining to Captain Hollis, in the courteous grammar of armed men, that the Meridian would heave to for inspection.

He watched it happen from a quarter mile away, which was both too far to do anything and exactly far enough to understand every detail of his own defeat. The searchlight found the deck. Figures crossed aboard. And then — this was the part he made himself memorize, because grief teaches through detail — the hold lamps came on, and men began to shovel.

Cargo break. Osta had said it at Coral's dinner table like a menu item, and here it was, executed: shovels moving coal aside plank by plank, six tons of honesty being peeled like fruit, while Ansel Sandoval stood in the cold with rope-burned hands and understood that in roughly twenty minutes a deckhand was going to pull an oilcloth bundle out from under the coal line and an army lieutenant was going to unwrap it and find—

Nothing.

Because it wouldn't be the ledger. It couldn't be. He'd made sure of that an hour ago, standing in Coral's cellar with a knife and two identical bundles, playing the oldest courier's trick in a book that had gotten men like him killed on three rivers: two packages, two destinations, and only God and the courier knowing which was which. The Meridian carried paper — real dispatches, dead diplomatic traffic from his own satchel, worthless, deniable, poisonless. The true ledger slept upstairs in a cedar chest under Coral's winter coats, because the second rule of the trick was that you never put both eggs on the same river.

So the plan hadn't died, exactly. What had died was the illusion that they were unseen. By morning, Osta would be holding a bundle of meaningless diplomatic paper pulled out of a coal hold, and he would draw from it the only conclusion available to a mind like his: that the Meridian was a decoy, that coal shipments were compromised, that someone in Coral's Bridge was running something, and that whatever the real cargo was, it was still in town, still moving, still looking for a way across.

They had bought their package a funeral and given away the whole grave.

Ansel turned from the fence and walked home through the frozen lanes, ankle ticking, mind already redrawing maps he'd spent fifteen years folding away. Plan one was public now. Which meant the clock they were living on had changed shape: it was no longer just New Year's amnesty bearing down on them. It was Osta, methodical and unhurried, widening his search in rings, like ice spreading on a pond — and the center of every ring, the fixed point every circle would eventually touch, was a customs house on the hill with very tidy books.

* * *

They came for the barge crew at nine in the morning, and by ten the whole town knew.

News travels fastest to the places that fear it most. I heard it from Mrs. Aldery at the baker's, who heard it from the ferryman, who had watched the Meridian tied alongside the checkpoint barge all night with lanterns burning like a wake. Held. Crew detained. Cargo broken. Something taken off in an officer's launch under guard, wrapped in oilcloth, the way you'd carry anything from a stolen relic to a body.

I bought my bread. I thanked her. I walked home at a pace I calibrated to read as either innocence or dignity, whichever the street preferred, and only when my front door closed behind me did I lean against it and feel the full weight of what I already knew in my bones before Mrs. Aldery opened her mouth: the army was now holding physical proof — false proof, but proof shaped like intent — that someone in this town was smuggling. And the ring around us had just drawn tighter.

Ansel was in the cellar when I came down, and he looked the way I imagine a man looks at his own funeral: rueful, attentive, mostly composed.

"They'll find dispatches," he said, before I could ask. "Old diplomatic traffic. Genuine enough to be believed, dull enough to be useless. Hollis's crew knows nothing — they were paid in coal dust and ignorance. Osta will interrogate them for a week and get nothing, because there is nothing to get." He paused. "That was the design."

"And the flaw?"

"The flaw is that Osta isn't a fool. He'll hold a bundle of meaningless papers and ask himself why anyone hid meaningless papers in a coal hold, and there's only one answer worth his time: to hide something else. We haven't lost the ledger, Coral. We've lost our anonymity, and anonymity was worth more than any single route. Every road out of this town is now a road he's watching harder."

I sat down on the cellar steps. Above us, faintly, Lieutenant Osta's boots crossed my kitchen floor, unhurried, a man walking around inside his own hypothesis.

"So we've told the army," I said slowly, working it through the way you work a splinter toward the skin, "that something valuable exists. Something worth disguising. Worth killing for, maybe. And that it hasn't left yet."

"Yes."

"Then we've made it more dangerous to keep than to burn." I heard my own voice go flat and strange. "That was always your alternative, wasn't it? Destroy it, and no one hangs for the names."

"Coral—"

"No." I stood. Fifteen years of dusting and denying had ended three weeks ago in a false-backed drawer, and I would not go back to being the custodian of silences, not even silences offered for my protection. "Two hundred names, Ansel. My schoolteacher. My godmother. The ferryman who taught me to whistle. They sheltered people at the risk of everything, and their reward was to be written down in my father's secret hand, and now you want to solve the problem of them by making them never have existed." I took a breath, and the next part came out steadier than I expected, which is how I knew it was true. "If the river won't carry them, I will. I've lived my whole life in this town being nobody's daughter in particular. I can walk anywhere in it carrying anything, because nobody watches the customs clerk's plain daughter. If the barge failed, then we stop hiding the ledger in things. We hide it in me."

Ansel looked at me for a long moment, and I watched something move across his face that I'd seen once before, at the lockhouse, when he confessed to leaving my father on the last mission — guilt and awe sharing a bench, neither willing to sit first.

"That's plan two," he said quietly. "There is no plan two."

"There is now," I said. "You taught my father's routes for fifteen years. Teach me."

Above us, the boots stopped crossing the kitchen, and stood still.
