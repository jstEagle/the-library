## 15. Interrogation by Lamplight

They detained me at nine o'clock in the evening, which I considered a small courtesy. A man who means to hang you might at least let you finish the washing-up, and Lieutenant Osta did. He even waited while I banked the kitchen fire, as though we were guests departing on the morning coach and he was merely seeing me to the door.

"Lieutenant," I said.

"Madame Vane." He held the customs house door for me, and I walked out of my own house between two soldiers as if I were being escorted to a party I'd rather not attend but couldn't politely refuse.

They took me not to the garrison office — that had windows, and windows could be watched from outside — but to the lockhouse by the second checkpoint, a stone building with one lamp, one table, one stove, and a chair positioned with the particular cruelty of furniture arranged by someone who has thought about it. Osta had thought about it. The chair faced the wall, so that I would have to turn to look at him, and turning would be my idea, and everything that happened after would proceed from my having turned.

I sat facing the wall for eleven minutes. I counted. My father taught me to count when frightened — *count the river's slats, Coral, count them until your heart agrees with the numbers* — though of course he never said *when frightened*. He only ever said it as a game, on the dock, when I was seven, his hand warm on the back of my neck, both of us watching the water slip through the pilings.

The lamplight made an orange oval on the plaster in front of me. There was a crack running through it like a dried riverbed. I studied it the way scholars study manuscripts, and when Osta finally said, "You may sit wherever you like, Madame Vane, it is your town," I turned around slowly, as if I hadn't been counting every second of his silence.

"Thank you, Lieutenant. It's kind of you to notice."

He smiled. He had a good smile, which was the worst thing about him. Some men are ugly in their cruelty and you can brace against the ugliness; Osta was pleasant the way still water is pleasant, right up until you understand what's underneath it. He poured coffee from a dented pot into two tin cups and pushed one across the table, and we sat like old friends at a wake, because what else do you do at a wake but drink something hot and speak carefully of the dead?

* * *

"You've lived in Coral's Bridge how long?" he began, and this told me everything about how the night would go, because he knew exactly how long. He had pulled files. Rask had warned Ansel about the files; apparently nobody warns the women anything, which suited me fine, since warning me would have required someone knowing enough to warn.

"All my life."

"And before that?"

"I'm told it was quite cold. July, but cold. My mother claimed the river kept the season honest."

He wrote that down. Actually wrote it down, in a small notebook with a stub of pencil, my mother's joke about the weather entered into the record of the republic. Somewhere in a capital archive there would now exist a card reading *Vane, Coral — mother believed river influenced climate.* I found this so absurd that I nearly laughed, and the laugh would have been dangerous, so I converted it into reaching for my coffee.

"Your father," Osta said. "Emory Vane. Customs clerk."

"Customs clerk," I agreed.

"For how long?"

"Until he died."

"Which was?"

"Fifteen years ago this autumn. You'd know better than I, Lieutenant — you have the files." I said it mildly, apologetically, the way you'd point out to a guest that they've mislaid their own umbrella.

For just a moment the still water rippled. Then he smoothed it. "Humor me. Dates are how I think."

So I gave him dates. I gave him the whole respectable chronology, the one I had maintained for fifteen years the way you maintain a garden — not because anyone admires it, but because if you stop for one season it goes to ruin and everyone can see. Born in the customs house. Schooled at Miss Aldery's, up the hill. Father quiet, punctual, fond of his pipe and his ledgers. Mother gone earlier, fever year. No visitors of note. No unusual shipments. A childhood like a plain gray dress: unremarkable, buttoned to the throat, and absolutely nothing underneath.

Osta listened with his chin propped on his knuckles, and around midnight he began laying out his cards, one by one, faceup, the way he does — I understood now that the interview was a game of patience played with two decks, his and mine, and he wanted me to see his.

"Your father received a pension," he said. "From the provincial treasury. Small. Irregular. Customs clerks of his grade didn't receive pensions."

"He carried mail during the border troubles. Hazard pay."

"The border troubles ended six years before the pension began."

"He carried mail after, too. He liked the work. Men of his generation didn't retire, they just got slower."

A card, laid down. Another. "There's a gap in the harbor manifests. Eleven months, the year you turned nine. Every clerk on the river has continuous manifests except Emory Vane."

"Paper burns, Lieutenant. He kept his records in the back room over the stove. There was a chimney fire — you can ask Mrs. Havel across the lane, she remembers the smoke."

"There was no chimney fire in the municipal record."

"My father filed his incident reports with the port authority, not the municipality. He said the municipality lost things." I sipped my coffee. It was terrible. I drank it like it was communion wine. "He was right, as it turns out."

* * *

Somewhere past two in the morning, I realized I wasn't afraid anymore, and that frightened me more than fear had.

It wasn't bravery. Let me be clear about that, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does — and I suspect someone will, because men like Osta keep everything, my mother's weather theory included. It was not courage. It was recognition. Sitting in that lockhouse chair with the bad coffee going cold in my hands, watching a career interrogator circle and circle and find nothing to land on, I felt something horrible dawn in me, slow and orange as the lamplight:

My father had done this before. Not to me — *with* me. For years. Every question a neighbor asked that he answered a half-beat too slowly, so it sounded thoughtful instead of rehearsed. Every story he told twice, identically, down to the color of the sky, because identical stories hold and improvised ones fray. The way he never spoke ill of the government, never praised it either, occupied the exact bloodless middle where nobody bothers digging. The way he taught me to count the river's slats without ever once saying *this is how you will keep your face still when a man asks you about your family.*

He had trained me the way you train someone for a thing you pray they'll never need to do. By silence. By example. By fifteen years of dinners eaten across a table from a man who was, all along, rehearsing me.

I looked at Osta across the lamp and thought: *you are not the first man to try to take this house apart. You're only the first one I've watched do it.*

And grief came then — late, sideways, in the wrong register — grief for the father I'd defended all these years without knowing what I was defending. I had spent fifteen years believing I was protecting his reputation as a dull little clerk, and it turned out I'd been guarding the door of something enormous, and he'd left me the key inside my own habits without ever telling me where he'd put it.

"Madame Vane." Osta's voice, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "You've gone somewhere."

"To sleep, mostly. It's past two."

"What are you thinking about?"

"My father. Since you ask about nothing else."

"Tell me what he was like."

And here is the trick I discovered, the one I hated myself for discovering: the truth works. Not the whole truth — but true details, true texture, deployed like any other ammunition. So I told him true things. That my father hummed off-key when he balanced the quarterly books, always hymns, always wrong. That he gave the ferryman's orphaned boys his boots every winter and bought new ones and never explained the holes in the budget. That he once made me return a ribbon I'd taken from a wrecked cargo barge, walked me the whole mile in the rain, and stood outside the shop while I gave it back, and never raised his voice, and I cried for hours, and he said, *Coral, some things aren't ours, and the sooner your hands learn that, the safer your hands will be.*

Osta wrote that down too. *Safer hands.* Let him chew on it. A man who reads everything finds nothing, because everything cancels out.

* * *

Toward dawn he changed tactics, which meant he was losing, though neither of us said so.

He set down the notebook. He folded his hands. He became, briefly, human — or performed humanity so well that the distinction stopped mattering, which may be the definition of a good officer.

"Coral," he said, using my first name for the first time, and it landed like a hand on bare skin. "I don't think you're a courier. I don't think you're an ideologue. I think you're a woman who loved her father and doesn't want to hear what he was. And I am offering you the chance to keep not hearing it. Tell me what was in the coal shipment. Tell me who moved it. Do that, and the Vane file closes as a clerical irregularity, and you go home to your museum."

And there it was. Not the ledger — he didn't say *ledger*, and I noted its absence the way you note the absence of a name in a will. He didn't know precisely what we'd moved. He knew only that something had been moved and that the barge was held, and he was fishing with a very fine line in a very large river, hoping I was too tired to feel the hook.

I was tired. God, I was tired. Twenty hours without sleep, and my hands ached from clenching a cup, and somewhere across the dark town Ansel was presumably awake too, ankle strapped, waiting, and every minute I sat here was a minute he couldn't move.

But here is what my father's silences had built in me, brick by unspoken brick: I knew what a bargain cost. My father could have taken the province's money openly, could have been comfortable, could have told me everything over supper. He chose the expensive silence instead, because cheap truth buys comfort and expensive silence buys other people's lives, and he judged — correctly, it turned out — that I'd rather inherit the silence than spend the lives.

"Lieutenant," I said, and I heard my father's mildness come out of my mouth like smoke, "the coal shipment belonged to Meric & Sons, downriver. Your inspectors broke open four crates of it in front of half the town and found coal. If there was something else in it, I promise you the person who hid it didn't tell the customs clerk's daughter, because no one has ever told me anything in this town. I found out my own mother's death date from a gravestone. Ask anyone."

Every word true. Every word arranged. I lay the last card down and saw him understand, finally, that my deck was cut from the same stock as his.

He looked at me for a long time. The lamp guttered. Outside, the ice on the river boomed once, deep, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"I believe you believe what you're saying," Osta said at last, and closed the notebook. "That's the trouble with this town. Everyone believes what they're saying. Nobody believes anything."

* * *

They let me go at first light — released, not freed; a soldier walked me home through streets blue with snow-shadow, past the bridge where Sergeant Rask stood his post looking like a man carved out of the same winter as the river. Our eyes met for less than a second. His face gave away nothing, which I have since learned is his entire character, but he shifted his weight, almost imperceptibly, toward the north end of the span, and I tucked that shift away like a coin in a mitten.

At the customs house door the soldier saluted — actually saluted, the republic's manners persisting past its conscience — and left me.

Inside, the house smelled of banked fire and the soldiers' tobacco, and Ansel was not there, which was correct, which was the plan, and still my chest went tight as a knot until I saw the signal: the third floorboard in the hall, the loose one, set crosswise. *Safe. Marsh side. Wait for dusk.*

I stood in my father's hallway in my dead father's house, in the gray hour before the lamps go out, and I let myself shake for exactly sixty seconds. I counted. Then I straightened, went to the kitchen, and raked the fire back to life, because whatever came at dusk would need a woman who could feed people first.

On the table, someone — Ansel, before he'd gone — had left a scrap of paper weighted under the sugar bowl. One line, in his careful courier's hand:

*Ferran has announced it. Bridge closes New Year's Eve. One inspected crossing at dusk — his.*

I read it twice, put it in the stove, and watched my father's training and my father's house and my father's whole silent architecture of a life curl around the flame and catch.

One crossing. At dusk. Baited with our own deadline.

I put the kettle on, because the dead train us for exactly this, and I had learned my lessons better than either of us ever knew.
