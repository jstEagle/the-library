## 16. The Confession at the Lockhouse

The lockhouse sat where the canal gave up pretending to be useful and let the river take over — a squat stone building with a rusted winch, a door that had forgotten how to shut properly, and the particular smell of places that have been abandoned by everyone except mice and regret. It had been a smuggler's waystation once, back when my knees worked and my conscience was merely bent rather than broken. Now it was the only roof within walking distance of the marsh route that I could reach on a twisted ankle without announcing myself to every patrol between here and the north road.

I had chosen it for its anonymity. I had not chosen the company, but company arrived anyway, because Coral Vane had apparently decided that if I was going to limp off into the frozen marshes alone, she would follow at a distance of thirty paces with her chin up and her father's stubbornness wrapped around her shoulders like the gray coat she wore.

"You're supposed to be answering Lieutenant Osta's questions," I said when she came through the door, bringing cold air and a parcel of bread that smelled like civilization.

"I answered them for six hours last night. He's tired of me. I've developed a cough he finds suspicious." She set the bread on the table, which was a plank across two barrels, and looked around the lockhouse the way you'd look at a photograph of someone you used to love. "You knew this place."

"I knew it."

"The marks on the beam. The chalk numbers." She touched the doorframe, where fifteen years ago someone had scratched tallies in a code only four living people could read. Three of them were dead. "This was one of his stations, wasn't it. Father's."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't insult her by pretending it was. I lowered myself onto the barrel that served as a chair, ankle throbbing, and watched her run her fingers along the tallies like a woman reading braille.

"He trained me," she said, still not looking at me. "All those years. The drills about what to say to strangers, the way he made me memorize boat schedules as a game, the rule that we never spoke of his work past the third question. I thought he was a careful man. I thought careful was all it was." Her hand dropped. "He was building me into this. Into a person who could sit across from Lieutenant Osta and lie for six hours without blinking. And he never told me. He just — built."

"Coral—"

"Sit down," she said, though I was sitting. "Stay sitting. Because I've spent two days being furious at a dead man, and I have enough of that left over to be furious at you too, and I want to know why before I decide how much." She finally turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Fifteen years, Ansel. You knew him. You knew what he did — you must have, you were his courier, Rask's wanted circular says as much. Fifteen years you knew what those silences in our house meant, and you let me go on dusting his ledgers and believing in the customs clerk. Why?"

There are questions you prepare answers for, and questions that arrive like weather. This one had been coming since the night at her father's study when she'd found the ledger, and I had rehearsed nothing, because every answer I owned was a coward's version of the truth.

So I gave her the true one, or the start of it, which is to say I started talking about something else entirely.

"It was October," I said. "Eighteen ninety-six. Your father came out of the capital with a full set of dispatches — coded, sealed, the whole liturgy — names of forty-one people who'd sheltered writers and organizers through the winter. The generals had gotten a court clerk to talk, and there was going to be a purge through the whole river valley, town by town, starting here. Starting with this house." I nodded at the tallies on the doorframe. "Your father's plan was simple and insane, which was his signature style. Split the courier work. He'd carry half the warning south by the marshes; I'd carry half north to the consulate barge at Havel, where foreign eyes would make it awkward to shoot the messengers. Two men, two routes, twice the chances."

Coral had gone very still. It occurred to me that she was hearing her father described as a young man for the first time — not the customs clerk with ink on his cuffs, but the other man, the one who ran stations under the floorboards of respectable houses.

"We got as far as the ferry crossing at Miren," I went on. "And the patrol caught us at the landing. Not a chase — an ambush. They'd been tipped. Someone sold us, though I've spent a fair portion of my life failing to prove who." My ankle chose that moment to ache in sympathy, and I shifted on the barrel. "We scattered. Your father went east along the towpath. I went north with the satchel. And the thing you need to understand, Coral, the thing I need you to understand, is that at the moment I turned north, your father was calling my name."

The lockhouse was quiet. Somewhere outside, ice creaked on the canal like old furniture.

"He'd taken a bullet in the shoulder going over the fence at the landing. Nothing immediately fatal — he was walking, angry, alive. He called my name and he said, *Ansel. The names.* Which could mean two things. It could mean *help me carry them.* Or it could mean *save them first.*" I heard my own voice flatten, the way it does when you've said a thing too many times inside your own skull. "I chose to hear the second. I have told myself for fifteen years that he meant the second. That any man in that position, knowing what those names meant — forty-one families, Coral, children among them — would want the paper saved before the man. That leaving him on the towpath bleeding was not abandonment but obedience to his own priorities."

"And was it?" she asked. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it. She asked it the way her father used to ask me to confirm a count of crates — professionally, wanting the accurate figure, not the comfortable one.

"No," I said. "Or I don't know. Both, maybe. Here's what I do know. The marshes were passable in three directions from Miren. I took the fastest one, the one that kept me moving away from the patrol. There was a slower route, along the drainage cut, that would have let me circle back within the hour. I stood at the fork for perhaps ten seconds. Then I walked north, and I did not look back, because I was afraid that if I looked back I would turn around, and if I turned around, the satchel would be captured with me, and forty-one families would burn." I spread my hands. "That's the noble arithmetic. Here's the ignoble truth underneath: I was twenty-six years old, I was terrified, and your father was the bravest person I ever met, and brave people make excellent excuses. Every coward needs a braver man to point at and say, *he would have wanted it this way.*"

Coral sat down slowly on the opposite barrel. For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the river muttered against the lock gates, patient as a debt collector.

"They never found a body," she said finally.

"No."

"For fifteen years I let myself believe that meant he might be alive somewhere. A cell, an island, a labor camp with bad records. Some part of me kept the customs house exactly as he left it — the coat on the hook, the pipe in the drawer — because tidying it felt like burying him, and I hadn't earned the right to bury a man without a grave." Her jaw tightened. "But you knew. You knew he didn't make it out. How?"

"Because three days later, the purge lists were posted in the valley towns anyway. Forty-one names, most of them crossed out with red — arrested, not warned. If your father had reached the southern stations, half those names would have been safe. They weren't." I looked down at my hands, which have carried a great many things and dropped precisely one. "Half the network died that October, Coral. The half that survived was the half I carried north. And the army has wanted the courier who survived ever since, because a courier who can testify knows every station, every name, every family. Rask's circular isn't about punishing me. It's about finishing the job your father interrupted."

"So the ledger in my father's study—"

"Is the master record. The copy he kept behind the false back because he trusted no single courier with the whole truth — except that he did, in the end, trust one, because he sent me back for it, or meant to, the next spring. And then October happened, and I never came back to this town again. Until now." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughter does when it's carrying more than it can lift. "Fifteen years of avoiding Coral's Bridge because I couldn't walk past your house. And then I picked it for my last delivery, because apparently at fifty-one a man gets sentimental about the scene of his worst act. Retirement. I actually believed I could retire."

Coral stood. She crossed to the window, where frost had grown ferns on the glass, and stood with her back to me, and I understood that whatever she said next would decide whether I spent the rest of this story as her ally or simply as a man she happened to know.

"You told me once," she said, "that you owed your father a debt. At the customs house, the first night. You said it lightly, like a joke about card games."

"I remember."

"Was this the debt?"

"This was the debt. Though I phrased it badly. You don't owe a debt to the dead man. You owe it to everything he left unfinished." I got up despite the ankle, because sitting felt like another form of running. "Coral, I'm going to tell you the rest, and then you can decide what I am to you. The confession isn't finished until it's inconvenient."

She turned around. "Then finish it."

"After the barge was seized — after Osta held the coal shipment and we lost plan one — I sent word through the lockkeeper's boy to a name in the ledger. Doret Marchand, the ferryman. He was one of your father's station-keepers. The drop schedule in the margins still works, Coral; the marsh routes are watched, but the *water* routes aren't, not yet, not in winter. There's a consul's launch that comes upriver for the New Year's fireworks, foreign flag, diplomatically untouchable. If the ledger reaches that deck before midnight on the thirty-first, two hundred names stop being evidence and become testimony. After midnight, they become ash with everything else in the records office." I took a breath. "And I'm not going to be the one carrying it."

"Why not? Your ankle will heal in—"

"Because Osta has requested the courier files from the capital. Rask told me this morning. Within days — maybe hours — there will be an officer in this town holding a fifteen-year-old description of my face, my routes, my handwriting. I am the most identifiable object in Coral's Bridge, more than the ledger itself. Wherever I go, scrutiny follows like a dog." I met her eyes. "But that's the use of me. That's the only use of me left. A man who cannot hide can still be *looked at*. If they're looking at me, they're not looking at whoever's carrying the ledger. I intend, on New Year's Eve, to stand in the most conspicuous place in this town and let every soldier in Colonel Ferran's command get a long, satisfying look at the courier they've hunted for fifteen years. While they arrest me, question me, gloat over me, and draft their reports — you'll be walking."

Silence. The frost ferns on the window caught the last gray light and held it.

"You're proposing to be bait," she said.

"I'm proposing to be useful, finally, at the end of a very long invoice."

"And what happens to you at that checkpoint, Ansel? After they've had their long look? Do they shake your hand?"

"They do not."

"They hang you."

"Probably. Or imprisonment, if Ferran wants testimony first — but I won't give any, and eventually—" I shrugged, because there are facts you can shrug at once you've lived alongside them long enough. "Coral, I chose the dispatches over your father once. I have spent fifteen years telling myself it was for the names. On the thirty-first I can make it true retroactively. There is no other currency in which I can pay what I owe, and I'd think less of you if you let me pretend otherwise."

She was quiet for so long that I counted the sounds of the river twice through. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — not softer, exactly. Settled. Like a ledger column that has found its total.

"When I found the false drawer," she said, "the first thing I felt wasn't grief. It was anger at *you* — at the stranger sleeping under my roof who clearly knew more about my father than I did. I've been carrying that anger for days, waiting for it to justify itself." She came closer, stopped an arm's length away, and looked at me with her father's exact level gaze, and I understood where she'd learned the trick of asking questions that already contained their verdicts. "Here's what I've decided. I don't forgive you for October. I don't think it's mine to forgive — the man on that towpath would have to do the forgiving, and he can't. What I can tell you is what the ledger says. Two hundred names, Ansel. Two hundred families who sheltered people because Elias Vane asked them to, knowing it might kill them, and did it anyway. He spent himself completely. You spent fifteen years hiding from the receipt." Her hand came up, almost gently, and tapped my chest once, over the pocket where I kept my courier's credentials, the ones I'd been meaning to surrender since autumn. "So here are my conditions. On New Year's Eve, you will spend yourself too. All of it. At the bridge, in the open, loudly — not as penance, because penance is cheap and private. As payment. Publicly, where the whole town sees what a courier looks like when he finishes his route. You don't get to die quietly in a cellar feeling sorry for yourself. You get to be *seen*."

"That's," I said, and found my throat had closed on the rest.

"If you come through it alive," she went on, mercilessly practical, "you'll spend the rest of it testifying. Every name, every station, every route, under oath, in front of whatever court survives Ferran. That's the second condition. No more silence from you, ever, about anything. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I managed.

"Then we understand each other." She picked up her bread parcel, divided it with the economy of a woman who had fed soldiers graciously all week while hating it, and handed me the larger half. "Eat. Sleep. Heal the ankle as fast as your aged body allows, because I need you upright in five days." She moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the frame, beside her father's old tallies. "Ansel. One more thing."

"Anything."

"The fork at Miren. The drainage-cut route, the slow one that circled back." She didn't look at me; she looked at the tallies. "I checked the ledger's marginal map last night. It's marked there. In his handwriting. *Courier's fallback route* — with a note." Her voice stayed level, but only just. "*If Ansel takes the cut, wait at the lockhouse. He always chooses the fast road, but the cut is the right one.*"

The river said nothing. It had said nothing for fifteen years.

"He knew," Coral said quietly. "He knew which way you'd go, and he left you the way home anyway. So whatever you decide you owe him — he'd already forgiven the debt. You're the only one still holding the bill." She opened the door onto the cold. "Five days. Be worth his handwriting."

Then she was gone, gray coat swallowed by the gray afternoon, and I stood alone in the lockhouse with bread in my hand and fifteen years rearranging themselves around me like furniture after an earthquake. On the doorframe, the tallies caught the light. I put my finger in the last groove her father had cut, and it fit the way an old key fits — badly, but unmistakably.

Outside, far off toward town, a bugle sounded the evening assembly, and beneath it, fainter, came the boom of the first trial fireworks being tested over the water. Five days, she had said. Five days until New Year's Eve, until the deadline written into law, until the bridge closed and reopened as a trap.

I ate the bread. I began, painfully, to walk.
