## 17. Ferran's Trap

The proclamation went up on the customs house door at nine in the morning, and by half past, the whole town had invented three different ways to die reading it.

I heard it from Coral first, because she came down the stairs of her own house with her coat already buttoned and her face arranged into the particular blankness she'd been practicing all winter — the face of a customs clerk's daughter who has never once wondered what was behind the false-backed drawer in the study. She set a folded sheet of paper on the kitchen table between us, next to the bread I hadn't eaten.

"Read it," she said. "Then tell me we're not already dead."

I read it. The paper was army stock, the print crisp, the language the kind of officious mercy that always smells like a trap before you find the teeth. *By order of Colonel Ferran, commanding, the river bridge at Coral's Bridge will be closed to all traffic on the thirty-first day of December, in observance of the expiration of the general amnesty.* That part was expected; we'd known the date since the barge failed. It was the second paragraph that did its work.

*One crossing will be permitted at dusk, for persons holding proper papers, subject to full inspection. Persons unable to present themselves at this hour should arrange their affairs accordingly.*

"Arrange their affairs accordingly," Coral repeated, when I'd read it twice. "He makes murder sound like a tailor's appointment."

"He's not wrong to," I said. "It is one."

She sat down across from me. Outside, snow was coming down in that indifferent way it has in this country, as if the weather had read the proclamation too and decided none of it was its business.

Here is what Ferran had done, laid out plainly: he knew — had known since the coal barge was held at the second checkpoint, since his men broke open crates and found nothing but honest coal, which is somehow more suspicious than contraband — that someone in his town was moving something worth moving. He could not search every house, every coat, every loaf of bread between here and New Year's Eve. So he had done something cleverer. He had closed every door except one, and then hung a clock above it.

Anyone with unfinished business — anyone carrying papers, names, a ledger with two hundred families written inside it — now had exactly one road out, and it ran across the bridge at dusk, under full inspection, through whatever sergeant or lieutenant Ferran chose to stand at the end of it. And if they didn't come? Then midnight arrived, the amnesty expired, the records burned, and the thing they'd been carrying became ash along with everything else. Either way, Ferran won. The only variable left was whether he won alone.

"He doesn't know what we have," Coral said. "He suspects."

"He suspects enough. A man like that doesn't need certainty. Certainty is expensive. Suspicion is free, and it works just as well when you're building a cage." I pushed the bread toward her, an old courier's habit — eat when you can, because hunger makes decisions — and watched her ignore it, another habit of hers.

"What are our other roads?" she asked. She said *our*, and I noticed that I no longer flinched at the word. Fifteen years of working alone will teach you to flinch at company; three weeks of Coral Vane had undone most of it.

"The marsh route is watched, and my ankle is still lying about how much it can carry. The north road has had a checkpoint since the requisition — Corporal Dey's post, and Dey searches like a man who enjoys it. The river's frozen past the bend, and below the bend there's the barge inspection, where we have already taught them to look hard at cargo." I ticked them off on my fingers, and my fingers kept coming up empty. "There's the ice itself, if you fancy drowning within sight of salvation."

"I don't."

"Then we have one road, and it's the one he built for us."

Coral was quiet for a moment. Through the floorboards we could hear Lieutenant Osta overhead — billeted in her father's house these two weeks, walking her father's floors, sleeping — I had no doubt — in some corner of her father's study, pulling files the way other men pull teeth. Osta's footsteps had a rhythm I'd learned to read the way sailors read weather. Slow pacing meant thinking. Fast pacing meant he'd found something. This morning he was pacing slowly, which frightened me more.

"There's one thing you haven't said," Coral said finally. "He built the trap for a smuggler. But he doesn't know the smuggler is you."

"He knows someone is. And Ivo Rask knows exactly who."

"You trust Rask?"

"That," I said, "is the wrong question. The right question is whether Rask trusts himself, and I've watched him stand at that bridge for three weeks trying not to answer it."

* * *

I spent the middle of the day doing what old couriers do best, which is walking places slowly and memorizing them. My ankle had settled into a dull grudge rather than a scream, and I used it as an excuse to move at the pace of a bored retiree, which is precisely the pace no one watches.

The town wore its requisition like a too-tight collar. Soldiers on corners. The records office sealed with wax and wire, the seals stamped with the same cipher pattern I'd carried in my satchel for fifteen years — that was the joke I'd stopped telling even to myself, that the codes I'd once run were now being used to lock the doors against me. Somewhere behind those wax circles sat the town registers, the birth rolls, the tax lists. Two hundred names, and beside each one in Ferran's arithmetic, a case number waiting for the furnace.

At the foot of the bridge, I let myself look at the thing itself for the first time in daylight without pretending I wasn't looking.

They were building stands.

Not grandstands, nothing so theatrical — but carpenters were up ladders along the near abutment, rigging lantern lines and a low rail barrier, funneling the footpath into a single lane maybe six feet wide, walled with planks. At dusk, in winter, dusk comes early and leaves fast. Anyone crossing would do it in a lit corridor, six feet wide, flanked by soldiers, at the exact hour when the last light dies and every face becomes a question mark. Full inspection meant tables, meant opening bags, meant turning out pockets, meant — if Ferran got lucky — meaning.

And at the far end of the lane, checking the lantern spacing himself, stood Sergeant Ivo Rask with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, looking like a man guarding the exit of his own conscience.

He saw me. I made sure he did — that was the point of walking slow past the abutment, an old man admiring carpentry. His eyes did the thing they always did: the flicker of recognition, the immediate, practiced flattening, the soldier's trick of seeing nothing while seeing everything. Fifteen years ago a wanted circular had gone out with my face on it, young then, and Rask had held it in his hands recently enough that I'd watched him compare it to me over the checkpoint table like a man comparing a coin to its worn portrait. He hadn't said a word then. His brother owed me a pardon — literally, physically, a folded document I'd carried through three checkpoints in a boot heel — and debts like that don't sit quietly in a man like Rask. They sit up nights.

But debts can be outweighed. Duty is heavy, and Ferran's gallows are heavier.

I turned away before either of us had to be seen noticing anything, and my ankle turned with me, and I swore at it privately, and went home through the falling snow.

* * *

Osta was at the table when I got back.

That was new. In two weeks of billeting, the lieutenant had maintained a careful theater of ignoring me — the retired courier, the family friend, the lame man who drank tea and discussed river levels. I was furniture with a limp. Now he sat at Coral's kitchen table with a cup of her coffee and a single folder square in front of him like a place setting, and he smiled at me with the specific warmth of a man who has just finished a puzzle and wants to watch you sweat while he hides the last piece.

"Monsieur Sandoval," he said. "Sit. Please."

I sat. Coral stood at the stove with her back to us, doing something unnecessary to a kettle, and I caught her eye for a quarter second — the whole vocabulary we'd developed, compressed into one glance. *Steady.*

"You have been in the town, I think, this morning?" Osta's accent came from the capital, rounded and soft, a gentleman's accent. He opened the folder without hurry. "Walking. For the air."

"For the ankle," I said. "The doctor recommends movement."

"The doctor is wise." He turned the folder around and slid it across the table. Inside was a photograph — grainy, older than the war, older than the republic's collapse, the kind of print that had been copied so many times its edges had gone soft. A younger man looked out of it. Thinner. No limp. Eyes that hadn't yet learned what mine had.

"Do you know him?" Osta asked.

I looked at my own face for longer than an innocent man would need and less time than a guilty one would want, and I said, "No."

"No," Osta agreed pleasantly, and took the photograph back. "Neither does anyone here. It is remarkable, no? Fifteen years, and nobody knows this man. He carries dispatches for the old network, he moves papers past checkpoints in boots and bread and coffins — we have the stories, monsieur, such stories — and nobody, ever, has known his name." He sipped his coffee. "Either he is a ghost, or everyone who knew him has been very well paid, or very well frightened, or very fond of him. I have been considering which."

"Consider ghosts," I said. "Cheaper than the alternatives."

Osta laughed, genuinely, and I hated how much I liked him in that instant, the way you can like a well-made lock even as it closes on you. He rose, tucked the folder under his arm, and paused at the doorway with the air of a man remembering a courtesy.

"The crossing tomorrow, at dusk," he said. "You and Madame Vane should use it. Proper papers, of course — you have them, yes? Good. The colonel wishes everyone well who has nothing to hide." His smile returned, gentle as a scalpel. "And everyone else, monsieur — everyone else he wishes to meet."

The door closed behind him. At the stove, Coral set down the kettle with a click that was far too controlled.

"He showed you your own face."

"He wanted to see what I did with it."

"And what did you do?"

"Boredom," I said. "Mostly. A little rheumatism around the eyes."

Coral came to the table and sat, and for the first time since the proclamation she let the performance drop, and underneath it she looked — not afraid. That was the terrible thing. She looked like a woman doing arithmetic.

"He's not hunting a ghost anymore," she said. "He's confirming one. Tomorrow at dusk, he'll put faces to that photograph, one by one, six feet apart in a lantern lane. Every person who crosses gets compared. Ansel — if you cross, you're giving him the answer for free."

"If I cross, it's because the answer buys you the crossing."

"No." She said it flatly, no heat in it at all, which is how I've learned to recognize when Coral Vane means a thing absolutely. "Listen to me. He's spent three weeks building a net for a smuggler. Fine. Let him catch a smuggler. But the ledger isn't a smuggler's burden anymore — it's testimony. Two hundred names. If it crosses in a smuggler's bag and the smuggler hangs, it's evidence in a dead man's case, burned with the rest at midnight anyway unless it reaches the consulate barge before the amnesty dies. The consul's people must receive it as a surrender of records, formally, witnessed — that's the only thing amnesty law can't void. You told me that yourself, chapter and verse, back when you still talked about the plan like it belonged to both of us."

"I remember."

"Then hear the rest." She leaned forward. "Ferran built one door. One door means one crowd. Every soldier, every eye, every suspicion at the bridge at dusk, because that's the only hour anything can move. Which means at dusk, while the entire garrison stares at a six-foot lane of lanterns—" her voice didn't rise at all, "—the least inspected place in this town will be the water."

I stared at her. Fifteen years of routes and ciphers and dead drops, and the daughter of a customs clerk had just walked around my entire map.

"The consulate barge," I said slowly. "It's been at anchor below the bridge since the twenty-ninth. Waiting on the amnesty deadline to take custody of foreign property and personnel before the records burn."

"It's a boat," Coral said. "Boats come to docks. Docks are not bridges. Nobody builds lantern lanes around docks." She stood, and pulled the folded proclamation from her pocket, and set it on the table like a card played face-up. "One problem remains, and it's the same problem we've had all month. Someone has to be at that bridge at dusk, drawing every eye, giving the inspection something to inspect — someone worth searching, worth delaying, worth watching until midnight comes and goes. The trap needs its quarry, Ansel. Otherwise Ferran turns the garrison loose on the town by eight o'clock, and the dock, and the boat, and me."

The kettle began, very softly, to whistle. Neither of us moved toward it.

"You've thought about who," I said. It wasn't a question. Her face had the particular stillness of a decision already made, wrapped and addressed, merely awaiting postage.

"Ferran wants the courier," Coral said. "Fifteen years of circulars say so. And tomorrow at dusk, the courier walks up to the third checkpoint, presents himself, and gives Colonel Ferran the only gift he's ever wanted more than two hundred names." She met my eyes, and hers were bright and terrible and certain. "A confession. Sole responsibility. One man, one bag of decoy papers, one story that ends with everyone else clean."

"That man hangs," I said.

"That man," she said, "buys forty minutes. I've counted them. It takes the garrison eleven minutes to search the lane properly, nineteen to march a prisoner to the colonel, ten for Ferran to understand he's been handed the wrong end of the rope — I've watched him, Ansel, he's slow to believe a gift. Forty minutes. The consulate dock is seven minutes' walk if the fireworks cover the footsteps."

Outside, somewhere over the rooftops, the first experimental firework of the season cracked and bloomed — some shopkeeper testing his New Year's stock early, gold light washing briefly across the kitchen wall. Tomorrow night there would be hundreds. The whole sky, full of noise and light, and under it a bridge full of soldiers staring at a man who had volunteered to be seen.

I thought of her father, whom I had failed on a frozen road fifteen years ago, choosing dispatches over a man. I had promised her that debt would be spent at the bridge, and I had imagined spending it some nobler way than this — but debts don't get to pick their currency.

"All right," I said. "Forty minutes."

Coral nodded once, businesslike, and only then did her hands shake — after, when it no longer mattered, when the decision was signed. She poured the coffee neither of us wanted, and outside the fireworks went on practicing for the new year, and above our heads Lieutenant Osta paced slowly, slowly across the ceiling, a man who believed he had already built the only door.

He was half right. There would be a door tomorrow at dusk, exactly where he'd put it.

He just had the wrong traveler in mind for it.
