## 19. New Year's Eve

**ANSEL**

The fireworks began at half past eleven, and I have never been grateful for artillery in my life before or since.

They went up from the far bank — the army's own celebration, Ferran's little gift to the town he was strangling, green and gold blossoms over the frozen river — and every eye on the bridge checkpoint turned upward like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there. Sergeant Dravic's men, the ones Ferran had posted to inspect the single dusk crossing, craned their necks and forgot, for four seconds at a time, that they were soldiers.

Four seconds. I had spent fifteen years learning what a man can do with four seconds.

I stood at the edge of the customs house square with my collar up and my heart behaving badly, and I watched Coral Vane walk away from me into the crowd with two hundred names strapped against her ribs, and I thought: this is the last hour of my old life, and I am spending it standing still.

That was the plan. My whole part in it was to stand still, eventually, in the most conspicuous place available, and let myself be seen.

"You look like a man attending his own funeral," Rask had told me that afternoon, in the coal shed where we'd spoken in whispers among the anthracite.

"Close enough," I'd said. "The flowers are wrong, though."

He hadn't laughed. Rask doesn't laugh at things like that. He'd looked at me for a long moment with those flat river-gray eyes and said, "When you do it, do it loud. Osta will want to be the one. Make sure Ferran hears your name before Osta can bury you in a back room."

"Why?"

"Because a man who dies quietly can be made to have been anyone." He'd brushed coal dust from his sleeve. "A man who dies loudly has to be who he says he is. I need to know which man I'm arresting."

I hadn't asked him then which man he hoped it was. I still don't know. I suspect he doesn't either.

The fireworks cracked again — a triple burst, red this time, raining embers onto the ice — and the crowd in the square cheered the army that had taken their homes, their records, and their year. Fear looks a great deal like festivity when you dress it in rockets. I had seen that trick before, in other towns, in other years, and I had carried the paperwork for it and told myself it was just paper.

I checked my watch by the light of a brazier. Quarter to midnight.

* * *

**CORAL**

The ledger weighed nothing and everything.

That is the thing nobody tells you about paper. Two hundred names, fifteen years of my father's secret life, the whole hidden architecture of kindness that had raised me — and it weighed less than the hymnal I'd carried to church as a girl. I had sewn it into a canvas courier's wrap, flat against my stomach beneath my coat, the way Ansel showed me, and I kept waiting to feel it drag at me like sin. It didn't. It felt like a held breath.

The consulate barge was moored below the second bridge pier, lanterns lit, a flag I couldn't name hanging limp in the cold. Consul Weissmann had agreed — after Ansel's letter, after the coal barge fiasco, after I had stood in his cabin for twenty minutes while he explained, with diplomatic gentleness, all the reasons he could not possibly involve himself — to receive "a private document of humanitarian interest" at midnight, no witnesses, no receipt.

"No receipt," I'd repeated.

"Madam, if I gave you a receipt, I would be admitting the document exists." He'd adjusted his spectacles. "I am a coward in an expensive coat. But I am a coward who keeps his word, and my word is: put it in my hands before midnight, and it will be in my safe when the bells finish ringing, and no power in this republic will get it back. After midnight —" he'd spread his hands, apologetic as a shrug in a tailcoat — "after midnight it is merely old paper about old crimes, and old paper about old crimes is of no interest to anyone. Including me."

So: midnight. The army's fireworks had been scheduled, with typical military poetry, to end at ten minutes to.

I walked. That was the whole of my heroism — I put one foot in front of the other through a crowd of celebrating strangers, past the brazier-lit stalls selling roasted chestnuts nobody could afford, past Lieutenant Osta's adjutant checking papers at the quay steps, and I smiled at people, and I did not run.

Running was the one thing I could not do. Fifteen years of practice had taught my face to be furniture. My legs had no such training.

The adjutant at the quay was young — nineteen, maybe, with a chilblain on his ear and a list of names he was checking against the crowd with the solemnity of a priest at communion. I had rehearsed my answer to him the way I'd rehearsed everything since the coal barge: I am Coral Vane, customs clerk's daughter, walking off the last of the year's bookkeeping, going to look at the fireworks from the water, sir, if that's permitted.

"Name?" he said.

"Vane. Coral Vane."

His finger moved down the list. My heart did something acrobatic and unbecoming. Somewhere behind me a rocket screamed and burst, and the crowd gasped, and in that gasp his finger stopped.

Stopped. On a name. I could not see whose.

"Vane," he repeated, and looked up, and I prepared myself to lie with my whole body, to be the boring daughter of a boring clerk one final time —

"You're the customs house," he said. "The one with the good coffee. Corporal Denn says you water the coffee."

"Everyone waters the coffee," I said. "It's how you make coffee last through a war."

He almost smiled. The chilblain on his ear had split and he'd rubbed ash into it, which told me he'd been standing out here for hours and nobody senior had relieved him, and I thought, absurdly, *my father would have known your name by now, and your mother's name, and whether you had a sweetheart, and he'd have had you eating at our table by Thursday* — and then I thought, *my father knew two hundred names like yours, and carried them in a false drawer, and I dusted around them for fifteen years* — and I moved past him onto the quay while he was still deciding whether he'd been complimented or insulted.

The barge was forty feet away. The bells would start in six minutes.

* * *

**ANSEL**

At eleven-fifty I walked to the bridge checkpoint the way a man walks to his own trial, which is to say I walked there because it was the only direction left.

Ferran's trap had been elegant, I'll grant him that. Close every crossing, then announce one inspected crossing at dusk on New Year's Eve — the exact hour the amnesty died, the exact hour anyone with something to hide would have to move. He'd baited the hook with our own deadline. Anyone who came to that bridge tonight was confessing by the act of arrival.

Which is why I came to the bridge.

Not with the ledger — Coral had that, God and Weissmann's cowardice willing — but with myself. Fifteen years the army had hunted the courier who moved dissidents down the river towns, the ghost with the satchel, the man on the wanted circular Rask had once kept folded in his breast pocket like a love letter. Ferran had built a trap for that man. So I decided the trap should catch him. Every soldier on that bridge would be looking at me, and every question would be asked of me, and while they asked them, Coral Vane would be forty feet from a consulate barge, walking at a funeral pace, with two hundred lives sewn into her coat.

It was, when I examined it honestly — and I had examined it honestly all day, lying on the cot in the coal shed with my ankle throbbing like a second heartbeat — the first fully unselfish thing I had done since the last mission. Since the lockhouse, since I'd told her the truth with my hat in my hands: *I chose the dispatches. I left him on the towpath and I chose the dispatches.* She had not forgiven me. She had done something better. She'd said, "Then spend it at the bridge," and I understood that she meant I would spend my life there, or at least the part of it that mattered, and that this was the closest thing to absolution on offer.

The checkpoint was a barricade of ploughs and timber across the bridge road, lanterns hung on it like ornaments on the world's grimmest tree. Four soldiers. Sergeant Dravic with his ledger of crossings. And behind them, up on the bridge span, the silhouette of Ferran himself, watching the fireworks with his hands clasped behind his back, a general admiring his own artillery.

I stopped ten feet from the barricade and waited for the next burst.

It came — gold, enormous, the finale beginning early — and in the white wash of it I pulled off my cap and stood where the light would find me, and I said, in a voice I hadn't used in fifteen years, my real voice, the courier's voice:

"Sergeant. You'll want to check your circulars."

* * *

**CORAL**

Weissmann's clerk met me at the barge rail and looked through me as though I were weather, which was, I understood, the entire arrangement. A gangway. A cabin. A lamp turned low.

Inside, Weissmann stood with his watch open in his palm like a small silver verdict.

"Madam Vane." He did not say *you're late* or *you're early* or *are you quite certain*. He said, "It is four minutes to midnight."

I unpinned my coat. My fingers were steady — fifteen years of dusting, of pouring coffee, of smiling at soldiers, all of it had been rehearsal, and I finally understood that my father had trained me for this without ever once telling me, every silence a lesson in how to keep your hands quiet — and I unwound the canvas wrap and took out the ledger and put it on his desk between his inkwell and a photograph of some consulate function where everyone looked cold.

"Two hundred and six names," I said. "Dates. Safe houses. My father's hand. The marginal cipher is Ansel Sandoval's — he can decode it if you ever need it, though I've learned most of it myself."

Weissmann looked down at the ledger and did not touch it, the way a man doesn't touch a thing that will burn him.

"You understand what this is," he said. "Legally. After midnight, it is —"

"After midnight it's memory," I said. "Before midnight it's evidence. You're going to make it evidence. That was your word."

He closed his watch. Somewhere outside, the first bell of midnight began — the great cracked bell of the customs house tower, the one my father had climbed to every New Year's Eve of my childhood, the one I had thought rang only for the year.

He put his hand flat on the ledger. He drew it toward himself. He said, "It will be in my safe when the bells finish," and for the first time all night, the coward in the expensive coat looked me in the eye.

I had done it. I stood in a foreigner's cabin with my coat unbuttoned and the weight gone from my ribs, and I had done it, and the feeling was not triumph. It was more like setting down something you've carried so long you'd forgotten it was heavy — and then realizing you can't remember who you were before you picked it up.

Then, through the cabin's small salt-fogged window, across the black water, I heard it: the arrest roll, being read from the bridge checkpoint by lantern light, a clerk's flat voice carrying over the ice the way voices do on cold nights.

And the name was Ansel Sandoval.

* * *

**ANSEL**

Dravic's hands shook when he unrolled the circular. Fifteen years of damp and folding had not been kind to it, but a face is a face, and mine has always been too memorable for my own good — my mother said so, and so, eventually, did the republic.

"That's you," Dravic said. Not a question.

"That's me," I said. "Ansel Sandoval. Courier. At your service, and at the service of two hundred families you're about to hear a great deal more about."

"Sir —" one of the younger soldiers, to Dravic, not to me. "Should we send for —"

"Send for the Colonel," Dravic said, and his voice cracked on the word Colonel, and I watched a fifteen-year-old debt move through him like weather. He knew. He'd always known, I think, since the day I'd handed him the pardon for his brother and walked out of his checkpoint alive. He knew what he was holding and what it would cost.

"Dravic," I said quietly, while the fireworks died and the first bell began. "The brother. Milo. Is he —"

"Alive. Married. Two children." He said it like a man reciting evidence against himself. "He doesn't know why he's alive. He thinks it was an administrative error."

"Then let it stay one a little longer."

Up on the span, Ferran turned from the fireworks. I watched his head come around, watch the lantern light find my face, watch fifteen years of hunting arrive in a man's posture all at once — the straightening, the stillness, the predator's arithmetic. He began to come down the span stairs, unhurried, a man who had waited a long time and intended to enjoy the last few steps.

And that was when I saw, over Dravic's shoulder, at the far end of the bridge road, Lieutenant Osta pushing through the crowd with three men at his back — moving fast, too fast, his coat open, his face lit with the particular joy of a man whose suspicion has finally been promoted to certainty — and I understood that whatever happened next would happen in the space between Ferran's slow descent and Osta's fast arrival, and that I had no say in it at all.

The bell rang the second stroke.

Dravic looked at me. I looked at the water, where somewhere in the dark a consulate barge sat with two hundred and six names in its safe, if the bells finished, if the coward kept his word, if Coral Vane had walked her funeral pace fast enough.

The bell rang the third stroke, and Osta reached the barricade, and said my name like a man biting into it.

* * *

**CORAL**

I was still at the rail of the barge, watching the bridge, when Weissmann came to stand beside me — uninvited, which from him was practically a speech.

"Your courier," he said. "The one whose name they're reading."

"He isn't mine," I said. "He's theirs. He's letting himself be theirs." I watched the lantern light at the checkpoint, the small knot of men, the slow figure coming down the span stairs. "He's buying this. All of it. Every second they spend on him."

Weissmann was quiet a moment. The bell rang on — six, seven — and the fireworks were over, and the river was black and honest.

"Madam," he said at last, "in my profession we are required to believe that nothing is ever finally safe. But I will tell you what I believe tonight, off the record, in violation of my own nature." He touched the brim of his hat toward the bridge. "I believe that man on the bridge has just made himself the most valuable prisoner in this republic, and that everything he did for fifteen years, and everything you did tonight, now depends on what a sergeant with a guilty conscience does in the next ten minutes."

I looked at the bridge, at the small distant theater of it — Osta's raised arm, Dravic standing between my courier and the soldiers, Ferran descending like winter itself — and I found that I was not praying. I had done my part. The names were safe. Whatever came now, two hundred families would wake tomorrow to a year with their truth in it.

But I watched anyway. I watched the way you watch a door you've just closed on someone you love.

And across the water, faint under the last stroke of the midnight bell, I heard Dravic's voice rise — not reading a name, not giving an order, but shouting, shouting loud enough to reach the far bank, loud enough to reach me:

*"The Colonel will question him himself! Nobody touches him until the Colonel arrives — nobody —"*

The bell finished. The year turned. And on the bridge, under the first cold minute of it, Sergeant Ivo Rask stepped out of the crowd and placed himself, deliberately, between Ansel Sandoval and the rest of his life.
