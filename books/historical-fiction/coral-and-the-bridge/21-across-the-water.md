## 21. Across the Water

The fireworks began at half past eleven, which was either a mercy or an insult, depending on how you felt about the republic choosing its own death rattle for celebration.

I felt insulted. But I was grateful for the noise.

From the levee I could see the bridge lit up like a festival — lanterns strung along the rail, soldiers in their dress coats, Colonel Ferran himself standing at the midpoint like a man posing for a portrait he'd commissioned. The one inspected crossing. The trap with a bow on it. Every eye in town was aimed there, and that was the whole point of Ansel's plan: while they looked at him, nobody would look at a woman in a gray coat walking the opposite direction along the frozen towpath with two hundred names pressed against her ribs.

The ledger rode inside my coat, bound flat against my chest with the sash from my father's old dressing gown. It was warmer than I expected. Paper holds heat, apparently. Or perhaps my body had simply decided to lend all of mine to it, the way you'd cup a match in wind.

I had rehearsed this walk for six days. Not the walking — walking is easy; I'd been walking since before I could talk, presumably — but the face. The face was the hard part. For fifteen years I had practiced being unremarkable, and now I needed to be remarkable in exactly one way: a respectable woman out to see the fireworks, alone, slightly hurried, entirely boring. Not running. Running is a confession. A woman who runs is carrying something. A woman who walks too fast is merely late for her own life.

So I walked. Past the chandlery where Mrs. Aldous had hidden printers in her cellar and never once told me, though she gave me sweets every Sunday for a decade. Past the ferry office where old Bram kept his ledgers of false cargo, whose kindness to a fatherless girl had apparently been funded by my father's conspiracy. The town I had lived in all my life turned out to be a town I had never seen. I walked through it now seeing everything, and everything looked back at me with the faces from the ledger.

A firework went up — green, then gold — and the crowd on the levee gasped on schedule. Somewhere behind me, toward the bridge, a voice was calling names. I didn't let myself listen. Listening is also a confession.

* * *

The consulate barge sat at the downstream mooring, flying the flag of a country I could not have found on a map at gunpoint, which I suppose was rather the point of flags. Two lanterns burned at its rail. A sailor in a foreign uniform watched me come down the gangplank stairs with the expression of a man who has been told to expect nothing and is determined not to be surprised by it.

"I'm here to see the consul," I said.

"It is nearly midnight," he said, in our language but reluctantly, as though each word cost him a small fee.

"Yes," I said. "It is."

He looked at me the way men had been looking at me all week — Lieutenant Osta included — trying to decide whether I was furniture or weather. I had learned something in Osta's lamplit interrogation, something I hated knowing: that when a lie is load-bearing, you don't decorate it. You say the smallest true thing that will hold the weight.

"The consul is expecting papers," I said. "Before midnight."

That was true. Ansel had gotten word to the consulate three days ago through the dead-drop schedule that still functioned after fifteen years, which said something about dead drops versus governments — the dead drop, at least, had never changed administration. The consul was expecting papers. He did not know what papers. He did not know me. But the phrase worked on the sailor like a key cut from another key: not quite right, but the lock thought about it.

"Wait," he said, and went below.

I waited. Above the town the fireworks kept up their cheerful artillery, and beneath them, fainter, the arrest roll continued at the bridge. I could hear the rhythm of it even if I refused the words — a name, a pause, a name, a pause — the litany of a town surrendering its past to a bonfire scheduled for morning. At midnight, amnesty expired. At midnight, every pre-war case closed forever, every record burned, every name struck, no testimony admissible again. Ferran had announced it from the customs house steps with the satisfaction of a man closing a ledger he found distasteful.

Which meant the ledger against my heart had exactly the lifespan of the year.

The sailor came back up. "You will give the papers to me," he said.

"No," I said.

His jaw tightened. "The consul does not receive—"

"He receives these." I put my hand flat on my coat, over the ledger, and made myself stand the way my father must have stood in this same town fifteen years ago when some frightened stranger came to his door at night. Calm. Bored, almost. The authority of a woman holding something heavier than the man questioning her can imagine. "Tell him the courier's daughter is standing on his gangplank in the cold, and that she will leave when he comes up, or at midnight, whichever arrives first. And tell him that after midnight what I'm carrying will be worth exactly the price of the paper."

I don't know where the speech came from. That's a lie; I know precisely where it came from. It came from a childhood spent watching my father decline to explain things, and from one week of learning why. Silence, it turns out, is a discipline. Spoken at the right moment, it is louder than shouting.

The sailor went below again. This time he stayed longer.

* * *

While I stood there, I permitted myself — for the first time all week — to think about Ansel.

I had sent him toward the bridge an hour ago with the only thing I had left to give him, which was my attention fixed firmly elsewhere. That was the design. He walks into the light; I walk into the dark; the army has one appetite and one meal. He had shaved, absurdly, before we parted. Fifteen years a fugitive and he chose his surrender to include a straight razor borrowed from the lockhouse, as if presenting himself to Sergeant Rask as a gentleman rather than a ghost.

"You'll want to look like a man with nothing left to hide," he'd said, scraping his jaw by lamplight.

"You are a man with everything left to hide," I'd said. "That's rather the point."

"Yes." He'd studied himself in the little mirror, the way you study a stranger. "Coral. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong."

"If it goes wrong," he repeated, with the patience of a man who had carried dispatches through a war and knew exactly how often things went wrong, "the ledger matters more than either of us. More than both of us together. You understand that. Your father understood it. It's the only sentence of his creed I ever managed to learn."

"I know," I said. "I've read it two hundred times. There are two hundred reasons in it."

He'd smiled at that — a real smile, tired and crooked — and then he'd gone out into the fireworks, and I had watched him go, and I had not said the thing I most wanted to say, which was that I forgave him. I had decided forgiveness was a thing you spend, not a thing you say. He would spend it at the bridge. Saying it early would have been like paying a debt twice.

Now, on the gangplank, with the year dying above me, I wondered whether I'd been wise or merely stingy.

* * *

The consul came up at twenty minutes to midnight.

He was younger than I expected, round-faced, with the unhurried air of a man whose country had never lost a war because it had never arrived in time for one. He looked at me, then at my hand still pressed over my coat, and something in his face shifted from diplomatic to actual.

"Madame Vane," he said.

"Not madame. Miss."

"Miss Vane. You have something for me."

"I have proof," I said. "Two hundred names. Families in this district who sheltered dissidents before the war — dates, safe houses, routes. My father recorded them. The army is about to burn every record that corroborates them. After midnight, these people will have committed crimes that officially never happened, and if any of them are ever charged again, there will be nothing — no evidence, no precedent, no protection. What I'm handing you is the only copy in existence of the fact that they existed as anything other than loyal subjects."

He listened with his head tilted, the way you listen to a transaction. Good. Transactions I could do. Grief I could not have survived saying aloud just then.

"And your authority?" he asked gently. "For delivering this?"

"My father's," I said. "And mine. I've read every page."

"That may not be enough. My government is careful about—"

"Your government," I said, and heard my voice do something new, something with edges, "is standing on a barge in a river in a country that is about to erase two hundred families from its own memory. In four months your newspapers will be full of what happens here, and someone will ask your embassy what it knew. You can know this. Or you can spend the rest of your career not knowing it. Those are the options, and the second one expires at midnight."

The fireworks cracked overhead. Downriver, faintly, the roll call went on. Name. Pause. Name.

The consul held out his hand.

* * *

Here is what I had not anticipated: that giving it away would feel like amputation.

I had carried the ledger for nine days — against my skin, in my coat, in my dreams. I had slept with it. I had learned its weight so completely that my body had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a bone. When I drew it out and unwound my father's dressing-gown sash and put it in a foreign diplomat's gloved hands, some part of me went with it, and the cold came in where the paper had been.

He opened it there on the deck, snow beginning to sift down through the lantern light, and turned pages with a care that surprised me. He stopped at the middle. Read. Looked up.

"These are marked," he said. "In the margins."

"Cipher," I said. "Courier schedules. One of the couriers decoded them last week." I did not say his name. I could not yet say his name without my voice doing something unforgivable. "The marginal marks are a dead-drop route through the marshes. Still functional, apparently. Governments rot faster than chalk marks on a fence post."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the consul's face. He closed the ledger and held it against his chest — my gesture, returned, and I understood suddenly that I had not invented it, that everyone who holds such a book holds it the same way, against the heart, like a shield worn inside-out.

"This will go to my embassy tonight," he said. "By boat, ahead of the deadline. Copies within the month. Miss Vane — whatever happens in this town tomorrow, these names are now outside the reach of anyone who wants them burned. Do you understand what you've done?"

"Yes," I said. And then, because triumph is a debt too, and I have learned this year that debts are meant to be paid promptly: "No. Tell me. I've been too busy to find out."

He did smile then. "You've taken two hundred people out of a bonfire. Whatever else happens — whoever pays whatever price — that cannot be undone. Not by Colonel Ferran, not by midnight, not by anything."

Midnight. The word went through me like the ice through the marsh in chapter — no. Like the ice cracking under me in the marshes, the day Ansel twisted his ankle and we learned the routes had aged. I turned to the town.

The clock in the courthouse tower — the old clock, the one that had survived shelling, requisition, and my father's entire career — began to strike.

Twelve strokes. The whole town counted them; I could hear it in the pauses between fireworks, the held breath of a population waiting for its own pardon to expire. On the final stroke, a cheer went up from the bridge — the drunk, desperate cheer of people celebrating the last New Year of the world they knew — and under it, clear across the water, I heard the arrest roll change.

It stopped being a list.

It became a single name.

* * *

I want to tell you I didn't hear it. I want to tell you the wind took it, or the fireworks buried it, or that I had already turned back to the consul and missed it entirely. I have spent my life perfecting the art of not hearing things; it is the family trade; I should have managed it.

But there are silences and there are silences, and this one was shaped like a man's name spoken through a speaking-trumpet at the third checkpoint of Coral's Bridge, and it went into me the way the ledger had gone into the consul's hands: completely, irreversibly, against the heart.

*"Ansel Sandoval. Courier. Wanted in fourteen districts. Surrendered voluntarily at 11:52 this evening, claiming sole responsibility for the transport of seditious documents."*

Sole responsibility. He had done it. Even now, even surrendered, even weeping Sergeant Rask's prisoner, he was still carrying the dispatches — except the dispatches were us, were the town, were two hundred families and a customs clerk's daughter, and he had walked into the light and declared himself their entire weight so that no eye would turn downriver.

The crowd at the bridge roared. I couldn't tell if they cheered the name or the fireworks or the year. It didn't matter. Cheer is cheer; it covers everything; it had covered my father for thirty years.

"Miss Vane." The consul's hand was at my elbow. "You're bleeding."

I looked down. My palm, the one that had pressed the ledger all those miles, had a line across it — paper-cut, shallow, from the ledger's edge when I handed it over. A thin red thread on white skin. I stared at it with the detachment of a woman watching someone else's hand.

"It's nothing," I said. "It's the toll."

"The toll?"

"For crossing," I said. "Everything costs something. I learned that this year."

Snow was falling harder now, filling the space between the barge and the shore, softening the lights of the bridge until they blurred like memory. Somewhere behind me a clerk was writing Ansel Sandoval into a book of prisoners, and somewhere ahead of me a launch was carrying two hundred names toward an embassy, and I stood on the gangplank between the two facts with a cut on my hand and a silence in my chest where a courier used to be.

Fifteen years ago, my father taught me that the safest thing in the world was not to know. This year I learned the second-safest thing: that once you know, you cannot unknow, and that knowing has a price list, and I had now paid every item on it except one.

Because there was one entry left. I could feel it the way you feel a bill arriving.

They would try him. They would need a trial — Ferran loved procedure the way vain men love mirrors — and a trial meant testimony, and testimony meant witnesses, and there was exactly one person in the world who had read every page of the ledger, decoded its margins alongside the courier himself, and could stand in a courtroom and say *I know what these names mean, I carried them, I am the courier's other half.*

The consul cleared his throat behind me. "There will be questions," he said carefully. "About how these documents reached me. About the courier mentioned in the margins. You understand that my government will require—"

"Yes," I said, turning back to him, wiping my palm on my coat, leaving a red smear on gray wool that I decided to keep. "I imagine they'll require a witness."

Below us, the launch cast off into the dark with the names aboard. Above us, the last firework of the old year went up and burst and fell. And I, Coral Vane, who had spent fifteen years practicing silence, stood on a foreign gangplank in the first minute of the new year and began, quietly, to practice speech.
