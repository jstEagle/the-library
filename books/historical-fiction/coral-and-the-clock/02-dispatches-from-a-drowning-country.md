## 2. Dispatches from a Drowning Country

The morning the dispatch arrived, the fog over the embassy quarter was so thick that the gulls sounded like men calling to each other across a drowned world. I remember this because I counted them—four cries, then silence, then four again—as I walked the nine blocks from my lodging to the consulate, and because counting has always been how I keep fear at arm's length. Nine blocks. Thirty-one steps per block on the even-numbered streets, twenty-nine on the odd. Fourteen lampposts. One bakery whose windows breathed the smell of yeast and char into the grey air like a living thing.

I did not go in that morning. I had gone in the day before, and the day before that, and there is a discipline to hunger as there is to everything else: a courier who becomes predictable at a bakery counter becomes predictable everywhere. So instead I bought yesterday's heel from my coat pocket, already wrapped in newsprint, and ate it walking, and let the baker's wife see me pass her window without stopping. She had learned my face by autumn. She would never learn my name. That was not an accident; that was architecture.

The consulate building had been a merchant's house once, back when the republic still shipped things other than refugees. Its bones were gracious—wide stairs, tall windows, ceilings painted with fruit and vines by some artisan whose descendants were now, presumably, queueing at a port somewhere—but its flesh had gone shabby with exile. The wallpaper peeled in long curls like shed skin. The chandeliers had been sold two years ago to pay clerks' salaries, and their absence left pale circles on the ceiling, ghosts of light. We worked among these small humiliations with the politeness of people sharing a sinking boat.

Ambassador Reyes received me in the room that had been the merchant's library. He liked it because the shelves were still full—the ambassador's predecessor had refused to sell the books, on the grounds that a government without a country must keep at least its paper—and because the window overlooked the garden, where a single fig tree grew against the south wall, stubbornly, absurdly, in a climate too cold for figs.

"Wendell." He did not look up from the desk when I came in. He never did, at first. It was a habit of his, a courtesy performed in reverse: he gave you those first thirty seconds unwatched, to arrange your face. "Sit. Coffee?"

"Thank you, no."

"You look thinner."

"I look exactly the same as last week."

"That," said Reyes, "is what worries me." He signed whatever he had been signing, set down the pen, and only then raised his eyes. He was sixty-something and made of angles, a man who had spent forty years being underestimated on account of his smallness and had built an entire career out of other people's mistake. "How is your mother?"

The question landed the way questions about my mother always landed—a stone dropped into a well I kept covered. *She is well. Her heart is steady. She likes the boarding house. The nuns look after her.* All true, in the way that a photograph is true. None of it a lie large enough to hang a man for.

"She writes every week," I said. "She counts the days until the war ends in her diary. She says she will fill one book for each year."

"And does she ask what you do here?"

"My mother believes I am a clerk for a shipping firm."

"Then she is either deceived or very wise." Reyes smiled faintly, took a folded paper from the drawer he locked every night, and slid it across the desk. "You'll carry this today. And Wendell—" He waited until I looked up. "You'll read it first. I want it in your memory, not just your satchel. Paper burns. You don't."

*You don't.* It was the nearest thing to a compliment he ever gave.

* * *

I read it standing at the window, because Reyes preferred that couriers stand; sitting invited lingering, and lingering invited reading twice, and reading twice invited questions. The cipher was ours—the old riverine code, water-names for cities, currents for troop movements, silt for supply shortages—but underneath the cipher the meaning came through clean and cold as a hand around the throat.

The dispatch was from our military attaché in the capital, and it confirmed what we had all been pretending not to know for half a year. The southern front had folded. Not broken—folded, the way a table folds, tidy and total, so that the thing it supported simply ceased to be horizontal. Two armies dissolved into countryside. The capital's electricity failed for eleven hours a day now; the ministry telephones worked, when they worked, by favor of the enemy, who had not yet cut the lines because they no longer needed to. The government had moved its archives three times since spring, and each move had grown smaller, and the third archive was reportedly stored in a wine cellar belonging to a minister's cousin, which told you everything about how much of the republic remained to govern.

And then the sentence that mattered, set down with the attaché's characteristic dryness, as if he were reporting grain prices:

*Re: maritime evacuation. Consular sources confirm the vessel designated for final withdrawal will sail no later than end of quarter. There will be no second ship. Advise all essential personnel to settle their affairs.*

No second ship.

I stood at the window a long moment, longer than protocol allowed, watching the fog thin over the garden. The fig tree stood in its own small weather, leaves shivering. Somewhere behind me Reyes turned a page and did not hurry me, and I understood that he had given me the standing-and-reading time deliberately, that this too was part of his reversed courtesy. He wanted me to feel the weight of the thing before I carried it. A courier who knows what he carries walks differently. Slower, sometimes. More careful at corners.

I committed it to memory as I had been trained—not word by word but shape by shape, the way one remembers a face—and when I had it whole I folded the dispatch along its creases and said, "Where am I taking it?"

"The naval office, via the usual route. Then nothing else today." A pause, and then, in a voice pitched so casually I knew the casualness was constructed: "Wendell. When did you last see your family?"

"Two years ago." This was almost true. Two years, three months, and I had seen my mother through the glass of a train window while Tomas stood on the platform beside her, both of them waving, neither of them crying, because we had agreed—we had *agreed*, Tomas and I, on that platform, in the last minute before the whistle—that tears would make it harder for the ones leaving to disappear properly.

"And they are safe? They are out?"

"Yes." The lie came out level and smooth, worn smooth by repetition, a pebble of a lie. "My mother is with relatives in the north. My brother—" I hesitated, and the hesitation was real, though not for the reason Reyes supposed. "My brother is settled abroad. We correspond when it's safe."

"I see." Reyes looked at the fig tree. His hands lay flat on the desk, and I noticed—they were always steady, his hands, they were famous for being steady—that the right thumb pressed hard against the wood, whitening at the nail. "Then you should know that names are being drawn up. Berths on the final sailing. Thirty-one places, and I am told there are three hundred petitions already, from people who have served this republic in exile for years and have the paperwork to prove it. But some berths are mine to assign. By right of service." He turned his head then and looked at me directly, and his eyes held that terrible gentleness I had seen him use on grieving widows and panicked junior attaches, the gentleness of a man who has delivered bad news so often he has learned to deliver it warm. "You have served better than most, Wendell. Better than anyone I have, if it comes to that. When the list is drafted, yours will be on it. I want you to think about that. Think about who you would bring."

"Mr. Ambassador—"

"Think about it," he repeated mildly. "Don't answer now. Answers given quickly are answers given for the listener." He slid the dispatch across the desk toward me, and the moment closed like water. "The naval office. Usual route. Go."

* * *

I carried the dispatch the way I carry everything: inside me first, then on paper. Through the fog-thinned streets, past the bakery, past the shuttered hat shop whose window still displayed a single felt hat nobody would ever buy, down to the harbor road where the wind came off the water smelling of diesel and salt and something colder underneath, something mineral, like the smell inside a vault.

The usual route meant the fish market, and a boy named Anselmo who sold cockles from a barrel and took sealed papers folded into the lining of his cap in exchange for coins that were worth three times their printed face by now. Anselmo was fourteen and had the serenity of a much older man; his father had been shot in March, though he never said so, and I never said so, and this mutual silence was the closest thing to friendship either of us could afford. He tipped his cap. I bought a twist of paper holding six cockles I didn't want. Neither of us looked at the cap. The transaction took eleven seconds.

Afterward I stood at the harbor rail and watched the ships.

That was a mistake I made perhaps once a month, the way other men drink. The harbor was fuller than it had been in spring—tramp freighters riding high and empty, coastal steamers with their lifeboats doubled, a hospital ship with a great red cross painted on its funnel that someone had thought, optimistically, would protect anything. And tied up at the far pier, half-hidden by cranes, gray and patient as a sleeping animal, a liner. Too big for the coastal trade. Chartered, everyone said, by a committee that existed officially nowhere. The ship for the last evacuation, if it existed, looked like that: too big, too quiet, waiting.

Thirty-one berths. Three hundred petitions.

I tried to do the arithmetic Reyes had handed me and found my mind sliding off it, the way a hand slides off wet rock. If I had a berth—if I had a berth and a guest—then the arithmetic was simple and obscene. My mother, yes, instantly, without deliberation; my mother whose heart was failing, who counted days in a boarding house run by nuns, who believed her surviving son was a shipping clerk. My mother took one place. And the second place—

Tomas was dead. Officially dead, dead by telegram, dead in a government envelope that my mother had received with a stillness I have never been able to describe and never will. Dead two years. Except that I had never believed it, not entirely, not in the cellar of myself where belief actually lives—because there had been no body, no witnesses, only the word of a regime that lied the way rivers run, constantly and downhill—and because mothers do not believe such telegrams, and my mother's disbelief had become my inheritance, a debt I carried without acknowledging it.

But the ledger did not balance on disbelief. The ledger said: two places. Mother. And whom? A brother who might be dead? A name I could not put on any manifest without telling every authority in this city, including the ones who hunted us, exactly who I was and where I came from? I had spent five years making myself untraceable. I had burned photographs, changed the spelling of my own name twice, buried my mother's language under careful consonants. To claim a berth for Tomas Vasquez would be to unseal everything—to open the trunk, unfold the flag, walk out into the light with my whole self showing.

And if Tomas lived, where was he? In whose hands? Under what number?

The wind came harder off the water. Down the pier, dockworkers were loading crates marked with the insignia of an embassy I won't name—one of the lucky ones, withdrawing early, saving its silver and its secretaries—and one of the crates slipped the net and split on the stones, spilling not files but china, white cups rolling and cracking, and the workers laughed the way tired men laugh at anything breakable.

I thought: *there will be no second ship,* and I thought: *my mother counts days,* and the two thoughts ground against each other like millstones, and between them I fed something and crushed something, and I could not tell which was which.

Guilt, I have learned, is not a sharp feeling. Sharp feelings pass through you and are gone. Guilt is a slow pressure, a rising tide; you stand in it and it climbs—ankle, knee, waist—and every rational step you take to escape it only takes you into deeper water. I had lied to Reyes to protect my mother, lied to my mother to protect her, lied to myself to protect us all, and now the tide of all that well-meant lying had reached my chest, and out in the harbor the gray ship waited with its thirty-one berths like thirty-one lit windows in a drowning house.

Anselmo passed me on his way back up the quay with his empty barrel, and paused, and said, in our ritual exchange, "Good cockles today, señor."

"They looked fine," I said.

"Fine enough." He shifted the barrel on his shoulder. Then, not looking at me, in a voice too low for the gulls to bother with: "There's a woman asking after the consulate. Since Tuesday. Dark coat, doesn't buy anything, stands where the fog is. Anselmo notices people. It's how he stays alive."

My heart performed a small cold maneuver. "What does she ask?"

"Nothing yet. She watches who goes in." He resettled the barrel, tipped his cap. "A man with nothing hidden wouldn't care," he observed, philosophically, and walked away whistling.

I stayed at the rail a while longer, doing what I was best at: being unremarkable. A courier finishing his cockles. The fog was burning off; the harbor emerged detail by detail, rope and rust and gull-shit and the great gray patient ship, and I memorized the dispatch one more time from habit—*no later than end of quarter, there will be no second ship*—and felt it settle into me like swallowed stone.

Three hundred petitioners. Thirty-one berths. One dead brother whose handwriting I had dreamed about last week and told myself it meant nothing.

I turned up the hill toward the consulate, and the fog thinned ahead of me, and standing where the fog had been, outside the shuttered hat shop, in a dark coat, looking not at the consulate door but directly—at me, only at me, as if I were expected, as if I were late—stood the stranger from the bakery street, the woman with no name.

She did not smile. She did not approach. She simply looked at me the way one reads a letter one has waited a long time for, and then she turned and walked away, unhurried, and left me on the quay with the tide of everything I had been refusing to count rising past my ribs.

Behind me, out in the harbor, a ship's bell rang the hour, once, flat and clear across the water.

It sounded, to my ears that morning, like a clock beginning to run.
