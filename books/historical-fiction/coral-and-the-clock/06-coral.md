## 6. Coral

The city taught her hunger in its own dialects. There was the hunger of the stomach, which Coral knew well enough by now—she had learned to eat slowly, to make a heel of bread last through an afternoon of waiting. But there was also the hunger of the eyes, and that was the one that had brought her across borders, over mountains, into the gray salt wind of this harbor town where the exiled government kept its offices like a man keeps a fist closed over his last coin.

She was watching the consulate door when Wendell Vasquez came out of it.

She had been watching for eleven days. She knew the rhythm now: Wendell left at half past eight each morning with the flat, unhurried walk of someone who has practiced being invisible until invisibility became a kind of architecture. A coat cut deliberately dull. Shoes worn at the heel but polished. Eyes that touched everything once and rested on nothing. Coral had been trained by worse teachers than fear; she recognized the performance because she gave one herself every day.

But there were things practice couldn't seal. When Wendell passed the flower stall at the corner, their step faltered—not much, a half-beat, the length of a breath—and Coral understood that the white flowers, the ones sold for graves here, meant something to them. When the church bells rang the hour, Wendell's lips moved, just barely, counting. Counting what, Coral didn't know yet. She would learn.

*Tomas's brother,* she thought, and the name of him rose in her chest like water in a locked room. *Tomas's little sibling, grown into this careful stranger.*

She had a photograph. One photograph, taken before everything, passed to her in the prison by a guard who owed Tomas a debt he never named: Wendell at perhaps nineteen, unsmiling, standing beside their mother with one hand hovering behind her shoulders as if already bracing to hold her up. The photograph had been folded so many times the creases had gone silver. Coral could close her eyes and see it entire—the light, the awkwardness of the boy or girl or both who hadn't yet learned that the world would force them to choose a word for themselves. In the photograph Wendell's face was open. The person coming down the consulate steps now wore a face like a shuttered window.

Coral waited three more days before she walked through the consulate door.

* * *

The lie she had prepared was small and well-made, the way good lies are: a translator, newly arrived, seeking work with the delegation. Her credentials were forged but forged beautifully, by a man in the capital whose craftsmanship she still trusted, and her accent sat exactly where it should—a coastal lilt, neither from here nor from anywhere worth noting. She had rehearsed the story in front of cracked mirrors in four boarding houses. She had not expected the mirrors inside herself to be the problem.

Because the moment she stood in the corridor and Wendell looked up from the dispatch case they were locking, something in her chest went sideways. It was the hands, she realized later. In the photograph, Tomas's hands had been long-fingered and restless, always on the edge of gesture, and Wendell had the same hands, stilled by discipline, folded around a leather strap as though the strap might bolt.

"Mademoiselle?" Wendell said. The voice was low, careful, gendered in no direction she could name, and she heard Tomas in it again—that same habit of pitching the voice downward, making it smaller, a survival skill the family must have shared like a recipe.

"I'm looking for work," she said. "Translation. I have papers."

Wendell took her documents and read them the way she imagined they read dispatches: thoroughly, without expression, missing nothing. She watched their eyes move and understood that they were checking the forgery against some internal ledger, and she felt the old cold crawl up her spine—the cold of the transfer truck, the cold of the lists, the cold of being a name in someone else's file—and she made her face do what faces did in prison: nothing at all.

"You have good references," Wendell said at last. "For someone so young."

"I've been alive longer than I've been young."

Something flickered then, quick as a fish under ice. Recognition, almost—of the sentence, of the tone, of the person who might say such a thing. Wendell looked at her one second too long, and Coral knew with a sinking, singing certainty that this was a person who noticed things, that her whole plan rested on being noticed only in ways she chose, and that she had already failed.

"I'll keep your papers on file," Wendell said. "We may need translation work next month. Don't leave the city."

"I won't," said Coral, who had spent a year doing nothing else, and left before her face could betray how much she wanted those words to be true.

In the street outside she pressed her back to the wall and breathed until her heart stopped hammering. Then she checked, as she always checked, the tailor's mark on her coat sleeve—the little stitched anchor from the shop in her hometown where she'd bought it two winters ago, with money Tomas had earned copying sheet music, the two of them laughing about how a coat should outlast a war. She'd meant to unpick the mark. Every day she told herself she would unpick it, and every day she didn't, because it was the last stitched thing his money had ever touched, and destroying it felt like another small death.

That afternoon she saw Wendell pause on the corner, glance at her coat, and look away too quickly.

The crack had opened. Now came the question of what to pour through it.

* * *

She wrote the first letter four times before she got it right.

The problem was the handwriting. It had to be Tomas's—there was no point otherwise, no door it could open—and so Coral sat in her rented room under a lamp with a moth battering itself against the shade, practicing the looping capitals of a man she loved, copied from letters he had written her that she had memorized and burned and could still see whole behind her closed eyes.

*Dearest W.* — no. Too tender; Tomas would not risk tenderness in a letter that might be read. She started again.

*I am where the clocks stop.*

Seven words. She wrote them slowly, letting her hand remember the specific weight of his letters, the way the T crossed with an impatient slash, the way his a's opened at the top like small doors left ajar. She had learned his handwriting in the prison by reading everything he slipped her across six inches of stone bench: fragments of poems, maps drawn from memory, once a full letter about his mother's cooking that had made Coral cry into her sleeves at night so the other women wouldn't hear. He wrote the way some people prayed—steadily, secretly, certain someone was listening even if listening wasn't guaranteed.

She had loved him for nine months inside those walls, which is to say she had loved him for nine years compressed into the density of a place where time did not move. Prison time was its own element, thicker than water; you sank through it. They had met over the laundry, over a miscounted shirt, over her furious insistence and his absurd, unquenchable courtesy. He had told jokes in a whisper. He had known the Latin names of weeds growing in the yard cracks. He had held her hand through the wall's thickness during the night the executions happened, neither of them sleeping, both of them breathing in a rhythm they discovered they already shared.

And then the transfer. The trucks. The guards with lists, and her name on one list and his on another, and the moment at the checkpoint when a soldier's boot caught on a loose crate and the whole line stumbled and Coral ran—she had run, God help her, she had run and not looked back, and she had carried that not-looking-back ever since like a stone sewn somewhere behind her sternum.

One year. Fourteen months of finding the trail of a family scattered like ash: the mother moved twice, the sibling vanished so completely it was almost professional, a disappearance so thorough that Coral had needed three fixers, two bribed clerks, and one very dangerous favor to trace a courier's route to a consulate in a fog-bound port town. She had done it because Tomas had asked her to, in the last letter before the transfer, pressed into her palm so fast the paper was warm: *If I don't come back to you, find my family. Tell Elena I counted days for her. Find W., whoever W. has become. They will know what to do. They are the clever one.*

So she had found the clever one, and the clever one had a face like a locked drawer, and Coral was about to make them open it whether they wanted to or not.

She folded the letter, sealed it plain, and slept four hours with her shoes on, as she always did, in case of night visitors. What she did not write, what she kept in the deepest pocket of herself where even her dreams weren't allowed, was the other thing—the circular with her description, the alias flagged, the reward printed in neat official type for a woman seen escaping near Puerto Sombra. If Wendell knew that, would the clever one still help her? Or would the clever one do precisely what clever people did, which was protect what remained?

She decided, lying in the dark listening to the harbor groan, that Wendell would never know. Some deceptions were load-bearing. Remove them and the whole house came down.

* * *

She delivered the letter herself because she could not bear not to see it received.

She chose the moment carefully: evening, the embassy quarter going amber, Wendell crossing the small square by the fountain where the water had been shut off for the season. Coral fell into step beside them the way you'd join a procession, natural as weather, and slid the envelope under the flap of Wendell's satchel with fingers that did not shake, though everything under her skin was shaking.

"A gift," she said. "From a mutual acquaintance."

Wendell stopped. The square was nearly empty; a lamplighter worked his pole at the far corner, and his shadow stretched long between them like a third figure. Wendell looked down at the envelope, and Coral watched the color leave their face in stages—confusion, recognition of the handwriting, and then something raw breaking through the courier's practiced surface like a flood through a cracked dike.

"Where," Wendell said, and the word came out ruined, half-swallowed. "Where did you get this."

"Read it first."

Wendell read it. Coral watched them read it, watched their thumb press white against the paper's edge, watched their lips shape the seven words silently. *I am where the clocks stop.* She saw the moment the phrase landed and missed—she saw them reach for a meaning and find the shelf empty—and she felt, unexpectedly, a stab of tenderness so fierce it frightened her. This stranger, this careful creature, was Tomas's blood. Grief moved through their face in the same currents it moved through his.

"It's his hand," Wendell said quietly. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Two years. He's been dead two years. We were told—" Wendell stopped, jaw working. "There was a notice. Official. My mother lit candles."

"The notices are cheap here," Coral said. "They print them by the thousand. It saves them having to answer questions."

Now Wendell turned fully toward her, and up close their eyes were extraordinary—dark, quick, ringed with sleeplessness, holding hers with a force that made her want to confess everything at once: the prison, the laundry, the trucks, the running, the year of hunting them down, the way she said Tomas's name aloud only once a month, on the first, like communion. She stood in the flood of that gaze and gave them none of it.

"Who are you?" Wendell asked.

"A translator," said Coral. "Who sometimes carries messages."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that's free."

A gull screamed somewhere over the rooftops. The lamplighter moved on. Wendell's hand, still holding the letter, trembled once—a single fine vibration, like a struck string—and then stilled by visible effort, and Coral thought: *there it is, there's the steel, that's what Tomas meant by the clever one.*

"More letters exist," she said, because hope was a lever and she intended to use it, and hated herself a little, and used it anyway. "I can get them. I can tell you where he is. But not in a square. Not for nothing."

"What do you want?"

"Your help. Your access." She let that hang just long enough to be measured. "You work in places I can't enter, Wendell Vasquez. I know things you can't learn alone. That's all. For now."

She watched them flinch at their own name in her mouth—watched them register that she knew it, that she had always known it—and she turned and walked away before the conversation could turn into an interrogation, before her own face could crack along its fault lines. Behind her she heard Wendell call out once, voice stripped of all its careful competence:

"How is he? Is he—"

"Alive," she said, not turning, because that word she could give freely, that word was true, and she let it fall behind her like a coin into the dark.

* * *

That night she allowed herself the ritual. She locked the door, drew the curtain, and lay on the bed with the photograph held above her, Tomas's face in lamplight, his arm slung carelessly around a friend since disappeared, his grin crooked and entirely unguarded. Nine months she had kissed that mouth through a wall's gaps and a yard's blind corners. Fourteen months she had survived without it.

"I found them," she told the photograph. "The clever one. You were right—they're careful. Careful as a bank vault. I'm going to need you to haunt me a little, my love, because I don't know if I can pick this lock alone."

The moth had followed the lamp from room to room for three nights now. It settled on the photograph's corner, wings opening and closing like slow applause, and she let it stay.

Below her window the harbor clocks struck ten—all the clocks of this city working, all of them running, none of them stopped—and Coral closed her eyes and thought of a naval prison where time stood still, and of a letter in a dead man's living hand sitting tonight on a courier's table, and of how thin the ice was under all of them, and how, impossibly, it was holding.

Tomorrow she would go back to the consulate. Tomorrow she would begin teaching the clever one what the silences meant.

What she did not know—could not know, walking the edge of her own deception—was that three streets away, in an office smelling of ink and cold coffee, a man with patient eyes was pinning a fresh circular to his wall, and on it was a sketch of her face.
