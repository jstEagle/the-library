## 10. The Mother Who Counted Days

The tram out of the capital smelled of hot iron and salt, and Wendell rode it the way they rode everything now—shoulders loose, face empty, a man reading a newspaper who was not reading a newspaper. The paper was three days old and mostly advertisements, which made it perfect. No one watches a person reading about linseed oil and church bazaars. No one memorizes the face of someone that bored.

The line followed the coast north, past the shipyards where the cranes stood like patient herons, past the tanneries whose stink rose even through closed windows, and then out along the cliffs where the sea showed itself in slices—grey, then green, then suddenly enormous, the color of hammered tin under a high white sun. Wendell had made this journey eleven times. They knew it by the sound of the rails: the long rattle through the industrial flats, the hush as the track turned to face open water, and then the particular groan the wheels made at Kilometre 34, where the cliff had settled and no one had come to fix it. Eleven visits. Elena counted them. Wendell knew she counted because she told them so once, without accusation, the way she told people the weather.

*Fourteen weeks since the last one,* she had said, standing in the doorway of the boarding house with her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding a door shut from the inside. *I didn't say anything. I'm only saying I noticed.*

That had been in spring. It was nearly autumn now.

Wendell got off at the Penha do Mar stop, where the platform was a plank road and a bench painted the blue of faded church doors, and walked the last ten minutes up through the town. The boarding house stood on the second street from the water, behind a wall of bougainvillea gone half-wild, its blossoms so dense they seemed to be trying to swallow the gate whole. Senhora Beatriz took in seven boarders, most of them widows of fishermen, all of them old enough to have survived everything the century had done so far and too tired to be impressed by anything else. It was exactly the kind of place where a woman with a bad heart and no country could live quietly. It was exactly the kind of place Wendell had chosen for her—far from the embassy quarter, far from the lists and the roundups, a town so small the security services hadn't bothered to keep a man there.

That was what Wendell told themselves, anyway, on the tram, on the cliff road, every time. The town was too small to matter. The mother was too hidden to find.

They carried a basket: coffee, which was becoming currency; oranges; a bolt of the soft grey wool Elena liked for shawls; and, wrapped in wax paper at the bottom where nothing could spill on it, a letter. The letter said nothing. That was its whole art. It reported the weather in the capital, the price of sardines, the health of a fictional acquaintance named Dona Amália whom Elena had never met and had stopped asking about two years ago. Wendell wrote these letters the way they memorized codes—with total discipline, every sentence load-bearing, every kindness calibrated. *I am well. Work is steady. Eat slowly.* Beneath the words, unwritten, ran the true dispatches: *Tomas is dead. Do not hope. Hope will kill you faster than your heart will.*

Elena met them at the door before they knocked. She always did. She claimed she could hear Wendell's footsteps on the cobbles from three streets away, and Wendell had learned not to test this by changing their gait.

"You're thinner," she said, instead of hello.

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I rest when you visit more often." She held the door wide. She was small—she had always been small, but grief and the failing muscle in her chest had folded her further, so that she seemed assembled at half scale, bird-boned, her white hair pinned with the same tortoiseshell combs she'd worn in the old house above the printing shop. Her eyes were what undid people. They were quick and brown and entirely unsentimental, and they went over Wendell's face the way a tailor goes over a seam, checking for failures invisible to anyone else.

She found something. She didn't say what. She only took the basket out of their hands, which was her way of taking custody of the visit itself, and led them into the back room where the light came in off the water.

* * *

The room smelled of eucalyptus and camphor and, faintly, of the sea that came through everything in this town—through the walls, through the linens, through the bread. Elena's chair sat by the window. On the sill beside it, arranged with the precision of an altar, stood a row of small objects: a chipped figurine of the Virgin, a brass thimble, a dried orange studded with cloves, and a calendar page torn out and folded into a creased square. Wendell had seen the folded page on every visit for a year and had never asked about it. Asking would mean knowing, and there were already enough things in the room that knew.

Elena made the coffee herself, refusing help with a flick of her fingers, and Wendell watched her move between the stove and the table and catalogued what the months had taken. The pause at the top of the stairs—there was no stair now, but she paused anyway, out of habit, gathering herself. The way she breathed with her lips slightly parted, rationing air like a woman counting coins. She was seventy-one years old and had crossed a border in the back of a fish truck, had buried a husband, had surrendered a son to a war she refused to name as a war because naming it would make it official, and she did all of it now at a walking pace that would have alarmed a doctor. She had dismissed the doctor.

"Your landlady tells me you've been promoted," Elena said, setting down the cups. "Higher wages. More responsibility."

Senhora Beatriz told Elena nothing; Elena told Senhora Beatriz things, and later quoted them as reports. Wendell drank the coffee. It was terrible and scalding and exactly right.

"There's no promotion."

"No?"

"A cost-of-living adjustment. Everyone got it."

"Mm." Elena sat, folded her hands over the grey wool—they would get to the wool later; first she inspected the gift, ran her thumb across it, judged the weight, approved it silently—and looked out at the water. "And your work. Still carrying papers for men in good suits."

"Mother."

"I'm allowed to say it. You're in my house."

"It isn't your house."

"My name is on the rent book, which in this country is closer to ownership than ownership." But there was no edge in it. She was doing what she always did at the start of these visits: circling, probing, reading the shape of what Wendell carried the way Wendell read the shape of a sealed envelope. Elena Vasquez had spent thirty years married to a printer and twenty of those years feeding men who came in the back door with ink on their cuffs and names they couldn't say aloud. She could smell a secret the way other women smelled rain coming.

"The oranges are from the market near the consulate," Wendell offered.

"They're very good."

"Dona Amália is well."

"Wendell." Elena said their name the way she said it when they were nine and had lied about a broken window—gently, terribly, with the full authority of someone who had already forgiven them and was merely waiting for the truth to catch up. "Sit properly. I want to talk to you and you're going to want to leave halfway through, so sit in a way that makes leaving difficult."

Wendell sat properly.

* * *

Outside, the light had begun its slow slide toward evening, and the water below the street turned the color of pewter shot with copper. A fishing boat came in, low in the water, and the widows on the front step commented on its catch in voices pitched to carry no further than the wall. Somewhere a radio played a fado, the singer's voice frayed at the edges, holding one long note the way a drowning man holds a spar.

"I dream about Tomas," Elena said.

Wendell kept their hands around the cup. The ceramic was too hot. They held it anyway.

"Not the dreams you're thinking of," she went on. "Not the ones where he's dying—I had those, the first year, after the notice came. Those I could manage. You wake up and you know what's true and you put your feet on the floor and the day is simply the day." She turned the folded calendar page over in her fingers, not opening it. "These are different. In these he's working. He's sitting at a table with a lamp, doing something with his hands, and he looks up at me and he's annoyed, actually annoyed, the way he used to be when I'd stand too close while he set type. And he says—" her voice caught, just once, and she rode through it the way she rode through everything, at her own pace, breathing in measured portions— "he says, *Mãe, I haven't got time to be dead. I'm behind on my work.*"

"Elena—"

"And last month," she continued, as if Wendell had not spoken, "he handed me a printed sheet. In the dream I couldn't read it. My eyes wouldn't focus. But I woke up certain it said the same thing as always. *Behind on my work.*" She set the folded page down on the sill, next to the Virgin and the thimble. "So I did the arithmetic. Two years since the notice. Two years of a dead man complaining to his mother that he's busy. Either my heart has begun writing fiction—and I'll grant you it's had cause—or my son is alive, somewhere, being worked too hard by people who don't feed him."

The room was very quiet. The sea filled it.

"Tomas is dead," Wendell said. Their voice came out level, official, the courier's voice, and they hated it even as they used it. "The notice was confirmed. There was a witness. I told you everything."

"You told me everything except the thing I needed," Elena said. "You told me a confirmed death with a witness, in the passive voice, twice, both times while looking at my left shoulder instead of my eyes. You were very thorough. Your ambassador would be proud." She picked up her coffee and drank, her hand steady, and Wendell understood suddenly and completely that the tremor everyone feared—the tremor of the failing heart—was a thing she summoned and dismissed at will, a tool, like Wendell's blankness, like the unremarkable face. "I raised two children in a print shop. Do you know what I know about documents? I know that the state writes notices the way men write to mistresses they're leaving. Lots of formality. Very little truth."

"The notice—"

"The notice says what the notice says." She waved it away, a small sharp motion, cutting the thread of the argument cleanly. "I'm not asking you to confirm a rumor. I'm not asking you to tell me where he is or how or any of the dangerous things you think I can't survive hearing. I'm seventy-one, Wendell; I've been shot at by a government I helped cook soup for. What I'm asking is smaller and harder." She turned from the window then, and fixed them with the full weight of those quick brown eyes, and Wendell felt, absurdly, five years old, holding a broken window in their hands.

"I need you to stop lying to me," she said. "Even kindly. Even especially kindly. Because here is what kind lying does, my love—it tells me my son is dead and my daughter has vanished into some ministry basement and I am a poor old woman to be managed. Every gentle lie is a hand on my shoulder turning me toward the door. You think you're protecting me. What you're actually doing is deciding, alone, what I'm permitted to know about my own family. And I have news for you about that, which is that it's the loneliest thing anyone has ever done to me, and I have been through a war."

Wendell set the cup down. Their hands were not steady now. The cup clicked against the saucer and the sound was enormous in the small room.

"If I could tell you anything true," they began, "don't you think I would—"

"No," Elena said. "Actually, I don't. I think telling the truth is the one thing you can't afford, so you've made it the one thing you've forgotten how to do. You've gotten so good at being nobody that you've done it to me too. I'm not your mother anymore, Wendell. I'm your dependent. You file me under *safe* and *fed*, same as the boarding house, same as the coast, same as the letters about Dona Amália—who, by the way, does not exist, and never did, and we both know it, and it was a kind thought the first forty times."

The fado on the radio ended. In the silence after, the sea came in and out, in and out, patient as breathing.

"I dream he's alive," Elena said, softer now. "Every night for a year. Mothers in this country have been wrong before—God knows we've been wrong—but we've also been right, right when every official document in creation said otherwise, and the only instrument we have is the dreaming, because it's the only instrument they haven't figured out how to confiscate." She reached across the table and took Wendell's hand. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "So here is what I'm asking, and then we'll eat the sardines and discuss the wool and neither of us will mention any of this again. If he's dead, look me in the eyes and say it, once, plainly, and I'll believe you, and I'll stop, and you'll have bought yourself a mother who asks for nothing. And if he's alive—if there's any part of you, any drawer in any office in that beautiful sealed building of yours, that holds one scrap of paper with his handwriting on it—then you bring it to me. Not the whole truth. One true thing. That's all. One true thing, Wendell, before my heart finishes what it's been threatening, because I will not spend what's left of it being protected from my own children."

* * *

They ate the sardines. They discussed the wool—Elena held it to the light, pronounced it too grey, decided she liked it. Senhora Beatriz brought bread and gossip and was deflected with practiced ease. The evening came down over the water in layers of gold and bruise, and the widows lit their lamps, and the town performed its ancient pantomime of being too small to matter.

At the gate, in the dark under the bougainvillea, Elena kissed Wendell on both cheeks and held the third moment—the old family blessing—a beat longer than usual, her palm cool against the back of their neck.

"One true thing," she murmured. "Whenever you're ready. I'm not going anywhere quickly. My heart has bad manners; it keeps promising to leave and staying."

Wendell laughed despite everything, and walked down the dark street toward the tram stop with the basket empty against their hip, and did not look back, because looking back was a luxury couriers surrendered early and completely.

At Kilometre 34 the rails groaned their familiar complaint, and the train swung out to face the black water, and Wendell sat with the newspaper they weren't reading and felt the shape of the secret shift inside them—not lighter, but reorganized, the way a load shifts in a falling crate. *One true thing.*

They had one. They had carried it for six days, burning in the bottom of them like coal in a sealed hold: a letter in looping capitals, slipped to them by a stranger in a coat marked by a tailor from home. *I am where the clocks stop.*

The tram rattled north through the dark. Somewhere behind them, in a boarding house above the sea, their mother sat by a window with a folded calendar page and waited without waiting. Somewhere ahead, in the capital, a stranger named Coral knew the location of a prison and the price of a brother.

Wendell closed their eyes and made the decision they had been avoiding since the consulate steps: they would go back to Coral tomorrow. They would decode the sentence. Whatever it cost.

Their mother deserved one true thing. And Wendell was done, starting now, paying love in lies.
