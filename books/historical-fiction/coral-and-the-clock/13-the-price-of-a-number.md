## 13. The Price of a Number

Coral had learned in prison that every plan has three prices: the one you expect to pay, the one you dread, and the one no one mentions until your hands are already around it. The first two she could name easily enough. It was the third she carried now, folded small beneath her ribs, and she had promised herself she would speak it aloud tonight, because a plan built on someone else's ignorance was not a plan at all—it was a trap with better manners.

The boarding house room Elena had lent them smelled of camphor and sea salt, the windows fogged with the breath of a harbor that never fully slept. Wendell sat across the small table from her, sleeves rolled, forearms braced on the wood as though steadying it against weather only they could feel. A single lamp threw their shadows up the wall, two figures taller than the room deserved.

Coral laid out her papers the way she had once laid out contraband letters under a blanket in cell block C: flat, quick, everything face-down until the moment of showing.

"Before I begin," she said, "I want you to understand what I am not offering. I am not offering hope. Hope is what they give you in prison to keep you walking to the wall on your own feet. What I am offering is arithmetic."

Wendell's mouth curved, faintly. "You always talk like this?"

"Only when I'm terrified." She said it plainly, because she had learned that plainness was a kind of armor too, and because she wanted Wendell to hear the fear and keep listening anyway. "Arithmetic, then. There is a man inside Puerto Sombra—the naval prison, where the clocks stopped—who is called Aurelio. He was a paymaster before the purge. He kept the guards' accounts, the commandant's accounts, the accounts the commandant doesn't put in any ledger at all. He is still inside because he is more useful earning than dead, and he hates the men who put him there with the patient, bookkeeping hatred of a man who has done sums for six years."

She turned the first paper over. A hand-drawn diagram of the prison's administrative wing, copied from memory by a woman who had walked those corridors once, blindfolded, counting her steps.

"Aurelio can deliver Tomas. Not a rescue—rescues are for novels. He can have Tomas reclassified. A clerical death. Prisoner 4-1-9 dies of pneumonia on a Tuesday; a body goes into the lime pit—or something that will pass for a body if anyone looks, and mostly no one looks—and a living man walks out the labor gate with papers saying he is who his new documents claim. It has been done twice before. Both times successfully. Both times expensively."

"How much?" Wendell asked. Not flinching at the word. Coral had come to understand this about them: they met money, danger, grief—all of it with the same level gaze, the courier's instinct that anything named could be carried.

"Four hundred in gold, or its equivalent. Half now, half on delivery. I have most of it. Tomas had—arrangements. There is a woman in the old quarter who holds things for people who may not return, and she has held ours."

"Equivalent," Wendell repeated. "Meaning what he'll actually take."

"Meaning he'll take favors too, and the favor he wants is one only someone like you can give." Coral paused. This was the edge of it, the place where the second price showed its teeth. "He needs papers moved through official channels. Prison transfer authorizations, medical certificates, the stamps of offices that don't know they're stamping anything at all. Forged papers burn in the light of real bureaucracy. But a courier's papers—" she spread her hands, "a courier's papers are fireproof."

* * *

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a tram sang along the coastal line, and somewhere below a dog barked itself hoarse at the tide. Coral watched them the way she had learned to watch everything in prison—noticing, cataloguing, surviving on the small intelligence of other people's faces.

She knew what Wendell was calculating before they spoke. She could see it move behind their eyes like weather over the harbor.

"I can move documents," Wendell said slowly. "Records requests, cross-filed errands—I've been carrying paper between ministries so long they've stopped seeing me. If the papers look routine, I can get them stamped within a week." A pause, thinner. "But reclassification requires verification. You said that. Someone has to confirm the body, confirm the number, sign the certificate. The certificate has to be signed *inside*."

"Yes," Coral said.

"And I'm the only one who can be inside. Your face is known there—you told me. Aurelio can't leave. And a stranger wandering the administrative wing three weeks before an evacuation would draw more attention than the papers themselves."

"That's the shape of it."

"So I go in." Wendell said it the way they recited dispatch codes—flat, precise, already filed. "As what? The records office sends inspectors. Do they send them to prisons?"

"They sent one in March. A Mr. Ferreira, auditing prisoner labor allocations. Ferreira drinks. Ferreira's reports are copied wholesale from the prior year's. Ferreira, if a certain amount of money changes hands with the right clerk, will be ill for ten days and will gratefully accept a substitute who arrives with his letter of authorization and his glasses and his correct, boring questions." Coral slid the last paper across the table: a photograph of Ferreira's credentials, a list of his habits, the exact shade of his ink. "You would have four hours. Perhaps five. You verify the prisoner against the records—Tomas's number, his scars, the break in his left knuckle he got falling from the print-shop roof when he was nineteen. You sign. You leave. Ten days later, 4-1-9 dies of pneumonia."

"And Tomas walks out the labor gate with a stranger's name."

"With a stranger's name, onto a fishing boat Aurelio has already paid, and then—" Coral's voice caught, just slightly, on the seam between plan and prayer, "—onto the ship. Reyes's ship. If we have a berth to put him in."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved to trim the wick. In the silence Coral could hear the harbor working, chains and water and the low complaint of hulls, the sound of a city preparing to be abandoned by anyone clever or lucky enough to leave.

"You've been building this for a year," Wendell said at last. "Since you crawled out of that transport truck."

"Since before that, if I'm honest. Since the first night I heard him say my name through a wall." She let that stand. She had told Wendell about the prison in fragments—a shared blanket divided by concrete, letters passed hand to hand in the laundry, the transfer, the ditch, the running—but never all at once. Some stories lost their shape if you told them whole. "I built it without knowing who could execute it. Then I found you. So now I need to tell you the third price, Wendell, because you've earned it and because I won't build a floor over a hole."

Wendell looked up.

"The fixer network that touches Aurelio—the woman who holds our gold, the clerk who sells Ferreira's sick days—they are watched. Loosely watched, carelessly watched, but watched. And there is a circular with my description on it. Not my name—my name died in the ditch—but my face, my height, my left ear." She touched the ear in question, absently, the way one touches an old scar. "If Voss's people connect me to you, they connect me to the consulate, and the consulate connects to the manifest, and everything we are doing becomes evidence. Every document you carry. Every stamp you win for us. They wouldn't stop at Tomas. They would take the whole thread and pull."

* * *

She had expected anger. Or worse, the quiet recalibration of a person deciding how far to retreat. She had rehearsed her answers to both. What she had not prepared for was the way Wendell simply nodded—slowly, the nod of someone confirming coordinates—and began rolling down their sleeves.

"All right," they said.

"*All right?*" Coral stood. Her chair scraped. "That's all you have to say? I just told you I am bait walking around on legs, and you say *all right*?"

"What would you like me to say?" Wendell asked, and there was something almost gentle in it, which made it worse. "That I'm afraid? You know I am. That the plan is dangerous? Coral, I memorize codes for a government that has already fallen and simply hasn't admitted it. I buy bread from a man who knows my face and not my name. I have spent five years being unremarkable on purpose, and it bought my mother a room by the sea and my brother a lime pit." Their voice stayed level, but Coral saw the tendons in their hands go taut. "Now there is a version of the world where Tomas lives, and it costs four hundred gold pieces, some forged paper, and four hours wearing a coward's glasses. That is the cheapest thing anyone has ever asked of me."

"It isn't four hours." Coral came around the table. "That's what you're not hearing. If the inspection goes wrong—if the commandant's adjutant is diligent for one afternoon in his entire lazy life—you are inside the walls when the mistake is discovered. You know what happens to couriers inside those walls. You carry ciphers in your head. They do not let you out again quickly, or kindly, or ever."

"Then I'll be diligent back."

"Wendell—"

"You want me to say I'm frightened." They rose too, and now they were close enough that Coral could see the lamplight doubled in their eyes, could see the exhaustion underneath the resolve like sediment under clear water. "Fine. I am terrified. I have been terrified since you handed me a letter in a dead man's handwriting, and I will be terrified every hour until he stands on that gangway. But my mother taught us that fear is information, not instruction. And everything I know tells me this is the door." They exhaled. "Besides. You went through a ditch to get here. You crossed half a coastline with a wanted face and a pocketful of other people's letters. Did anyone ask your permission to spend you?"

Coral wanted to answer *yes, Tomas did, every day, that was how he loved things—by asking*. But the words snagged on something larger, the recognition that Wendell had just handed her own argument back to her wearing their voice, and that it sounded different, heavier, true.

"He used to do that," she said quietly. "Turn my arguments around on me."

"He was always better at arguing than getting caught at it." A ghost of a smile, then gone. "Tell me about the knuckle. If I'm going to identify him, tell me everything."

So Coral sat back down and told her: the roof fall, the scar like a pale river delta; the way he held his shoulders when he thought no one was watching; the hymn he hummed tunelessly, off-key on purpose, because the real melody was code and the guards had never noticed that a man who couldn't sing somehow always sang the same wrong notes. Wendell listened with their courier's stillness, taking it all into whatever sealed place they kept such things, and when she finished they repeated the details back to her perfectly, once, the way they memorized dispatches, and Coral felt something in her chest crack open that was not entirely pain.

* * *

Later—much later, when Elena's clock in the hall had struck two and the lamp had burned low—Coral stood at the window watching the harbor lights and felt the recklessness settle into her companion like ballast. She recognized it. She had worn it herself for a year: that calm that comes not from confidence but from having finally run out of safer options, the terrible liberty of people with nothing left to protect except the attempt.

"You should sleep," she said.

"In a minute." Wendell was writing—copies of nothing, practice strokes, learning Ferreira's cramped hand. "Coral. When this is done. When he's on the ship." They didn't look up. "What happens to the woman holding the gold? The one with the circular and the left ear?"

"She disappears," Coral said. "That's the design. That's always been the design."

"Designs change." Wendell set down the pen. "Mine just did."

Outside, the tide turned, and far across the black water the prison at Puerto Sombra kept its clockless dark, waiting for them with the patience of all things that believe they are permanent.
