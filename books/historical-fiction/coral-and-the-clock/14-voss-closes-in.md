## 14. Voss Closes In

Anton Voss believed in paper the way other men believed in God.

Paper did not lie, and it did not forgive, and it never forgot a name once that name had been set down in ink. Paper could be filed, cross-referenced, summoned back from the dead of a drawer years after the hand that wrote it had stopped writing. People lied to him daily—smiling consular clerks, sweating dock agents, widows with fabricated marriage certificates—but the paper always told on them in the end. A date that didn't fit. A stamp from the wrong office. An accent that survived even in handwriting, curling at the ascenders like a tongue reaching for home.

So Voss read. Every morning, before the light had properly committed itself to the day, he read: manifests, circulars, intercepted correspondence purchased from postal clerks whose salaries his office supplemented, police gazettes from four countries, the shipping registers of every vessel authorized to touch the capital's harbor between now and autumn. He read the way other men prayed—kneeling at his desk in the security attaché's residence with the shutters half-drawn and a cup of coffee going cold beside him, steam long gone from it, because reading required both hands.

It was on such a morning, three days after the translator woman had first been seen at the consulate, that the circular came back to him for the second time.

He had seen her photograph months ago—a poor print, grainy as a dream, a face that managed to be memorable only by refusing to be anything at all. *Wanted for questioning in connection with the escape of prisoners under transfer, Puerto Sombra district.* The file had grown thin additions over the year like rings in a tree: a sighting in the port city, an alias, another alias, then nothing. Files that went quiet were the dangerous ones. Noise meant movement; silence meant intention.

Now the same face had been described to him twice in nine days, once by a clerk at the records office who remembered a woman asking after naval prisoner registries—"polite, well-spoken, asked about transfer schedules as if she were planning to ship furniture"—and once by one of the consulate's own doormen, whom Voss paid in small notes folded into a newspaper each Friday. A woman seeking translation work. Dark coat. Waited nearly an hour without fidgeting. Left without leaving a name.

Voss set down the circular and looked at it for a long time, the way a man looks at a key he has finally found the lock for.

The coat, the doorman said, was fine wool, badly aged—the kind of coat that had been good once and refused to admit it. And there, when Voss held the description against the light of his memory, was the detail that mattered: a tailor's mark at the cuff. Not a label. A mark. Stitched thread, the old way, the provincial way, done by hand in towns where a tailor knew your shoulders the way a priest knew your sins.

He knew that mark. Everyone who had worked the interior knew it. It belonged to a shop in a river town two hundred kilometers north, a town that had given his office more trouble per capita than any city ten times its size. A printing town. A town of pamphlets and strikes and, in the last two years, of quiet arrests conducted before dawn so the neighbors would have less to talk about.

A town, Voss noted in his neat horizontal script, that had also produced one Tomas Vasquez. Printer. Resister. Officially deceased these two years, though Voss had signed enough death certificates born of administrative convenience to distrust the genre. And—his pen paused above the page—one Wendell Vasquez, younger sibling, formerly of the same address, currently unaccounted for in any registry Voss could access, which was itself a kind of signature. Invisible people did not happen. They were made, carefully, by someone with something to protect.

He wrote the name *Vasquez* and sat looking at it while the coffee finished dying beside him.

* * *

Ambassador Reyes received him at four o'clock in an office designed to exhaust visitors. Voss respected the design. The chairs were elegant and slightly too low; the light through the tall windows fell in such a way that a guest squinted or turned away; the ambassador himself sat behind a desk of dark wood with nothing on it but a telephone, a blotter, and a single folder squared to the desk's edge like a weapon laid down but not holstered.

"Señor Voss." Reyes did not rise. His handshake, when Voss crossed the room to take it, was dry and brief, the handshake of a man conserving something. "You honor our declining establishment."

"I'll be brief," Voss said, taking the low chair and refusing to let its geometry humble him. He arranged his own folder on his knee. "The evacuation vessel. The *Meridian*, sailing from Pier Nine. I require the passenger manifest."

Reyes lit a cigarette with unhurried ceremony, and smoke rose between them like a curtain being drawn halfway. "You require it."

"My government requires it."

"Your government," Reyes said mildly, "requires many things it does not receive. That is what makes governments endurable." He drew on the cigarette and studied Voss through the smoke with eyes the color of wet slate. "The manifest contains thirty-one names. Behind those names are three hundred petitions, most of them desperate, some of them fraudulent, all of them now my personal responsibility. If I begin handing that list to foreign security officers, Señor Voss, I will not be able to refuse the next request, or the one after that. The list will leak. Leaked lists become targets. Targets become empty berths. Do you follow me?"

"I follow you," Voss said. "I don't believe you."

Something moved at the corner of the ambassador's mouth—not quite a smile; more the place where a smile had been surgically removed. "No. I suppose you don't."

"The woman is not on your staff," Voss said. "She has appeared at your consulate twice. She has consulted the naval prison records under circumstances my office finds irregular. She matches a wanted circular issued sixteen months ago regarding the escape of prisoners under armed transfer." He let each sentence settle like stones in shallow water. "And she has been seen speaking at length with a member of your courier staff."

For the first time, Reyes's stillness changed quality—from the stillness of indifference to the stillness of attention. To anyone else it would have been invisible. Voss had spent twenty years learning to see the difference, the way a fisherman sees the second kind of ripple.

"You have been busy," Reyes said.

"It is my occupation to be busy." Voss opened his folder and removed the circular, laying it face-up on the ambassador's bare desk, deliberately breaking its symmetry. "Her name—or one of her names. I'll have the manifest by week's end, Ambassador. You may give it to me now, with my gratitude and my discretion, or I may obtain it through channels neither of us will enjoy. Your republic is concluding its affairs. There will be audits afterward. There are always audits. I would prefer that yours find nothing remarkable."

Reyes picked up the circular. He read it longer than the photograph deserved, and Voss understood that he was not reading the paper; he was reading his own position, weighing it, finding the load-bearing walls.

"There is no woman on my staff," Reyes said at last, setting the circular down and sliding it back across the desk with two fingers, a gesture of exquisite dismissal. "There is no woman in my employ, retained or otherwise. If a private citizen visits a public consulate seeking work, she commits no offense I am empowered to police. As for the manifest—it is not mine to give. The berths were allocated by the joint evacuation committee, under international guarantee. You may apply to them."

"The committee will take six weeks to answer an application. Your ship sails in eleven days."

"Then we will both learn patience," Reyes said, "or its absence." He stood, ending the audience with his whole body, and extended the dry brief hand. "Good afternoon, Señor Voss. Give my regards to your government. Tell them we kept every promise we made, and note carefully which promises those were."

In the corridor, descending the embassy stairs past portraits of ministers who no longer governed anything, Voss permitted himself the small luxury of certainty. Reyes knew the woman. More than that—Reyes had known Voss would come, had rehearsed the refusal, had chosen to spend credibility refusing it. Men did not spend credibility on nothing. Somewhere inside that beautiful exhausted building, the woman from the circular was not merely known but protected, and protection at that altitude meant she was attached to something the ambassador valued more than his own comfort.

Valued, perhaps, as much as a clean evacuation.

Voss stepped out into the late light, which lay along the boulevard like brass laid down for a funeral, and made three entries in his notebook. *Manifest: delayed, not denied.* *Reyes: compromised, method unknown.* *Courier: identify.*

Eleven days. Bureaucracy moved slowly everywhere except where it mattered, and there it moved like a blade through silk. He would have the manifest. He would simply build the pressure until handing it over became the ambassador's mercy rather than his defeat.

* * *

The docks at dusk were Voss's favorite classroom.

He went in plain clothes—a gray jacket, a stevedore's cap that no one looked at twice—and walked Pier Nine end to end while the *Meridian* sat at her mooring taking on stores, cranes swinging cargo nets overhead in slow pendulum arcs, the air thick with diesel and brine and the tarred rope smell of a working waterfront. Two hundred men worked the pier. Coaling gangs, winch crews, customs examiners, a cordon of harbor police whose sergeant Voss had bought years ago and who now touched his cap brim without appearing to see him.

Three hundred petitioners for thirty-one berths. Voss had reviewed the arithmetic until he could recite it like scripture. Desperate people massed at a dock produced exactly two things: crowds and information, and crowds were only information waiting to be organized. Whoever watched the pier controlled the story of the sailing. At present, everyone watched the pier—the exiles' committees, the foreign legations, the local police, the smugglers who sold forged priority letters to the gullible. Everyone, and therefore no one.

He found his man at the winch shed at the pier's landward end: a broad-shouldered stevedore named Ferro with a broken nose and a gambler's still hands, who owed money in three establishments and allegiance nowhere at all. They walked together along the seawall, and Voss talked quietly with his eyes forward, the way he had been taught, so that any watcher would see two men discussing wages.

"You'll be working the sailing," Voss said. It was not a question. Ferro's gang held the *Meridian*'s contract.

"I'll be working it."

"And you'll see everyone who comes near that gangway. Passengers, porters, officials, beggars. Especially the ones who don't fit. A woman, perhaps. Dark coat, good wool gone thin. Foreign-educated voice. She may come with a man, or meet one. The man works in the consulate quarter—you'd know the type by the shoes. Clean shoes on a dirty street."

Ferro spat tidewise off the seawall. "And if I see them?"

"You send a boy to the Café Imperial with a message. Nothing written. Just words. 'The coat is at the pier,' or whatever you've seen. You'll be paid monthly whether you see anything or not, and paid better the month you see something true." Voss took his arm briefly, a comradely pressure. "One thing, Ferro. Don't follow anyone. Don't ask questions. See and report. Curiosity on a dock gets men dropped into the water, and I dislike paying for corpses."

"I don't have curiosities," Ferro said. "I have rent."

"Then you'll do admirably."

Voss left him at the seawall's end and continued alone past the shuttered fish market, where the last light failed among the moored hulls and the harbor water moved black and patient beneath the pilings. From here he could see the *Meridian*'s bridge lights come on one by one, a small constellation assembling over the pier, and beyond it the dark mass of the harbor entrance where the sea began—that flat final border across which, in eleven days, thirty-one people would pass out of reach forever.

Thirty-one berths. One wanted woman. One unregistered courier with clean shoes. One dead printer who was very likely not dead, because in Voss's experience the dead stayed conveniently alive whenever killing them officially was easier than killing them actually.

He stood a moment longer in the tar-and-brine dark, consulting the ledger he kept behind his eyes, moving names like weights across a scale. The woman would surface again; women with missions always did. The courier would carry her somewhere, or carry something to her, and paper—the god he prayed to—would eventually testify. Reyes would hold out four days, five at most, before the pressure from the committee and the legations bent him, because ambassadors were men who had chosen comfort so long ago they had forgotten it was ever a choice.

Four days. Perhaps five.

Voss turned up his collar and walked back toward the lit streets, and behind him the tide came in over Pier Nine, indifferent, punctual, keeping its own count of the days.

That night, before sleep, he wrote one line at the bottom of the Vasquez page in his careful horizontal hand:

*Watch the mail. Couriers always write home.*

And forty kilometers up the coast, in a boarding house smelling of salt and camphor, an old woman was counting days on a calendar she thought no one read—while in the city, sealed and stamped and utterly unknown to either of them, a dispatch marked UNDELIVERABLE was already traveling back toward the hands that had sent it.
