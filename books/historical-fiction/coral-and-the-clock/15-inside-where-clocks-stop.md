## 15. Inside Where Clocks Stop

The bus to Puerto Sombra smelled of diesel and wet wool, and Wendell spent the two-hour ride rehearsing a name that was not theirs.

*Inspector Ardan. Ministry of Records, Division Four. Credentials in the left breast pocket, do not touch them again.* The forged travel orders Coral's fixer had produced were beautiful work—heavy paper, the right watermark, a stamp so precise it might have been stolen rather than copied. But papers were only the first door. The second door was the face above them, and faces had to be worn like uniforms: buttoned, pressed, unremarkable. Wendell had spent five years learning to be no one. Now they had to be someone specific, and the difference felt like walking backward off a pier.

Rain came sideways off the sea as the bus crested the last headland and the prison appeared below—a gray quadrangle stitched to the rocks at the harbor's edge, its walls furred with salt, its watchtowers standing against the low clouds like four fingers of a closed hand. Puerto Sombra. On the map it was nothing, a naval annex, a footnote. In certain circles it was a word spoken only in the plural of fear: *they took him to Puerto Sombra*, people said, and did not finish the sentence, because there was no finishing it.

Wendell pressed their forehead briefly to the cold glass and thought of Coral's instructions, given in a whisper on the night bus back from the records office, her breath quick with everything she wasn't saying. *They'll search your case. Let them. Act insulted, not afraid. Inspectors are always insulted.* She had taken Wendell's hand then—hers steady, Wendell's not—and held it hard enough to leave marks, as if she could press the plan into the bones where trembling couldn't reach.

*You will see him,* she had said. *That is the cruelty and the gift of it. You will see him and you cannot let your face know him.*

The bus shuddered down into the town. A boy sold roasted chestnuts on the quay; gulls argued over fish guts; a woman beat a rug from an upper window with slow, resigned strokes, as though even the dust here had learned to move at prison tempo. Everything ordinary. That was the horror of such places—that they sat inside ordinary towns like stones inside bread, and the towns rose and baked around them every morning all the same.

Wendell got off at the administrative gate, straightened the borrowed hat, and became Inspector Ardan.

* * *

The search was exactly as Coral had promised: two guards, methodical, bored. They went through the document case page by page while Wendell stood with hands folded, wearing the expression of a man whose time was being stolen by idiots. It cost something to produce that face. Somewhere beneath it, the real Wendell—the one who had memorized codes and burned drafts for five years, who had made a religion of being forgettable—was screaming in a small sealed room. The screaming stayed where it belonged.

"Purpose of visit," said the older guard, a man built like a mooring post.

"Audit." Wendell let the word come out flat with contempt. "Division Four believes this facility's prisoner registers are six weeks behind schedule. I am here to establish whether the delay is incompetence or obstruction. Which would you prefer me to write in my report?"

The guard's jaw worked. He stamped the ledger without another word. Bureaucracy, Wendell thought, stepping through into the yard, was its own country, and in that country insolence was a passport. They had crossed its border years ago and never surrendered citizenship.

A clerk named Ferrán—Coral's fixer's man, though to look at him he was only a tired functionary with ink-stained cuffs—escorted Wendell along whitewashed corridors that smelled of carbolic soap and the sea's permanent damp. His patter about filing systems was flawless and meaningless. Between his sentences ran the real message, delivered in the code they had agreed upon, each phrase innocent to any listening ear:

"The east wing holds the older ledgers. *Mind the third stair, it's uneven.* The prisoners' effects are catalogued separately now. *Since the escape, everything changed.* You'll want to verify against the daily counts yourself. *He's in the east wing, third block, count verified Tuesday—alive.*"

Wendell's heart performed no theatrics. Five years of carrying dispatches had taught them that the moment of receiving the message was the most dangerous moment of all; the body must learn to treat revelation like weather, arriving and passing without comment. So they nodded, asked a dry question about pagination, and followed Ferrán up the uneven third stair.

And there—the first thing, before anything else—the absence.

No clocks. Of course. Coral had told them the story: after the escape of '34, the commandant had ordered every clock removed from the prison, convinced the escapees had timed the changing of the guard to the second by watching the ward clock through its window. Nothing replaced them. The walls bore pale rectangles where pendulum cases had hung, ghost-furniture, and the silence they left behind was worse than ticking would have been. Time in Puerto Sombra did not pass; it accumulated. Men grew old here the way driftwood wears smooth—not marked by hours but sanded down by their endless repetition.

*Tomas is where the clocks stop.* Two years of that silence. Two years of a man living inside a hole where time should have been.

Wendell kept their stride even and their eyes on the ledgers, and hated the absence more than they had ever hated anything with a face.

* * *

The audit itself was blessedly mechanical. Ferrán had arranged for Wendell three hours alone with the east wing registers, ostensibly cross-checking transfer documents, actually standing in a corridor of shelved names while pretending to read them. Wendell read anyway. It seemed a kind of trespass not to. Page after page of numbers where men used to be—Prisoner 2214, theft of army rations; Prisoner 1988, sedition; Prisoner 2051, unknown offense, transferred, returned, transferred again. The republic's collapse compressed into a clerk's hand, columns of suffering ruled neatly in pencil.

Tomas was not Tomas here. Wendell found him on the third block's register under a number Coral had whispered like a password—**4177**, entered fourteen months ago, transferred from an inland camp, occupation listed as *printer*. Alive as of Tuesday's count. Alive. Wendell touched the edge of the page and allowed themselves three seconds—counted against their own heartbeat, the only clock they carried—to feel the vertigo of it. Hope, they discovered, was a form of altitude. Despair you could live at; despair was sea level, breathable. Hope took the air out of you fast, made your ears ring, made every step downward afterward feel like falling.

Ferrán reappeared at the appointed hour with keys. "Inspection of the block," he announced for the benefit of the escorting guard, a young one with a chapped face. "Division Four wants conditions witnessed. Standards," he added, with the weary irony of a man quoting regulations he didn't believe in.

The corridor of the third block was dim, lit by high slit windows webbed with wire. Cells on the left, bars on the right, and between them a running grate set low in the wall—ventilation, the young guard explained, though it looked more like a wound the building hadn't closed. The prisoners were at labor detail; the wing held only echoes, dripping somewhere, the sea working patiently at the foundations below. Wendell walked with a clipboard they did not need, marking boxes at random, letting the guard see only a functionary's boredom.

Ten steps into the corridor, they saw him.

Not at once as a person—as a color, first. A smudge of darker cloth at the far end of the row, kneeling, scrubbing the flagstones with a long-handled brush, one prisoner left behind from the detail to finish what the others had missed. Then the shape resolved: broad shoulders gone narrower than memory, dark hair grown long and pushed back from the forehead with the same impatient sweep, the same gesture, the identical gesture, that had moved across a kitchen table twenty years ago over homework and bread crusts—

Wendell's lungs forgot their trade.

*Do not stop. Do not look twice. You are a man named Ardan and you have never loved anyone in this building.*

They kept walking. One step. Another. The brush moved in slow arcs; the kneeling figure did not look up; the water on the stones spread thin and gray. Ten feet. Wendell had crossed oceans, borders, checkpoints, five years of careful erasure—and here, at ten feet, in the smell of lye and stone dust, stood the reason for all of it, scrubbing a floor, unaware or pretending to be unaware, alive, alive, *alive*.

Tomas looked up.

It happened the way weather happens—without permission. Perhaps the footsteps broke some rhythm; perhaps some animal sense older than either of them turned his head. His eyes came up through the lamplight, passed over the inspector's boots, the clipboard, and arrived at the face above them.

Wendell had prepared for this the way one prepares for drowning: knowing the theory, doubting it would hold. Their face held. Some deep-buried muscle of craft, trained by years of being unremarkable, locked everything down but the pulse in the throat, which they could do nothing about, which hammered visibly, they were sure, they were *sure*—

But Tomas's eyes. God, his eyes. For one full second they were empty, a prisoner's flat assessment: guard, clerk, threat, nothing. And then they changed. Not recognition—not yet—but a kind of gravitational pull, attention snagging on something it couldn't name, the way a compass needle shivers before it finds north. His mouth began to open.

Through the grate, low in the wall, Wendell spoke.

Four words. Spanish, pitched for the vent, buried beneath the scrape of the brush and the drip of the pipes and the young guard's bored shuffling ten paces back:

"*Mamá still counts days.*"

Nothing else. No name, no promise—names were contraband, promises were suicide—but those words were a key cut to one lock alone. Only two people alive knew what Elena Vasquez did every morning at the window: counted days aloud, one for each day since the family scattered, a rosary without saints. Only two people alive could say that sentence and mean home.

The brush stopped.

Wendell did not stop walking. That was the discipline, the terrible discipline: to keep moving while behind them a man knelt motionless on wet stone, understanding arriving through his body faster than his mind could safely permit. Wendell reached the end of the corridor, turned, and let their gaze pass back down the row one final time, inspector's survey, clipboard raised, impersonal.

Tomas was looking directly at them. His face had not moved—it couldn't, trained by two years of clocks that didn't tick—but his eyes were flooded, absolutely transformed, and his hand had risen without his knowing, pressing flat against his own chest as though holding shut a door behind which something enormous was breaking loose. His lips shaped a single soundless word. Even at ten feet, even across five years of official death, Wendell knew its shape the way one knows the shape of one's own name.

*Wendell.*

They marked a box on the clipboard. They turned away.

* * *

The rest was mechanics, and mechanics saved them. Ferrán signed the inspection log; the young guard returned the document case unsearched this time, chastened by the earlier display of rank; the gate, the yard, the administrative office where Wendell dictated two paragraphs of dry findings to a stenographer while their handwriting would have betrayed them. *Conditions adequate. Registers current within acceptable variance.* Somewhere a report would be filed that meant nothing and protected everything.

Only one task remained, and it required the chapel.

Every facility kept a chapel; the navy was pious by regulation. Puerto Sombra's occupied a low room near the east wing, smelling of candle wax and mildew, its hymnals racked in scarred wooden pews, its altar bare except for a crucifix that had lost one arm and kept the other extended anyway, as if gesturing at some argument it intended to win eventually. Wendell entered alone—"a moment for prayer before departure," which no bureaucrat in this country would dream of refusing—and stood among the empty pews listening to the sea grind the harbor rocks below.

The verification token sat in their breast pocket: a small brass button, civilian-made, with a shank soldered into the shape Coral had described—proof to the fixer inside that contact had been made, that the outside was real, that payment and papers would follow. Small enough to swallow in an emergency. Warm from riding against Wendell's heart all day, as if it too had been praying.

Wendell drew a hymnal from the rack—the third pew, fourth book from the left, the location Coral had specified from a year of memories she gave sparingly, each one costing her—and opened it. Pages of numbered verses, worn soft by thumbs. Men had sung from this book while the clocks were gone and the walls thickened; there was something unbearable in that, songs rising through a building designed to make time stop, the human voice insisting on rhythm when rhythm had been confiscated. Wendell slid the token into the binding's gutter, deep in Psalm 23, *though I walk through the valley of the shadow*—and yes, they chose that psalm deliberately, sentiment be damned, because some jokes belonged only to God and the smuggled—and pressed the covers closed. The brass disappeared into the book like a coin into water.

Then, because no one was watching, and because they had perhaps thirty seconds of being no one left, Wendell allowed their forehead to rest against the pew back. The wood was smooth, polished by generations of exactly this gesture—foreheads, griefs, centuries of them. They did not pray in words. Words were for dispatches. They simply held the image of what they had seen: a brush stopped mid-arc, gray water spreading, a hand pressed to a chest, a brother's mouth shaping their name into air too heavily guarded to carry it.

*I saw you. I am coming back. Mamá still counts days.*

They replaced the hymnal, fourth from the left, exact, and walked out into the gray afternoon wearing Inspector Ardan all the way to the gate.

* * *

The bus back did not leave until evening. Wendell spent the interval in the town's one respectable café, drinking coffee that tasted of chicory and watching the prison wall over the rim of the cup the way one watches a sleeping dog—without staring, without turning away. Rain had stopped; light broke raggedly through the clouds and lay along the wet quay. Fishing boats came in. Boys dove for coins thrown by dockworkers. The world insisted on itself, ordinary and shining, and Puerto Sombra sat at the edge of it holding its stolen hours.

Wendell counted what they had accomplished against what it had cost. Contact confirmed. Token placed. Tomas alive—Tomas *knowing*, now, that the world had not forgotten him; that was worth everything, and dangerous beyond measuring, because hope in a place like that could get a man killed as surely as a knife. Ferrán would retrieve the token tomorrow. Papers within the week. The fixer's plan, Coral's fierce architecture of rescue, assembling itself piece by piece.

Three days. The ship sailed in three days. The extraction was planned for the night before.

Wendell paid for the coffee with Inspector Ardan's expense money, left Inspector Ardan's hat brim low, and boarded the evening bus carrying nothing contraband at all—only a heartbeat that had not slowed since morning, and ten feet of distance they would spend the rest of their life trying to close.

The bus pulled out along the coast road as the light failed. Below, the prison's searchlights woke one by one, sweeping the darkening water, patient as tides. And it was then—head against cold glass, exhaustion finally breaking through discipline—that Wendell noticed, three seats back, a man in a civilian raincoat who had boarded at the last stop outside the naval administration building. He had not bought coffee at the café. He had not waited anywhere. He consulted a pocket notebook, wrote something brief, and looked out his own window at the same searchlights.

Perhaps nothing. Dockworkers rode this bus; clerks; the whole gray population of a garrison town going home.

But the coat was very good—too good for a clerk—and as the bus rounded the headland Wendell caught, reflected in the dark glass, the man's eyes fixed steadily not on the prison behind them but on the back of Wendell's neck.

Wendell did not turn around. They watched the road ahead instead, black and wet and winding toward the city, toward Coral, toward the arithmetic of berths and brothers already waiting there—and began, quietly, methodically, to compose the lie of who Inspector Ardan had been today, just in case anyone ever asked.
