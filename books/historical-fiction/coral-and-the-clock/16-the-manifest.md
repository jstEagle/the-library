## 16. The Manifest

Ambassador Reyes had learned long ago that power lived in paper, and so did death.

He sat in his office above the consulate with the shutters half-drawn against the afternoon glare, the harbor below him glittering like spilled coin—a beautiful country dying by increments, and he its increasingly ceremonial mourner. Thirty-one years of diplomatic service had taught him to read a room before he entered it and to read a man before the man finished his first sentence. Anton Voss, seated across the desk with his hat on his knee and his hands folded over it like a man at a funeral he was enjoying, required no reading at all. Voss was here for blood, and he had brought the paperwork to prove it.

"Ambassador." Voss's voice was mild. That was the worst of him—the mildness. Men who shouted were men who could be managed; Voss spoke as though reciting inventory. "I wonder if I might see your passenger manifest for the *Aurora Mercedes*. Sailing on the nineteenth."

Reyes let the silence sit between them exactly three seconds. Long enough to suggest consideration. Not long enough to suggest fear.

"You know," Reyes said, rising to pour coffee from the silver pot that had survived two revolutions and one fire, "in thirty-one years, I have been asked for manifests by customs officers, harbor masters, two prime ministers, and once by a bishop who was convinced a cardinal was smuggling paintings. You are the first to ask me in the voice one uses for ordering fish."

"I'll take the coffee black."

"Of course you will." Reyes poured it anyway, cream and two sugars, because pettiness was the last luxury of a drowning government and he intended to spend every drop of it. He set the cup before Voss and remained standing, which gave him the small satisfaction of looking down at the man's neatly parted hair.

"The manifest is a consular document," Reyes said. "Protected under the evacuation protocols your own ministry countersigned in March. Perhaps you've forgotten; your ministry has signed many things lately that it later found inconvenient."

Voss did not reach for the coffee. He simply looked up, and there it was—the patience, the terrible unhurried patience of a man who knew something Reyes did not yet know.

"March was March," Voss said. "This is October. My government now considers certain passengers on that manifest to be matters of state security rather than matters of mercy." From his breast pocket he drew a single folded sheet, laid it on the desk, and smoothed it flat with two fingers, an archivist's gesture, almost tender. "A circular. Issued four days ago. A woman traveling under the name Coral—no surname confirmed—wanted in connection with the escape of a prisoner during transfer from Puerto Sombra, fourteen months ago. There is reason to believe she has been seeking contact with persons bearing the surname Vasquez."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Reyes did not move. Thirty-one years. He had kept his face through artillery audible from the embassy garden, through the night the president's brother came begging for forged papers, through the morning he had signed away his own nephew's berth to a stranger with better documents. He kept it now. But inside his chest, something cold and precise began counting, the way a clock counts when the house has gone quiet.

"Vasquez," Reyes repeated, as though tasting an unfamiliar dish. "A common enough name in the southern provinces."

"And Vasquez is also the surname of your courier. Wendell Vasquez. Six years of service. Decorated twice, though never publicly—one does not decorate ghosts." Voss finally lifted the coffee and drank, black after all; he had refused the sugar only to watch Reyes pour it. "A remarkable servant, by all accounts. Unremarkable in person, they say. That is the highest compliment in your profession, isn't it, Ambassador? To be unremarkable?"

"It is the highest compliment in any profession," Reyes said. "You would know, if you had ever achieved it."

Something flickered behind Voss's eyes—not anger. Amusement. That was worse.

"The manifest," Voss said again, gently, as if reminding a child of a chore. "Or the ship's papers. Without them, the *Aurora Mercedes* does not clear the harbor on the nineteenth. She will sit at Pier Nine, three hundred desperate souls aboard, while my officials inspect her hull, her cargo, her ballast, her crew—thoroughly. Repeatedly. Until she misses her tide window, and then until she misses the next one, and so on, until either the papers appear or the war arrives at this harbor ahead of schedule. I understand the naval guns are forty kilometers out now. One can hear them at night, if one listens."

One could hear them at night. Reyes heard them every night. He stood at his window with his coffee and counted the intervals like a man counting the pulse of a patient he already knew would die.

"If I give you the manifest," Reyes said slowly, "you will take these people off my ship one by one, into your interrogation rooms, and some of them will not come back."

"Some of them," Voss agreed pleasantly, "are not who they claim to be. That is rather the point of inspection." He set down the cup and stood, settling his coat with a small efficient motion. "You have twenty-four hours, Ambassador. Deliver the manifest to the port authority by noon tomorrow—or deliver your courier, if that arrangement suits your conscience better—and the ship sails clean. Otherwise I seize the papers at noon and we discover together how many of your three hundred petitioners can survive a winter in this harbor."

At the door he paused, hat in hand, and turned with something almost like courtesy.

"For what it is worth—and it is worth little—I do not believe you are protecting a spy. I believe you are protecting a family. It is a weakness, but a human one. My mother had it too." He put on his hat. "Noon tomorrow, Ambassador."

The door closed. Reyes stood for a long moment in the sudden quiet, listening to the sea guns far away, keeping their terrible time.

Then he opened his desk drawer, took out the manifest—he had never intended to withhold it; withholding was merely the price of dignity—and read it through for the hundredth time. Thirty-one berths. His finger stopped at number nineteen, where the clerk's hand had written *Coral — translator, papers pending*, and beside it, in his own annotation, the small private mark he used for persons under his protection: a single stroke, like a candle flame.

*Reyes-adjacent alias.* That was what Voss's circular would call it, once some clerk cross-referenced the consul-general's protection mark with the wanted list. Reyes-adjacent. As if a person could be an adjacency. As if all of them—Coral, Wendell, Elena in her boarding house by the sea, Tomas in a cell without clocks—were merely satellites of one another, orbiting some vanished center that had once been called home.

Reyes took out his pen and began, very carefully, to think.

* * *

The trouble with twenty-four hours, Reyes reflected that evening, was that it sounded generous and was not. Twenty-four hours was one tide. It was one night's sleep, denied. It was precisely enough time for a man to draft six cables and burn five of them.

By eleven o'clock he had filled his wastepaper basket. Option the first: surrender the manifest, sacrifice perhaps a dozen passengers to Voss's interrogators, save the remaining two hundred and eighty-eight, and live out his exile knowing exactly what his signature had purchased. Option the second: refuse, lose the ship entirely, and wrap himself in principle while the harbor filled with people who would never leave. Option the third—

Option the third was the one he kept circling, because option the third required Wendell Vasquez, and Wendell Vasquez deserved better than to be spent like currency. And yet. And yet. Voss had named the trade himself, almost carelessly: *deliver your courier, or the papers.* A known courier with ciphers in his head was worth more to Voss's intelligence service than any single fugitive woman. Which meant, inverted like everything else in this rotten autumn, that Wendell's surrender might buy more than the ship. It might buy the whole manifest's safety. It might even buy time for whatever reckless thing the Vasquez brothers were surely planning, because Reyes had read dispatches long enough to know when a courier was carrying two kinds of mail, and Wendell had been carrying two kinds for weeks.

But to offer that trade—to even think it in the privacy of his own skull—felt like the beginning of betrayal, and Reyes had promised himself, thirty-one years ago, that he would betray no one whose name he knew.

So instead he did the smaller thing. The oblique thing. The thing that cost him something real and saved nothing measurable.

At midnight he summoned his attaché, a sleepless young woman named Ferran who had learned never to ask questions, and gave her a dispatch folder. Inside was a routine ciphered message addressed to Wendell's dead-drop at the bakery on Calle Ancha—the baker who knew faces and not names; Reyes had always liked that detail, had found it almost liturgical. The message itself said nothing. It was filler, padding, the ordinary weekly traffic confirmation. But on its face, before sealing it, Reyes instructed Ferran to process it through the port liaison channel rather than the usual pouch—the channel that, as of Tuesday, ran directly past Voss's new inspection office at the harbor authority.

Ferran frowned at the folder. "Sir, that channel is compromised. We flagged it ourselves on Tuesday."

"I know."

"It will come back marked undeliverable."

"I am aware of how the postal system functions, Lieutenant."

She looked at him then—really looked, the way the young sometimes did, with their whole unguarded faces—and Reyes saw her understand it: the message was not meant to arrive. Its entire purpose was the stamp. *UNDELIVERABLE*, in red ink, processed through a channel Wendell would recognize as watched. It would tell the courier, in the only language available, three things at once: *the manifest is taken; the harbor office is Voss's now; and someone paid a price to tell you.*

It would also, incidentally, document for any future tribunal that Ambassador Emilio Reyes had knowingly routed sensitive traffic through enemy channels—which was the cost. That was the part that made it real. Every warning has a payer. He had learned that late, but he had learned it.

"Seal it," he said. "Send it tonight."

* * *

The stamp came back at nine in the morning, red as a wound, and Reyes held the returned envelope for a moment before sending it on to Wendell's second drop, feeling in his palm the full weight of everything he could not write.

There had been a time—he allowed himself the memory, briefly, because mornings were for weakness—when he had believed diplomacy was a matter of eloquence. That the right sentence, delivered in the right room, could hold back armies. Thirty-one years had cured him. Diplomacy was a matter of stamps. Of what got delivered, and what came back, and who paid the postage in silence. His whole magnificent useless career had been conducted in a language of returned envelopes, and now, with the harbor guns close enough to rattle his windows at night, he had sent his finest work: a blank letter that screamed.

At ten o'clock he drafted the formal response to the port authority, buying hours with procedure—*the manifest will be released upon completion of consular audit, per Article Nine of the evacuation protocols*—knowing it would buy perhaps six hours, maybe eight. A clerk's stalling. A candle flame drawn in pencil.

At eleven, Ferran brought him the morning intercepts, and among them, buried in harbor traffic, a line that stopped his pen mid-word: *Naval transport scheduled, Puerto Sombra to capital, prisoner transfer, sailing 0600 the nineteenth.*

The nineteenth. The same dawn as his ship.

Reyes set down the intercept and looked out the window at the harbor, at Pier Nine where the *Aurora Mercedes* lay with her three hundred berths and her thirty-one tickets, at the water that carried everything away and brought nothing back. Somewhere in this city, a courier was about to learn that clocks had begun running faster than ships. Somewhere a woman named Coral was being hunted by a man who drank his coffee black. And somewhere in a cell where time had been removed from the walls, a printer's hands were presumably still ink-stained, still alive, still counting nothing.

Twenty-four hours. He had spent them badly and well, as one spends everything at the end.

Reyes pulled a fresh sheet toward him and wrote, in his own hand, the note he would deliver personally to Wendell Vasquez that afternoon—no codes, no channels, just four sentences in a courier's own borrowed idiom:

*Your package was returned undeliverable. The post road through the harbor is closed. All packages now travel at the sender's risk, and the last collection is sooner than the posted schedule.*

He read it twice. It was not enough. It was everything he could afford.

Far out across the water, the guns spoke once, twice, and fell silent—as if even the war were waiting to see what the courier would do next.
