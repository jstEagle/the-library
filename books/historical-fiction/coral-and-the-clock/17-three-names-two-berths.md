## 17. Three Names, Two Berths

The arithmetic came to me at four in the morning, the way terrible sums do—arriving uninvited, patient, exact, standing at the foot of the bed like a creditor who knows he will be paid.

Two berths. Three names.

I had known it for days, of course, in the way one knows a storm is coming from the color of the light. But knowledge kept at arm's length is not the same as knowledge held, and somewhere between the third bell of the night watch and the first gray seeping under the door of my room above the chandlery, the numbers stopped being abstract and became flesh. Tomas, who might walk out of Puerto Sombra in four days if the fixer was paid and the papers held and the verification done. The ship, which sailed in three. Elena, whose heart beat like a clock with a worn mainspring, who had already packed her small suitcase twice and unpacked it once because hope was too heavy to carry and too cruel to set down.

And me. Wendell Vasquez, who had spent five years learning to be no one, now asked to decide which someones would continue to exist.

I dressed in the dark. I had become expert at dressing in the dark; it is a courier's skill, along with memorizing codes and burning drafts and eating bread without tasting it. My hands did their work while my mind did its terrible accounting, and by the time I reached the harbor road the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the evacuation ship sat at her berth like a promise someone else would have to keep.

She was called the *Meridian*. I had walked past her every morning for eleven days, and every morning she seemed larger—not grander, but more consequential, the way a loaded gun on a table changes the shape of a room. Three hundred petitioners for thirty-one berths. Reyes had given me two. Two berths, and behind me a trail of names: my brother's, my mother's, Coral's, my own. Four names if you counted mine, and I had stopped, lately, being sure that I should.

* * *

Coral was waiting for me at the bakery.

That was the first sign that something in her had shifted. She knew my routines better than I had taught her—she had learned them the way she learned everything, by watching until the watching became knowing—and she knew I bought bread from old Ferro every morning at half past six, from the man who knew my face but had never once asked my name. For Coral to place herself inside that ritual meant she wanted to catch me before the day caught me. Before the consulate swallowed me into its corridors of sealed envelopes and whispered instructions. Before I could hide again inside competence.

She stood at the counter pretending to study the pastries, and her coat—the one with the tailor's mark from our hometown stitched discreetly into the lining, the mark I had recognized before I recognized anything else about her—hung on her the way flags hang on windless days: present, but without conviction.

Ferro handed me the loaf and looked between us with the mild curiosity of a man who has outlived his interest in other people's stories. I paid. Coral fell into step beside me, and we walked toward the water without agreeing to.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"Neither did you."

"I slept." Her accent had thickened; it always did when she was frightened or lying, and I had long since stopped trying to distinguish which. "I dreamed about the prison. That counts as sleep the way a stone counts as a pillow."

The harbor spread below us, gray-green and restless. Fishing boats were going out, their lamps still lit against the early murk, and beyond them the *Meridian* rode at anchor with her boilers breathing smoke, impatient as any living thing that has been told to wait.

"The fixer sent word," Coral said quietly. "Four days. He needs the forged transit papers finalized, and he needs the verification token placed—he needs you, Wendell, or he won't move at all. He doesn't free numbers for strangers."

"I know."

"And the ship sails in three." She said it flatly, the way one reads a headline about a distant flood. Then she turned to me, and in the low light her face was stripped of all its borrowed composure, all the translator's calm she wore like a uniform. What was underneath was younger than I expected, and harder. "So we stop pretending there is a version of this where everyone lives. We choose. Out loud. Today."

* * *

We walked to the breakwater, where the stones were slick with spray and the wind came off the sea carrying salt and diesel and the faint sweetness of kelp rotting in the shallows. There was a bench there, iron and rotted wood, facing the open water as if the sea might be persuaded to offer testimony. We sat. Gulls argued over something invisible below the wall.

"Say the choices," Coral said. "All of them. Don't soften them."

"Coral—"

"Don't soften them." Her hand found mine on the cold slat between us—not tenderly, not yet, but firmly, the grip of someone bracing another person for a blow they intend to help them survive. "You have spent your whole life softening things for the people you love. Kind lies. Careful silences. I've watched you do it for weeks, and it's beautiful, and it is going to kill us all. So say it plainly, just once, just here, where only the gulls are listening."

The wind pulled at my collar. Far out, a freighter's horn sounded, long and low, like an animal mourning something it couldn't name.

"Elena takes one berth," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, stripped of its diplomatic varnish. "That is not negotiable. Her heart will not survive another winter here, and she will not survive the country that's coming, and she has spent enough of her life being brave on behalf of other people's secrets. Elena sails. That's the fixed point. Everything else moves around it."

"Yes," Coral said. "Go on."

"That leaves one berth and three people. Tomas, who is alive, who has been alive for two years while we mourned him, who is a number in a cell where they took down the clocks so the prisoners would lose track of God. Me. Or you." I made myself look at her. "And whichever of us doesn't take it—if Tomas isn't freed in time, whoever stays is staying in a country that will be Voss's within months. A courier with ciphers in his head. Or a woman on a wanted circular with a photograph that looks exactly like her."

The gulls screamed. The sea said nothing.

"So here is what I have decided," Coral said.

"Don't."

"You haven't heard it."

"I know what you're going to say, and don't."

"Wendell." She turned fully toward me then, and took both my hands in hers, and her fingers were cold as the breakwater stones. "I escaped during a transfer. Do you understand what that means? It means I know the transfer routes. I know the guard rotations at Puerto Sombra better than your fixer does—I lived under them for fourteen months. I know which warders can be bought and which ones pretend to be bought and then report you. I have false papers that have already passed one inspection, papers in a name that has never been whispered in this country's police stations because it belongs to no one. You are a courier. You are valuable, and known, and if Voss takes you, everything in your head goes with you—every cipher, every route, every name of every person who ever trusted you with an envelope." Her voice cracked, just once, and she pressed on through the crack. "Tomas is my—"

She stopped. The word she didn't say hung in the salt air between us, enormous.

"He told me about you," she said instead, softer. "Before. In there. He talked about his brother who could memorize anything, who burned drafts like other people burned letters, who disappeared so well that even the family stopped being able to find him. He talked about you the way people talk about saints. I found you partly for him and partly because I needed to believe that someone could vanish that completely and still exist." Her thumbs moved across my knuckles, unconscious, maddening. "Give me the berth. Give me the money for the fixer. I'll use my own papers for the prison—they expect a woman on the transfer lists anyway, it's cleaner—and I'll bring him out. You sail with Elena. When the war turns, he comes back for me. He promised me once that he would, and Tomas does not break promises, he only breaks himself against them."

"No."

"Wendell—"

"No." I pulled my hands free, and immediately regretted the loss of them, and did not take them back, because taking them back would have been surrender and I could not afford surrender, not with three days left. "You want me to say things plainly? Fine. Plainly: your plan requires you to enter Puerto Sombra with a face that is printed on a circular sitting in at least three police offices in this city. Plainly: if Voss has connected the circular to the consulate—to us, to me, and I think he has, I have seen a man reading a newspaper on the corner of the Calle Real for two days who never turns a page—then the moment your papers touch an inspector's desk at the prison gate, you are finished. And not quickly. Not the way we get to imagine it, clean and dramatic. Slowly. In a cellar. With questions." My voice was rising and I let it rise; there was no one to hear but the birds. "And you want me to sail away on a ship with my mother, drinking tea, while the woman I—" 

Now it was my turn to stop.

The word sat between us. Neither of us picked it up. Both of us knew it was there, had known for weeks, had been circling it the way you circle a fire—close enough for warmth, careful of the burning.

"Plainly," Coral said, barely above the wind. "Finish it plainly."

"If you go into that prison, I lose everything I have left," I said. "Tomas alive in a cell I can't reach. My mother on a ship crossing an ocean away from me. And you in the ground or worse, and all of it chosen, all of it arranged, by you, to spare me." I stood, because sitting still had become impossible; the grief in me had outgrown the bench. Below the breakwater the waves broke green and white and indifferent. "You think I haven't run the same sum? I've been running it since four in the morning. Take Elena, give her my berth, stay myself, trade myself to Voss for Tomas—a courier with codes in his head is worth more than one prisoner, maybe Voss deals, maybe he doesn't, but it keeps you off the tables and my mother safe and it costs only what I'm worth, which after five years of being no one, I confess I am no longer entirely sure of—yes. I've run that number. Every hour since midnight I've run that number. So do not stand there and tell me yours is the only sacrifice on the menu."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The tide worried at the stones beneath us, patient, rhythmic, keeping its own time.

Then Coral began to laugh—a small, broken sound, half sob, aimed at nobody, at the sea, at the whole obscene arithmetic of the year. She laughed until it turned into something else and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, and I sat back down beside her because there was nowhere else in the world to sit.

"We are monstrous people," she said thickly, "to fall in love in the middle of this."

"I know."

"We're going to ruin each other's exits."

"It's likely."

She leaned into me then, her shoulder against mine, her weight real and warm and mortal, and we watched the fishing boats come back in with the morning's silver flashing in their holds, and I understood that this was what the war had made of tenderness: two people arguing over who would be permitted to die, and calling it love, and being right.

* * *

The message from Reyes reached me at noon, delivered by the embassy's junior attaché with the bland efficiency of a man carrying paper he has not read: *Ambassador Reyes requests the courier's final confirmation of passenger names by 18:00 tomorrow. Manifest closes thereafter. No amendments possible.*

Final confirmation. The words had the ring of a church bell, of a verdict, of a door being locked from the outside.

I carried the slip to the harbor office where the manifest clerk worked behind a grille of brass and smoked glass, and I stood there holding it, and through the window I could see the *Meridian*'s third funnel being painted, fresh black over old black, the ship dressing herself for departure the way the dead are dressed for burial.

Two berths. I thought of Elena's suitcase, packed and unpacked and packed again. Of Tomas's looping capitals saying *I am where the clocks stop*, as if time itself had been arrested along with him. Of Coral's cold hands and colder courage, offering herself up like coins from her own purse.

Three names. Two berths. And underneath them all, in letters so small I had refused to read them until now, a fourth line: the name I had been erasing for five years, the name that fit no berth and no escape and no future, the name that was, perhaps, the only currency I still possessed outright.

I folded the slip into my breast pocket, over my heart, where I carried everything I intended to burn.

There was one person I had not yet asked. One person whose claim on the arithmetic outweighed every calculation Coral and I had made in our beautiful, ruinous stubbornness. My mother, who counted days. Who dreamed true. Who had told me once, in another life, across the border, in the dark of a refugee train: *Wendell, a lie told kindly is still a lock on someone else's door.*

I bought second-class ticket to the coast. The train left at seven.

By the time I reached Elena's boarding house, the sea fog had come in thick as wool, and the lamps of the little town burned haloes in it, and my mother was awake—of course she was awake, she had been counting—and when she opened the door and saw my face, she knew before I spoke. Mothers always board first. She stepped back to let me in, and said, quietly, in the old language, the one from before everything:

"Tell me all of it. Even the parts you came here to keep."
