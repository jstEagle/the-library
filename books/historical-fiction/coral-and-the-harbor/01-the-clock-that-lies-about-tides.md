## 1. The Clock That Lies About Tides

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in observatory livery who would not come past the door of Ashgrove & Sons, Clockmakers, and who spoke to Freya as if reading from a script he did not understand.

*The master clock at the Saltmere Observatory has fallen into irregularity. The Society requests the attendance of a competent tradesperson. Wednesday. Seven o'clock sharp. Come round to the service stair.*

*A competent tradesperson.* Not *the clockmaker*. Not even *the apprentice of Mr. Ashgrove*, though her father's name was on the shop and his tremor had put the work in her hands for two years now. The Society wanted hands, not a name, and Freya understood that well enough. Hands could be paid and dismissed. Names had to be remembered.

She went at half past six, because she had learned that arriving early was the only kind of power an outsider got to keep. The fog was up over the harbor, thick as wool, and the town moved through it in murmurs — fishwives calling directions they already knew, a bell buoy somewhere off the mole clanking like loose rigging. The observatory stood on the headland above it all, a white drum of a building that caught what little light the morning offered and gave back nothing. It had been built eight years ago with money from the Crown and from subscribers whose names were gilded on a board in the entrance hall. Magistrate Corliss's name sat at the top of the list, larger than the others, as everything of Corliss's was.

The service stair wound up the outside of the building, iron and salt-eaten, and Freya climbed it with her toolbag knocking against her knee — the good bag, calfskin, holding her files and pliers, her loupe, her oil horn, and a folded square of chamois for carrying small parts without losing them to cracks in the floor. Her father had told her once that you could tell a clockmaker's whole character by what she carried. He had not said what it told the world when she carried it up a tradesman's stair while gentlemen entered by the front.

*Invisible,* she reminded herself. *You are here to fix a clock. You are nobody until you leave.*

A steward met her at the top, a dry man named Pell who looked at her the way one looks at weather — noting it, disapproving of it, unable to do anything about it.

"You're early," he said.

"Clocks run early or late. I prefer to know which."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth that might have been amusement, quickly suppressed. He led her down a corridor smelling of lamp oil and cold stone, past a door marked *Transit Instrument*, past another marked *Computing Room*, from behind which came the scratch of pens and the low murmur of men saying numbers aloud to each other. At the end of the corridor, a wide oak door with a brass plate: *MASTER CLOCK. Authorized Persons Only.*

"He's been losing," Pell said. "Four seconds a day last month. Eleven seconds yesterday. The Astronomer is — displeased." The way he said it suggested *displeased* was a word chosen from a menu of far worse ones. "You'll report to me, not to him. And you'll not speak to the gentlemen if they pass through."

"And if a gentleman speaks to me?"

"Then you'll answer briefly and look at your tools."

Freya nodded and thought, but did not say, that she had spent twenty-two years looking at her tools and found them better company than most gentlemen anyway.

Pell unlocked the door and left her alone with the clock.

It filled the room the way a heart fills a chest. A long-case regulator, taller than any man, its case black walnut gone amber at the edges where hands had touched it across decades, its pendulum hanging dead still — which was the first wrong thing, because no keeper worth his salt would let the master clock stand stopped, not even for repairs. The second wrong thing was the smell: machine oil, yes, but under it something sharper, chemical, like the solvent printers used to strip type. Someone had been working on this clock recently, and recently, and had not cleaned up after himself properly.

Freya set her bag down, opened the case, and forgot to be invisible.

The movement was beautiful. She would give even the Society that. A regulator of the old school, weight-driven, with a deadbeat escapement and a gridiron pendulum that some craftsman decades dead had tuned so finely it could feel the difference between a warm Thursday and a cool one. She let down the weights carefully, checked them against her hand, and began at the beginning, the way her father had taught her: train, escape wheel, pallets, suspension. Look before you touch. Listen before you look.

Eleven seconds a day was not wear. Wear wandered; it drifted like fog, a second today, three tomorrow, never the same twice. This loss was steady, deliberate, growing by fixed increments — which meant something in the train was *changing*, day over day, in a way that worn brass does not change.

She found it within the hour.

At the third wheel of the going train, where there should have been a plain pinion, someone had fitted a piece of Freya's trade that she had seen perhaps four times in her life and never in a tide-regulator: a variable-ratio gear, two concentric rings of teeth cut to different depths, joined at a slip-plate that allowed the effective diameter to shift along a slow screw. A cam, essentially. As the cam turned — and it turned on its own secondary train, driven off the great wheel, patient as erosion — the wheel's ratio crept, and the crept ratio stole time, second by second, day by day, so smoothly that no observer watching the dial would ever see anything but an aging clock gently failing.

Except that this clock did not only show the time. It drove the recording apparatus.

She saw that when she followed the secondary train to the back of the case, where a pen assembly rode above a paper-covered drum that revolved once a day. The harbor's tides were logged here — had been logged here for years, the height of every high water traced in ink by a float-line running down through a sealed conduit to the basin below. The observatory's tide tables were famous. Customs relied on them. The Admiralty bought them. Courts accepted them.

Courts had accepted them two years ago.

Freya stood very still in the cold room, her loupe still raised to her eye, and made herself not think the thought yet. She examined the slip-plate instead. Recent work — the screw heads were bright, unmarked by corrosion, and one bore a faint file mark at an angle no careful workman would have left. The fitting was skilled. Better than skilled. It was the work of someone who understood escapements the way she did, and had used that understanding to make a clock lie.

Because that is what it amounted to, stripped of all its elegant engineering. A lie. A slow, deniable, self-erasing lie, written into the harbor's own record by the harbor's most trusted instrument.

She thought of Tomas Wren then, the way everyone in Saltmere thought of him: the smuggler hanged on the quay in '46, convicted on tide evidence — on the sworn testimony that his boat could not have cleared the narrows on the flood as he claimed, because the records proved the water had failed him by two feet. Two feet. The customs men had brought the observatory's tables to the assizes like scripture, and the jury had read them like scripture, and Wren had gone to the gallows protesting that the water had been high enough, that he'd felt the hull lift on the flood, and everyone had pitied him the way one pities a drowning man who insists the sea is shallow.

Freya closed the case door softly, as though the clock were sleeping.

She took out her notebook — a plain thing, gray board, the kind she used for jobbing estimates — and wrote nothing in it. Not yet. Instead she memorized: the position of the third wheel, the pitch of the slip-screw, the file mark, the date stamped on the replacement pinion's shoulder — *1746*. Not last year. Not last month. Three years old, installed a season before the trial, and never once mentioned in the maintenance log that lay open on the Astronomer's desk across the room, its columns marching back through the years in tidy hands, recording cleaning and oiling and adjustment, recording nothing at all about the third wheel.

Someone had committed forgery inside a machine, and then committed a second forgery around it, in ink, in a ledger, in the plain sight of respectable men.

Footsteps in the corridor. Freya slid the notebook away and was adjusting the crutch of the pendulum, wearing her best expression of ordinary puzzlement, when the door opened without a knock.

He was sixty-odd, silvered, dressed better than the occasion required — Magistrate Corliss, unmistakable even out of his court robes, with the particular stillness of a man accustomed to rooms falling quiet when he entered. Behind him hovered Pell and a thin, ascetic man she took for the Astronomer.

"Ah," Corliss said. "The clockmaker." His eyes went over her — the toolbag, the smudged fingers, the apprenticeship apron — cataloguing her the way she had catalogued his pinion, and finding her, apparently, harmless. "Our records must be kept exact, you understand. The harbor depends on us. The law depends on us." A pause, mild as milk. "How long will you be?"

"A week," Freya said. "Perhaps ten days. The fault is subtle."

"The fault is always subtle," Corliss said pleasantly. "That is what makes it a fault. Take whatever time you need, Miss—" he glanced at Pell, who supplied nothing, "—whoever you are. But understand: when the clock runs again, it will run true. Nothing else will be permitted."

He said it the way other men commented on the weather, and then he was gone, taking the corridor's air with him, and Pell was showing her the storeroom where she might work, and Freya was nodding and being brief and looking at her tools.

But her hands, as she laid them out on the bench in order, were not quite steady, and it was not fear. It was the other thing, the dangerous thing — the thing her father called *the itch of the mechanism*, the need to open whatever is closed and know why it ticks.

Somewhere below the headland, the fog lifted off the harbor for a moment, and Freya heard, distant and ordinary, a cart in the street, a dog, a woman calling a child home.

*Forget the face,* she would remember thinking later, though no such note had yet been shown her. *Forget the water. Forget the man on the quay.* She picked up her finest file and looked at the locked case of the master clock.

Ten days, the magistrate had given her.

She intended to use every hour of them.
