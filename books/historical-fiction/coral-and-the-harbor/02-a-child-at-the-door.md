## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm came in off the water an hour before dusk, and by full dark it had swallowed Saltmere whole.

Freya heard it through the observatory's east wall — a long, grinding roar, the sound of the harbor being shaken like a bottle. Rain hit the cupola above her room in the attic of the north wing, and the old building groaned around its own bones. She had been given a bed among the instrument crates, a candle stub, and instructions from the housekeeper to make herself scarce after supper. Gentlemen did not care to trip over tradespeople on their way to the wine.

She was not tired. Her hands were still humming from the day — six hours bent inside the master clock's case, cleaning two years of neglect out of its works, cataloguing every wheel and pallet against the repairs she'd need parts for. Tomorrow she would begin in earnest. Tonight she sat cross-legged on the crate that served as her chair, mending a torn hem by candlelight because thread was cheaper than patience, and listened to the wind try to pry the roof loose.

It was the knock she noticed before anything else. Or not a knock — a fumbling, a dragging thud against the great front door, felt more than heard beneath the storm.

She waited, counting seconds the way she counted everything now. The knock did not come again. But something in the quality of the silence afterward bothered her. A visitor would have hammered until answered. A drunk would have cursed. This had been soft, and single, and final — the sound of something set down and left.

Freya put down her needle. She told herself it was none of her business. She told herself that a woman with no position, no references beyond her dead father's name, and one week's employment at the Saltmere Observatory Society had precisely zero standing to open doors after dark. She told herself all of this while pulling on her boots and taking the candle.

The front hall was a canyon of cold stone, lit gray by lightning through the fanlight. She unbarred the door with both hands — the bar was meant for keeping weather out, and it fought her — and hauled the door open against the wind.

Nothing. The steps, awash. The drive a river. The yew trees bent double.

Then she looked down.

A child sat on the lowest step, curled into himself like a shell, soaked through to the skin. Five years old, perhaps six — small for either. He wore a man's coat cut down badly at the shoulders, and his bare feet were purple with cold. His eyes were open and fixed on her, wide and black and utterly still, and he did not cry, did not shiver visibly, did not do any of the things a child left in a storm ought to do. That stillness frightened her more than weeping would have.

In his lap, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain, lay a folded note.

Freya crouched, holding her cloak up as a shield against the slanting rain, and took the paper. The writing was cramped, hurried, pressed hard into the page:

*He is nobody's. See that he is gone by light and that no one remembers his face. Do this and the matter is closed.*

No signature. No direction. Just that — *no one remembers his face* — in ink that had run only slightly at the edges, meaning it had been written indoors, deliberately, by someone with time to think.

The boy watched her read it. Then, slowly, he lifted one small hand — not toward her, but toward his own face, covering it, as if he had understood every word and was already obeying.

Something turned over in Freya's chest, hard, like a mainspring snapping home.

"Inside," she said. "Now, before we both drown."

He didn't move until she reached for him, and then he came willingly, gripping her sleeve with fingers gone stiff as fishhooks. She got him into the hall and barred the door behind them, and there they stood in the lightning-strobe dark, dripping, while thunder rolled down the coast like artillery.

* * *

The kitchen, thank God, still held embers. Freya fed them, set the kettle on, and stripped the boy out of the ruined coat by the stove's glow. He let her do it all without a word — and it was more than shock, she realized as she rubbed warmth into his arms. It was practice. He knew how to be silent. Knew how to be handled by strangers, how to be undressed and fed and moved along, the way a stray knows how to be kicked and how to eat fast when food appears.

He had a scar at his hairline, old and healed crooked. He had a chipped front tooth. And when the blanket slipped as she settled him near the fire, she saw, burned into the skin over his collarbone, a mark she recognized without knowing why: a small anchor, done in a sailor's crude dotwork, half-faded with age.

"Where is your mother?" she asked, though she knew the shape of the answer.

The boy looked at the fire.

"Your father?"

His jaw tightened. He pulled the blanket up under his chin and stared into the coals, and his silence was not empty the way a hearing child's silence is empty. It was full. It was a locked room, and Freya had spent enough of her life around mechanisms to know a lock when she saw one — and to know that some locks are made by their owners, and some are fitted by other hands.

She made him bread steeped in milk with a scrape of sugar, and he ate the way she'd predicted, fast and neat, watching the door between bites.

The note lay drying on the table where she'd left it. Twice she caught herself reading it again. *Gone by light.* Whoever had written it had known exactly which door to leave him at — the observatory, the town's grandest threshold, where a found child would be an embarrassment quickly disposed of rather than a parish problem. Someone powerful enough to be certain of what would happen next. Someone who wanted the boy erased quietly, by other people's hands, so the erasure wouldn't count against them.

And someone, Freya thought, watching the boy watch the door, who might come back to check that the instruction had been followed.

* * *

Astronomer Hale found them at seven the next morning, because of course he did. The storm had blown itself out by dawn, leaving the sky rinsed blue and the harbor littered with wreckage, and Hale descended into his own kitchen smelling of laudanum and last night's port, and stopped dead at the sight of a small boy asleep in a chair by the ashes.

"What," he said, "is that."

"A child." Freya kept her voice level. She'd learned long ago that levelness was the tradeswoman's armor; gentlemen rarely struck at anything flat. "Left at the door last night. There's a note."

Hale read it once, and she watched his face do something complicated and then settle into a smoothness that was worse than alarm. "Burn it."

"Sir—"

"Burn the note, Mrs. Ashgrove—" he'd never once asked whether she was actually a widow; the honorific was simply assumed for any woman past girlhood, "—and take the child to the parish overseer before the house stirs. This is an observatory, not a foundling wheel."

"The parish overseer is Mr. Quill," Freya said. "Who is, I believe, Magistrate Corliss's brother-in-law."

The silence stretched. Hale's eyes went to the note again, and she saw him arrive, unwillingly, at the same arithmetic she had: if the note's author wanted the boy invisible, delivering him to Corliss's own family was not disposal. It was delivery.

"That," Hale said slowly, "is not our affair."

"No, sir."

"Then why are you arguing?"

"I'm not arguing, sir. I'm asking what to do with him." She let the pause sit. "You said gone by light. It's past light. Whatever was decided, it was decided for last night, and last night is over."

Hale pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose. He was not, she suspected, a cruel man — merely a comfortable one, and comfort has its own ruthless arithmetic. "Keep him out of the corridors. Keep him out of sight until I can write to the Society's stewards and establish what is — proper." He said the last word as though tasting spoiled milk. "And Mrs. Ashgrove — the note burns. If anyone asks, the child was here when you woke and says nothing, and neither will you."

"Yes, sir."

He left. Freya picked up the note, carried it to the stove, and held it over the embers until the corner caught — then, on impulse she couldn't have defended, snatched it back and clapped the flame out against the iron, saving three-quarters of the paper and most of the writing. She folded the scorched fragment small and sewed it into the lining of her work apron, under the pocket where she kept her tweezers. She could not have explained why. She only knew that things written in haste by powerful men had a habit of becoming important later, and that burning evidence felt less like obedience than like finishing someone else's errand.

* * *

She should have sent him away that afternoon. She told herself she would, once the rain-slick roads dried, once she learned where he belonged. In the meantime, she had work, and no one to mind him, and so he came with her — trailing two steps behind like her shadow, barefoot and silent, into the clock chamber at the top of the tower.

She hadn't meant to show him anything. But he stood on tiptoe to see the pendulum swing, and something in his face — the first expression other than guarded blankness she'd seen on him — made her lift him onto a stool so he could watch properly. The great weights rose in their channels; the escapement ticked its patient, metronomic heart; and the boy leaned forward until he was nearly inside the case, mouth open, drinking in the machinery the way she had once, at her father's bench, at exactly that age.

"Pendulum," she heard herself say, pointing. "Long pendulum swings slow. That's the whole secret. Everything else is just cleverness about keeping it swinging."

He turned to her and nodded once, solemn as a bishop, and pointed at the escape wheel and raised his eyebrows. A question.

Freya laughed — surprised to hear the sound come out of her. "That? That's the part that lets it go. Tick by tick. Lets a little time escape, catches it, lets a little more go. Otherwise it would all pour out at once and mean nothing."

He studied the wheel for a long moment. Then he touched his own chest, and then the wheel, and looked at her with those black steady eyes, and Freya felt the observation land somewhere beneath her ribs and stay there.

She fed him supper in the kitchen. She found an old ship's blanket in the stores and made him a bed in the warm corner behind her clock crates, telling herself it was temporary, one night, until arrangements were made. When she doused the candle he was already asleep, curled small, one hand closed around the button of her sleeve — he had grabbed it as she tucked the blanket, and not let go, and she had not had the heart to unwinding his fingers.

Lying awake later in the dark, listening to the tower clock count the hours honestly or dishonestly — she no longer fully trusted her own ear — Freya turned the night over in her mind. An abandoned boy. A note from a powerful hand. An anchor scarred into a child's skin, faded two years or more.

Two years.

Tomas Wren had swung from the customs-house gallows two years this spring. Smuggling, they said, and the harbor records proved the tide had stranded his boat where no honest tide could have — proof printed in the observatory's own almanac, sworn by the magistrate, accepted by everyone because the records were impeccable. She remembered the widow Wren standing at the dock rail afterward with a small boy's hand in hers. Remembered thinking, distantly, *there but for my father's name go I.*

Surely not. Half the harbor's children had sailor's tattoos somewhere in their families.

But the boy had gripped her sleeve the way children grip the one fixed thing in a moving world, and he had looked at the escape wheel and pointed at his own heart, and Freya Ashgrove, who had come to Saltmere to keep her head down and fix a clock, lay staring into the dark and understood with cold, growing certainty that she was not going to give this child to anyone's parish — and that tomorrow, when she opened the master clock's case, she would be looking at its works with different eyes entirely.
