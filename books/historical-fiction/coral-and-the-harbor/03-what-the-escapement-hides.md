## 3. What the Escapement Hides

The boy had been gone from her thoughts for exactly four hours when Freya realized she was thinking about him again.

She pushed the thought away and bent closer to the master clock's movement. Dawn came gray through the observatory's east windows, and she had the great case open, its brass heart exposed on the workbench like a patient under the knife. Mr. Pellworthy, the astronomer, had left a pot of coffee and instructions not to disturb anything she did not understand. She understood everything. That was the problem.

The escapement was wrong.

Not broken — wrong. There is a difference, and every craftsperson learns to feel it in their fingertips before their mind catches up. A broken thing resists you, rattles, refuses. This mechanism ran smooth as poured honey. Too smooth. She had spent two full days on routine maintenance — cleaning pivot holes, re-seating the suspension, truing the hands — and through all of it a small dissonance had been ticking at the back of her skull, the way an off-key string will trouble a musician who cannot yet name the note.

This morning she stopped working around the problem and went straight at it.

The anchor escapement itself was sound. Fine work, London-made, twenty years old and barely worn. But below it, driving the count wheel, sat a gear that did not belong. She lifted it out with tongs and turned it under the lamp.

Her first thought was that it was beautifully done.

That was what frightened her.

The gear had been cut down — re-toothed by hand, and by someone very good. A standard wheel of thirty-six teeth had become one of thirty-four, its profile reshaped so skillfully that at a glance, in bad light, to anyone who did not count the teeth, it would pass as original. Two teeth missing out of thirty-six meant the wheel turned fractionally slow. Fractional, compounded over hours, over days, over *years* —

Freya set the gear down on a square of chamois and made herself do the arithmetic.

A clock loses time. Anyone who has owned a cheap watch knows this; clocks drift, and clockmakers correct them. But this clock did more than tell time. It drove the tide-recording apparatus bolted to the observatory's seaward wall — a drum of paper turned by clockwork, a pen riding the water's rise and fall through a float-and-wire system, inscribing the harbor's tides hour by hour, year by year. The custom house relied on those records. The courts relied on those records. Insurance men from London relied on those records.

If the clock ran slow by even ninety seconds a day, the recorded high tides would fall consistently late against the true moon. Not obviously late. Not the kind of error any clerk would flag. Just enough that the official tables and the official record would disagree — always in the same direction, always by the same margin.

And someone had chosen the margin deliberately. Freya could see that now in the tooth profile: the alteration wasn't crude sabotage, wasn't wear or accident. Someone had wanted the record skewed by a precise amount and had engineered it with the patience of a saint.

She sat back on the work stool and listened to the tick. The whole tower ticked with it, the way a ship ticks.

*Why?*

The question arrived before the answer, and the answer arrived unwelcome, because it came dressed in a memory.

Two years ago. She had been sixteen, still apprenticed formally to her father before his lungs failed, and Saltmere had talked of nothing else for a month. The *Merrow's Luck*, Tomas Wren's lugger, taken in the channel on a night the revenue cutter claimed the tide should have been higher — claimed Wren had miscalculated the sandbar passage, that no honest seaman would have attempted the outer channel on so low a spring tide, therefore the low tide must have been deliberate cover for a run of contraband. The customs men had produced the observatory's tide tables in court. Impeccable records, they said. Kept by machinery, kept by gentlemen, kept by brass and paper that could not lie.

Tomas Wren swore he had crossed that bar a hundred times on lower water. The court preferred the machine.

They hanged him in Exeter. His wife stood in the Saltmere market square afterward with a face like a closed door, and people looked away, because what do you say to a woman whose husband was killed by arithmetic?

Freya had believed it then. Everyone had. Machines didn't lie; that was the entire point of machines. Her father used to say a clock was the only honest man in any room.

She looked at the gear on the chamois cloth and felt something shift in her chest — not grief exactly, and not yet anger, but the cold space where both would soon live.

Someone had made the machine lie. And they had made it lie in a way that would take years to notice and longer to prove.

* *

She worked through the morning the way a surgeon works when she finds something during a routine operation — carefully, silently, changing nothing outwardly.

She counted the teeth three times. Thirty-four. She measured the wheel's diameter against the train calculation in her father's old pocketbook, which she carried everywhere the way other people carry prayer books. The numbers agreed with her fingers. Slow by approximately ninety-one seconds per day, if driven at the original rate — but whoever altered the wheel had also adjusted the pendulum bob, she saw now, moving the rating nut a few threads to partially compensate. Partially. The net error landed precisely where the designer wanted it: large enough to matter in the records, small enough to hide inside ordinary mechanical drift.

It was, Freya thought, the most evil thing she had ever held, and it was beautiful.

She did not remove it. That decision took her an hour of stillness to reach, her hands folded in her lap like a schoolgirl's while the clock ticked its false tick above her.

Because if she replaced the gear with a true one, the record would correct itself — and the correction would show. Twenty years of tide logs, all leaning the same direction, and then one week suddenly true. Any competent examiner would ask what changed. And the answer would be: *the clockmaker noticed*. Then the question would follow, swift as a blade: who else knew? Who ordered it? And the person who ordered it would move to protect himself, and Freya Ashgrove, journeywoman clockmaker of no family and less standing, would be a problem with an easy solution. People who forged court evidence did not hesitate at smaller crimes.

So instead she did what her trade had trained her to do: she observed, she measured, she wrote it down.

She took the gear's exact dimensions. She made a rubbing of the tooth profile in soft pencil on thin paper. She noted the pendulum compensation, the position of the bob's rating nut, the date code stamped on the original London wheel — 1729 — versus no stamp at all on the altered one, because it was not original, because it had been added later, and *when* became the next question.

When had someone opened this clock and rebuilt its voice?

She climbed into the case with a lantern and searched where no one searches: behind the movement, in the joinery. Servicemen are vain creatures. They sign their work without meaning to. On the inside face of the case's side panel, half-hidden by the movement's mounting bracket, she found scratches in the wood — tool marks from prying, and beneath the bracket's lowest screw, a shallow gouge and a smear of candle wax. Wax. Whoever worked here had done it by lamplight, slowly, in secret, taking hours. And the dust: the panel's tongue-and-groove joint held a seam of dust unbroken except along one narrow path. The panel had been removed perhaps once. Perhaps twice. In twenty years.

She copied the observatory's maintenance ledger yesterday — part of her contracted duties, nothing remarkable in it. Now she fetched it and read it properly, line by line, and found what she expected to find: annual entries. *Cleaned and rated. Cleaned and rated. Cleaned and rated.* Signed initials, year after year. P.H. — Pellworthy's predecessor, dead these six years. And then, in autumn of 1746, between two routine entries, a single line in different ink, a different hand:

*Escapement attended. Irregularity corrected. No further action required.*

No initials. No explanation. Autumn 1746. The *Merrow's Luck* was taken in November of 1746. The trial sat in December.

Freya put the ledger down very gently, as though it might go off.

* *

She told herself she would think clearly after she walked. The town gave her fog and herring-smell and the usual midday crowd around the fish quay, and none of it helped, because now she saw the whole harbor differently — as a thing that had been measured wrongly on purpose, a landscape falsified the way a portrait can be flattered. The sandbar where Wren's lugger struck. The channel the court said he couldn't have crossed honestly. The sea itself, entered into evidence and perjured.

At the market she bought bread and, without planning to, bought a second apple. She caught herself holding it and understood she had been walking toward the widow's cottage before her mind admitted the errand. She turned around. Walked home. Put the apple on the shelf where it sat like an accusation.

Because here was the wall she kept striking: she did not know yet what she knew. She had a gear and a ledger line and a terrible suspicion shaped like certainty, and suspicion is worth nothing against a magistrate's word except a hanging for the person who holds it too loudly. Corliss funded this observatory. Corliss's name was carved over the door in letters bigger than God's. If she went to anyone — the customs house, a justice, a newspaper — with a story about altered gears, she would be a madwoman or a liar, and either way she'd be finished before noon.

She needed proof that lived outside her own hands. Tables from before the alteration and after, compared against true astronomical prediction. Records from the burned customs house archive, if anything survived. Another craftsman — a London horologist, someone with a reputation — willing to examine the wheel and swear it was re-cut. Proof, plural, layered, patient.

And time. The gear had been sitting in that clock for however long it took to kill a man, and it would keep another week while she worked out how to use it.

That was the resolution she reached by evening: not bravery, nothing so grand. Just the tradeswoman's discipline of *measure twice, cut once*, applied to something larger than brass.

She banked the fire and was turning to the stairs when she remembered — with a jolt, the apple cold in her memory — the boy asleep upstairs in the loft she'd given him two nights ago, this child of all children, son of the man her clock had helped hang. She had taken him in out of simple decency on a storm night. Now the act looked different. Now it looked like fate arranging its pieces with a horologist's patience, setting the orphan at the door of the one person in Saltmere who would eventually open the right cabinet.

He was awake when she climbed up. Sitting cross-legged in the lamplight, solemn, watching her with those dark eyes that missed nothing. On the floor beside him, drawn carefully in spilled flour from the bread sack, he had made shapes. Letters, she realized. Badly formed, laboriously copied — copied from somewhere.

From the note in his bundle. The one ordering everyone to forget his face.

She knelt down slowly, her heart going hard. "Who taught you your letters?" she asked, though he couldn't answer, never answered.

He pointed one small finger at the flour letters, then at the folded note, then — deliberately, watching her the whole time — he pointed at her, and then drew one more shape in the flour.

A *W*.
