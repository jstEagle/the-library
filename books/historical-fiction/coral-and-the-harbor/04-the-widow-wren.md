## 4. The Widow Wren

Freya told herself she was walking to the east end of Saltmere for the air.

The morning after the storm was rinsed clean, gulls stitching the sky, the harbor lying flat and pewter-bright below the cliff path. A fine day for it. A fine day for anything except what she was actually doing, which was seeking out a woman whose husband had been hanged on evidence Freya now suspected came out of the clock she'd been hired to mend.

She had left the boy at the observatory with a plate of bread and honey and a warning to Mrs. Petch that he was her cousin's child, come from upcountry, shy and simple, and not to be questioned. The housekeeper had looked at Freya the way housekeepers look at lies — with professional indifference — and said nothing. That was one thing about Saltmere: everyone kept something, so everyone kept quiet.

The boy hadn't spoken. He hadn't since the night on the doorstep. But when she'd tucked the blanket around him before dawn, his hand had shot out and gripped her sleeve, two fingers only, the way a man grips a rope in heavy weather. Not pulling her closer. Just holding. Checking she was still there.

She had thought of that grip all the way down the cliff stairs.

Tomas Wren had been hanged in the spring two years past. She remembered it because her master, old Hollis, had closed the shop that day — the only time in eleven years he'd closed for anything but a funeral in the family. *A hanging is a funeral,* he'd said, when she asked. *Just one where we all hold the rope.* She'd been seventeen and hadn't understood him. She thought she might be beginning to.

The Wren cottage sat at the elbow of Fisher's Row, where the street bent away from the water as if ashamed of it. It was small, whitewash gone gray, a garden of leeks and shell-paths maintained with a fierce tidiness that told its own story: a woman who would not let grief make her sloppy.

Freya stood at the gate longer than she liked to admit. Then she lifted the latch.

* * *

Maren Wren was mending a net in the doorway, and she knew Freya's face before Freya got a word out. Everyone in Saltmere knew the clockmaker's apprentice, if only because apprentices were rarely women and never forgot themselves enough to look you in the eye. Freya lowered her gaze now, out of habit, then forced it back up. She was tired of lowering things.

"Mrs. Wren. I'm Freya Ashgrove. I work at the observatory."

"I know where you work." Maren didn't rise. Her hands kept working the net, needle flashing. "Whole town knows. They say you've got the master clock open on the table like a gutted fish."

"They say right."

"Hm." The needle paused. Resumed. "Come to take my measure for a mourning watch? Two years late, but clocks keep their own time, don't they. That's the whole promise of them."

"No," Freya said. "I've come about your son."

The net went still. Maren Wren set it down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you want to throw, and stood. Up close she was younger than her gray streaks suggested, maybe five-and-thirty, with the deep-water calm of a woman who had already survived the worst thing and found it hadn't killed her after all, which was its own kind of wound.

"You'd best come in," she said.

* * *

The cottage interior was one room, immaculate. A hearth. A loom shoved to the wall, idle. On the mantel, no portrait — portraits cost money — but a carved wooden bird, a tern, wings swept back mid-dive, the kind of thing a sailor whittles over long watches. Tomas Wren's hand, Freya thought. The last honest thing he made.

Maren poured tea without asking. Then she stood with her back to the hearth and said, "Say what you came to say, girl, and say it low."

"The night of the storm. There was a child left at the observatory door. Perhaps seven years old. Dark hair. A scar through his left eyebrow." Freya's voice stayed level; she was proud of that. "There was a note. It said the town should forget his face."

The hearth ticked. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"That scar," Maren said finally, "he got at four years old, falling off the harbor steps, and his father carried him home and swore a blue oath about the missing rail, and the town mended the rail inside a week because nobody ever said no to Tomas about his boy." She picked up the poker and set it down again. "You're telling me the observatory has my Jem under its roof."

"I'm telling you I have him under my roof, or near enough. I've taken him to my lodging. The astronomer wanted him turned out."

"And you took him instead." Maren studied her. "Why?"

Because he gripped my sleeve. Because someone wrote *forget his face* as though a child were an error in a ledger to be ruled off. Because I opened a clock and found a lie inside it and I can't stop seeing the shape of the lie now, everywhere, in everything.

"I couldn't leave him," Freya said. Which was all of it, really.

Maren crossed the room in three steps and took Freya by both shoulders, and her calm cracked down the middle like river ice in March. "Listen to me. Listen. You will carry him back to that door tonight and knock, or you will carry him to the packet boat and put him aboard for Rye, anywhere, but you will not keep him here, and you will not go asking after him, and above all you will not—" her voice dropped to nearly nothing— "you will not write anything down."

"Write anything—"

"I know how it works in this town." The gray eyes were terrible and close. "My husband ran cargo because the excise men taxed flour to starvation and called it law. Everyone on this row knew it and nobody minded, because Tomas paid his crews fair and never touched a wreck. Then the customs house produces tide tables — observatory records, sworn, impeccable — proving the *Merrow* could only have run on nights Tomas stood confessed ashore. Twenty years of beautiful numbers, and they hanged him on them at the assizes in a single day, because numbers don't have faces and can't be cross-examined." Her grip tightened. "Now you come to me from that same observatory, saying there's a child and a note and something wrong with the clock. And you think I'll thank you. Girl, I'll bury you next to him if you keep on this road."

So it was true. Before Maren said another word, Freya knew it, felt it settle into her bones the way a truth settles — heavier than proof, arriving ahead of it. The escapement she'd found, the offset cam riding the tide-recording wheel, the gentle systematic skew that pushed every recorded tide by exactly the margin needed to make Tomas Wren's alibi impossible. She had told herself there might be some innocent explanation. A repairman's shortcut. An error compounded over years.

Twenty years of beautiful numbers. Numbers don't have faces.

"There *is* something wrong with the clock," Freya said quietly. "Someone built a lie into it. Gear by gear. It's elegant work. It was meant to be found by no one."

Maren released her and turned away, and for a long moment the only sound was the fire. When she spoke again, her voice was scraped smooth and empty, the calm restored like a net mended over a hole.

"Do you know what happened to the men who spoke for Tomas at trial? Silas Crane swore he'd seen Tomas at his wife's bedside on the night in question. Lost his boat license within the month — some irregularity in his papers. Young Nat Fennel wrote a letter to the magistrate asking questions. Pressed for debt before the ink was dry. Nobody threatened them. Nothing was done to them. Things simply *happened*, the way stones happen to roll downhill." She looked back over her shoulder. "Magistrate Corliss funds your observatory. His name is cut into the lintel. And you sleep under his roof with his orphan and poke at his clock."

"I don't sleep under his roof. I lodge above the chandlery."

"How fortunate for you. How long do you imagine that lasts?"

* * *

Freya drank her tea because refusing seemed like an insult to the dead. Maren talked a little more — of Jem, of how he'd been hidden with a cousin in Boulogne when the arrests began, of how the cousin had written that the child stopped speaking the week his father hanged, of how the letters had stopped coming eight months ago, and how Maren had told herself the cousin was careless with the post, and had known, in the pit of her, that she was lying to herself.

"He was sent back," Maren said. "Someone brought him across the Channel and set him down at your door. Someone who wanted him found by *you* — or wanted him found and forgotten, which amounts to the same corpse in different clothes. Either way, whoever moved him has friends in this town, and those friends answer to Corliss. If he disappears properly — if you hide him and hide him well — perhaps he lives. If anyone starts asking questions about tides and gears and forged alibis—" she spread her hands, "—then Jem dies of a fever, or drowns, or runs away to sea, aged seven. Children vanish in Saltmere all the time. The records will show nothing, because our records are very good."

Freya set down her cup. Her hands were steady. She noticed them being steady the way you notice a held breath.

"Mrs. Wren. Your husband's trial used tide tables from that clock. I have found the mechanism that altered them. I haven't touched it yet. It's still sitting in that clock, lying, hour by hour, and it will go on lying until Corliss decides it doesn't need to anymore — until he has the whole machine replaced and every trace melted down. When that happens, the proof dies too."

"Then let it die," Maren said. "Let the clock lie. Let Corliss grow fat on his lies until God corrects him. I had a husband and now I have a memory, and you are not going to spend my son to buy the dead man's reputation."

"Tomas is dead either way. Jem isn't safe either way." Freya stood. "You think hiding him keeps him safe. But somebody already delivered him to the most watched building in Saltmere with a note saying forget him. They know he exists, Mrs. Wren. Forgetting is their project, not mine. Men who forge twenty years of tide tables don't leave loose children floating about. Sooner or later they'll tidy him away, and no one will even be able to prove he was here — unless someone writes it down."

Maren's face did something complicated. Rage and terror and, underneath, horribly, hope — hope being the cruelest of the three, because it couldn't be reasoned with.

"Get out of my house," she said, not unkindly. "And think very hard about whose grave you're digging."

* * *

Freya climbed the cliff path slowly, working it over. Loyalty to the living against duty to the dead; Maren had framed it that way, and it was a good frame, and Freya kept trying to fit herself into it and failing. Because it wasn't duty pulling at her. Duty was cold. This was the boy's two-fingered grip on her sleeve. This was the tern on the mantelpiece, wings back, diving. This was the specific gravity of a lie sitting in brass and steel, ticking away, wearing the respectable face of Science while it hanged a man and silenced a child.

Let sleeping dogs lie, the widow said.

But Freya had opened the clock. She had seen that the dogs were never asleep. They were fed, and groomed, and set.

By the time she reached the observatory door, she had stopped pretending she'd come down the hill for the air. She let herself in, nodded to Mrs. Petch, and climbed to the clock room — and stopped on the threshold with her hand still on the latch.

The case of the master clock stood open. She had left it closed. She always left it closed.

Inside, on the great train wheel where the falsifying cam had sat, there was a gap, bright and raw, where metal had recently been removed. The lie was gone from the clock.

Someone had gotten there first.
