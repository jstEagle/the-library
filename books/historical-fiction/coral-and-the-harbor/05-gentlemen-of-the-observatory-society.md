## 5. Gentlemen of the Observatory Society

The invitation arrived on cream paper with a wax seal Freya could not afford to break and could not afford not to.

*The Observatory Society requests the company of Miss Freya Ashgrove at its quarterly dinner, in recognition of her services to the great clock.*

She read it four times by candlelight, the boy asleep in the truckle bed she'd rigged behind her workshop's curtain. *Services to the great clock.* As if she'd polished it. As if she hadn't spent three weeks elbow-deep in a mechanism that had been taught to lie.

Her master, old Hollis, would have said: *When they invite you upstairs, girl, it means they've noticed your boots.* She went anyway. A refusal would be noticed worse.

She wore her good grey dress, the one with only one mend, and left before dusk so she could walk past the harbor and look — as she now could not stop doing — at the tide pole standing off the customs house quay. The water lapped it at half-flood. Somewhere under that ordinary grey surface lay the arithmetic that had hanged Tomas Wren: numbers copied faithfully from a clock that had been bent out of true, entered into logs that no one had thought to doubt, because who doubts an observatory?

The boy, Jem, had watched her dress from the curtain gap with those wide silent eyes. She'd told Mrs. Pellow next door to listen for him, made up a story about a cousin. Lies came easily now. That frightened her more than anything Corliss could do.

* * *

The observatory dining room had been transformed for the evening — the charts rolled up, the instruments shrouded, long tables dressed in white linen with the Society's silver laid out in ranks. Candles everywhere, so many that the great dome above seemed to float on a sea of small flames. Twenty gentlemen in good coats, their wives arranged like ornaments at the far end, and Freya the only person in the room with ink permanently under her fingernails.

A servant showed her to a seat near the foot of the table. She was halfway through her first glass of wine — watered, but wine — when she felt the change in the room's attention, the way you feel a door open behind you.

"Miss Ashgrove."

Magistrate Corliss stood beside her chair. He was smaller than his reputation, a neat man in dove-grey with soft hands folded over his waistcoat, and eyes that did all the work of a much larger face. Up close he smelled of ambergris and something faintly medicinal.

"Sir." She rose. Around them, conversation dimmed pleasantly, the way it does when entertainment approaches.

"You must forgive the irregularity of inviting a tradeswoman to dine." He smiled at the table, including them in the joke. "But we are not an ordinary society. We deal in facts here. In precision." He turned back to her. "And you, I am told, are precise."

"I try to be, sir."

"May I?" He did not wait for permission; he sat in the neighboring chair and drew it close, so that they formed a private island amid the clatter of soup plates. "Tell me about my clock."

"Your clock, sir?"

"The observatory's master. The one that keeps our tides, our time, our — " he made a small gesture, encompassing the candles, the silver, the whole soft weight of the evening — "reputation. Mr. Fenwick tells me you have had it apart almost entirely."

"Repairs require it. The escapement was worn. The strike train had slipped."

"And now?"

"Now it keeps honest time," Freya said, and heard the words land a half-second too heavily, like a coin dropped on a counter.

Corliss studied her. It was not an unkind study. That was the worst of it. A cruel man would have rushed; this man took his time, the way she herself took time over a mechanism, listening for the tooth that didn't sit right.

"Honest time," he repeated, tasting it. "You know, Miss Ashgrove, most people imagine that clocks merely follow time. Like a dog following a master." He helped himself to a candied plum from the dish between them and turned it in his fingers without eating it. "But a clock of this quality doesn't follow anything. It *leads*. When the master clock says the tide turned at three minutes past four, then the tide turned at three minutes past four. The harbor pilots set their watches by us. The customs house sets its ledgers by us. The courts — " the plum stopped turning — "have been known to set their verdicts by us."

"Then it is fortunate," Freya said, "that it now keeps honest time."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not anger. Recalculation.

"Yes," he said softly. "Isn't it."

Down the table, Astronomer Fenwick rose to propose a toast to the King, and the gentlemen surged to their feet, and for a moment there was blessed noise. Corliss remained seated. So did Freya. They were two figures becalmed in a sea of standing men.

"I confess I admire your trade," he said when the toast died. "So few crafts leave evidence of tampering. A baker's loaf leaves no record of what was done to the flour. But a clock — " he tapped the tablecloth once, precisely — "a clock remembers. Every alteration leaves a signature in the metal. A file mark. A replaced pinion. An amateur might repair such signatures away, in the name of tidiness." He looked at her fully now. "A careful craftsman preserves them. Documents them. Keeps a record of what she found, in case anyone should ever ask what the instrument was doing before she touched it."

The candle between them guttered and steadied. Freya discovered that her hands, resting on the table, were perfectly still. Some cold, clear part of her mind observed this with professional interest, the way she observed a balance wheel settling into oscillation.

"I document everything I find, sir," she said. "It is how I was trained."

"I know how you were trained." He smiled again, warmly, and stood, smoothing his coat. "Hollis was the best in three counties. It grieved him, I understand, the last years. Money troubles." He said it as if reporting weather in a distant county. "Debts are heavy things. They make people do work they would rather not do, and say things they would perhaps prefer unsaid. You will find, Miss Ashgrove, as you rise in the world, that nearly everything can be understood through debts."

He bowed over her hand, which she had not offered, and moved down the table to laugh with the customs collector, and the dinner resumed around Freya like water closing over a stone.

She did not taste the meat course. She did not hear the toasts to the Society's patron — whose name, she noted with the new cold clarity, was Corliss. She sat among the candle flames and performed the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the night she found the altered wheel.

He knew. Not suspected — *knew*. Knew what the clock had done, because it had been made to do it on his order or with his blessing. And tonight he had not threatened her. He had done something infinitely worse: he had told her, in the language of debts and signatures, that he assumed she could be bought, because everyone in her position always could.

Hollis. Her dead master's debts. Had he worked on that clock before her? Was that why the observatory had summoned his apprentice, of all the clockmakers in England — because they believed the debt passed down like the tools?

She thought of Jem asleep behind her workshop curtain, breathing small and even. Sleeping dogs, Widow Wren had begged. Let them lie, for the child's sake.

But the dogs were not sleeping. One of them had just dined beside her and found her interesting.

* * *

The carriages were called at eleven. Freya walked home alone through streets gone quiet and blue with moonlight, and her fear walked with her, and then, somewhere near the fish market, it stopped walking and fell behind.

Because she understood, finally, what the evening had been. Not a probe. A courtesy. Corliss had shown her the shape of the trap so that she could step into it voluntarily, gracefully, grateful for the invitation. He expected her to burn her notes, keep her mouth shut, and take whatever modest advancement followed. That was what people did. That was what the world asked of women with mended dresses and ink under their nails.

Instead she climbed the stairs to her workshop, lit two candles instead of one, and unlocked the drawer where she kept her drawings of the altered escape wheel — the filed pallets, the substituted pinion, the ratio by which every recorded tide had been skewed just enough to place Wren's boat where the customs men needed it on the morning of the raid.

She spread the pages flat and weighted their corners.

Then she took a fresh quire of paper, ruled it, and wrote at the head of the first page, in her best drafting hand:

*Being a true account of the master clock of Saltmere Observatory, its alterations, and the records procured thereby.*

Underneath, she began to write. Tooth counts. File marks. Dates. Names — starting with Fenwick, who signed the maintenance ledger, and ending where she knew it ended, though she had no proof yet, only the smell of ambergris and the memory of soft hands folding over a waistcoat.

Outside, the fog came up the harbor, and the town slept, and the tide turned against the pole at exactly the hour the rebuilt clock said it would.

Freya wrote until the candles failed. Then she sat in the dark and listened to the boy turn over in his sleep, and made herself a promise with the clean hardness of a newly cut gear: before this was finished, Magistrate Corliss would learn that some instruments, once set right, cannot quietly be set wrong again.

Three days later, the printer's devil Tam Rooker came for his mother's mended clock and stayed, at Freya's request, to look at a page she could not copy alone.
