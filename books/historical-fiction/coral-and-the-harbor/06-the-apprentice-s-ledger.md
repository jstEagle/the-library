## 6. The Apprentice's Ledger

I started the ledger on a Tuesday, in the back room of my uncle's shop, with the shutters drawn and a single tallow candle burning low enough that its light wouldn't leak into the street.

The book itself was nothing — a plain quarto of cheap rag paper, the kind apprentices use for practicing their figures. I'd bought it from Hollis the stationer along with two ounces of ordinary sealing wax, a bundle of quills, and a paper of pins, and I'd made idle conversation about hemming my winter cloak until he'd stopped looking at me with interest. That was the trick of it, I was learning. Never buy anything unusual. Never ask for anything you can't explain twice.

I ruled the first page myself, ink and straightedge, the way my father taught me when I was nine years old and first allowed to touch his tools: *a mechanism explained badly is a mechanism misunderstood.* At the top I wrote no title. A titled ledger could be named by a witness. This one would have to speak for itself if it ever spoke at all.

Then I began to draw.

I drew the great wheel as it should have been, from the plans in Merrick's own hand that hung framed in the observatory workroom — the master's original design, venerated and never once consulted. Beside it, I drew the great wheel as it actually was, the one I had measured three times now with brass calipers smuggled out under my apron and returned before anyone missed them. The difference lived in the teeth. Forty-two cut to a modified profile on the left flank, each one shaved by perhaps a twentieth of an inch. Invisible to any eye not trained to look. Sufficient, over the span of a tidal cycle, to retard the strike train by eleven seconds.

Eleven seconds did not sound like much. I had thought so too, the first night, staring at the gear until my eyes watered.

But the tide tables were kept by the clock. Every high water, every low, logged to the minute against the observatory's standard time — the standard time, the *only* time, the time the customs house set its watches by and the harbor court set its law by. Eleven seconds, accumulated and corrected unevenly across the month, shifted every recorded tide in Saltmere by a quarter hour or more. And a quarter hour of tide is the difference between a channel a smuggling lugger can cross dry-shod and a channel that drowns her crew against the mole.

It had drowned them once already, in a manner of speaking. It had drowned Tomas Wren, who was never there at all.

*On the night of the fourteenth of October, 1746,* the harbor log swore, *the tide stood at half-flood at nine of the clock as recorded at the Observatory of Saltmere, whereupon the lugger* Merrow *did ground upon the Shingle Spit in full view of the revenue watch.* Wren's alibi rested on the same records that condemned him — he claimed, and his witnesses claimed with him, that the tide would not serve a crossing until nearly midnight. The clock said otherwise. The clock was the town's honest servant, wasn't it? Brass does not perjure itself.

Brass does exactly what it is told. That is its whole virtue, and I had spent two years learning to make it obey me, and someone before me had made it obey Magistrate Corliss.

I wrote all of this down. Not in sentences — sentences could be read over a shoulder — but in the language of my trade. Gear profiles. Tooth counts. The arithmetic of drift, worked and reworked, each calculation checked backward the way I'd check a escapement before closing a case. Anyone who opened the book would see a clockmaker's practice exercises. Only someone who knew what the observatory clock looked like inside would understand they were reading a confession — not mine, but the machine's.

When the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, I closed the ledger, wrapped it in oilcloth, and slid it behind the loose brick in the shop's back chimney, where my uncle had hidden his brandy from my aunt for twenty years and where, I reasoned, no one who knew the house would ever search for anything valuable.

I sat for a moment afterward in the dark, hands still inky, listening to the tick of the shop regulator — an honest clock, small and dull and true — and I felt something I couldn't immediately name. Not fear, though fear was there, familiar now, almost furniture. It was closer to what I felt when a stubborn movement finally came alive under my fingers after a week of failure. Purpose, taking up its load in the train. Something beginning to turn.

* * *

Ned Pellow worked the press at Crane & Sons, and he was the only soul in Saltmere under seventy who talked to me like a person rather than a curiosity in a skirt.

He was nineteen, ink to the elbows and freckled to the eyebrows, and he had been printing the harbor notices, the tide calendars, and the assize broadsides since he was twelve. He was also, which mattered more, the sort of boy who remembered everything he printed. He'd once recited to me the entire cargo manifest of a ship lost off the Skerries in 1739 because he'd set the type for the insurance notice and it had stuck in his head like a song.

I found him stacking dampened sheets in the yard behind Crane's on Thursday, and I said, carefully, watching the windows, "Do you remember the tide tables for autumn of '46?"

"The official ones?" He didn't look up. "Or the ones they burned?"

My heart stopped against my ribs. "The ones they what?"

"Burned." He set a sheet down and wiped his hands, casual as anything, but his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Customs house fire, March of '47. Very convenient fire. Ate the ledgers, the logs, the correspondence files — everything older than two years, gone. Except." He picked the stack back up. "Except the tables get reprinted every year for the almanac. Set fresh, but copied forward from the old plates. And the old plates don't burn, because the old plates live here." He tapped the rack of type-metal forms beside him with one inky finger. "Autumn '46 is sitting in bay fourteen right now, if you've a mind to see it."

"How long have you known about the fire?" I asked.

"Since it happened. My master drank with the customs surveyor, and the surveyor cried into his beer about losing six years of seizure records the week before Corliss brought his case against Wren." Ned finally looked at me, and there was nothing boyish in his face at all. "Nobody asked why the fire ate only the old papers and left the current year untouched. Nobody ever asks. But I set type, Miss Ashgrove. I notice things."

We stood in the yard in the smell of wet paper and lampblack, two people deciding, without saying it outright, to be dangerous together.

"I need copies," I said. "The tables as printed, and the plates themselves compared against whatever survives — the harbor master's duplicates, the churchwardens' almanac, anything. I need to know whether the printed record matches what the observatory clock recorded, or whether both were made to agree."

"And if they were?"

"Then I'll have proof the falsification wasn't one man's error. It was a system."

Ned was quiet a long moment. Somewhere beyond the wall, the press clattered through its rhythm — the sound Saltmere heard every day of its life and never listened to.

"There's a way to pull proofs after hours," he said at last. "There's a way to do most things after hours, if your feet are quiet. But Miss Ashgrove —" and here he hesitated, and I saw how young he suddenly was, "— if this goes wrong, they won't hang a printer's devil. They'll just stop him being hired anywhere from here to Falmouth. My mother takes in washing."

"I know," I said. "If it goes wrong, tell them I came to you about a jobbing order for tide calendars. It's even half true. I'll swear to it publicly if I have to."

"You'd do that?" Genuine astonishment. As if a gentlewoman — or whatever I was, apprentice, tradesman's niece, nobody — would lie for a washerwoman's son.

"I'm building something, Ned," I said. "Out of paper. It has to be built honestly or it's worth nothing, and honest means everyone who helps gets protected first. That's the whole design."

He grinned then, sudden and bright, and spat in his palm, and offered it, and I shook it, ink and all, because some agreements are older than manners.

* * *

The proofs came to me folded inside a delivery of printed handbills to my uncle's shop — *LOST, ONE GRAY GOOSE, ANSWERS TO HONESTY* — which made me laugh aloud and which Ned, when I thanked him, admitted he was proud of. Beneath the goose bills lay six leaves of thin proofing paper, pulled from bay fourteen: the tide tables for October of 1746, exactly as distributed to every harbor office, customs post, and courtroom in the county.

I took them to the observatory that same afternoon, because the cruelest and safest place to compare them was under the noses of the men who'd never imagine it. I spread them on the workbench beside the clock, ostensibly checking the new escapement against published standards, and I worked until my neck ached.

They matched. Of course they matched. Table after table, day after day, the printed tide agreed with the clock's record to the minute — agreed beautifully, flawlessly, with the smooth consistency that no honest instrument and no honest sea had ever achieved in the history of measurement. The sea is irregular. Tides run early and late with the weather and the moon. Two years of perfect agreement was not accuracy.

It was choreography.

And there, on the fourteenth of October, the table showed half-flood at nine of the clock precisely. The very figure that had hanged Tomas Wren. Pulled from the same type-metal that should have been melted down and recast years ago, surviving its own convenient fire like a cockroach.

I copied the figures into my ledger that night, working by feel more than light, my pen scratching in the dark behind the chimney brick. Page by page. Tooth by tooth. There was no grand gesture available to me, no dramatic accusation I could shout in the marketplace and be believed. There was only this: a girl with calipers and a printer's boy with quiet feet, assembling the truth the way one assembles a clock — one small true part at a time, none of it impressive alone, all of it meaningless until the moment the whole mechanism is closed up and set running.

I was writing the final entry of the night — *plate matches clock; clock matches gallows* — when I heard the front bell of my uncle's shop ring, past midnight, heavy and official.

Through the floorboards came my uncle's startled voice, and then another voice answering, smooth as poured wax, and I knew it before I reached the stair.

Magistrate Corliss, calling at this hour, asking after me. Asking whether I'd been keeping well. Asking, my uncle repeated faintly up the stairwell, whether I had found anything *unusual* in the observatory clock.

I blew out my candle and stood in the dark, listening to my heartbeat count off the seconds — honest time, at least, however briefly it was mine.
