## 7. Corliss Closes the Case

The notice appeared on the observatory door on a Tuesday, nailed up with four brass tacks that gleamed like coins on a dead man's eyes.

*By order of Magistrate Aldous Corliss, patron of the Saltmere Observatory Society: the premises are to be closed for renovation and inspection. All instruments shall be catalogued. All records shall be secured. Until such time as works are complete, no person shall enter without writ.*

Freya stood in the drizzle and read it twice, her satchel of tools heavy against her hip. Renovation. The observatory's roof had leaked for eleven years and nobody had climbed a ladder for any of them. Nobody had cared about the damp in the stairwell or the cracked pane in the meridian window until she had spent three weeks with her hands inside the master clock.

She had been careful. She told herself she had been careful. She worked only at night after the stewards went home, kept her findings in her head, wrote nothing down where anyone could find it. But the clock had been sealed now — she'd watched the steward turn the key that morning — and Corliss was not a man who closed doors without knowing what was behind them.

"You're not allowed in," said Hobbes, the under-steward, when she reached for the handle anyway. He was a soft man made softer by fear; he wouldn't meet her eyes. "Magistrate's orders, miss. Truly. I'm sorry."

"I'm the one he hired to fix it."

"Then you'll be summoned when it's done." He hesitated, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Miss Ashgrove. There's a gentleman waiting on you at your workshop. Went by not ten minutes since."

Her blood went cold in a way the harbor fog never managed.

* * *

The boy was where she'd left him, sitting on the workbench with his legs swinging, sorting gears into little families by size the way she'd taught him. He looked up when she came in, and then he looked past her, at the doorway, and his hands went still.

The gentleman filled most of the frame. He wore the gray coat of the magistrate's men and the bored expression of someone doing arithmetic about how much trouble he could be bothered to cause. Behind him, two porters leaned against the wall, arms crossed, watching the street with the patience of men who had done this kind of errand before.

"Freya Ashgrove?" He didn't wait for confirmation. "Corliss. The magistrate. He'd like a word about the observatory clock. Thought we'd save everyone the journey and collect you now."

"The boy stays here," Freya said.

Something flickered across the gentleman's face — not surprise exactly, but interest. "What boy?"

And there it was. The trap laid flat and plain, its teeth showing. She had spoken the boy aloud, named him as hers, in front of witnesses, and she could see the gentleman understanding what he'd been given. *A child left at your door. A hanged smuggler's son, living above your workshop.* Belonging, she thought, was exactly as dangerous as they said. To have something was to be hurt through it.

"He's my apprentice's nephew," she said. "From Millbrook. Here for the winter."

"Millbrook," the gentleman repeated pleasantly. "We'll want the apprentice's name, then. And the sister's."

"You'll want a great many things." She moved to stand between him and the bench. The boy hadn't made a sound — he never did — but she felt his stillness change quality, the way water changes before a squall. "Tell His Worship I'll attend him tomorrow at his convenience. Tonight I'm expected at my guild meeting, and my absence would be remarked upon."

That was a lie. It was also armor. Corliss's power lived in things going unremarked; a missing clockmaker noticed by forty guildsmen was a loose thread he couldn't yet afford to pull.

The gentleman considered her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was worse than the boredom. "Tomorrow, then. Nine o'clock, the magistrate's chambers. He does so like to talk about clocks." He tipped his hat to the boy — a slow, deliberate, horrible courtesy — and left.

Freya bolted the door. Then she put her back against it and slid partway down it before catching herself, because the boy was watching, and she was all he had, and being all someone had meant you didn't get to slide down doors.

"We're leaving Saltmere tonight," she said. "Both of us. Pack nothing that talks."

* * *

They didn't make it out.

That was the thing she understood later, walking the dark road toward the Widow Wren's cottage with the boy's hand in hers and her heart going like a broken escapement: they were never going to make it out, because Corliss didn't need to catch them fleeing to end them. He needed only to close the roads behind them, slowly, politely, one notice at a time. By morning there would be a question about the apprenticeship papers. By evening, a rumor about the clockmaker girl who'd meddled with instruments beyond her station. The walls didn't crash down on people like Freya Ashgrove. They assembled themselves, brick by brick, out of other people's wish to be safe.

Marta Wren opened the door before she knocked.

"They came to you too," Freya said.

"To warn me." Marta stood aside to let them in. The cottage smelled of tallow and dried kelp, and every surface was scrupulously, defiantly clean. "Magistrate's clerk, all politeness. Said the matter of my husband was settled forever, that reopening it would be unkind to his memory, and that widows who grieved loudly sometimes found their pensions reconsidered. Pensions." Her mouth twisted. "I have no pension. He knows that. It wasn't for me."

Freya set the boy on the settle. He curled toward his mother's side with an instinct so old it broke something open in her chest, and Marta's hand came down on his hair automatically, the way hands do, and stayed there.

"Marta. They know he's alive. They know he's with me."

"I know."

"And you still say let it lie."

Marta was quiet for a while. The fire ticked. Out on the flats, the tide was turning — Freya could feel it in her teeth these days, God help her, the way you feel a wrong note in a familiar tune.

"I say let it lie because everything I love is standing in this room," Marta said finally. "Two years ago they took my husband and called it law. If I shout now, they'll take my son and call it charity — some respectable family, some new name, and I'll never learn it. That's what 'settled forever' means, Freya. It means they've decided, and the deciding is the law, and the law has the roads and the courts and the printing presses." She looked down at the boy. "But."

"But?"

"But I married a man who fixed boats. And he used to say — you don't argue with the sea, love. You just keep bailing, and you watch for the tide to change. Because it always changes. It's the one thing they can't forge." Her eyes came up, and there was something in them Freya hadn't seen before, something that had been two years dying and was not dead. "You found something in that clock. Don't tell me what. Don't write to me about it. But if there's a day when the tide turns — a real day, a public day, when it costs them more to silence it than to swallow it — you come to this door, and I'll walk into that inquiry and say my husband's name so loud they'll hear it in London."

"It's coming," Freya said. "There's an inquiry convening. The charter ship."

"I've heard. When?"

"Any day. A week. Less."

Marta nodded slowly, and pressed her son's hand once, and let go of it, and Freya understood that this letting-go was the bravest thing she had ever witnessed: Marta sending her boy back into danger because the alternative was teaching him to live inside a lie small enough to fit in a cottage.

* * *

Nine o'clock, the magistrate's chambers. Corliss received her alone, which frightened her more than an audience would have. He was smaller than his reputation, gray and neat, with the hands of a man who had once been good at something and had chosen instead to be powerful.

"Miss Ashgrove." He gestured her to a chair and didn't offer refreshment. On his desk lay a document she recognized with a lurch: the observatory's tide tables, twenty years of them, bound in green buckram. Her own repair invoices sat beside them. "You've had the master clock apart for — what is it — five weeks?"

"Six."

"A long time for an escapement."

"It was a long job."

He turned a page of the tide tables with one finger, unhurried, a man petting a dog he owned. "These records are the finest in the county. Ships' masters trust them. Courts have relied on them. The Wren trial, notably — the tide evidence was decisive, and the tide evidence came from that clock." He looked up. His eyes were pale and patient and absolutely empty of anything but attention. "So when you took the mechanism apart, Miss Ashgrove, you became custodian of a great deal of history. History that certain parties might be tempted to reinterpret. For spite, for money, for vanity." He closed the volume softly. "I'm sealing the observatory today. Renovation. Everything will be examined by proper authorities — proper ones — and put right, and when it reopens, the record will be whole again. Undisputed. Permanent."

"And if someone disputes it anyway?"

"Then that person," Corliss said, with genuine mildness, "will be asserting that the laws of this harbor have been built on a fraud. Which is slander against institutions far larger than me, and treated accordingly. I'd hate to see a talented young woman end her career over a gear." He rose; the interview was over. "Your invoices are approved, by the way. Paid in full. You'll find gentlemen are generous with craftsmen who remember their place in the mechanism."

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the seawall, and stood where the breakwater met the harbor light, and let the wind try to take her hat off while she thought.

He had confirmed everything and confessed nothing. He knew what the clock had done. He knew she knew. And he had just told her, in the language of polite men, precisely what happened to people who stepped outside the record he controlled.

Most people, at the seawall, would have turned around. Would have taken the paid invoice and the intact career and the quiet life, and told themselves that some wrongs were structural, load-bearing, too deep in the building to cut out without bringing the roof down.

Freya Ashgrove thought about tolerances. About how a clock ran true not because no force opposed it, but because its maker had accounted for every opposition — friction, weather, wear — and built the mechanism to overcome them anyway. She had six weeks of observations in her memory, exact to the fraction. She had a printer's devil who owed her and hated the magistrate. She had a widow with a voice like a bell and grief two years old and ready. And somewhere out on gray water, carrying a ship full of charter papers and lawyers, was the one thing Corliss could not seal, postpone, or renovate: an inquiry he didn't control, arriving on a schedule even he had to obey.

The tide was turning below her, the current combing the harbor into long silver lines, running the way it always ran, honest as arithmetic, indifferent as justice.

They came for the boy at dusk.

Not the magistrate's men this time — parish officers, two of them, with a paper authorizing removal of "an unregistered minor residing at the Ashgrove premises." Someone had moved faster than she had, cleaner than she had, and by the time a neighbor's boy ran to find her on the seawall, the officers were already at her door, and Marta was already screaming from the cottage lane, and the boy—

The boy was gone from the workbench. The window above the shop stood open to the rain, its latch sheared cleanly away, and on the floor beneath it lay a cold chisel from her own rack, placed dead center, deliberate as a signature — and pinned under it, a single folded note in a hand she had never seen before:

*Harbor stairs. Midnight. Come alone if you want him back — and bring nothing written.*
