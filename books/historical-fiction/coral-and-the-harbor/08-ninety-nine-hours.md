## 8. Ninety-Nine Hours

The boy brought me the news, though he didn't know it. He had taken to meeting the mail packet at the quay each morning — not to fetch letters, but to watch the gulls fight over the fish gutters — and it was there he saw the harbor master hoist the signal flag: a red pennant with a white cross, snapping in a wind that smelled of coming weather.

I was filing a pallet arbor when he came pounding up the observatory stairs, took my sleeve, and pointed down at the harbor. I leaned over the sill and understood at once what the flag meant.

*The *Providence* Bess.* The charter ship out of Kingsport. She carried surveyors, charter commissioners, men with seals on their fingers and ledgers of their own — the inquiry into Saltmere's harbor charters, convened whenever she docked, and dockings could not be predicted, only answered. For two years Corliss had enjoyed the luxury of an inquiry that never arrived. Now it was four days out, riding a fair wind.

Four days. Ninety-nine hours, if I counted from that moment, and I did count them; I counted everything now.

I had thought I would have weeks. I had planned as if the world were a well-run clockwork, each wheel turning at its appointed rate. But the sea does not consult horologists. By Friday the commissioners would sit in the customs hall, hear petitions, weigh charters, and leave again — and whatever was not written by then would never be written at all.

The boy tugged my sleeve again. He looked a question at me. I couldn't answer it honestly — *yes, everything now depends on the next four days, and you are the reason I am frightened and the reason I cannot stop* — so I put my hand briefly on his head and said, "Run down to Mrs. Wren's. Tell her—" and stopped, because he couldn't carry words. I tore a corner from my repair log, wrote *Come tomorrow. Early. Bring nothing*, and folded it into his fist.

He went like an arrow. He always knew when something mattered; children smell urgency the way dogs smell weather.

I turned back to the clock.

* * *

The ledger lay under the third floorboard of the workshop, wrapped in oilcloth — my apprentice's ledger, though no master would ever sign it. Eleven pages so far. Diagrams of the escapement as found: the count wheel with its filed tooth, the compound gear train cut to a ratio no honest maker would defend, the tide-recording pen rigged to lag nineteen minutes behind true high water. Nineteen minutes. On the morning of Tomas Wren's trial, nineteen minutes was the difference between a tide that could carry a revenue cutter over the bar and one that could not. Nineteen minutes had hanged him.

I spread the ledger open beside the clock's exposed movement and worked until my eyes burned. Every alteration photographed in ink: measurements, ratios, the tool-marks on the modified pinion where someone had cut new teeth freehand — and here was the thing I had circled twice, the thing that made my hand shake even now — the cut was left-handed. A left-handed file worker. I knew exactly one man in Saltmere who filed left-handed: Corliss's own clerk, Mr. Ambrose Fenn, whose handwriting appeared on the note that had been pinned to the boy's blanket.

*Forget this face.*

I would not forget it. That was the whole of my argument with the world.

By lamplight I finished page twelve, then thirteen: a comparison table, honest tide times reconstructed from the almanac against the observatory's recorded times, the discrepancy plotted month by month for the twenty years the clock had been lying. The pattern was not random. It clustered — 1741, 1742, 1743 — precisely around every date on which a seizure, an arrest, or a condemnation of cargo had depended on tidal testimony. Someone had tuned the harbor's memory like an instrument, and played it whenever it profited them.

Three in the morning. The clock above me ticked its false tick, patient as a liar. Ninety-four hours left.

* * *

Mrs. Wren came at dawn, gray-faced, smelling of the fish-curing sheds where she now worked. She read my face before I'd spoken a word.

"It's come, hasn't it," she said. "The ship."

"Friday. Customs hall." I told her what I needed: her testimony about the night the records were read into the trial, the impossible tide times, the way the magistrate's men had produced the observatory logs as gospel. She listened with her hands locked together, knuckles white.

"I begged you once to let it lie," she said quietly. "For Jem's sake."

"And I've tried to keep him out of it. He sleeps upstairs; he knows nothing but clocks and gulls."

"That's not what I meant." She looked at the window, at the light coming up over the water. "I mean myself. I'm a cured-fish widow, Freya. I stand in a shed all day among men who drink Corliss's health. If I walk into that hall and speak against him, they will take everything I have left — and they'll start with the boy, because taking him hurts you, and hurting you shuts your mouth."

"Then we don't give them the chance. The commissioners can grant protective custody—"

"The commissioners leave Saturday." Her voice cracked on it. "And Corliss stays."

We stood in the noise of our own fear for a moment. Then she straightened, and something in her face changed — not courage exactly, but the exhausted stubbornness of a woman who has already lost the worst thing and discovered she is still standing.

"Tomas used to say a tide waits for no petition," she said. "All right. Friday. I'll speak. But find me something stronger than grief to hold in my hands, or they'll eat me alive."

I pressed the ledger's thirteenth page into them — a copy only, the original hidden elsewhere now. "Then hold this."

* * *

The printer's devil, Kit Sorrell, met me in the alley behind the press at noon, breathless, ink to the elbows. He was sixteen and terrified and gloriously reliable, which is the best combination a conspirator can have.

"Burned customs house archives," he said, before I'd asked. "That's why you're here. I got in."

My heart actually stumbled. "Kit—"

"Don't thank me yet." He pulled a satchel from under his coat. "Half of it's water-ruined, and the fire took the east racks entire. But the tide ledgers were iron-boxed — old habit from the smuggling scares — and the boxes held." He handed me a bundle, tied with tarred cord. "Copies of the original tide observations, the ones the observatory *should* have matched. Plus the cutter logs for October '43. And one more thing." His grin had no joy in it. "A requisition slip. Signed Fenn. Dated three days after Wren's arrest. For the removal of 'certain instruments' from the observatory to the magistrate's residence. Brought back a week later."

Fenn's name. Again. The left-handed file and the requisition slip and the note at the door — a rope of evidence, strand by strand, and every strand the same man's knots.

"Copy these tonight," I said. "Two sets. One comes to me, one goes somewhere neither of us can reach — the chapel poor-box, Father Ellery, tell him nothing, just place it."

"He already asked after you," Kit said. "Sunday. Asked if you still kept the observatory clock."

The alley seemed to narrow. "What did you tell him?"

"That you kept everyone's clocks. Which is true." He shouldered his satchel. "Ninety hours, mistress Ashgrove. Best get back to yours."

* * *

I spent the evening doing the hardest work of all: unmaking my own repairs' predecessor. Restoring the clock to truth was delicate in a way forgery is not — anyone can break a thing; only craft makes it honest again. I cut and fitted a new count wheel, correct to the minute, tested against my regulator through forty-one hours of continuous running. I rebuilt the recording pen's linkage so it would write high water when high water happened, not nineteen minutes after. And I left the falsified components intact — cleaned, catalogued, boxed with wax-sealed labels — because the demonstration would need both truths side by side: the mechanism as corrupted, and the mechanism as corrected, ticking in the same room, telling two different stories about the same harbor.

Thursday night I ran the final trial. The corrected train turned beneath my lamp, steady, unlovely, truthful. Nineteen minutes reclaimed from twenty years of theft. I sat on the floor with my back against the case and let myself feel, for exactly one minute by my own watch, the size of what was coming.

Then the boy came down the stairs. He'd woken; he always woke when I worked late, and padded down to sit near the warmth of the lamp. He looked at the open movement, then at me, and made his small wordless sound — a rising note, a question.

"Tomorrow," I told him. "Men are coming to ask questions about the harbor, and I'm going to answer them honestly. You'll stay with Kit Sorrell at the print shop. You'll be safe."

He studied my face with those grave eyes, then reached out and touched the ledger where it lay open — touched the little inked drawing of a gear, deliberately, the way he touched everything he wanted to keep.

A knock at the street door, soft and deliberate. My chest went cold. Nobody knocked softly at midnight unless they shouldn't be there.

I blew out the lamp, put my palm on the boy's head — *stay, quiet, stay* — and went up the stairs into the dark to see who had come, at last, to find out how much I knew.
