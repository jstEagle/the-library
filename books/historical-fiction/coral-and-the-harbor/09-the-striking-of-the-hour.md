## 9. The Striking of the Hour

The inquiry convened in the customs house at ten o'clock, and by nine Freya's hands were shaking so badly she could not thread a screw.

She held them out over her workbench — the little folding table she'd carried up the harbor road herself, the clock's escapement cradled in a box lined with wool — and watched them tremble like something caught. Ninety-nine hours of work, and now this. She thought of her master, old Hobb, telling her that a clockmaker's hands should be colder than her fears. *Warm hands mean a warm head, girl, and a warm head drops things.*

She pressed them flat against the wood until the shaking slowed.

The customs house had been chosen for its size, and it wore the crowd badly. Benches packed with Saltmere faces she knew: fishwives and pilots, the harbormaster in his good coat, the printer's devil Tam hovering near the doors with his satchel of copied tide tables hugged to his chest like a hymnal. Up in the gallery, roped off, sat the people who mattered — three commissioners from London, gray men with gray ledgers, and behind them the observatory society in their finest, Magistrate Corliss at the center of them like a stone at the middle of a ring.

He looked rested. That frightened her more than anything else. Four days ago he had stood in her workshop doorway and told her, with perfect courtesy, that a woman who meddled in records she didn't understand would find Saltmere a narrow town indeed. He had looked tired then. Now he looked like a man attending the confirmation of his own virtue.

And beside the commissioners' bench, small and straight-backed in her widow's black, sat Mara Wren.

Their eyes met across the room. Mara did not smile. She had not smiled since the night she'd come to Freya's door at dark and said, *If I do this, I do it for him, not for you* — meaning the boy, meaning Cael, who was at this moment two miles inland at the Widow Tice's farm, where no Corliss man could reach him without crossing half of Saltmere first. Freya had walked that route three times yesterday just to count the turns.

The chief commissioner rapped his stick. "This inquiry sits to examine the charter claims of the port of Saltmere and matters arising from the customs records thereof. We will hear the petitioners."

Freya stood. Her legs held. That was most of the battle, she'd decided; everything else was craft, and craft she could do.

* * *

She had asked for one hour and gotten forty minutes, and she spent them the only way she knew: by building the truth in front of them, piece by piece, on the table before the bench.

"The master clock at Saltmere Observatory," she began, "keeps time for the harbor. Every tide reading, every shipping log, every entry in the customs books for twenty years has been stamped against its chimes. Which means that if the clock lies, everything measured against it lies too."

"Clocks don't lie," said the second commissioner mildly. "They're machines."

"Yes, sir." She lifted the escapement from its box and set it on the felt. "But they can be taught to."

She talked them through it the way she'd rehearsed it on Tam, on Mara, on the empty air of her workshop at midnight. The anchor escapement, the escape wheel, the relationship between beat and increment. Then she showed them the gear.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Whoever had made it — and she no longer doubted who had commissioned it — had cut the teeth with real skill, an irregular tooth spacing so subtle that any casual inspection found nothing wrong. Only when the wheel turned under load did the irregularity assert itself: a drift of eleven seconds per hour, cumulative, adjustable. Enough to shift a recorded high tide by a full fathom's worth of interval if you let the error run long enough before correcting it.

"Tomas Wren was convicted," she said, "on sworn testimony that his ship cleared the point at a tide state impossible for his draft. The tide tables entered into evidence came from this observatory. They were stamped by this clock." She set the gear down and stepped back from the table, palms open. "I have removed it. I have kept it. And I have here—" Tam was already moving up the aisle, satchel open— "the original tide tables from the burned customs archive, recovered from the printer's proofs, showing what the tides actually were on those nights. Set beside the observatory logs, they show a discrepancy that begins"—she let them see the ledger page—"*here*. Eleven days after this gear was installed."

Silence. Somewhere in the gallery a man began breathing audibly through his nose.

Corliss rose. He did it slowly, unhurried, a man swatting a fly with the same care he'd use to bless an altar.

"Commissioners. What we are hearing is an apprentice — a *journeywoman*, I beg pardon — offering the fevered theory of a girl who has held a screwdriver to a great instrument for, what is it, Miss Ashgrove, six weeks? You ask this inquiry to overturn settled judgments on the testimony of a cog." His voice warmed into reasonableness, the voice of a man explaining weather to children. "Gears wear. Records drift. If every clerical variance were treason, no harbor in England would keep a book."

"That is precisely why I brought the gear," Freya said, "instead of the story about it."

She turned to the commissioners. "Wear is random. This is not. The tooth spacing repeats on an exact cycle — I can demonstrate it turning right now, and I can show you the matching cycle in the corrected logs, twenty years running, always in the months when smuggling prosecutions peaked. Someone tuned this clock the way you'd tune an instrument, gentlemen. To play a particular song."

The third commissioner, who had not spoken yet, said quietly, "Turn it."

* * *

They set the escapement in the demonstration frame the commissioners' clerk provided, rigged to a weight on a line through the window casement, and Freya made the wheel turn while the room leaned in. She heard the moment it caught — a soft, wrong tick, tick... tick — the irregular heartbeat she had lain awake listening to for weeks. The clerk marked intervals against his own watch. His face went carefully blank, which told her everything.

Corliss sat back down. It was the first thing he had done all morning that looked his age.

Then the clerk read the corrected tables aloud against the observatory logs, and the room changed. It was strange how quiet it was. No shouting, no uproar — just the dry voice receding numbers, and around Freya the sound of a town doing arithmetic it had refused to do for two years. She watched the harbormaster pull his own pocket log toward him and begin checking entries. Watched a fishwife in the third row go still, then furious, in the particular way of someone remembering a drowned neighbor.

"Enough," Corliss said. It came out too fast. He stood again, and now the warmth was gone from his voice, and what remained underneath made several people lean away from him. "This is slander dressed as horology. I have funded that observatory for fifteen years. I have—"

"You funded the renovation," Freya said, "that sealed the clock tower four days after I reported my repairs to the society. You declared the Wren matter closed forever the same week I found the gear." She hadn't meant to say it yet; it left her anyway, steady and cold. "Magistrate, if there is nothing to find, why did you lock the room?"

"I locked nothing," Corliss said, "that was not already mine."

He said it before he could stop himself. Freya saw the instant it landed — saw him hear his own words, saw the gallery understand them — and the silence afterward was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

* * *

They called Mara Wren at noon.

She came down the aisle with her chin up and her hands folded, and when she reached the bench she did not look at Corliss at all. She looked at the commissioners, and she told it plainly, in the flat clear voice of a woman who had rehearsed grief until it obeyed her.

She told them about the night the revenue men came, and the note delivered next morning in a magistrate's hand, and the trial that lasted less than an hour. She told them about being warned, afterward, that a widow with a child might find the harbor unwelcoming if she asked questions. She told them about the child.

"My son is alive," she said. "You will not hear that from any record in this town, because every record was made to say otherwise. I sent him away to keep him breathing. I let his father hang unavenged to keep *myself* breathing. And I have hated myself for both, every single day, for two years." Her voice cracked once and she mastered it. "Miss Ashgrove came to me and asked me to remember out loud. I told her to let sleeping dogs lie. She would not. So here I am, lying awake instead."

The chief commissioner asked her one question: whether she swore the child at the observatory door was Tomas Wren's son.

"He has his father's hands," Mara said. "Ask anyone who mended nets beside him."

* * *

It went against Corliss through the afternoon like water finding cracks. A society member resigned on the steps rather than testify. The clerk produced the renovation order bearing Corliss's seal, dated suspiciously. An old tide-keeper, wheezing and terrified, admitted he'd been paid to transcribe the observatory logs "as corrected" without correction notes. Each blow alone might have been survived. Together they were a wall coming down, and everyone in the room could hear it.

Corliss stopped defending himself around four o'clock. He simply sat, gloved hands flat on the rail, staring at the escapement on its felt like a man looking at the thing that had been living inside his own house. When the commissioners formally ordered the seized records impounded and referred the whole matter to crown counsel, he laughed once — a short, awful bark — and said, mostly to himself:

"Twenty years. Do you know what it takes to keep a lie running for twenty years? Every bell in this harbor rang on my word."

"Yes," Freya said. She hadn't planned that either. "That's why they're going to ring honestly now."

* * *

They released her near dusk, and she did not go home.

She climbed the observatory hill in the last light with her tools over her shoulder and Mara beside her, past the seal on the tower door, which she broke without pausing — it was her seal to break now, or close enough that no one would argue. Inside, the master clock waited where she had left it, stripped of its lie, cleaned and trued and reassembled to keep honest time, wound and set against the regulator she'd carried up from London standards by post.

She set the pendulum swinging. She checked the beat. She waited, hand on the case, watching the minute hand crawl toward eight.

Below them, lamps were coming on all over Saltmere. Word had run ahead of them through the streets — it always did, in a harbor town — and figures were drifting up the hill road, and gathering in the yard, and standing in the cold with their hats off as if for a funeral or a christening.

Mara touched her arm. "What happens when it strikes?"

"Everyone in town hears it," Freya said. "And every ear that's been setting its day by this clock for twenty years hears it run true for the first time. That's all. That's enough."

The minute hand climbed the last degree. The escapement took hold — clean, even, *right*, the sound she had been chasing through other people's instruments her whole life. The hammer drew back.

The first stroke rolled out over the harbor like something breaking surface after a long dive, and Freya Ashgrove stood in the tower with her eyes shut and counted eight true hours, and below, in the dark, the whole of Saltmere stopped to listen.

When the last stroke faded, Mara gripped her hand hard, and Freya opened her eyes — because far down the coast road, cutting through the fog toward the harbor lights, a rider was coming fast, and behind him, faint but unmistakable on the evening wind, came the sound of wheels.

The charter ship's dispatches had arrived early.
