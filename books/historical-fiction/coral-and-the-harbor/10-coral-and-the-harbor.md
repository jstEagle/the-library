## 10. Coral and the Harbor

Spring came to Saltmere the way honesty comes to a man who has lied for years — slowly at first, then all at once. The fog thinned. The harbor turned from pewter to green. And on the first Tuesday of April, the town crier stood at the foot of the observatory steps and read the inquiry's finding in a voice meant to carry across water.

Tomas Wren, fisherman and father of Saltmere, was innocent of the crimes for which he was hanged.

Freya heard it from the workshop bench, where she was fitting a new pallet stone into a lady's carriage watch. Her hands stopped. They did not shake — they had done their shaking months ago, in the gallery of the customs hall, with two hundred eyes on her and the falsified escapement laid out on green baize like a confession made of brass. Now her hands only went still, the way a clock goes still between one tick and the next.

Through the open window she could hear the crowd. Not cheering — Saltmere was too careful a town for cheering, at least while Corliss still walked free on his own recognizance. But there was a sound underneath the reading of the verdict, low and persistent, that Freya recognized after a moment: people saying the name *Wren* to each other, over and over, passing it hand to hand like something warm on a cold day.

The boy sat cross-legged on the floor by her feet, sorting gears by size into wooden trays. He had taken to wearing an apron three sizes too large, rolled at the waist, because he wanted to look like he worked here. He did work here, in his way. He handed her tools before she asked. He never spoke.

He looked up at her now, sensing the change in the room the way animals sense weather.

"They said his name," Freya told him. "Your father's name. All of it. *Innocent.*"

The boy considered this with those grave dark eyes — Wren's eyes, everyone said so now, though no one had dared say it when it might have cost them something. Then he went back to his gears, and Freya went back to her pallet stone, and above them the master clock ticked through the morning, keeping honest time, second by second by second.

* * *

The trial of Magistrate Aldous Corliss opened three weeks later and lasted eleven days, which the town agreed was nine days longer than anyone expected him to fight. He fought anyway. His counsel argued that whatever modifications existed in the observatory's clock had been made by persons unknown; that a woman apprentice's testimony regarding escapements was, at best, hearsay dressed in technical language; that the tide tables copied from the burned customs house archives were themselves of doubtful provenance.

It did not survive the first week. The printer's devil — Jem Holloway, seventeen, ink-stained to the elbow and braver than any boy Freya had ever met — took the stand and described, in the flat voice of someone reciting multiplication tables, how he had climbed through the archive window on four separate nights while Freya kept watch below. Then the widow Maren Wren took the stand, and everything after that was aftermath.

She wore grey. She spoke quietly. When the prosecutor asked whether she wished to say anything about her husband, she said only: "He told me the night before they took him that the tides would speak for him someday. I thought grief had addled him." Her hands folded in her lap. "They have spoken."

Corliss did not weep or rage. That was somehow worse. He sat upright through all eleven days, listening to the machinery of his own making disassembled before him bolt by bolt, and his face held the expression of a man watching a repair he cannot afford. When the foreman of the jury rose to deliver the verdict — guilty on charges of forgery, conspiracy, and the subornation of evidence resulting in death — Corliss looked once toward the gallery, toward the small figure sitting beside Freya Ashgrove, and then he looked away, and Freya understood that he had finally, in the eleventh hour, done the arithmetic honestly.

They took him down through the side door. The crowd outside let him pass in silence. In Saltmere, silence was the loudest thing there was.

* * *

The letter came in May, delivered by a nervous clerk of the Harbor Commission who waited on the workshop step while Freya read it twice.

The Commission, it seemed, wished to regularize her position. The observatory required a clockmaker of record — the phrase was theirs, underlined as if they were proud of having thought of it — and given her service to the inquiry, and the "unfortunate vacancy" left by old Master Pell's retirement, the appointment was hers if she would accept it. There was a salary attached. There was a set of keys. At the bottom, in smaller script, was the clause that made her read the letter a second time: *the appointee shall hold full authority over the timekeeping instruments of the harbor, including the tide records, and no alteration to said instruments shall be made save under her hand and seal.*

Her own seal. She would have to commission one.

She thought of her first day at the observatory, nearly a year gone now, climbing the fog-slick stairs with her toolbox and her borrowed coat, invisible among the gentlemen of the Society, determined only to finish the job and leave without being noticed. Master Pell had introduced her to the assembled members as "the girl from Ashgrove's shop" — not even her own name, just her dead master's, as if apprentices were furniture that changed hands. She had smiled the way you smile when smiling is cheaper than speaking.

Now the clerk shifted his weight on the step. "There will need to be a formal signing," he said. "Before the Commission. And the Society will want — well, they'll want to be seen approving it."

"Of course they will," Freya said.

"You'll come, then?"

She folded the letter. Outside, the harbor lay flat and bright, and somewhere beyond the point a charter ship was working its way in on a tide that the master clock had predicted to the minute, because now it told the truth about tides.

"I'll come," she said. "And I'll be early. I find I've developed opinions about punctuality."

* * *

The signing was held in the observatory's great hall, beneath the orrery with its slow brass planets. The whole apparatus of the town turned out: the Commissioners in their chain of office, the Society gentlemen in their best coats, Maren Wren in the third row where Freya could see her, and Jem Holloway grinning by the door with his sleeves rolled up as if he half expected to be sent running for type.

Freya signed her name — *Freya Ashgrove, Clockmaker of Record* — and felt the nib catch slightly on the paper, and pressed harder, and finished the name the way you finish anything worth finishing: deliberately.

Then came the guardianship papers. This part she had not expected to be public. The Commissioner read the relevant passage aloud — the wardship of the child known in the inquiry records as *the boy Wren*, committed to the care and authority of Freya Ashgrove until his majority — and Freya realized, with a lurch, that she had assumed this would happen in some back office, quickly, before anyone thought better of it. Instead it was happening here, under the planets, in front of everyone, as if it mattered. As if she mattered.

The clerk brought the pen. The boy stood beside her, gripping a corner of the tablecloth, watching the proceedings with the fixed attention he gave to complicated mechanisms.

"There is a formality," the Commissioner said gently. "The child must be identified on the record. A Christian name, if he has one."

Freya looked at the boy. He looked back. In eight months she had learned every language of his silence — the tilt of his head for *yes*, the flattening of his mouth for *no*, the particular way he breathed when he wanted to laugh and didn't trust himself to. But he had never given her a name, and she had never pressed. Names could be dangerous things to give away; someone had taught him that with a note pinned to his coat ordering the world to forget his face.

"He hasn't spoken one," Freya began. "We can enter him as—"

"*Fwee-ah.*"

The word came out round and unpracticed, a coin from a pocket where it had been saved a long time. It was not quite her name. It was better than her name — it was her name the way a child mints it, worn smooth, entirely his own.

The hall went very quiet. The Commissioner stopped writing. Somewhere in the third row, Maren Wren put her hand over her mouth.

The boy looked around at all the faces turning toward him and did not retreat into silence. He planted his feet. "*Fwee-ah,*" he said again, louder, pointing at her with the absolute certainty of a witness identifying what is most true in the room.

"That," Freya managed, "will do very well for the record."

She wrote it out beneath her own signature — *guardian of the boy Fwee-ah, called Ashgrove-Wren by his guardian's wish* — and her hand was steady, and her eyes were not, and she decided that history could keep whichever account it preferred.

* * *

That evening she carried him up the observatory stair on her shoulders, because he asked with a look, and because the evening was clear enough to see the whole harbor laid out below: the masts, the customs house with its new roof, the crooked lanes going gold with lamplight. The tide was coming in, filling the harbor the way spring fills a channel, patient and unstoppable and exactly on schedule.

At the top of the tower the master clock stood in its case, polished, regulated, striking the hour with a sound like a clean sentence ending in a period. Six strokes. The boy counted them on his fingers, getting to six without help for the first time.

Below, lights were coming on along the waterfront. Somewhere among them, Maren Wren was lighting hers. Freya thought of Tomas Wren telling his wife that the tides would speak for him, and of how long a lie can stand — twenty years of doctored logs, a whole town's convenience built on a bent gear — and of how little it takes, in the end, to bring it down. One apprentice who wouldn't look away. One boy who wouldn't stay forgotten. One gear, cut honest.

"Look," she told the boy, and pointed west, where the sun was going down over the point in bands of coral and gold — the color the harbor turned at the end of a fair day, the color she had started calling *his* color in her head without ever meaning to.

He looked. He leaned his small weight against her neck. And as the light drained off the water and the lamps took over from the sky, he said — softly, conversationally, as if remarking on the weather —

"Coral."

Freya closed her eyes and held him and let the clock count the seconds behind them, every one of them true.

"Yes," she said. "Coral and the harbor. Both of us. All of us."

Far below, the tide turned, and began its long, unhurried, honest work of coming home.
