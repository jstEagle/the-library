## 1. The Light That Keeps Nothing

The lamp wanted trimming at four minutes past six, and Jude Villalobos trimmed it at four minutes past six, because the lamp did not care that it was November, or that the war had eaten three years of the world, or that his hands were stiff with cold. The lamp cared about oil and wick and nothing else, which was one of the reasons he loved it.

He worked by feel more than sight now. His fingers knew the brass collar of the burner, the little wheel that raised the flame, the exact quarter-turn past snug where the glass chimney seated without a squeak. Elena used to say he could light the lamp in his sleep, and once — though he never told her this — he very nearly had. He'd woken at midnight certain the light was out, climbed the ninety-one steps in his nightshirt, found everything burning exactly as it should be, and stood there in the warm round room feeling like a fool and a fraud until the relief of it made him laugh out loud into the dark.

That was before. Before the telegram. Before the winter she didn't come back from the mainland.

Now he lit the lamp twice a day, sunset and the hour before dawn, and between those two acts he kept the rest of the station alive through small stubborn ceremonies: the log written in his careful schoolroom hand; the lens wiped with a chamois dampened in vinegar, so the salt would not etch it; the stairs swept top to bottom on Wednesdays; the fog bell's mechanism oiled whether it rang or not. He had done these things so many times that they had worn smooth as sea glass, and when he performed them he could almost believe he was performing them for her, that she was somewhere in the house below, mending a net or arguing with a kettle, and would call up presently to ask if he'd remembered the vinegar.

He always remembered the vinegar.

* * *

The morning of the first of November came up gray and mild, the sea lying flat as poured pewter, and Jude went down the cliff path to check the crab pots because checking the crab pots was what Tuesdays were for. Three pots, each tied off to its own buoy, each hauled hand over hand with the rope burning pleasantly through his palms.

The first pot held one crab, indignant. The second held nothing but a tangle of kelp and a whelk shell with a hermit crab in residence, which Jude tipped back over the side with an apology. "Rent's due," he told it. "You've been squatting."

The third pot held two crabs and, wedged in the mesh, half a child's wooden boat, painted blue, its little sail long gone. He turned it over in his fingers. Somewhere along this coast a child had lost it, and somewhere along this coast a child was probably crying about it, and the thought sat strangely in his chest, heavy and unwelcome, like a stone swallowed whole.

He put the boat in his coat pocket without deciding to, and climbed back up.

Elena would have known what to do with it. She had a genius for broken things. Half the objects in her workshop were things other people had thrown away — clock guts, cracked lenses, a ship's figurehead someone had sawn off for firewood, which she'd propped in the corner and called "the widow" and spoken to in passing as though it were a shy lodger. *Morning, widow,* she'd say. *Sleep well?* And Jude, who had married her precisely because she talked to figureheads, would carry her tea down to her and watch her work and understand nothing and be perfectly content.

The workshop stood apart from the house, a low stone building with a slate roof and windows facing the water, because everything Elena built drank from the tide. Tide-driven automata, she called them. Machines that wound themselves on the breathing of the sea. She had been at it eleven years, and in all that time she had finished perhaps a dozen pieces: a gull that flapped and cried on the hour; a set of chimes that played when the swell ran high; a little tin fish that swam lazy circles in a tank of seawater and refused, on principle, to perform for visitors. The rest — the great unfinished rest — filled the benches and hung from beams and crowded the floor in patient, hopeful parts.

She had died with three machines on her bench. Jude had not touched them since.

He passed the workshop door every day. He looked at it the way you look at a closed door in a dream, knowing what's behind it and unable to make your hand reach for the latch. Some mornings he stood outside it a while, listening to the wind move through the gaps in the eaves, making the building breathe. Then he would go in and sweep the kitchen instead, or re-caulk a window, or count the oil drums again even though he'd counted them Sunday and they had not, in the interval, learned to multiply.

History, he had decided, happened elsewhere. It happened in Flanders and on the Atlantic convoy lanes and in the offices of men whose signatures moved ships and boys around like checkers. Here there was only weather and repetition, and he had arranged his life into a shape so regular that the war itself seemed to pass him by at a respectful distance, the way storms split around a headland. A supply boat came fortnightly. A naval inspector came quarterly. Otherwise the world left him alone, and he left it alone, and the light burned, and that was the whole arrangement.

He was a keeper. He kept things. It had taken him years to admit how much of that was grief wearing a uniform.

* * *

Wednesday. Swept the stairs. Wrote the log. The wind had come around to the northeast overnight and carried a smell of iron in it, the smell of weather being assembled somewhere over the horizon, and he noted it in the margin of the logbook the way Elena used to note things — small annotations, private shorthand. *Wind NE, freshening. Gulls low.* She'd have added something else. She read weather like a letter from a friend, full of news and hints. *Rain coming Thursday, and somebody's bad temper with it.*

He caught himself writing in her style and stopped, pen lifted, ink gathering at the nib in a fat black bead. Then, deliberately, he wrote: *Gulls low. Expecting company of some kind.* And underlined it, and felt slightly ridiculous, and did not cross it out.

The supply boat was not due until Friday. But at midday, standing at the gallery rail with his tea going cold, he watched a small gray shape working up the coast against the wind — too small for the supply boat, wrong lines altogether, running without lights in daylight hours when any honest vessel would show them. Fishing boats ran dark only when they were doing something worth hiding.

Jude lowered his telescope, considered, and then raised it again. The boat hugged the headland, slipped behind the far rocks at the point, and did not come out. Ten minutes. Fifteen. When it finally emerged it was riding higher in the water, lighter by whatever it had carried, and it stood away south without once looking toward the lighthouse.

Which was sensible. Nobody looked at lighthouses. Lighthouses were furniture — fixed, reliable, blind in the way that only things meant to see can afford to be. He wrote in the log: *Vessel sighted, small, gray, course S. Nothing further.* And then, after a moment, because the habit of honesty was the last one he trusted himself with: *Did not stop.*

He told himself it was none of his business. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, he reflected, practically a trade skill.

* * *

Thursday brought the rain Elena's ghost had promised, a fine persistent drizzle that turned the cliff path to grease and made the whole world sound like whispering. Jude spent it indoors, which meant he spent it losing arguments with the silence.

The house had nine rooms and he needed three, and the other six had developed the particular silence of rooms nobody uses — a thick, attentive quiet, as if the furniture were holding its breath. Her chair by the window still had the cushion she'd embroidered, crooked letters spelling *SIT*, because Elena believed instructions should be cheerful. Her boots were by the door where she'd kicked them off, toes outward, forever mid-entrance. He had not moved them. Moving them felt less like tidying and more like amputation.

In the afternoon he did what he did most rainy afternoons: he took the lamp key from its hook and walked down and stood outside the workshop door, and this time — because the drizzle had gotten into his bones and made him reckless — he tried the latch.

It opened. Of course it opened; she'd never locked it; locking things was for people who expected the world to take something from them, and Elena had never once expected that. The door swung inward on hinges he'd oiled every month for two years without ever following them inside, and the smell came out to meet him — linseed and brass polish and dried seaweed and machine oil, the smell of her, so sudden and complete that he had to put his hand flat against the doorframe and stand there like a man who'd been struck by something gentle.

The widow the figurehead watched him from the corner. *Well,* she seemed to say. *About time.*

He went in.

Everything was where she'd left it, down to the pencil lying across her blueprint chest, its point sharpened, waiting. On the bench nearest the window sat the three unfinished machines, draped in cloth he'd folded over them the week after the funeral — the only time he'd entered, just to cover them, as though they were sleeping things that might catch cold. He lifted the first cloth now.

Underneath was a bird. Life-sized, brass and copper, wings folded, its body a beautiful empty puzzle of gears and cams and sockets waiting for parts that lay sorted in labeled trays beside it. Its eyes were tiny garnets. Its beak was open, mid-song, and had been mid-song for two years.

"Hello," Jude said. His voice sounded strange in here, too loud, unpracticed. "You'll have to excuse me. I don't know anything about anything."

The bird said nothing, being unfinished, but the tide moved beneath the floor — he could hear it, the deep slow respiration of the water in the sea-cave the workshop was built over, the cave Elena had chosen because the building literally breathed with the ocean, intake pipes and drive shafts running down into the dark. Even unfinished, even silent, the place was alive. She'd built a heart into the walls.

He found her notebook open on the second bench, pages dense with her slanted handwriting and diagrams, arrows flying everywhere like migrating birds. The last entry was dated the day before she sailed for the mainland. He read it twice.

*The songbird is nearly ready to sing. When it does, everyone will hear it. J. will pretend to grumble and secretly be proud. Note: order garnets — no, ask Mrs. Ferro on Harbour St., she has the good ones. Note: tell J. about the bird. Must tell J. about the bird.*

She had never told him about the bird. There had been no time, or she'd thought there was all the time in the world, which is the mistake everyone makes exactly once.

Jude stood in his wife's workshop with the rain ticking on the roof and the tide breathing under the floor, and he felt something shift in his chest — not healing, nothing so kind, but moving, the way ice moves on a river in late spring, groaning, cracking, beginning reluctantly to go downstream.

"I'll finish it," he told the bird, because talking to unfinished things was apparently contagious. "I haven't the faintest idea how. But I'm told I'm good at showing up daily."

He stayed until the light failed, reading her notes, learning the names of tools he'd dusted around for years without knowing their purposes. When he finally climbed back to the house, soaked and stiff-kneed and absurdly tired, he slept better than he had in two years, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Friday. Supply boat day. He rose early, counted the oil drums (eleven), swept the gallery, and stood at the rail watching the horizon with the specific patience of a man who has nowhere else to be.

The boat came up on the afternoon tide, wallowing a little in the leftover swell, and Jude went down to the landing stage to receive crates and sign papers and exchange the twelve sentences that constituted his entire social week. The boatman was a broad, red-faced man named Tull, who talked the way weather does, continuously and without much meaning to.

"Rough crossing," Tull said. "News is, they're saying peace talks might amount to something this time. Real ones. Might all be over by Christmas, they're saying." He snorted. "They said that in 'fourteen, mind."

"Mm," said Jude, signing.

"Anything need taking back?"

"No."

"Quiet as ever up here, then?"

"Quiet as ever."

Tull squinted up at the tower, then at the workshop below it, its windows catching the last gray light. "Still keeping her mother's junk shop, are you? The missus' machines?" He said it without malice; Tull had delivered supplies here since before Elena died, and had once called her work "witchery" to her face and been so thoroughly charmed by her reply that he'd helped her haul a drive shaft up the cliff for free.

"It keeps me busy," Jude said.

"Aye, well." Tull gathered his papers, hesitated, and glanced back at the workshop again — a glance a half-second too long, a glance with weight in it. "Odd thing," he said. "Could've sworn I saw a light in there Tuesday night. Coming round the point, I mean. Thought maybe you'd taken up her witchery after all."

"You saw the moon on the glass," Jude said. "Happens all the time."

"Suppose it does." Tull shrugged, climbed into his boat, and pushed off, and Jude stood on the landing stage watching him go, aware of two things at once: that he had lied smoothly and without effort, and that Tull had looked at the workshop the way a man looks at a house he's been paid to remember.

The tide was turning. Far below, in the sea-cave, the water began its long inhale, and somewhere overhead a real gull cried once and fell silent.

Jude climbed the cliff path slowly, his pockets full of nothing, his mind full of the bird with the garnet eyes and the words *must tell J. about the bird*, and the odd gray boat that had run dark behind the point, and the way Tull's eyes had lingered. None of it connected yet. It was just weather assembling over the horizon, pressure falling, the barometer in his chest ticking quietly downward.

At the top of the path he paused, out of old habit, to look back at the light — his light, burning already against the dusk, steady and blind and faithful.

Behind him, down in the house, he heard — or thought he heard, over the wind — a knock at the door.

Nobody knocked. Nobody ever knocked.
