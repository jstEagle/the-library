## 2. A Knock Between Storms

Nobody knocked. Nobody ever knocked.

Jude stood at the top of the cliff path with the wind pulling at his coat like a dog that wanted walking, and listened to it again — three raps, spaced politely, almost apologetic, the knock of someone who had never knocked at this door before and hoped never to do it again.

The supply boat came Tuesdays. Tull had already come and gone, and had looked at Jude the way Tull sometimes looked at things he wasn't supposed to be looking at, which was to say like a man reading a letter held upside down. The naval cutter came when it pleased, which was rarely, and its officers did not knock; they hammered, as though the door owed them money. Fishermen did not come up here at all. The cliff path ended at the lighthouse, and beyond the lighthouse there was only grass, gorse, sky, and the long gray conversation between rock and sea.

So who, Jude asked himself, descending the path with his heart doing something unfamiliar and aerobic, was knocking?

*Elena*, said a small voice in him, the voice he kept locked in the workshop with the machines, and he told it firmly to hush, because Elena had been dead two years and dead women did not knock, they only lingered, in brass and blueprints and the particular way he still set out two cups every morning before remembering to set out one.

He rounded the corner of the house and stopped.

On his doorstep, curled against the door itself as if trying to post herself through the letter slot, lay a child.

She could not have been more than eight or nine. Small for her age, probably — Jude's calibration of children was rusty, being based largely on memory and on the supply of children one meets while keeping a lighthouse on the end of the world, which is to say none. She wore a man's oilskin coat rolled at the sleeves and cuffs, far too big, the kind fishermen wore, salt-stiffened, and beneath it a dress that might once have been red and was now the color of weather. Her hair was dark and full of wind. One shoe was missing. She was asleep — actually asleep, which struck Jude as either heroic or deeply suspicious — with her cheek pressed to the boards and her fists tucked under her chin, and she did not wake even when the door, pushed by the gale at his back, swung inward an inch and bumped her gently on the forehead.

The child slept through it. The child, Jude suspected, would sleep through the end of the world, provided the end of the world arrived quietly enough.

Pinned under one small fist was a note.

Jude crouched. This put him at the level of the doorstep, where he spent a surprising amount of his life — scrubbing gull droppings, retrieving dropped wicks — and he thought, absurdly, *well, at least I'm dressed for it.* He worked the note free. The paper was cheap and going soft with damp, the ink bled but legible, written fast by someone whose handwriting had given up on ceremony:

**Forget this face.**

No signature. No explanation. Just those three words, underlined twice, the second underline dragging off the edge of the paper as if the writer's hand had been jerked — by urgency, by grief, by a boat rocking in a swell behind some dark point of land.

Jude read it four times. The wind read it over his shoulder, snatched at it, and he folded it into his pocket where the barometer of his chest had begun ticking in earnest.

"Right," he said aloud, to the child, to the storm, to nobody. "Up you come."

She weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing. He gathered her the way you gather something breakable that has already been broken once — carefully, and with a stupid helpless anger at whoever had done the breaking. Her head lolled against his shoulder. Her breath was slow and even and smelled of salt. Under the oversized coat she was shivering in fine continuous tremors, like a wire humming, and the missing shoe explained itself: her other sock was soaked through, her small foot cold as a stone from a stream.

The gale chose that moment to arrive properly. It came around the headland with the sound of a crowd deciding something, drove rain sideways across the yard, and slammed the door wide open behind them with a bang that should have woken the dead, let alone the sleeping.

The child slept on.

"Well," Jude told her, wrestling the door shut with his shoulder, "you're either very brave or very tired, and my guess is both."

* * *

Inside, the keeper's house was warm in the way Jude kept it: not comfortable, exactly, but defended. A fire banked low. A kettle always within reach of coals. Everything square, everything in its place, everything arranged so that no object ever needed to be touched twice or thought about once. Elena had called it — in the early years, when she still teased him — *the museum of Jude*. Now the joke belonged to him alone, along with everything else of hers, and he carried the child through the front room past the two cups on their shelf and into the kitchen.

He laid her on the settle by the fire, and then stood over her, hands on hips, confronted with a problem entirely outside his competence.

Jude Villalobos could keep a great Fresnel lens turning in a hurricane. He could rebuild a clockwork escapement blind, in the dark, by feel. He could predict the tide to the minute and the weather to the hour and had once, memorably, talked a panicky assistant through landing a boat in a cross-sea using nothing but shouting. But he did not know what one did with a child. Children were a country he had never visited. He and Elena had meant to go there someday — there had been talk, late and hopeful, plans made softly in the dark — and then the war had eaten the years, and then Elena had gone, and the country had closed its borders, and that was that.

The child's hands were filthy. Her face was smudged. There was a scratch along her jaw, healing. Under the grime she was pale in the way of someone who had missed several consecutive meals, and there was a bruise high on one cheekbone, yellowing, days old.

Someone had hit her. Someone had put her in a boat — the gray boat, running dark behind the point, the one he'd watched at dusk and filed away under *odd, deal later*. Someone had rowed her to the base of the cliff in a rising gale, and a child had climbed a cliff path alone, in the dark, in the wind, one shoe lost somewhere on the rocks, and knocked on the door of the last house in the world.

And left a note saying *forget this face.*

"Forget your face," Jude repeated to the kettle, setting it on the hob. "That's a strange thing to ask of a man who remembers where every one of four hundred and twelve screws lives."

But he knew, didn't he. He knew exactly what kind of face got forgotten in wartime: the faces of deserters' daughters, of smuggled men's children, of anyone connected to anything the Navy would rather not have happened. He had spent four years perfecting invisibility himself — answering no questions, writing no letters, being the light and not the man — and he recognized the craft in others. Whoever wrote that note had known precisely whom they were trusting. Not the authorities. Not the church, not the constable, not the mainland. Him. The keeper. The man who saw ships and said nothing.

Which meant somebody out there believed Jude Villalobos had something worth hiding in. The thought sat strangely, like a coat that fit better than he deserved.

The kettle sang. He made tea he didn't want, warmed milk in a pan because he had read somewhere — in one of Elena's books, stacked in the workshop among the gears — that children drank warm milk, and brought both to the settle.

She woke the way animals wake: all at once, silently, upright, eyes open and fixed on him with an expression he had seen on trapped birds. Her whole body had gone taut. Her eyes went from his face to the door to the window to the knife block, cataloguing exits, and finding none that mattered, she simply stared.

"It's all right," Jude said, which was the sort of thing people said, and immediately felt foolish, because it wasn't all right, obviously, or she wouldn't be here with one shoe. He set the mug down slowly on the floor between them and stepped back to the stove, giving her room, the way you'd give a frightened cat room. "Milk. It's not poisoned. If I wanted to poison anyone I'd have done it to Tull years ago and improved the county."

No reaction. Not a flicker. She watched him the way the sea watches a seawall.

"You're on my doorstep," he continued, because silence and Jude had a long acquaintance and he had learned to fill it the way one fills sandbags, "asleep, in a gale, wearing a coat belonging to a man roughly three times your size, with a note instructing me to forget your face. Which, I'll have you know, is a very inefficient instruction, because I haven't decided yet whether I've even seen it."

Nothing. Her eyes dropped to the milk. Steam rose off it. After a long moment — long enough that Jude had time to count the gusts against the window, five, six — she reached out, took the mug in both hands, and drank without stopping until it was gone. Then she held the empty mug out toward him, arms straight, like a soldier surrendering a weapon.

He took it. Their fingers didn't touch. She drew the sleeves of the great coat down over her hands and disappeared inside it, and Jude understood that the coat was armor, and that underneath it she had decided — provisionally, the way you decide to trust ice — that he was not going to hurt her. Provisionally. Pending further review.

"I expect you've got a name," he said.

She looked at him.

"A name," he tried again, patting his own chest. "Jude. That's mine. You'd be...?"

She looked at him with the enormous patience of the young for the old and stupid, and said nothing. Not refused — nothing so dramatic. She simply declined, gracefully and completely, to exist as a topic of conversation.

"All right," Jude said. "Cinderella, then. Until we establish better." He fetched bread, cold ham, the heel of a cheese Tull had sworn was cheddar and was clearly committing fraud as such. She ate the way she had drunk the milk — completely, efficiently, watching him the whole time, as if eating were another exit to be catalogued and this one at least available. When she finished she wiped her mouth on the enormous sleeve and looked at the board to see if there was more, and there was more, because Jude had already cut more, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, which from this child was practically an embrace.

* * *

By full dark the gale had settled into its long work, the steady fury rather than the tantrums, and Jude did what he always did: he went up the tower.

He took the lamp because the child was asleep again, curled on the settle with the oilskin coat as a blanket, and because the light did not care about foundlings. The light had never cared about anything. That was the arrangement — he fed it, trimmed it, turned it, and it burned, and between them they kept ships off the Shallows, and neither of them asked questions of the other. It was, he often thought, the most honest relationship of his life.

Tonight the routine felt different with company below. He wound the clockwork. He polished the lower segment where salt mist had crept in. He checked the mercury bath, the bearings, the vent. And all the while some low, unexamined part of him kept listening downward, through iron stairs and stone, for the sound of a child breathing in his kitchen, and kept being astonished to hear it.

*Hand her over at first light*, he told himself, trimming the wick. *Constable on the mainland, or the parish officer, whoever it is now that everyone competent has been shot at for four years. Note and all. Not your business. Nothing is your business. Your business is the light.*

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the only plan, the sensible plan, the plan of a man who had spent the war being careful the way other men spent the war being brave. Forget this face — well, he'd forget it. Hand it to the proper channels and let the channels do what channels did, which was grind slowly and remember nothing.

Except.

Except that whoever wrote that note had chosen him over the channels. Which meant the channels were part of whatever was being fled. Which meant handing her over might be precisely the wrong thing — the thing the note was written to prevent, disguised as the thing duty demanded. Or it meant nothing of the sort and he was inventing conspiracies because his mind, rusted shut on routine, had been forced open tonight and didn't know where to stop.

And except, quieter, in a part of himself he didn't visit often: the house had sounded different tonight. Smaller sounds. A child breathing in the kitchen. The small industry of her getting comfortable on the settle, the sigh she hadn't quite made. Two cups' worth of presence in a one-cup house.

He hated how much he noticed it. He noticed it the whole watch.

* * *

Around midnight, checking down the stairs between turns of the clockwork, he found her standing in the lamp room doorway.

She must have climbed the stairs alone, silently, which in this tower was a skill that took most adults a month. She stood with one hand on the stone and the rotating beam washed over her — lit, dark, lit, dark — and she watched the great lens turn with her mouth slightly open, and in the sweep of light her eyes caught it and threw it back, brown shot with gold, wide as saucers.

Jude, who had prepared a speech about dangerous stairways and children belonging in bed, discarded it entirely.

"That's the lens," he said instead. "First-order Fresnel. French glass. There are one thousand and twenty-four prisms in it, give or take the cracked one I don't discuss with visitors." The beam swept her again, and he watched her flinch — very slightly, very controlled — each time the light crossed her face, as if she weren't sure yet whether being seen was safe. "It turns night into a sentence. One flash every seven and a half seconds. Ships read it like a name and know where they are."

She pointed at the lamp. Then at her own eye. Then out at the black rolling world beyond the glass.

"Yes," Jude agreed. "That's the whole job, really. You've grasped it faster than the Naval Board did."

She looked at him then — a quick, sharp sideways look, the look of someone checking whether they'd just been laughed *with* — and Jude realized he had made a joke, out loud, voluntarily, for the first time in approximately two years, and that it had landed, and that the child's mouth had done something at one corner that in a less guarded person would have been the beginning of a smile.

Then it was gone, packed away, and she pointed at the lamp again, and at the stairs, and raised her eyebrows in a question.

"The light?" he said. She nodded. He considered lying, decided against it, and patted the rail. "You can help trim it. Since you're up anyway. But if you tell anyone I let a stranger near my lens, I'll deny it to a tribunal."

She came and stood beside him at the rail, small and grave, and together they checked the wick height, and she copied his movements exactly — she was, he noted, a copier, precise, watchful, learning everything the first time — and when the mechanism needed winding she took the crank when he offered it and hauled it around with both hands and set her whole small body into it, and when the clockwork clicked home she stepped back and looked at the work with an expression Jude recognized from his own mirror. Satisfaction. The particular satisfaction of a thing made to run.

Oh, he thought, with a feeling like a stair missing in the dark. Oh no. Elena would have adored you.

* * *

He put her back on the settle eventually, and she went without protest, burrowing into the oilskin coat, and was asleep again inside minutes — the sleep of the genuinely exhausted, which is bottomless and dreamless and slightly alarming to watch. Jude banked the fire and sat in his chair opposite and did not read the book in his lap.

At first light, he told himself again. Constable. Channels. Note and all.

The fire ticked. Outside, the gale leaned on the house and sighed. In the workshop below the floorboards, Elena's unfinished machines stood in their dust sheets like patient furniture, waiting for hands that had stopped coming, and above them in the loft of his ribs some long-stowed cargo shifted against its lashings.

He looked at the note, unfolded it on his knee for perhaps the twentieth time. *Forget this face.* Fast writing. Frightened writing. A man's hand — or a woman's; fear flattened everybody's letters alike. Beneath the fold, faintly, there was the ghost of an earlier draft, something blotted out, and he tilted it to the firelight and could just make out the shadow of two words before the obliteration: *tell J—*

Tell J. Tell Jude. But the writer had struck it through, decided against asking, and sent the child anyway, with nothing but a coat, a key — he'd seen the cord at her neck, a small brass key he had pretended not to see — and an impossible request.

Forget this face.

Jude folded the note away. Across the room, the child slept with her fists under her chin and her one shod foot sticking out of the coat, and the fire moved on her face, and he understood, with the mild detached surprise of a man watching his own lighthouse go dark, that he had already failed the instruction. He had looked. He had looked thoroughly, repeatedly, and by any honest accounting he would remember this face as long as he remembered Elena's — which was to say forever, which was to say the whole point of him.

"At first light," he said out loud, to hear how it sounded.

From the settle, without opening her eyes, without a word, the child reached down and pulled the oilskin coat up over her face — hiding it, deliberately, offering him a head start on forgetting — and Jude sat in his chair by the dying fire and laughed, once, a rusty unpracticed sound that startled them both, and knew that first light was coming whether he wanted it or not.

It came early. It always did.

And with it, hammering across the water from the point, came the engine-note of a naval launch — arriving hours ahead of any schedule Jude had ever known.
