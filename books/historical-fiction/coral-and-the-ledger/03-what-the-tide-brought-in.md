## 3. What the Tide Brought In

The man's hands are too big.

That is the first thing Coral decides about him, watching from the top stair with her chin on her knees. His hands are enormous, knuckly, the color of old rope, and they do everything slowly — the kettle, the cups, the folding of a cloth that he lays over the chair where she is supposed to sit. Big hands usually mean grabbing. But these hands move like they are afraid of breaking something.

Everything in this house moves slowly. The clocks tick wrong, one after the other, none agreeing. The whole place smells of oil and salt and something else underneath — dust that used to be somebody's perfume, maybe, or soap kept in a drawer so long it became memory instead of soap.

She does not come down yet. Coming down means being caught. Being caught means questions, and she has practiced not answering questions until her throat has learned to stay shut like a mussel at low tide.

The man turns around and sees her anyway.

He doesn't startle. He just looks at her for a long moment, the way you look at weather coming in over the water — deciding whether to worry or not.

"There's bread," he says. "And there's tea, but I make it badly, so fair warning."

He goes back to the kettle. That is the strange part. He doesn't come up the stairs after her. He doesn't say *come here* or *what's your name* or *where did you come from*, which are the three things every grown person says first, always, like they're reading off a card.

He sets two cups on the table. One of them he pushes to the far side, to the chair with the folded cloth.

Then he busies himself with his boots.

Coral counts to forty in her head. She learned counting from Papa. Forty is how long you wait when hiding under a boat hull while boots go by on shingle. Boots going by, voices going by, don't breathe, count to forty, then run the other way.

Nothing comes. The house just keeps ticking its disagreeing clocks.

She comes down.

* * *

Boat. Black water. A lantern swinging on a hook and making the whole world swing with it.

*"Hold tight now, little fish."*

Papa's voice. But wrong. Tight. Papa's voice when he laughs is like a door opening into a room full of light. This voice is a door held shut.

*"Whatever happens, whatever you see, you hold my face in your mind. Can you do that? Hold it tight as you hold this key."*

Key. Cold brass against her breastbone even now, even in the warm kitchen, hanging under her dress on its cord. She touches it sometimes without meaning to, the way you touch a bruise.

*"Don't show anyone. Not anyone. It's only for—"*

Then the engine, then shouting that wasn't Papa, then running, running along a wall with wet stone that scraped her palms, and a woman's arms lifting her onto a cart among fish crates, and the smell of fish and fear being the same smell forever—

"Coral."

She blinks. The kitchen. The big hands. The man is holding out bread with butter already melted into it, and he says her name again, careful, like setting down glass.

"The note said Coral," he explains. "It didn't say anything else useful. So if that's wrong, you can tell me."

She takes the bread. Her stomach makes a decision before her head does.

The man nods once and eats his own breakfast standing up, looking out the window at the gray sea, and does not watch her eat, which she knows is deliberate, because everyone watches her eat, everyone needs to see her swallow, needs proof of life like a ledger entry. This man looks at the sea instead and lets her be an animal about the bread, and afterward she is almost embarrassed, and the embarrassment feels strange and new, like finding a room in your house you never noticed.

* * *

Tests. She runs tests.

Test one: she leaves her cup at the very edge of the table where it will surely fall. If he shouts, she'll know what kind of house this is. He picks the cup up silently, moves it inward, and later — she watches sideways — he wedges a little felt pad under the table's short leg so it won't rock anymore.

Test two: she cries in the night. Real crying, the kind she can't stop, muffled in the blanket of the narrow bed in the room he gave her, the room with a quilt made of hundreds of small sailcloth triangles. She cries loud enough that he must hear. He does not open the door. But sometime in the dark she hears slow footsteps pass outside it, back and forth, back and forth, like a lighthouse beam made of a man, sweeping past and past, keeping watch without coming in. In the morning there is a plate of biscuits outside her door shaped like stars.

Nobody has ever let her cry alone before. Mama would have gathered her in. Papa would have made faces until laughing beat crying. Alone-crying is terrible. But somehow the footsteps passing, passing, passing were also a kind of gathering-in, and she sleeps after all.

Test three: the garden. There is a strip of ground behind the station where stubborn green things fight the wind, and she goes out and pulls up every onion and lays them in a row, roots up, like little bald heads, which is a crime, she knows it is a crime, and waits.

The man comes out. Looks at the onions. Looks at her.

His mouth does something complicated. It wants to be angry. Then it wants to laugh. The two fight each other openly, right there on his face, and the laugh wins by cheating — it escapes through his nose first.

"Onions," he says gravely, "have done nothing to you."

And that is all. He replants them with her, showing her how deep, his giant hands making holes in the earth while she drops them in, and neither of them speaks, and it is the closest thing to a conversation she has had since the boat.

She is losing. The tests are supposed to find the trap, and instead she keeps finding biscuits, felt pads, replanted onions. It is disorienting, like walking downstairs in the dark and there being one fewer step than your legs expected.

* * *

A fragment: Papa's workshop smells of metal shavings and linseed oil, and he lets her hold the tiny screwdriver, the one with the red handle, and says, *"Steady. Steady as a surgeon. There — she turns."* And inside the little brass body something catches and releases, catches and releases, and a bird opens its silver throat.

*"Wings are easy," Papa says. "Song is hard. Song is where you keep what mustn't be lost."*

Another fragment: Mama's hand flat on the table, and papers under it, and Papa saying low, *"If it ever comes to it, the bird knows. The bird remembers everything I taught it."* And Mama looking afraid, and Coral not understanding, because birds forget songs, everyone knows birds forget—

Another: the harbor wall. Rain. Papa crouching to her height, rain running off his nose, saying numbers to her fast, *"Fourteen, little fish, fourteen turns, can you remember fourteen?"* and her nodding and nodding, and him kissing her forehead hard, and pushing her toward the woman with the cart, and the last thing, the very last thing she sees of him is his hand rising, not waving, more like saluting, like soldiers do, except Papa was never a soldier, Papa was only ever a maker of small beautiful impossible things—

She wakes herself up gasping and holds the key so hard the teeth print marks in her palm.

Fourteen. Fourteen turns. Of what? Of the key? Of the winding? Somewhere there is a thing that wants turning, and the wanting of it sits in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she cannot give it away, and she cannot carry it alone, and she is seven years old, nearly eight, and the world has gotten much too large for her pockets.

* * *

Days pass. She learns the shape of him.

He rises before light and climbs the tower, and the great lamp turns and turns above her like a patient eye. He eats standing. He talks to himself in half-sentences, apologizing to objects — *sorry, old girl* to a sticky drawer; *you and I both* to a rusted hinge. Once she hears him talking in the closed room at the end of the hall, the room she isn't allowed in yet, the room that smells of oil and something sweeter, machine-smell mixed with flower-smell, and his voice in there is different. Softer. The voice people use at bedsides.

There was someone. Someone who is gone now. She understands gone-ness the way she understands tides: completely, from the inside.

She starts leaving him things. It is only fair. He leaves star biscuits; she leaves a grey feather on his windowsill. He fixes the wobbling stool in the kitchen without being asked; she lines up three white shells on the woodpile in a pattern that means nothing except *I see you*. He never mentions any of it, but she notices that the feather stays on the sill for days, moved slightly each time so it catches the light, and the shells get a roof of slate propped over them against the rain.

Two people trading small salvations across a kitchen, pretending not to notice.

It is almost enough. Almost.

Because the note said *forget this face*, and the man with the big hands has not forgotten her face — she can tell, because sometimes she catches him looking at her with an expression like arithmetic, like he is adding up what her presence is going to cost him — and someday soon he will finish the sum, and the answer will be *authorities at first light*, which is what he told the teapot on the second morning when he thought she was asleep. Authorities. Men in offices with stamps. Men who ask questions and write down answers and send small girls to places with rules and bells and no sea.

So she has to decide. Before he decides. That is the arithmetic she is doing back at him.

And deciding means telling. Or showing. And showing means the key, and the key means Papa's secret, and Papa's secret is the reason boots ran along the harbor wall, and secrets that cause boots to run are heavy beyond seven years, nearly eight.

* * *

On the sixth day it rains straight down like the sky is emptying itself for winter, and he lets her into the tower because there is nowhere else to put her. Up close the lamp is enormous, a hive of glass and brass, and the lens turns and throws its blade of light across the water and reels it back in, over and over, faithful as breathing.

"Careful near the glass," he says. "It's the only vain thing on this coast. Doesn't like fingerprints."

She puts one fingerprint on it immediately.

He looks at the ceiling as if asking it for strength. She puts another one next to the first.

"You," he says, and there it is again, the laugh escaping through the nose, "are going to be trouble of a very specific kind."

Trouble. The word lands in her warm. At home — home, the word itself is a cut — at home, Papa called her his *little fish*, and Mama called her *my heart*, and nobody ever called her trouble, because trouble implies you matter enough to disrupt something. Trouble means your absence would leave a smooth surface, and your presence leaves ripples, and ripples mean you are real.

She stands in the turning light and lets it sweep over her, gold, dark, gold, dark, and makes her decision the way the tide makes its decisions — slowly, and then all at once.

That night, after the star biscuits, she takes the cord from around her neck. The key is warm from her skin. Fourteen turns, Papa said. The bird remembers everything you teach it.

The man is washing dishes, his back to her, sleeves rolled over those enormous rope-colored forearms, humming something tuneless and unbothered.

Coral walks to the table. She sets the key down on the wood between them, directly in the lamplight, where he cannot possibly miss it. Brass teeth. Small. Loud as a gunshot, to her.

He goes still. Water drips off his hands. Very slowly, he dries them, and turns, and looks at the key, and then at her, and she lifts her chin and does not flinch, because Papa's daughter does not flinch, Papa's daughter holds faces in her mind and counts to forty and turns keys fourteen times, whatever they belong to, whoever she has to trust.

"Ah," the man says softly, into the long silence. "So that's what this is."

And then, gently, pulling out the chair opposite hers and sitting down like a man sitting down to the most important meal of his life:

"Why don't you show me what it opens?"
