## 4. The Officer Who Counts Waves

The naval cutter arrived on a Tuesday, which Jude resented on principle. Tuesdays were for polishing the lens housing and winding the clockwork that turned it, and a man who disrupted his Tuesdays was a man who did not understand what kept ships off the rocks.

He saw the boat first as a smudge against the grey water, then as a shape with too much white at its bow, and by the time he had climbed down from the gallery he could make out the officer's coat — dark blue, brass buttons catching what little light the morning offered. Jude stood in the doorway of the station and performed, deliberately, the arithmetic of an unremarkable man: one lighthouse keeper, one kettle not yet boiled, one day interrupted.

*History happens elsewhere*, Elena used to say, laughing at him gently, and he had believed her through four years of war because the war had cooperated. It happened in France, in newspapers that arrived damp and late, in the names read aloud at the church two villages south. It did not happen on his rock. His rock was oil and wicks and the small eternal argument between tide and stone.

The boat bumped the landing. The officer came up the steps without asking leave, which told Jude everything before the man opened his mouth.

"Keeper Villalobos." The officer removed his cap, revealing hair cropped so close to the skull it looked drawn on with pencil. He was young in the way officers were young — thirty perhaps, but carrying himself like a monument to himself. "Lieutenant Harrow. Coastal inspection. Your light's logged irregular."

"Irregular," Jude said. "It's logged every night. That's rather the point of a log."

Harrow smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were pale and busy and moving over Jude's shoulder toward the station behind him. "May I come in?"

"You may wipe your boots."

* * *

Jude made tea because making tea was what he did when he wanted to appear to be nothing more than a man with a kettle. He had learned this trick years ago and had never needed it until now, and he hated — immediately, viscerally — how well it worked. Harrow sat at the kitchen table like a man auditioning for the role of someone comfortable, and Jude poured, and the storm-silled window rattled once in greeting.

"Four years keeping this light alone?" Harrow asked.

"Three years, eight months alone. Before that, two keepers." Jude set down the cup. "My wife kept it with me until she didn't."

"My condolences." Harrow said it the way men said *good weather*, and moved on without pausing for grief to land. "You'll have seen traffic on the water. Small craft. Fishing boats, of course, but other things at night. Unlit things."

"The sea is full of unlit things, Lieutenant. That's why they pay me."

Harrow laughed — a real laugh, surprisingly warm, and that was worse than the smile. A cold man you could brace against. A charming one got inside your Tuesday. "I'm told there was a boat, three weeks ago. Running dark along this stretch. You'd have been the only soul awake to see it."

"Was there?" Jude sipped his tea. He watched his own hands do it — steady, unhurried, the hands of a bored man — and marveled a little, from some quiet balcony inside himself, at how easily they obeyed. "Then whoever it was owes me a fright. Dark running in these waters is how you end up on my list of repairs."

"No names? No faces?"

"Lieutenant." Jude set the cup down and gave Harrow the full benefit of his most useful possession: a face weathered into perfect unreadability, lined by salt and grief into something that looked like honesty because it looked like nothing at all. "At night I see a light or I don't. If I see one, I note the bearing. If I don't, I hope very hard that nobody is out there. Faces don't survive forty fathoms of dark water. Ask any keeper you like; we're all of us nearly blind by trade."

Harrow studied him for a long moment. The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the sea counted its waves, patient as an auditor.

"You're very good at this, Keeper," Harrow said at last, lightly.

"At what?"

"At giving me nothing."

"That's not nothing." Jude nodded toward the logbook on its shelf, and fetched it, and opened it to the proper pages with the crispness of a man with nothing whatsoever to hide. "That's three years and eight months of bearings, weather, lamp hours, and oil consumed. It is the single most boring document on this coast, and I am proud of it."

And here was the trouble, Jude thought later, watching Harrow run a finger down the columns: the lie was easy. Not just the words — the whole posture of it, the boredom, the mild indignation, the tea. Some part of him had simply stood aside and let the machinery work, the way Elena's automata worked, gears doing what gears were cut to do. He had spent four years practicing invisibility, and now the practice paid out smoothly, and he found he despised the payment.

Because a man who could lie this easily was a man who had something to lie about. And Harrow knew it. You could see it in the way the pale eyes came up from the logbook at last, satisfied, and settled on Jude like a fisherman marking a spot where the line had twitched.

* * *

Harrow did not leave immediately. That was the second thing Jude filed away. Inspectors came, poked, left. Harrow drank his tea slowly. He admired the stove. He asked after the supply schedule — *when does your boatman call? Regular fellow? Reliable?* — and about the state of the road, which did not exist, and about whether Jude ever went mainland-side, and whether he'd served, and where, and why not, each question arriving dressed casually but all of them marching in the same direction.

"I was exempted," Jude said. "Lighthouses are exempt. War needs its lights."

"Does it," Harrow said. It was not a question. He rose, replaced his cap, and paused at the door with the air of a man remembering something pleasant. "There's talk of armistice, you know. Any week now, they say. Peace talks going well."

"So the papers say."

"When peace comes, there'll be a great deal of tidying up. Loose ends." Harrow smiled his good smile again, and this time Jude thought he saw, briefly, the machinery behind it — the counting. "A man likes his accounts in order before the books close. You understand. Keepers are accounting men, really."

"We count oil," Jude said. "Good day, Lieutenant."

He watched the cutter pull away from the landing and stand out toward the mainland, and only when it was a smudge again did he realize his hands had gone cold around the doorframe.

* * *

He should have felt relieved. Instead he spent the afternoon doing his rounds twice, once for the work and once for the walking, and the walking told him the truth: the visit had not been an inspection. It had been a measurement. Harrow had come to see what stood on this rock and how much of it was watchable, and he had found a widower with a clean log and a smooth face, and he had marked him anyway. *You're very good at this, Keeper.* Men did not say such things to the innocent. They said them as a courtesy. Between professionals.

Invisibility, Jude understood now, had been a luxury of the war's neglect, not a right. The moment someone decided to look, no amount of practice would save him. He had spent four years becoming furniture, and furniture, it turned out, could be searched.

He thought of the workshop door, downstairs and locked, and of what slept inside it — Elena's half-finished tide-engine, her cabinets of brass and gutta-percha, four years of dust gathering on the shoulders of machines that had never learned their jobs. He thought of the note he had burned three weeks ago, the ash of it still in the grate: *Forget this face.* He had assumed it meant hide the child. Now, standing in the doorway with the wind pushing past him toward the mainland, he understood it had also meant: *someone will come asking.*

They had come asking.

* * *

Coral found him at dusk on the gallery walk, where he had gone to check the wind vane for no reason whatsoever. She had taken to following him on his rounds in the evenings — not helping, exactly, but orbiting, the way small moons orbit large distracted planets. She held out her hand. In it she had placed, with tremendous ceremony, three limpets, which appeared to be either dinner or tribute.

"For me?" Jude said.

She nodded solemnly.

"Thank you. They look like excellent limpets."

She studied his face while he said it, the way she studied everything — with the total attention of a creature deciding whether the world was safe today. Then her eyes narrowed slightly, and her head tilted, and he realized with a jolt that she had noticed what the lieutenant never had: that his voice had gone wrong somewhere, flat in the wrong places, that something under the surface of the day was moving.

Children who had been put in boats at night and sent away from burning things, he suspected, learned to read faces the way keepers read weather. You did not get to be wrong often.

"It's nothing," he said, and then, because lying to Harrow was one thing and lying to this child was another, and the difference surprised him: "Well. It's something. A man came today. An officer. He was asking questions about boats."

Coral went very still. The wind moved her hair; she did not.

"He didn't find anything," Jude said. "And he won't. But we're going to be careful for a while. That means—" he searched for a way to say it that wasn't frightening, and failed, and settled for true, "—that if anyone else comes to this rock, you go to the workshop and lock the door, and you don't come out until I knock three times. Can you remember three knocks?"

She held up three fingers. Her hand was steady. Her eyes were not.

"There's my keeper," Jude said, and was startled by his own word choice — *my keeper* — as though he had assigned her the post permanently, as though the light already needed two.

She went down the stairs ahead of him, limpets clutched to her chest, and paused halfway to look back at him once — a quick, measuring glance over the shoulder, the glance of someone checking an exit. Then she was gone into the stairwell, and Jude stood alone in the rising wind with the first stars coming out over a darkening sea.

Somewhere out there, past the last of the light, a naval lieutenant was counting waves, and writing things down.

Jude turned the great lamp on, watched the beam swing out over the water, and admitted to the empty gallery the thing he had been refusing to admit since the boat pulled away:

He had lied beautifully. And beautiful lies, in wartime, were the first thing experienced men went looking for.

The beam swept the horizon, and swept it again, finding nothing, illuminating nothing, keeping nothing — and below his feet, behind a locked door, a child who had crossed that same black water sat among a dead woman's unfinished machines, waiting for three knocks.
