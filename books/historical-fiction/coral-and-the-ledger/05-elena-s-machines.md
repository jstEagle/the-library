## 5. Elena's Machines

The rain had spent itself by morning, leaving the island scrubbed and glistening like a thing newly born. Jude stood at the kitchen window with his third cup of tea going cold in his hand and watched Coral inspect a tide pool with the seriousness of a general surveying territory. She had rolled her trousers to the knee despite the cold. She was, he had noticed over four days now, entirely unbothered by weather, by silence, by him.

She was also — and this was becoming a problem — bored.

He'd discovered it the evening before, when she had taken to stacking his clean plates into towers of increasing ambition while he mended a net. Not mischief, exactly. Engineering, of a sort. She had looked at him afterward with an expression that said, plainly: *this is what I have to work with? Four days, one lighthouse, and a man who mends nets?*

"The workshop," he said aloud, to no one, because talking to himself was another habit grief had left him, "is not a toy chest."

Coral looked up from her tide pool.

"It's *definitely* not a toy chest," he told her.

She blinked at him once, slowly, and went back to whatever she was interrogating out of the water. But she had heard. He could tell by the set of her shoulders, which had gone from bored to interested in the way a cat's do when it hears the cupboard where the fish is kept.

Jude drank his cold tea and considered his position, which was weak.

* * *

Elena's workshop sat on the seaward side of the station, a low stone building that had once been a smokehouse and had been transformed, over eleven years of marriage, into something no sane person would recognize as a workspace. Benches everywhere. Drawers labeled in her looping hand — *gears, good; gears, bad (do not throw away); springs, mysterious*. Brass filings ground into the floor so thoroughly that on damp days the whole room smelled faintly of pennies. And everywhere, everywhere, the machines.

They were her great obsession and her great joke, both at once. Elena had believed the sea wanted to be useful. Not metaphorically — she had said this at dinner parties on the mainland, to engineers who smiled kindly and to one or two who did not smile at all — she had believed that all that energy arriving twice a day on every shore in the world was an invitation, and that a well-made machine ought to accept it. Tide-driven automata, she called them. The rest of the world, when it bothered to notice at all, called them Elena's toys.

There had been a wind-up bird that dipped its head when the tide turned. A little brass crab that scuttled along a track powered by a waterwheel no bigger than a hat. A clock that never needed winding because the harbor did the work for it, rising and falling in its glass tube, patient as anything.

And there were the unfinished ones. There were always unfinished ones. Elena used to say that a finished machine was a sentence ended, and she hated ending sentences.

Jude hadn't opened the workshop door since March.

He stood in front of it now, key in hand, with Coral beside him vibrating slightly, the way a kettle does just before it sings. He had told himself this was practical. The child needed occupying. A mind like hers, locked up and given nothing, would find its own trouble eventually, and better she should be amazed than desperate. That was the story he was telling himself, anyway. The truer story was that he had been standing outside this door for six months like a man afraid of his own kitchen, and he needed someone to make him go in, and she was small enough and stubborn enough to do it without knowing.

"She's messy in here," he warned. "Fair warning. If you step on a pin, I warned you."

Coral nodded gravely, as though pins were exactly what she expected from life.

He unlocked the door and pushed it open, and morning light came through the salt-fogged windows and fell across the benches, across the drawers with their labels, across the dust that lay over everything like a second, grayer snowfall, and Jude found that his eyes had gone hot and his throat had closed and he had to stand there a moment in the doorway, one hand on the frame, breathing.

Coral did not rush in. He had braced for her to bolt past him — she was a child, children bolted — but she stopped at the threshold too and looked around the room with an expression he couldn't name until he realized he was wearing it himself. It was the look of people entering a church built by someone they loved.

Then she saw the crab.

It sat on its track near the window, brass legs folded, half a decade old and the first thing Elena had ever made that worked properly. Coral crossed the room in three strides and crouched before it, and Jude opened his mouth to say *it doesn't run anymore, the mechanism needs* — and then shut his mouth, because Coral had reached out and, without any hesitation whatsoever, lifted the little hinged gate on the water reservoir and tipped in the cupful of seawater she was apparently carrying. For some reason. Because she was Coral.

The wheel turned. Dripped, caught, dripped again. The crab unfolded one leg. Then two. Then it took a shuffling, ridiculous step along its track, and another, and Coral made a sound — not a word, never a word, but a sound like a spark struck off flint — and clapped both hands over her own mouth as if she'd sworn in chapel.

"You kept it oiled," Jude said stupidly, to the crab. He had. He didn't remember deciding to. Every month, all through the war, he had come in and oiled everything and left without looking at any of it properly, the way you visit a grave.

The crab walked its little circuit and folded itself closed at the end, satisfied, and Coral turned to Jude with her whole face open and alight, and he understood suddenly what Elena had known about rooms like this one: they were not full of machines. They were full of arguments for hope, each one small enough to hold.

"Right," he said, roughly, because if he didn't give his voice a job it would betray him. "Well. Since you've already got employment lined up." He fetched the oil can from its hook and put it in her hands. "Rule one: oil before you admire. Rule two—" he pointed at the drawer labeled *springs, mysterious* "—that one stays shut. Even I don't know what's in it. I've made my peace with that."

Coral saluted. Where she had learned to salute he did not know, and decided not to ask, because the answer might be a war.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and it was the strangest morning of Jude's life, which was saying something, considering the last four years.

She had a gift. That was the plain fact of it, apparent within the hour and getting more obvious by the minute. She couldn't yet read Elena's labels, but she could read the mechanisms themselves — turning things over, frowning, finding the gear that had slipped its tooth, holding pieces up to Jude with the flat, expectant stare of a surgeon requesting instruments. When he explained something she watched his hands instead of his mouth, which was, he slowly realized, the correct way to learn it, and the way he himself had learned from Elena, and the recognition of that landed in his chest with a weight he had to keep setting down and picking back up.

By noon they had resurrected the dipping bird, two clocks, and a small brass fish whose purpose neither of them could determine and whose existence neither of them was prepared to question.

"There's more," Jude said finally, wiping his hands. His back ached in the good way, the way it did after honest work. "Down here."

The far wall held the long bench, and beneath the long bench, the cabinet. It was Elena's own design — she had built it herself, refusing help, swearing magnificently at the hinges — a tall case of dark wood with brass fittings and a glazed front door, and inside it, under a muslin cloth, something large. Larger than anything else she'd attempted. She had covered it the winter she died and never uncovered it again.

Jude knelt in front of the lock. It was a good lock. Of course it was a good lock; she had picked it out the way other women picked suitors.

"Elena," he said conversationally, fitting the key, "if this thing bites her, I'm disowning you."

The door swung open on a breath of cedar and machine oil, and the smell hit him like a hand laid flat against his sternum, because it was *her* smell, it was the smell of their whole house in the early years, and for a moment Jude stayed kneeling on the dusty floor of his own workshop with his eyes closed and let it have him.

When he opened his eyes, Coral was already peering past him, and inside the case, beneath the cloth, something gleamed.

He drew the muslin off.

It was a figure — human-shaped, or nearly, the way a good automaton is nearly: a woman formed of brass and copper, seated, perhaps a foot and a half tall, her lap occupied by an open book. Her joints were visible, honest, beautiful, each one a small engineering essay. Her face was smooth and unfinished, features only suggested, and in her breast was a cavity, empty, waiting for something. Around her wrists ran bands of gearing connected by drive chains to a reservoir system far more sophisticated than anything in the rest of the room — twin feeds, tide and counterweight, meant to work in opposition, the rise and fall of the sea translated into motion so fine that —

"So she could turn the pages," Jude said. His voice came out strange. "That was the idea. She told me about it once. A machine that reads. She said if she could build a machine that read, then reading would be proven to be a kind of living thing. She laughed at herself for saying it. Then she started building it anyway."

He looked at the seated figure, at the blank face, at the empty cavity in the breast where some final mechanism should have gone, and felt the shape of the absence so precisely that he had to look away.

"That's where she stopped," he said quietly. "Right there. She knew what was missing. She wrote to a supplier in Rotterdam about it. The letter's still on the desk upstairs. She never sent it."

*Papa.*

The word stood in the middle of the workshop like a struck bell.

Jude turned his head very slowly. Coral was not looking at the automaton woman anymore. She was looking at the underside of the bench lid, which he had thrown open twenty minutes ago to get at the spare chain, and where — he had forgotten, how had he forgotten — Elena had pinned her working drawings. Blueprints, sketches, notes in her looping hand, layers of them, some yellowed and curling. And tucked among them, unmistakably not in Elena's hand, a single sheet of heavier paper, drawn in a different style altogether — precise, confident, annotated in Spanish in a small, quick script.

Coral's finger was on it. Her finger had gone to it the way water goes downhill. Her lips were pressed white.

*Papa,* she had said. One word. Her first. Given not to Jude, not to the crab, not to the light — given to a drawing pinned under a bench in a dead woman's workshop on an island at the end of the world.

Jude reached out and eased the sheet free with fingers that were not quite steady, and Coral let him take it but did not step back, stayed close enough that he could feel her breath, quick and shallow, a small animal's breath.

The drawing was of a mechanism. Not the reader-woman — something smaller, denser, a nested assembly of cams and cipher wheels arranged around a central spindle, with marginal notes that jumped between Spanish and English and a private shorthand that was neither. At the top corner, in that same quick hand, a title:

*Máquina de lectura — para el cuaderno — para cuando E. termine la suya.*

*Reading machine — for the ledger — for when E. finishes hers.*

Jude read it three times. The workshop sounds fell away — the drip of the tide-clock, the tick of the resurrected birds, the sea beyond the walls — all of it receding, leaving only the paper in his hands and the child beside him and the words *for the ledger*, which meant nothing, and everything, and would not stop meaning.

"Coral," he said carefully. "Did your father know Elena?"

Coral looked up at him. In the gray window light her eyes were the exact color of deep water over sand, and in them was something he had not seen before: not fear, not boredom, not the careful watchfulness she wore like a coat. Recognition. The terrible relief of a person who has carried a message alone through enemy country and has finally, finally seen the roof of the house it was addressed to.

She took the drawing gently from his hands. She turned it over.

On the reverse, in the same quick script, smaller, as if written quickly and hoping not to be noticed, was a line Jude would carry for the rest of his life — though he did not yet know what it would cost him, or save.

*If I am lost, she will finish it. Tell Villalobos he owes me.*
