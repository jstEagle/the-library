## 6. The Ledger in the Bird

The morning it happened began, as most mornings did, with kippers and a negotiation.

Jude had learned that feeding Coral was less a matter of setting food before her than of arranging it so that she felt she had won something. She would not eat fish if it was simply given to her. But if he set his own plate down, sighed heavily, and pushed one kipper to the edge as though surrendering it under duress, she would take it with the grave satisfaction of a general accepting territory. He had begun cooking extra kippers purely to lose them.

"You're robbing me blind," he told her. "There are laws against this, I'll have you know."

Coral chewed and said nothing, which was how she answered nearly everything, and looked out the window at the gray sea doing its gray business against the rocks.

It had been eleven days since the storm delivered her to his doorstep like an errant parcel. Eleven days of her silent presence threading itself through his routines until he could no longer tell where the routine ended and she began. She had opinions about how the lamp's brass fittings should be polished — in circles, never back and forth, a fact she communicated by seizing the cloth from him and demonstrating with a scowl. She had opinions about Elena's chairs, about which window caught the best light for reading, about the correct way to hold a teacup, which was apparently any way other than Jude's way.

She was, he realized with some alarm, becoming company. And he had promised himself he would be rid of her by now.

He had walked her toward the village twice, intending to hand her to the constable and be done with the whole impossible business. Both times something had stopped him — the first time it was the weather, the second time it was nothing he could name, only the memory of her hand in his, small and cold and gripping hard when the gulls screamed overhead. The constable, he told himself, was a formality. A thing to be arranged properly. After the next supply boat. After he'd found her warmer boots.

He was running out of afters, and he knew it.

* * *

That afternoon he took her into the workshop again, because rain had come in sideways off the water and there was nowhere else worth being.

He still hesitated at that door. It had been three years, and his hand still paused on the latch the way it pauses on a wound you're not sure has healed. Elena's workshop occupied the seaward room of the station, a long low space with windows fogged permanently by salt, and it was the closest thing to a third person their marriage had ever produced. She had built machines in here the way other women kept gardens. Tide-driven automata: a crab that scuttled along a track when the float-chamber filled; a lighthouse keeper made of tin who wound the clockwork with a crank every six hours, mimicking Jude so faithfully that visitors had asked, uneasily, which of them was real; a family of gulls that flapped and dipped on hidden cams whenever the tide turned, as if the sea itself were keeping birds as pets.

Elena had called them her argument with God. *You make tides,* she'd say, tapping the workshop bench with a screwdriver, *and I'll make them useful.*

Since her death, Jude had gone in perhaps once a month, to oil what needed oiling and to not-look at what needed finishing. The machines ran on without her, patient and pointless, winding themselves down and being wound up again, a whole little economy of brass laboring for no audience.

But Coral had an audience in her. From her first day in the workshop she had watched the tin keeper wind its crank with an intensity Jude hadn't seen in anything living for years. She learned the machines' schedules. She stood at exactly the right minute for the tide-turn, hands folded, waiting for the gulls to perform, and when they did she did not laugh — Coral almost never laughed — but her whole face opened like a window thrown up in a shut house.

Today, though, she wasn't watching the gulls. Today she went straight past them, past the crab, past the tin keeper frozen mid-crank between cycles, and stopped in front of the long cabinet against the east wall.

Jude's stomach performed a small, cold maneuver. That cabinet held Elena's unfinished things — the pieces he couldn't bring himself to touch, wrapped in linen like bodies prepared for burial. He had not opened it since the funeral. He had not let anyone open it. The key lived on his ring, and his ring lived on his belt, and the arrangement was understood by both of them without ever having been discussed.

"Coral." His voice came out sterner than he intended. "Not that one."

She didn't turn around. Her fingers hovered over the cabinet door, not touching it, trembling very slightly, the way a hand trembles over a stove it knows is hot.

"Not that one," he said again, softer.

Then she spoke.

"Papa."

One word. Small, cracked, unpracticed — a hinge that hadn't turned in months shrieking into motion. It didn't sound like much. It sounded, in fact, like almost nothing, two syllables dropped into a dusty room.

Jude felt it go through him like a nail through plank.

* * *

Afterward he could never reconstruct those next minutes in proper order. They came back to him later as a series of bright, disconnected frames, the way shipwrecks come ashore: not the sinking, only the scattered cargo.

He saw himself kneeling beside her. He saw her turning to him at last, her eyes enormous, wet, furious with the effort of speech. He saw her mouth work twice more around words that wouldn't come, and then give up on words entirely and begin instead to gesture — and her gestures were fluent, urgent, a whole language pouring out of her now that the dam had cracked. She pointed at the cabinet. She pointed at herself, at her chest, then made of her two hands the shape of something small. She cupped her palms together like a bowl. She brought them near her lips and blew across her thumbs.

A bird, he thought. Something bird-shaped, small enough to hold.

"Is something of your father's in there?" he asked stupidly, as if the cabinet were a person who might answer for itself. "In my wife's cabinet?"

She nodded so hard her hair flew.

His key ring came off his belt. He was distantly aware of his own hands shaking as he worked the lock — shaking worse than they had shaken at her funeral, worse than the night of the storm, and some detached corner of his mind observed this with clinical interest while the rest of him stood at a door he had nailed shut three years ago and heard knocking from the inside.

The cabinet exhaled camphor and machine oil and, beneath both, something faintly sweet that was simply *Elena*, the smell of her sleeves, and Jude had to close his eyes for one breath, two, before he could look.

Linen-wrapped shapes. The half-built automaton whale that had been meant to breach from a water tank on high spring tides. The music box with no song chosen yet, its cylinder blank. And there, on the second shelf, resting on a bed of cloth as though placed carefully by someone who expected to return—

A songbird.

Brass, life-sized, exquisite even under its dulling film of dust. Wings folded, head cocked at the impertinent angle of a real songbird appraising you. Jude lifted it out and the weight surprised him — too heavy for its size, its belly dense with clockwork.

"It's hers," he said aloud, mostly to keep himself anchored. "Elena made this. Or — started it. She never finished the singing part, I remember she—" His voice snagged. He set the bird on the workbench and Coral's hands rose toward it, hovering, asking permission. He nodded.

She touched it the way you touch a sleeping animal. Then she turned the bird gently over, ran her small fingers along its breast with terrible certainty, and pressed — a feather, a specific feather, the third from the left below the wing.

There was a click, delicate as a knuckle cracking, and the breastplate of the bird swung open on a hinge invisible to the eye.

Inside the hollow body, where the song mechanism should have been, there was instead a tightly rolled sheaf of papers, bound in oilcloth and tied with waxed thread, and beneath the papers, fitted into the mechanism's mounts as precisely as if the bird had been built around them, a small cipher drum etched with columns of letters and numbers that made no sense at all.

Neither of them moved for a long moment. Rain ticked against the salted glass. Somewhere below, the sea kept its own accounts.

"That's not Elena's," Jude said quietly. He knew it the way he knew the station's every creak — Elena wrapped her bundles in blue linen, always blue, and tied them with string, never thread, and she would sooner have died than hide paper inside a finished bird without telling him. This had been added. Someone had opened the cabinet after Elena closed it forever. Someone who knew the workshop well enough to find the right machine and the right feather and the right trick of the spring.

Someone who had been here — or known someone who had been here — after the funeral.

He looked at Coral. Coral looked at the papers. And in her face he saw not a child's curiosity but a child's dread, the particular dread of someone who already knows what the papers say and wishes with her whole body that they said something else.

* * *

He untied the waxed thread with a care that surprised him, as though the papers might startle. There were pages of them, densely written in a cramped, disciplined hand — not Elena's, not anyone's he recognized — and every line was gibberish. Numbers interleaved with letters, grouped in fours, annotated with dates. The dates were recent. The earliest was fourteen months old; the latest was nine days before Coral arrived on his doorstep.

Beside each block of cipher, in the margin, small plain words had been written like labels on drawers: *coastlight. ferryman. harbormaster. naval.*

Naval.

Jude sat down slowly on Elena's stool, which complained under him the way it had complained under Elena, and read the margins again. Whatever the cipher hid, the shape of it was visible now, the way the shape of a ship is visible in fog before the ship itself. Money moving. Names protected. Dates coordinated. And the word *naval* recurring like a heartbeat.

Deserters, he thought, and the thought arrived whole and cold, as if it had been waiting outside for some time. Smuggled deserters. Boats leaving at dark from quiet stretches of coast, men vanishing ahead of military police, and money flowing upward to whoever made the darkness possible. It had been a rumor during the war, one of hundreds. He had heard such rumors the way he heard weather reports from distant seas — true somewhere, certainly, but elsewhere. History happened elsewhere. That had been his religion for four years, and he had been devout.

He turned to the last page, and the world narrowed to a single marginal note.

The final entry, dated nine days before Coral appeared, carried a label written more firmly than the rest, underlined once, as though the writer had wanted to be sure his own hand remembered it:

*naval — Lt. H. — the officer who counts waves — paid thrice.*

Harrow.

Lieutenant Harrow of the Royal Navy, who had stood in Jude's kitchen ten days ago with his careful boots and his careful questions, asking about boats and faces, smiling with all his teeth. *You see nothing out here, I expect. Nothing anyone would pay for.* Jude had lied to him smoothly and hated how easily it came, and had gone to bed congratulating himself on his invisibility.

Paid thrice. The officer who counts waves had been counting money.

Jude became aware that Coral was watching him — had perhaps been watching him for several minutes — with an expression far too old for her face. She climbed onto the workbench stool beside him, laid one small hand flat on the papers, and looked up at him with a question she could not yet put into words.

"He hid this here," Jude said. "Your papa. In Mrs. Villalobos's bird." He swallowed. "Do you know why he chose this place? Why he chose—"

He stopped, because he had almost said *me*, and the sentence frightened him. Why her father had chosen a widowed keeper on an indifferent stretch of coast, a man with no stake in any war and no appetite for any history — that question had teeth on both ends. And behind it swam another, larger and colder: if Mateo — surely that was the name, she had shaped it with her mouth often enough these eleven days, *Mateo, Mateo* — if Mateo had trusted this place, this man, with the record of crimes committed by an officer of the Navy, then Mateo had believed something about Jude Villalobos that Jude no longer believed about himself.

That he was somebody. That he counted. That his door meant something besides *go away*.

Coral tugged his sleeve. When he looked down she pointed at the cipher drum, then at the tin keeper frozen at its crank, then swept her arm across the whole workshop — the gulls, the crab, the whale-in-progress, all of Elena's tide-driven congregation — and finally pressed her fist over her heart and looked at him with fierce, wet eyes.

*Fix it,* the gesture said, as plainly as speech. *Make it work. Make it sing.*

Jude looked at the cipher drum nested in the bird's body like a heart awaiting transplant, and at the pages of numbers that named names, and at the girl beside him who had crossed a war to bring them to his door, and he understood with sudden terrible clarity that his invisibility was over. Had perhaps been over for eleven days. History had not happened elsewhere. It had washed up on his step in the body of a child, and it was asking him to finish a machine.

"The decoding part," he said slowly, thinking aloud, thinking fast. "Elena designed a reader once — a letter-wheel driven off the tide-governor. The plans are..." His eye fell on the cabinet, on the blueprints folded in the drawer beneath where the bird had slept. Unfinished. Of course unfinished. Everything of hers was unfinished, including, he suspected more each day, himself.

He would have to finish it. All of it.

"All right," he said to Coral, and his voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "All right, then."

Outside, far down the grey water, a shape was coming around the headland. A naval launch, flying the White Ensign, making steady progress against the ebb — hours early, days early, for no inspection schedule Jude knew of.

At its bow rail stood a slim figure in a peaked cap, counting waves.

Jude closed the bird's breastplate with a click, and slipped the ledger into his coat.
