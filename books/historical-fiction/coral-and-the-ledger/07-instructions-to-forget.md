## 7. Instructions to Forget

The letters were not where a sensible man would have put them.

Jude found them three days after he found the ledger, on a morning so clear the sea looked apologetic for its recent behavior. He had spent those three days in a state he could not properly name — not fear exactly, and not grief, but some cousin of both who had arrived uninvited and kept putting his boots on the furniture. The ledger sat wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the flour tin, because it was the only place in the station Coral never went. She had declared war on flour at some point in her short tenure here, having discovered that a fistful of it thrown into the wind made her look, briefly, like a small furious ghost.

The bird — the songbird automaton whose brass belly had opened to reveal the whole rotten architecture of Lieutenant Harrow's career — now lived on the mantel, between Elena's photograph and a chipped mug of pencils. It watched the room with its ruby eyes and said nothing, which was more than could be said for Jude's conscience.

It was Coral who found the letters, though she didn't know what she'd found.

She had been in the workshop all morning, sitting cross-legged on the floor where Elena used to sit, taking apart a failed gull-wing mechanism with a screwdriver she held the way other children held candy — protectively, and with an implication that anyone trying to take it would lose a finger. Jude came in with tea and stopped in the doorway.

Coral had pulled the workbench drawer out too far. It had come free of its runners entirely, tipped forward, and disgorged onto the floor a false bottom that was not behaving like a bottom at all. Beneath it, in a cavity that must have been measured to the millimeter, lay a bundle of envelopes tied with tarred twine.

She looked up at him. Her eyebrows did most of her talking. *Well?*

"I didn't know that was there," Jude said, which was true, and felt like a confession anyway. Four years in this house, four years treading softly through every room his wife had loved, and he had never once thought to pull a drawer apart to see what it was hiding. He had assumed grief was thorough. It turned out grief only tidied; it didn't inventory.

He set down the tea and knelt and cut the twine with his thumbnail, slowly, the way you open something you already know will change things.

Seven letters. Each envelope addressed in a small slanted hand — not Elena's hand, not anyone's hand he knew — to *J. Villalobos, Keeper, Gannet Point.* Dated across two years. Every one of them unopened.

Every one of them beginning, when he finally slid the first page free, with the same words:

*If you are reading this, then my daughter has reached your door, and I am beyond asking.*

Jude read the first letter standing up. He read the second one sitting down. By the third, he had sent Coral outside to count waves — a task he invented with such gravity that she actually saluted — and he read the rest alone in the workshop with the door shut and the tide-engine ticking beneath the floor like a patient heart.

* * *

*Gannet Point, March 1916*

*Villalobos,*

*If you are reading this, then my daughter has reached your door, and I am beyond asking.*

*You don't know me yet. That is a strange thing to write to a man I feel I know well, having watched him from the water for two years now, but there it is. I run boats at night along this coast. You keep the light that tells me where the rocks are. Between us we have an arrangement neither of us agreed to.*

*I want you to understand why I'm writing before you decide what to do with these pages. Burn them if you must. But read them first. A man should know what he's burning.*

*There is a trade running through this coast that the newspapers will never print, because the men arranging it wear uniforms and sign their names in ledgers instead of on shipping manifests. Deserters come south in fishing smacks. Some of them are cowards, God knows, and some of them are boys of sixteen who saw a thing at the front that broke something in them no court-martial can mend. They pay to leave. Someone very highly placed takes a cut of every fare. I carry them because I cannot carry anything else and still sleep.*

*I am telling you this in a letter rather than at your door because letters wait. Letters let a man be angry first and reasonable after. I have found anger makes poor doors to knock on.*

*Your wife buys lamp oil from my cousin in Port Sable. She speaks of you the way sailors speak of weather they trust. She says you believe history happens elsewhere. She says it kindly.*

*I am writing to tell you that history is coming to Gannet Point, friend, and it is coming by sea.*

— M.

* * *

Jude sat with that one for a long while. *She speaks of you the way sailors speak of weather they trust.* Elena had gone to Port Sable twice a year for supplies, always alone, always coming back with her pockets full of oddments and her head full of talk — a cousin of the shopkeeper, she'd say, a clever fellow, half smuggler, all charm, you'd like him, Jude, you'd hate how much you liked him.

Mateo. She had said his name to him across their own kitchen table, probably a dozen times, and he had filed it under *other people's lives* and thought no more about it.

He had been so good at thinking no more about it. It was practically a profession.

* * *

*Port Sable, August 1916*

*Villalobos,*

*Your wife is the finest engineer I have ever met and I include in that judgment a man in Bilbao who once built a clock that could predict fog, which sounds like a joke but saved eleven crews including mine.*

*I told her everything. Not because I drink — I barely do — but because she asked me a question no one else had thought to ask, which was not "what are you doing" but "how does the money move." Everyone else wants to know about boats and boys. Elena wanted to know about arithmetic. She said, and I quote because I wrote it down on my hand: "A crime this size leaves a paper trail wider than the boats do."*

*She is building you a machine, friend, though she won't say what for yet. She has taken over your back workshop entirely. She showed me a little brass bird that opens its own chest. I laughed and she looked at me with such seriousness that I stopped laughing, and then she smiled, and I understood that the smile was the real answer and the seriousness was just the lock on it.*

*I have begun keeping records of my own. Names. Dates. Amounts. Where the money goes after it leaves my hand. She says if it is ever written down properly, in code, in a thing too small and too foolish-looking for any searching officer to bother with, then someday it can be proved. Someday being a word I cling to.*

*I should tell you the rest, since you are clearly reading these in order and deserve fair warning. In September of 1914 I pulled a drowning man off the Gannet shoals. Tall fellow, half dead, tangled in his own oilskins. He had fallen from a supply boat in weather no sane man launches in. I ask nothing for rescues. It is the one trade where charging a fee seems vulgar.*

*But I remembered his face. And when I learned he was the keeper of the light that had guided me home through that same storm — well. The sea keeps accounts, Villalobos. So do I.*

— M.

* * *

The workshop went very quiet around him. Even the tide-engine seemed to hold its breath.

September 1914. Jude knew the date the way you know a scar's shape without looking — by touch, in the dark, always. The night the supply boat *Meridian* shipped water in the outer race and he went into the black water with no rope and no witness and no particular expectation except cold. He had come to on a deck that smelled of fish and tar, coughing up half the Atlantic, and a man above him said, in Spanish-flavored English, *You're lucky, friend. Most things the sea takes back, it keeps.*

Jude had thanked him. Had pressed coins on him — had insisted, god help him, *insisted* — and Mateo had taken exactly one coin, bit it theatrically, pronounced it genuine, and handed it back. *For the light,* he'd said. *Keep it lit. That's payment enough.*

And then the man had vanished into the war's grey margins, and Jude had filed the whole night under *things survived* and closed the drawer, and had not once — not once in four years — understood that the drawer locked from the other side.

A debt. Mateo had named it himself, lightly, like a joke between friends. But debts of salt were older than jokes, older than courts, older than the navy and all its ledgers. You did not get to decide whether you owed. You only got to decide when the bill came whether you would pay it like a man or squirm.

And the bill had come. It had come with rain in its hair and a brass key on a cord around its neck and no voice at all.

Jude got up, walked to the window, and looked out at Coral on the shingle below, counting waves with tremendous diligence and absolutely no interest in the total. She had arranged pebbles to mark each ninth wave, because she was, he was discovering, a person of systems. She had her mother's jaw. He didn't know that for certain — he'd never met the woman — but he'd have bet the station on it.

*I sent her to you,* the letters said, in effect, *because you owed me silence. I am sorry for it. I am sorrier than I can fit in ink.*

He went back to the bundle.

* * *

*Bilbao road, February 1917*

*Villalobos,*

*A short one, because I write this in a barn with a pencil I stole from a farmer who will be compensated.*

*They are close to me now. The officer I will not name in ink — though if you ever meet a naval man who counts waves when he's nervous, that's the one — has moved from taking bribes to wanting the whole route. He wants to be the trade, not a toll on it. Men who start by selling mercy end by owning it.*

*Elena and I have finished the arrangements. She is better at the hiding than I am; I would have chosen a strongbox and been robbed inside a month. She chose the bird. She says the best hiding places look like sentiment. Nobody searches a toy.*

*Here is what I need you to know, and I need you to know it plainly, because everything else can be misread:*

*I do not expect to survive the winter. I say this without drama. I have carried too many secrets through too many checkpoints, and my luck is a thin coin getting thinner. If I fall, the ledger falls with me unless someone carries it out of reach. I have written instructions and hidden them where only my daughter could find them, because she is the only person alive I trust completely, and she is seven, which is a terrible quality in a courier and the only one that matters.*

*I am sending her to you. Not to the magistrate, not to the church, not to my cousins inPort Sable who are good men and terrible liars. To you.*

*Because you owe me. Because you keep a light nobody watches and a face nobody remembers, which tonight is worth more than gold. Because your wife built the cage the truth sleeps in and you at least will not let it starve.*

*And because — forgive me, keeper, this is the selfish part — because I have watched you from boats for three years, standing at your rail every dusk, keeping your little fire against all that dark, and I thought: there is a man who has decided to need no one. That is a fine policy for lighthouses and a cruel one for men. My Coral cracks things open. Doors. Oysters. Hearts. Give her a chance to work on you.*

*Forget my face if you must. I have a common sort of face; the sea has already half claimed it. But do not forget hers. That instruction is not a request. It is the whole of what I ask, and it is the opposite of what the note in her pocket says, and I hope to God you are clever enough to see which one is the lie.*

*Keep the light lit.*

— M.

* * *

Jude read that last line four times.

*Do not forget my face.* The note pinned to Coral's coat the night of the gale, in handwriting he now recognized as not Mateo's at all but Elena's — Elena's small slanted script, Elena's practical mercy, written years ago in anticipation of a night like that one. *Forget this face*, the note said, because a forgotten child was a safe child, invisible like him, passed under the eyes of searching officers without catching on anything.

And the letters said the opposite. *Do not forget hers.*

His wife and her smuggler, leaving him contradictory instructions across the width of two years and the length of a grave. One telling him to make the girl invisible to save her. The other telling him that invisibility was the cruelest thing he owned.

He laughed — one syllable, appalled — and the sound bounced around the workshop and came back embarrassed.

* * *

*Undated — found folded separately*

*Last one, and I'll be quick. The boat comes at midnight and the moon is against me.*

*Two things.*

*First: the key she wears is brass and it opens nothing you'd guess. Elena will have told you by now, or the machine will tell you itself when it's ready, but hear it from me: the bird sings the ledger. Not holds it — sings it. Every name, every bribe, wound into the tune the way Elena wound tides into gears. She called it "a testimony that performs." I called it mad. She said all testimony performs, the only question is whether it's honest. I miss arguing with her. Whoever reads this: so will you.*

*Second. If you're angry — and you must be, you have every right, a man dumps a child and a conspiracy on your doorstep and calls it honor — then be angry at me specifically and let it stop there. The girl is only guilty of being loved by the wrong smuggler. The officer is guilty of the actual crimes. And you, Villalobos — you are guilty only of believing, for four quiet years, that the war could not reach your rock.*

*It reached mine. Now it's yours. I'm sorry. I'd apologize longer but the tide is turning and tides, unlike magistrates, keep their appointments.*

*Tell her, when she's old enough, that her father's last voyage was his best one. Tell her the cargo mattered.*

*Don't tell her I was frightened. She'll know anyway. She always knew.*

— M.

* * *

Jude folded the letters along their old creases, tied the twine, and put the bundle inside his coat, against his chest, where it weighed approximately nothing and approximately everything.

Then he went out onto the gallery head and stood looking at a sea that had known about all of this — the boats, the boys, the bribes, the whole wet machinery of mercy and graft — for years, rolling it under, keeping it, saying nothing, the way the sea says nothing about everything. He had believed himself a spectator. A keeper of a light at the edge of events, untouched, uninvolved, history happening comfortably elsewhere.

He had been *correspondence*. He had been a character in a story being mailed to him chapter by chapter, and he had left every letter unopened on the hall table of his own attention.

Worse: he had been *infrastructure*. His light had guided the smuggler home. His wife had built the vault. His debt had paid the freight. He hadn't been standing outside the story at all; he'd been load-bearing.

Below, Coral had abandoned wave-counting and was building something in the pebbles — he could see it was a boat, eventually, the way children's cairns resolve into intention. She worked with her tongue between her teeth and her father's jaw set firm against a world that had taken everything from her except the ability to arrange stones.

*Forget this face,* one note said.

*Do not forget hers,* said another.

He realized, standing there with the wind sorting through his collar, that the contradiction had a solution, and the solution had been sleeping in his house for weeks, drinking his tea, dismantling his drawers, and teaching herself his dead wife's tools.

The letters were evidence. The ledger was evidence. But evidence needed a voice, and the voice was down in the workshop in pieces — Elena's unfinished decoder, blueprints half-complete, gears waiting on a casting that had never been poured. Mateo had said it himself: *the machine will tell you itself when it's ready.*

It wasn't ready. Which meant the deadline wasn't the armistice, or Harrow, or the weather.

It was him. It was whatever he chose to finish.

Jude came down the stair and out to the shingle, and Coral looked up at him, eyebrows raised in their usual silent question — *well?*

"Coral," he said, and then found he had to stop and begin again, because his voice had gone rough as a winter channel. "I knew your papa."

She went very still. A whole body's worth of stillness, wave to keel.

"He wrote me letters," Jude said. "I haven't answered them until now. I'm going to start."

She considered this for a long moment with the gravity of a harbormaster reviewing papers. Then she picked up a flat white pebble, pressed it into his palm, and closed his fingers over it — a transaction concluded, a receipt issued, terms accepted without a word spoken.

That night, long after she was asleep, Jude cleared the workshop bench to bare wood, laid out Elena's blueprints, and lit every lamp he owned.

He was halfway through tracing the third gear-train when he noticed the notation in the margin — Elena's hand, small and slanted, arrowed to the main escapement wheel. Not an engineering note. A sentence:

*If Harrow's name appears, go to Wren, not the naval court. Trust the civilian. — E.*

Jude stared at it. Harrow's name. As though Elena had known — years in advance — *whose* name it would be.

He looked at the bird on the mantel through the open door, singing silently to the dark, and understood that his wife had left him one more letter, and this one was addressed to exactly the trouble she had predicted.

He picked up his pencil and began to work.
