## 9. Harrow Returns

The supply boat had come and gone two days early, which was the first wrong thing. The second was that it had not come to bring anything.

Jude noticed the absence before he understood it — the station's rhythm was built on deliveries, and a boat that took without landing crates left a hole in the morning the way a missing tooth leaves a hole in a smile. He stood at the gallery rail with his coffee going cold and watched the cutter sit offshore, riding low and patient, like a dog that knows better than to bark twice.

Coral saw it too. She had taken to reading the water the way other children read primers, and she touched Jude's sleeve and pointed, then drew a small circle in the air — *a boat* — then pressed her flat hand down against her other flat hand — *staying.*

"I see it," Jude said. "Probably they've mixed up their days. Sailors are famous for it. Half the wrecks on this coast are just men who couldn't read a calendar."

It was a joke, and Coral gave him the small huff of breath through her nose that served her for laughter, but Jude did not feel funny. He felt the particular cold in his stomach that had nothing to do with the wind off the sea, the cold that said: *he is coming back, and this time he is coming aboard.*

He had four hours' warning, as it turned out. Four hours and one whaleboat pulling for the landing stage with six men in naval coats and Lieutenant Harrow standing in the bow like a figurehead that had opinions.

* * *

"There is no need for all this," Jude said, when Harrow came up the rock stair with his men strung out behind him, boots ringing on the wet stone. "The light is lit. The logs are current. I have nothing you could want."

"Contraband," Harrow said pleasantly. He had removed his cap, which somehow made things worse; it gave his face room to be friendly in. "There's a general order, Villalobos. Coastal stations are to be searched. Deserters move along lights like moths along a shore. You understand. It isn't personal."

"Nothing ever is," said Jude. "That's what makes it so tiring."

Harrow laughed — a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and Jude thought, with a lurch, *he could have been liked, in another life.* Then the laugh folded itself away and the officer was back, crisp as starched linen.

"We'll begin with the dwelling," Harrow said. "Then the outbuildings. My men will be thorough. You're welcome to follow along and object to everything; most keepers do. It passes the time."

Jude followed along and objected to nearly everything, and it did not pass the time so much as grind it finer. He watched them go through Elena's kitchen drawers — through *his* kitchen drawers now, though the word still stuck — turning over her spoons, holding her good scissors up to the light as though deserters might be folded small enough to fit between the blades. One of them shook out the flour bin and stood for a moment dusted white to the elbows, looking like something the tide had failed to finish.

And Coral — Coral was nowhere, which was exactly where Jude had told her to be.

* * *

He had heard the whaleboat's oars before any of them could have seen it, an old keeper's trick, sound arriving ahead of sight across open water. There had been time to say only: "Lamp room. Behind the lens carriage. Whatever happens below — whatever you hear — you stay there until I come for you, or until the sun goes down, whichever comes first, and if neither comes, you stay anyway."

She had looked at him with those grave dark eyes and then she had done something she had never done before: she had put her arms around his middle, briefly, hard, like a hatch slammed shut against weather. Then she was gone up the stair, silent as smoke.

Now, downstairs, a naval rating held up Elena's darning egg as if it might be coded correspondence, and Jude said, with a steadiness he did not entirely feel, "That's for mending socks. If you find the war in there, do let me know."

"Mind your tone, keeper," said the youngest of the ratings, who looked about seventeen and seasick even on dry land.

"He doesn't have one," said Harrow mildly. "That's what I like about him." He set the darning egg down himself, carefully, and Jude revised his estimate of the man upward by a fraction and hated doing it. Monsters were easier when they were simple. "The workshop next."

* * *

The workshop door stood at the end of its short covered passage, and between the dwelling and the door lay twenty feet of open rock, and across those twenty feet Jude's heart conducted a private argument with God.

He had meant to deal with the cabinet lock. He had *meant* to. Ever since the night he'd found the ledger in the songbird — ever since Coral had said *Papa*, one word, dropped into the world like a match into straw — he had known that a scratched lock was a confession. But the days were short and the work was heavy and the decoder ate every spare hour, and so he had oiled the hinge and straightened the hasp and told himself the scratches were old, were faint, were the kind of thing no one would notice unless he was looking.

Harrow was the kind of man who was always looking.

"Locked?" Harrow asked, arriving at the door with his hand already on the latch.

"It's kept locked," Jude said. "There are tools. Machinery. A child could lose fingers." He did not say: *and there is a machine inside that will name you in front of a magistrate.* He let his face do what faces on this coast learned to do, which is nothing at all.

"Your key, then."

Jude produced it from the ring at his belt, unhurried, and Harrow took it and unlocked the door and stood aside to let his men in, and for one long moment the two of them stood alone in the passage while wind came off the sea and pulled at their coats.

"You've had visitors," Harrow said.

"Fishermen sometimes shelter in the lee. I feed them. They leave."

"Mm." Harrow stepped past him into the workshop, and Jude watched the man's eyes make their circuit — the benches, the half-dressed automata under their canvas shrouds, the tide-shafts coming up through the floor like the ribs of some buried animal — and then stop, and settle, and stay.

On the cabinet.

The cabinet that held the songbird. The cabinet whose brass plate lock wore, if a man leaned close enough, three bright scratches in a fan, new metal shining through the patina like bone through skin.

Harrow leaned close enough.

"This lock's been forced," he said. Not accusing. Conversational. As if remarking on the weather.

"It sticks," Jude said. "Brass swells in salt air. Any keeper on this coast will tell you the same."

"Any keeper on this coast would have filed it smooth, then." Harrow straightened. He did not touch the scratches. That was the worst of it — he simply let them exist, let them sit in the air between the two of them like an unexploded shell. "You know, Villalobos, I've searched forty stations since spring. Do you know what I've learned? Men hide things badly. They hide them in the places that matter to them. Wives' graves. Bibles. Boats. Workshops." He smiled at the room in general, at Elena's machines sleeping under their sheets. "This is a fine place. Your wife's work, I understand. She was known, before. There are people in town who remember her."

"She would be pleased," Jude said, "to know her reputation survives her. She'd be less pleased it survived as a search warrant."

Something flickered behind Harrow's eyes — irritation, or maybe respect, or maybe both wearing each other's coats. He turned away from the cabinet at last, and Jude breathed again, and then Harrow said, lightly, terribly:

"Where's the child?"

* * *

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"What child?" said Jude, and heard his own voice do the unforgivable thing, the thing he had trained it out of years ago for the benefit of grieving relatives and inspectors: it paused. A quarter-second. A hairline crack in a poured wall.

"The child seen at Miller's Point," Harrow said, examining a wrench as though the answer might be engraved on it. "Three weeks ago. Put ashore from a boat by night. The coxswain of the patrol remembers a lantern. The point is four miles north." He set the wrench down. "You keep the only light within walking distance, and children walk toward lights, Villalobos. It's in their nature. Moths and children and drowning men."

"I keep a light," Jude said. "Not a lost-and-found."

"No. Of course not." Harrow moved along the bench, trailing one gloved finger through the dust, or what passed for dust in a workshop that was cleaned nightly by a man with two kinds of grief and a broom. "Though it's strange. Your stores ledger shows flour drawn at double rate since August. Tea as well. And honey." He turned, and the lamplight from the high window caught his face and made it briefly very young. "You don't strike me as a man with a sweet tooth."

"My wife kept bees," Jude said. "I draw her rations still. It's a habit."

"That," said Harrow, "is either the truest or the falsest thing anyone has said to me this year." He buckled the strap of his gloves with a small snap. "I'll tell you what, keeper. I'm going to leave my men here to finish the inventory — every crate, every locker, every machine. Top to bottom. It may take some hours. In the meantime, you and I are going up to the lamp room to inspect the apparatus. General order. And we'll talk about bees."

* * *

The stair to the lamp room was a hundred and thirty-two steps, and Jude counted every one of them twice, once upward and once downward in his mind, trying to think of anything — *anything* — that would put a naval officer and a hiding child in the same room and end well.

There wasn't one. There was Coral behind the lens carriage, in the pocket of shadow the rotating array made as it swept, a pocket that opened and closed, opened and closed, like a slow eye blinking. He had shown it to her himself, weeks ago, because she loved the machinery and because the great lenses turning in their bath of light made even his own ruined heart sit up and pay attention. He had told her: *when the carriage swings, count. You'll always know how long you have.*

A hundred and thirty-two steps was not long enough.

"The focal distance on these is extraordinary," Harrow was saying behind him, breathing easily, damn him. "French work?"

"Scottish."

"They say a light like this can be seen nineteen miles."

"On a clear night. Twenty-one."

"And tonight?"

Tonight was gray, low cloud, the beam going out into nothing and coming back from nothing, and Jude said so, and Harrow said, "Pity," in a voice that made it clear he was not discussing the weather.

They came up into the lamp room and the light took them — that sudden rotary dazzle, the whole round world strobing gold and shadow — and Jude walked in first, heart slamming, mind running the geometry. Coral was behind the carriage. The carriage was eight feet of bronze and glass, turning. To reach the far side unseen a person would have to move with the sweep, timing each gap, and Coral knew how, Coral was good at it, Coral had spent rainy afternoons practicing exactly this while Jude pretended not to watch —

But Harrow was walking around the lamp, counterclockwise, casual as a tourist, and the sweep was coming, and in the gap Jude saw the shadow of a small foot withdraw, quick as a minnow, and Harrow saw —

Harrow stopped.

Harrow tilted his head.

And then, above them both, gull-struck and groaning, the clockwork rotation train chose that exact moment to skip a tooth. The whole carriage lurched — a sound like a giant clearing its throat — and the beam stuttered, hung, and resumed, and in that stuttering second of darkness Jude did the only thing there was to do.

He knocked over the oil locker.

It went down with a magnificent clangor, a full-throated industrial catastrophe, cans rolling in every direction, one splitting its seam and spreading a slow silver tongue of kerosene across the deck plates, and Jude swore with genuine feeling and dropped to his knees among the wreckage, and shouted, "Get back — the plates are seamed, if that reaches the lamp ring—" and there was shouting, and Harrow was swearing and hauling him upright by the collar, and for ninety full seconds the lamp room was nothing but noise and emergency and a keeper being incompetent at the top of his lungs.

When it was over — spill dammed with a rag, cans restacked, Jude's hands scraped raw and his pulse hammering somewhere near his ears — Harrow stood looking at him across the oil-slicked deck, and the beam swept over his face, once, twice, three times.

"Well," Harrow said. "You're either the clumsiest man in His Majesty's service or the best liar I have ever met."

"I'm not in His Majesty's service."

"No," said Harrow. "More's your luck, or you'd be shot." He settled his cap. Below them, faintly, came the sound of boots on rock — his men descending, the search ending, the whaleboat waiting. He paused at the head of the stair, and the friendliness drained out of his face the way oil drains along a seam, leaving something older and colder underneath, and he said:

"Double rations, Villalobos. Honey. Tea. A scratched lock. A stutter in the light that no competent keeper would allow." He smiled, and it reached nowhere near his eyes. "I'll be back within the week. And next time I won't come with a general order. I'll come with a writ, and men who aren't seasick, and I'll find what you're keeping, because you've started lying badly, and bad liars are the easiest arithmetic in the world."

He went down the stair. The door boomed. The whaleboat pulled away across the gray water, and Jude stood in the rotating light with his bleeding hands and listened to the carriage turn.

Behind the lens, very quietly, Coral began to cry.

* * *

He held her a long time among the turning glass, her face pressed into his coat, oil and salt and tears making a mess of them both, and he did not say *it will be all right*, because he had promised himself he would never lie to her, not even kindly.

Instead he said, "We have a week."

She lifted her head.

"A week," Jude said, and over her shoulder the beam swept out across the darkening sea, steady now, steady as he could make it, "to finish your father's machine — and teach a certain lieutenant some very hard arithmetic."

Down in the workshop, under its canvas shrouds, the tide-engine waited.
