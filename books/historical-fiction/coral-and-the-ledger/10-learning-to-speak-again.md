## 10. Learning to Speak Again

The storm passed in the night the way storms do on that coast — not leaving so much as losing interest — and morning came up rinsed and pale and smelling of wet kelp. Jude found Coral already at the workshop door when he came down from dousing the lamp, her small hands pressed flat against the wood as though she could warm it from the outside.

"You'll want the key for that," he said.

She looked back at him with the particular expression she had perfected over their weeks together: patient, faintly pitying, as if he were a very large and well-meaning dog who had failed to fetch something obvious. She held up her hand. Dangling from her fingers was a file no longer than a thumb.

"Where did you—" He stopped. He had locked that workshop every night since Elena died, out of habit more than need, because grief will make rituals of anything. The lock was scratched, he now saw, scratched thoroughly and with intent, and there was a fine dust of brass filings on the threshold like pollen.

Coral slipped past him before he could decide whether to be alarmed, crossed the workshop floor on her toes, and went straight to the bench where Elena's smallest tools lived. She lifted a jeweler's screwdriver, turned it over twice to check its weight against some private standard, and set it down again. Then the calipers. Then a spool of wire, which she unspooled a hand's width and rewound tighter than it had been.

She was, Jude realized slowly, taking inventory.

"She'd have liked you," he said, before he thought better of it. His voice did something strange on Elena's name these days — a catch, like a key finding a worn lock. He hadn't spoken it aloud inside this room since spring.

Coral didn't look up, but she went still for a moment, listening the way she listened to everything, as though words might arrive from any direction and must not be missed. Then she picked up the screwdriver again and sat herself down on Elena's stool — Elena's stool, which Jude had not moved in two years except to sweep under it — and began, with great concentration, to take the face plate off the clockwork gull.

"That's not how—" Jude began, and then shut his mouth, because actually it was exactly how. She had found the hidden catch on the second try. It had taken him an hour his first time, and he'd broken a wing feather doing it.

He made himself useful instead of hovering. There was always oiling to do, gears to wipe down, the tide-chains at the workshop's seaward wall to inspect where they ran down into the water and turned the great slow shafts when the sea breathed in and out. Elena's machines drank the tide itself; that had been her madness and her genius both. *Why wind up a spring,* she used to say, hair full of salt, *when the ocean winds itself for free?* The smaller automata ran off stored power, but the big ones — the half-built things shrouded along the north wall — waited for the moon's pull like patients waiting for a doctor who never comes.

Jude checked the chains and came back to find the gull's breastplate off and its heart exposed, and Coral frowning at a gear that had seized years ago. She looked up at him and pointed at the gear, then at the oil pot, then raised one eyebrow in a way that was pure challenge.

"All right," he said. "But I'm supervising."

She let him drip the oil while she worked it in with a pin, and inside ten minutes the gull's wings gave a shudder, a stutter, and then — with a whirring sound like a small miracle clearing its throat — beat once, twice, three times against the air.

Coral laughed.

It was a rough sound, rusty, barely a laugh at all — more the shape of one, the memory of one being remembered — but it filled the whole workshop and then some, and Jude stood there holding the oil pot like a man who has been handed something breakable and is terrified he'll drop it.

*When did I last hear laughter in this room?* He couldn't find the date. It receded backward through the years like a boat into fog.

The gull flapped its three beats and stopped. Coral wound it, tried again, got five beats, and turned to him with such fierce triumph on her face that he had to look away and pretend to check the oil level, which did not need checking, because his eyes had gone unreliable.

* * *

That evening he taught her stars, mostly by accident.

It happened because supper was done, dishes washed, and the girl was restless in the way he'd learned meant trouble or discovery, depending entirely on whether he intercepted her first. Outside, the sky had scrubbed itself clean after the storm, and the first stars were out over the water like nails holding the dark up.

"Come on, then," he said, and took the lantern, and led her up the spiral stair because there was nowhere else restless children belonged at dusk but high places.

The gallery walk around the lamp room was narrow and railed, and Coral went to the rail immediately and leaned far enough out over the drop that Jude put a hand on her collar without apology. Below them the sea worked at the rocks, patient as a clerk counting money.

He pointed. "Vega. That bright one, straight up this time of year. And that orange fellow near the horizon — Betelgeuse. Say it."

She shaped the word silently, lips moving.

"Bettle-joos," he pronounced for her. "It's Arabic. Means the giant's armpit."

Coral made a face of profound disgust, which he chose to interpret as the highest form of delight, and pointed at another star with urgent demand.

"That's Polaris, the north star. That one matters most here. Everything turns around it, and it doesn't move. Sailors steer by it. Keepers set the light by it." He found himself standing closer behind her, guiding her arm, drawing lines from star to star with her pointing finger. "There's the Dipper. Follow the two stars at the pot's edge upward and they walk you straight to Polaris. Every time. Lost men have trusted it for a thousand years and it hasn't failed one yet who asked properly."

She traced the line. Found the star. Looked back at him to confirm, and something in her face when she found it — the little jolt of *I know where I am* — struck Jude square in the chest, because he recognized it. It was the same look he'd had his first winter alone at the light, when knowing exactly where every star sat had been the only thing standing between him and the howling.

They stayed up there an hour. He named what he knew and invented modestly where he didn't, and Coral absorbed names the way sand absorbs water — instantly, completely, leaving no evidence on the surface. When he finally called an end to it she stood at the rail one moment longer, looking up, and he saw her lips moving still, rehearsing. Keeping the inventory.

*Betelgeuse. Vega. Polaris.* A girl who had been given nothing portable except a brass key and a face she'd been told to forget, collecting the sky.

*Forget this face,* the note had said. He thought of it more often than he let himself admit, sitting with his tea going cold. Whoever wrote it — Mateo, it had to have been Mateo, the ledger said as much in its coded corners — whoever he was, he had asked an impossible thing. Forget the face of a child who laughs at clockwork birds and points at stars and takes apart locks with a nail file before breakfast.

*You may as well ask the light to forget the ships it saves.*

Downstairs, he tucked her into the cot in the old storeroom, the one Elena had lined with cedar for wool that nobody had needed since rationing began. Coral pulled the blanket to her chin and watched him with those grave dark eyes, and just as he reached the door, her voice came into the dark room, small and clear and complete:

"Goodnight, Jude."

Not a word. A sentence. Subject, verb, the whole architecture of speech, aimed at him like a lantern beam.

He gripped the doorframe. "Goodnight, Coral," he managed, and closed the door gently, and then stood in the hallway for a long minute with his heart going like a loose flywheel.

* * *

The days after that were doors opening one after another.

She did not speak freely — not yet, not the way other children rattled on — but words arrived now in twos and threes, deployed with sniper precision. *"More bread." "Gull's broken." "Wind's coming."* She had a sailor's instinct for weather and a keeper's for machinery, and Jude discovered that narrating his work aloud — which he had done alone for two years to fill the silence and had hated himself for — suddenly had a purpose and an audience. He explained the lens rotation, the mercury bath, the wick trimmers, why the fog signal ran on schedule rather than on sense. She listened like a court stenographer.

And she worked. Every afternoon she disappeared into the workshop and emerged at supper with grease to her elbows and some small machine resurrected. Elena's sketches had been strewn across the drafting table since chapter one of their acquaintance, and Coral studied them with her brow furrowed, tracing Elena's quick confident lines — a crab that walked the tide-line collecting samples, a lamp that turned to follow the sun, the great tide-engine half-shrouded on the north wall.

One gray afternoon he found her standing before the tide-engine itself, the canvas thrown back, her hand resting flat against the cold iron flank of it the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. The thing was Elena's masterwork-in-progress, taller than Jude, all exposed gearing and promise, built to drink the tide through the seaward chains and turn that motion into — Elena had never quite finished deciding what. Motion into message, perhaps. Motion into music. Her notes spiraled around the drawings in handwriting that got wilder as the war went on.

"It doesn't run," Jude said from the doorway. "It never has. She ran out of time before she ran out of ideas."

Coral looked at him. Then at the engine. Then she held up her hands and mimed — slowly, deliberately, unmistakably — turning a great wheel.

"Not tonight." He said it automatically, the way he said *not yet* to everything Elena had left unfinished, and Coral's expression shifted to that patient pity again, the one that saw right through him.

That night he lay awake listening to the sea work the chains below the workshop, the long rhythmic groan of the tide hauling at iron, and thought about wheels, and turning, and how long a man can stand in front of a door pretending he doesn't have the key.

* * *

It happened on a Thursday, though he wouldn't remember that later; he'd remember the light.

Late October light, low and gold and slanting through the workshop's salt-hazed windows, laying bars of amber across the benches. Coral was cross-legged on the floor amid the guts of the clockwork gull, which she had disassembled entirely — *entirely*, every gear laid out on a cloth in rows sorted by size, a display of methodical violence that would have made Elena weep with pride — and Jude was at the drafting table trying to make sense of a page of Elena's notes on gear ratios.

"The third ratio's wrong," he said, mostly to himself. "Or she rounded wrong, or I'm reading her shorthand wrong, which is likelier. She wrote numbers the way other people swear. Here, look—"

Coral came to stand beside him. He walked her through it, pencil tapping the page, and to his astonishment she spotted the error before he did — a transposed digit, plain as day once seen — and jabbed the spot with her finger, then looked up at him with her whole face open.

"You're better at her numbers than I am," he said. "That's not a compliment I give lightly."

She smiled. Then the smile faltered into something else, something more careful, and she looked down at the page, and her finger moved to the corner where Elena had signed her sketches — *E.V., 1916* — in her looping hand.

"Elena," Coral said quietly, tasting the name.

"Yes," Jude said. His throat was tight. "That's who owned all this. Who owned that stool you've stolen."

Coral nodded slowly. Her hand went to the cord at her neck, to the brass key that lived there beneath her shirt, and she held it through the fabric the way she did when she was thinking about her father, and Jude pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice was a kindness too.

Then she turned and faced him fully, and the gold light was on her face, and he saw that she had been building toward something for days the way she built toward everything — silently, methodically, parts laid out in rows.

"Are you going to forget my face?"

Six words. Seven syllables of air. The question hung in the workshop among the hanging tools and the dust motes and the smell of oil and salt, and Jude felt it land somewhere below his ribs and detonate quietly.

Because he knew where it came from. He knew the note was folded somewhere in his desk drawer, the ink gone brown, the instruction impossible. *Forget this face.* Someone had told this child she was forgettable. Someone had made forgetting her the price of safety, and she had carried that instruction in her chest for months alongside the brass key, and she had waited — God, the patience of her — until she was sure enough of the answer to risk asking.

"No," Jude said.

It came out rough. He crouched down to her level, which put his knees in complaint, and took off his cap because it seemed like a moment that called for a bare head.

"I am a very bad forgetter," he said. "Ask anyone. I can tell you the exact number of steps from the lamp room to the kitchen, the name of every ship that's passed since spring, and what your father's handwriting looks like, though I've only seen four words of it. I remember everything. It's the curse of lighthouse keepers — we're hired to watch and remember, and we're no good at stopping when we're off duty." He swallowed. "So no, Coral. I'm not going to forget your face. Not tomorrow, not in fifty years. If the good Lord sees fit to take my mind piece by piece, yours is a window I intend to leave till last."

Coral's chin trembled once. She mastered it, the way she mastered everything, through sheer administrative force. Then she stepped forward and put her arms around his neck, briefly, hard, her cheek against his stubble, and just as quickly stepped back and busied herself with the gull's gears as if nothing whatsoever had occurred.

Jude stayed crouched a moment longer, one hand braced on the bench, breathing.

Then he rose, put his cap back on, and cleared his throat in the manner of men who have recently experienced weather indoors. "Right. Well. Since you're better at her numbers than me, you can help me check the rest of these ratios, because if we're ever going to wake that great iron whale on the north wall, we'll want her arithmetic correct. And after that—" he glanced toward the window, where the light was failing gold into gray, "—after that I'll teach you Cassiopeia. It looks like a crooked W. Even you can't forget a crooked W."

Coral grinned and fetched the cloth of gull-parts, and they worked side by side as the dark came on, the lamps lit, the tide turning its endless pages below the floor.

It was later — much later, when she was abed and the station slept — that Jude stood at the workshop window with the last of Elena's tea gone cold in his hand, and saw, far down the black water, a light that did not belong.

A naval light. Moving slow along the channel, pausing, sweeping the shore.

Harrow's patrol boat, or one like it. Counting waves again. Counting boats and faces.

Jude set down the cup and stood in the dark, watching it pass, and understood with calm certainty that whatever peace he had begun to build in this room, the world outside had not finished with either of them.
