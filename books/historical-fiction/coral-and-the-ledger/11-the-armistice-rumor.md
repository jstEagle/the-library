## 11. The Armistice Rumor

The supply boat *Puffin* came on a Thursday, which was wrong, because the *Puffin* always came on Tuesdays. Jude Villalobos noticed this the way he noticed everything about boats now — with a small cold instrument in his chest that took measurements without being asked.

He was on the gallery deck polishing the lantern glass when he saw her rounding the headland, riding low in the water, her stack trailing smoke that the wind tore sideways. Coral was below, in the workshop, arguing silently with a set of Elena's fine jeweler's screwdrivers. She had developed a whole language of furrowed brows and pointed fingers and one particularly expressive sniff, and Jude had become fluent in it without quite meaning to.

He debated not telling her the boat had come. Then he decided that a man who kept secrets from a child for her own good was a man one lie away from becoming Lieutenant Harrow, and went down the stairs two at a time, which his knees registered as a formal complaint.

"Coral." She looked up from the workbench, screwdriver suspended mid-air like a conductor's baton. "Boat day. Stay inside until I whistle."

She nodded, then held up the screwdriver and pointed at the half-dismantled escapement of the songbird automaton with an expression of profound accusation.

"Yes, all right, the spring is stubborn," Jude said. "Springs generally are. They're made of disappointment and steel." He pulled his oilskin off its peg. "Don't force it. If your father taught you anything about springs, let it be patience."

The words were out before he could catch them — *your father*, tossed off as casually as if they spoke of Mateo Ferrer every day, as if he weren't a name written in a coded ledger hidden inside a brass bird, as if he weren't somewhere beyond the sea or under it. Coral's face did something complicated and then settled into the careful blankness she wore like a coat. Jude put a hand briefly on her head, which she permitted, and went out into the wind.

* * *

The boatman was named Tam Broadhurst, and he had the particular shifty warmth of a man who has decided to be extremely friendly in order to avoid being asked anything at all.

"Jude! Good man. Terrible weather we've been having. Or lovely. Whichever." He was hauling crates up the slipway with more energy than the task required.

"You're a day late, Tam."

"Wind," said Tam, with the air of a man blaming a large dog for eating the dinner. "Winds. Everywhere. Look — I've brought your flour, your lamp oil, and —" he lowered his voice and produced a newspaper from inside his coat like contraband, which, given the mood of the coast these days, it practically was — "the news. The real news, not the gazette rubbish."

Jude took it. The paper was three days old and soft from Tam's coat, and across the front page, in type usually reserved for disasters and royal births: **PEACE FEELERS EXTENDED — TALKS MAY CONCLUDE WITHIN WEEKS.**

He read it twice. The words didn't change. The small cold instrument in his chest began to take new measurements.

"Weeks," he said.

"So they say. Could be talk of talk of talk — you know how it goes. But my cousin's wife's brother works at the naval yard, and he says the officers are already drinking to it." Tam grinned, then seemed to register that Jude was not grinning back, and adjusted his face accordingly. "Bad news, is it? Most folk'd be glad."

"It's news," Jude said neutrally. "Thank you, Tam. Is there post?"

"None. Nobody writes to lighthouse keepers." Tam loaded him down with crates, patted him twice on the shoulder — once too many times — and got himself back into the boat with the haste of a man who had delivered what he came to deliver and wished to be elsewhere when anyone thought better of it.

Jude stood on the slipway a long moment after the *Puffin* had shrunk to a smudge against the grey horizon, holding the newspaper against his chest so the wind couldn't take it.

Then he whistled.

* * *

Coral read faster than he expected. She had never been taught, or so she claimed by shrugging when he'd asked, but she sat cross-legged on the workshop floor with the newspaper spread out like a tide chart, her finger moving down the columns, her lips pressed thin.

When she reached the headline she looked up at him, and her eyes asked the question so plainly that he answered it aloud, because she deserved to hear it said by someone.

"If the war ends, the wartime courts close," he said. He sat down on the floor beside her, knees cracking, and smoothed the paper flat between them. "Everything those courts have power to punish — smuggling, bribery, treason, the whole rotten ledger of it — it becomes old news. Amnesty follows peace the way gulls follow a fishing boat. Nobody prosecutes a crime from a war that everyone is desperate to forget."

Coral's finger found the name in the article. Not a name, precisely — a title. *Naval tribunal.* She tapped it, then drew a circle in the air: the courts, closing like a hand.

"That's right. And Harrow knows it too. Better than we do. He'll have been drinking to it since Tuesday." Jude stared at the headline until the letters blurred. "While the guns are firing, the ledger matters. The moment they stop, it's just paper. Paper about dead men and paid men, and no court alive with the authority to care."

Coral picked up a pencil from the floor. In the margin of the newspaper, in small precise letters, she wrote:

**HOW MANY DAYS?**

Jude opened his mouth to say nobody knew, and stopped. Because that wasn't quite true. A man could do arithmetic on almost anything if he refused to look away from it.

"The talks started in earnest last month," he said slowly. "Tam's cousin's wife's brother aside, papers travel here late but they travel. If the feelers are real — and Harrow's behavior suggests they are — then we have weeks. Call it thirty days. Call it forty if the generals get sentimental." He looked at the workshop around them: Elena's benches, Elena's tools, the great unfinished skeleton of the decoding machine rising in the corner like the ribcage of some patient whale, the songbird lying open-breasted on the workbench with its coded heart exposed.

Forty days. The machine was perhaps half built. Perhaps less than half; Jude had been honest enough with himself, in the small hours, to know that "half" was a number men invented to keep working.

Coral wrote again, pressing hard:

**THEN WE FINISH IT IN THIRTY.**

"There's no 'we' about the difficulty of it," Jude said. "Some of these mechanisms defeated your father, and he built them."

She fixed him with a look. It was the look of a nine-year-old who had spent a month dismantling and reassembling every machine in the room while he slept, and it said, with devastating clarity: *I am not my father. My father did not have you.*

"Well," said Jude. "That's true."

* * *

They worked. That was the whole of the next several days, and it would have been the whole of the account if the account were a list, but lives are not lists, and so: they worked, and in the working other things happened that neither of them named.

Jude discovered that Coral had a gift for tolerances — the tiny, forgiving adjustments that separate a mechanism that runs from one that merely intends to run. She would hold a gear up to the lamplight, turn it slowly, and then shake or nod her head with the gravity of a magistrate. He learned to trust the shake. Three times he re-cut a tooth she had shaken her head at, and three times the assembly afterward ran smoother for it.

"Elena would have liked you," he said one night, without planning to. "She used to say the sea sends tools to those who listen. I thought she meant driftwood and salvage." He turned a screw a quarter-turn. "She may have meant you."

Coral went pink to the ears and became extremely interested in a washer.

The rhythm of the days changed shape. Where before there had been the light, and the chores, and the workshop in the gaps, now there was the machine, and everything else arranged itself around the machine like iron filings around a magnet. Jude kept the light burning — that duty admitted no rearrangement; ships did not care about ledgers — but he trimmed his sleep to a nub, and Coral refused to sleep much either, and they compromised the way families do: he let her stay up until ten, and she pretended to go to bed at ten, and he pretended not to hear the small feet on the stairs twenty minutes later.

On the fifth evening after the newspaper, she fell asleep at the workbench anyway, cheek on her folded arms, one hand still curled loosely around a pair of pliers. Jude eased the pliers away, hung his coat over her shoulders, and worked alone for a while in the lamplight, listening to her breathe and to the sea throw itself against the tower, and thought about a man he had never thanked properly, because thanking a man properly requires being able to find him.

*Thirty days,* he thought, and the number had teeth now. *Maybe less.*

* * *

It was Coral, the next morning, who raised the flaw in the plan — not in words, since words still came to her sparingly, like shy birds landing on a wire, but in the directness of her gestures. She dragged the chair to the workshop door, pointed at the lighthouse, pointed at the machine, and then crossed her arms and looked at him with terrible patience, waiting for him to do the arithmetic she had already done.

"I can't be in two places," Jude admitted. "Yes. I know."

She pointed at herself, then at the light.

"No," Jude said, with a firmness that surprised them both.

Coral rolled her eyes — a gesture she had certainly learned from him, which was humbling — and then knelt on the floor and began, with a stick of charcoal from the bench, to draw. She drew the lamp room. She drew the rotation gears, the lens carriage, the clockwork drive that turned the great lenses through their patient circles. And then, with quick sure strokes, she drew a small additional mechanism, a governor of some kind, wired into the drive train, and beside it she wrote:

**IT DOES NOT NEED YOU EVERY MINUTE. IT NEEDS SOMEBODY.**

Jue crouched down beside the drawing. He studied it for a long time. The design was sound. It was more than sound; it was elegant, in the way of things understood rather than memorized.

"When did you read the drive manual?" he asked.

She shrugged, which meant *weeks ago*, which meant she had been reading his things for weeks, which meant — he reviewed a month of evenings in his mind — she had probably known the machine's flaws before he had finished cataloguing them himself.

"All right," he said. "Here is the arrangement, and it is not negotiable. When the storm watch comes — and it will come, that sky over the water is gathering its arguments even now — you may tend the rotation. From inside the lamp room, door barred, and only the rotation, nothing else. The moment anything behaves strangely, you wake me. Agreed?"

Coral extended her hand with tremendous ceremony. They shook on it, her small grip fierce in his.

"And Coral." She looked up. "Thank you."

She smiled — rare as sun in November, worth more — and wrote beneath her drawing, in charcoal letters she immediately tried to smudge with her sleeve before he could see them smile-wide:

**PAPA SAID YOU WERE THE BEST KIND OF STUBBORN.**

* * *

The confirmation arrived on Sunday, and it arrived wearing a naval coat.

Jude saw the cutter first — not Harrow's usual launch but a proper patrol boat, gray and businesslike — and had Coral tucked into the false space behind Elena's tool cabinets before the anchor splashed. By the time boots hit the slipway he was mending a net on the porch with the expression of a man who had never done anything in his life but mend nets and polish glass.

Lieutenant Harrow climbed the path with two sailors behind him, and something about him was different. He had always carried himself like a man certain of his welcome; today he carried himself like a man who no longer required one.

"Keeper." Harrow didn't return the greeting. He stood looking past Jude at the tower, the workshop, the low buildings, the way a buyer looks at livestock. "There's news."

"There often is."

"The talks." Harrow's mouth did something that was not a smile. "They've accelerated. Weeks now, not months. Possibly less. There are men in my profession, Keeper, who intend to be standing on dry ground with clean records when the music stops." His eyes came back to Jude and rested there, heavy as wet canvas. "And men who won't. I've wondered, these past months, which sort you are. I've decided it doesn't matter. What matters is whether you're a sensible man in the next few weeks. Sensible men are finishing things. Are you finishing anything, Keeper?"

"Finishing the winter fuel stores," Jude said. "Finishing a net. Finishing a very slow argument with a stubborn spring."

Harrow held his gaze for a long moment, and Jude understood, with the clarity that arrives sometimes in the last second before a wave breaks over you, that Harrow knew. Not everything — not the bird, not the girl behind the cabinets — but enough. Enough to count the days alongside them, running his own arithmetic, planning his own forty.

"Fuel stores," Harrow repeated softly. "Of course." He turned to go, then paused at the top of the path. "Storm coming, they say. Worst of the season, maybe worst of the year. Boats won't run in it. Cutters won't run in it. Whatever any man means to do on this coast, Keeper, he'll do it before that storm — or during it, if he thinks the weather is his friend." He touched his cap, an gesture entirely devoid of respect. "Good day."

Jude watched the cutter pull away until it was gone. Then he went inside, unbarred the cabinet, and found Coral standing among the shadows with her arms wrapped around herself, and he did not pretend, because pretending had stopped being useful sometime in the last five minutes.

"You heard."

She nodded.

"He's right about the storm," Jude said. "And he's right about the deadline. Which means our schedule isn't ours anymore. We finish the decoder before the storm, or during it, or we don't finish it at all — because if it's not done when the armistice bells ring, it never will be." He looked toward the workshop, where the great half-built machine waited in the dark with its brass ribs and its patient, unfinished heart. "So we don't sleep much, and we don't make mistakes, and we trust each other. That's the whole plan."

Coral unwrapped her arms. She walked to the bench where the songbird lay, and touched its small bronze head with one finger, gently, the way you touch something sleeping.

Then she rolled up her sleeves.
