## 12. Sabotage

Jude smelled it before he saw it.

He had woken before dawn as he always did, out of habit rather than necessity, and stood at the kitchen window waiting for the kettle while the sky went from slate to pearl over the water. The wind had softened in the night, settling into that long gray hush that followed a hard blow, and he was thinking — absurdly, pleasantly — about pancakes. Coral had mentioned them exactly once, three days ago, in the roundabout way she mentioned everything she wanted, saying "Tully's boy says his mother makes them with blackberries," and then going very quiet, which was her version of a shout.

So he was standing there with flour on his hands when the smell reached him. Char. Wet char, and under it something sharper — linseed oil, scorched.

The flour went onto the floor.

He crossed the yard without his coat, without boots properly laced, and stopped at the workshop door because his body knew what his mind refused to arrange into words. The door stood ajar. Beyond it, the morning light came through the little seaward window at the wrong angle, wrong color, because the window was choked with smoke residue, and in the middle of the room, where Elena's blueprint chest had sat for eleven years like an altar he didn't pray at but never once thought of moving, there was a black circle of ash and twisted tin.

Someone had built a fire inside the workshop. Inside *her* workshop. On the stone floor, contained, deliberate — set against the chest and nowhere else, fed with oil from the lamp shelf, banked and smothered before it could spread. This was not carelessness. Carelessness would have taken the whole building and, God forgive him, might have been easier to bear.

Jude crouched at the edge of the scorched circle for a long time. The heat had split the chest's oak staves. Inside, where four years of Elena's drawings had lain in careful rolls — the tide-regulator, the walking lamp-trimmer, the great songbird with its brass throat — there was only fused paper, black lace, crumbling when he breathed on it.

He heard small feet behind him and did not turn around fast enough to stop her.

Coral looked at the ruin for maybe ten seconds. Then she did something he had not seen her do since the night she arrived: she made a sound. Not a word. A thin, cracked noise low in her throat, like a hinge that had rusted shut being forced open, and she dropped to her knees at the ash circle and began picking through it with both hands, sifting, hunting, her mouth working around shapes that wouldn't come.

"Coral." He knelt and caught her wrists gently. "Careful, love. The edges will be sharp."

She pulled one hand free and pointed — at the chest, at the floor, at the ceiling — a rapid, furious geometry, and then pressed her fist against her own heart and stared at him with eyes so full of accusation that he felt it land somewhere physical.

"I know," he said. His voice came out rough. "I know. Someone did this. On purpose."

She yanked her other wrist free and wrote in the ash with her fingertip. He watched the letters form, shaky but legible, and felt the last warm room in his chest go cold.

*WHO.*

"That," said Jude Villalobos, keeper of the Point Adelaida light for nineteen years, "is a very good question."

* * *

They held a kind of inquest at the kitchen table, because Coral insisted on one, dragging her chair around until they sat shoulder to shoulder and laying out her evidence on the scrubbed wood like a merchant laying out cloth.

Her first exhibit was drawn rather than written: a coat. She drew it with surprising economy — shoulders, buttons, a high collar — and then shaded the sleeves dark and added a cap, and Jude looked at it and felt his stomach drop through the floorboards into the cellar that wasn't there.

"A naval coat," he said.

She nodded, lips pressed white.

"Last night. You saw a man in a naval coat, last night." He kept his voice level the way he kept the light level — by mechanism, not by feeling. "Where?"

She pointed toward the yard, then toward the workshop, then made a small motion with two fingers, walking.

"Between the house and here. What time?" He caught himself; the child had no clock in her head. "Was it dark? After supper?"

A nod. After supper. While Jude had been up in the lamp room trimming wicks, sealed in glass and routine, exactly as invisible as he'd spent four years making himself.

Coral added to the drawing now, tongue between her teeth. She gave the man height — taller than Jude, she indicated with a flat hand well above her own head — and then, after a moment's struggle, a face. It came out as a smear of dissatisfaction. Then she flipped her hand over and drew, beside the naval man, a second figure: broad hat, drooping moustache, a boat.

Tully.

The supply boatman came twice a month with flour, lamp oil, coal, letters, and gossip, and he had been coming for longer than Coral had been alive. Tully who fished Jude's dropped hammer out of the harbor without being asked. Tully whose boy traded riddles with Coral over the crates. Tully who, Jude realized with a slow, sick turning, had been on the rock just nine days ago — and who had lingered near the workshop while Jude signed the manifest, asking, hadn't he, whether the old lady's machines still worked, in that joking way of his, hadn't he put his hand on the workshop door and peered in and said *you could sell these to a museum, keepers shouldn't hoard*?

"You think it was both of them," Jude said slowly. "The sailor and the boatman. Together."

Coral considered this, then shook her head and held up one finger, then the other, then pushed them apart.

"Not together. Two visits?" Or: two men, separately, one plan. Her finger stayed raised, though, and she tapped the naval coat again, harder, leaving a graphite dent in the table. Whatever else was true, she was certain of this: the man in the coat had come in the night, and men in naval coats did not come to Point Adelaida at night by accident.

Jude sat back. The kettle had long gone cold on the stove. Outside, the gulls were working the tide line, screaming their small hungers, and the world looked exactly as it had yesterday, which struck him suddenly as an obscenity.

Here was the arithmetic he did not want to do. If the man in the coat was Harrow's, then Harrow knew — or suspected enough to spend a match on — and a match meant urgency, and urgency meant the lieutenant was no longer content to visit in daylight with polite questions about boats. And if Tully had carried the man out again, or carried him in, or simply been paid to look elsewhere, then the one thread that connected their island to the world's bread and coal had been cut and re-tied by someone else's hand.

And underneath all of it, quieter and worse than either: *someone stood in Elena's workshop while I slept.* Someone had moved among her machines by lamplight, chosen her chest, fed her drawings to a fire, and walked away across Jude's own yard without waking him.

He had spent four years perfecting invisibility. He had never considered that it worked in both directions — that a man nobody sees is also a man nobody guards against.

Coral tugged his sleeve. She had turned her paper over and written, in her careful ascending hand:

*ARE YOU ANGRY AT ME*

"Angry at—no." The word came out too loud and he gentled it. "No. Why would you think that?"

She pointed at herself, then at the workshop, then shrugged with her whole body, palms up: *I brought the ledger here. I brought all of this.* Which was nonsense, and he told her so, firmly, taking her ink-stained hands in his flour-dusted ones.

"You were six years old and your father put you in a boat," he said. "If blame is being handed out on this rock, it lands on grown men who chose their beds, and I'll thank you to remember that I am the tallest thing in this household and therefore catch the most weather." That got the ghost of a smile, which had been the entire object of the exercise. "Now. We have work. Ashes tell stories if you know how to read them, and your father's daughter is going to learn."

* * *

The ashes told three things.

First: the fire had been fed with lamp oil from the workshop's own shelf — the half-empty can, now missing entirely, presumably carried off so no one would notice how much had gone. Whoever did it had known the room. Had known where the oil lived, where the chest sat, how to bank a fire on stone so it ate one thing and starved the rest.

Second: under the chest's collapsed bottom drawer, protected by tin and stubbornness, one roll of paper had survived — scorched at the edges but whole. Elena's earliest drawing, dated the year before Coral was born: the tide-engine's escapement, annotated in her quick slanted hand. *Note to self: the sea keeps better time than any clockmaker. Stop arguing with it.* Jude sat down on an upturned crate when he found it and had to stay there a while.

Third: footprints. The workshop floor was fine gray stone dust, the leavings of Elena's endless filing and fitting, and in it, faint but legible along the wall where the intruder had hugged the shadows, boot prints. Naval-issue heels — Jude had stamped a thousand inspections in that pattern himself. But crossing them, nearer the door, broader and blunter: a workman's boot, tar-crusted at the toe, of a make sold in every harbor chandlery on the coast.

Two sets of prints. One coat, one boat.

He was still crouched over them when Coral appeared at his elbow holding the surviving roll against her chest like a rescued kitten, and he showed her the marks without a word. She studied the tar-crusted print for a long moment. Then she looked up and mouthed a name she couldn't say aloud, shaping it with exaggerated care so there could be no mistake.

*Tul-ly.*

"Yes," Jude said. "That's what I see too."

He wanted, very badly, to be wrong. He inventoried every reason: Tully had children. Tully had ferried them medicine in the winter the fever came. Tully had once, memorably, argued a customs clerk into the ground over a miscounted crate of Jude's lamp glass, unpaid, out of pure orneriness. Men did not become traitors between supply runs.

But men did become frightened, and frightened men became paid men, and Lieutenant Harrow — Jude could see him now, that unhurried way he had of counting waves while he asked about boats — knew precisely how to make a man frightened. A widow's pension docked here, a rumor of smuggled cargo there. Tully's route, his license, his boy's school fees, all of it hanging from a single signature at the naval office.

"He didn't strike the match," Jude said aloud, working it through, and Coral tilted her head at him, listening the way she listened to everything, storing words like coins. "Or maybe he did — I don't know yet. But someone paid for the fire, and the payment went through a man with tar on his boots, and that man comes back in—" he glanced through the window at the flat pewter sea, doing the count automatically, "—nine days. Maybe less, if the weather holds."

Coral set the salvaged drawing down and took up her pencil. She wrote slowly, pressing hard, each letter a decision:

*WHAT DO WE DO.*

Jude looked at the question for a long time.

What did they do? Report it, and hand Harrow the exact excuse he'd been fishing for — keeper loses control of his station, keeper harbors persons unknown, keeper's judgment compromised, send him mainland and open every crate at leisure. Say nothing, and let whoever came next finish the job — the workshop today, the bird tomorrow, Coral's father's coded confession burned to nothing, and the armistice coming on its schedule regardless, closing the courts like a shopkeeper shutting shutters.

Nine days ago he would have reported it. Nine days ago he had believed history happened elsewhere, and his only duty was to keep the glass clean and the wick trimmed and himself out of the record.

"We do what your father did," he said finally. "We write things down. Everything we know — the prints, the coat, the dates, the missing oil can. In two hands, in two places, so fire can't take it twice. And we don't tell Tully we know. We watch him. When he comes with the next boat, we smile, we sign for our flour, and we count everything down to the last nail in the crates." He paused. "Because a man paid to burn a thing will usually agree to un-burn it, if the price goes the other way. And a man paid only to look away—"

Coral finished the thought in the air between them, tapping the naval-coat drawing once, twice.

"—is looking away from something," Jude agreed. "Which means the next visitor won't come by boat in daylight at all."

He sent her to the house for paper and ink, and while her footsteps crossed the yard he stood alone among Elena's machines, in the room where the smell of char would live forever now under the smell of oil and salt. The walking lamp-trimmer stood in its corner with its brass arms folded, patient as a saint. He laid his hand on its cool casing, an apology he didn't voice.

"I'm sorry," he said anyway, because the room had heard worse secrets than his.

Then Coral called from the doorway — not spoke, called, her second word ever given freely to the world, pitched high and cracking and absolutely clear across the ruined morning:

"Jude! Jude, come *look!*"

He went at a speed he hadn't managed in four years.

She was standing at the yard gate, pointing down the cliff path to the landing stage, where the tide was low and brown with storm-leavings. Wedged among the wrack, snagged on the iron ring where supply lines were made fast, lay a coil of burnt paper's cousin: an oil can. Dented, empty, its handle blackened — thrown from the yard into the sea, and the sea, keeping its own ledger as always, had handed it straight back.

And stamped into its base, small and official and unmistakable in the strengthening light, was the naval stores mark of the Admiralty yard at Corren Head.

Not Tully's chandlery oil. Navy oil, navy can, navy boots — and one pair of blunt workman's prints by the door, belonging to a man who would be standing on Jude's landing stage within the week, holding out a manifest for flour and lamp oil, meeting his eye, and lying to his face.

Jude picked up the can. It was lighter than it should have been, the way evidence always is.

"Right," he said quietly, to Coral, to the can, to whatever was listening. "Now we know it isn't only Harrow's hands. It's his *stores*." He turned the stamped base toward her. "Which means when Tully's boat rounds the point, my girl, we're going to have a conversation — and this time, I intend to be the one asking the questions."
