## 13. The Boatman's Confession

The supply boat came on Thursdays, tide permitting, and for eleven years it had come without incident — crates of oil, sacks of flour, salt pork, lamp wicks, and once, memorably, a piano that Tolly Ferren swore he could neither explain nor return. Tolly was the only boatman who would make the run to the point in anything short of a hurricane, and Jude had always assumed this was because Tolly charged accordingly and loved an argument about the invoice.

Now Jude knew better. Now he knew what else the man carried in his hold besides kerosene.

He waited until Thursday. He told Coral to stay inside with the door barred and the stove banked, and he told her it wasn't because of anything dangerous, it was because grown men sometimes needed to have boring conversations about invoices, and she looked at him with those grave dark eyes that saw straight through every lie he'd ever told, including the ones he told himself, and said nothing at all, which from Coral was its own kind of eloquence. She had been speaking more these days — small sentences, careful as a person crossing ice — but silence remained her sharpest instrument.

"I'll leave the lamp lit in the kitchen," he said. "If I'm not back by supper—"

She held up one finger. Then she pointed at him, then at her own chest, then made a small motion like turning a key.

"Yes. All right. I'll lock up."

She shook her head slowly, the way a schoolteacher does when a boy has given the wrong answer twice.

"Fine. If I'm not back by supper, you take the bird and you go to Mrs. Cade's cottage down the coast path, and you knock, and you show her—" He stopped. Showing anyone anything was now a complicated business involving coded ledgers and corrupt lieutenants. "You show her your face. She won't forget it."

Coral considered this, decided it was acceptable, and shut the door. Through the window he watched her drag a chair to the sink so she could wash the breakfast cups, standing on her toes, methodical as Elena had been, and his chest did the thing it had been doing lately, the painful expanding thing, which he had decided not to examine too closely on the grounds that examination might frighten it away.

Then he went down to the landing to meet the boat.

* * *

Tolly Ferren was fifty-odd years of weather and grievance built around a core of surprising gentleness. He had hands like knotted rope, a beard that seemed to be attempting to escape his face in several directions at once, and a way of talking to his boat — a squat little ketch called the *Perseverance*, named, Tolly claimed, after his own most marketable virtue — as though she were a wife of long standing with whom he was perpetually mid-disagreement.

"Not the tide, mind," he was saying as Jude came down the shingle. "Not the wind neither. It's you, girl. You've got a grudge against that mooring and I'd like to know what it's done to you."

"Tolly."

The boatman turned. Whatever he saw in Jude's face stopped the next complaint in his throat. His eyes went, briefly, involuntarily, toward the workshop above the station — toward the blackened scar where the blueprint chest had burned — and then back, and Jude knew then that he had been right, and wished with everything in him that he had been wrong.

"Villalobos." Tolly busied himself with a cleat, pulling a line through it that did not need pulling. "You're looking peaky. Salt air'll do that. Want my advice, get yourself to the mainland once a season, see some faces that aren't seagulls—"

"You went into my workshop."

A gull screamed somewhere overhead, delighted with everything.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Tolly said, "and furthermore I resent the tone."

"The chest. Elena's blueprints. Burned two nights ago while I was tending the light. The lock wasn't forced. Whoever did it came up the goat path from the water, which means they came by boat, which means they knew the light would keep me in the tower and the fog would keep them invisible." Jude climbed onto the gunwale and sat there, arms on his knees, so that he and Tolly were nearly eye to eye. "There are exactly three people who know these waters well enough to run the goat path in fog. Two of them are dead. One of them is holding a coil of rope very tightly and not looking at me."

For a long moment the only sound was the water worrying the hull and the gull making its opinions known.

Then Tolly sat down heavily on a crate of lamp oil and put his face in his hands.

"Ah, hell," he said, muffled. "Ah, hell and all its fishes."

* * *

They ended up in the wheelhouse, because Tolly said if a man was going to confess he'd rather do it sitting down and out of the wind, and because — Jude suspected — the wheelhouse felt to Tolly like the inside of his own skull, cluttered and familiar and smelling faintly of tar. There were two stools and a chart table and forty years' accumulation of things a sensible man should have thrown away: a broken barometer, a tin mug fused permanently to its saucer by rust, a photograph of a woman with Tolly's same escaping beard pinned above the wheel.

"She died," Tolly said, catching Jude's glance at the photograph. "Two years back. Don't say you're sorry, Villalobos, you already did your sorrying at the funeral and it doesn't improve with repetition." He poured tea from a battered pot into the rust-fused mug and pushed it across. It was terrible tea. Jude drank it anyway. "So. You want to know how it happened."

"I want to know why."

"Money first, if you must have the order of it. Why d'you think?" Tolly laughed, and there was no pleasure in it at all. "Lieutenant Harrow comes to me in September, all brass buttons and reasonable voice, and says, Ferren, you run supplies to that lighthouse, don't you, and wouldn't it be convenient if sometimes you ran other things as well, small things, unlisted things, and here's a sum that would buy your girl Nellie those new boots the doctor says she needs for her feet, on account of how they grew wrong."

Jude kept his face still. He knew about Nellie. Everyone within thirty miles knew about Nellie Ferren, who had been born with feet that pointed the wrong way, and whose surgeries cost more than a boatman made in a year, and who was — by every account — the single brightest thing in Tolly Ferren's life, the reason he worked the winter runs no sane man worked, the reason he haggled over invoices like a man defending a fortress.

"And I took the money," Tolly said simply. "First time, I told myself it was the boots. Second time, I told myself it was the follow-up operation. After that I stopped telling myself things, because I'm a poor liar even in my own head, always have been. Ask my late wife." He turned the mug in his hands. "But the blueprints — that weren't money. That were fear."

"Harrow found out I had something."

"Harrow found out the child is alive and with you, which amounts to the same thing, and it scared him proper. He came to me last week with his coat all buttoned up and his jaw set like a man swallowing a fishhook, and he says, Ferren, there's papers on that rock. Papers that could hang better men than me. And I says, what papers, sir, I only carry flour, and he looks at me like I'm the stupidest creature God ever floated, and he says, burn whatever's written down out there, or I'll see your Nellie's father swinging before spring." Tolly looked up. "And there it is. That's the whole confession, Villalobos. I burned your dead wife's drawings because a man in a naval uniform threatened my daughter, and I believed him, because I've seen his kind before and they mean it. They always mean it."

The wheelhouse creaked around them. Out on the water, the *Perseverance*'s reflection broke and mended itself endlessly.

"Elena drew those plans over six years," Jude said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Some of them she never finished because—" He stopped. Some doors didn't open cleanly. "They were the only handwriting of hers I had left that wasn't letters. Letters end. Plans don't. They're always pointing forward, saying *next*, saying *build me*."

Tolly nodded slowly, and to his credit he did not apologize again, having apparently taken Jude's earlier instruction about repetition to heart.

"I know what it is to lose a woman who filled a room," he said instead. "That's partly why I did it so badly, if it helps. Which it won't." He rubbed his beard. "I stood in that workshop twenty minutes before I could strike the match. Saw all them half-made birds and little walking things, and the chalk dust on the bench, and I thought, this is a church, Ferren, and you're about to rob it. And then I thought about Nellie in the hospital ward asking when Papa's coming back, and I struck the match, and I ran like the coward I am."

"You're not a coward."

"Aren't I? Funny, I'd reached the opposite conclusion."

"A coward wouldn't be sitting here telling me. A coward would've let Harrow wonder whether the job was done and sailed on." Jude set down the terrible tea. "A coward especially wouldn't have left the chest half-dragged toward the window, like a man trying to throw it clear before the fire caught. Like a man changing his mind halfway through damnation."

Tolly went very still.

"You noticed that."

"I notice most things. Occupational hazard of living alone with a lamp." Jude looked at him. "Why didn't you throw it?"

Silence. The gull outside landed on the wheelhouse roof with a thump, directly overhead, like judgment arriving early and clumsy.

"Because of your girl," Tolly said at last. "Because when I was shoving that chest toward the window, I heard a floorboard go upstairs, and I knew someone was awake, and I thought — if it's the child, and she comes down, and she sees me burning her father's world..." He shook his head. "I couldn't be that thing in front of a child, Villalobos. I can be most things. I've been a smuggler's ferry and a liar and a coward, but not that. So I lit the corner furthest from the chest and bolted, and prayed the sea air would keep the fire hungry in the wrong direction, and apparently it did, more or less." He spread his rope-knotted hands. "There. Now you've the whole of it. Money and fear and one moment of being better than I meant to be. What happens now?"

* * *

What happened now, Jude found, was that he had absolutely no idea, and this frightened him more than the fire had.

Because the arithmetic was simple enough, if you did it cold. Tolly Ferren had committed arson against the property of the Northern Lighthouse Board, at the instigation of a serving officer, during wartime. One word from Jude to the magistrate at Wick and Tolly would lose the boat, the livelihood, and quite possibly his liberty — and Nellie Ferren would lose the boots and the operations and the father, and be turned out into a world with wrong-turned feet and no one to argue with harbormasters on her behalf. And none of it, not one ember of it, would touch Harrow himself, who would simply find another boatman, colder and less conflicted, and continue.

And yet the man had burned Elena's hands. Her actual hands, in graphite, the way she held a pencil crooked, the little arrows she drew in the margins saying *mind the gear train here, Jude, you always rush.*

"Here's what I keep coming back to," Jude said slowly. "Harrow has you either way. You've noticed that, I expect. So long as he knows what you did and holds it over you, you'll burn whatever he points at. The next thing will be bigger than a chest. He's not a man who stops escalating; escalation is the whole of his character. So the question isn't whether you owe me. The question is how long you're willing to be his."

"Until spring," Tolly said bitterly. "Until they hang me or he tires of me. Whichever."

"Or until the ledger comes out."

Tolly's head came up sharply.

"Coral's father kept accounts, Tolly. Names, sums, dates — every bribe, every smuggled deserter, every official whose palm got greased, written in code and hidden in a songbird in my workshop for two years while I dusted it and never knew." He watched the color leave the boatman's weathered face. "Yes. Your name may well be in it. So may mine, in a manner of speaking — there's a debt involved I'm still untangling. But here's the thing about a ledger: it cuts both ways. It hangs the guilty and it clears the coerced. A man who took Harrow's coin under threat, who tried to throw the burning chest out the window — that reads differently in evidence than a man who volunteered."

"You'd speak for me." Tolly's voice was rough. "After what I did."

"I'd state facts. Facts are kinder than I currently feel." Jude stood, and the wheelhouse ceiling made him stoop, which somehow suited the conversation. "I'm not forgiving you today, Tolly. Maybe not soon. Elena's plans are ash and I'll grieve them like the rest of her, which is to say badly and at length. But I'm not handing you to the magistrate either, because you've a daughter with bad feet and a good heart, and because everyone on this coast is surviving at a price, and the only unforgivable thing would be pretending I'm not among them." He paused. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When the time comes — and it's coming, sooner than any of us wants — and I ask you to carry something across this water for me, you carry it true. No detours, no delays, no unlisted passengers in the other direction. You become my boatman, body and hold, until this is finished."

Tolly looked at him for a long moment. Then he spat ceremonially into the bilge, which Jude understood to be the most binding oath available to a man of his persuasion, and stuck out the knotted hand.

"That's blackmail, Villalobos," he said, shaking it. "Coercion of a desperate father. I hope you're proud."

"I'm learning from professionals."

* * *

He walked back up the shingle with the crate of supplies he'd insisted on carrying himself — over Tolly's protests that it was his dock, his boat, his bloody job — and halfway up the goat path he heard it: a thin, high sound riding on the wind off the water.

Whistling. Six notes, rising, falling, repeated. The songbird's tune — the coded melody Mateo had wound into brass and clockwork — whistled perfectly, deliberately, by a child who was supposed to be behind a barred door.

Jude's heart performed an alarming maneuver. He took the path at a speed no man his age should attempt with a crate of salt pork, crested the rise, and there was Coral — not at the station at all, but sitting primly on the flat rock above the landing where she could see the boat, the wheelhouse, and everything, wrapped in Elena's old shawl, whistling her father's tune into the wind with the serene confidence of a lighthouse announcing itself to ships.

She saw him. She stopped whistling. She pointed, gravely, at the *Perseverance*, then at Tolly emerging red-faced from his wheelhouse, then at her own ears.

"You were listening," Jude said.

She nodded. She tapped her ear, then pointed out at the water, then made a shape with her hands — a boat, rocking — and mimed, with great economy, a figure climbing from a boat onto rocks at night.

"You heard the boat the night of the fire. You came out. You saw him."

Another nod. Then she frowned, and her small hands hesitated, searching, and finally she pointed at Tolly's boat, then at her heart, then turned her palm upward in a question.

Was he sorry?

Jude set down the crate and sat beside her on the cold rock, and out on the gray water the *Perseverance* raised sail and turned for the mainland, and Tolly Ferren stood at her helm looking back at the lighthouse for exactly as long as pride permitted before turning away.

"He's sorry," Jude said. "It's more complicated than sorry, which is usually how it goes with people. But yes. He's sorry. And we may need him."

Coral considered all of this with the seriousness it deserved. Then she leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against his arm — the smallest possible weight, warm through his coat — and together they watched the boat shrink along the gray line of the sea.

It was only later, banking the stove while she copied stars onto scrap paper with a carpenter's pencil, that the cold arrived, sliding in under the warmth like water under a door. Because Tolly had confirmed it without meaning to: Harrow knew the child was alive. Harrow knew papers existed. Harrow had moved from questions to matches, and matches were not a man's second resort — they were his signature.

Which meant the next visit would bring more than one boatman.

Which meant, Jude thought, watching Coral's pencil travel carefully between constellations, that whatever they intended to build, they had better build it fast.
