## 14. Rebuilding From Memory

The ashes of the blueprint chest had not yet stopped smelling of smoke when Jude did something he had not done in four years of keeping the light: he sat down at Elena's workbench and wept.

He did not make a ceremony of it. He simply lowered himself onto her stool, which still held the shape of her habit in its worn seat, and let the grief come up out of him like water finding a crack. Coral stood in the doorway for a while, watching. Then she came over, climbed onto the bench beside him, and put one small hand flat on his back between his shoulder blades, the way you'd steady a wobbling thing.

They stayed like that until the lamp guttered and neither of them moved to trim it.

* * *

In the morning — or what passed for morning under the storm-gray sky — Jude laid out what remained on the long table: three scorched pages Coral had pulled from the wreckage before he could stop her, a half-melted brass gear, and the songbird automaton itself, sitting in its nest of rag like an egg that refused to hatch.

"That's everything," he said. "The chest is gone. The drawings are gone."

Coral frowned at the table with the particular intensity she brought to all problems, as if she could stare solutions into existence. Then she went to the slate hanging by the kitchen door — the one they used for grocery lists and the ongoing argument about whether gulls had opinions (Jude maintained they did; Coral's position was that opinions required souls) — and wrote, in her careful schoolroom hand:

**PAPA EXPLAINED IT TO ME. AT NIGHT. ON THE BOAT AND AFTER.**

Jude read it twice. "Explained *what*, exactly?"

She wiped the slate with her sleeve and began to draw.

It took her most of an hour, and what emerged was not a blueprint — it was a child's memory wearing a blueprint's clothes. Circles inside circles. A wheel with teeth drawn slightly too large because teeth are interesting. Arrows going every direction at once, because to a seven-year-old, everything a machine does is simultaneous and miraculous. But here and there, in the middle of the charming chaos, were details no child would invent: a notch pattern on the great drum, a sequence of numbers along one edge, a small annotation in Mateo's cramped hand that she reproduced stroke for stroke without understanding it.

*"The bird sings the ledger,"* Mateo had told her, she wrote next. *"But first the wheel must learn the tune. Papa said the wheel is stupid. It only knows how to count."*

Jude sat back. His heart was doing something complicated.

"Coral," he said slowly, "your father talked to you about this? A cipher wheel? He explained the counting?"

She nodded, then hesitated, then added to the slate: **HE SAID IF ANYTHING HAPPENED, THE GIRL WHO LISTENS AT DOORS WILL KNOW MORE THAN SHE THINKS.**

"He wasn't wrong," Jude murmured. He looked at her drawing again — really looked — and saw that beneath the wobbly circles was a workable architecture. The drum. The counting train. Elena's tide-regulator could drive it; the songbird's pinned cylinder would feed it. Two dead people's cleverness, one living child's memory, and his own hands.

"Well then," he said, and heard something in his own voice he hadn't heard since before the war ended — not hope exactly, but its first cousin. "We'd better get to work. We have perhaps two weeks before the guns stop and the world forgets how to be ashamed of itself."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm neither of them announced.

Days belonged to the light: trimming wicks, polishing glass, logging weather, eating soup. But nights — after the midnight winding, when the beam swung its patient circle over black water — nights belonged to the workshop.

Jude worked by lamplight with his sleeves rolled, filing brass, and Coral worked beside him on a crate turned chair, elevated to bench height, entrusted with real tasks. She sorted gears into families by tooth count, an occupation that suited her orderly soul so completely that Jude suspected she'd been a librarian in some previous life. She held small parts steady while he soldered, unflinching at the hiss. And she was his memory of Mateo — whenever the decoder's logic tangled, he would describe the problem aloud, and she would close her eyes and go somewhere internal, consulting a father who existed now only in her head and in a brass bird, and come back with answers.

"The drum," she said one night, around a boiled sweet he'd given her to keep her awake. "Papa said the trick is that it lies."

"Lies?"

"Counts wrong on purpose." She arranged boiled sweets on the bench — she had five, and was rationing them by unknown principles. "Every third turn it skips. So anyone who reads the tune straight gets nonsense. Only someone who knows about the skipping gets words."

Jude set down his file. "That's why my first decoding attempt produced a recipe for plum duff."

Her eyes went wide and delighted above the scarf she wore against the cold. It was the closest thing to laughter he'd seen from her since the boat, and it undid him more thoroughly than any storm had.

"Plum duff," she repeated aloud — her spoken words still came sparingly, each one spent like coin from a thin purse — "in navy code."

"There are worse secrets in that ledger," Jude said grimly, "but few tastier."

He rebuilt the skip-tooth that night while she watched, and when the trial cylinder finally clicked past the third pin and *held* instead of advancing, Coral raised both arms over her head like a referee calling a goal, and Jude laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone in a room with a child, for the first time since Elena died — and the sound startled them both so much that the cat fled the workshop entirely.

* * *

Some nights the work went badly, and those nights taught Jude more than the good ones.

There was the evening he cracked a casting because he rushed the cooling, and swore with a fluency that made Coral sit bolt upright, and he had to spend ten minutes explaining that yes, those were sailor's words, and no, she mustn't use them at the supply boatman, and yes, *especially* not that one.

And there was the night the wind backed around to the northeast and began to moan in the chimney, and Coral went silent and gray, and Jude found her pressed into the corner behind the coal bin with her hands over her ears.

He didn't try to talk her out. He fetched his coat, wrapped it around her, and sat down on the cold flags beside the coal bin, his back against the wall next to hers, and talked about nothing. The proper way to shingle a roof. The scandal of the lighthouse board's new paint budget. Whether Elena's clockwork heron had ever actually caught a fish or merely pretended to for dignity's sake.

"Pretended," came a small voice eventually, from inside the coat. "I saw it drop one. On purpose. It was embarrassed."

"There," Jude said. "Dignity preserved." And after a while her hand found his sleeve, and after a longer while the chimney gave up its moaning, and they went back to work as though nothing had happened, which was, he understood, exactly what she needed: for the fear to be allowed and then passed over, not poked at.

* * *

On the ninth night, the decoder spoke.

Not spoke — *clicked*, in the pattern the bird sang, translated through the lying drum, transcribed by Jude's pencil onto paper that trembled slightly under his hand. Names. Dates. Sums. A route and a price for every deserter smuggled through the coves, and beside several entries, initials of the officials whose palms had been crossed with silver.

And there, recurring like a stain: **R.H.**

Harrow.

Jude read the page twice. Then he became aware that Coral was tugging his sleeve.

"You did it," she said — full sentence, clear as a bell, and he realized with a jolt that her sentences had been growing all week, coming easier the way muscles do, and he'd been too absorbed to notice. "Papa would be—" She hunted for the word. Her father's vocabulary had clearly included many things she hadn't yet decided she was permitted to say. "*Proud,*" she settled on. "He was proud of finished things."

Jude looked at the decoded page, and at the girl, and felt the strange arithmetic of it settle in his chest: a dead man's cunning, a dead woman's machines, and this small survivor between them, stitching the two together.

"It's not finished," he said gently. "Half the ledger's still coded, and the machine needs a final component — the governor wheel. I'll have to cast it." He rubbed his eyes. "Two more days. Maybe three."

Coral considered this. Then she got up, fetched the slate, and wrote:

**WE FINISH IT TOGETHER. LIKE FAMILY DOES.**

She held it up. She watched him read it. And Jude Villalobos, who had perfected the art of having no expression at all, found he could not manage even a passable imitation of composure.

"Together," he agreed, his voice rougher than he intended. "Like family does."

She didn't smile, exactly. But she climbed onto her crate, picked up her tweezers, and got back to work, and that was better than smiling. That was *home*, in the shape of a child sorting brass by lamplight while a storm leaned against the windows and lost.

* * *

They celebrated the near-completion the only way the station allowed: cocoa, made properly with the good tin, drunk among the shavings at two in the morning while the decoder ticked softly to itself like a contented clock.

"Do you know stars?" Coral asked suddenly.

"A fair number. Want to learn?"

They climbed to the lamp gallery, because the storm had blown itself out and the sky had been scrubbed clean. Jude pointed out the Plough, Polaris, the slow procession of winter constellations rising over the sea. Coral listened with her whole body, chin tilted up, mouth slightly open, and asked questions that revealed she had assumed stars were fixed to the sky like nailheads and was troubled to learn otherwise.

"So nothing stays where you put it," she concluded.

"Not up there. Everything moves. But it moves *reliably*." He tapped the railing. "That's the whole secret of a lighthouse, really. You can't promise anyone the sea will be kind. You can only promise to be where you said you'd be, shining."

Coral was quiet for a moment, working this over.

"Are you going to be where you said you'd be?" she asked.

"Yes," Jude said. "However it goes. Whatever it costs."

She nodded once, satisfied, the contract executed, and went inside to bed. Jude stayed on the gallery a while longer, looking north along the dark coast, thinking about governor wheels and wartime courts and the fact that Lieutenant Harrow knew, or soon would know, that the station's little family had not yet surrendered.

The beam swept its circle. Out on the water, far off, low, running without lights — a hull where no hull should be, moving toward the cove.

Jude watched until it vanished behind the point, and told himself it was fishermen, and knew he was lying, and went down to check the locks anyway.

* * *

The knock came the next afternoon, while the decoder lay disassembled under sacking and Coral was, per their drilled plan, in the lamp room pretending to count waves.

Lieutenant Harrow stood on the step in his immaculate coat, shaking rain from his cap, wearing the smile of a man who has already counted your cards.

"Keeper Villalobos," he said pleasantly. "Just passing. Though I wonder — might you have need of anything from the mainland? Better accommodations, perhaps? For yourself. And any... dependents."

Behind Harrow, at the foot of the path, a naval cutter rode at anchor where no cutter had business being, and Jude understood the interview had begun before the door was opened.

"I can't imagine what we'd need from the mainland," Jude said evenly.

"No," said Harrow. "I suspect you can't. Not yet."
