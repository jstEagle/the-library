## 15. What Duty Costs

The supply boat came on a Thursday, which was wrong, because the supply boat always came on Tuesdays.

Jude noticed it from the lamp room while polishing the brass that did not need polishing — a task Elena had called "praying with your hands," and which he had continued long after he stopped believing in anything to pray to. The vessel sat low in the water and rode crooked, the way boats do when they carry more men than cargo. It was not the *Petrel*. It was naval gray, and it flew the pennant of the Coastal Survey Office, which everyone from here to the mainland knew was a fiction kept alive chiefly by Lieutenant Harrow's insistence on believing in it.

By the time Jude came down the spiral stair, Harrow was already standing at the base of the tower with his hands clasped behind his back, studying the rocks as though counting them. He had brought no escort this time. That, more than anything, told Jude what kind of visit this would be. Men bring witnesses when they mean to bully you. They come alone when they mean to buy you.

"Lieutenant," Jude said. "You're a week early for nothing and two days late for everything."

"Keeper Villalobos." Harrow smiled the way a ledger balances — precisely, and without warmth. "I've missed your hospitality."

"I haven't got any."

"That's what I like about you." Harrow turned and looked out at the water, letting the silence stretch until it creaked. "Do you know how many waves break on this coast in a year? I've tried to count. It can't be done. The number refuses to be known. Some things are like that." He glanced sideways. "Some men too."

Jude said nothing. In the workshop behind the cottage, under a tarpaulin, beneath a false floor Coral had helped him build with her small careful hands, the decoder was nearly whole. Its brass ribs caught lamplight at night through the seams of its crate, breathing faintly when the tide ran strong, as if it were some patient animal dreaming. Three nights ago it had produced its first complete page of decoded text — names, dates, sums. One of the names had been Harrow's.

"You'll have had the armistice rumors," Harrow said. "Everyone has them. Peace talks at Brest, at Washington, at the moon, depending on which fisherman you ask. But rumors have a way of becoming weather." He took a folded paper from his coat. Not dramatically — he handled it the way a man handles a receipt for something already paid. "I have an order here. Signed. Dated before any armistice. Transfers are still valid instruments, whatever happens to the war."

He held it out. Jude looked at it and did not take it.

"Promotion," Harrow said. "Principal Keeper, mainland district. Hesketh Light, near the town. Electric lamp — none of this oil-and-elbow business. A house with four walls that don't leak sideways. Wages doubled." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "And a school nearby. There's a girl of about nine living here illegally on a station manifest that lists one keeper, no dependents. Children need schools. Children need *names*, Keeper. Hers isn't anywhere except wherever you're hiding it."

The wind moved through the grass below the seawall with a sound like pages turning.

"The item," Harrow said. "That's all. You know what I mean. The little brass thing your late wife never finished — oh yes, I know it was hers; I knew Elena Villalobos by reputation, the whole coast did, the clever widow of the clever light. A machine like that is government property in wartime, salvage, technically. Hand it over, sign a receipt, and by Friday you're a promoted man with a legitimate daughter and a future. Refuse—" He shrugged, a small economical motion. "—and you're an accessory. To desertion. To smuggling. To whatever else turns up when I turn this station over stone by stone, which I will do, Keeper, with the full weight of my office behind me, if you make me do it the ugly way."

"You've already tried the ugly way," Jude said. "You burned her blueprints."

Something flickered across Harrow's face — not guilt, but the annoyance of a man whose contractor had botched a job. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Jude agreed. "Nobody ever does. That's rather the point of you."

Harrow's smile thinned. He set the transfer paper on the fence post between them, weighted it with a stone, and stepped back. "It stays valid until the guns stop. After that—" he looked at the sea, and for one strange moment his voice dropped its polish, going flat and tired, like a man reading his own inventory, "—after that, none of us get to fix our ledgers. Do you understand me, Villalobos? Whatever you think you're protecting, there is a window closing. I am offering you the last decent price anyone will ever pay you for it."

*You saved my life once,* Jude thought, though the thought belonged to Mateo, to the drowning night, to a stranger's hand in black water. *And this is what your death has bought. A window.*

"I understand you completely," Jude said aloud. "That's the trouble."

He picked up the paper. Harrow's shoulders eased by a fraction — a man who believed every soul had a number, and who thought he'd finally guessed Jude's.

Jude tore it in half. Then in half again, and let the pieces go over the seawall into the wind, where the gulls investigated them briefly and found them worthless.

"The item stays," Jude said. "So does she. So do I. And Lieutenant — when the magistrate comes, and he will come, because your name is written down in your own hand in ink that doesn't wash out — I'll put in a word that you came here first with money instead of fire. It won't help much. But it'll be true, and these days that seems worth something."

Harrow looked at him for a long moment. There was no rage in it. That was the worst part. There was only recalculation — a clerk discovering an entry he could not balance, marking it, setting it aside for the audit to come.

"A lighthouse," Harrow said quietly, turning toward the boat, "is just a promise that someone will still be standing somewhere when everything else goes dark. Terrible thing, keeping promises on this coast. Ask the sea how many keepers it's collected." He paused at the water's edge. "I'll come back in weather, Keeper. Bad weather. The worst there is."

Then he was in the boat, and the gray vessel rode away crooked, and Jude stood alone on his rock understanding exactly what he had done: he had chosen ruin, and ruin had given him a date.

* * *

Coral knew before he said anything. She was nine years old and had survived a war by reading rooms the way other children read primers, and when Jude came through the door she was sitting on the workbench with her boots swinging, watching his face with Elena's old magnifying lens held up to one eye, which made her look like a tiny furious scientist.

She lowered the lens.

"We should finish it faster," she said. She spoke carefully now, in full sentences, hoarding words like coins, spending only the good ones.

"We will."

"He's coming back."

"Yes."

"In bad weather." She turned the lens over in her hands. "Papa said bad weather was honest. It tells you beforehand everything it's going to do. It's people in good weather you have to watch." She hopped off the bench and went to the decoder crate and began, without being asked, sorting the wire spools by thickness, the way he had shown her, the way her father must once have shown her mother's ghost or whoever had taught her hands their patience.

Jude stood in the doorway of Elena's workshop — of *their* workshop now, though the word still snagged on something in his chest like a nail in wool — and watched a child work, and felt the full weight of what refusal meant settle onto his shoulders the way ballast settles into a hull: not painfully, but permanently, changing how the whole vessel rides.

He had torn up a promotion today. He had also, in the same motion, torn up electricity, dry walls, a school, a birth certificate with a real name on it, a life in which Coral would grow up among other children instead of gulls and gears. Every choice had a bill attached. He had spent thirty years believing history happened elsewhere, and now it lived in his house, decoded itself in his cellar, ate at his table with jam on its chin.

"Well," he said. "Faster, then."

"Faster," Coral agreed, and handed him the smallest spool, and they worked until the tide turned.

* * *

But at night, alone, the arithmetic changed.

That was the thing nobody warned you about decisions: they were made in daylight, by daylight men, and then they went home with you and sat by your bed in the dark and asked their questions again in smaller, crueler voices. Jude lay awake in the narrow room that had been Elena's, listening to wind test the shutters, and the voices were patient tonight.

*She could be on the Tuesday boat,* they said. *There's a woman on the mainland, the ferryman's sister, takes in children, asks nothing. You have coin enough. Write the letter in the morning. Put her on the boat with the note pinned to her coat, the way her father did. Forget this face — you've had practice. You forgot Elena's face for three years, didn't you? Walked past her workshop door every day and told yourself grief was the same thing as duty.*

"It wasn't the same thing," Jude said out loud, to the dark.

The dark did not argue. The dark never needed to.

Because here was the truth he kept arriving at and leaving, arriving at and leaving: Harrow would come in the worst weather of the year, and men like Harrow did not come to negotiate twice. There would be fire — there had been fire already, blueprints, and next time the crate, and after the crate perhaps the cottage, and after the cottage perhaps the child, because children were witnesses, and witnesses were the one commodity a corrupt officer could not purchase. Keeping Coral near him kept her inside the blast radius of his own stubbornness. Sending her away kept her safe and cost him everything, and God forgive him, lying in the dark, he genuinely could not tell which impulse was love and which was cowardice, whether sending her away would be sacrifice or simply the oldest trick in the widower's book — dressing up abandonment as protection and calling it virtue.

He got up. He lit the lamp. He found paper.

He wrote: *To Mrs. Calloway, care of the Petrel —* and stopped, because his hand remembered the last note he'd read that began with instructions about a child. *Forget this face.* He had spent months refusing to forget this face. It was the first instruction anyone had ever given him that he had wanted to obey and couldn't.

He wrote: *Dear Mrs. Calloway. I am sending you a girl of nine who mends clocks better than I do and asks questions like falling stars. Her name is Coral—*

Her name was Coral, and she was afraid of deep water and not afraid of anything else, and she had taught herself Elena's tools in six weeks, and when she slept she slept with one fist closed around a brass key on a cord, and three days ago she had fallen asleep in the workshop chair mid-sentence while explaining gear ratios to him, and he had carried her to bed and stood there a moment too long in the doorway, and the doorway had felt like something. It had felt like something.

Jude looked at the letter. Four lines. It would take five more to finish, and one boat, and one morning.

From across the cottage, faintly, through two walls, came a sound. The songbird automaton, restored to the mantel of the workshop, wound for morning release so Coral woke to it — its small brass throat producing the coded tune that only two living people understood, notes that were numbers, numbers that were names, a dead man's testimony sung sweet enough to wake a child gently.

She had wound it herself last night. She wound it every night. She said it was so Papa wouldn't be late.

Jude sat down slowly on the edge of the bed with the letter in his hands, and understood, finally and completely, that he was not holding a plan. He was holding a temptation wearing a plan's clothes, and it had come to his door the way Harrow had come to his door, with reasonable terms and terrible prices, and the answer was the same answer, and it would cost what it cost.

He fed the letter to the stove.

The paper curled, flared, and was gone, and Jude Villalobos sat watching it with his head in his hands, choosing ruin a second time, finding it slightly easier the second time, the way any hard thing gets easier — and that frightened him more than Harrow did.

When he went out at midnight to trim the wicks and check the wind, the wind had changed its mind since supper. It had swung round to the northeast and gone cold and heavy, with a smell in it he knew the way a man knows a knock at his own door. Old keepers called it the *grave-gale*. The glass in the barometer was falling so fast it seemed personal.

By dawn the horizon wore a scud of iron, and the signal flags at the mainland station, seen through the glass, flew the one he had been dreading and expecting in equal measure:

Storm warning. The worst of the year.

And out on the gray water, right on schedule, riding low and crooked, a naval vessel was making for his light.
