## 16. The Great Storm Warning

The barometer had been falling for two days, and by the morning of the third it hung so low that Jude half expected it to swing clean off its nail and bury itself in the floorboards.

"Twenty-eight point six," he announced to the kitchen at large, which was to say to Coral and the teapot.

Coral looked up from her slice of bread and marmalade, her eyebrows doing most of the talking, as they always did. She had developed a whole language of eyebrows in the months she'd been with him — one raised meant *explain*, both raised meant *that sounds bad*, and the slow flat lowering of both, which she deployed now, meant *I understood every word you said and I am choosing not to be frightened by it.*

"It means a storm," Jude translated, setting the barometer's brass case back on its shelf. "A proper one. The kind Elena used to call *the sea clearing its throat.*"

Coral pointed at the window, then made a gesture — small fingers rising like a wave, curling, crashing against an invisible shore.

"Bigger than that one. The biggest this year, I'd wager." He sat down across from her with his own tea. "The supply boat won't run in it. Nothing will run in it. For perhaps two days, it will be just us and the rock."

He said it lightly, because there was no sense frightening her, but the truth of it settled into his stomach like a swallowed stone. Two days isolated. And the final component of the decoder — the bronze escapement wheel, the last piece, the piece everything else had been built around — needed to be cast tonight or the following night, while the furnace was hot and his hands were steady, and if the storm knocked out the lamp or flooded the workshop or simply ate all the hours the way great storms did—

Well. Then four months of lamplight work would be so much scrap, and Mateo's ledger would remain a bird's song no magistrate would ever hear.

Coral was watching him with that unnerving directness children have, the look that says *you are lying about something and I will wait until you confess.* She pushed her plate aside, took the stub of pencil she kept on a string around her neck alongside the brass key, and wrote on the back of yesterday's tide table:

**WE CAST IT TONIGHT. BEFORE THE WIND COMES.**

"When did you get so good at telling me what to do?"

She smiled. It was still a rare thing, that smile, and he found, as he always found, that it rearranged something in his chest.

* * *

The casting could not be rushed. That was the trouble with bronze, and with most things worth doing: the metal wanted its own pace, and hurrying it only bought you cracks. Jude spent the morning preparing the mold from the pattern Coral had carved herself — a wheel the size of a saucer, teeth fine as watchwork, cut over eleven nights under his instruction while she bit her tongue in concentration and occasionally swore in a whisper she'd clearly learned off her father, since Jude had certainly never taught it.

"*Maldita sea,*" she murmured now, examining a tooth she'd nicked with the file.

"We'll sand it out," Jude said. "And we don't tell Father Anselm I heard that."

She grinned and went back to work.

By noon the wind had arrived ahead of the storm itself — a scouting party of gusts that came down off the headland, rattled the shutters, and made the gulls fly sideways. Jude stood in the doorway of the workshop and read the sky the way other men read newspapers. The horizon had gone the color of pewter. There was a green tint low in the east that he did not like at all. Green sky meant weight of water; weight of water meant the sea would come up the rocks looking for things to take.

"Coral," he called inside. "How would you feel about finishing early?"

They worked through the afternoon with the door barred and the lamp lit at two o'clock as though it were evening. The crucible went into the furnace at three. By four the bronze was a cherry-red eye in the dark of the coals, and Jude — sleeves rolled, leather apron cinched, sweat running despite the cold draft knifing under the door — lifted it with tongs that weighed as much as Coral did.

"Stand back now. Behind the shield. If this goes wrong it goes wrong *quickly.*"

Coral retreated behind the sheet of boiler iron they'd rigged for exactly this purpose, though only her body obeyed; her face leaned out around the edge, watching with an intensity Jude suspected she'd also inherited from her father.

The pour was not beautiful. The bronze came out of the crucible sullen and reluctant, and for one horrible moment it hesitated at the gate of the mold as if reconsidering the entire enterprise, and Jude's heart stopped in his chest — and then it ran, bright and furious, filling the channels, and the workshop filled with heat and light and a smell like the beginning of the world.

Then it was done. Then there was nothing to do but wait for the mold to cool, which would take hours, during which any impatience would ruin the wheel forever.

"Well," Jude said, sitting down hard on his stool. His arms were trembling. "That's the easy part finished."

Coral emerged from behind the shield and looked at the smoking mold the way pilgrims look at relics. Then she wrote:

**IF IT IS GOOD WE FINISH THE MACHINE TOMORROW?**

"If it is good, tomorrow we assemble the escapement, thread the spring, and by tomorrow evening—" he felt absurd, saying it aloud, as if speaking it might jinx it the way sailors jinxed a calm by praising it— "by tomorrow evening the machine reads the ledger."

Tomorrow evening. And the armistice, if the rumors were true, might come within the fortnight. It was going to be close. Closer than he liked. But it was possible, and after four months of *impossible*, possible felt like wealth.

He should have known better than to say anything hopeful out loud. The wind chose that exact moment to arrive properly.

It hit the station like a shoulder against a door. The whole building groaned; the shutters strained their hinges; somewhere upstairs a window they'd thought was latched banged twice and went quiet in a way that promised trouble. Rain followed within minutes, not falling but arriving horizontally, flung against the stone with malice.

Coral grabbed Jude's sleeve. Her eyes had gone wide, and her lips moved without sound — the old habit, the muteness reaching up for her again the way it always did when fear got large enough.

"It's all right," Jude said, and put his hand over hers. "We've a roof built by men who knew what they were about, and a light that's stood worse, and me — and I am extremely stubborn. Ask anyone. Ask the customs office. They have it in writing."

Her grip didn't loosen. He didn't ask it to.

* * *

The knock came at seven o'clock, when the storm had settled into its long rage and they were eating stew by lamplight with the barometer still falling and the mold cooling on its bench like a sleeping animal.

Three knocks. Measured. Unhurried. The kind of knocking done by a man who knows he'll be let in.

Jude and Coral looked at each other across the table. Her pencil was already in her hand.

**HARROW**, she wrote. She didn't phrase it as a question.

"No," Jude said quietly. "Harrow wouldn't knock. Harrow would send." But even as he said it he was crossing the room, taking down the shotgun from above the lintel — a formality, mostly; he hadn't the shells for it — and propping it where a visitor wouldn't see it and Coral would.

He opened the door on a wall of rain and the boatman, Tam Pellowe, soaked through his oilskins and holding his hat on with both hands, which told Jude everything before Tam opened his mouth, because Tam loved that hat more than he loved two of his actual children.

"Keeper." Tam's voice fought the wind and lost. "Got word for you, and I'll be quick, for I don't fancy the harbor road in this."

"How did you even launch in this?"

"I didn't launch, I *fell down the hill* and the boat followed. Keeper — I was at the naval yard this afternoon, provisioning." Tam glanced past Jude's shoulder, and his weathered face did something complicated. "There's a cutter fitting out at the quay. Gunboat's gig, marines' kit, grapnels and fire axes. Lashed down for sea tonight."

The wind howled through the gap and Jude felt cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. "Tonight. In *this?*"

"They'll come round the far side of the point where the water's lee-ish till they make the landing rocks. It's madness, keeper, and that's the point of it. Nobody sane puts a boat out tonight. Nobody's watching. Nobody can follow." Tam wiped rain — or something else — from his eyes. "And keeper — I'll say what I've got to say and then forget I said it, right? It's Harrow's party. He's aboard himself. And the orders they were reading at the yard—" Tam dropped his voice to nearly nothing, though nothing couldn't be heard over this wind anyway, which was maybe why he risked it— "weren't contraband orders. It was *arson* first. Burn the outbuildings, then search the ashes. Fire covers a lot of sins, my old mother used to say."

The world narrowed to the size of a doorway. Behind Jude, faintly, he heard Coral's stool scrape back.

"The workshop," Jude said. His own voice sounded far away.

"That'd be my guess, yes." Tam jammed his hat down harder. "Look — I'm sorry. I'm sorry as I ever got mixed in with him, and I've said too much already, and my boys—" He stopped. Started again. "When do you think they'll land?"

Jude did the arithmetic without wanting to. The tide. The lee of the point. The dark. "Midnight. Perhaps after. They'll want the worst of it, so no honest boat can put out after them."

"Aye, that's my figuring too." Tam stepped back into the rain, then turned, shouting now against the gale: "Keeper! Whatever you're mixed up in — finish it! Men like him count on storms to hide behind, and there's nothing in this world angers me like a man who hides!"

And then he was gone, down the black hill toward his boat and his long mad pull for home, and Jude shut the door on the roar.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed. Rain drummed the shutters like a crowd demanding entry. On the bench, the mold continued its patient cooling, utterly indifferent to the fact that the world had just changed shape.

Coral came to stand beside him. She was very pale, but her jaw was set in a way that Jude had learned to recognize and privately admired and slightly feared.

"He comes at midnight," she wrote. Her hand was steady. "We have five hours."

"To do what?" Jude heard his own voice crack and didn't much care. "Coral, the wheel won't be cool enough to break from the mold for another four hours at best. Four hours, and then fitting the escapement is delicate work, a full day's work done quickly, and they'll be here burning things before the bronze is even—"

He stopped. Because she was looking at the furnace. Then at him. Then at the furnace again, with one eyebrow raised in the particular way that meant *explain*, except that this time she wasn't asking him to explain anything. She was telling *him.*

"No," Jude said.

She wrote fast, the pencil digging little trenches: **THE FURNACE STAYS HOT ALL NIGHT ANYWAY. YOU SAID SO. WHILE IT COOLS IN THE MOLD WE PREPARE EVERYTHING ELSE. SPRINGS. FRAME. THE PAPER CYLINDER. WHEN THE WHEEL IS FREE WE FIT IT AND IT TAKES ONE HOUR NOT ONE DAY BECAUSE WE WILL HAVE DONE EVERYTHING ELSE ALREADY.**

"That's—" He looked at the bench, at the four months of parts laid out in their careful rows, at the frame waiting empty as a cradle. "That's actually sound."

**YES**, she wrote, and the flatness of it nearly made him laugh, which he suspected was the intent.

"And when they come ashore at midnight with their fire axes?"

Coral set the pencil down. She reached instead beneath her collar and drew out the brass key on its cord — her father's key, the one thing she'd carried from the boat, from the running, from the whole drowned country of her former life. She held it a moment. Then, deliberately, she looped the cord over Jude's head, so the key lay against his chest like a small warm verdict.

Then she pointed, firmly, at the tide-engine.

Elena's largest machine. The great iron patient in the corner of the workshop that had never been finished for want of purpose — the engine meant, once, to harvest power from the turning of the tides, all those tons of moon-pulled water going uselessly about their business twice a day while Elena drew diagrams on the backs of bills. Jude had kept it oiled out of grief and love and had never once imagined what it might be *for.*

Now he looked at it, and at the storm-driven sea stacking itself against the rocks below, and at the memory of Tam's voice — *men like him count on storms to hide behind* — and he began, slowly, horribly, delightfully, to understand what his wife had built.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, Elena. You clever, impossible woman."

Coral was watching him with both eyebrows fully raised, and this time there was no translation required. It meant: *tell me everything, quickly, because midnight is coming.*

Jude rolled his sleeves back down and picked up the lamp.

"All right," he said, as the wind threw its whole weight against the station and the sea answered from below like something ancient waking. "Five hours. We cast our wheel, we wind our springs, and we give the Lieutenant a night he'll remember. Together, then — no through it but through."

He unlocked the workshop door, and the storm came in over their shoulders like a partner to the dance.
