## 17. Night of the Rising Water

The barometer had been falling all day, the way a man falls who has decided to fall — not stumbling but committing, each hour taking it lower with a kind of grim satisfaction. By noon the glass read 28.6 inches. By three it had given up pretending and settled at 28.1, and Jude Villalobos stood in the doorway of the lamp room watching the horizon fold itself into a wall of bruised slate and thought: *well then. Here it is.*

The supply cutter's last message had come by flag that morning, half-glimpsed through rain squalls before the signalman on the headland gave up and went home to a roof he trusted: *worst of the year. batten all.*

"Batten all," Jude repeated to Coral, who was standing beside him with her chin tipped up at the sky, hair whipping loose from its braid. "Which means tonight we do everything twice as fast and twice as carefully, because there won't be a second chance at any of it."

She looked at him sidelong — her look for *I heard you, but I'm checking whether you mean it* — and then held up one finger, disappeared down the spiral stair, and returned two minutes later with the chalkboard they kept by the kitchen door. On it she wrote, in her careful schoolroom hand:

**THE BIRD?**

And beneath it, after a pause long enough that he could see her thinking:

**AND THE MACHINE.**

"I know," Jude said.

He did know. That was the whole trouble. The decoder sat in the workshop below, three-quarters finished, needing only its final component — the brass governor casting that was even now cooling in the little sand mold he'd packed that morning, timed so the metal would be workable by evening. One night's filing and fitting away from done. And the ledger-bird, wrapped in oilcloth inside the locked cabinet, holding the names of eleven men who had sold other men to their deaths, Harrow's name chief among them.

And the light. The light above them both, which had to burn from dusk until whatever dawn managed to claw through this, or the *Maribel* out of the north with her hold full of coal would find the headland with her hull instead of her eyes, and forty souls would go into water cold enough to stop a heart in four minutes.

Jude had spent most of his life believing that hard choices happened to other people, in newspapers, in places with pavements. He was beginning to suspect he'd been misinformed.

"We split it," he said, and immediately hated how it sounded, because Coral's face did the thing it did when she was afraid of being sent away — the small tightening around the eyes, the hand drifting up to touch the brass key at her throat. "Not us," he said quickly. "Me. I split me. You stay inside, doors barred, and if anyone comes who isn't me—"

*I'll blow the whistle,* she wrote. She had found Elena's old dog-whistle weeks ago in a drawer and claimed it with the seriousness of a soldier claiming a rifle. She wore it on the same cord as her key now, which struck Jude as either very practical or very telling, and he had decided not to examine which.

"Good." He crouched to her height. "Coral. Look at me. Tonight is going to be loud and strange and the house is going to shake, and every bit of that is just weather doing what weather does. It isn't coming for us specifically."

She considered this. Then she wrote: **IT FEELS LIKE IT'S COMING FOR US SPECIFICALLY.**

"It always does," Jude said. "Elena used to say storms are like tax collectors. They don't know you personally. They just want everything you've got."

That earned him the ghost of a smile, which he counted as a victory, and then the first real gust arrived and took the words right off the chalkboard's edge along with two roof slates and any remaining hope of an early supper.

* * *

By seven o'clock the world had been reduced to sound.

Jude worked the lamp with the shutters rattling like an audience that wouldn't settle, trimming the wick, topping the oil reservoir, testing the clockwork rotation with his ear close to the great lens — listening for the faint hitch that meant salt grit in the gears. There it was, third rotation, barely a whisper of resistance. In fair weather he'd have let it go. Tonight he stripped and cleaned the escapement by lamplight with his hands moving on their own while his mind ran arithmetic he didn't want to do.

The sums were simple and terrible. The lamp needed him perhaps twenty minutes in every hour: wick, oil, mechanism, a look at the ventilators. The governor casting needed perhaps ninety minutes of filing and fitting, and it needed them *tonight*, because tomorrow the storm might still own the coast and the armistice rumor was growing legs by the day. The workshop, meanwhile, sat fifty yards down a path that in this wind was fifty yards of being beaten about the ears, with a stretch of exposed rock in the middle that would be slick as soap and twice as treacherous. And if Harrow came tonight — and Jude felt it in his teeth that Harrow would come tonight, because a clever coward picks his weather — then the workshop needed guarding too, and guarding was a duty that couldn't be done in twenty-minute installments.

Two lights to keep, he thought. One in the tower. One in the ledger. And only one of him.

"You can't be two places," he said aloud, to hear something human over the wind, "and wishing you were three is just showing off."

The lamp turned. The beam swept out and was eaten almost instantly, swallowed whole by rain, so that from the sea it must have seemed less like a light than a heartbeat — there, gone, there. He thought of the *Maribel*'s master somewhere out in that chaos, counting the beats. *Hold on, friend. I'm here. It's all I can promise, but I'm here.*

He went down to check on Coral, found her installed in the kitchen with every candle in the station arranged around her in a defensive circle, working — of all things — on the decoder's gear train in miniature, filing a spare pinion with a steadiness of hand that would have shamed most grown men. She'd draped a dish towel over her head against drafts. She looked, Jude thought, like a very small and determined monk.

"If the machine business doesn't suit," he said, "you could always take the light."

She showed him the pinion. It was better than his.

"All right, no need to gloat." He warmed some milk and bread on the stove, made her eat, watched her eat, made himself eat though his stomach had other opinions. At the door, pausing into oilskins, he said, "Every hour, on the hour, I'll knock twice. If I miss an hour—"

Her chalk was already moving. **IF YOU MISS AN HOUR I COME LOOKING.**

"That's the opposite of—" He stopped. Looked at her face. Understood that he could stand here arguing until spring and lose. "Twice on the door means safe. Three times fast means come. Agreed?"

She nodded once, the way judges nod.

* * *

The path to the workshop took four minutes in calm weather. It took nineteen that night, and Jude would remember all nineteen.

Wind off the sea didn't blow at a person so much as *at* them, personally and with intent; twice it picked him up by the shoulders of his coat and walked him sideways across the rocks like a landlord evicting a tenant. Rain came horizontal, hard enough to feel like thrown gravel, and the sea below the path was running so high and black that each swell showed white only at the last moment, a sudden tooth in the dark. He moved bent nearly double, one hand on the guide rope, and he talked to himself the whole way because silence in that wind felt like agreeing with it.

"—stupid, this is stupid, the whole plan is stupid, Elena would say so—" A gust, a stagger, a regained foothold. "—she'd also have come anyway, so perhaps I'll stop invoking her—"

The workshop door fought him like a live thing. He got inside and slammed it and stood a moment in the dark, dripping, heart slamming, listening to the building groan around him — Elena's building, Elena's father's building before that, timbers her grandfather had known, all of it complaining together like a family argument conducted at volume.

He lit the lamp. The casting sat in its mold where he'd left it, perfect, gleaming faintly gold. He got the file out and began.

There is a particular peace in fine work done under bad circumstances; Jude had learned it keeping a light through six years of nights, and it came back to him now like an old song. File, test, file, breathe. The governor took shape between his hands — a small thing, hardly bigger than a plum, balanced to within a hair, the piece that would let the decoder's wheels turn at tide-speed without tearing themselves apart. Elena had designed its ancestor years ago for her tide-engine, and Jude had told her once it was the cleverest piece of brass he'd ever seen, and she had said, *it isn't the brass that's clever, Jude, it's the patience,* and he had loved her so much in that moment he'd had to leave the room.

"Patience," he told the governor now. "All right. Let's see if any of hers rubbed off on me."

He was maybe halfway done when the whistle sounded.

Three blasts, fast — muffled by walls and wind, but unmistakable, the pattern they'd agreed, and Jude was moving before the third note died. He took the stairs two at a time, burst out into the worst of it, and the wind hit him like a debt collector. Halfway up the path he saw it: a figure at the kitchen door, lantern in hand, tall, in a greatcoat—

No. Not tall. Small. Small and drenched to the bone and holding the dog-whistle in her fist and *why*, why had she come out, unless—

Unless the knock had come wrong. Unless someone had knocked *for* him.

"Inside," he shouted, and scooped her up, and she was shaking and pointing back toward the door, and by the lantern's guttering light he saw the chalkboard hanging from its nail, and on it, added beneath her old instructions in a hand that was not hers:

**KNOCKED TWICE. NO ANSWER 9 O'CLOCK. CHECKING. — C.**

She hadn't heard danger. She'd followed *protocol*. His own stupid protocol, executed to the letter, because he had missed the nine o'clock knock — because he'd been elbow-deep in the governor with the wind screaming so loudly he couldn't have heard the end of the world.

"You—" He set her down inside, knelt, took her face in two cold-slicked hands. "You did exactly right. You did perfectly. But listen to me — the knocking, next time — I may miss hours, Coral. Hours. The wind eats sound up there. New rule: if I miss, you stay put. You *stay put*. Being brave and being sensible are different jobs and tonight I need you sensible, do you understand?"

Her lip trembled — cold, fury, fear, probably all three — and then she nodded, and dug in her pocket, and produced, sodden but intact, a scrap of paper she'd clearly written in the kitchen before ever coming out:

**YOU MISSED THE HOUR. THAT MEANS COME. YOU MADE THE RULE.**

Jude looked at it for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, helpless, half-drowned laugh — and pulled her against his oilskins. "Yes," he said into her wet hair. "I made the rule. God help me, I make all the rules and then I live in the country where they're enforced."

* * *

He would not leave her alone again. That was decided, and he wasted no time mourning the alternative, because there wasn't one. So the new arithmetic went: the workshop trips happened with Coral, or not at all — and since the governor had to be finished, they happened with Coral, roped together at the waist with the washing line, which she found so absurd that she grinned the entire four-minute lurch down the cliff path, and he found it so absurd that he grinned too, two soaked idiots tethered like laundry, and the grinning helped more than any amount of talking would have.

They finished the governor at half past midnight, the two of them bent over the vise by lamplight while the workshop screamed around them — Coral holding the lamp steady with both hands braced on the bench, Jude fitting, testing, filing the last whisper of brass away until the piece dropped into its housing with a click so soft that in any other week he'd have missed it entirely.

It turned. Balanced. True.

Coral reached over and touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch something sleeping.

"Yes," Jude said, and his voice cracked on it. "That's yes."

The decoder wanted assembling now, tonight, while the brass was warm in his memory and the fear was warm in his blood — but the light called first, and they went together, rope and rope, up through the dark. He trimmed the wick. He topped the oil. Somewhere out in the black the *Maribel* was still beating northward through hell, and the beam went out to meet her, again and again, there-gone-there, faithful as breath.

And it was in that lamplit hush at the top of the tower, Coral asleep at last against his coat on the watch bench, that Jude heard the thing under the wind.

Not wind. Wind rose and fell. This was steady, and low, and wrong — a sound like breathing through a keyhole, coming from downhill.

He crossed to the seaward window and wiped the glass with his sleeve.

Down the slope, half-hidden in rain, orange light flickered where no light should be. Not lightning. Lightning jumped; this crept. This *fed*.

The workshop windows glowed from the inside, and smoke was already flattening itself along the ground beneath the rain, and Jude understood with a terrible, weightless clarity that Harrow had never intended to raid anything at all — that fire was cheaper than courage, and always had been.

Coral woke at the sound of his chair going over.
