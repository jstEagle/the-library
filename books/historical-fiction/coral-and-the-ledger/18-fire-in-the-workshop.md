## 18. Fire in the Workshop

The storm arrived the way debt collectors do — first a polite knocking at the shutters, then the whole house shaken by the collar.

Jude had been expecting it for two days. The barometer had fallen like a man fainting, and the gulls had gone inland in a great gray committee, which was the sea's way of serving papers. What he had not expected was how much of the night's arithmetic he would have to do in his head while his hands did other things entirely: trim the wick, wind the clockwork rotation, check the mercury bath that let fourteen tons of lens turn on a cushion of silver smoother than any bearing man could cut.

Two duties. One of him.

The light first, always the light first — Elena had teased him about that once, that he loved the lamp the way other men loved horses, grooming it, speaking to it. But tonight the workshop mattered just as much, because tonight the decoder sat finished at last on Elena's old workbench, its brass fingers poised over the cipher drum, waiting only for the ledger to be fed into its jaw. Four years of her half-finished genius and six weeks of his stumbling completion, all of it combustible, all of it sitting fifty yards from a door Harrow knew how to open.

"Coral!" He had to shout twice before she appeared at the foot of the tower stairs, hair wild, one boot on and one boot in her hand. "You remember what we said?"

"Stay in the lamp room," she said. She had been speaking more every day now, as though words were coins she'd hoarded and was finally spending carefully, one at a time, checking each one's weight. "If you don't come back up in ten minutes, I come down after you."

"If I don't come back up in ten minutes," Jude said, "you stay put until morning and then you take the ledger to Father Anselmo in the village."

She gave him the look. The one that said, quite clearly, that they both knew she would do no such thing, and that lying to a child was poor policy even when the child permitted it.

"Ten minutes," he said, and went down into the dark.

* * *

The rain came sideways. It came with such malice that it seemed personal, each drop flung rather than fallen, and the wind moved through the station yard in great shoving gusts like a crowd trying to get out of a burning theater — which, Jude would think later, was exactly the wrong image to be carrying.

He crossed to the workshop bent almost double, lantern hooded, and made his inspection by feel as much as sight. Shutters barred. Door braced with the oak plank. Decoder wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, because brass might survive fire but salt air killed it slowly and he had promised himself nothing else in this world would kill anything of Elena's again.

He was halfway back across the yard when he saw them.

Three shapes by the seawall, moving low, and the wind carried him the smell of something that had no business at a lighthouse in a gale: coal oil. Not lamp oil — the cheap, tarry coal oil that fishermen used to strip barnacles.

Everything in Jude went very quiet and very cold, the way it had gone the day the telegram came about Elena, as though some clerk deep inside him had received notice of an enormous loss and was filing it before allowing him to feel it.

They were going to burn the workshop. Of course they were. Harrow had bet on the weather isolating the station, and here was the weather keeping its end of the wager, and there was no cutter coming, no magistrate, no witness for forty miles of drowned coast. Just three men, a can of coal oil, and a match.

Jude did the arithmetic again. Three men. One keeper. A child upstairs.

He turned back toward the tower to fetch Coral down through the cellar passage, and that was when the world went orange.

* * *

They say you hear fire before you see it, but that is a lie told by people who have never stood close to one in a storm. What you hear is the wind changing its mind — the gale suddenly feeding instead of fighting, roaring into the workshop windows like a guest who has finally been offered a drink. What you see is light where light should not be, pouring gold through the shutter seams, and what you feel, God help you, is warmth, a horrible welcoming warmth on your face from thirty yards off in freezing rain.

"No," Jude said, out loud, to no one. "No, no—"

He ran. It was madness; he knew it was madness; the sensible keeper, the invisible man, the survivor of four years of war conducted at a safe distance would have gone for the boat, or the cellar, or anything but the burning building. But inside those walls was the decoder, and inside the decoder's cabinet — because Coral had insisted, because they had both agreed the safest place for it was the machine itself — was Mateo's ledger, and inside the ledger was the only true sentence anyone would ever say about the drowned boys of the *Meridian*, and Jude Villalobos had spent his whole life letting true sentences go unsaid.

The door was already hot. He got the brace off anyway, palms singing, and shouldered through into hell.

* * *

Coral felt the fire before she saw it too — through the soles of her boots, a tremor in the iron stairs, and then the whole lamp room lit amber from below like the inside of a struck match. She went to the window and looked down and saw the workshop burning, roof glowing, and saw a figure at the door, and knew the coat even at this distance, even through smoke.

Papa had a coat like that. Papa had gone away wearing a coat like that, and men in coats like that had come to the house afterward and turned everything over with their boots.

She did not think. Thinking was for later. She took the songbird from her pocket — they kept it on her person now, always, the little brass bird with its hollow belly — no, wait, the bird was in the workshop, on the bench, next to the decoder, where they'd wound it that very evening to hear it sing its coded tune. Only the ledger was safe, tucked in the machine's cabinet.

Only the ledger. And the bird.

The bird had Papa's tune in it. The bird was the only voice he'd left her.

Coral went down the stairs two at a time.

* * *

Inside, the workshop was dying beautifully, which was the cruelest thing about fire, Jude thought — it ate Elena's work with what looked like delight. Her tide-charts curled black off the walls. The great half-built albatross automaton she'd meant to power by wave-action spread its wings as if launching at last, and burned. Her tools — her lovely patient tools, each with a wear-mark of her grip — glowed in their racks like coals.

Smoke layered the ceiling in a rolling tide of its own. Below it, perhaps four feet of visibility, less. Jude got low and crawled toward the workbench, lungs already raw, and had his hands on the oilcloth-wrapped decoder when he heard, impossibly, over the roar:

"JUDE!"

Not a word she used often. His name, screamed in a child's voice from the doorway.

He turned and the smoke shifted and there she was, small and terrible and brave, already inside, already past him, heading for the bench where the songbird sat.

"Coral! Get OUT!"

"Papa's bird!" she screamed back, and dove under the smoke.

There are moments, Jude would write much later in a logbook no one ever read, when every choice arrives at once and you must simply pick your ruin. He could seize the decoder — the evidence, the justice, the whole point of six weeks' labor — and drag it out, and lose her. Or he could go after her, and lose everything else, including possibly both of them, and let the truth burn with the roof.

It took him no time at all. That was the strange part. All winter he had wrestled with the choice between safety and justice, duty and love, weighing them nightly like a miser with coins, and now the fire made the arithmetic simple in a single second: there was no version of tomorrow worth having that did not have this child in it.

He left the decoder on the bench and crawled after her.

* * *

She had reached the far side, where the bird sat on its little perch-stand among Elena's glass vials, and she had it in both hands when the ceiling beam above the bench let go.

It came down like judgment, trailing flame, and Jude did not decide to move — his body simply refused the loss before his mind could file the paperwork. He crossed the space in three lunges, got an arm around her, twisted, and took the beam's trailing edge across his shoulder as they went down behind the anvil block. Something tore in him, hot and grinding, and he heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize, and then there was no time to know what was broken because the whole room was coming down in stages now, like a curtain call.

"The cabinet!" he shouted into her ear. "The ledger — it's in the machine —"

Her face, smeared with soot, went white around the eyes. Then she did the bravest thing he ever saw anyone do, before or since: she let go of the bird. Set it down. Gave it up. And pointed at the decoder.

Together — his left arm dead and dragging, her small hands surprisingly strong on the oilcloth — they hauled it clear of the bench as the second beam fell where the bird had been. The songbird rang once against the flagstones, a single bright note, its last coded syllable, and then the roof found it.

Coral made a sound like the tide going out.

"Look at me." Jude caught her chin. Smoke poured past them both; the door was a memory of orange somewhere behind. "Look at me. We have the ledger. We have each other. Everything else was wood and metal and I will build you ten thousand birds if I live, do you hear me? Now breathe low and follow me, and do NOT stop."

She nodded. They went.

* * *

Later, Jude could never reconstruct the crossing. He remembered heat as a living thing that gripped. He remembered the cellar hatch, blessedly cool for six feet of passage, and Coral ahead of him, and his shoulder screaming. He remembered coming out into the yard where the rain hit them like mercy, like absolution, and the two of them lay flat on the wet stones gasping while the workshop roared behind them, and the decoder — the ledger — sat between their bodies, dented, scorched at one corner, intact.

Then he remembered looking up, because Coral had gone rigid against his side, staring past him toward the seawall.

A man stood there in a naval greatcoat, rain streaming off the brim of his hat, watching his handiwork with the mild satisfaction of a clerk balancing a column. Two others were already moving off into the dark toward the boathouse. The man looked at Jude. Looked at the girl. Looked at the scorched box in the mud between them, and understood, with visible displeasure, that his arithmetic had failed.

"Lieutenant Harrow," Jude said. His voice came out ruined, a grate of ash. "You're standing on my property."

"I'm standing on Crown property, keeper." Harrow's voice was calm, unhurried, terribly reasonable — the voice of a man who had never once been interrupted. "And everything on it. Including that item, which is government correspondence, stolen, and the girl, who is — well. A complication." He took a step forward. Behind him, lightning showed the sea standing up in hills. "The storm will pass. Fires burn out. This needn't be difficult."

And Jude, broken-shouldered, soaked, having just watched four years of his wife's hands go up in smoke, began — to his own astonishment — to laugh. It hurt abominably. He laughed anyway.

"You've made one error, Lieutenant," he said. "You keep counting waves. But you've never once counted what the tide brings in."

Harrow frowned. And then, from the direction of the point, through a gap in the storm, came a sound that neither of them expected: a whistle. A ship's whistle. Deep, insistent, unmistakable — a cutter feeling its way up the coast through the break in the gale, running early, running straight for the fire-glow on the headland.

Harrow turned toward the water. When he turned back, his face had changed, and his hand had gone inside his coat, and Coral grabbed the decoder, and Jude got his good arm around her, and above them all the great lamp turned and turned and turned, throwing its long arm of light across the water — across the cutter, across the rocks, across the man in the naval coat — hiding nothing from anyone anymore.
