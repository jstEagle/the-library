## 20. Armistice Morning

The bells woke him.

Jude Villalobos had not slept so much as he had lain down beside his exhaustion for a few hours and let it sit on his chest like a cat. When the sound came rolling across the water — distant, cracked, absurdly cheerful, as if the church bells of the mainland town had all decided at once to lose their minds — his first thought was that something had gone wrong. His second thought was that everything had gone wrong, catastrophically, permanently. His third thought was Coral's small hand on his shoulder, shaking him with the urgency of someone delivering urgent news about the weather.

"Jude. *Jude.* The bells. It's over. They're saying it's over."

He sat up in the chair he had never actually left, in the lamp room where they had spent the night among Elena's lenses, and the light came through the cracked great lens in fractured pieces, laying gold seams across the floor, across Coral's face, across the sleeping songbird automaton she still held against her chest like a hot coal she had carried out of a fire — which, he supposed, was exactly what it was.

"It's too early for peace," he said, because his mouth was running ahead of his sense. "Peace usually has the decency to wait until after breakfast."

Coral laughed. Four months ago she hadn't spoken at all; now she laughed at him nearly every day, and each time it did to his chest what the tide does to a sandcastle. "Maybe it couldn't wait," she said. "Maybe it was embarrassed."

*It certainly took its time being embarrassed,* Jude might have said, but the bells were still ringing, and beneath them now — fainter, stranger — came another sound: an engine. A boat engine, steady and official, cutting through the chop of the storm's aftermath toward the island.

They looked at each other.

"The magistrate," Coral said.

"The magistrate," Jude agreed, and discovered that his hands, which had been steady enough to fight a man and hold a burning ledger and keep a light burning through the worst night of the year, were now trembling like a first-year keeper's.

* * *

The morning after the Great Storm looked as though the sea had thrown a party and forgotten to clean up. Kelp hung in the gorse like bunting. The supply cutter's dinghy had ended its life wedged sideways in the rocks below the station, and Harrow's two men — the ones who had fled when the tide-engine closed its brass arms around their lieutenant — sat on the doorstep under the watchful eye of Mr. Penhale, the boatman, who held a boat hook with the air of a man who had recently reevaluated his entire professional life and found it wanting.

"He tried to run at dawn," Penhale reported, jerking the boat hook toward the younger of the two sailors, who had the bruised, sheepish look of a man caught doing something foolish by his mother. "Made it forty yards. In this mud. I've seen crabs move faster."

"I've seen *dead* crabs move faster," Coral said gravely.

"Don't encourage her," Jude told Penhale, who was grinning.

But the grin faded when the cutter came around the point — a proper naval vessel, white ensign crisp even at this distance, and standing at the bow a small, square woman in the black coat of a civilian magistrate, holding onto the rail with both hands and looking, even from the gallery, profoundly displeased about boats in general and this boat in particular.

"That's Magistrate Orrey," Penhale said, and swallowed. "She sentenced my brother once. For smuggling *limes*." He paused. "He was guilty," he added quickly, catching Jude's look. "I'm just saying. She doesn't smile."

"Neither do I," said Jude, "and I'm told I'm having quite a good morning."

* * *

Magistrate Orrey came up the cliff path like weather. She was not tall, but she occupied her full height and several inches besides. Her hair was pinned with what Jude suspected was actual structural hardware, and she carried a leather satchel the way soldiers carry rifles — slung, ready, personal.

"You are the keeper Villalobos." It was not a question. She looked him up and down — the singed eyebrows, the bandaged forearm, the soot that no amount of seawater had fully removed — and her expression suggested she was filing him somewhere. "You are also, I am told, the man who trapped a serving naval lieutenant inside a machine."

"In my defense," Jude said, "he was trying to arrest us. Illegally. After setting fire to my workshop. During a hurricane."

"And you are the child," Orrey said, turning to Coral before Jude could work up a proper head of indignation.

Coral stood very straight. She still had the bird against her chest. "I'm Coral," she said. "And this is Papa's bird, and inside the bird is Papa's book, and inside the book is every name Lieutenant Harrow ever paid, and he wrote them down himself, and I watched him do it, some nights, through a crack in the wall, and I can show you which page is yours to read first."

There was a silence.

Orrey looked at Coral for a long moment. Then she looked at the bird. Then, remarkably, the corner of her mouth moved — not a smile, nothing so reckless as a smile, but a tectonic adjustment in that direction.

"Well," she said. "Someone taught this child to give testimony."

"She taught herself," Jude said. "Mostly. I handled the stars."

* * *

They did it in the lamp room, because it was the only room in the station with chairs remaining and because, as Orrey put it, "the light is where the crime was committed against, so the light is where it gets recorded."

Harrow was brought up from the boathouse where Penhale and, at Coral's insistence, the tide-engine were guarding him. He looked smaller in daylight. That was the thing about men like Harrow, Jude thought — they needed darkness the way other men needed bread, and the storm had spent itself, and the armistice bells were still ringing faintly across three miles of gray water, and there was nowhere left for him to be large.

He tried anyway.

"This is irregular," Harrow said. "Wartime courts are dissolving. Within weeks there will be amnesty provisions. Whatever's in that ledger will be old news, Madam Magistrate. Paper dust."

"Then it's fortunate," Orrey said, opening her satchel and producing ink, paper, and a stopwatch, "that we're doing this today."

She set the bird on the chart table. Coral stepped forward and, with fingers that only shook a little, wound the key at the bird's back — the same key she had worn around her neck through a boat, a storm, a stranger's door, a fire. The automaton shivered, spread its wings, and began to sing.

Only it wasn't singing. Not really. It was a sequence of notes, yes, but arranged according to a cipher Mateo had built into its barrel — and as it sang, Coral turned the pages of her father's real notebook, the one they had pulled from the ashes with scorched edges, and read aloud, note by note, name by name.

"Harrow, Lieutenant Ambrose. Received: forty pounds, March 1917, for the passage of the *Marta C.* Sixty pounds, June 1917, passage of the *Sable*. One hundred and twenty pounds, January 1918—" her voice caught, steadied — "passage of the *Esperanza*, cargo one man, one woman, one child, payment taken, passage not delivered, cargo reported drowned."

The lamp room was very quiet. Outside, the gulls made their idiot complaints, and the bells went on ringing, and the repaired beam of the cracked lens swept its slow circle over the water, over the rocks, over the place where the *Esperanza* had gone down with everyone aboard except a girl who had learned to hold her breath a very long time.

"Enter it," Orrey said quietly to her clerk, a pale young man who was writing with the speed of a man afraid the words might escape. "All of it. Timestamped. Witnessed." She glanced at the stopwatch. "Eleven minutes past ten, the eleventh of November, nineteen hundred and eighteen."

Harrow laughed. It was not a good laugh. It was the laugh of a man discovering the floor was further away than advertised.

"You understand what you've done," he said to Jude. "You could have stayed invisible. I offered you a way out. I offered you *money*—"

"You offered me a transfer," Jude said. "To Cleveland Point. The fog signal there is notorious. I'd have hated it."

And then, because four months ago he had believed history happened elsewhere — in ministries, in trenches, in newspapers read a week late — and because he wanted to hear himself say it out loud at least once, in front of the child and the magistrate and the man who had burned Elena's workshop to its stones:

"My wife built machines that moved with the tide. Your men burned them last night. But the tide came back this morning. It always does. You should have paid more attention to the water, Lieutenant. It keeps better records than you do."

* * *

It took two hours. Orrey was thorough the way storms are thorough — she took Harrow's statement over his objections, took Penhale's confession (which Penhale delivered in a voice like a man unloading gravel from his chest, and which ended, twice, with "and then I saw the little one's face, ma'am, and I couldn't"), took Jude's account of the night, took Coral's testimony with the door open and Coral sitting on a crate so her feet didn't dangle, because Orrey had noticed they didn't reach the floor and had apparently decided this mattered procedurally.

At half past twelve, the clerk blotted the final page.

"Entered and sealed," Orrey said. She closed the satchel. And then, in the smallest voice Jude had heard from her yet: "Two hours ago, a telegram reached the mainland court. Preliminary amnesty discussions begin tomorrow. If we had waited — if your letter had arrived a week later, if that storm had come three days sooner, if any one of a hundred things had gone differently —" She stopped. Adjusted her coat. "Well. History is mostly a matter of arriving on time. Most people don't manage it."

"Most people don't have a child who won't forget a face," Jude said.

Coral looked up at him sharply. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. But she was smiling, and the bird on the table chose that moment to ruffle its wings and emit one final, pure, unciphered note — the note Mateo had tuned it to end on, Elena would have told him, the tonic, the home note, the one you return to.

* * *

They ate on the gallery, all of them — even Orrey, who accepted a bowl of Jude's fish stew with deep suspicion and finished it with suspicious speed, and even the two prisoners, who were fed on the steps below like large, contrite dogs. The bells across the water had stopped by then, replaced by whistles, by horns, by the distant unhinged joy of a whole coast discovering simultaneously that the war was over and that nobody had prepared anything.

"Four years," Penhale said, staring at the mainland. "Four years, and it ends while I'm holding a boat hook on a rock. I always thought there'd be more ceremony."

"There'll be ceremony later," Jude said. "There's always ceremony later. This part — this quiet bit, with the stew — this is the actual part."

Coral leaned against his arm. She had not let go of the bird since the lamp room. "Jude," she said, quietly, under the others' talk. "When they take Papa's names to the capital. Will they say mine? That I was cargo?"

He thought about lying. He had gotten terribly good at lying, these past months, and hated every minute of it. He thought about softening it, the way you sand a rough edge for a child's hand.

"They'll say," he said finally, "that the cargo of the *Esperanza* included Mateo Ferro's daughter, age nine, who survived, who kept the evidence for two years without knowing she was keeping it, and whose testimony entered the record today at eleven minutes past ten." He looked down at her. "That's not cargo, Coral. That's a witness. There's a difference, and Magistrate Orrey knows it, and now the record knows it, and records last."

She considered this with the enormous seriousness she brought to everything, including breakfast.

"Records last," she repeated. "Like stars."

"A bit faster, but yes."

* * *

Toward evening the cutter weighed anchor. Harrow went down the cliff path between two naval policemen, upright, correct, already composing whatever version of events he would carry to his trial — and Jude watched him go and felt, not triumph, not satisfaction, but something quieter and stranger: the specific lightness of a debt discharged. Somewhere out there, in the dark water off a beach in the north, a man named Mateo had once hauled a drowning stranger out of the sea and asked for nothing. Today the nothing had finally been paid, with interest, in the only currency that mattered.

Orrey lingered at the top of the path. She shook Coral's hand as though concluding a treaty. She looked at Jude for a moment longer.

"The boy's records," she said. "The child's guardianship. There will be paperwork. There is always paperwork." A pause, during which the tide turned audibly below them. "Unless she already has a guardian."

"She has a keeper," Jude said. "It's a related position. Slightly worse pay. Considerably better view."

"Mm," said Orrey, and for one full second she smiled, entirely and dangerously, and then it was gone, filed away forever, and she descended to her boat.

* * *

Night came down soft for the first time in a week. The workshop was a black square of ruin against the dark hillside, and tomorrow there would be ashes to sift and beams to clear and decisions to make about what rose from them. Tomorrow. Tonight, Jude climbed to the lamp room, lit the lamp out of pure habit, and found Coral already there, cross-legged on the floor, winding the bird.

It sang its coded song into the rotating dark, and she sang along — she knew the cipher now, knew which notes were names, and hummed them like a lullaby that happened to be an indictment.

"Coral," Jude said. "Tomorrow, when the magistrate's letters go out — there will be questions. About you. About where you belong." He sat down beside her, his back against the warm housing of the great lens. "I want you to know that whatever the papers say, whatever they decide —"

"I know," Coral said, not looking up from the bird.

"— because I intend to be extremely difficult about it, and I thought you should be warned —"

"*I know,*" she said, with the exquisitely patient tone of a nine-year-old managing a slow adult, and held the bird up so it could see him, or so it seemed. "Look. Papa gave me the key. Elena gave me the machines. You gave me the stars." She smiled, and outside, far across the water, the mainland towns were lighting bonfires for the end of the war, little orange sparks along the whole curve of the coast, and here on the rock the great lens turned and turned, sending its own answering fire out to the edge of the dark. "I'm not lost anymore, Jude. I'm *kept*."

Jude opened his mouth to say something adequate and found nothing there, and so instead he simply put his arm around her shoulders and watched the light go round, and neither of them spoke again until, much later, Coral pointed sleepily out the glass at the eastern horizon and said:

"What's that star called, the low one, the bright one?"

And Jude looked, and smiled, and began: "Ah. That one has a story."
