## 21. Ashes and Seeds

Jude had counted the tides for thirty years, and he had never once counted ashes.

He stood at the edge of what had been Elena's workshop and tried to understand the arithmetic of it. The roof was gone. The walls stood to half their height, blackened and cracked like the ribs of some enormous sea creature that had beached itself there and died in the night. The tide-engine — his great clumsy triumph, the machine that had pinned a lieutenant to the floor while justice arrived on a supply cutter — sat in the ruins looking sheepish, its brass arms folded in on themselves, streaked with soot and salt-scum but fundamentally whole. Everything else was sorted into categories by the fire's whim: melted, charred, or mysteriously spared.

A spade leaned against the doorframe. Coral had put it there.

"You're sure," he said, not for the first time that morning.

She nodded, also not for the first time, and held up the slate she'd taken to carrying since the storm. On it, in her careful new handwriting — letters learned from him, from Elena's surviving notebooks, from the labels on jars of screws — she had written: **DIG FIRST. DECIDE AFTER.**

It was good advice. It was, he suspected, advice Elena would have given, if Elena gave advice instead of simply building things until you understood them. So Jude took up the spade, and Coral took up the second spade, the small one, and they began to dig through the ashes of the only home her hands had ever really known.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and grief worked alongside them, changing shape as grief does when it is given something to do.

The first hour was terrible. Every shovelful turned up something: the twisted remains of a cam shaft he'd machined for her father's songbird; the glass bulbs of Elena's tide-gauges, fused into lumps like amber with nothing inside; the iron frame of the workbench, standing stubbornly upright amid the rubble as if waiting for someone to set tools back upon it. Jude found himself cataloguing losses the way he'd once catalogued ships' lights — this one gone, that one gone, all of them gone — and had to stop and sit down on the doorstep and breathe the way he'd taught Coral to breathe when the storms made her small again.

Coral, meanwhile, had found something.

She came to him holding it out flat on both palms, the way one carries an injured bird or an egg too precious to grip. It was a tin box, rust-bled at the corners, half-melted shut. Inside — once Jude worked the lid loose with a screwdriver — lay Elena's wedding ring and three brass gears wrapped in waxed cloth, and a note in Elena's looping hand that read: *For whoever breaks these, replace them better.*

Jude laughed. It startled him, the laugh, coming up out of him like a bubble from deep water, and it turned halfway through into something else that he let happen because Coral was watching and Coral deserved to see that grown men could do such things. He put the ring on his smallest finger, where it sat like a lifebuoy on a very large sailor, and he put the gears in his pocket, and he said, "Well. We've our instructions."

Coral wrote on her slate: **REPLACE THEM BETTER.**

"That's the spirit," said Jude, and picked up his spade.

By noon the digging had become archaeology, and by afternoon it had become something almost like gardening, though neither of them knew it yet. They laid things out on a canvas sheet in rows: salvageable tools, scrap brass, the fire's odd mercies. A drawer of Elena's sketches had survived nearly intact beneath a collapsed cabinet, the paper scorched brown at the edges but every line legible — automata Jude had never seen finished, designs for machines that sorted shells and wound clockwork and, on the last page, a rough drawing of a device with no purpose Jude could determine except that it appeared to make tea.

"She did think of everything," he murmured.

Coral tapped the tea machine. Then she pointed at herself. Then she pointed at the sky, and made drinking motions, and raised her eyebrows with tremendous implication.

"Absolutely not," said Jude. "One miracle per household."

But he was smiling when he said it, and she saw, and that was worth more than tea.

* * *

The magistrate had come and gone two days after the armistice bells, taking Harrow away in irons and the ledger away in an oilskin case, reading names aloud on the quay as though reciting a homily. There had been forms to sign. There had been a naval captain who shook Jude's hand too hard and called him *a credit to civilian vigilance*, which was the sort of sentence that meant nothing and cost nothing and was offered precisely because it cost nothing. There had been questions about whether he intended to press claims for the destruction of property, and he had said he'd let them know.

He was letting them know now, in his own time, by deciding not to rebuild.

The realization didn't arrive all at once. It arrived the way weather arrives, in pieces, over several days of digging and sorting and sitting on the doorstep drinking chicory while Coral arranged salvaged gears into spirals on the flagstones. He kept catching himself measuring the old workshop in his mind — where the bench had been, where the lathe had screamed, where Elena used to stand with a file in her hand telling him that precision was a form of love — and every time he measured it, he felt the loss close around his throat like wet rope.

And then, one evening, he caught himself measuring something else.

He was standing where the north wall had been, looking at the gap in the world the fire had opened, and he thought: *the light comes through here now.* The low gold light of late October fell across the ruined floor and lay there in long warm stripes, and the ground beneath the ash was — he crouched and rubbed some between his fingers — actually rather good soil, all things considered. Ash and salt and forty years of swept shavings composting into something dark and crumbly. Elena would have had opinions about the pH. Elena had opinions about everything and had built machines to prove them.

"You couldn't grow anything in here," he said aloud, to test how the words fit.

From across the ruin, Coral looked up. She had developed an unerring instinct for when he was talking to ghosts, and she waited, patient as tide, for him to decide whether the conversation continued.

"The soil," he said. "It's good. Underneath." He knelt and dug his fingers in and turned over a clump, and she came running to kneel beside him and do exactly the same, because everything he did was now, apparently, science.

They sat back on their heels, the pair of them, filthy to the elbows, and looked at the little hole they'd dug in the floor of a burned building.

"I keep thinking I ought to rebuild it," Jude said. "Exactly as it was. Same walls, same bench, same everything. Put it all back so nothing happened." He turned a clump of soil over in his hands. "But that's not how I got her back, is it."

Coral tilted her head.

"Elena," he said. "Your papa. All of it. I got them back by letting them change into something else. The bird became the ledger. The ledger became the truth. Grief became—" he gestured vaguely at the child, the ruin, the ring on the wrong finger, the whole improbable arrangement of his life. "This. Whatever this is."

Coral considered the matter with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she wiped her slate clean and wrote, in letters that were getting sturdier every week:

**SCHOOLROOM.**

Jude stared at it. "What?"

She erased it and wrote again, slower, pressing hard: **SCHOOL. ROOM. FOR ME. AND OTHER CHILDREN. THERE ARE OTHER CHILDREN.**

"There are," Jude admitted, thinking of the fishing families down the coast, the ones who'd started leaving bread on his step during the storm weeks and hadn't entirely stopped. "Half of them can't write and the other half can't stop."

**AND GARDEN,** Coral added. She drew, with great economy, a rectangle, and inside it circles, and above the circles a sun with an expression suspiciously like Elena's.

Jude looked at the plan for a long moment. Schoolroom and garden. Machines that made tea instead of testimony. Children where the lathe had been. Beans climbing the south wall where the tide-pipes had run.

"It won't be hers," he said quietly. "That's the thing, Coral. If I build it back exact, it stays a tomb. If I build it different—" His throat did something inconvenient. "If I build it different, it gets to be alive. That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

Coral reached over and took one of the brass gears from Elena's tin, the ones marked *replace them better,* and pressed it into the soil at the bottom of their little hole, and covered it up, and patted it flat.

"That's not how seeds work," Jude said.

Coral shrugged, eloquently, in a manner that suggested that perhaps he didn't know everything about how seeds worked, and Jude, who had spent a lifetime learning how much he didn't know, decided to allow it.

* * *

He wrote the letter to the naval authorities that evening, at the kitchen table, with Coral doing sums across from him under strict orders not to peek, which she ignored completely and he pretended not to notice.

*Regarding your inquiry of the 14th,* he wrote. *I decline compensation for the workshop. Rebuild it yourselves if you like, somewhere else. What stands on that ground now belongs to the living. — J. Villalobos, Keeper.*

He read it over. He added: *P.S. The tide-engine will require servicing before any attempt is made to remove it. It has opinions.* Then he crossed that out, then un-crossed it out, then left it, because life was short and Harrow was in custody and a man was entitled to his small amusements.

Coral slid her slate across the table. **WHAT WILL YOU BUILD FIRST?**

"A door," Jude said. "A big one. Facing east, where the morning comes in. Elena always wanted an east window; said the workshop was gloomy as a whale's stomach. Then benches. Low ones, so you can reach. Then a stove, because mathematics in winter is cruelty." He ticked items off on his fingers, and found, to his mild astonishment, that the list kept going, that the future had gotten longer without asking his permission. "Then seed boxes along the south wall. Turnips, probably. Cabbage. Whatever forgives a beginner."

**I WILL DIG THE GARDEN,** Coral wrote. **YOU BUILD. I DIG. WE ARE TWO KEEPERS NOW.**

Jude stopped with his pen in the air.

She hadn't meant it the way it landed. He could see that — she'd simply repeated the phrase from the supply boatman's chatter, *keepers of the light*, applied to the pair of them with a child's blunt arithmetic. But the words sat on the slate between them and refused to be ordinary, and Jude found he had to look out the window at the tower for a while, at the lamp room turning its slow faithful circles against the early dark, before he could trust his voice.

Because the thing was — and he had known it for weeks, and had not said it, because saying things makes them breakable — the thing was that he had already rearranged his entire interior architecture around this child. The light needed two keepers. Not for the machinery; the machinery ran itself, Elena had seen to that, bless her and her redundant wick-feeders. It needed two keepers the way a story needs a listener. He could tend the flame alone forever. But the meaning of the flame, the reason for it — that required someone to hand it to.

"Coral," he said.

She looked up, wary in the instant way of children who have learned that serious adult voices can precede departures.

"There's something we should settle," Jude said, "before the walls go up. Before the schoolroom and the garden and the turnips. Because buildings are promises, sweetheart, whether you mean them or not, and I want to mean this one properly, out loud, so there's no fog in it."

Coral's hand had found the cord at her neck — her father's key, the one that had opened the cabinet, that had opened everything — and she gripped it now the way sailors grip railings.

"Your papa sent you to me," Jude said. "And I have wondered, every day since, whether I was keeping you out of debt or out of love, and I've finally done the sums, and it turns out the debt ran out months ago and nobody told me. You're not here because Mateo asked. You're here because the light needs two keepers now. One to tend the flame." He swallowed. "And one to remember why."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Out on the point, the sea went about its ancient business.

Coral did not reach for her slate. Instead she did something she had never done: she spoke, unprompted, in the clear small voice she was still learning to spend, and she spent it all at once.

"Then I'm staying," she said. "Forever and also always."

"Forever and also always," Jude agreed, and his voice broke only slightly, and only in the middle, which for a man of his construction was practically a fanfare.

She came around the table and he gathered her up, this child of tides and ledgers, this girl who had walked out of a storm into the exact shape of the hole in his life, and he held on while she held on, and outside the window the light turned, and turned, and turned.

After a long while she pulled back and pointed firmly at the garden side of the slate diagram, then at the darkness outside, then at him, eyebrows raised with terrifying administrative intent.

"Yes, all right," Jude said, wiping his eyes with his wrist. "First thing tomorrow. We'll turn the soil by lamplight if we must. Though I warn you—" he stood, and lifted the lamp down from its hook, and held the door open onto the cool salt night and the sound of the sea and the faint, far-off beginning of another fine clear evening of stars— "if we're going to be planting things, you'll need to learn the names of the constellations. Farmers who can't find the Pleiades get laughed at down the coast. It's a hard world."

Coral took his hand. Above them the sky was swept and enormous, washed clean by the storms, thick with the old faithful lights that Elena had loved and Mateo had steered by and that Jude was only now, at last, learning to look at without looking away.

"Start with that bright one," Coral said. "The one that doesn't twinkle."

"Ah," said Jude, smiling into the dark. "Now *that* is a story with your family in it."
