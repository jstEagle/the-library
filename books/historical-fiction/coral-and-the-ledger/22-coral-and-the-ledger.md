## 22. Coral and the Ledger

The songbird still sings at dusk.

It has never needed winding — that was Papa's genius, and Elena's before him in her own way, though they never met. The tide comes up the channel below the headland twice a day, and the great brass weight in the cellar rises and falls with it, and somewhere in the works of that little gilded bird the motion becomes music: eleven notes, then silence, then eleven notes again. A coded tune. A ledger set to melody. Only two people alive know what the notes mean, and one of them is me, and the other makes his bread now by burning it, which is a longer story than this one.

I am twenty-six years old. I keep the light at Punta Salaz beside Jude Villalobos, who is not my father, except that he is.

* * *

People ask sometimes — supply boatmen mostly, new ones, the young ones with quick eyes — how a woman came to be an assistant keeper on a coast so mean that even the navy gave up posting men here. They ask politely, the way people do when they suspect the answer is long. I have developed a repertoire of answers. *My family has kept this light for years.* True, if you count generously. *The old keeper took me in.* True, and inadequate in ways I no longer try to explain. My favorite, deployed only for the ones who seem like they might actually listen: *He owed my father a debt, and paid it in years.*

That one is truest, and it always makes the good listeners go quiet, because everyone on this coast knows what a debt of salt means. You don't pay those in coin.

Tonight the wind is coming out of the northwest, which in November means it means business, and I have just finished polishing the lower lens when I hear the bell on the kitchen door — two rings, our signal, invented years ago because Jude refuses to shout up stairwells like a barbarian — and I know without going down that it's time for the bird.

We keep the same hours we have kept since I was eight years old and mute as a stone: light tended at dusk, supper after, and then the mantel, where the songbird sings its eleven notes while Jude follows along on paper, translating, and I check him, and he pretends to be offended when I catch an error, and I pretend not to enjoy it.

Twenty-two years of this. Some families have photographs. We have a ritual.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the night I arrived, which is almost nothing, and everything, and both versions are true:

I remember a boat. I remember a man's voice saying my name the way you say a word you're trying to memorize in a language you'll need later. I remember running — running is the oldest memory I have, older than language, older than grief; I think I was born running — and then a door, and wind, and sleep like falling into deep water.

And then: waking. A ceiling of dark beams. The smell of lamp oil and salt and something else, something I didn't have a word for yet but do now — machine oil and cedar, the smell of Elena Villalobos's workshop, which even then, abandoned and half-finished, had a presence in that house like a person. Like a third resident nobody mentioned at meals but everybody thought about.

Jude stood in the doorway holding a note in one hand and a pot of porridge in the other, and he looked at me the way I would learn he looks at broken machines: not with pity, exactly. With an appraiser's attention. Working out what was damaged, what was missing, whether the thing could be made to run again.

I did not speak for forty-one days. He never once asked me why. That is the whole of it, really, if you want to know why I stayed. Everyone else in my short life had demanded words from me as rent. Jude let me live in the house of my own silence until I chose to leave it.

The first word I ever said to him was *Papa*, and it wasn't to him — it was about my father, pointing, gesturing, leading him by the sleeve down into that smelling workshop to the bird on the shelf. But I have often thought since that the word did double duty without my intending it. I said *Papa* and meant Mateo Villanueva, smuggler of deserters, keeper of ledgers, dead man. And somewhere in the machinery of me, another meaning was already being machined, slowly, over years, with the patience of a tide-driven gear train.

* * *

The bird sings. Eleven notes. Jude's pen scratches.

"The fourth phrase is wrong," I say, from the doorway.

"It is not wrong. It is *interpretive*."

"You've added a note."

"I have *elided* a rest. There is a difference, and the difference is called musicianship."

I come and stand behind his chair and look over his shoulder at the translation sheet, the same sheet he has copied and recopied for twenty-two years until the original cipher is nearly worn through, and there it is: he has indeed elided a rest, and the rest matters, because the rests matter, because the rests are where Papa hid the names.

You should understand what the tune is. Not the plot of it — you will have read your histories, if you have read any, and the Villanueva Ledger is in them now, entered into record at the eleventh hour of the war by a magistrate named Oyarzun who arrived on a supply cutter in a storm and read aloud in a lighthouse kitchen with rain hammering the glass. Twelve officials. Three shipping magnates. One naval lieutenant whose name I will not write here because he has descendants who are, I am told, dull and respectable farmers in Galicia, and I wish them no harm. The ledger bought safe passage for deserters and refugees, and paid for it with bribes, and recorded every payment in a cipher my father designed himself, because Papa believed — this is in his letters, the ones Jude decoded one by one through that first winter — that a man who does illegal kindnesses owes the future an honest account of them. *Let them judge the whole of me,* he wrote. *Not the part that flatters.*

But beneath the ledger of bribes, in the rests between the phrases, Papa hid the other list. The names of the saved. Two hundred and eleven souls who came through this coast in the dark years, who slept in cellars and hay lofts and once, memorably, in a church confessional, and went on to live lives that would otherwise have ended in a trench or a courtyard at dawn.

The courts got the first list. We kept the second.

Some histories are published. Some are sung to a gilded bird at dusk by a man who is not your father and a woman who used to be unable to speak.

* * *

After the singing, tea. After tea, the argument, which is also a ritual.

"You should take the mainland post," I tell him. I don't mean it. I have said it every autumn for nine years, ever since the Trinity House board finally, grudgingly formalized my position and offered Jude a comfortable shore station in Bilbao with electric lights and no stairs.

"And abandon the finest tide-engine on this coast," he says, "to retire next to a fishmonger's?"

"There'd be no stairs."

"I would miss the stairs." He says it with dignity. "A man my age needs an adversary."

He is seventy-one. His hands, which once could feel a misaligned gear by listening to it, shake slightly now unless he is holding a tool, in which case they go mysteriously steady, as if tools were a kind of medicine. His hair went white the year we rebuilt — not rebuilt, he would correct me; *repurposed* — the workshop into a schoolroom, and he claims it was the smoke, and I claim it was me, and neither of us has ever won that argument, which is how we both like it.

Here is the thing about chosen family that the blood kind never tells you: it is built, joint by joint, the way Elena built her automata, and because it is built you know its workings, and because you know its workings you can maintain it. Love by design. Love with blueprints. When Jude and I quarrel — and we do, spectacularly, usually about whether the schoolroom children should be taught navigation before or after they can reliably not fall in the cistern — we quarrel like engineers, each of us able to point to the exact load-bearing element the other is neglecting. It is very hard to stay angry at someone when you can see their mechanism.

Elena I know only through her machines and her marginalia, but I love her too, in the way you love a grandmother who died before you could meet her and left you her hands. Because they are her hands, mine. I found that out the hard way, the winter Jude broke his wrist on the ice and I had to finish the escapement repair on the tide-engine alone, working from her notebooks, and my fingers knew things my mind didn't. She left drawings of me before I existed. I mean that almost literally — there is a sketch in the 1913 notebook of a small figure standing in the workshop doorway, seen from behind, and under it she wrote: *Someday someone will come through that door who belongs to the work.* Jude swears she was thinking of an apprentice. I have decided otherwise. Dead women are entitled to their mysteries, and living ones to their inventions.

* * *

The storm picks up around ten. Northwest gales here don't howl so much as lean, a sustained muscular pressure on the tower like a large patient animal testing a fence, and the old lens — cracked in '18 and mended by a Barcelona craftsman who charged us triple and apologized for it — throws its beam with a faint ticking asymmetry I can hear from the watch room, a sound like a clock with a secret.

Jude goes up for the midnight tending even though it's my night, because the barometer fell faster than he likes, and at his age and in this weather I have learned not to fight him for the stairs. Instead I climb halfway and sit on the landing with the lamp, the way I did when I was small and afraid he would fall, and listen to him move above me: the scrape of the burner tray, the murmur of him talking to the lens. He talks to it. He has always talked to it. He calls it *the widow's eye* and claims Elena named it that and I have never had the heart to verify this against the notebooks, because some stories deserve to remain unexamined if they are kind.

"Coral," he calls down, conversationally, as if we were across a kitchen table rather than thirty feet of iron stair. "Do you remember Harrow's face?"

This is our other ritual, the one we do not perform for audiences. Once a month or so, in the dark, one of us asks the other to remember something we could safely forget.

"Perfectly," I call back. "Grey coat. Wet braid. He counted the waves while he talked to you. Left to right, with his thumb."

"Left to right. Good." A pause, the sound of the wick being trimmed. "And mine? Do you remember mine? From that first morning."

"Porridge pot. Note in the other hand. You looked at me like I was a machine that might be worth fixing."

A laugh, echoing strangely down the stairwell. "I looked at you," he says, "like a man who had been keeping a light for nothing, and had just been informed of the cargo."

I sit with that a while, on the cold landing, listening to the sea work itself up below. People imagine grief as weather, and sometimes it is, but the older I get the more I think it is a ledger too — entries made in haste, in the rests, in the silences between the eleven notes. My father's face I barely remember; I have his hands and his cipher and two hundred and eleven reasons to forgive him everything. My mother I remember only as a shawl that smelled of oranges. Harrow I remember perfectly, and I have examined that memory the way Jude examines a gear, looking for wear, for motive, for the flaw that explains the failure, and I have concluded at last — this took me twenty years — that there is no flaw. He was simply a man who counted things and loved none of them. Some people are built that way. It is not a malfunction. It is the whole design.

What I cannot examine, because it has no parts, is the door. The door with the wind behind it and the note pinned to my coat. *Forget this face.* Whoever wrote it — Papa, I have always believed, though it hardly matters now — meant it as mercy, and it arrived as an erasure, and it became instead the founding document of my entire life. I have spent twenty-two years refusing the instruction. I remember faces. I keep them. I put them in the schoolroom register and the margins of navigation charts and, every dusk, into the eleven notes of a gilded bird that will go on singing after both of us are gone, because we have taught the mechanism to outlive its makers. Elena taught us that. Everything I know about permanence, I learned from a dead woman's workshop.

Above me, Jude finishes the tending. The beam steadies into its long patient sweep — dark, dark, flash, dark — and I hear him pause at the top of the stairs, and I know without looking that his hand is resting on the rail over the exact spot where, seven years ago, I painted a small brass plaque: *E.V.V., who built the light that keeps something.*

He descends slowly. At the landing he sits down beside me without comment, which is how we share most things, and for a while we just watch the beam go round.

"Peace talks again in the papers," he says at last. "Third one this year. They'll manage it eventually, and then some clerk will file the war away and that will be that."

"You sound disappointed."

"I sound," he says, "like a man thinking about deadlines." He turns the lamp down to its night level, and the shadows gather kindly. "Because I am. Coral — the magistrate's office wrote again. They want the bird."

The wind leans against the tower. The beam sweeps. Somewhere below us in the cellar, the tide turns, and the great weight begins its slow rise, and eleven notes up through the floorboards, faint as memory: the fourth phrase, rest elided, waiting to be corrected.

"They want to deposit it in the national archive," Jude says. "Glass case. Plaque. Very dignified."

"And what did you tell them?"

He is quiet for a moment, and in that moment I hear the whole architecture of him — the grief, the guilt, the debt of salt, the twenty-two years of dusk rituals — settle into place like a train of gears engaging.

"I told them," he says, "that we would give our answer in writing. By return boat." He looks at me sideways, and there is, unmistakably, the ghost of the appraiser's look from that first morning — working out whether the thing can be made to run. "Which means, my girl, that you and I have a letter to compose. And I warn you: I intend to argue that some histories are kept alive deliberately."

The bird sings. The light turns. I reach past him for the pen.
