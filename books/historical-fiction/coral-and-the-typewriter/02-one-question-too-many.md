## 2. One Question Too Many

The knock came at half past nine, which was the first wrong thing about him.

Sable knew the rhythms of Copperlane by their footfalls. The milk boy came at six, flat-footed and hurrying. Mrs. Pell from the chandlery knocked at seven sharp, wanting her tide tables. The rent collector thudded at month's end like a man who resented stairs. Nobody official climbed Fenwick's narrow stair at half past nine on a Tuesday morning — and nobody official knocked the way this one did: twice, evenly spaced, patient as a clock.

She was mid-line on the Kettering shoals, pen loaded with the good iron-gall ink she reserved for Aldous's atlas plates, and for one long second she simply did not move. The wet line gleamed up at her from the chart, a thread of black coastline that had no business existing on any legitimate survey. Beneath it, layered into the same plate in a lighter hand, ran the true work: depth soundings that weren't soundings, a channel marked in coral-pink that led nowhere a government vessel would ever think to look.

*Hide it. Always hide it.* Aldous's voice, three months dead and still the steadiest thing in her head.

She slid the shoals plate beneath the stack of drainage surveys for the Greyhaven Commission — dull as ditchwater, blessedly so — blotted her fingers, and opened the door.

He stood a step down from the landing, which put them nearly level, and she understood immediately that he had done that on purpose. A tall man makes himself smaller on a cramped stair; this man had instead chosen the step where neither of them would loom. Dark hair going grey at the temples early, a coat brushed but not new, and eyes the pale unremarkable brown of weak tea — except that they were doing something tea-colored eyes had no right to do, which was *counting*. She felt the count happen. Her shoulders. Her ink-stained fingers. The single lamp burning at ten in the morning.

"Miss Whitlock?" His voice was quiet, pitched for a small room rather than a stairwell. "Elias Vane, Office of Census and Enumeration." He produced a leather folio and opened it to show a stamp, an inked ribbon signature, a photograph of himself looking mildly bored. "I'm completing the household record for this address. May I come in? I'll be brief."

"You're three weeks late," Sable said. "The enumeration was done in spring. I answered every question a man with a clipboard asked me, and he had a cold."

"Corrick. Yes." Vane closed the folio without hurry. "Corrick's work is being audited. Whole districts of it. I'm redoing the addresses where his figures were—" he paused, choosing the word the way a surgeon chooses a blade, "—incomplete."

Something moved in her chest, slow and cold, like a fish turning under ice. *Incomplete.* Corrick had been sloppy, everyone said so. But audits didn't fall out of the sky. Audits were ordered.

"Then you'd better come in," she said, "before my ink dries and I forget to be hospitable."

* * *

The workshop swallowed him the way it swallowed everyone — in smells and clutter and the particular hush of a room built for close work. Aldous had bought the old sail-loft forty years ago and never once cleared anything out of it. Charts hung from the rafters like drying kelp. Three desks crowded the long window, each with its own lamp, its own constellation of weights and pens. The smell was linseed and cut paper and the faint salt ghost of the harbor two streets below.

Vane stopped just inside the door and let the room have him. She watched him take it in — not gawking, the way Maren's city cousins would have gawked, but reading. His gaze went to the hanging charts, then to the press in the corner, then to the floor, where decades of ink had stained the boards in overlapping continents.

"You worked for Master Fenwick," he said. Not quite a question.

"I'm his apprentice. Was." She set herself behind the middle desk, the survey desk, and picked up her pen as if she'd merely paused to answer the door. "Since I was fourteen."

"And since his death?"

"The commission work continues. Someone has to finish the drainage surveys or Greyhaven floods in its own ambition." She touched the top sheet without looking at it. "You'll find me listed under his household already. Apprentice, resident, unmarried. Corrick wrote it all down while sneezing on the ledger, if you want to copy it out again."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. It might have been the beginning of a smile that thought better of itself. "You don't care for the census."

"I don't care for repetition, Mr. Vane. Ask your questions. The ones I haven't answered."

He opened the folio on the corner of her desk — after a glance asking permission, which she granted with a tilt of her chin, which felt like a move in a game she hadn't agreed to play. He wrote her name, her age, her occupation. His handwriting was small and exact, upright, without flourish. Then he said, pleasantly, as though it were nothing:

"Master Fenwick kept apprentices before you, I believe. Where did they go?"

The pen in her hand did not pause, because she had trained it not to, because Aldous had spent nine years beating hesitation out of her line. But some other part of her — the part that had counted footfalls on stairs since the night the coroner called it an accident — went very still.

"One went to the Admiralty. One married a printer in Corvane and got fat. One drowned off the Shelf, poor devil, and one decided charts were a young person's trade and took over his father's bakery." She looked up. "Do you need their names and heights too?"

"Names would do." He wrote as she gave them, unhurried. "And Fenwick himself. Did he travel much?"

"He was a mapmaker. Traveling was the trade."

"Recently, though. In the last two years."

"Down the coast. Up the coast. Once to the islands to correct the reef plates." *Stop,* she told herself. *Answer what's asked. Volunteering is bleeding.*

"Alone, on those trips?"

"No. With me, until my sea legs failed him. Then with a hired deckhand." She let a beat pass, and put the hook out carefully, the way you test ice. "Why does the census care whether a dead cartographer liked boats?"

Vane capped his pen. For a moment the only sound in the workshop was the harbor — a gull, the far groan of a crane, water slapping pilings with the patience of things that outlast everyone.

"The Office cares about accuracy," he said. "Corrick's returns for this quarter show eleven residents in this building's tax record and nine in his count. Discrepancies attract attention. Attention generates follow-up visits." He rose, straightened his coat, and looked around the room one more time — and this time she saw him look *for* something, the pretense of general appreciation falling away for perhaps two seconds, his eyes moving along the wall where the chart presses stood, measuring them.

Two seconds. Then the courteous mask returned, seamless, and he inclined his head. "Thank you for your time, Miss Whitlock. I'll note the household as complete."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and turned halfway back.

"One more thing. Purely clerical." His voice stayed mild as milk. "Fenwick was working on something when he died. There's mention in the Guild registry of a commissioned atlas — coastal series. Do you know where it went?"

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. She kept her seat. Nine years of Aldous Fenwick's discipline lived in her spine, and every one of them said the same thing: *a lie is a chart, and a chart is only as good as its calmest line.*

"Incomplete," she said. "Plates scattered across commissions. The Guild can have whatever's finished when the drainage surveys are done, and welcome to it. Dead men's atlases don't pay apprentice wages."

Vane considered her. Under that weak-tea gaze she felt catalogued, cross-referenced, entered into some private ledger of his own in that small upright hand — and beneath the feeling of it, unwelcome as damp in a wall, something else stirred. He was, she thought furiously, exactly the sort of man who would bring you soup correctly salted and ask afterward what you'd hidden in it.

"Of course," he said. "Incomplete. Most things are." And he was gone, his footsteps descending the stair at an even, patient pace, twice-knocked rhythm all the way down.

* * *

She gave him four minutes. Long enough to reach Copperlane Corner; short enough that he wouldn't have done anything clever with the time. Then she crossed the workshop, threw both bolts, and stood with her forehead against the door's cool grain.

*Liar,* she told herself. *Smooth liar. Good liar.* The atlas wasn't scattered. The atlas was thirty-one finished plates sewn into a false binding of drainage surveys, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping inside the false bottom of Aldous's chart press not nine feet from where Elias Vane had stood measuring the presses with his eyes.

Thirty-one plates. Escape routes drawn into coastlines for who-knew-how-many families whose names she didn't yet know, in an atlas her master had died protecting — died, whatever the coroner's verdict said about a wet stair and a weak heart, because Aldous Fenwick had climbed those stairs sober every night for thirty years and knew every warped board by feel.

And now the census knew the atlas existed.

No — not the census. Some audit-shaped thing wearing the census's clothes, sending out men like Vane: courteous, precise, quiet-voiced, with eyes that counted presses. Men who chose which step to stand on. Men who asked about travel habits the way a pickpocket asks the time.

She went back to the desk, took up the Kettering shoals plate, uncovered it, and looked at the coral-pink channel running through it like an artery. Her hands, she noticed, were steady. That frightened her more than the visit had — that the lying came this easily now, that grief and secrecy had fused somewhere along the way into a single steady alloy.

*He'll be back,* she thought. Men like that always came back, because the first visit was never the inquiry, only the casting of the mold.

And underneath that thought, in a corner of her mind she resolved to ignore, another one sat quietly insubordinate: *he had kind lines around his eyes when he thought no one was checking.*

She pulled the lamp closer and inked the shoals.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour, and somewhere between here and Copperlane Corner, Elias Vane was writing down everything she had said — and, she suspected with a sensation like a splinter working inward, several things she hadn't.
