## 3. The Ledger Under the Floor

Three months of dust had settled into the grooves of Aldous Fenwick's workbench, and for three months I had let it lie.

That was deliberate. Grief has its own cartography, and mine required certain regions remain untouched — his chair pushed in at its exact angle, his reading spectacles folded on the blotter where he'd left them the night he went into the water, his tin of peppermints still three-quarters full because he always said sweets were for visitors and I was never a visitor. To sweep the bench would be to admit he wasn't coming back to make a mess of it again. So I worked around the dust the way a river works around a stone, inking my plates by daylight at the window and leaving his corner of the room exactly as death had arranged it.

But tonight I was going to disturb it.

I had told myself it was practicality. The census man's visit had lodged under my skin like a splinter too deep to draw out with the fingers. *Did Mr. Fenwick keep papers beyond his survey work? Did he receive visitors from up the coast? Would you say he traveled often, Miss Quintana?* Each question had been dressed so neatly in civility that I hadn't felt the blade until afterward, lying awake in the loft, replaying the conversation stitch by stitch. A census taker counts heads. He does not ask whether the dead man kept a second set of books.

Unless someone sent him to ask.

I lit two lamps instead of one, and I pulled Aldous's chair out from the bench, and I sat in it. The wood was cold. It creaked under me with a sound like an old man clearing his throat, and I had to press my palms flat against the desk and breathe until the room stopped swimming.

"All right," I said aloud, to no one, to him. "I'm sorry. But I'm going through everything."

I started with what was visible, because that was how Aldous himself had taught me to search a coastline: the obvious features first, then the subtle ones, then the ones that only reveal themselves when the light comes from the wrong direction. His survey folios, all accounted for, all delivered to the Harbor Commission before his death — or nearly all. There was a gap in the sequence, folio forty-one through forty-four, and I remembered now, with the particular lurch of noticing something you'd chosen not to notice, that those folios had been on this very bench the week he died. The Commission's clerk had asked after them. Aldous had promised delivery. Then there had been the storm, the slipway, the inquest's tidy verdict of *accidental drowning*, and no one at the Commission had pressed a dead man's apprentice for missing paperwork.

Someone else, evidently, had been pressing.

I emptied drawer after drawer onto the floor in careful stacks — nibs and burnishers, jars of pigment ground fine as flour, bundles of correspondence that were exactly as dull as correspondence ought to be: ink orders, guild dues, a politely furious letter about an unpaid invoice from a paper merchant in Corvane's Row. Two hours in, my knees ached from kneeling and my hands were black to the wrist, and I had found nothing but the ordinary sediment of a meticulous man's life.

It was the peppermint tin that undid me.

I knocked it reaching beneath the bench's lower shelf, and it rang wrong — not the thin rattle of mints against tin but a dense, muffled clunk, the sound of a thing sitting inside another thing. I pried off the lid. The mints had been replaced with a lump of beeswax, and pressed into the wax, face down, was a small brass key stamped with no maker's mark at all, only a number: **9**.

Nine. I turned it over in my fingers and looked, really looked, at the floorboards for the first time in six years of working in this room. Aldous had taught me to read coastlines; he had also taught me, without ever saying it plainly, that every honest surface conceals a dishonest structure beneath it. There were nine boards between the bench leg and the wall. The fourth was scuffed differently from its sisters — not newer, but *handled*, its edges worn round in a way that came from fingers lifting, not boots crossing.

The key fit a lock recessed flush into the board itself. When I lifted it, the smell came up first: oilcloth and camphor and something older, the mineral breath of a cellar that hadn't been opened in years.

Underneath lay a box wrapped in oilcloth and tied with sailmaker's twine, and beneath the box, a second package, flat and heavy, wrapped the same way. I took both out and set them on the bench where the spectacles still waited for their owner, and I cut the twine with the same knife I used to trim plate edges, and I did not let myself think about anything at all while I did it, because thinking would have stopped my hands.

The flat package was a ledger. Bound in grey calf, unmarked spine, the kind of book a shipping agent uses for manifests or a pawnbroker for pledges. I opened it.

The first page defeated me entirely.

Columns of figures marched down the paper in Aldous's small upright hand — but they weren't figures. They were words, or fragments of words, compressed into a cipher I didn't recognize: numerals standing in for letters, letters standing in for other letters, dots and ticks riding above the line like birds on a wire. Beside each entry, in the margin, a reference code in red: *K-7/3. D-2/11. M-19/1.*

I knew that referencing system.

Every mapmaker does. It was how we keyed marginal notes to plates — the letter for the chart sheet, the numbers for grid position. K-7 meant plate seven of the northern coastal series. Third grid square. These codes weren't decoration. They were pointing somewhere. They were pointing *into the atlas*.

I sat back, and the chair creaked its old-man creak, and the lamp flame leaned in the draft from under the door as if it too were trying to read over my shoulder.

*Did Mr. Fenwick keep papers beyond his survey work?*

"Apparently," I whispered, "he did."

* * *

The cipher resisted me for the better part of an hour, and then it gave way all at once, the way a lock gives way — not gradually but in a single humiliating click, once I stopped looking for cleverness and remembered who had built it.

Aldous Fenwick was the most patient man I ever knew and the laziest cryptographer imaginable, because he believed, correctly, that the best hiding place for a key is in plain sight among ten thousand things that look identical. His cipher was built on his own tide tables. Every numeral stood for a word by its position in the year's printed tables — the height of the water at Greyhaven on such-and-such a date, reduced by a rule we both knew by heart. Anyone who stole the ledger would find a book of numbers. Only someone who had spent four years copying tide heights into fair hand would look at a column of figures and think, *wait. I've written these before.*

He'd hidden the key inside my own apprenticeship. I almost laughed. I almost did other things instead, but the laugh caught in my throat and turned into something colder as the columns began to resolve.

Because they resolved into names.

Not all at once — first into phrases, then into places, and then, with terrible inevitability, into people. *Harrow Street, third house past the chandlery.* *The Voss family, six souls.* *Fisherman's Walk, top floor, the widow Ansel.* Line after line, entry after entry, page after page, and beside each one the red marginal code tying the entry to a specific square of a specific coastal plate — the very plates I had spent three months inking in secret, the very margins whose faint pencil annotations I had assumed were surveyor's shorthand, depth soundings, tidal notes.

They were not soundings.

I pulled the finished plates from their hiding place behind the legitimate surveys and laid them side by side on the floor, kneeling over them like a woman kneeling over a body, and I read the margins against the ledger, and the atlas opened itself to me like a flower I had been watering without knowing what it was.

In plate seven, grid square three, the pencil note read: *cove dries 0400–0730, path to cliff road, sheltered from N watchtower.* In the ledger, beside *Voss, six souls,* the code K-7/3. In plate eleven, grid square two: *old lime kiln, door seaward, holds eight dry at spring tide.* Ledger: *Ansel, widow, plus dependents.* Chart after chart. Cove after cave after ruined pier after stretch of shingle passable only at low water and only invisible from the harbor patrol's line of sight. Every mark I had lovingly inked over, believing I was preserving a surveyor's observations, was a door. The atlas wasn't an atlas.

It was a timetable of vanishings.

I put my hand flat on the page to stop it from trembling and felt, absurdly, as though the paper were trembling under my palm instead, as though the whole atlas were a living thing whose purpose I had finally understood and could not unknow. This was why the plates ran north to south in an order that made no commercial sense. This was why he'd refused the Commission's request to standardize the scale. This was why he worked late with the shutters closed and burned his drafts and smiled that particular closed-door smile when clients admired his coastlines. He wasn't mapping the shore.

He was mapping the way *off* it.

And the ledger gave the maps their passengers. Families. Names, ages, occupations, addresses — all rendered in cipher, all tagged with a departure code that I worked out within minutes because it followed the same tide-table logic: a date, and a landing point, and a vessel designation that was usually nothing more than a single initial. *M.* *C.* Once, chillingly, just a question mark.

Resettlement. The word had begun appearing in the gazette six months ago, smooth as varnish — a administrative program, the articles said, relocating undocumented coastal laborers to inland work settlements for their own protection, for orderly industry, for the general good. The articles never said where. No letters came back from anyone relocated. I had read the notices the way one reads weather reports about distant counties, with the mild sympathy of a person for whom the weather is currently fine.

Aldous had not read them that way. Aldous had read them the way a man reads a list of the condemned, and then he had gone quietly to work with his charts and his tides and whatever network of shipmasters and harbor men shared his single-letter initials, and for God knows how many years he had been walking families down dark beaches at low tide and putting them aboard boats that officially carried ballast and salt fish.

And three months ago he had drowned, alone, at night, on a slipway he had walked a thousand times, in conditions the inquest called treacherous but which I now remembered — with the awful clarity of a memory being re-shelved — had been calm. Flat calm. I had said so at the time. I had been a grieving girl of twenty, and a coroner had looked at me over his spectacles and explained about undertow, and I had let myself be explained to.

Nobody slips on a calm night carrying folios forty-one through forty-four to a rendezvous with a shipmaster whose initial is probably inked somewhere in this ledger.

They killed him for paper. That was the thought that arrived last and stayed longest, sitting in my chest like swallowed stone. Not for gold, not for treason in any shape the law could name — for *paper*. For ink on calfskin. Because somewhere in this city was a man — or a bureau, or a ministry with a rubber stamp and a filing cabinet full of quiet little lists — who understood exactly what Aldous's beautiful coastlines meant, and who wanted the atlas whole and the atlas-maker at the bottom of the harbor, in that order. And whoever that was had already come asking once, wearing a census taker's coat and a courteous smile, and I had lied to him competently and then lain awake admiring his hands.

I laughed then, properly, one short ugly syllable of it that startled the lamplight. Grief and horror and fury all trying to exit through the same narrow doorway.

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there among the scattered plates. Long enough that the first lamp burnt its wick crooked and began to smoke; long enough that the sounds of the street died away below and left the workshop in that particular harbor-city silence where you can hear the water slapping pilings six streets off. I relit, retrimmed, and forced myself to method, because method was the only prayer I'd ever been taught.

If the atlas was a timetable, then the ledger was a register of souls, and registers have arithmetic. I counted. I was trained to count — it is half of what mapmaking is, everything reduced to measure — and so I counted, entering each name in a fresh column in my own notebook because some instinct older than sense told me the ledger must not leave this room again, and neither must its contents.

One hundred and forty-three entries in Aldous's hand, spanning eleven years.

Forty-one entries in a second hand — smaller, tighter, the letters pinched — beginning fourteen months ago and stopping abruptly mid-page, three weeks before the drowning. Someone had been helping him. Or someone had been keeping their own parallel record, and the two records had met here in this box, and I had no way of knowing which hand belonged to friend or traitor. The pinch-written entries stopped at the same date as Aldous's life. Either his partner had fled, or his partner had reported him, and the ledger offered no testimony either way. Paper is honest about measurements and silent about everything that matters.

And the purge was accelerating. Eleven years, ninety-one departures in the first decade of entries. Fifty-two in the final fourteen months. Whatever the resettlement program called itself in the gazettes, on the ground it had grown teeth, and the teeth were growing faster. At this rate — I did the arithmetic twice because I did not want the answer either time — at this rate the harbor would be stripped of every family on these pages within the year. The program wouldn't slow down. Programs like this never slow down. They only run out of names.

Which meant the atlas couldn't stay hidden behind my survey maps like a guilty pleasure. It was ammunition, or a lifeboat, or a death warrant, depending entirely on whose hands held it — and there existed, as of tonight, precisely one census taker's worth of evidence that someone official already suspected it existed.

I gathered the plates, squared their edges, and slid them back into their hiding slot. The ledger I wrapped last, rewinding the sailmaker's twine with hands that had steadied themselves by pure stubbornness. Then I lifted the false board to return the box—

—and stopped.

There was writing on the underside of the board. Of course there was; Aldous never trusted a hiding place he couldn't verify, and what better place for a verification mark than a surface no thief would think to turn over? I angled the lamp flame toward it and read the small pencil line, and it was nothing, it was almost nothing, just a tally and a name:

*Nine boards. Fourth from the wall. — B.Q.*

B.Q. Beatrix Quintana. My father's initials, scratched in his own cramped hand, in a workshop he had never, to my knowledge, entered — or rather, in a workshop he had entered long before I was born, when the apprentice who would inherit it was a stranger named Aldous Fenwick and my father was young enough to help a friend hide a floorboard. My father the harbor pilot, who drowned twelve years ago and whose funeral my brother and I had stood at in silence because we had no other language left between us. My father, who had helped build the first plank of this. Who had perhaps helped build the first route.

The atlas was older than Aldous's mercy. It was older than his death. It was a inheritance passed hand to hand like a coal kept alive in ash, and I was not the second keeper of it, nor even the third.

I sat back on my heels in the middle of the wrecked, lamplit room, surrounded by the ordinary debris of an extraordinary man's days, holding a floorboard with my dead father's handwriting on it, and I understood that there was no version of my life after tonight that did not include this ledger. I could burn it. I could deliver it to the Commission and wash my hands. I could do what Aldous had done, which was neither — which was to keep counting, keep inking, keep walking people down beaches in the dark until the dark walked him down instead.

The lamp gutted. Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it in the changed voice of the water under the pilings, the long suck and shift of the harbor exhaling.

One hundred and eighty-four names. One hundred and eighty-four families' worth of souls folded into cipher and coastline. I picked up my notebook, turned to a clean page, and began cross-referencing the entries against the second hand's additions, name by name, address by address — because if the pinch-written hand was a traitor's, I needed to know which names had been given away; and if it was a friend's, those names might still need saving.

I got as far as the ninth entry on the thirty-first page before my own hand stopped moving.

The entry was in the pinched script. The address was Fisherman's Walk. The occupation was listed as *dock laborer, night shift, Corvane's Row quays.* And the surname, rendered in the tide-table cipher I now read as easily as print, was one I had spent six years teaching myself never to think of.

*Whitlock.*
