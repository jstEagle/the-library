## 4. A Name She Knew

The ledger had been waiting for her.

Sable understood that now, three nights into the work of reading it. It sat beneath the floorboard by the hearth where Aldous Fenwick had kept his tea tin and his spare nibs, and once she had pried the board loose — a trick of leverage with the flat of a chisel, done in the dark so the neighbors would not hear carpentry — she had found it wrapped in oilcloth exactly the way he wrapped plates he did not want ruined. He had treated this book like a finished chart. Like something worth delivering.

She had not opened it immediately. That was the part she would remember later with a kind of shame: she had made herself tea first. She had trimmed the lamp to a steady yellow, arranged her blotting paper, wet her pen out of pure habit though she had no intention of writing anything. She had prepared herself to *study*, because studying was what Aldous had taught her, and studying felt safer than whatever the other thing was — the thing where you simply looked at what was in front of you and let it change you.

Then she had opened the ledger, and the other thing had begun, and it had not stopped since.

* * *

The book itself was unremarkable, which was its genius. Bound in scuffed calf, its spine cracked from use, it looked like any shipping agent's daybook: columns of tonnage, berth counts, tide tables copied in Aldous's small upright hand. A customs man could have flipped through it front to back and found nothing but a fussy old mapmaker's fascination with harbor logistics.

It was the margins that held it.

In the gutters of each page, in a hand so faint it might have been ghost-writing, ran names. Not all the pages. Only those whose charts carried the markings Sable had spent two weeks decoding — the coral-pink hatching along certain stretches of coast, the lighthouse symbols drawn one degree off true north, the depth soundings that read as nonsense until you realized they were letters, each fathom a position in the alphabet. The marked charts were escape routes. She had proven that to herself beyond doubting on the second night, tracing the pink-hatched coastline from Greyhaven south to a cove called Merrow's Teeth that appeared on no official survey at all, and from there across sixty miles of open water to an inlet in the free islands where no extradition treaty reached.

Now, on the third night, the ledger gave the routes their passengers.

Each name in the margin corresponded to a berth entry in the main column — a cargo weight that matched no cargo, a passenger count that never appeared on manifests. Aldous had hidden people inside arithmetic. Eleven names on the first signature of pages. Fourteen on the second. She counted them the way you count wounds, without wanting to and without being able to stop: forty-one names in all, each followed by a date, each date falling after the government's Resettlement Act came into force, each berth booked on a ship whose captain owed Aldous Fenwick some unrecorded debt.

And beside every name, in the final column, a small notation. Most read *cleared* or *sailed*. Some read *held*. Three, crossed through with a single line so neat it might have been correcting an error in a tide table, read *taken*.

Sable had sat very still for a long time when she found the first of those. *Taken.* The word did its work quietly, like water finding a seam in stone. Aldous had not died accidentally, falling from his own sea wall in a fog he'd walked that path beside for thirty years. He had died protecting paper — and the paper was worth killing for, which meant somewhere there were men who knew what it contained, and men who knew meant men who would come looking for the rest of it. For the atlas. For the hands that had touched it.

For her.

She had blown out the lamp then and sat in the dark with her heart going like a dockside hammer, listening to the rain on the workshop roof, and she had thought, with terrible clarity: *He knew. He wrapped this in oilcloth and put it under the floor by the fire because that is where I sit when I do the ledgers. He left it where my hands would find it.*

* * *

By the fourth night she had stopped flinching at every sound and started working properly. Fear, Aldous used to say, is a current; you do not fight a current, you read it and set your sails. So she read. She built herself a system: the ledger open on the drawing table, the atlas's marked plates fanned beside it, her own notebook — coded in the surveyor's shorthand Aldous had taught her, innocent-looking as weather observations — recording everything.

Cross-referencing took hours. Each marginal name had to be matched to a route, each route to a sailing date, each date checked against tide tables and harbor schedules to see whether the crossing Aldous had arranged had actually happened. She was halfway through the third signature when her pen stopped moving.

*Whitlock, Thomas. Dockworker's row, berth 6.*

She read it the way you read your own name on a gravestone — recognizing every letter, refusing the whole.

The ink had gone brown with age at the edges of the strokes; the entry was old, among the first Aldous had recorded. Beside it, in the final column: *held*. And beneath that, added later in slightly different ink, a single line that made the room tilt: *Corvane — 14th sailing. One place.*

Tom.

Her brother's name sat in the gutter of page nineteen like a splinter working toward the bone. She stared at it and the workshop receded — the smell of linseed and salt, the tick of the barometer, all of it gone — and she was eleven years old again in a narrow house on Dockworker's Row, watching Tom, who was sixteen and already had their father's shoulders, stand in the doorway with a duffel bag and say the words that had divided her life into before and after:

*"You stay here polishing his maps, then. Stay here being his good little student. I'm done begging for scraps off a mapmaker's table."*

Their father had been a chart-maker too — a lesser one, a copyist who never got the coastal commissions, who drank the disappointment down in pint measures and died of it at fifty-one with an unfinished estuary survey pinned to his board. Aldous Fenwick had taken Sable on as an apprentice within the month, had seen in her the talent her father had only talked about having. Tom had gotten nothing but the shoulders and the anger, and he had gone to the docks, where strength converted directly into wages, and he had not come to the funeral.

No. That wasn't true. She corrected herself now, ruthlessly, the way Aldous corrected a misdrawn headland. Tom had come to the funeral. He had stood at the back in a coat too thin for the weather, and when it was over and she had turned to look for him — wanting, God help her, wanting despite everything — he had already gone. Just the flattened grass where he'd stood. She had decided that meant he didn't care. It had been easier than deciding it meant he cared and couldn't say so, because if it meant that, then the silence of six years was hers as much as his, and she had built too much of her life on the clean story of the brother who abandoned her to give it up.

*Held,* said the ledger. *One place on the Corvane.*

Aldous had known about Tom. Had known and done something — booked him a berth, held it, waited. Waited for what? For Tom to come asking? Tom would never ask. Or — and the thought arrived cold and complete, like a sounding line touching bottom — Aldous had waited for *her*. For whoever inherited the ledger. For the apprentice he trusted to finish what the routes were for.

He hadn't just left her the atlas. He had left her the people in its margins.

Sable put down her pen very carefully, because her hand wanted to shake and shaking hands smudge ink, and that was the first rule of the craft, learned at her father's table and confirmed at Aldous's: whatever happens in you, the line stays true.

* * *

She should have gone to bed. Instead she pulled the atlas's plate for the southern approaches and laid it under the lamp, and there it was in the pink hatching and the falsified soundings: the Corvane's route, dated by tide table to the fourteenth of the coming month. Nineteen days out. One berth.

Nineteen days. She began doing sums before she could stop herself — the arithmetic of panic, which always divides by less than you need. Time to find Tom, who lived somewhere on Dockworker's Row and worked shifts she didn't know and might not welcome her. Time to convince a proud, angry man that a stranger's ledger had sentenced him and saved him in the same stroke. Time to forge papers — and she had never forged anything in her life, only heard Aldous describe the techniques with a craftsman's relish while insisting he'd never used them himself, which she now understood to be one of the two or three lies he'd told her, and forgivable, and useless. Time to alter the atlas's final page so that after the Corvane sailed, the route it followed would cease to exist anywhere in the world except memory.

Time to become, in short, the person Aldous Fenwick must have believed she already was.

She laughed once, short and ugly, at the presumption of a dead man's faith, and then she pressed her palm flat against the ledger, over her brother's name, and the laugh turned into something else in her throat and stayed there.

Because here was the thing she could not get around, the reef beneath all her smooth reasoning: part of her had been glad when Tom disappeared from her life. Part of her had found his absence restful, like a cleared workspace. He had stood for everything she'd refused to become — the grievance, the pride that ate itself, the fists-first answer to a world that required finer tools — and every year of her apprenticeship had been, in some small quiet way, a year of not being Tom. She had chosen ink over blood. She had chosen it at seventeen and reconfirmed the choice at every funeral and festival since, and now a dead man's handwriting had overturned the choice in a single brown-inked line, and she discovered that underneath six years of settled resentment, the older thing was still there, intact, patient, monstrous in its simplicity:

*He is my brother. Whatever he is, whoever he has decided to be, he is the boy who taught me to swim off Merrow's Point and swore me to secrecy when he stole pastries from Kessel's cart, and he is going to be taken — held, removed, resettled, whatever word they are using this season for making people vanish — and I am the only person in Greyhaven holding the paper that says otherwise.*

Guilt and urgency arrived together, indistinguishable, twin tides. Guilt for the gladness. Urgency because *held* meant still here, still reachable, still saveable — but only barely, only if she moved.

* * *

She dressed for the docks before dawn, in clothes she hadn't worn since her father's burial: a plain grey coat, boots with tarred seams, her hair bound up under a working cap. In the mirror of Aldous's hall stand she saw a stranger, and approved. A mapmaker's apprentice walking into Dockworker's Row would draw eyes. This woman — pale, tired, muffled to the chin — drew none.

The fog was coming in off the water in long grey ropes as she crossed the lower town, past the fish market setting up in the blue half-light, past the customs house where the lamps still burned. She kept to the seaward side of the street out of habit and then understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the morning, that the habit itself was the danger: anyone watching the workshop would know her walk, her route, her times. She forced herself to vary course, ducking down a cut between warehouses that stank of tar and rotting kelp, and came out on the dock road from the wrong direction, heart hammering, feeling foolish and hunted both.

Dockworker's Row rose ahead of her — two storeys of salt-stained brick leaning together over a street the sun rarely reached, washing lines strung like rigging between the upper windows, children already running the gutters. Berth 6 would have a green door. She remembered the green door. She had thrown a snowball at it when she was nine and Tom had laughed until he choked.

She stopped at the corner of the Row and let herself look, really look, the way Aldous had taught her to look at a coastline — not for what she hoped to see, but for what was there. There: a woman beating a rug over a rail. An old man coiling rope on a step. And at berth 6, the green door repainted a darker green sometime in the years since, a young woman emerging with a jug, presumably for milk, presumably someone's wife now, someone's lodger —

It didn't matter. What mattered was the shift whistle she could hear beginning far off at the eastern quays, and the men already moving toward it in twos and threes with their collars up, and somewhere among them, loading cargo in the dark before dawn because the dark was when the cheap ships came in, a broad-shouldered man with their father's jaw who did not know yet that his name was written in a dead cartographer's book, marked *held*, held for her.

Sable pulled the cap lower and started down the Row, her boots loud on the cobbles, her mind drafting and discarding openings — *Tom, it's Sable; Tom, I need to talk to you; Tom, you're in danger* — knowing that whatever she finally said, the truth would have to come out sideways, carefully, because you could not tell a man who hated maps that a map had come to save him.

Not unless you told him the rest of it first. Not unless you told him about the coral lines and the false soundings and the forty-one names, and about the old man who had known her brother's name without ever meeting him, and about the single berth riding south on the Corvane in nineteen days' time.

The green door was ten steps away. Behind it, faintly, someone was whistling — a dockside tune, tuneless and cheerful, and the sound went through her like a sounding weight dropping thirty years in a moment, because their father used to whistle exactly that badly, and apparently it ran in the blood along with the shoulders and the anger and whatever stubbornness kept a man living six doors from the sister he'd renounced.

Sable raised her hand to knock.
