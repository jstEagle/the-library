## 5. The Harbor at Low Tide

The tide went out at eleven and took the smell of the harbor with it, leaving behind the honest stink of mud and tar and old fish. Tom Whitlock had learned to tell time by that smell. Day shift men needed their pocket watches; night men needed only their noses.

He hoisted another crate of cured herring onto his shoulder and carried it up the gangplank of the *Merrivale*, a coaster bound for the southern ports with a hold full of Greyhaven's fish and Greyhaven's lies. The crate weighed as much as a man. He let it. The weight was simple, and simplicity was what he wanted tonight, because the new foreman was walking the quay again with his little book and his little questions.

"Name?" The foreman had asked each of them on the first night, three weeks ago, when he'd come down from the Port Authority offices in his pressed coat with two harbor constables trailing him like gulls after a trawler.

"Whitlock," Tom had said. "Thomas."

"Born?"

"Greyhaven."

That much was true. Everything else he'd learned to make dull. He worked the herring run because herring paid by the crate and herring didn't care where a man's father came from. He lodged above the chandlery on Rope Walk because the landlady asked no questions and the rent was honest. He drank at the Anchor & Chain on Sundays because that was where dockworkers drank, and a man who drank nowhere was a man somebody wondered about.

Keep it dull. That was the whole art now. His father had taught him charts and coastlines, had shown him how to read the water off the point at Marrow Head, how the channel bent east of the shoal like a snake crossing a road. His sister had inherited all of it — the fine hand, the patient eye, the way their father could make ink sit up and behave. Tom had inherited the shoulders and the temper, and he'd left home at nineteen with both, and he told himself every day that this was a fair trade.

"Whitlock." The foreman's voice cut across the deck. "You're stacking those crooked. The mate'll have you restow the lot."

Tom looked at the stack. It wasn't crooked. It was the straightest work on the ship, and everyone on the quay knew it, including the foreman, whose name was Pell and whose hands had never held anything heavier than a pen.

"Aye," Tom said, and kept loading.

That was another thing he'd learned. Aye. Yes, sir. No trouble here. The words were cheap and they bought passage through a shift. Pell watched him a moment longer, wrote something in his book anyway — he always wrote something, whether there was cause or not; the writing itself was the message — and moved on down the quay toward the coal heap where the Orlan men worked and never met anyone's eyes.

Tom set the crate down in the hold and stood in the dark a moment, letting his breath come back to him. The lamp above the hatch swung with the ship's gentle roll, throwing shadows that climbed the walls like slow animals. Down here it smelled of brine and pitch and the ghost of whatever cargo had come before. He liked the hold. You couldn't be seen from the hold. That was worth more than wages these days.

Because Pell's little book wasn't the only one. Three weeks of new foremen, new constables, new clerks who wanted birthplaces and papers — papers that most working men along this coast had never needed in their lives, papers their grandfathers hadn't needed. And it wasn't just Greyhaven. The lads from the northern yards said the same thing was happening in Harwick and Cold Harbour, census men going door to door with their columns and their ink, counting people like crates, marking some crates differently than others.

*Resettlement.* That was the word the gazette used, printed small and wrapped around nothing, as if the word itself explained why the Kessler family's shop on Cordwain Street stood empty with the sign still hanging, or why nobody at the Anchor & Chain mentioned the Odum brothers anymore, or why their table by the window stayed vacant long enough that someone finally moved it against the wall.

Tom didn't talk about it. Talking about it was how you got written into a book. But he thought about it, down in holds and up on decks and lying awake above the chandlery listening to the tide turn, and what he thought was: they are making lists, and one day a list will have my name on it, and no amount of straight-stacked herring will take it off.

His father would have known what to do. Old Whitlock, who'd been a surveyor before the drink took him, who could walk a shoreline once and draw it from memory after. Who'd died two years ago with his sons barely speaking to each other across the grave, Sable in her black coat on one side and Tom on the other, and neither of them saying the thing that needed saying, which was *I'm sorry* and also *it was your fault*, both at once, in the language of silence that siblings perfect.

And Fenwick. Aldous Fenwick, his father's old friend, the mapmaker with the workshop up on Chandler's Lane, who'd given Tom an apple every time he'd come by as a boy and told him that a coastline was just a promise the land made to the sea. Tom had heard Fenwick was dead. Fallen, they said. Down a stair. Three months back.

Tom had heard that, too, about staircases: men who spent their lives among dangerous ledgers didn't usually die on stairs.

He climbed back into the light and took the next crate.

* * *

It was past two when he saw her.

The *Merrivale* was half-loaded and the crew had broken for bread and tea on the quay, and Tom stood at the rail with his hands around a tin cup, watching the low tide expose the flats below the pier — the black ribs of an old wreck showing through the mud like something surfacing after a long drowning. He liked low tide for exactly that reason. It showed you what was always there underneath.

Then he looked up, and she was there.

Sable stood at the far end of the pier, near the bollards, in a grey coat with her collar up against the offshore wind. She was pretending to watch the wreck. She was doing it well, too — the stillness, the idle gaze, the posture of a woman pausing on an evening walk — but Tom had grown up beside that stillness, had seen it a thousand times over a drafting table, and he knew what it looked like when Sable Whitlock was concentrating on not being noticed. She leaned slightly away from the wind so her hair wouldn't move. Ordinary people didn't do that.

His first feeling was anger, clean and familiar, rising like water into a lock. Four years since their father's funeral. Four years since she'd stood in their father's kitchen and told him — told *him*, the son who'd stayed in Greyhaven while she went off to apprentice in comfort — that he ought to come round more, that their father had asked about him, as if guilt were a debt collectors could call on at any hour. As if Tom were the one who'd gone missing. He'd said things that night he'd meant and didn't regret, and she'd said things she probably hadn't meant and should have, and then she'd walked out of his life as neatly as she walked out of everything, without looking back, because looking back was for people without somewhere better to go.

So the anger came first. The anger always came first, and he was grateful for it, because behind it came everything else, and everything else was fear.

Because she wasn't there by chance. Sable Whitlock did nothing by chance; she plotted her walks the way she plotted coastlines, with soundings. And she was standing at the end of a pier watching the night shift of the herring fleet, at a time when foremen were keeping books on men's birthplaces, at a time when Fenwick had fallen down a staircase — Fenwick, whose workshop she'd vanished into as an apprentice, and if half the rumors on the quay were true, never really left.

She knew something. Or she feared something. Either way she was standing forty yards from him in the wind, and every instinct he had said: *walk over there. Say her name. Ask her.*

And every other instinct said: *not here. Not with Pell's book open and constables drifting the quay and the whole harbor learning to write down names.*

"Whitlock." One of the older loaders, a barrel-chested northerner called Hedley, came to stand beside him at the rail. He spoke quietly, eyes forward, the way men had started talking to each other lately — sideways, out of the corners of their mouths, like smugglers. "Woman at the end of the pier. Been there since the last crate went up. Know her?"

"No," Tom said.

It came out fast and flat and he hated how fast it was. Hedley looked at him. Hedley had been loading ships since before Tom was born and missed nothing that happened between the tide marks.

"No," Tom said again, slower. "Never seen her."

"Mm." Hedley drank his tea. "Pell's been asking the northern boys where you lodge. Rope Walk, you told him?"

"I told him true."

"Hedley." Tom waited. The wind pushed at them, carrying the clank of the coal heap and the creak of mooring lines.

Hedley drained his cup and crushed it slowly in his fist, and when he spoke again his voice was lower. "There's a meeting Sunday. Not the Anchor. Somewhere else. About the papers — about what we do if they start demanding them on the gates instead of in offices. Men are tired of waiting to find out what our own names cost." He glanced at Tom, quick and sideways. "You should come."

"Why me?"

"Because you stack straight and lie badly." Hedley almost smiled. "Both useful. I'll send word where."

He walked off toward the hold, and Tom stood at the rail with his heart going harder than the loading had made it go.

A papers collective. He'd heard whispers for months — dockworkers and fishermen and a few clerks, pooling what they knew about which documents could be questioned and which couldn't, which officials took bribes and which took pleasure, how a man born on one side of a line drawn in an office could prove he belonged on the other. He'd stayed clear of it. Staying clear was the whole strategy: keep your head down, keep your ledger boring, survive the weather until it passed.

But the weather wasn't passing. The weather was settling in, the way winters did, and Hedley was right about the gates. First they counted you in offices. Then they counted you at the gates. Then the count became the gate.

He looked down the pier.

Sable had turned. She was looking directly at him now — no pretense left, or maybe she'd decided pretense was wasted on a brother — and even across forty yards of dark timber he could feel the weight of her attention, the way he'd felt it all his life whenever he'd botched a knot or lied about a chore. Their father used to say Sable could catch a falsehood at a hundred paces, same as she could catch a bad bearing.

She didn't wave. She didn't come closer. She stood there a long moment, and then she did the thing that frightened him more than any foreman's book: she gave him a single nod — deliberate, slow, unmistakable — the nod you give a person when you want them to know you've seen them and they've seen you, when the acknowledgment itself is the message.

*I know you know. Now we both know.*

Then she turned and walked away up the pier, unhurried, her boots measured on the planks, and disappeared into the dark beyond the fish sheds.

Tom stood at the rail until the tea in the other men's cups went cold and the cry came to resume loading, and he worked the second half of the shift with his body doing the labor and his mind elsewhere entirely, running the sums he'd been avoiding for four years.

Because here was the truth under all his anger, the truth he kept in the deepest part of the hold: he had known, standing at their father's graveside, that the fight between them was mostly pride, and that pride was a luxury, and that someday something would happen that would make luxuries impossible. He had simply assumed the something would come from his side of the water — that he'd swallow it first, or she would, in some ordinary year, over some ordinary dinner.

Not like this. Not with her appearing at his workplace like a signal fire, in the worst spring the harbor had seen in living memory, three months after her master fell down a staircase.

When the shift ended at dawn, the sky over Greyhaven had gone the color of dishwater, and the men shuffled off toward their lodgings and their beds. Pell made his rounds with his book, ticking names, and paused in front of Tom a beat longer than necessary.

"Late finish for you tomorrow," Pell said. "Double hold. The *Corvane*'s taking on ballast and they want extra hands."

"The *Corvane*," Tom repeated. The name meant nothing to him. Another hull, another hold.

"That's right." Pell smiled without warmth and wrote something down. "Dawn shift. Don't be late, Whitlock. They're keeping close track of who works what, this season."

Tom walked home through the waking streets with his shoulders aching and his mind moving faster than his feet. The *Corvane*. He turned the name over and found nothing beneath it but the feeling of a hook turning over in the water, quiet, patient, baited.

At the chandlery he climbed the stairs to his room and stood at the window while Greyhaven opened below him — carts and gulls and smoke, the whole gray city pretending to be ordinary. Somewhere up on Chandler's Lane, his sister was sleeping, or not sleeping, or standing at her own window doing sums of her own.

She'd nodded. She'd wanted him to know she'd seen him.

Which meant that whatever she'd come to say, she hadn't dared say it on a public pier — and that she would come to say it somewhere private, soon, because Sable Whitlock never left a chart unfinished.

He lay down in his clothes and stared at the ceiling and tried to hate her, and found, to his considerable alarm, that he mostly couldn't remember why.

Three streets away and one floor up, on Chandler's Lane, a light was still burning in a workshop window.
