## 6. Terms of Reunion

The tenement on Cordwainer Row leaned into its neighbor like a drunkard leaning on a friend, and Sable climbed its stairs with the ledger strapped flat against her ribs beneath her coat, where it lay against her heart like something stolen. Which, she supposed, it was — or would be, if Aldous Fenwick had meant it for anyone but the grave.

Three flights. The banister came away under her glove in a fine gray powder of rot. On the landing a woman sat shelling peas into her lap and did not look up, which in Greyhaven was a courtesy and a currency both.

The door she wanted bore a brass numeral that had once been a six and was now mostly a rumor. She knocked twice, then — remembering herself, remembering him — a third, sharp, the way their father had knocked when he'd come home from the yards wanting his supper and no argument.

Footsteps. A pause at the threshold long enough for her to imagine him deciding not to answer. Then the door opened on a chain's width, and one eye appeared in the gap — brown as hers, red-rimmed from coal smoke or something else — and narrowed.

"We're full," Tom said.

"Tom."

"Full up. Every bed." But the chain was already sliding, because some part of him had recognized the knock before the voice, and he hated that part, and she watched him hate it as the door swung wide.

He had grown thinner since the funeral. That was two years ago now, and still her first measure of him was always *thinner*, *older*, *more like Father* — the jaw heavy under stubble, the shoulders of a man who hauled iron by night and slept badly by day. His room behind him was a monk's cell with pretensions: a cot, a stove, three books, a photograph of their mother face-down on the shelf as though even the dead needed privacy here.

"You can't be here," he said.

"Hello to you too." She stepped past him because standing in the hallway was how you got remembered, and being remembered on Cordwainer Row was how you got reported. She unbuttoned her coat but did not remove it; the ledger stayed where it was. "Your landlady shells peas in the corridor. Charming building. I could hear the roof thinking."

"Sable." Her name in his mouth sounded like an accusation filed under the wrong title. "Why are you here?"

She turned and looked at him properly, and the looking hurt more than she'd budgeted for. "Because you're on a list."

Something moved through his face — fast, swallowed. He closed the door and shot the bolt, and when he faced her again he had arranged himself into carelessness, arms folded, one shoulder against the doorframe. Their father's posture. He'd stolen it wholesale. "There's always a list. Dockworkers are born on lists. What list this week? Left-handed men? Men who whistle on Sundays?"

"The resettlement list." She said it flat, so it couldn't be mistaken for drama. "Census category nine. 'Persons of irregular domicile requiring relocation for the public good.' Do you want me to read you your own file number?"

The stove ticked. Downstairs the pea-shelling went on, small and steady as rain.

Tom unfolded his arms slowly. "You've been in Fenwick's papers."

"I finished his atlas."

"You *what*—"

"Keep your voice down." She pulled the ledger out then, because there was no longer any sense pretending she'd come for nostalgia, and laid it on his table between the tin cup and the cold lamp. It looked smaller out in the light than it had in the workshop's dark, this thing three people might have already died around. "Before he died, he was marking charts. Marginal codes — depth soundings, tide windows, signal days. I thought they were survey notes. They weren't. They were routes. Ways out of the harbor past the patrol cutters. And this—" she touched the cover, didn't open it yet, made him lean toward it, "—is the key to the margins. Names. Families. Berths."

"And my name's in it."

It wasn't a question and she didn't treat it as one. She opened the ledger to the ribboned page and turned it toward him and watched him read his own name in Aldous Fenwick's careful clerk's hand: *Whitlock, Thomas — Cordwainer Row — dock, night shift — priority: immediate.* Beneath it, in a different ink, a date that was four days before the night Aldous Fenwick fell down his own workshop stairs with his skull opened like a dropped melon.

"He knew about you," Sable said quietly. "He'd marked you before he died. He was going to move you himself."

Tom didn't touch the page. He stood over it the way a man stands over a well he suspects is deep. Then he laughed — one syllable, no humor in it, just air escaping a pressurized place.

"The old man knew my name and I never knew he knew it."

"He knew everything on that waterfront. You loaded his paper shipments for two years, Tom, don't look like that, I found the manifests—"

"That's not—" He turned away, ran a hand through his hair, and when he spoke again it came out low and vicious. "Two years I worked that quay. Two years. And my own sister's master gets me off the purge list before my own blood even answers my letters."

"Your letters." She heard her own voice climb and dragged it back down. The walls here were paper. "Don't. Don't do that. Don't stand in this room and rewrite history at me."

"One letter, Sable. After the funeral. One. *Condolences of the season, business takes me inland.* I framed it mentally. Hung it in the parlor of my head."

"I was seventeen and burying my father and you were twenty-two and drinking the eulogy—"

"I was working double shifts to pay for the coffin!" It burst out of him before he could cage it, and then he stood there breathing hard, appalled at the size of what he'd let out. He crossed to the window instead, put his back to her, spoke to the glass. "You wouldn't know. You weren't here. You were being educated by a mapmaker while I learned what a hold full of wet guano smells like in August."

"Yes." She said it without heat, and the absence of heat was somehow worse. "And every time I wrote after that — and I wrote, Tom, four times, five — you sent them back unopened. I know because they came back to me. Unopened. So we can stand in this room all evening trading receipts, or we can talk about the nineteen days you may have left in this city."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, a child began to cry and was hushed with terrible efficiency.

"Nineteen days," Tom repeated, not turning.

"The Corvane. Tramp freighter, ostensibly bound for Calla with ballast slate. Actually making a run north with eleven families aboard. Harbor master Doss holds the manifest — Fenwick paid him years ago, the arrangement's still live, I confirmed it yesterday. There is one berth left. One. And it has your name penciled next to it if I say so."

Now he turned. And she saw it hit him — not the danger, not yet; men who work nights among steam and swinging cargo believe in their own luck the way other men believe in God. What hit him first was the arithmetic of it. One berth. One sister.

"Why are you telling me the number?" he asked. "You're not the one holding it. You're not sailing."

"No."

"So who is?" A beat. "Sable. Who's holding the berth?"

"You are," she said. "As far as Doss knows, as far as the Corvane knows, the last passenger is T. Whitlock, cartographer's assistant, traveling north for reasons of health. I'll have papers for you inside a fortnight. Clean ones."

He stared at her. Then, horribly, he smiled — the old smile, the one from before the funeral, crooked and warm and absolutely immovable. "You forged them already."

"I will forge them."

"You've already started. Look at your hands." She looked down at her own fingers before she could stop herself — ink in the knuckle creases, a chemical stain along the thumb she hadn't managed to scrub. He saw her see it, and the smile faded into something more complicated. "You always were the dangerous one. Father thought you'd be the ruin of yourself. I used to fight him about it."

The sentence landed strangely, an ambush of tenderness, and she had to look at the ledger to keep her composure. "What did you tell him?"

"That you'd be the making of this family instead." He came back to the table, and when he sat, he sat heavily, like a man accepting a verdict read in a language he only half spoke. He put one blunt finger on his own name. "Immediate priority. That means they're close to sweeping the docks, doesn't it. The new foremen asking about birthplaces — that's not wage administration, is it."

"It's a census," she said. "A very patient census."

He nodded slowly. Outside the window the lamplighter was working down the row, and the gas flames came up one by one like witnesses raising their hands.

"If I go," Tom said, "the collective folds. Forty dockmen trying to get their papers straightened — real papers, honest status — and the moment I'm gone they're leaderless and suspicious of each other. Half of them are on your ledger too, aren't they? I recognize those streets." His finger moved down the column. "Okafor. The Brandt brothers. Old Mrs. Halloran who cooks for the foremen. Fenwick was trying to save half my crew."

"And he died for it," Sable said. "So we don't get to decide it was worth less than your collective. We don't get that luxury, Tom. Somebody already spent his life on these pages. All we get to decide is whether we throw them away out of pride."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "Nothing currently on offer is fair. This is what's on offer."

He was quiet a long time. She watched fear arrive in him finally, not all at once but in installments — in the way his thumb worried the edge of the table, in the glance he kept cutting toward the bolted door, in the sudden stillness of a man recalculating every footsteps-on-the-stairs of the last month. She hated watching it. She hated that part of her, the older-sister-in-all-but-age part, that also felt relief watching it, because fear was the only argument that had ever moved him.

"Nineteen days," he said again, softer now.

"Departure's at dawn tide. I'll come for you the night before. You pack nothing that can't fit in a coat. You tell no one, not the collective, not Okafor, not a priest. Especially not a priest — Father Merrin's confessional leaks, Tom, everyone on that quay knows it."

That startled a real laugh out of him, brief and rusty. "You have been inland too long."

"I've been alive too long for this city's taste." She closed the ledger and strapped it back beneath her coat, and the doing of it steadied her, the ritual of concealment, hands that knew their work. "I have to go. I'm expected — someone's been asking questions at the workshop. A census man. Official."

Tom's head came up sharply. "Name."

"Vane. Elias Vane."

"Vane." He said it like tasting something. "Grey coat, gray eyes, counts things with his chin when he thinks nobody's watching? He was at the harbor office Tuesday. Watching the manifests get stamped."

The cold went through her slowly, evenly, like water rising in a hold. "Did he see you?"

"He sees everyone. Question is what he keeps." Tom stood, and for a moment neither of them moved, siblings across a table with a dead man's book between them, and the whole gulf of two years suddenly irrelevant — because terror, she was learning, was also a kind of reunion, and they stood in it together like other families stood in parlors.

"Sable." He caught her hand as she reached for her collar. His grip was cargo-callused and shaking, just slightly, and he did not pretend otherwise. "Nineteen days. If your papers wobble — if anything goes wrong at that quay — you put me on that deck anyway and you burn the rest. Promise me. Whatever else we are to each other, promise me that."

She looked at her brother, whom she had mourned alive for two years, and lied to him with the fluency she'd once reserved for census men.

"I promise," she said.

At the door she paused with her hand on the bolt, and he said her name once more — just her name, nothing attached to it, an entire apology compressed into two syllables — and she let it stand for everything it couldn't.

The stairs took her down past the pea-sheller, past the crying child now silent, out into the lamplit street where the fog was coming in off the harbor the way it always did, erasing the far end of the row first, working toward her like something patient reading a list.

Behind her, through the tenement's thin walls, she heard Tom drag his cot across the floor to brace the door.

And ahead of her, waiting under the corner lamp with his gray coat buttoned and his notebook open, as though he had merely paused there to check an address — as though he had not been waiting, she was quite sure, for *her* — stood Elias Vane.
