## 7. Elias Returns

The Bureau of Enumeration occupied the third floor of the Custom House, a long room of green-shaded lamps and the smell of cold tea and carbon paper. Elias Vane had a desk by the window — a courtesy, Cray called it, though the window looked only onto the harbor's grey shoulder and the gulls that hung over the fish market like scraps of dirty linen. He had come to think of it as his window all the same. A man needed one thing in a government building that he could call his own.

He filed his reports on Tuesday mornings. That was the rhythm of it: canvass through the week, transcribe on Sunday nights at his lodgings with the gas turned low, deliver the fair copies on Tuesday with his columns ruled straight as fence posts. Nine districts, four hundred and eleven households, and not one figure out of place. His supervisor, a soft man named Prewitt who measured diligence in ink coverage, had twice held up Elias's returns to the junior clerks as examples.

*Observe,* Prewitt had said. *Nothing left blank.*

Elias thought about that phrase more than he wanted to. Nothing left blank. It was supposed to be praise.

This Tuesday he carried his packet up the stairs through the smell of wet wool — rain again; Greyhaven in autumn was less a city than a condition — and laid the folder on Prewitt's desk with the usual small ceremony. Prewitt thumbed the edges, nodded like a horse being fed, and Elias was halfway back down the room when the door of the corner office opened.

"Vane."

Inspector Cray's voice was quiet in the way of a man who never needed to raise it because he knew everyone else would lower theirs. He stood in his doorway holding a cup of something dark, and behind him the office was dim except for one lamp trained on a wall that was no longer bare. Elias had noticed the wall last week. It had grown, since then.

"Have you eaten?" Cray asked.

"I've had breakfast, sir."

"That wasn't the question." But he stepped back from the doorway, which was answer enough, and Elias went in because a man who hesitated at Cray's threshold became interesting, and interesting men were followed home.

The wall had been a shipping chart when Elias first saw it — Greyhaven roads and approaches, soundings in fathoms, the whole coast rendered in the confident hand of some hydrographer dead forty years. Now it bristled with pins. Red pins for households flagged under the resettlement statutes. Black pins for persons of interest. And threaded between them, in red thread so fine it looked like arterial work, a web connecting perhaps thirty points to a single card pinned at the center, above the harbor itself.

The card said: **FENWICK**.

"Aldous Fenwick," Cray said, settling behind his desk without inviting Elias to sit. "Chart-maker. Dead three months. Fell down his own stairs after two whiskeys, according to the coroner, who is either honest or cheap." He drank. "You did his district."

"I enumerated the household before his death. Yes."

"And afterward?"

"The workshop was closed. I recorded it vacant pending probate."

"Mm." Cray set the cup down and turned it slowly, once, like a man winding a watch. "Fenwick made maps, Vane. Do you know what else he made?"

"Charts. Coastal surveys. I believe he did commission work for the harbormaster."

"He made lists." Cray's finger rose and touched the card, not hard, the way one touches a bruise to see if it still hurts. "Marginal markings on those charts. Codes. Names folded into compass roses and depth numbers. The man hid a registry inside an atlas — families, routes, dates. Paper boats, Vane, sailing out of my harbor every few months with my missing passengers aboard." He leaned back. "Fenwick is past questioning. Which leaves the accomplice. Someone finished what he started. Someone who knows where the atlas is."

"You're asking me to speculate, sir."

"I'm asking you to do your job." Cray opened a drawer and drew out a single sheet — a duplicate census form, Elias saw with a small cold drop of recognition. His own hand. District Four. "You flagged this one yourself. Workshop, one occupant deceased, then re-occupied. Probate heir arrived from Carrowmere in September. And here—" his nail found a line of Elias's neat notation, "—*visitor recorded, female, purpose unstated.* Your handwriting, Vane. You have an eye for anomalies. It's why I let you keep the district."

*Let.* The word sat in the room like a third chair.

"The visitor gave her name," Elias said. "Sable Whitlock. Apprentice to the deceased. She was retrieving personal effects."

"Whitlock." Cray pronounced it the way a man bites a coin. "Brother works the Corvane's docks — no, don't tell me, I know, Tom Whitlock, loader, second shift. Brother and sister both orbiting a dead mapmaker's shop, and the sister visiting the heir within a fortnight of the funeral." He smiled, and it changed nothing about his eyes. "Coincidence has a shape, Vane. I've spent twenty years learning its silhouette. This is it."

Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up. Elias watched rain crawl down the glass and understood that he was standing at a door he could not un-open. Behind him lay the tidy career: straight columns, straight rules, nothing left blank. Ahead lay whatever lived in the space Cray was carving out of the word *accomplice*.

"What would you have me do?"

"Finish the district properly. Re-interview the heir — Maren Fenwick, frightened creature by all reports, will fold like damp paper. Confirm the sister's comings and goings. Dates, durations, purposes." Cray slid the census duplicate across the desk. "And bring me anything unusual from your field notes. Anything at all. You keep notes separately, don't you? Men with your eye always do."

Elias took the sheet. Their fingers did not touch; there was a precise inch of air between his hand and Cray's, and he would remember that inch later, how carefully they had both avoided crossing it.

"I keep thorough records," he said. "That's all."

"Thorough," Cray agreed pleasantly. "That's all I ask."

* * *

His lodgings were two rooms above a chandler's on Rope Walk, and on Sunday nights they smelled of lamp oil and drying ink while he transcribed. He worked at the table under the window, and anyone watching the lit square from the street — someone was, he'd assumed since the spring; the same silhouette across the way, too patient for a burglar — would have seen a diligent clerk doing diligent things.

Which was true, and incomplete.

Because beneath the fair copy, clipped inside the back board of his transcription folder, lived another document. He had started it without deciding to — that was the thing he couldn't explain to himself, that his hand had simply begun, one night in August, writing a second list alongside the first. Not falsifications. He hadn't falsified anything, and he clung to that distinction the way a drowning man clings to the distinction between falling and diving. The Bureau's copy contained everything required by statute. His private ledger contained everything required by conscience.

Anomalies, he called them. A widow in District Two whose lodger appeared on no other rolls. A family in Seven gone between Tuesday and Friday, dishes still in the rack, rent paid ahead — resettled, the word was, though no settlement ever wrote back. A church deacon who kept two birthdates. Small discrepancies, each defensible alone. Together they made a pattern, and the pattern pointed toward the water, and the water pointed toward ships that sailed heavy and returned light.

Tonight he added the newest entries. The Whitlock siblings went in under his steadiest hand: *Sable Whitlock, cartographer, resident District Four (workshop premises). Thomas Whitlock, dock laborer, Corvane berth, District Six. Association: deceased subject A. Fenwick.* Then he sat back and looked at what he'd written and felt the specific shame of a man who has just done a kind thing clumsily — because writing their names in his private book was protection only if his book stayed private, and a liability if it didn't. He was keeping duplicates of everything now. The Bureau's truth and his own, side by side, and someday the two stacks of paper would be compared by someone who was not him, and one stack would hang him.

He wondered, not for the first time, which one.

Rain kept on. Down at the corner a constable's boots slapped hollow through puddles. Elias dipped his pen, held it over the page, and for a long moment wrote nothing — the nib hovering, a small black point of hesitation, dripping one slow bead that would have bloomed like a wound if it fell.

He was thinking about her hands.

She had answered the workshop door with ink to the second knuckle, he remembered — Prussian blue ground into the cuticle, the mark every cartographer wears and no honest laundress can remove. She had lied to him beautifully. Not badly, the way amateurs lie — badly is forgivable — but well, with structure, with a lie built in layers the way you build a seawall: general statement, corroborating detail, small self-damning admission to buy credibility. *I came for my drawing instruments, I doubt I'll return, he kept such a clutter, poor man.* And all the while her eyes had gone past his shoulder to the street, twice, timing, counting — a woman calculating whether the census taker at the door had come alone.

He had stood on that step holding his clipboard and felt something crack open in his chest that had no business opening during working hours. Recognition, maybe. Here was another person maintaining two sets of books.

"You look tired, Mr. Vane," she had said at the end, kindly, and it was not flirtation, it was reconnaissance, and he had admired it even as it disarmed him.

*Purpose unstated*, he had written in the official record. What he had not written: that when she closed the door he had remained on the step a full ten seconds, listening to bolts, feeling like a man who has read half a letter and must now decide whether reading the rest would be theft or duty.

The bead of ink fell. It spread through the fibers of the page in a slow dark flower, precisely over the word *Whitlock*, and Elias watched it bleed until the name was illegible — ruined, unrecorded, erased by an accident no one could prove was one. Then he took out a fresh sheet and rewrote the entry clean, because a man who destroyed records was a fool, but a man who hesitated this long already knew which side of the question he was leaning toward, and lying to himself about it was the one lie he hadn't yet perfected.

He blew out the lamp at midnight. In the dark, the silhouette across the street was still there.

* * *

Monday brought fog, and with it Inspector Cray, walking the districts himself — rare enough to be alarming. They met by chance, Cray insisted, at the mouth of Chandler's Lane, though Elias noted the inspector had positioned his chance neatly on the route between the tram stop and District Four.

"Working day, Vane?"

"Re-canvassing. Correcting the record."

"Good." Cray walked with him, hands clasped behind him like a bishop inspecting grounds. The fog ate the ends of streets; ships' horns spoke to each other across the water in mournful code. "Maren Fenwick. What's your impression?"

"Frightened. Grieving. Out of her depth in a city she hasn't lived in since childhood."

"Cowardly, then. Good. Cowards are efficient." Cray stepped around a barrow of kelp. "And the apprentice? Whitlock?"

"I haven't concluded."

"You have, though. That's your trouble." Cray stopped, and the fog closed around them so completely that the harbor might have ceased to exist, might have been a rumor all along. "I've watched you work, Vane. You see the anomaly before you see the person. It's a gift. Most men need years to separate the two." He produced, from nowhere, a photograph — small, silvered, the kind portrait studios issue by the dozen. A woman at a doorway, mid-turn, caught unawares. Sable Whitlock. "My men follow clumsy methods. Doors kicked, papers scattered, witnesses frightened into silence. You'll get further with courtesy. You have a courteous face." He pressed the photograph into Elias's pocket as though returning a borrowed glove. "Find out where the atlas goes, and who carries it, and I'll consider your file exemplary. Fail, and I'll consider your file closely. Either way, Vane —"

"— I'll be thorough," Elias said.

Cray smiled into the fog. "That's all any of us can be."

He walked away, and the fog took him in three steps, and Elias stood alone on Chandler's Lane with a photograph in his pocket growing warm against his thigh like something alive.

He should turn it in to the office. Standard evidence handling. Instead he climbed the hill to District Four, and at the top of Wren Street he stopped, because below him the workshop windows showed lamplight at an hour when no legitimate business kept lamps — two figures moving behind the glass, one tall, one slighter, bent together over something on the big drafting table, heads close as conspirators or sisters.

He knew the slighter one by the way she stood. Weight on the left foot. Hands moving when she talked, quick, certain, drawing shapes in the air — mapping her words, the way she mapped everything.

Elias took out his notebook, and in full view of anyone watching — Cray's watchers, there were always watchers — he made a great show of consulting his route schedule, of marking a household visited, of being exactly the dull instrument his office believed him to be. Then he walked on past the workshop without turning his head, past the smell of ink and lamp oil leaking under the door, past the sound of a woman's voice saying something that made the other laugh, low, briefly, like weather clearing.

*Some columns should stay blank*, he thought, and hated himself for the poetry, and kept walking, and did not report the lamplight until Wednesday, and then reported it a day late and a street wrong.

It was the first lie he had ever entered in an official record. It fit his handwriting perfectly.
