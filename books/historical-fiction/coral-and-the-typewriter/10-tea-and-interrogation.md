## 10. Tea and Interrogation

Elias Vane had filled out four hundred and eleven census correction forms in his three years with the Office of Enumeration, and he could not remember a single name from any of them. That was the nature of the work: a river of paper, each sheet identical, each household reduced to columns and figures, the whole city pressed flat between ruled lines. He had told himself, when he took the post, that there was a kind of honor in it — that a count was a country's way of knowing itself, that every name entered in fair ink was a name guaranteed to exist.

He no longer believed that. He was not sure when he had stopped.

The correction form for the Fenwick workshop sat on his desk for two days before he filed it out, and when he finally wrote the pretext in the margin — *discrepancy in occupancy declaration; follow-up required* — his pen hesitated over the word *required*. Nothing required this. The discrepancy, if it had ever existed, had been resolved by Aldous Fenwick's death. A dead man's household did not need counting.

But Elias went anyway, on a Thursday morning thick with harbor fog, carrying the form like a lantern he didn't intend to light.

* * *

The workshop door was opened by a woman he had not met before.

She was perhaps thirty, with the pale, unweathered look of someone raised inland, wearing a traveling coat still creased from a trunk. She held the door at half its width, the way people do when they are not certain they want visitors, and looked at him with the particular wariness of someone who has already been visited by officials and found the experience instructive.

"Miss Fenwick," Elias said, and inclined his head. "Elias Vane, Office of Enumeration. I called some weeks ago regarding the household record."

"Yes. Sable said — that is, she mentioned." The woman's hands found each other and clasped. "Maren Fenwick. His daughter. I've come to settle the estate."

"Then my condolences are overdue, and my apology with them." He produced the form. "A small matter only. The record lists two occupants of this address as of the spring enumeration. With Mr. Fenwick's passing, I'm obliged to confirm who remains."

Something crossed her face — quick, unguarded, gone before he could name it. Fear, he thought. Or the memory of fear.

"Sable Whitlock remains," Maren said. "She was his apprentice. She's — helping me. With the estate."

"Of course." He wrote nothing yet. "And yourself? Do you intend to register at this address?"

"I haven't—"

"Maren." The voice came from within, warm and level, and then Sable Whitlock was there behind her, one hand resting on the younger woman's shoulder as though she had been standing there all along, listening, waiting for exactly this moment. She wore an apron dusted with lampblack and there was a smudge of it along her jaw like a bruise. "Don't keep the gentleman on the step. It's beastly weather."

Her eyes met Elias's, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with census forms — an acknowledgment, wry and steady, of the game they had begun weeks ago and neither had agreed to stop playing.

"Miss Whitlock." He stepped inside when she stood aside. "I see the workshop survives you."

"Barely. Miss Fenwick arrives to claim her inheritance and finds me drowning in her father's chaos. We've been sorting papers all week." She gestured at the back room, where, he noted, the door to Aldous Fenwick's private study stood closed — where on his first visit it had stood open, shelves visible, drafting table crowded with legitimate survey work. "Tea? You look like a man who's been walking in fog since dawn."

"I have been walking in fog since dawn."

"Then tea is not courtesy, it's triage. Sit."

* * *

The back room had been rearranged since his last visit. Elias catalogued the changes without appearing to: the drafting table angled away from the window; a new lock, brass and recent, on the study door; the smell of the place subtly altered, ink and old paper overlaid now with something sharper — solvent, or acid, or whatever is used to clean plates. A workshop in the midst of being tidied, or in the midst of being hidden.

He sat where the light was worst, deliberately, so their faces would be better lit than his.

Maren poured the tea with hands that trembled only slightly, and Sable talked — easily, amusingly, a running commentary on the trials of sorting a dead cartographer's affairs. Did he know that Fenwick had kept every rejected draft of every chart for thirty years? Four hundred sheets of coastline drawn and redrawn, bays migrated north an inch at a time as the old man corrected his own certainties. Did he know there were letters from the Survey Office going back to before the war scares, complaining about Fenwick's refusal to round his measurements?

"He was difficult," Sable said, refilling his cup unasked. "Genius usually is. But you didn't come to hear me eulogize him. You came about the record."

"The record," Elias agreed, and laid the form flat. "It lists two occupants as of April. Mr. Fenwick and yourself."

"Yes."

"Now there are three names in play — his death removes one, adds Miss Fenwick temporarily, and raises the question of your own status here. Are you resident, Miss Whitlock, or employee?"

The question was innocuous. It was designed to be innocuous. And he watched her understand that, watch her weigh it, watch her choose charm as her answer.

"Resident until the estate settles," she said. "After that — who knows. Perhaps I'll be unemployed and notorious. The mapmaker's apprentice who couldn't find work in her own trade because her master's clients all scattered after his funeral." She smiled as she said it, making a joke of her precarity, and it was beautifully done. Too beautifully. "Is unemployment a column on your form, Mr. Vane? I'd hate to fall through the cracks of the empire's arithmetic."

"There is no column for that."

"A failing on the state's part. Half of Greyhaven would qualify."

He laughed despite himself, and hated how easily she drew it out of him, and wrote *resident, temporary* in the margin with a hand far steadier than his attention deserved.

"And Miss Fenwick," he said. "How long does she stay?"

"Oh, that depends on the lawyers, and the lawyers depend on the tides, everything in this city depends on the tides—" Maren began, and then her voice did the thing Elias had been listening for since the door opened. It accelerated. "—and we can't very well ship the atlas anywhere until—" 

She stopped.

The room went very quiet. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past toward the harbor.

Sable did not stiffen. That was the remarkable thing. Elias watched her not stiffen — watched her lean forward instead, unhurried, and top up Maren's teacup though it needed nothing, the gesture absorbing the silence like sand absorbing spilled ink.

"What Maren means," Sable said pleasantly, "is that Father Aldous's atlas has caused us nothing but arguments with the estate assessors. They want to value it. We want it valued. Everyone is disappointed. It's charts, Mr. Vane — beautiful, worthless charts of a coastline everyone already knows by heart. You'd think we were hiding crown jewels the way those men paw through it."

"The atlas," Elias said. "It exists, then. Complete?"

"Nearly. The last plate was never finished." She met his eyes without a flicker. "His hands shook too badly at the end. I suppose you could say the atlas died with him — or most of him did."

It was a perfect answer. It was exactly the answer a person with nothing to hide would give, delivered with exactly the right note of grief-tempered pragmatism. And it was wrong. He knew it was wrong, the way you know a chart's depth markings have been fudged — not by any single figure, but by the shape of the whole, the way the soundings avoid one stretch of water entirely.

Because Elias had seen the shipping manifests. He had spent three evenings cross-checking the departures of the past year against the passenger rolls, and he knew that coastal atlases of the Fenwick quality did not sit in workshops awaiting estate assessors. They moved. They were bought, copied, smuggled. Someone had been finishing a dead man's work, and the woman pouring his tea had lampblack on her jaw and solvent in the air of her sitting room.

"Shaking hands," he said mildly. "Yes. I read the coroner's report. A fall on the stair. Terrible thing, a man surviving thirty years of cliff surveys and losing his life to his own hallway."

"Aren't stairs always the traitor?" Sable said. "More tea?"

"No, thank you." He closed the form. "I have what I need. Miss Fenwick — you'll want to register formally if your stay extends past the month. The office penalizes late declarations."

"I'll — yes. Thank you. I'll do that." Maren's voice had recovered some steadiness, but her eyes went to Sable first, asking permission even now.

Elias rose, gathered his coat and case, and paused at the door with the form tucked under his arm, and let himself do the thing he had been refusing to let himself do since the fog on the walk over: look at Sable Whitlock plainly, not as a subject of inquiry but as a woman he had thought about at inconvenient hours, whose lies he admired even as they tightened around his throat.

"Miss Whitlock. A word about the form, before I go."

"Of course."

He stepped into the doorway, out of Maren's hearing, and spoke quietly, watching the rain instead of her face.

"The office uses standardized ledgers. Every household, every person, entered under fixed headings. Name, origin, occupation, association. Most clerks believe a complete ledger is a good ledger — every blank filled, every ambiguity resolved." He adjusted his collar against the damp. "But I've handled enough of them to know that a full column isn't the same as a true one. Some entries, once written, can't be unwritten. People have been resettled on thinner evidence than a clerk's thoroughness."

Behind him he heard her breath change — a small thing, controlled instantly, but he heard it.

"So my professional advice," he went on, still watching the rain, "is this. When you correct your records — any records — consider which questions actually require answers. Because in my experience, some columns should stay blank."

Silence, except for the rain.

"Mr. Vane," she said at last, and her voice had lost none of its charm but gained something underneath it, something that might have been warning or might have been gratitude or might have been both braided together, "you give very strange advice for a census man."

"I give advice I intend to follow myself." He turned then, and let her see his face, and did not pretend otherwise. "Good morning, Miss Whitlock. Miss Fenwick. Mind the stairs."

He was down two steps before her voice caught him — low, pitched for him alone:

"Why tell me this?"

He did not turn around. He stood in the rain-fog at the foot of the workshop steps with his case in his hand and the correction form against his ribs, and he answered the question honestly, which was the most dangerous thing he had done in three years of service.

"Because I've started keeping a list," he said, "of the questions I refuse to ask. And I find I don't trust myself to hold it alone."

Then he walked into the fog, and did not look back, and did not see her expression — but he carried the image of it anyway, invented and inevitable: the mapmaker's apprentice in her doorway, holding a lie in each hand, deciding what to do with a man who had just handed her the truth wrapped in a warning.

By nightfall he would regret it. By week's end, Inspector Cray would ask him directly what he had learned at the Fenwick address, and Elias Vane would stand in his superior's office with the standardized ledger open between them and discover whether his blank columns were mercy or suicide.

He suspected he already knew.
