## 11. The Forger's Hands

The lamp had a bad wick. It guttered every few minutes, and each time it did, Sable Whitlock's hand stilled above the paper, waiting for the flame to steady before she let the pen touch down again. She had learned that much in three nights: forgery was mostly patience, and patience was mostly fear wearing a calm face.

The stamp sat before her on a square of black cloth, taken from the bottom drawer of Aldous Fenwick's desk — the drawer he had kept locked even from her, pried open only last week with Maren's silent, white-lipped permission. The Registrar's Office of Greyhaven Port, it read, circling an anchor and the year. The brass was warm-looking under the lamplight, dignified, ordinary. It had pressed its seal into a hundred legitimate documents while Aldous lived. Now Sable wanted it to press lies into the world, and the stamp itself could not tell the difference.

That was the trouble. The stamp was innocent. She was not.

She inked the pad — too much, she knew at once, the roller leaving a glossy black skin like something drowned — and pressed the test sheet down. When she lifted it, the impression bloomed at the edges, fat and blurred, the anchor's flukes running together like wet feathers.

*Fourth sheet ruined tonight.*

She set it aside with the others. Nine of them now, fanned across the workbench like the shed skins of some failed creature. Each one a small confession. If Elias Vane ever saw this bench, these sheets would hang her faster than any ledger.

She did not allow herself to think of Elias. She thought of him constantly.

*Ink again. Less this time.*

She rolled the pad thin, breathed, and pressed. The impression came up pale in the center where the pressure had faltered, dark at one corner where her wrist had tilted. An examiner would see it in three seconds. Any clerk worth his salt would see it in two.

The clock in the workshop's corner read half past one. Nineteen days had become twelve. Tom's berth on the *Corvane* was a fixed point now, a star she steered by, and between her and that star lay nothing but paper — paper that had to bear the Registrar's seal so perfectly that no man inspecting manifests at dawn would look twice.

Aldous could have done it. That was the bitter arithmetic of it. Her master had forged documents for eleven years — harbor passes, cargo certifications, whole identities folded flat as charts — and no one had caught him until someone killed him for it. He had made forgery look like drafting, because that is what it was: precision in service of a lie, which is what all precision is, if you follow it far enough down.

She wiped her hands on her apron and started sheet ten.

This one was better. The pressure came off her palm evenly, the way Aldous had taught her to bear down on a ruling pen — *from the shoulder, girl, not the wrist; the wrist is where trembling lives.* The anchor took shape cleanly. For one full second she allowed herself the dangerous luxury of hope.

Then the lamp guttered, and in the stutter of light her elbow caught the ink bottle, and a black tide rolled across the sheet, drowning the seal, drowning the false name she'd already lettered beneath it — *Thomas Merrick, dockhand, b. Greyhaven* — drowning everything.

Sable put her head down on the workbench, carefully, avoiding the wet, and laughed until the laugh turned into something else, and then she let that happen too, quietly, the way she did everything now.

* * *

"You're holding it wrong."

The voice came from the doorway and stopped her heart mid-beat. She came up so fast she knocked the black cloth square to the floor, and the stamp rang against the boards like a dropped coin, spinning, coming to rest face-up by Maren Fenwick's boot.

Maren looked at it. Then at the nine ruined sheets. Then at Sable, whose hands were black to the second knuckle and whose face, she was certain, had gone the color of the paper.

They had been living in the same workshop for six days without once saying anything true to each other. Maren slept in her father's old room and kept the door shut; Sable kept the atlas wrapped in oilcloth under the floor and pretended not to watch Maren walk past the loose board. They shared bread and tea and silence, two women orbiting the same grief at different speeds.

Now Maren crouched, picked up the stamp, and turned it over in her fingers with the absent tenderness of someone handling a childhood toy.

"He kept this in the bottom drawer," she said. "I used to think it was a toy anchor. I was seven." She stood. "You're pressing straight down. That's your whole problem."

"Maren—"

"Father said the Registry men press straight down because they don't care what the seal looks like, only that it's there." Maren crossed the room, pulled the stool from the drafting table, and sat down at the lamp as if she had been sitting there all along, as if the past six days of closed doors had been a dream. She gathered up the ruined sheets, spread them out, and studied them the way Aldous had studied coastlines — patiently, hungrily, looking for the error in the world.

"Here." She tapped one. "Pressure's good on this one. Better than mine, first try. But you're using the pad like a printing press. Ink, paper, smash." She shook her head. "He'd have boxed your ears."

"He didn't box anything," Sable said. "He corrected margins."

"Yes." Something moved behind Maren's face — the old wound, or the old love, hard to say. "Yes, he did."

Sable stayed by the door. There was still a version of this night where Maren took the stamp and the ruined sheets and the knowledge of them straight to Inspector Cray, who had already visited her once in her inland town and left his questions behind like burrs in wool. Sable knew that version was possible. She also knew, watching Maren's hands move over the failed seals — recognizing, cataloguing, *remembering* — that it was not likely.

"How do you know any of this?" Sable asked.

Maren didn't look up. "Because I grew up in this room too. Before you. You forget that. Summers, mostly, but summers are long when you're a child and your father is a secret." She selected a fresh sheet and laid it true against the bench's edge. "Fetch the kettle. Not for tea."

* * *

The trick, it turned out, was water.

Maren filled the shallow developing tray that Aldous used for washing photo plates, and floated the test sheet on the surface, seal-side down.

"Ink sits on top of paper," she said. "Water goes into paper. You can use one against the other." From the shelf of chemical bottles she took a small stoppered flask, and the smell that rose from it was faintly sweet, faintly sour, like pears going bad. "Gum and alum. Father's recipe. Don't ask me the amounts; I measured by nose when I was twelve and I've never measured since."

She brushed the solution onto the back of the floating sheet with a camel-hair brush, feather-light, and waited. The lamp hissed. Rain had begun against the workshop windows, soft and insistent, the harbor fogging over somewhere below in the dark.

"Now watch."

She lifted the sheet out with both hands and laid it, seal-down, onto the clean paper. She placed over it a blotter, and over the blotter the flat brass weight Aldous used for holding charts in curl. Then she pressed — not hard, just steadily, leaning her slight weight into it, counting under her breath.

When she peeled the original away, the seal remained on the blotter, a ghost in wet ink. And on the clean sheet beneath — there. A perfect transfer. Crisp flukes. Clean ring. Every hairline of the engraving carried over whole, without the tremor of any human hand, because no hand had drawn it. It was not an imitation of the stamp. It was the stamp's own print, moved house.

Sable stared at it. Something turned over in her chest, some load-bearing beam of her understanding shifting.

"That's why you never need a steady hand," Maren said, wiping her fingers. "Steady hands get caught. This way the forger leaves no hand at all. Just the office's own mark, exactly where it should be." She paused. "He showed me the summer I was fourteen. I thought it was a magic trick. He said—" her voice caught, recovered, flattened out, "—he said, *one day a girl will need this more than the Registry needs its seals.* I thought he meant me."

The rain thickened against the glass. Sable looked at the transferred seal, and then at Maren, and understood that she was being handed something heavier than a technique. She was being handed a bequest.

"He knew," Sable said quietly. "At the end. He knew what was coming."

"He always knew things he didn't say. It was his worst quality and it got him killed." Maren said it without bitterness now, or with a bitterness worn smooth, like glass in a tideline. She began brushing the solution onto a second sheet. "Your turn. You'll ruin five before you get one. Everyone does. I ruined six."

"I ruined ten tonight with the stamp alone."

"The stamp's a blunt instrument. This is surgery." She slid the prepared blank toward Sable. "Float it. Back only. If the gum touches the front, the sizing lifts and the surface goes chalky, and a chalky patch on a document is a shout in a quiet room."

Sable floated the sheet. Her hands were steadier than they had been all night — she noticed it the way you notice a headache leaving. The brush moved in long even strokes, and Maren watched and said nothing, and the silence had changed temperature entirely. It was no longer the silence of two strangers sharing a dead man's rooms. It was the silence of a bench in a workshop, master and apprentice, except neither of them was quite sure anymore which one was which.

"Blotter," Maren instructed. "Weight. Count to thirty — not twenty-five, not forty. Thirty. Time is part of the instrument."

Sable counted. At twenty her heart tried to rush her; at thirty, she lifted the weight, drew the blotter back, and peeled the donor sheet away.

The seal came up perfect.

Not nearly perfect. Perfect. The anchor sat in its ring on the clean paper as though the Registrar himself had leaned across the bench and pressed it there, moments ago, with the boredom of ten thousand repetitions.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. The lamp burned steady now, as if it too had decided to cooperate.

"There," Maren said softly. "That's how he did it. Eleven years, and they never caught the transfers. Only the ledger betrayed him, and only because someone talked." She looked at Sable across the lamplight, and her eyes were her father's eyes — the same grey, the same habit of seeing the structure under the surface. "So. Now we come to the real question, apprentice."

"I'm listening."

"What else does the Corvane's manifest need besides a seal?" Maren began ticking items off on her ink-stained fingers, brisk now, businesslike, and Sable realized with a small shock that Maren had been thinking about this too — that behind her closed door, in her father's bed, under her father's roof, she had been doing the same desperate arithmetic. "A signature. Clerk's initials in the corner. The date stamp, which is a different stamp, which means a different transfer. And a reason to travel that matches whatever story the man tells when they question him on the quay."

"Tom can't act," Sable said. "He has a face like a slammed door."

"Then we give him a story so dull no door slams. A man moving cargo for Harrow & Sons, transferring to their Corvane run. Boring men pass. Angry men get searched." Maren pulled a fresh sheet toward her and uncapped the good iron-gall ink. "Names first. Thomas Merrick is fine. Who's the second?"

The question landed on the bench between them and lay there.

Sable had known it was coming. She had known since the night she found Maren's own name in the ledger's later pages, added in Aldous's hand in what must have been his final weeks — *F., Maren — flagged, inland inquiry pending* — and had said nothing, and had lain awake doing terrible mathematics ever since. One berth. Two people she loved. The problem did not change because they were both being polite about it at a workbench at two in the morning.

But Maren was looking at her with her father's grey eyes, and there was something in them Sable hadn't expected: not entitlement, not fear. A kind of offer.

"I'm not asking for it," Maren said. "I'm asking who you've written down. Because whoever sails on the Corvane is going to carry the atlas out of Greyhaven, and that's not a passenger, Sable. That's a courier."

The rain drummed. The lamp held.

"The atlas stays with me," Sable heard herself say. "It doesn't leave this city. If it leaves, the routes leave with it, and then the next family after the Corvane has nowhere to go."

Maren was quiet a moment. Then she nodded slowly, and something passed across her face that might have been relief, or disappointment, or both wearing the same coat.

"So you stay. And the atlas stays. And someone sails carrying papers instead of charts." She dipped the pen, wrote *T. Merrick* at the top of a practice sheet in a clerk's slanted hand, and pushed it across. "Then we'd better make these documents flawless. Because if Cray's examiner finds one flaw, he won't stop at one man. He'll follow the paper backward. To this bench. To this lamp. To us."

"To both of us," Sable said.

"To both of us," Maren agreed, and for the first time since she'd arrived, she smiled — a quick, crooked, unmistakably Fenwick smile, and Sable's throat tightened, because it was Aldous's smile exactly, resurrected at two in the morning over a tray of gum and alum and crime.

They worked until four. Maren taught her the date stamp, the clerk's initials — *R.G.H., always R.G.H., he presses lightly, see, lighter than the others* — and the trick of aging paper over the steam of the kettle so a new document smelled of drawers instead of dries. Sable ruined five sheets and perfected six, and somewhere in the small hours she stopped feeling like a woman committing forgery and started feeling like a woman completing an apprenticeship that had been interrupted by a funeral. Every transfer was a lesson Aldous had planned from the grave. Every ruined sheet was tuition paid.

Near dawn, Maren stretched, cracked her knuckles, and gathered the ruined practice sheets for the stove.

"Keep three," Sable said suddenly. "The best ones."

"Why?"

"Because if I'm ever questioned, I want to be able to hand over evidence of clumsy attempts and believe it myself." She heard how it sounded even as she said it, and grimaced. "That's a lie."

"It's a *plan*," Maren said. "Father would have approved. He liked lies with structural integrity." She fed the rest to the fire and watched them curl. Then, with her back still to Sable, she said: "There's something you should know. Cray didn't just visit me inland. He wrote. Twice since I arrived here. He wants me to come to the Registrar's Office Thursday. He says it's about my father's estate."

The fire popped. Sable felt the warmth drain out of the room anyway.

"Estate," she repeated.

"That's the word he used." Maren turned around. In the grey pre-dawn light her face was composed, but her hands, Sable noticed, had found each other and were holding on. "He also wrote a sentence I keep reading over and over. He said: *We have reason to believe your father's final work remains in Greyhaven, and that it may be in the possession of persons unknown to you.*"

Sable looked at the bench — at the tray, the stamps, the six perfect transfers drying under the blotter, the whole night's inheritance spread out like evidence.

"Thursday," she said. "That's three days."

"Yes," said Maren. "And I have to decide whether I walk into that office as his daughter — or as his accomplice. So do you. Because I don't think he invited me there to talk about paper."
