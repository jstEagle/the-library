## 12. What the Harbor Master Saw

The fog came off the water at dusk the way it always did in Greyhaven, thick as wool and smelling of tar and old fish, and Silas Doss stood at his office window and watched it swallow the harbor one piling at a time. Forty-one years he'd worked this quay. Nineteen of them as harbor master. He knew the water the way a priest knows his prayers, and he knew — had known for three months now, since they pulled Aldous Fenwick out of the channel with his skull cracked like a dropped egg — that the peace of this port was finished.

Behind him, the ledger lay open on his desk. Not the official one, with its columns of tonnage and crew lists and customs stamps. The other one. The thin black book Aldous had put into his hands eleven years ago, along with an envelope of banknotes and a sentence Doss had never forgotten:

*There will come a night when a ship leaves lighter than she should. You won't ask why, and I'll make certain you never have to.*

Doss poured himself two fingers of brandy and did not sit down. His knees had been bad since the winter of '04, but tonight he wanted to stand. Standing made it easier to feel like a man still capable of walking away from all of it, even if he'd never once done it.

* * *

He thought about the first time, because an old man's mind wanders where it shouldn't and the fog outside made everything feel like memory anyway.

It had been the *Merrow's Daughter*, back then — a coastal tramp so rusted through her hull should have been a violation in itself. Eleven souls aboard her that night instead of four, tucked into a false bulkhead aft of the hold that Doss had sworn to himself he hadn't seen, except that Aldous had walked him down the pier beforehand, arm looped through his like they were two old friends out for air, and said, *You'll see what you see, Silas. The question is what you write after.* And Aldous Fenwick had a way of saying things that made cowardice sound like craftsmanship. What you write after. As though the manifest were a chart and truth merely one coastline among many.

Doss had written four. The envelope under his door the next morning had held more money than he made in a season. He'd meant to send it back. He'd meant to, the way a man means to give up drink every Monday morning.

But then he'd gone down to the pier two nights later, alone, and watched the families come aboard — no, that wasn't right either. He hadn't watched them board; he'd been careful never to watch them board. What he'd watched was Aldous, standing at the gangway with his hat off and his gray hair damp in the spray, shaking each man's hand as though seeing him onto the evening packet to the capital. Shaking hands. That was the thing that had undone Doss's good resolutions. Not the money. Not even the fear. It was the courtesy of it, the dignity Aldous gave these people who were being erased — as if their leaving were not a flight but a departure, the kind you dressed for.

So Doss wrote four, and the *Merrow's Daughter* sailed light by seven, and for eleven years the arrangement held. Twice a year, sometimes three, a ship went out heavier than her papers claimed, and Doss took his envelope, and Aldous bought him dinner at the Gull & Anchor twice annually — always the same booth, always the same toast, *to accurate record-keeping*, delivered so dry that only the two of them ever laughed.

Then in March, Aldous Fenwick fell down a ladder in his own workshop, according to the coroner, and the dinners stopped, and the envelopes stopped, and Doss told himself the network was dead and he was glad of it. Glad. He'd rehearsed the word until it almost fit.

* * *

The knock came at half past eight, soft, three raps, then a pause, then two more. Doss's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

That was Aldous's knock. Had been, eleven years. Three and two, like a telegraph signal for *safe to approach*. Nobody living knew it but the two of them.

He set the glass down, crossed the room, and opened the door on a young woman in a plain gray coat with the collar turned up, hood darkening in the mist. For one absurd moment he thought the fog had produced a ghost of Fenwick himself — and then he saw the eyes, sharp and steady, and the ink stain on the side of her right hand, deep blue-black, the stain of someone who worked a pen or a brush the way a laborer works a shovel.

"You're the apprentice," he said.

"And you're expecting me," she said. "Or you wouldn't have answered."

He should have shut the door. He catalogued, in the space of a breath, all the reasons he should shut the door: the girl was twenty-some years old and unknown to him; the coroner's verdict might not suit her; men were asking questions all over this port lately; and Silas Doss was too old and too comfortable to start a new conspiracy from scratch with a stranger's daughter.

Instead he stepped back and held the door wide, because eleven years of a dead man's trust does not dissolve just because the man has died. Trust like that doesn't die. It just changes hands or rots.

"Miss Quintana," he said. "Sable Whitlock, I should say." He closed the door and shot both bolts. "Aldous spoke of you. Never your whole name — he was careful even with me — but he spoke of you. Said you inked a coast like you were remembering it rather than inventing it."

She didn't smile. She pulled back her hood, and Doss saw that she was prettier than her wariness allowed her to look, and more frightened than either. She stood in the middle of his office like a woman standing on a dock that was already casting off, committed and unsteady at once.

"He also said," Doss went on, returning to his desk, "that if anyone ever came to me using his knock, it would mean one of two things. Either the work needed finishing—"

"—or the work needed burying," Sable said. "I know. He told me the same about you. Though his version had fewer compliments in it."

"His version of everyone had fewer compliments in it. He saved those for the charts." Doss sat, gestured her into the chair across from him, and she sat the way people sit when they intend to leave quickly — spine straight, hands folded, weight forward. "Well then. Which is it? Finishing or burying?"

"Finishing," she said. "One ship's worth of finishing."

Doss looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the fog pressed against the window like something listening. He thought again of the envelope, of the dinners, of Aldous's hand on his arm at the gangway, and he heard himself say, in the voice of a man reading aloud from a document he'd hoped was lost:

"The *Corvane*. Departing on the spring tide, nineteen days from tomorrow. Cargo manifest filed as mixed freight and fourteen passengers. Thirteen berths sold."

Sable went very still. "You know the number already."

"I've known it since the manifest was posted Tuesday morning. I read every manifest, Miss Whitlock. I have read every manifest for nineteen years, and for eleven of them I read them twice — once for the Port Authority and once for a dead man." He turned the black ledger around on the desk so she could see it, though he kept two fingers on it, an anchor and a leash at once. "Thirteen berths. Thirteen passengers. One unclaimed."

Her breath came out slowly, carefully, the way a person exhales when they're carrying something breakable. "One berth," she repeated. "Exactly one."

"Exactly one. Which tells me whoever inherited Aldous's arithmetic has been counting the same as he used to. One berth is not an accident. One berth is a design — room enough to save, not room enough to be caught saving." He leaned back, and his bad knee throbbed its agreement with the whole sorry enterprise. "Now. Before we go one word further, you're going to tell me who you are in this. Not your name. Your part. Because there are three kinds of people who come to a harbor master asking after empty berths, girl, and two of them are wearing government boots this season."

* * *

She told him. Not all of it — Doss had spent a lifetime reading what people left off documents, and the omissions were plain enough. She told him about the atlas, the plates, the ledger coded in Aldous's marginal hand. She told him about the census taker without naming him, describing him so precisely — courteous, patient, asks the question a beat after politeness permits — that Doss grunted and reached for his brandy. She did not tell him about the brother. But when she said the word *berth*, her voice snagged on it, the way thread snags on a nail, and Doss, who had watched ten thousand departures and knew what grief looked like before it had a name, understood that the single berth was not an abstraction to this woman. It was a person. Possibly two.

He let her finish. Then he poured a second glass and pushed it across the desk to her, and she surprised him by drinking it like a workingman, straight down.

"Now hear what I have to say," Doss said, "because it's the reason you climbed my stairs, whether you knew it or not." He tapped the open manifest ledger. "Departure day inspections are changing. For eleven years, the Corvane's predecessors sailed on my certification alone. My stamp, my word, my columns. That's over. Word comes down from the Ministry — and it came down through Inspector Cray's office, which should tell you everything — that beginning this season, every outbound passenger vessel gets a field inspection of manifests against persons present. Not a review of paperwork. A count of bodies. Cray's own men on the quay, going berth by berth, face by face, paper by paper."

"When did this order arrive?"

"Six days ago."

Sable closed her eyes briefly. Doss did the arithmetic for her, because he was a man of columns and it was the only kindness he had left to offer: "Nineteen days until the tide. Six days since the order. Whoever is planning to fill that berth has had six days fewer to prepare papers that will hold up under a nose-to-nose inspection by men trained to smell forgery." He paused. "And it gets worse, so I'll say it plainly and once. Cray requested the inspection duty personally. Assigned it to his own detail. That man doesn't inspect, Miss Whitlock. He hunts, and he calls it inspecting afterward. Whatever name goes on that berth — whatever paper you bring me — it will be read by someone who is not looking for errors. He is looking for a story. A forged document is a fact. A nervous passenger is a story. Cray collects stories."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanged, patient and idiotic, marking a shoal nobody had cleared in a decade. Doss listened to it and thought: forty-one years, and the bell keeps ringing whether anyone steers clear or not. There was a sermon in that, probably. He was glad he'd never been the sort of man to go looking for sermons.

"The old network," Sable said at last. Her voice was level again, and Doss recognized the effort that cost her because he'd made the same effort at his own desk for thirty years. "Aldous's people. Pilots, agents, clerks. How many are still breathing?"

"Breathing, four. Usable—" He spread his hands, honest to the last column. "Two. Myself and Tam Ossler, the shipping agent who books the Corvane's berths. Tam is faithful but he frightens like a spooked horse, so you'll deal with me and I'll deal with him. The pilot, Hearn, retired inland in February and wants nothing but his garden. The purser on the Merrow line was reassigned — which is Ministry language for *asked questions and was removed*. Everyone else Aldous trusted was either never told enough to matter or is now telling the Ministry everything they were told." He met her eyes. "The network breathes, girl. Barely. It breathes the way an old man breathes in winter. But it breathes."

"That's more than I hoped for."

"It's less than you need." He pulled the manifest sheet toward himself and turned it so she could read it, and watched her do exactly what he'd known she'd do: run her finger down the passenger list with the same sure stroke she presumably laid along a coastline, hunting the gap. Row twelve. Blank where a name belonged. A blank space on a page, and the way she looked at it you'd think it was a wound.

"One berth," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "He built it this way. Even dead, he built it this way."

"Aldous never designed anything with a spare part in it," Doss agreed. "Which brings me to the last thing, and you'll forgive an old man for asking it straight." He folded his hands on the ledger. "Whoever fills that berth needs more than a paper. They need a face that survives scrutiny, luggage that makes sense, a reason for travel that holds up when Cray's man asks it twice in different words. Papers I can help with — Tam can slip the booking, I can certify the count. But the person themselves, Miss Whitlock — the flesh-and-blood passenger standing on my quay while a Ministry inspector looks into their eyes — that person will need to be calm. Calmer than you look tonight, if you don't mind my saying."

For a moment he thought she'd take offense. Instead something moved behind her eyes — some decision being weighed on scales he couldn't see — and she said, "It isn't me. I'm not sailing."

"No," Doss said. He'd known that too. People who intend to leave a port don't visit harbor masters at night; they buy tickets in daylight. "No, I didn't think it was you." He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the fog-shrouded masts, and asked the question with his back to her, which was how he asked all the questions that mattered:

"But you're going to tell me it's one person, aren't you? One name for one berth. Clean arithmetic, the way Aldous liked it." He turned. "Only you've got the look of a woman doing sums that won't resolve. So before you bring me your papers and your plan, let me save us both some sleep. If there are two names for that one blank line on row twelve — you settle it before you set foot on my quay again. Because on departure day, Cray's men will count thirteen bodies on that deck, and they will count them twice, and there is no power under God or the Ministry that can make a berth hold two people. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sable Whitlock stood, drew her hood up, and looked for a moment less like an apprentice and more like her master — that same terrible steadiness, the look of a person who has already begun redrawing the map to fit the territory.

"I understand," she said. "One line. One name." At the door she paused, hand on the bolt, and without turning around added: "Master Doss. When this is over — whichever way it ends — I'd like to buy you dinner at the Gull & Anchor. Same booth."

Doss felt something crack open in his chest that he'd sealed shut in March.

"To accurate record-keeping," he said roughly.

She slipped out into the fog, and Doss stood at his window a long while, watching the lamplights of the quay bloom and blur, listening to the bell buoy toll its patient warning over a shoal that had never yet been cleared. Then he went back to his desk, dipped his pen, and began — as he had eleven years ago, as he would until the end of his days —

to decide what to write after.
