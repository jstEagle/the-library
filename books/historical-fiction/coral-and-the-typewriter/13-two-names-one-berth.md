## 13. Two Names, One Berth

The ledger did not forgive her for reading it twice.

Sable had gone through the coded pages three times that evening — once with the atlas open, once with the atlas closed, and a third time in the desperate hope that the ink would rearrange itself into something kinder. It refused. There, between *Hollis, wright, four souls* and *Ivory, midwife, two souls*, sat the entry she had been dreading since Maren Fenwick set down her teacup and said, too casually, *They came to see me in Ashford before I ever decided to come here.*

**FENWICK, M. — daughter of subject. Inland residence lapsed. Present location: Greyhaven, suspected. Priority: raised.**

Raised priority. The phrase sat on the page like a hook sitting in flesh. Sable knew what the government's clerks meant by it, because Aldous had taught her the vocabulary of the thing he'd died fighting: raised priority meant a file moved from a shelf to a desk; a desk to a man; a man to a door knocked upon at an hour chosen for its cruelty.

She closed the ledger and pressed her palms flat against its cover as if she could hold the names still by force alone.

In the corner of the workshop, Maren slept in Aldous's old chair, her head tipped against the wing, her father's reading shawl pulled up around her ears. She had cried earlier — quietly, the way people cry who learned young that crying loudly accomplishes nothing — and then she had washed her face at the basin and said, "Well. Now you know why I really came."

Now Sable knew. Not inheritance. Not grief alone. Maren Fenwick had run inland-to-coast the way rabbits run for burrows, and the burrow was burning behind her faster than anyone had guessed.

Two names. One berth.

Sable lit the lamp down to its last inch of oil, took out a fresh sheet of survey paper — the good rag stock, the kind that took ink without bleeding — and began, because she could not help herself, to make a map of the problem.

* * *

She drew the harbor first. Old habit. Her hand knew Greyhaven's coastline better than her own face in a mirror: the curl of the breakwater, the shallow bite of Quay Three, the long throat of the channel where the Corvane would have to swing wide on the tide. Then, above the harbor, in smaller script, she wrote the true geography.

*Corvane. Departs tide after next. Nineteen days minus twelve now passed. Seven days remain.*

*Doss confirms: one unclaimed berth. One. The rest bought, sealed, promised.*

*B. Whitlock. Dockworker. Marked. My blood.*

*M. Fenwick. Daughter of the man who made me everything I am. Marked. Raised priority.*

She stared at the two lines until they blurred.

There was a kind of arithmetic that cartographers understood and no one else seemed to: that every map is a lie agreed upon, every projection a compromise, because you cannot flatten a sphere without tearing something. Mercator had torn the poles. Sable now understood that she was going to tear something too, and the only question left — the question she walked around all night like a woman circling a wound — was which edge of her world would be stretched until it broke.

Blood, whispered one voice, and it sounded like their father's voice, low and gravelly, the voice that had said *family is the only country that doesn't issue you papers.* Tom was hers. Whatever distance had grown between them — six years of silence, a funeral neither of them had managed to stand beside each other at, all the accumulated silt of a childhood that had ended badly — he was made of the same clay. Their mother's stubbornness. Their father's hands. When Sable imagined the resettlement trains, the gray cars, the lists read aloud in stations, she imagined Tom's name in a stranger's flat mouth and something in her chest turned over like a keel in a storm.

But then — promise. And the promise had a face too.

Maren stirred in the chair and did not wake. Forty-one years old, windburned from the coach ride, hands cracked from a lifetime of tending other people's gardens while her father drew maps for strangers' salvation. She had come into this workshop three weeks ago and within a day had begun restoring it — oiling the stiff hinges, sorting Aldous's pens by nib-width, finding his hiding places faster than Sable had found them in five years. She had looked at the wet-transfer trick in Chapter Eleven of their shared education and said, "Papa showed me that when I was nine. He used sugar water. You've been using gum arabic, that's why your edges feather." She had given Sable her father a second time, in small domestic details, and Sable had not known until tonight how much of her heart had quietly reorganized itself around that gift.

And Maren was flagged *raised*. Which meant, in the cold grammar of Cray's bureaucracy, that she would be taken first.

Tom, at least, had the docks. A hundred dockworkers watching each other's backs, a papers collective forming in the back rooms of taverns — he'd told her that much before storming out over the question of whether he'd even take the berth. He had people. He had plans. He had, God help him, a cause he'd chosen over his own skin, and though it terrified her, some buried part of Sable respected it more than she could say.

Maren had a workshop full of incriminating paper and a dead father's name, which in Greyhaven in 1911 was worth less than nothing. It was a debt.

Sable put down the pen. Picked it up. This was the trap of arithmetic — it pretended to be neutral. Two lives weighed on a scale, and the scale asked only for numbers. But lives were not weights. They were directions. Saving Tom pointed somewhere; saving Maren pointed somewhere else; and whichever direction she chose, she would live the rest of her life facing away from the other.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight, she tried the arguments on like coats.

*The argument for Tom:* Blood is the oldest contract. Aldous himself would have said so — half his escape routes were drawn for families, whole families, mothers routed with sons, brothers carried on sisters' documents. He never once split a family when he could help it. If she gave the berth to Maren and Tom were taken, she would be breaking the master's own deepest rule while wearing his apron. And Tom would go obediently, furiously silent, the way he went through their father's funeral, and he would never — the thought arrived with total clarity — he would never forgive her, and worse, she would deserve it.

*The argument for Maren:* Maren is marked because of the atlas. Because Aldous drew those routes, because he hid them in coastlines, because Sable chose to finish what he started rather than burn it. Every day the atlas existed in this workshop, the danger around Maren thickened like fog off the estuary. Sable had, in effect, painted a target on her guest. Duty wasn't only about who you loved first. It was about whose fire you had fed.

And underneath both arguments, quieter than either, ran a third current she kept trying not to hear:

Elias.

She hated that he entered the calculation at all. But honesty — that brutal, lamplit honesty she could practice only on blank paper at three in the morning — demanded it. If the raid came, if Cray's men climbed these stairs, then whoever remained in Greyhaven would need someone on the inside of the machinery hunting them. Elias Vane was a crack running through that machinery, widening a little more each week. He had warned her — *some columns should stay blank* — in a voice that cost him something to use. He was, whether he knew it or not, becoming part of her network, the way Doss was, the way the old bribed harbor clerk had been. A network needed tenders. It needed someone ashore.

And Maren could carry the atlas. That was the practical heart of it, the fact Sable kept circling back to like a compass needle finding north: the Corvane sailed toward safer ports, and the atlas needed to reach safer ports, and the person best suited to deliver it — Fenwick by name, Fenwick by training, Fenwick by blood — was Maren. Send Maren and you saved a life *and* the truth. Send Tom and you saved a life and left the atlas in a workshop slated for raiding, in the hands of a forger's apprentice who might be arrested any morning.

It was such a clean solution. That was what frightened her. Clean solutions in Sable's experience always required somebody to be erased neatly from the equation, and the somebody was always a person, and the person was always Tom.

She remembered him at eleven years old, holding their father's broken sextant, insisting he could fix it. He couldn't. He'd made it worse. But he'd sat up three nights with tweezers and wire, refusing help, refusing sense, until their father pried it gently from his hands and said, *Some things you mend by letting go, boy.*

She wondered if this was that. She wondered if she would know, if Tom were standing in front of her, whether this was the brother asking to be saved or the man choosing to stand — and whether she had any right to know the difference from the outside.

* * *

The lamp guttered. Sable trimmed the wick and found Maren awake, watching her from the chair with her father's exact shade of gray eyes.

"You've been writing all night," Maren said. "Not charting. Writing." She rose, wrapped the shawl tighter, and crossed the room to stand over the sheet. Sable didn't cover it. There seemed little point anymore.

Maren read the two lines. *B. Whitlock. M. Fenwick.* Her face did something complicated and settled, finally, into a calm that Sable recognized because she practiced it herself daily: the composure of a person deciding to lose something gracefully.

"It's mine to offer," Maren said. "I'm offering it. Take the arithmetic out of the middle of the night and look at it in daylight — you'll see it the way everyone will see it. Your brother. Your blood. No one in this city, not Doss, not the collective, not your census man, would fault you."

"Everyone would fault me," Sable said. "You'd fault you. That's the worst of it."

"Yes," Maren agreed, and almost smiled. "Papa used to say the hardest charts are the ones where every heading is true. You can sail any of these courses, Sable. They all get somewhere. You just have to choose which shore you can bear to stand on."

"That's not comfort."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Sable looked down at the harbor she'd drawn, the little channel, the tiny careful ship. Nineteen days ago she had believed the hardest thing in the world would be forging a stamp convincingly enough to fool an inspector. She had been a simpler woman then, ruined sheets and burnt fingers and one problem at a time.

"Seven days," she said aloud. "I need seven days. Don't decide anything irrevocable in seven days, either of us. And Maren—" she caught the older woman's wrist, felt the fine tremor in it that composure couldn't quite kill, "—if I send you, you carry the atlas. You finish the final page if anything happens to me. Swear it."

"I swear," Maren said. Then, softly: "And if you send Tom?"

"Then I'll carry it myself. Until they come for me."

The lamp finally died, and they stood together in the gray pre-dawn light, two women over one harbor, and outside the window the tide was turning — low water slackening, beginning, imperceptibly, to rise.

A knock came at the street door below. Not the workshop's stairs — the street door, heavy, official, three measured raps.

Maren's hand closed on Sable's arm. Through the frosted glass of the transom, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots, several pairs, and a voice Sable knew almost as well as her own — but wrong somehow, flattened, formal, speaking to someone beside him:

"Cray wants the manifests checked against the upstairs premises. Start with the chart-room floor."
