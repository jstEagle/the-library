## 14. Tom Refuses

The tenement on Cordwain Row smelled of boiled cabbage and river rot, as it always did, but that evening it smelled of fear too — Tom Whitlock could name fear the way other men named fish, having handled enough of both. It came off the walls these days, seeped up through the floorboards from the neighbors who whispered behind their doors about foremen and manifests and men in gray coats taking names at the dock gates.

He was mending a net when Sable knocked. Three raps, precise as a surveyor's chain. He knew her knock now; two weeks of knowing it again after eleven years of not, and still his hands went stiff every time he heard it.

She came in without waiting, which was like her, and she stood a moment in the doorway with the lamplight behind her so he couldn't read her face, which was also like her. Their father's face had been an open book. Sable's was a sealed ledger, and Tom had spent his whole life failing to crack the cipher.

"You've been to the harbor," he said, working the netting shuttle through the mesh. "Doss told me you'd been asking at the office."

"I've been everywhere." She hung her coat on the one nail by the door, an act of ownership so small and so characteristic that something in his chest twisted. "Tom, sit down."

"I'm sitting."

"Sit properly. This isn't news you take standing up."

He set the shuttle down. The lamplight caught her face then, and he saw what she'd been carrying: not dread exactly, but the particular exhaustion of someone who has done arithmetic all night and hates the answer.

"The Corvane sails in five days," she said. "There's a berth. There's been one berth this whole time, and I've been holding it for you."

"Then hold it another five days and I'll fill it." His voice came out lighter than the thing felt. "You make it sound like a funeral."

"Sable—"

"You're going to say there's a complication." He stood, because his body decided before his mind did, and his body had always been quicker. "You've got that look. The Fenwick look. Aldous used to wear it when a chart wouldn't come clean."

Something crossed her face at the old man's name — grief worn smooth as sea glass — and Tom understood, in the way he sometimes understood things too late, that he'd said it wrong. That he kept saying everything wrong where his sister was concerned.

"There are two names," she said quietly. "Yours and Maren's."

The room went very still. Outside, a tram bell rang somewhere down Cordwain Row, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"Maren Fenwick," he said.

"She's flagged. Same as you. In the same ledger, on the same list, under the same order for resettlement. Cray's men have already visited her once. They'll visit again, and next time they won't bring tea." Sable's hands were folded in front of her like a woman at graveside. "The berth belongs to whoever needs it most, and I can't — Tom, I have sat with this for three nights and I cannot find a way to make it be both of you."

He laughed. It came out ugly, surprising even himself, a bark of noise that scattered into the dark corners of the room.

"You can't find a way," he repeated. "Eleven years, Sable. Eleven years you didn't cross the street to find me, and now you stand in my kitchen doing sums over whether I live."

"That's not fair."

"It's entirely fair. It's the fairest thing anyone's said in this room in years." He heard himself rising, heard the old anger coming up out of the cellar where he kept it, the anger he'd fed at their father's funeral and every day since, and he hated the taste of it and drank it anyway. "You know what I did when Da died? I stood at the grave and I looked at you across the hole in the ground and I thought, *there*. There is the person who will not look at me. You had your maps and your master and his fine workshop full of ink and instruments, and I had a hook chain and a bunk I shared with three men, and neither of us—"

"Don't you dare put Aldous in this."

"He's all over it! He's in every line of that ledger of yours!" Tom flung his hand toward the harbor, east, where the Corvane rode at anchor with its single empty berth like a held breath. "Who do you think taught you to choose paper over people? I learned to hate him secondhand, through you, through eleven years of watching you become somebody who could stand in a doorway and do arithmetic about her own brother!"

The words landed. He watched them land, saw them go into her like hooks into flesh, and some part of him — the part that had carried her on his shoulders along the breakwater when she was seven and terrified of gulls — screamed at him to stop. But the other part, the bigger part, the one built of eleven years of night shifts and nobody's name spoken kindly, kept hauling.

"And now," he said, quieter, crueler for being quiet, "you've found me again because the government found me first. That's the whole of it, isn't it? If the purge hadn't put my name on a list, you'd never have walked up those stairs."

Sable didn't move. That was the terrible thing about her; she never moved when you wanted her to. She just stood in the lamplight with her folded hands and let the words hit, one after another, taking each one like a chart takes a pin.

"Yes," she said finally. "That's the whole of it. And I'm ashamed of it, and I'm here anyway, and the arithmetic stands. One berth. Two names. Yours or Maren's."

"Then it's mine."

"Tom—"

"*It's mine.*" He crossed to her, close enough to see the lamp flame doubled in her eyes. "I'm your blood. She's a stranger who inherited a workshop. I'm sorry for her, truly, but you don't give the last seat off the quay to a stranger."

"She's not a stranger. She's his daughter. She's the closest thing I had to family for eleven years, which is more than I can say for you."

The silence afterward had weight, like water. Tom stood there breathing hard, fists at his sides, and slowly, degree by degree, the rage drained out of him the way a tide goes out — not vanishing but receding, leaving exposed all the wreckage it had covered. He looked at his sister, really looked, and saw the shadows under her eyes, the ink stains on her right hand that no amount of scrubbing would lift, the way she held herself braced against whatever he would say next.

She was terrified. She had been terrified this whole conversation, and she had stood there anyway and told him the truth, because that was what Sable did. She carried terrible things into rooms and set them on the table.

He turned away first. Went to the window. Below, Cordwain Row was lighting its lamps one by one, and beyond the rooftops he could feel the harbor the way he always felt it, a great slow animal turning over in its sleep.

"There's something I have to tell you," he said. "And you're going to want to argue, so let me get it all out before you start."

* * *

He told it facing the window, because it was easier not to watch her face while he did.

"After Da's funeral, when I stopped coming around — I didn't just get angry and stay angry. Angry needs feeding, Sable. Mine got fed at the docks. You know what it's like down there now. New foremen every month, and every one of them carrying a book. Not a cargo manifest — a book of *names*. Where were you born. What's your father's name. Show me your papers. And if your papers have a smudge on them, if the stamp's faded, if some clerk wrote *Whitlock* instead of *Whitlok*, you're flagged. You're a column in somebody's ledger, and columns get removed."

"I know what it's like," she said softly. "I've seen the lists."

"Then you know it's not just strangers on them. It's Halloran from No. 4 berth, who taught me to splice wire. It's the Okafor widow and her two boys. It's half the night shift." He pressed his forehead briefly against the cool glass. "Six weeks ago a group of us started meeting. Sundays, back room of the Rope & Anchor, then a cellar on Pilchard Lane when that got warm. Dockworkers, mostly. A schoolteacher. One of the harbor clerks — I won't say who. We call it the papers collective, though it's more than papers now. We copy documents. We teach men what questions the census men are allowed to ask and what answers they're obliged to give. We keep duplicates of everything so when they erase a man, there's a record left saying he existed."

Sable had gone very quiet behind him. He could feel her listening the way he used to feel weather coming off the water.

"Three weeks ago we took a vote," he went on. "Whether any of us marked for resettlement should go, or whether we should stand. And I voted to stand, Sable. I voted before you ever knocked on my door. Before the Corvane, before the berth, before any of it." He turned around then. "I was always going to stay."

"You don't have to be a martyr to prove you're brave."

"This isn't martyrdom. This is the first useful thing I've ever done with my life." He almost smiled. "You think I'm throwing myself away. I think I'm finally picking something up. Every Sunday there are more of us. Men who were scared alone and aren't anymore. You can't ship that across the sea in a berth, Sable. It doesn't fit in a berth. It only works if it stays."

"Tom." Her voice cracked on his name, and it undid him more than the shouting had. "They will take you. Not maybe. They *will*. Cray is closing in on the whole network — on the atlas, on the routes, on everyone Aldous ever helped. Staying isn't resistance if they've already got your name in a book."

"Then let them have the name." He said it simply, and meant it, and was startled by how light he felt meaning it. "A name's the cheapest thing I own. What they can't take is what I know — every berth, every blind alley, every foreman on the take, every route through the dock gates. Your master drew escape routes on charts. I *am* one. I've walked them all."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and he watched something move behind her eyes — the cartographer in her, he realized later, measuring terrain, calculating what could be held and what must be surrendered. She had spent three nights doing sums about his life. Now she was discovering that the variable she'd been solving for had already solved itself.

"You're determined," she said at last.

"I am."

"Determined men drown, Tom. I've charted coasts. Determination is what fills the wrecks."

"Stubbornness drowns. Determination gets counted." He crossed back to her and, for the first time since their father's funeral, took her hands. They were cold and ink-stained and smaller than he remembered, and they trembled slightly in his grip, and he understood that this woman who could lie smoothly to a census taker and forge a government seal was, underneath it all, his little sister, frightened and alone and trying to carry an atlas full of other people's lives. "Let the berth go to Maren. She needs it, and she's carrying your master's work out of the country, which matters more than my pride. I'll be the one who stays and keeps the door open behind her."

Her jaw worked. He watched her fight it — watched the whole war happen in her face, love against logic against the terrible habit of protecting people whether they wanted it or not.

"If you stay," she said finally, hoarsely, "you stay *careful*. You use the routes I can draw you. You let me update your papers — I'm better at it now than I was a week ago, and you don't ask why. And if Cray's men come to the docks—" her grip tightened on his hands, "—you run. Promise me you'll run. Standing your ground is only courage until the moment it's just a body in the harbor."

"Promise," he said, though they both knew promises between Whitlocks were soft currency.

She exhaled, long and unsteady, and something in the room changed — the air itself seeming to settle, the way a house does after a storm passes over. She pulled one hand free and pushed it through her hair, and for a moment she looked seventeen again, exhausted at the end of a long day in the workshop, and he loved her so fiercely it frightened him.

"Maren gets the berth," she said. "I'll tell her tonight. God help me, I'll have to tell her tonight." A broken laugh escaped her. "She'll argue too, you know. She'll try to give it back to you. This family of stragglers Aldous collected — none of us knows how to be saved."

"We'll learn," Tom said. "Some of us. The rest of us will hold the door."

She stayed long enough to eat a little of the bread and cheese he put in front of her, and they talked — carefully at first, then easier — about the collective and the routes, and twice he caught her eyes going distant and knew she was charting something in her head, plotting courses for people she couldn't say aloud. When she rose to leave, he walked her down the stairs into the lamplit street, and at the corner of Cordwain Row she stopped.

"There's a man," she said, not looking at him. "The census taker. Elias Vane. He's not what he seems, and neither am I, when I'm near him, and I don't know yet which way that will break. If anything happens to me—"

"Nothing will happen to you."

"If it does, go to Doss at the harbor office. Say 'the last page is blank.' He'll understand."

Before he could answer she was gone, quick and quiet up the hill toward the workshop, a slim dark figure threading between the lamps, and Tom stood at the corner watching until the darkness took her.

He walked back up the stairs slowly. The net lay unfinished where he'd left it. He sat, took up the shuttle, worked three meshes, and stopped.

Through the window, far off, the harbor lights burned along the waterfront, and among them, he knew, were men with books of names, and among the names was his. Five days until the Corvane sailed, carrying Maren and the atlas and his sister's sacrifice out to sea. Five days to put the papers collective behind locks and cellars before Cray's men came asking.

And somewhere across the city, in a workshop full of ink and inheritance, Sable was already moving toward something she hadn't told him about — he'd seen it in her face at the corner, the shadow of a decision not yet made. She had given up the berth too easily, he thought. Sable Whitlock never gave up anything without trading for something else.

Whatever she was planning, it had started tonight.
