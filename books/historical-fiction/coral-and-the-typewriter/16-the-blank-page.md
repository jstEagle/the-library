## 16. The Blank Page

The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil by the time Sable Whitlock finished the second name.

She set down the pen and held the paper at arm's length, the way Aldous had taught her to hold anything she might have to destroy in a hurry — lightly, by one corner, as if it were already ash. In the lamplight the document looked convincingly dull: a third-class passage certificate for the steamship *Corvane*, sailing on the nineteenth, berth and mess assigned, stamped with the harbor authority's seal in faded violet ink that was only slightly too fresh if you knew to breathe over it.

Two names now. *Maren Fenwick* in the first berth line, her hand steady enough that even she was impressed. And in the second — the name she had argued with herself about for six hours before writing it at all — *Thomas Whitlock*, occupation listed as *cartographic assistant*, which was a lie built on top of a lie built on top of a truth, since he had spent four years hauling crates and had never once touched a chart until last week.

Two people. One berth confirmed. The arithmetic did not close. It gaped open like a wound she kept probing to see if it hurt.

*You are gambling,* she told herself, and then corrected it, because Aldous would have corrected it: *No. A gambler hopes. You have calculated.*

She laid both papers side by side on the drafting table and weighted their corners with the brass objects of Aldous's desk — the dividers, the paper knife shaped like a gull's wing, the little brass anchor whose purpose nobody had ever known. Then she stood back and looked at what she had made.

The workshop around her had become something else entirely these past weeks. It was still cluttered — Aldous's chaos preserved like a fly in amber, his tea-stained mugs still ranked along the windowsill, his coat still on its hook as though he might come in from the fog any minute smelling of pipe smoke and salt — but now there were signs of a different trade layered over the old one. Sheets of ruined practice documents stacked under the blotter like a card player's discards. A saucer of diluted ink in three shades of officialdom. The seal press out of its drawer, warm from use. Maren's chair pulled up beside hers, angled so the two of them could work shoulder to shoulder over the same page, which they had done every night this week until Maren went home to sleep in her father's old bed and Sable stayed behind with the lamp.

Sable checked the seals again. She always checked them twice; the habit had been drilled into her with a ruler across the knuckles. *A forger who trusts herself once is a forger who hangs twice.* The wet-transfer impressions had taken beautifully — Maren's trick, Aldous's trick really, lifting an original stamp with dampened paper and pressing it onto the forgery so that the ink itself was genuine, only its location a lie. *The state can't tell you the stamp is wrong,* Maren had said, watching Sable's face as the transfer peeled away whole, *because the state made the stamp. All you've done is move it.* There was something almost theological in it. Forgery as translation rather than invention.

But the papers were only half the gamble. The other half sat wrapped in oilcloth on the shelf above the fireplace, where the chimney flue met the iron plate: the atlas. Aldous Fenwick's life's work, the thing he had died keeping out of Inspector Cray's hands, every coastal plate annotated in his fine marginal cipher with the routes that had carried — Sable knew now, she could not unknow — better than two hundred families out of Greyhaven and ports north while the government called it resettlement and meant something else.

Her plan, if it deserved the name, was this: the shipping agent on Pier Nine, a soft-bellied man named Quill whose ledger had more fingers in it than an octopus, would sell passage to anyone with money or leverage. The *Corvane* had one unclaimed berth. Doss the harbor master had confirmed it with the weary certainty of a man reading his own obituary: *one berth, Miss, and Cray's men will walk the manifest on departure day, so whatever you're carrying, carry it well.* Money might buy the berth. But money left a trail, and trails were what Cray lived on.

The atlas left no trail. The atlas was a treasure, and men like Quill understood treasures instinctively. Deliver it into his keeping, tell him exactly what it was worth and exactly who else wanted it, and let him do the arithmetic that came naturally to men who profited from other people's desperation: a second berth in exchange for a document that could buy him a house in the countryside and insurance against every official who had ever leaned on him. Or, failing that, the threat of it — because the atlas in the wrong hands was also a rope, and Quill would understand that a man holding a rope should be careful whom he handed it to.

It meant giving up the atlas. Giving up the escape routes Aldous had bled to protect, handing them to a profiteer who would use them until they wore thin and then sell them outright. Every idealist bone in her body recoiled from it.

But ideals didn't float, and Tom couldn't swim to safety on principle.

She thought of Tom's face when he'd said it — *I'm staying, Sable. I've got men depending on me, men with worse papers than mine* — and felt the old familiar anger rise and then, strangely, subside into something steadier. He had chosen. Fine. She had chosen too. She would forge his way off the docks whether he thanked her or cursed her, because that was what family meant when it had nothing else left: you saved them anyway, and let them be furious about it later, alive.

And Maren. Maren, whose name was in the ledger under her father's — flagged, marked, inheritable guilt. Maren, who had arrived claiming inheritance and found instead a conspiracy, and had not run inland but had rolled up her sleeves and shown Sable how to steal a stamp. Two names on the papers. One berth. The blank space where the solution should be.

Sable banked the lamp lower and took out a clean sheet of paper.

Not for forging. For confessing.

She had been avoiding this all evening — all week, if she were honest, all month — the way one avoids the last plate of a set, because once it was inked the work was real and finished and could betray you. But Doss's warning rang in her ears. *Cray's men will inspect the manifests.* If the papers wobbled under scrutiny — and forged papers always wobbled eventually; it was only a question of whose eye caught the tremor — then someone would have to stand between the forgery and the gallows, and there was exactly one person positioned to do it, and he was the same person she was most lying to.

Elias Vane. Census taker. Hunter in a clerk's coat. The man whose questions were too precise and whose silences were more precise still, who had stood in this very workshop with the census ledger open on his knee and looked at her the way a man looks at a chart when he suspects the coastline has been redrawn. *Some columns should stay blank.* He had said that to her, softly, on his way out the door, and she had spent three nights deciding whether it was a warning or a confession or a trap, and had concluded, with the calm that comes at the edge of ruin, that it was all three.

If she failed, he would find everything: the plates in the chimney, the ruined practice sheets under the blotter, the seal press warm in its drawer, and her name at the center of it like a compass rose. What happened then depended entirely on whether Elias Vane was the man his office said he was or the man his eyes said he was. She could not control that. But she could make sure that if he chose to save her, he did it knowing everything — not out of ignorance that could be punished as naivety, but out of knowledge, which was harder to prosecute and impossible to take back.

She dipped the pen. Wrote:

*Mr. Vane —*

*By the time you read this, either I am gone or I am caught, and in both cases you deserve to know the shape of what you stepped into. The atlas is real. The routes are real. The purge is real, and your inspector knows it, because he helped build the lists. Aldous Fenwick died for a piece of paper, and I have spent three months finishing what he started, and I am not sorry. If you are reading this, I failed, and my failure will cost you something whether you forgive me or not. I have watched you choose your questions carefully for weeks, and I think you have been choosing longer than I have. I won't insult you by asking you to lie for me. I'll only say this: the columns you left blank were the kindest thing anyone has done for me since Aldous died, and if there is a version of you that survives this year, I hope it is the version that kept them blank.*

*— S. Whitlock*

She read it over. It was too much. It was not enough. Every sentence was a door opened in a burning house. She almost struck through the last lines — *I think you have been choosing longer than I have* — because writing it made the feeling real, and the feeling was the one luxury she had forbidden herself since the night she'd first lied to him across this very table.

She left it in. Liars' letters were for liars who expected to survive.

She folded the sheet in thirds, sealed it with plain wax — no monogram, nothing to identify the sender at a glance — and wrote on the outside, in a deliberately bland clerk's hand: *For Mr. E. Vane, Census Office. To be opened upon conclusion of business at 14 Gullwing Lane.* Then she considered where to leave it.

Under the door of the census office was too public. By post was too slow and created a record. No. It had to be somewhere Elias himself would find, somewhere that required no delivery and no witness. She thought of his habits — she had catalogued them without meaning to, the way she catalogued coastlines — the way he always came to the workshop's front door, never the alley; the way he glanced at the tide table pinned by the harbor office as a matter of routine; the way his eyes went, each visit, to the drafting table and the lamp and then, briefly, always briefly, to her hands.

His visits were pretextual. His real errand was her. So the letter would wait where his next pretext would lead him: here, under the brass anchor on Aldous's desk, half-hidden, half-offered, like a coin left on a grave. If she succeeded, she would burn it unread. If she failed, he would find it among the evidence, and the finding would be its own small act of trust — the last map she could draw for him, pointing at her own heart.

*There,* she thought. *Now ruin has an address.*

* * *

Maren came at dawn, letting herself in with her father's key, bringing bread and a jar of dripping and the particular silence of a woman who has slept badly and decided to be useful about it. She set the food on the workbench without ceremony, looked at the two completed passage papers drying between the weights, and read them upside down, which was after all how she had learned to read documents, in her father's shop, before the inland years.

"Quill first," she said. "Before the manifests are drawn. Once Doss writes the list, changing it takes bribes we don't have."

"I know."

"And if Quill doesn't want the atlas?"

"Then I offer him the threat of it." Sable tore the bread and handed half across the table, an exchange so domestic it belonged to another century, another family. "A man like Quill doesn't have to be persuaded to want leverage. He only has to be shown which end of it he's holding."

Maren chewed slowly. Her father's grey eyes looked out of her tired face, and for a moment Sable had to look away, because grief ambushed her sideways like that, wearing other people's expressions.

"He'd hate this," Maren said quietly. "What we're doing to his atlas."

"He'd hate more what happens to us if we don't." Sable swallowed. "He drew routes for strangers, Maren. Hundreds of them. He never once got to see a single ship leave. We're going to watch two names walk up a gangway. I think —" her voice caught on something and she pushed through it — "I think that's the ending he was drawing toward all along. He just ran out of ink."

Maren said nothing for a long moment. Then she reached across the table, took the forged papers, and began packing them into the oilcloth wrapper alongside the atlas, her movements precise, unhurried, a cartographer's daughter handling the most dangerous cargo of her life.

"We deliver at ten," she said. "Quill opens his office at ten and he's greediest before lunch. Wear the grey coat. You look like somebody's respectable niece."

"The grey coat has blood on the cuff."

"Then fold the cuff. Nobody inspects a niece's cuffs." Maren paused, wrapper half-folded, and looked up. "Sable. If Quill takes the atlas and sells it to Cray anyway—"

"Then we'll know by noon, and Tom will have nineteen hours to run without papers, and I'll have written a letter to the only man in Greyhaven who might still stop it being worth nothing at all."

"That's not a plan. That's a prayer with punctuation."

"It's the only liturgy I know."

They worked in silence after that, wrapping, weighting, hiding. The atlas went back on the chimney shelf for one final hour. The letter went under the brass anchor on Aldous's desk, where the morning light through the dirty window fell across it like a pale flag.

At half past nine Sable put on the grey coat and folded the cuff. At the door she stopped and looked back at the workshop — at the mugs, the coat on its hook, the lamp burnt dry, the letter waiting under twelve grams of engraved brass — and she felt the calm settle over her fully now, the terrible weightless calm of a woman standing on a finished map at the very edge of the surveyed coast, where the ink stops and the water begins.

Beyond that line, nothing was certain except the price.

She closed the door behind her, and somewhere across the rooftops the nine o'clock bell began to ring.
