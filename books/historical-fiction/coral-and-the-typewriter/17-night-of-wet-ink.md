## 17. Night of Wet Ink

*Sable*

The lamp had burned down to its last finger of oil when Maren set the second pot of ink beside my elbow without a word. She had learned, in the three weeks since she'd come to claim her father's workshop, to read the language of my hands — the way my brush hovered meant I was counting something in my head, and the way it dipped too fast meant I was afraid.

We were both afraid. We worked anyway.

The final page of Aldous Fenwick's atlas lay pinned flat on the drafting table under four brass weights, its coastline half-erased and half-dreamed. The original showed a channel through the shoals east of Greyhaven — the route that would carry the *Corvane* past the customs cutters and out into open water where no one could follow. My task tonight was simpler than forgery and harder: after the ship sailed, the page had to lie. Any hand that opened this atlas after us was to see a dead channel, silted, impassable. The escape route would exist only in memory — mine, and now Maren's.

"Again," she said softly.

I lifted my pen and drew the false shoal for the ninth time, a long curling lip of sand across the channel mouth. Nine times was not vanity. Nine times was what Aldous would have demanded. *A map is a promise,* he used to say, tapping the table with his pipe stem. *And a liar's promise is worse than no promise at all.*

"You're pressing too hard on the eastern curve," Maren said. "Father did his shoals like breathing — light at the top of the arc."

"I know how he did them."

"Yes," she said. "You do." There was no sting in it. That was the strangest thing about Maren Fenwick — she carried her grief like ballast, steadying the boat rather than sinking it. "That one will do. That one is his."

I sat back and looked at it until my eyes stung. It was his. Somewhere in the last month my hand had stopped imitating Aldous Fenwick and started remembering him, and there was no difference anymore between forgery and inheritance.

* * *

*Maren*

Sable slept in her chair for forty minutes — I counted by the clock Father kept wound for thirty years — and then woke exactly as she always did, all at once, hands already reaching for the work. I envied her that. My own sleep came like a thief and left the same way, and I had learned not to trust anything I dreamed.

While she trimmed the new page to size, I packed the atlas. This was my part, the part I had claimed without asking. The plates went in order, each wrapped in waxed linen, each corner padded with wool so they would not chime against one another in transit. A cartographer's daughter knows that paper speaks. Loose sheets rattle; a chart slid carelessly into a case whispers along its edge like a drawn blade. Everything we sent out tonight had to be silent as a held breath.

"Agent Brask expects delivery before the eight o'clock bell," Sable said, squinting at the seam of the final plate. "Doss confirmed the manifest this afternoon. Two berths. Corvane, tide at noon tomorrow."

*Two berths.* She said it evenly, but I heard the arithmetic underneath, because I had done the same arithmetic myself, alone, in the dark of the guest room upstairs, and hated myself for doing it. Tom Whitlock would not take his berth; he had told her so with the stubborn calm of a man who has already buried his fear alive. Which meant the second berth was mine, bought with an atlas I had inherited and could not have saved alone.

"Maren." Sable was looking at me across the table. "Say it, whatever it is."

"I was going to say the wax is ready."

"No, you weren't."

I pressed the sealing iron down over the wick and watched it bloom red. "I was going to say thank you," I said. "And that I know you gave up something to make it two names instead of one, and I will not insult you by guessing what."

For a moment she looked young — younger than me, though I think we were nearly the same age; grief had simply aged us on different schedules. Then she nodded once, and we sealed the atlas.

* * *

*Sable*

The knock came at ten minutes past two in the morning.

Not Elias's knock. I had learned Elias Vane's knock the way you learn a voice — twice, unhurried, knuckle rather than fist, the knock of a man who expected to be invited in and would wait to be worth inviting. This was three rapid strikes, then silence, then three more, and beneath it, faint as a fish under ice, the sound of more than one pair of feet on the stair.

Maren froze with the atlas half-lifted. Her face in the lamplight was very pale and very still, and I understood in one cold drop that Inspector Cray had already been to her door once, before she came inland to Greyhaven, and that her body remembered him.

"Chimney," I breathed.

We had rehearsed this. God help us, we had actually rehearsed it, two women in a workshop at midnight walking the route with our eyes closed: the loose brick at the hearth's flank, the tin box of practice sheets — the ruined stamps, the failures, everything incriminating — swung out on its cord and lowered into the flue, the brick seated back with a palm, not a fist, so it wouldn't ring. Forty seconds, start to finish, if your hands didn't shake.

My hands shook. I dropped the cord once. The tin struck the chimney's throat with a soft metallic cough and hung there, swinging, and Maren put her whole arm into the dark flue to still it, soot to her shoulder, while downstairs the knocking came again, harder, and a voice called out with terrible politeness: *"Census verification. Open in the name of the Registry."*

"The atlas," I whispered.

She pulled her arm free and we got it into the sea chest together — lid down, blankets over it, a mending basket tossed on top with a shirt half-folded hanging out the side, the way a real household leaves things. Real households are messy. Forged ones are tidy. I kicked the drafting stool to an angle no careful person would leave it in and pinched out the second lamp between finger and thumb, and the smoke stung my eyes and I was grateful for it, because wet eyes look like ordinary tiredness.

Maren opened the door on the fourth round of knocking.

There were two of them. Neither was Cray — scouts, then, the thin end of him. The tall one held a lantern high so its light crossed the room in slow stripes. The short one had a ledger of his own, which I thought was almost funny: they had come to count us the way Elias had once come to count us, except these men counted like thieves.

"Apologies for the hour," the tall one said. "Registry business. There's an irregularity in the Fenwick record."

"My father is dead," Maren said, with just enough roughness in it. "His record can't get more irregular than that."

"We'd like to see the premises."

They came in because men like that always come in; the invitation is a formality they perform for their own ledgers. I sat down at the work table as though I had been sitting there all night, and I put my hands flat on the wood so they would not see them tremble, and I began, with deliberate slowness, to sharpen a pencil.

Small movements. Precise movements. That was the whole art of surviving the next six minutes: do nothing sudden, finish every small motion you begin, let the pencil travel the sharpener's throat in even strokes while the tall one's lantern walked across my charts of legitimate harbors, my surveys of river widths, my invoices from respectable clients. Let it pause at the sea chest. Let it pause longer.

"Family piece?" he asked, resting one hand on the chest lid.

"Our father's winter coats," Maren said. "You're welcome to smell the mothballs."

He smiled thinly and did not open it. Later I would understand why — later, Elias would tell me that scouts worked from lists, and that a sea chest full of coats matched nothing on any list, and that the banality of our lies was what saved us. But in the moment I only knew that his hand lifted away and my heart came down off the ceiling by inches, and I made myself keep sharpening the pencil, because a woman who stops in the middle of a small motion is a woman who admits the motion mattered.

The short one drifted toward the hearth.

I heard the fire's remains tick. I heard him crouch. I heard him hold the lantern into the flue, and the light came back out black-edged with soot, and I sharpened my pencil to a needle point and then kept turning it, turning it, giving my terror somewhere to go that wasn't my face.

"Soot," he said, disgusted, standing, wiping his palm on his coat. "Nobody's swept that since the old man died."

"Nobody has," Maren agreed. "It's on the list."

* * *

*Maren*

They asked routine questions at the door — names, births, whether I had transferred my inland residency properly, whether the apprentice was registered under the workshop or under her family name. Sable answered hers like coin counted out. I answered mine and discovered, halfway through, that I was no longer performing grief. It was simply true. Every question about Aldous Fenwick passed through the fact of him, dead these four months, murdered by paperwork and powerful men, and my anger did the lying for me and did it well.

At the threshold the tall scout turned. "The Registry may call again. With Inspector Cray."

"Then I'll make tea," I said, and shut the door on whatever his face did.

We stood in the middle of the room and listened to their boots descend — twelve steps, the landing, twelve more, the street door, the night swallowing them. Neither of us moved for a full minute. Then Sable crossed to the hearth, worked the loose brick, and drew up the cord hand over hand, and the tin box rose out of the dark trailing soot like something hauled from a well.

She set it on the table. We looked at it. Inside lay every failure of the past three weeks — mis-struck seals, smeared transfers, the whole graveyard of my father's tools learning new purposes — and it looked very small suddenly, and very heavy, the way evidence does.

"It has to go deeper," Sable said. "Or gone."

"Gone." I took it from her before she could argue. Some inheritances you don't share. "The harbor steps. Third piling from the end — there's a gap in the stones where the fishermen sink their bad luck. My father told me about it when I was nine. He never told me why he knew."

She almost smiled. "Of course he did."

We repacked the atlas in silence, faster now, the waxed linen whispering — no, not whispering; I wouldn't allow it — silent, silent as it must be. At the bottom of the case, beneath the plates, Sable tucked a folded square of paper I pretended not to see, addressed in her small upright hand. I knew the name on it. I had watched her write it a dozen ways in her sleep, nearly, murmuring it over the sealing wax.

Outside, the sky over the harbor had gone the color of wet slate. Ninety minutes until the eight o'clock bell. Six hours until the tide.

Sable shrugged into her coat and lifted the case, and paused at the door with the handle in her hand.

"If anyone stops us," she said quietly, "you're delivering your father's effects to his solicitor. Cry if you can. I can't."

"I can," I said. And when the knock of the night came again — only the wind, only the wind — I picked up my end of the burden, and we carried the truth down the stairs together into the grey beginning of the last morning.

But at the corner of Rope Walk, where the lamps thinned out toward the water, a figure detached itself from a doorway ahead of us and stood waiting in our path — broad-shouldered, patient, unmistakably a man who had been looking for this workshop all night, and had found the two women carrying it away instead.
