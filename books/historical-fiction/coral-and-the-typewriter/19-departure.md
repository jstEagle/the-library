## 19. Departure

The fog came off the harbor before dawn, and by the time we reached the quay it had burned away into a pale, washed light that showed everything and hid nothing. Greyhaven's docks had never looked so clean to me. The cranes stood like gallows. The gulls wheeled and screamed their indifference over stacks of crates bound for ports whose names I had inked a hundred times without ever thinking of them as places where a person could simply *arrive*, unknown and unmarked, and begin again.

Maren walked between Tom and me with the atlas strapped to her back in a sailcloth case, and I could feel it there — not its weight, exactly, but its *presence*, the way one feels a lit lamp carried through a dark street. Forty years of Aldous Fenwick's patient, dangerous cartography, every coastal chart threaded with the routes he'd drawn for people who had no other way out. It was leaving Greyhaven this morning, and I would not be going with it.

Tom hadn't let go of my hand since the corner of Wharf Road.

He hadn't spoken either, which frightened me more than speech would have. My brother was a man who filled silence with argument the way other men filled it with whistling. This quiet — his thumb pressed hard against the back of my hand, once, twice, like a man checking that a coin is still in his pocket — was the quiet of someone rehearsing a goodbye he hadn't admitted he was going to say.

"You could still board," I said. "Both of you. There are holds. There are—"

"Sable." Just my name. He shook his head slowly, watching the ship. "Don't make me say it twice."

The *Corvane* sat low in the water at Berth Nine, her hull streaked with rust below the paint line, her decks already crowded with the shapes of passengers who had come in the night to avoid exactly the kind of morning inspection we were about to walk into. Steam curled from her funnel in thin gray ropes. She was scheduled to make way at eight o'clock, and the harbor bell had just struck half past seven.

And at the foot of the gangway, flanked by four men in the gray coats of the Bureau, stood Inspector Cray with the manifest open on a clipboard, reading names.

* *

I had known, in the way you know all the terrible arithmetic of a plan and hope anyway, that the manifests would be inspected. Doss had warned us. *Cray's men will walk the deck with a list,* he'd said, in his office that smelled of tar and old bribes, *and your papers will hold unless he looks too long.* But knowing a thing and standing thirty feet from it are different animals entirely. Knowing is a map. Standing is the territory, and the territory has teeth.

We joined the line of departing passengers, and I made myself into what the forged documents said I was: Maren's cousin, a widow from upcountry, tired, unremarkable, traveling under a name I had built stroke by stroke at Aldous's lamplight — *Mrs. Cora Bellhouse*. I had practiced the signature until my wrist ached. I had not practiced being her.

Tom went first, carrying both trunks, dressed in Maren's late father's good coat, which hung a little wrong across his dockworker's shoulders. His paper said *Tomas Wren, fitter, bound for Calloway Point under contract.* The seal on it was Aldous's, transferred wet and perfect, the work of three nights and eleven ruined sheets. It was the finest forgery I have ever produced or ever hope to.

It did not matter how fine it was if Cray read the manifest against the passenger list and found *Whitlock* flagged in red.

The line moved. A woman ahead of us argued about excess baggage and was waved through. A young man with a cough surrendered his trunk to be opened and never got it back. Somewhere behind me a child cried, thin and constant, and the sound wound itself around my ribs and pulled.

Then we were three deep from the table, and I could see Cray's face properly for the first time. I had imagined him coarser. He was not. He was a neat, dry man with the eyes of an accountant, and he read each name off the manifest the way a man reads accounts he already knows are cooked — patiently, waiting for the error to surface. When he reached a certain page he stopped turning. His finger came down on a line and stayed there.

"Name," he said, without looking up. The question was aimed past the woman directly in front of him, straight at Tom.

"Wren. Tomas Wren."

"Step aside a moment, Mr. Wren."

Tom set down the trunks. He stepped aside the way men step aside when they understand that stepping anywhere else will end badly, and I watched my brother — who had faced foremen and blacklegs and once, our father told it, a whole strike line of mounted police — become small and careful in front of a clipboard.

Cray's finger moved down the column. Down. Stopped.

My heart performed a single enormous beat and then went very quiet, the way the sea goes quiet before a change of wind.

"Interesting," Cray said.

And then Elias Vane stepped out from behind the inspector's shoulder, holding a second ledger, and everything I had arranged in my chest for this morning — the calm, the widow's blankness, the story ready in my mouth like a coin under the tongue — all of it broke apart at once.

* *

Elias looked terrible.

That was my first thought, absurdly, before fear, before calculation: he had not slept, and his coat was buttoned wrong, one hole askew, and there was ink on the heel of his right hand the way there was always ink on Aldous's, and I loved him with a ferocity that had no business standing on a quay at ten to eight in the morning wearing another woman's wedding ring.

He crossed to the table and spoke quietly to Cray, and I heard only fragments — but they were fragments with weight.

"—verified against the corrected census, Inspector. The Whitlock entry was a transcription error on my part. Filed and amended." He laid his ledger open beside Cray's manifest. "Wren, Tomas. Fitter. Contract labor, Calloway Point. The Bureau's own cross-reference confirms it. I processed it myself."

"You amended a census record," Cray said, "eleven days ago."

"I correct records continuously. That is the work."

"The Fenwick workshop," Cray said, and his voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow worse than shouting, "produced documents bearing a seal that appears on four separate forged papers recovered in the last month. The inquiry has established that. The inquiry is also established," he added, with the smallest pause, "to be interested in everyone who passed through that workshop. Including census personnel."

The gulls screamed. The child behind me cried. Tom stood very still by the trunks, and Maren's hand found my arm and gripped it, and neither of us breathed.

"Yes," Elias said. "I passed through the workshop. Six times, on household enumeration. I interviewed Sable Whitlock three times. She answered every question put to her. If the Bureau wishes to charge her with something, Inspector, it should issue a warrant rather than detain her at a gangway on a manifest error that does not exist."

For a moment — I will remember this moment as long as I remember anything — Cray and Elias simply looked at each other, and the whole machinery of the state hung between them on a hinge made of one man's career.

"You understand," Cray said at last, softly, "what you are vouching for."

"I understand precisely what I'm vouching for," Elias said. "I'm vouching for the record. The record is accurate."

It was the most beautiful lie I have ever heard spoken, because every word of it was true except the meaning.

Cray closed the manifest. He drew a pencil line through one entry, wrote two short words beside it — *cleared, Vane* — and moved his finger down the list. "Next," he said.

* *

Afterward I could never reconstruct those last minutes in their proper order. Memory sorted them by weight instead of time.

There was Maren at the head of the gangway, turning once, her face very pale under the washed-out sky, the atlas case riding high on her back. She looked at me and did not wave and did not smile; she gave me a single nod, the nod of one cartographer handing a chart to another across open water, and then she was climbing, and the deck took her, and she was gone into the crowd of coats and trunks and steam.

There was Tom, who walked back from the table to where I stood and picked up the second trunk — the one meant for Calloway Point — and set it down beside mine with a finality that needed no words.

"They'll need papers," he said. "Every dockworker on this coast. Hundreds of men with birthplaces some clerk doesn't like. Somebody has to stay and build the ledger from this side." He almost smiled. "You taught me that, you know. Without meaning to. You stayed with the old man and learned to write things down. I stayed angry and never learned to write anything. Maybe now we've both got a trade."

"Don't," I said. "Don't give me the speech, Tom. Get on the ship."

"No," he said, gently, and took my hand again, the same grip as on Wharf Road, thumb pressed hard once against my knuckles. "This is the speech. This is all of it."

I hit him. Not hard — my fist landed on his shoulder like a thing falling off a shelf, and he absorbed it the way he'd absorbed every blow life had offered him, standing square, unoffended, immovable. Then I pressed my forehead against his coat, against Aldous Fenwick's coat that smelled faintly of cedar and sealing wax, and I held onto my brother on the quay of the city that wanted him gone, and I let him choose.

The whistle sounded — long, low, enormous, the voice of the ship herself — and the crowd on the gangway thickened and surged, and the deckhands began pulling in the lines, and somewhere behind us Cray's men were already folding their clipboards and looking around for the next list, the next name, the next error to surface.

And there was Elias.

He stood apart from the dispersing inspectors with his ledger under his arm and his coat still buttoned wrong, watching me with an expression I had seen on his face only once before — the day he'd stood in Aldous's workshop among the survey maps and said, *Some columns should stay blank.*

"You knew I'd be here," I said.

"I hoped you wouldn't be," he said. "I hoped you'd be on the ship."

"There was one berth."

"There was always one berth," he said. "With you there was always only ever going to be one berth. That's the shape of you, Sable. You draw the escape route and hand it to someone else." He looked out at the water, where the *Corvane* was beginning, imperceptibly, to move — a widening of dark water between hull and pier, a shudder of steam. "I led the raid on the workshop Tuesday night. You know that by now, or you'll learn it. I searched the chimney myself. There were no plates in the chimney. There were no plates anywhere. That is what my report says, and I will stand in front of an inquiry and say it again, and it will end me, and I want you to know that I have thought about it every hour since Tuesday and I would do it again tonight."

"Elias—"

"Don't thank me." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and he mastered it. "Thanking makes it a favor. It wasn't a favor. It was—" He stopped. Started again. "Read your own atlas, Sable. Some routes only go one direction."

The whistle blew a second time, longer, and the gap of black water widened to a boat's length, then two, and on the stern rail of the *Corvane* a small figure raised one arm — Maren, holding aloft something that caught the pale light and threw it back red, a scrap of ribbon or cloth tied to the rail like a surveyor's marker on a claimed point — and Tom's grip on my hand tightened until it hurt, and the three of us stood on the stones of Berth Nine while the ship carried the atlas, and the routes, and forty years of a dead man's courage out past the breakwater and into the open sea.

Love and loss sailed together, and we stood on the shore and let them.

"She'll copy it," I said, when the hull had shrunk to a dark line. "Page by page, port by port. It'll never stop moving. That's what he built it for — not to be kept. To be *passed*."

"And you?" Elias asked. "What do you pass on?"

Behind us, up Wharf Road, the city was waking — carts and gulls and the bell of the harbor master's office, and somewhere in it Inspector Cray already writing his report, and somewhere in that report my name, and somewhere beyond that name an inquiry with Elias's career hanging from its hook.

I turned toward the road. I picked up the trunk with the false bottom, the one holding every chart I had memorized and would spend the rest of my life copying from memory.

"Now," I said, "we find out what survives."
