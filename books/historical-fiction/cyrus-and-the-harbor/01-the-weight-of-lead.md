## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type spoke to her before the press did.

That was what her father had always said, back when she was small enough to see over the lip of the composing stick only by standing on a crate. *The lead remembers, Mira. Every letter has been held a hundred times before you held it. Your job is to make it forget.* She had thought it nonsense at eight. At nineteen, with her fingers blackened to the second knuckle and the winter light coming gray through the shop's high windows, she understood it as the truest thing anyone in Vellamare had ever told her.

Mirabel Vasquez pulled the ten-point Caslon from its case the way other girls her age pulled bread from an oven—without looking, without thinking, by pure animal knowledge of where things lived. The upper-left quadrant: lowercase e, most populous letter in the Spanish tongue, worn nearly smooth from a decade of her father's sermons and shipping notices and the municipal broadsheets that kept the shop solvent. Lowercase a beside it, then t, then the thin ranks of s and i and n. Her hands moved through the case's geography like a swimmer through familiar water while her mind stayed free to count the soldiers outside.

There were six this morning. Yesterday there had been four.

They walked the Calle de la Prensa in pairs, which was a military habit, not a police one—the junta's men hadn't learned yet that pairs made them targets, that a lone soldier could be reasoned with or robbed but two could only be watched. Their boots rang differently on the cobblestones than the harbormen's boots, hollow and certain, the sound of people who believed the ground would hold. She marked their rotation without lifting her eyes from the case. Twelve minutes past the corner fountain, twelve minutes back. In between, the street belonged to the shopkeepers and the gulls and one old woman who sold chestnuts from a brazier and spat, elaborately, in their passing wake.

*Twelve minutes,* Mirabel thought, and set an e in its slot, and thought: *that's how long it takes to print a lie if your press is warmed and your forms are locked.*

She didn't know yet whose lie. She only knew the city had begun to smell of them—the way a house smells of gas before anyone has struck a match, when everyone walks carefully and no one says why.

* * *

The shop occupied the ground floor of the family house on the Calle de la Prensa, a name so old it had become ironic: the street named for an industry mostly dead, three printers left where there had once been eleven. Her father's establishment—Vasquez & Son, though the sign still said Vasquez because Tomás had refused, for seven years running, to stand for the photograph of himself beneath it—was the largest of the three and the shabbiest. The press was a flatbed Columbian bought used from a Lisbon house that had gone under; the ink was mixed in-house from lampblack and linseed because the good stuff came from Barcelona and Barcelona prices had doubled since the summer. The type cases lined the back wall in their tall wooden racks, forty drawers of pigeonholes, each compartment a small country with its own population and its own politics.

Her father ruled the countries. He was seventy-one and moved through the shop like weather, slow and total, touching everything—running a thumb along the furniture of a locked form, testing the ink drum with a knuckle, tilting his head to read proof upside down faster than she could read it right side up. Don Emilio Vasquez had printed for the port for fifty years. He had printed cargo manifests and marriage announcements, the harbor pilot's tide tables, four archbishop's letters, and exactly one duel challenge, in beautiful italics, which he never spoke of. His hands were stained so deep with forty years of ink that the creases of his palms looked like etchings. When he stood behind her at the case and watched her work, she felt those hands hovering near her shoulders like a blessing she hadn't earned.

This morning he was upstairs, which meant she ran the shop alone until the church bells struck nine.

The job on the stone was ordinary: five hundred copies of a customs schedule for the Port Authority, gray prose in gray ink, the kind of work that paid for lampblack and kept the junta's censors convinced that Vasquez & Son was a boring shop run by a boring old man and his quiet daughter. Boring was armor. Boring was the whole strategy. Her father had drilled it into them both since the summer's troubles began in the capital: *We print what we are paid to print. We ask nothing, we keep nothing, we remember nothing.* Tomás used to laugh when he said it back to him. Tomás wasn't laughing anymore either.

She composed the customs schedule in her head three lines ahead of her hands, which was the mark of a compositor and not merely a girl who set type. Anyone could copy letters. A compositor read the galley as a river reads its bed—knowing before the water arrives where it will turn. She justified a line, tightened the spaces between words with slips of paper-thin brass, and felt the small clean satisfaction of a paragraph locking up flush at both margins. Somewhere in the walls of the house, pipes knocked twice and went silent. The building settled against the cold like an old man into a chair.

Outside, the soldiers passed again. Nine minutes early this morning. She noted it the way she noted everything now, in the ledger she kept behind her eyes: *rotation shortened. They are nervous, or they have been told something.*

Both possibilities smelled like gas.

* * *

Tomás came down at half past eight, dressed for the propaganda office in the good coat that had been their grandfather's, and paused on the bottom stair to look at her working.

He did that more lately—paused to look. Not the way fathers look at daughters working, which is a kind of pride so ordinary it doesn't need naming. Tomás looked at her the way a man looks at a map of a place he intends to own. She felt it between her shoulder blades and kept setting type.

"You've ink on your jaw," he said.

"You have ambition on yours."

He smiled because he was too clever not to enjoy being seen, even by a sister. Twenty-six years old, three years her senior, with their mother's dark quick features and none of their mother's kindness. Since September he had been climbing the junta's new bureaucracy like a man scaling a wall in gloves—carefully, smilingly, leaving no fingerprints. First errands for the district registrar. Then translation work for the civil liaison. Now, twice a week, he disappeared into the former customs house where the Propagatura had established itself, and came home smelling of pipe smoke and other men's cologne, speaking a language of *committees* and *coordination* and *the new stability* that Mirabel understood to mean: *we decide what may be said, and we are very reasonable about it.*

"The Port Authority schedule?" he asked, nodding at the stone. "Father still taking government work?"

"Father takes paying work."

"Take care, Mira." He adjusted his collar in the cracked mirror by the door—a vanity he performed every morning as if rehearsing for some audience not yet arrived. "The men I meet ask questions. About shops, about presses, about who prints what. I answer honestly, because honesty is safer now. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you'd sell us all for a desk with a window."

"What I'm telling you," he said, mild as milk, "is that our name is on a list somewhere. Every name is on a list somewhere. And lists get shorter." He kissed his fingers and touched the lintel of the door—an old superstition from their mother, kept alive out of habit rather than faith—and stepped out into the street.

Through the window she watched him cross the Calle de la Prensa directly toward the pair of soldiers at the corner, and she watched the soldiers watch him, and she watched Tomás give them a small nod, comrade to comrade, employee to employer, a nod that said *I am known here, I am protected, I am one of yours.*

One of them nodded back.

Mirabel returned to her case. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She would have expected them to shake after a thing like that—her brother wearing the junta's acknowledgment like a boutonniere—but steadiness was what the work had built into her, year upon year, muscle fiber and nerve. You did not shake at the case. You shook later, alone, or better yet you converted the shaking into rhythm and let the rhythm carry you through five hundred impressions of a customs schedule that nobody would ever read.

Still. *Lists get shorter.* She set the line and heard it again, in his pleasant voice, in his grandfather's coat.

* * *

At nine the bells of San Sebastián came down through the gray air, and her father descended with them.

Don Emilio moved slowly these days—not the deliberate slowness of authority but the careful slowness of a heart that had begun keeping its own books. He carried his coffee in a cup gone brown with decades of use, and he stood for a long moment inside the door, listening to the street the way sailors listen to wind.

"Early today," he said.

"Nine minutes. And yesterday they doubled at the fountain."

"Mm." He drank. Behind the spectacles his eyes were doing arithmetic she couldn't see. "How is the schedule?"

"Locked except for the last column. The figures from the Port Authority don't balance—they're off by eleven tons across the grain shipments. Someone at the Authority is stealing or someone at the Authority can't add."

"Both, likely." But he wasn't smiling. He crossed to the front window and stood beside it, angled so he could see the corner without seeming to, and Mirabel watched her father perform the same calculation she had been performing all morning, and saw something in his face she had almost never seen there.

Fear, worn badly. Fear like an ill-fitting coat on a proud man.

"Papá. What is it?"

"Nothing. The winter. My chest complains of the winter." He said it the way he said many things, which is to say convincingly enough that she might have believed him if she hadn't grown up watching him lie to censors for a living. A man who survived fifty years in this trade learned to keep a second face folded away like a handkerchief. It was out now. She could see the fold-lines.

"The eleven tons," he said suddenly. "Correct them. Make the figures balance to what the Authority *wishes*, not what is true. This month we do not embarrass anyone."

"They asked us to print the true figures—"

"They asked us to print *figures*." He turned from the window. "Mira. Come here. Look at me."

She came. Up close her father smelled of coffee and lampblack and something faintly medicinal that he pretended he wasn't taking.

"This month," he said quietly, "whatever comes through that door, whatever is asked, however strange the request or the visitor—you bring it to me first. Not to Tomás. To me. Swear it."

"Papá, what's coming through that door?"

"Swear it, Mirabel."

She looked into her father's face—that etched, ink-darkened, magnificent old face—and saw that beneath the fear there was something else, something older and harder, and it took her a moment to recognize it because it had no business living in the same skull as fear. It was resolve. Her father was afraid of something coming, and he had already decided what he would do when it came, and the two feelings were sitting in him together like strangers forced to share a bench.

"I swear," she said.

"Good girl." He patted her cheek with a hand that trembled, just slightly, just once, and then went to his stool by the press and began checking the rollers as if it were any ordinary Tuesday in the last December of the century.

* * *

The foreboding came for her in the afternoon, in the shape of nothing at all.

That was the thing about Vellamare that winter—nothing happened, magnificently. No arrests on the Calle de la Prensa, no shuttered windows beyond the usual few, no proclamation nailed to the customs house that she hadn't already heard secondhand. The harbor worked. The fish carts ran. The chestnut woman sold her chestnuts and spat at intervals as regular as bells. And underneath it all, the gas smell thickened: the rumor that General Aldana's ships had been refused entry to the outer roads; the rumor that Aldana himself had slipped over the border weeks ago, destination unknown; the rumor—passed from laundress to fishwife to dockhand to printer's daughter, worn smooth as beach glass by the journey—that names were being collected. Lists being made. Of sympathizers, of subscribers, of anyone who had ever shaken a certain general's hand or, more dangerously, ever done business with him.

Five hundred copies of the customs schedule came off the press by four o'clock, gray and balanced and lying about eleven tons. Her father locked up the form, distributed the type back into the cases—each letter going home to its little country—and the shop settled into the amber hour before closing, when the light came low through the windows and turned thirty years of ink residue on the floorboards into something almost beautiful.

Mirabel washed her hands at the basin. The water went gray, then darker, then the color of old iron. Some of the black never came out; it lived in the whorls of her fingerprints now, hers as permanently as anything in this life. She looked at her hands and felt the pride arrive, quiet and unannounced, the way it always did at day's end. *I am good at this.* Not the false modesty of a girl told to be humble, but the earned certainty of craft: she could set a page of type that a stranger would call beautiful without knowing why. She could keep a press alive with baling wire and patience. In a city full of people who could fire rifles, she was one of perhaps a dozen souls who could make a thousand strangers think the same thought by suppertime, and there was power in that, real power, sleeping power, and she had been taught to keep it asleep.

Her father doused the lamp over the stone. Half the shop fell into shadow.

"Mira," he said, from the darkness where his voice sounded older. "When I was a young man, in 'seventy-four, I printed a pamphlet for a man the government wanted silenced. Small thing. Eight pages. I hid the type for two years afterward—buried it in the yard like a corpse." He was silent a moment. "I swore never again."

"And now?"

The pause lasted long enough for the clock in the hallway to tick eleven times.

"Now I am an old man," he said, "and I find I remember the man more clearly than the fear."

She wanted to ask him everything—which man, which pamphlet, what memory was moving behind his spectacles like weather over the outer harbor. But the bell above the shop door rang, sudden as a shot, and both of them turned.

No one stood in the doorway. The street beyond lay empty, lamplit, the soldiers' boots audible three corners away and moving off toward the harbor. Only the cold air came in, stirring the loose papers on the front table.

And on the mat, square and white and sealed with red wax, lay an envelope with no name written on it at all.
