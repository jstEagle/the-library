## 2. A Letter Under the Door

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the first strange thing about it.

Nothing came to the Vasquez house on Tuesdays. Bills arrived on the first of the month. The *Vellamare Courier* was delivered before dawn every day except Sunday, when my father insisted we still print it and simply let it sit in bundles until Monday like bread going stale. Suppliers came on Thursdays. The census man had come once, four years ago, in August, sweating through his uniform shirt while Father offered him water he didn't drink.

But nothing came on Tuesdays. So when I opened the door at half past seven to shake the sand out of the mat—my one domestic duty, performed with the same precision I gave to distributing type, because a chore done carelessly is a habit that spreads—and found the envelope lying there, face down, wax seal up, I stood very still for a moment in the doorway and let the street look at me looking at it.

The street was empty. That was the second strange thing.

Vellamare at half past seven was never empty. The fish women should have been trundling their carts down toward the harbor, shouting prices at each other across the width of the Calle Ancha. The coffee seller set up his brazier by the fountain. The lamplighter's boy ran his rounds, extinguishing wicks with a brass cone on a pole, whistling something off-key no matter how many times he was told to stop. All of it should have been happening. None of it was.

I looked left. I looked right. The lamps were already out. At the far corner of the street, where it bent down toward the water, a soldier stood with his back to me, the morning sun catching the oiled shine of his rifle stock, and even from thirty paces I could see he wasn't watching the street. He was watching our door. He just wasn't watching it well enough to know he'd turned away at the wrong moment.

I picked up the letter. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, or perhaps I only imagined that, the way you imagine a stone is warm when a bird has sat on it. The wax seal was unbroken, deep red, stamped with a device I didn't recognize—a tower, or maybe a fist seen from above, fingers closed. No address on the front except *Vasquez*, written in a clerk's hand, the kind of handwriting that belongs to a man who writes all day and has stopped caring what his letters look like. No stamp. Hand-delivered, then. By someone who did not knock.

I stood in the doorway holding it and heard my own heart do something ugly and fast against my ribs.

"Mirabel." My father's voice from the back of the shop, muffled by the press. "Is that the courier? Bring me the shipping notices."

"It's not the courier," I said.

* * *

He was standing at the composing stone with his sleeves rolled up, which is how I always think of him: forearms dusted gray with ink, reading glasses pushed up into hair that had gone white early and stayed white stubbornly, a galley of type in front of him that he was checking with a proofing stick the way other men check their children for fevers. Emilio Vasquez did not read a page. He interrogated it. He could find a reversed letter in a full form by touch alone, running two fingers along the face of the type like a blind man reading grief.

My mother had died eleven years ago, and since then it had been the three of us: Father, Tomás, me. Tomás was already gone for the morning—he kept late hours now and earlier ones, coming and going like a man with appointments he couldn't name, wearing shirts I didn't remember buying him. Which left Father and me and the letter.

I put it on the composing stone beside the galley.

He looked at it the way he looked at a misprinted sheet—not with surprise. With diagnosis.

"Where?"

"On the mat. No stamp."

He wiped his hands on his apron, slowly, taking more time than the wiping required, and I understood that he was giving himself those extra seconds the way a compositor gives himself seconds before pulling a difficult lever—bracing the body for what the hands know is coming. Then he picked up the letter, turned it over twice, held it to the light from the window as if the seal might go transparent under sufficient inspection, and broke it with his thumbnail in one clean motion.

He read it once quickly. Then again, slowly, his lips moving over certain phrases the way they moved when he was setting copy in a language he didn't quite trust.

Then he folded it, put it in his breast pocket, and went back to the galley.

"That's it?" I said.

"The third line of this paragraph is set an em too tight," he said. "See how the spacing starves? Pull it and reset with thinner spaces."

"Father. What does it say?"

"It says," he said, without looking up, "that we are to continue doing honest work."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about my father's silences: they were not absences. They were structures. A man who works in lead learns that what holds a page together is not the letters but the space around them—the quads, the leads, the furniture, all the named emptinesses of the trade. My father built his conversations the same way. What he didn't say was set solid, locked up tight, ready to hold weight.

So I pulled the paragraph. I reset it with thinner spaces. And all day, while the shop filled with its ordinary noises—the creak of the treadle, the sigh of the rollers, the smell of oil and solvent and the particular cold-metal sweetness of fresh lead—I watched him, and he watched me not watching him, and neither of us said anything else about the letter until the light failed.

Which is not to say the day was silent. Vellamare spoke for both of us.

The city had been getting louder for weeks, but it was a specific loudness now, a new grammar of noise. Gunshots used to mean fiestas—firecrackers at the saint's days, muskets fired into the air at weddings until the harbor gulls fled out over the water in a black ragged sheet. Now gunfire meant something you counted. Three shots at dawn near the customs house, and nobody asked who. A volley at dusk two streets over, and the whole block turned off its lamps at once, a darkness that traveled downhill toward the plaza like water finding its level.

There were proclamations nailed to every post. I read them professionally—it was my trade to read printed matter professionally—and the prose had changed. The old government announcements had been pompous, florid, stuffed with titles and flourishes. These new ones were short. *By order of the Provisional Junta.* Lists of names. Lists of what would happen to people whose names appeared on lists. General Aldana's name had appeared on one ten days before, and after his name came the word *traitor*, and after the word *traitor* came a phrase I had set myself a hundred times in other contexts, for obituaries and legal notices: *by the sentence of a military tribunal*. Except this time it was not a sentence being reported. It was a sentence being pre-announced, like weather.

Aldana had been a hero of the coastal campaigns. There had been parades. I remembered my father standing at the shop window watching one of them go by, and Aldana—not yet general then, or newly one—riding past close enough to touch, and the crowd roaring, and my father not roaring, only nodding, the way he nodded at a well-printed page.

Ten days later his name was worth blood.

And now a letter on a Tuesday, sealed in red wax, addressed in a clerk's hand, naming us—how did I know it named us? Because it was addressed *Vasquez*, singular, as if the family were a single legal object, a thing that could be indicted whole. Because it had been hand-delivered by someone who did not knock. Because a soldier was watching our door badly.

Because my father had read it twice and then gone back to work, and my father had not gone back to work after Mother died for six weeks.

* * *

At closing, I banked the press, swept the stone, sorted the day's broken type into the hellbox while Father locked the cash drawer. Tomás did not come home for dinner, which no longer surprised anyone. Father and I ate beans and salt cod at the table behind the shop, under the lamp, and the silence between us was so heavy-set, so tightly locked up, that I could hear the flame of the lamp working at its wick.

"You knew," I said.

He cut his cod into small precise pieces and ate them.

"When the manifesto thing started—whatever it is—you knew. When the junta took the plaza, you knew. You've known for weeks, and you've been walking around this shop with it in your pocket the whole time, and you let me set the Wednesday edition like nothing—"

"Mirabel."

"One word. Tell me one word of what it says."

He put down his fork. He folded his hands. He looked at me across the lamp with an expression I want to describe correctly because I have thought about it every day since, and the correct description is this: he looked at me the way he looked at a customer who wanted something printed that he had decided not to print. Not angry. Not afraid. Finished deliberating.

"There are things," he said, "that get set in type once, and after that they exist whether or not they're ever printed. A promise is like that. It doesn't need ink."

"A promise." I heard my own voice climb and hated it. "Whose promise? To whom? Is it Aldana? Everyone in the city is saying his name like it's a disease, and there's a letter on our table with a seal I don't recognize, and you're talking about promises—"

"Eat your dinner."

"I'm not hungry."

"You'll need to eat," he said, "in the weeks coming. Eat your dinner."

In the weeks coming. Not *this week*, not *these hard times*. In the weeks coming, as though he had already measured out some stretch of future on his composing stone and found it long.

We finished eating without another word. He washed the plates—I washed the plates, he always washed the plates, he said my hands were the family's capital and capital doesn't do dishes—and I dried, and outside a patrol went down the Calle Ancha with boots that echoed wrong, too many boots for the number of men, or men marching out of step on purpose so you couldn't count them.

When I went to bank the stove, I saw him at the table with the letter open again under the lamp. He was not reading it this time. He was looking at the signature, or where a signature should have been, and rubbing his thumb over the paper as if he could feel the words in relief, like type pressed into a soft sheet.

Later, in my room above the shop, I lay awake and listened to the building. Every old building talks at night—joists settling, pipes knocking, wind finding the gaps around window frames—and I knew this building's voice better than my own. Tonight there was one sound too many. A small sound. Paper.

I got up and put my eye to the floor vent, the old heating grate that carried kitchen smells up to the rooms, and through it I saw lamplight and heard my father moving at the table, and after a long while I heard him speak. Not to me. Not to anyone. Two sentences, low, in the cadence of a man rehearsing:

"Not yet. Not while I draw breath to pull a lever."

Then the lamp went out.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that wouldn't fall. I came down to open the shutters and stopped at the front door first, because I am my father's daughter, and I checked the mat.

Empty. Just sand and a bottle cap and yesterday's ordinary world.

But across the street, leaning against the fountain as though waiting for a girl who was slow to rise, stood a young man in a laborer's cap, smoking a cigarette down to its last centimeter, and when I stepped out he dropped it, ground it under his heel without looking at it, and walked away down toward the harbor without hurrying, without looking back.

Without leaving anything behind at all. Except the certainty, settling into me colder than the morning, that whoever had delivered Tuesday's letter had not been delivering a warning to my father.

They had been delivering a countdown.
