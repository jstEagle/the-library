## 3. What Father Promised

The lamp on the composing stone had a stutter in it. Emilio Vasquez had been meaning to fix the wick for three weeks, and for three weeks he had instead adjusted his chair, tilted his head, and learned to read type in that unsteady amber light. A man could get used to almost anything if he refused to call it a problem.

It was past midnight. The shop was closed, shuttered, and should have been silent, but a press never is. Lead settles. Wood breathes. The great Heidelberg in the corner ticked as its iron cooled, like a dog dreaming at the foot of the bed. Emilio sat among his cases with a proof sheet he had no reason to be reading and every reason not to burn.

*To the people of Vellamare.*

Eleven hundred words in Aldana's hand, set and locked in the chase not two feet from where he sat, waiting for a morning that would never be permitted to come.

He heard Mirabel's tread on the stairs an hour ago—light, deliberate, pausing at the landing the way she did now, listening, before she went up to bed. His daughter had learned to listen at landings. That was what this year had done to children. Tomás came home smelling of tobacco smoke and other men's ambition, and said little, and smiled too readily, and Emilio told himself there was still time to correct whatever was going wrong inside that boy. But Mirabel—Mirabel stood at windows and counted soldiers. She cut type cleaner than any apprentice he'd trained in forty years, including the two who were now dead or fled, and she asked no questions, which meant she had already understood the answers without needing them spoken.

She knew something was locked in the chase. Of course she did. She dusted that press more faithfully than she prayed.

Emilio turned the proof sheet over, as if the blank back could argue with the front.

* * *

It had rained the day Aldana came, which everyone remembered afterward because rain makes memory feel like prophecy. But that was June. June of '97. The city had been merely corrupt then, not dangerous, and a general could walk into a print shop off the Calle del Plomo with wet shoulders and no escort, and the only consequence was mud on the floorboards.

"Vasquez." Aldana had said it like a man greeting a ship he intended to buy. He was broader then, before the mountains whittled him down, and he filled the doorway the way wealthy men fill doorways—not with size but with certainty. "They tell me you're the best compositor south of the capital."

"They tell you wrong," Emilio said. "I'm the second best. The best works north of it."

Aldana laughed and dripped on the floor, and Emilio, against all his instincts about men who arrived unannounced, liked him.

The general moved through the shop the way few civilians ever did—with reverence. He touched the cabinets. He looked into the type cases and read them the way a sailor reads rigging, knowing what each rope was for. When he reached the case of fourteen-point Caslon, old and pitted and beautiful, he stopped.

"This is the one."

"That's my best face," Emilio said. "It's for wedding invitations and obituaries. Things that matter."

"Good." Aldana turned. And here was the thing Emilio would spend two years turning over in the dark: the general did not look like a conspirator. He looked like a tired man asking for help. "There will come a time—they will see to it themselves—when I am silenced. Struck from the record. My name made into a thing people spit. When that day comes, I will have no newspaper, no pulpit, no floor of any parliament. I will have nothing but friends with steady hands."

"I'm a printer," Emilio said. "Not a friend."

"You're both or neither," Aldana said. "Everything else is invoicing."

Outside, the rain kept on. Somewhere down the street a cart driver cursed his mule in the elaborate, affectionate way of men who would take a bullet for the animal. An ordinary street. An ordinary afternoon. Emilio thought about that later too—the terrible ordinariness of the moment a promise gets made. Nobody strikes a bell. There's no cold wind. You are simply standing in your own shop with your sleeves rolled, and a man asks you for something, and your mouth opens.

"What exactly are you asking?" Emilio said.

"A pamphlet. One page, maybe two. My words, when the time comes, printed whole and uncensored and distributed wide enough that they can't be unpicked from the city's memory. That's all. No guns. no smuggling of anything but paper." Aldana held up a hand. "And hear me clearly, printer: I ask nothing today. Not today, not next year. I ask only that when the day comes—if it comes—you do not let my name become only what they print of it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I leave, and we drink nothing, and you go on printing wedding invitations." Aldana's eyes went to the Caslon case again. "But I watched your hands while I talked. They wanted the work. A man's hands know before he does."

That was true, God help him. Emilio had felt it even then, the itch in his fingers, the compositor's oldest sickness: words that need setting, truth that needs a form to hold it, and here was a shape waiting to be filled. Forty years of arranging other people's sentences into order, and some starved part of him had been waiting his whole life for a sentence worth the lead.

"I have children," Emilio said.

"Yes," Aldana said. "I've seen your son at the café on the plaza, arguing politics with the very men who'll arrest him if he keeps it up. And I'm told your daughter sets type better than you do, though you'd deny it under oath." He smiled, and for one moment the general was gone and just a man stood there, mid-fifties, bad knee, rain-soaked, afraid of the same future everyone else was afraid of. "I would not ask if I thought it were small. It isn't small. It may cost everything. I know that better than you do—I'm the one they're already writing speeches about hanging. So don't answer me today. Answer me the day it matters. All I want from you now is your word that you will *consider* it honestly. Can you give me that much?"

And Emilio—who had spent his life careful, who had survived three governments by printing shipping notices and obituaries and the occasional flattering piece about whoever was in power, who had a wife buried in the hill cemetery and two children upstairs eating dinner—heard himself say:

"You'll have more than that. You'll have the press. If they silence you, General, my shop speaks for you until it can't."

The words came out whole, finished, irrevocable, before any committee in his head had voted. Aldana didn't gloat. That was the worst of it. He simply nodded once, the way one officer acknowledges another, and shook Emilio's hand, and his grip was dry and strong and human, and then he was gone into the rain, and Emilio stood looking at the muddy prints on his floor and understood that he had just signed something no solvent could dissolve.

* * *

Two years. He had two years to repent of it, and he had used them badly.

Because the honest consideration Aldana had granted him never happened—or rather, it happened every night at the edge of sleep, in the half-second before dreams, and every morning the verdict was the same: *not yet.* The regime tightened and Emilio printed funeral announcements. Censors took chairs in the cafés and Emilio printed the customs manifests. Men he'd known thirty years were arrested outside the fish market and Emilio printed a lovely broadside for the Regatta Festival, sea-green ink, two colors, his finest work that season.

He told himself: a press that is seized prints nothing ever again. Survival is not cowardice; survival is keeping the instrument intact. He told himself the arithmetic was simple—one pamphlet against a family. One page of truth against Mirabel's whole unwritten life, against Tomás's fast tongue and faster ambitions. Any decent man of business would run the same sums.

But arithmetic was exactly the problem, and Emilio knew it, and this was the shame he could not say aloud even to the dark: honorable words do not divide cleanly. A promise given in peacetime accrues interest. Every month he delayed, the debt grew. Every festival broadside, every safe little shipping notice, was a payment made to the regime out of funds that belonged, by right, to a man gone into the mountains. And the man in the mountains had never once sent word, never pressed, never reminded—and that, somehow, made it worse. Silence from Aldana was itself a kind of invoice. *You know what you owe. I will not embarrass us both by naming it.*

So Emilio did the only thing his conscience would permit: he prepared, secretly, the way misers prepare. In the spring, working alone after hours, he cast the entire manifesto in the fourteen-point Caslon. Eleven hundred words. He locked them in the chase, wrapped the plate-forms, and hid them where no censor would think to look—in plain sight, inside the great case of broken type, the pied letters, the worn and cracked sorts that every shop accumulates across decades and no printer ever discards because broken type is the raw meat of recasting. He melted a little wax over the compartments. He told no one, not even Mirabel.

Why not Mirabel? Because telling her would make her guilty, and he was trying—God's bitter joke—to keep her innocent. Why not Tomás? He asked himself that nightly now, with the letter from March sitting in his memory like a swallowed hook. Because Tomás had changed sides so gradually, so reasonably, so *invoiced-ly*, that there had never been one visible moment to point to. Because Tomás had started saying *they* when he meant the government, and *we* when he meant the family, and lately Emilio wasn't certain those two categories pointed in opposite directions anymore.

* * *

The sealed letter had come nine days ago. Ordinary paper. Ordinary seal. A clerk's handwriting, courteous to the point of obscenity: *the Office of Public Order notes with concern certain associations of the house of Vasquez... particularly regarding services rendered or promised to the traitor Aldana... the family's cooperation in clarifying these matters would be viewed favorably...*

Viewed favorably. As though honor were a credit account and the junta the bank.

Emilio had read it twice, folded it, and put it in his coat, and burned nothing and said less, and Mirabel had watched him with those eyes and asked nothing, which was worse than any question. Nine days since. Nine days of the soldiers' boots on the cobbles outside, of the shop's shutters closed by four in the afternoon, of Tomás coming home late with his collar turned up and news he claimed not to have.

Now Emilio stood, knees cracking, and crossed to the case of broken type. He lifted the lid. The wax seals were whole. Beneath the pied and shattered sorts, wrapped in oilcloth, the forms lay exactly as he'd left them—eleven hundred words sleeping in lead, patient as seed.

He did not touch them. Touching them felt like waking something.

*A press that speaks when it cannot is a press that dies,* he told himself, the old survival litany. *And a press that dies prints nothing ever again.*

And then the other voice, Aldana's voice, dry and human in the rain two years gone: *Your hands wanted the work, printer.*

His hands wanted it now. That was the horror. Not duty—desire. At fifty-nine years old, with his heart misfiring in damp weather and his daughter counting soldiers at the window above, Emilio Vasquez wanted, more than safety, more than Tomás's forgiveness, more than one more ordinary morning, to stand at the Heidelberg and pull one clean proof of the truth with the good Caslon and see Aldana's voice come off the cylinder black and whole and *unpicking-proof*. Let the century end on it. Let them hang him after.

But wanting was not doing, and doing would fall on Mirabel like a roof beam, and so he stood there in the stuttering lamplight doing the terrible arithmetic one more time, adding columns that would not balance, subtracting his own life first and finding the sum still short.

Down the street, boots. More of them than usual, unhurried, in step. Emilio blew out the lamp and stood in the dark among his cases, listening, an old man holding his breath over eleven hundred words.

The boots passed. They always passed. Until the night they wouldn't.

And somewhere below the window, faint under the sound of the harbor, a lighter voice than any soldier's—Mirabel, he realized, not abed at all—was letting herself out the front door and into the dark toward the water, carrying questions he had been too much a coward to answer.

He should call her back.

He stood in the black shop, among everything he had promised, and did not.
