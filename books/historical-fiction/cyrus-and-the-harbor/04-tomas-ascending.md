## 4. Tomás Ascending

The propaganda office kept its headquarters in the old customs annex on the Calle del Sol, and Tomás Vasquez had walked past it every morning for eleven years without once looking up. Now he stood across the street for a full quarter hour, adjusting his collar in the brass plate of a shuttered tobacconist's window, rehearsing the first sentence until it stopped sounding like his father's voice and started sounding like his own.

*You need men who understand how this city actually reads.*

That was the sentence. That was the whole argument, folded into nine words. Everything else was ornament.

The morning was gray and smelled of gun oil and roasting chestnuts, which was Vellamare in miniature now: menace and ordinary life pressed into the same street. Soldiers at the corner checked papers with the bored efficiency of men who had done it four hundred times. A fishwife argued with one of them about a permit for her cart and won, somehow, through sheer volume. Tomás watched her go and thought: *that* is the city they think they've conquered. They haven't. They've only occupied its mornings.

He crossed the street before he could talk himself out of it.

Inside, the annex smelled of fresh ink and cold coffee—the two smells of his childhood, though here they were arranged differently than in his father's shop. Here the ink came off the press by the thousand sheets, and none of it meant anything except that someone with a rifle wanted it printed. Tomás felt something in his chest that he refused, firmly, to name as shame. Ambition, he told himself. It was ambition. It had always been ambition, since he was twelve years old and first understood that his father would die owning exactly what he owned, no more: a shop, three presses, a name good on maybe four streets.

Tomás did not intend to die owning exactly what he owned.

A clerk with a ledger directed him to the second floor, where a man named Ordóñez received petitioners at a desk too large for the room, so that anyone sitting across from him had to turn sideways like a supplicant at an awkward angle. Tomás recognized the trick immediately and admired it. He had read enough of his father's political pamphlets—against his father's knowledge, late at night—to know that power was mostly furniture.

"Señor Vasquez." Ordóñez did not look up from the paper in front of him. "Of Vasquez & Sons."

"Vasquez and Son," Tomás said. "My father is old. The shop is effectively mine."

That got the look up. Good. First blood.

"And you've come to—" Ordóñez consulted nothing, simply let the sentence hang.

"To offer you something more valuable than my father's arrest." Tomás sat, turned sideways at the required angle, and made himself comfortable there, which was its own small announcement. "You're going to take him anyway. I've read the lists. Printers who ever touched Aldana's correspondence are being called in this week. You'll seal the shop, inventory the type, and discover—about a month from now, after your clerks have catalogued it—that you have seized a print shop and learned nothing about Aldana's network, because my father doesn't know anything about Aldana's network. He knows type. He promised a man a manifesto five years ago in peacetime, and in peacetime a promise like that is just manners. He couldn't tell you who carries messages if you broke both his hands."

Ordóñez set down his paper. "You speak of your father very freely."

"I speak of him accurately," Tomás said. "There's a difference, and it's the difference between what you have now and what you could have."

* * *

Let the record show—and Tomás was a man who liked records, ledgers, receipts; let it always show—he had not gone to the annex intending any of this.

Three nights earlier he had sat at the dinner table while their mother said grace and Mirabel stared at the letter lying beside the bread basket like a dead bird, and he had felt the floor of his life tilt. The letter named them. *The household of Esteban Vasquez, printer,* it said, in the regime's flat bureaucratic hand, *is implicated in seditious correspondence with the traitor Aldana.* It didn't demand anything. That was the elegant part. It simply placed them on a list, and everyone in Vellamare knew what happened to names on lists: they waited there, patient as winter, until some officer needed a demonstration or some quota needed filling.

His father had eaten dinner as if the letter weren't there. Had said, when Mirabel asked what he intended to do, only: "I intend to keep my word." Four words, delivered over the stew, and Tomás had understood in that moment that his father was prepared to bury the whole family under those four words and call the burial honor.

Mirabel had looked at their father with something close to reverence. Tomás had looked at him and seen a man choosing a beautiful death over an ugly survival, which was easy to do when the ugly survival fell mostly on the children.

So: no. Not this family. Not if Tomás could arrange the arithmetic differently.

Here was the arithmetic as he worked it out walking the harbor wall that night, coatless in the wind because he deserved the discomfort, a penance that cost nothing:

Option one: do nothing. Result: soldiers within two weeks, shop sealed, family dispersed or worse, and the junta gaining a martyr printer whose presses would be spoken of in whispers for fifty years. Cost: everything. Return: nothing but a legend, and legends paid no rent.

Option two: run. His father would never agree, and a printer without his type was a priest without a God; he'd sooner stay and be shot beside his fonts. Cost: everything, again, plus the indignity of failure.

Option three: make the family useful instead of merely guilty. An informer with inside access to the printing trade—a man who knew every press, every plate, every smuggler who moved paper past the harbor watch—was worth ten arrested printers. And a man worth that much could buy things. Could buy, specifically, a struck name. *The household of Esteban Vasquez, exempted for services rendered.*

It was option three that Tomás carried into the annex, and he carried it honestly, insofar as he was capable of honesty on the subject. This was the part he would never be able to explain later, not to Mirabel, not to himself in the small hours: that he believed it. Truly believed it, standing in Ordóñez's office with his heart going like a triphammer under his waistcoat, that he was doing this *for* them. Protection dressed in its best coat. What was betrayal, compared to a brother alive? What was a manifesto, compared to a mother who still had a kitchen to stand in?

He was twenty-nine years old and he had spent his whole life as an appendix to his father's integrity. He was about to find out what he was worth on his own.

* * *

"You say you can give us Aldana's network," Ordóñez said. "Your file says your father barely met the man."

"My father met him twice. Once in '94, once in '96. Both times a man came afterward to discuss terms of printing—paper stock, format, payment." Tomás let the pause do its work. "A smuggler. Works the low-tide channels below the salt warehouses. If you have any harbor intelligence at all, you already know the name I'm going to say, and if you don't, then I'm more valuable in this chair than your whole second floor."

Ordóñez's face gave away nothing, which Tomás correctly read as: *the second floor has been guessing for months.*

"Reyes," Tomás said.

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Paper rustling somewhere down the hall. A press thudding three floors below, steady as a heartbeat. Somewhere outside, a bugle, badly played.

"And what does Reyes want with the Vasquez shop?" Ordóñez asked at last. "Assuming we grant your premise."

"He wants what your prisoner wants. The manifesto, printed and distributed before the year turns. Symbolic timing matters to men like Aldana. A century dying and a new word born—poetry sells better than rifles among dockworkers." Tomás shrugged, a carefully practiced gesture, the shrug of a man discussing weather. "My father has the plates. Has had them for years, hidden—I'd guess in the shop itself, because he trusts his type cases the way other men trust confession. If the plates are recovered, the network has nothing to deliver. No printing, no distribution, no moment. You don't need to catch smugglers. You need to catch metal."

"You're proposing to sell us your father's plates."

"I'm proposing," Tomás said, and here he leaned forward, and here was the speech he had built on the harbor wall, plank by plank, "to save this government from its own stupidity. Arrest my father and you get a sealed shop and a martyr. Recover the plates quietly—through me—and you get the manifesto itself, in your hands, before it exists. You print it if you want. Burn it. Better: you print your own version, corrected, and let the rebels distribute a forgery they don't know is a forgery. You have a propaganda office. Use it. Every copy Aldana's people put in a dockworker's hands could be a copy you wrote." He sat back. "I'm not selling my father. I'm offering you the whole war of ink, won in one transaction. And all it costs you is leaving one family off a list."

*It costs me*, said a voice from somewhere underneath his sternum, quiet and precise. *It costs me everything I was before I walked up these stairs.* He stepped on the voice. Later. He would reckon with it later, when the family was safe, when the commission was real, when there was leisure for conscience the way there was leisure for gout—afflictions of men who could afford them.

Ordóñez studied him for a long moment. Then he opened a drawer, took out a form, and slid it across the desk. It was a receipt. For *one (1) informational consultation, pending.* There was a space for Tomás's signature near the bottom, above a line of fine print he would read four times in the coming days and understand fully only much too late.

"You understand," Ordóñez said, almost gently, "that a man who informs on his father will be watched very carefully indeed. We use such men once, Señor Vasquez. Well, generally."

"I'm not informing on my father," Tomás said, signing. "I'm informing on a smuggler. My part ends when you recover the plates. After that, my family emigrates—you'll fund it, quietly—or the arrangement is void, and you can explain to your colonel why you let Aldana's manifesto walk out of this conversation unsigned."

Bold. Too bold—he saw it land wrong, saw Ordóñez's mouth flatten—but boldness read as leverage to men like this, and leverage was the only currency Tomás possessed.

"We'll want the location of the plates," Ordóñez said. "Not eventually. Soon. Say—within the week."

"A week," Tomás agreed, and heard his own voice steady, and marveled at it. "I'll need three days at least. These things must look natural. A search conducted too conveniently looks arranged, and arranged searches raise questions upstairs. Questions upstairs are the ones that ruin careers." He stood, turned sideways out of the too-narrow gap beside the desk, and shook the offered hand. Ordóñez's palm was dry and cool, a clerk's hand.

At the door, Ordóñez spoke again, and the gentleness was gone.

"One thing, Señor Vasquez. The exemption you want—for the household." A pause with paperwork in it. "Exemptions come from above my floor. I can recommend. I cannot promise."

"Then I'll keep recommending value," Tomás said, "until someone upstairs promises."

He walked out into the gray morning feeling taller, which he noted with clinical interest, because he was almost certain he had not actually grown.

* * *

The walk home took forty minutes. He took the long route along the harbor wall, and somewhere around the fish market he stopped being able to sustain the architecture of the argument and it began, brick by brick, to come apart in his hands.

Because here was the flaw, and he had known it was a flaw even as he signed: the plates. He had promised Ordóñez the plates within the week, and he did not know where they were. His guess—the type cases, the shop itself—was a guess. His father had hidden them in '96 and had said nothing since, not to Tomás, not to anyone, and his father was a man who kept silences the way other men kept ledgers, faithfully, daily, unto death.

And if he could not find them—if the search came up empty—then Tomás was not a valuable informant. He was a son who had sold his father's name to buy time he couldn't spend. The receipt in his pocket would become, overnight, evidence. Names signed to informational consultations had a way of appearing on subsequent lists themselves; he had seen the mechanism work on lesser men in the last month alone.

So he would have to find the plates. Quietly, before the junta did, before Mirabel noticed him turning over font cases in the family shop like a thief in his own inheritance. He would search his father's domain with a son's hands and a stranger's purpose, and he would have to be careful, because Mirabel missed nothing in that shop—Mirabel read the type cases the way priests read scripture, and she would notice a displaced sort the way she'd notice a missing day of the week.

Fine. Careful, then. Three days. He had bought three days, and in three days he would hand Ordóñez the metal and walk his family out from under the list, and whatever he lost of himself in the transaction he would pay gladly and count it cheap—

He stopped at the harbor rail. Below, the tide was going out, baring the black ribs of the mudflats where the smugglers' boats lay over like sleeping animals. Somewhere down there, presumably, was Reyes. Somewhere beyond him, Aldana. Somewhere behind him, on a table under a dead-bird letter, his father's four words: *I intend to keep my word.*

Everyone in this story intends to keep their word, Tomás thought. Father to Aldana. Mirabel to Father. Me to Mother, to the memory of the shop at closing time, to the boy I was at twelve, setting type badly and dreaming of owning the press instead of serving it. Everyone keeps their word, and everyone's word points at the rest of us like a barrel.

He turned up his collar and climbed the hill toward home, toward the shop, toward the long patient silence of his father's type cases, and began—without letting himself name it as searching—to plan how a son might go through his father's life, drawer by drawer, without leaving fingerprints.

Below him in the harbor, unseen, the tide kept falling, laying bare everything the water had spent all year covering.
