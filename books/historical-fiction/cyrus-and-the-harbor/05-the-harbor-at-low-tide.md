## 5. The Harbor at Low Tide

The harbor at low tide smelled like a confession—salt and rot and everything the city wanted buried. Mirabel had grown up two streets from it, close enough to hear the bells on the fishing boats, far enough never to have to look at what the tide left behind when it withdrew: black weed in ropes, broken crates, a shoe, once, that no one claimed. Her mother used to say the sea took back its debts twice a day. Mirabel had never understood what she meant until now, walking the exposed mudflats with her skirts pinned up to her knees and her father's letter folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had told him she was going to buy lamp oil. It was almost true; she carried the empty can as proof, swinging it at her side like a bell that rang nothing.

Reyes. That was all the name she had. A smuggler who ran Aldana's messages when there were messages worth running, according to Señor Ibarra, the coffee importer her father trusted more than he trusted anyone outside the family—which, after the letter arrived, was a category that seemed to grow wider every day. *Ask for Reyes at the harbor,* Ibarra had said, when her father sent her with a sealed question. *Low tide. He likes to see people coming.* Then he had looked at her the way men look at something they're not sure they want to be responsible for, and added: *Don't tell him your name until you have to.*

So she walked out onto the flats where the boats knelt in the mud like tired animals, hulls propped on their keels, keel-barnacles drying white in the late light. There were maybe thirty vessels in the inner harbor, and most of them were honest, which meant useless to her. Somewhere among them was one boat that moved things other than fish—one boat whose captain knew whether General Aldana was alive or dead, whether a promise made in peacetime still counted for anything in a city where soldiers burned newspapers in the plaza like festival bonfires.

Her shoes were ruined. She'd known they would be. The mud took each footstep with an obscene sucking sound, as if the harbor itself were reading her intentions aloud.

*You are being followed.* She didn't turn around. If someone was behind her—and the prickle between her shoulder blades insisted someone was—turning would only confirm it. She kept her pace even, kept the oil can swinging, let herself be exactly what she appeared to be: a shopkeeper's daughter on an errand, careless, unremarkable, invisible. Invisibility was a skill. She had spent twenty-three years perfecting it in a print shop full of men who saw the type but not the hands that set it.

*Let them follow. Let them see me buy lamp oil.*

Except she wasn't going to buy lamp oil, and if they followed long enough, they would see that too.

* * *

She found him by accident, which she would later understand was how Reyes preferred to be found.

At the far end of the flats, past the fish market's pilings, there was a stretch of mud where old hulls went to die—three or four wrecks beached and listing, their ribs open to the sky, too rotten for salvage and too stubborn to sink completely. Between two of them, half-hidden in the shadow of a capsized dory, sat a narrow black-hulled boat with its sail furled flat and its paint gone dull on purpose. A man crouched beside it, scraping barnacles off the hull with a knife, unhurried, as though the whole junta were a growth he'd get to eventually.

Mirabel stopped ten paces away. The mud did not suck at her feet this time; it held her fast, and she felt pinned, exposed, exactly as visible as she had spent her whole life avoiding being.

"I'm looking for Reyes," she said.

He didn't look up. He was somewhere past fifty, lean as the knife he held, with a face weathered into something like driftwood and eyes that missed nothing, which she could feel even without seeing them. "You found the flats," he said. "Flats are full of things people are looking for."

"My name is Mirabel Vasquez."

That got his attention. The knife paused mid-scrape, and he turned his head—not his whole body, just the head, economical as everything else about him seemed to be—and looked at her for the first time. Whatever he saw, he studied the way a customs officer studies a manifest: looking for the false line.

"Vasquez," he repeated. "The printer."

"The printer's daughter."

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes." She heard the word come out of her harder than she'd intended, and decided not to take it back. "There is."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, of a bid placed. He went back to his barnacles. "Ibarra sent you."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it. Instead she pulled the letter from inside her coat—the letter her father had received under the door three nights ago, the one naming the Vasquez household in a plot to print sedition for a man the state had declared dead before he was dead—and held it out.

Reyes looked at the envelope without taking it. "I don't read other people's mail."

"It's not other people's. It's mine now. We're all named in it."

"Then burning it would improve everyone's situation."

"Burning it won't stop them from coming."

"No," he agreed, mildly. "But it makes better theater when they arrive and find nothing." He finally set down the knife, wiped it, folded it away into some pocket of his worn canvas coat, and stood—a slow unfolding, joints announcing themselves, a man built by decades of work rather than diminished by it. Up close he was shorter than she expected and denser, like something packed for a long voyage. "Sit, then. No—" he raised a hand before she could move, "—stand. Mud's honest but it holds grudges. Walk with me. People talk less to two backs than to two faces."

They walked the tideline together, past the wrecks, toward the breakwater, and Reyes talked the way she imagined he sailed: watching the whole horizon while appearing to look at nothing.

"So. The general promised your father work, your father promised the general ink, and now the state has decided that paper is treason. Yes?" He glanced sideways at her. "You can nod. I've already said it."

"How do you know all of that?"

"Because I carried half of it." He said it without pride and without apology, a fact set down like cargo. "Two years ago, before any of this had a shape. When Aldana was just a loud old soldier with enemies in the right places and a manuscript nobody else would touch. Your father took it because your father is the last man in Vellamare who believes a press is a promise and not a product. I told him then it would cost him." A pause. "I didn't think it would cost him this much. Or this fast."

"The letter says conspirators. It says *apprehension and examination*. Do you know what examination means?"

"I know what it means when the junta writes it."

"Then you know why I'm here." She stopped walking and turned to face him, and the mud gripped her ankles like hands. "My father won't ask you for anything. His pride is—he's a good man, and it's making him stupid. He thinks if we do nothing, print nothing, say nothing, they'll lose interest. But I set type six days a week, señor, and I know how words work. They wrote our names down. Names don't lose interest. Names get acted upon." Her voice was shaking and she hated it and she kept talking anyway. "I need to reach Aldana's people. Whoever's left. Whoever is still willing to receive what my father promised to print. Can you get a message to them or not?"

For a long moment Reyes said nothing. The tide, far out, ticked against the breakwater stones, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere behind them in the fish market a woman was arguing about prices, shrill and ordinary, a sound from a country that no longer existed.

"You came down here alone," he said at last. "Unaccompanied. Unwatched, you hoped."

"I was watched. I didn't turn around."

That earned her a real look this time—longer, recalibrating. "Who watched you?"

"A soldier, I think. Grey coat, near the market stairs. He turned off before the pilings."

"He turned off," Reyes said, "because grey coats don't work the harbor. The harbor works itself. Everyone down here owes everyone else something, and the junta hasn't bought enough debts yet." He crouched suddenly, picked up a piece of broken shell, turned it over in his fingers. "Here's what I know about you, Mirabel Vasquez, since you were generous enough to hand me your name on open flats. You are either the bravest person I've met this year or the most frightened, and the two conditions look identical from the outside. And here's what you know about me: nothing. You've asked a stranger to connect your hunted family to a hunted general, on trust, with nothing offered and nothing promised."

"What would you offer?"

"That depends entirely on what you're asking me to carry."

"The manifesto," she said. "When it's printed. My father keeps his promises. Even the ones other people have made fatal." She swallowed. "I keep them too."

Reyes tossed the shell back toward the waterline. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—still unhurried, but stripped of its performance, the way a helmsman speaks when the wind shifts. "I'll tell you what I told your father two years ago. What you're carrying isn't paper. It's a match, and Vellamare is dry timber, and there are men who want the fire and men who fear it more than the junta, and both kinds will come for whoever strikes it. The rebels want that manifesto suppressed as badly as the colonels do—not because it's lies, but because it's true too soon, on their schedule, not yours. Understand? You wouldn't be hunted by one enemy. You'd be hunted by two, and they hate each other more than they hate you, which is the only thing that will keep you alive, because while they watch each other, they sometimes forget to watch you. Sometimes."

"Then I'm asking anyway."

"And I haven't said yes."

"No," Mirabel said. "You haven't."

He studied her again, and this time she met it, and neither of them blinked much, and the tide kept its own counsel below the breakwater.

"Low tide tomorrow," Reyes said finally. "Same hour. Not here—at the customs stair, third arch, where the fishermen mend nets. Wear gloves. Bring nothing written. If anyone asks, you're buying oysters from Catalina, who will confirm it, because Catalina hates the colonels more than she loves money, which is rare down here and therefore precious." He began walking back toward his hidden boat, then stopped, half-turned. "One more thing. The letter your family received. It named you all?"

"Father. Me. My brother Tomás."

"And your brother—where does your brother stand?"

"With us," she said. "With Father."

"Mm," said Reyes, in a tone that contained an entire unread page, and walked on.

* * *

She bought the lamp oil on the way home because the errand might as well be true. At the chandler's on Rope Street she paid, and made change conversation, and smiled at the chandler's wife, and performed ordinariness like a woman laying type: one character at a time, in the right order, so the sentence read clean.

Grey coat was gone. That should have comforted her. It didn't. Being watched, at least, meant knowing where the eyes were.

The walk home took her along the top of the seawall, where the whole harbor lay exposed below her—the kneeling boats, the dying wrecks, the small dark figure of Reyes already pushing his hull off the mud with a barge pole, silent as a thought, sliding out through channels only he could read. He had not said yes. He had not said no. He had given her a time and a place and a cover story and a warning dressed as a question about Tomás.

She turned the question over on the rest of the walk home and could not make it lie down flat. Tomás had been strange lately—out late, new boots, a new way of talking about the junta that sounded less like hatred and more like study. *Someone has to keep the family safe,* he'd said last week, at dinner, in the particular reasonable voice he used when he'd already decided something and was merely letting the rest of them in on it. Their father hadn't answered. Mirabel hadn't either. The silence at that table had been the loudest thing printed in the house all month.

By the time she reached the print shop, the streetlamps were lit and the soldiers at the corner had changed shift, and the window of Vasquez & Son glowed warm over the press like a lantern over a grave. Through the glass she could see her father at the composing stone, working late as always, his hands moving over the type with the tenderness of a man touching something he knew he might lose.

She stood outside a moment longer than she needed to. Down the street, a door opened and closed. Tomás stepped out of the shadows across from the tobacconist, buttoning his coat, moving quickly, head down—and he did not see her, or pretended not to, and turned the wrong way for home.

Mirabel watched him go until the dark took him. Then she went inside, set down the lamp oil, and picked up her composing stick.

Tomorrow, low tide, third arch. Tonight, there was type to set, and a family to sit down with at dinner, and a brother she intended to look in the eye.

The mud had held her fast on the flats and let her go. She understood now what her mother meant about the sea collecting its debts. Hers had just come due, and she had walked out to meet it, and the stranger with the knife had asked one question she couldn't answer:

Where does your brother stand?

She was going home to find out.
