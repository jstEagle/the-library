## 6. Soldiers in the Shop

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, while my father was still in his shirtsleeves and I was still sorting the morning's type from yesterday's forme. Not a knock, exactly—a fist on wood, three times, then again, as though once were insufficient to establish authority.

"Señor Vasquez." The voice through the door was polite in the way that made my stomach drop before my mind caught up. "Open, please."

My father did not move at first. He stood with the composing stick still in his hand, and for one long breath the shop was so quiet I could hear the lead cooling in my memory—the way it does when you've worked type long enough, that faint settling sound of metal becoming letters.

"Papa," I whispered.

"Yes," he said, which answered nothing. He set down the stick, wiped his palms on his apron—wiped them thoroughly, as if ink itself could incriminate—and unbarred the door.

Four soldiers came in. Then a fifth who was not a soldier, or wore his rank badly: a thin man with a ledger under his arm and a pencil behind his ear, the kind of clerk who arrives after violence has been arranged elsewhere. He had the look of someone who had counted many things and enjoyed none of them.

"Capitán Ossorio," he said, not offering his hand. "Propaganda Office. We are conducting an inspection of printing establishments." He unfolded a paper with a stamp on it, red wax gone dull in the gray morning light. "By order of the Provisional Government of Vellamare."

My father read the order the way he read proofs—twice, slowly, lips moving over the clauses. I watched his face do nothing at all. That was the first miracle of that morning, and I did not understand until much later how much discipline nothing requires.

"Of course," my father said. "We print wedding invitations. Obituaries. The shipping notices for Herrera & Sons. You can see the job books yourself."

"We will see everything," Ossorio said. "Everyone out of the way, please."

* * *

They began, absurdly, at the door. The clerk—he had no name he offered, so I thought of him as the Pencil—took down the shop's license number, the date of my father's registration, the number of presses, the number of employees. Two, he wrote, glancing at me without seeing me, which was fine. Invisibility is a skill like any other. I had spent nineteen years learning it in this shop: how to stand behind my father's shoulder at trade gatherings; how to answer questions about type sizes with just enough boredom that men stopped asking; how to be the daughter at the counter rather than the compositor whose kerning was better than her father's. The junta saw a girl in an apron dusted with lead. Let them.

The soldiers moved through the front room with their boots leaving crescent prints in the dust between the cabinets. One of them picked up a galley of the Herrera shipping notice, held it upside down, and put it back. Another opened a cabinet of ornaments—fleurons, rules, a case of printer's flowers we used on funeral cards—and ran his fingers through the little brass shapes as if panning for something.

Ossorio watched all this with the patience of a man being paid by the hour. Then he said, mildly, "You are Vasquez. The elder."

"I am Emilio Vasquez."

"You knew General Aldana."

I did not stop sorting type. That took everything I had. My hands kept moving through the California job case—b, e, h, i, k—because if they stopped they would shake, and if they shook he would see it, and what my hands knew my face would confirm.

"I printed for half this city," my father said. "Officers. Merchants. The bishop's office, when Bishop Ferreol wanted his pastoral letters done properly. Yes. Aldana came to me. Years ago. A bookplate, I believe. And some speeches, back when he was only a colonel making speeches."

"Before," Ossorio agreed. "And after?"

"After what?"

"After he became inconvenient." Ossorio turned a page in his ledger. The pencil came down from behind his ear. "There is a rumor, señor, that your shop holds something belonging to the general. Paper. Or metal. We would very much like to find it before someone less courteous does."

"Then I hope you find it," my father said, "wherever it is, because it is not here."

It was beautifully done. I have thought about it since—how flat his voice was, how perfectly bored, a craftsman interrupted at his craft. But I also knew what I alone in that room knew: three nights earlier, wrapped in oilcloth and carried like a sleeping child, the plates had come up from the cellar where they'd lain since spring. Aldana's manifesto. Forty pages set and cast in lead during the last week of peace, when my father had believed—God help us—that promises made in peacetime were a different currency than promises weighed under a junta. Forty pages of sedition, sitting twenty feet from where Ossorio stood, because my father had moved them at midnight into the one place he said no soldier would ever look closely.

I had helped him carry them. That fact sat in my chest like swallowed stone.

"Search it," Ossorio said, and the shop stopped belonging to us.

* * *

They were not gentle, but they were not vandals either—that was somehow worse. Vandals you could hate. These men worked with the thoroughness of people doing a chore: lifting formes off the stone and setting them back crooked by a finger's width, opening drawers and running knives through the paper stock, tipping the paper cutter on its side to check beneath. One soldier took the cellar. I heard him down there kicking through the empty crates, and I stood at the head of the stairs with a lamp, playing the daughter annoyed at the mess, while my heart counted the seconds of a search that could not find anything because there was nothing left to find.

Except there was. There were forty pages of the general's voice in lead, and they were upstairs.

My father had hidden them in plain sight, in the manner of a man who understood that soldiers search for what is concealed and pass by what is merely wrong. Case 14. It sat against the north wall with the other job cases, and it had sat there broken since before I could read—a cracked frame, two loose dividers, compartments spilling into one another so that its type was a jumble of mixed sizes, ruined sorts, orphaned characters nobody had sorted in fifteen years. Every shop has such a case. It is furniture. It is nothing.

Inside it, laid flat beneath the jumble, wrapped in oilcloth: the plates.

No one hides treasure in a broken drawer. That was my father's whole wager, and I stood in the middle of the shop pretending to sweep while four soldiers dismantled my life around it.

* * *

The Pencil found things to write down. He wrote down our subscription list—names of customers who took the shipping bulletin—and I watched him copy each name and understood that every name on that page would be visited within the week. He wrote down that we owned a paper knife with an ivory handle. He wrote down that the younger employee was female, approximately nineteen years. He asked my name and I gave it, because refusing was its own confession, and watched him write *M. Vasquez* without interest.

He should have been interested. I have wondered since whether history would have bent differently if he had looked up.

The tall soldier reached the cabinet wall at half past nine. I swept. My father stood by the window with his arms folded, wearing the expression of a man watching rain delay his work, and I marveled at him even as I wanted to scream at him to move, to stand between the soldiers and Case 14, to do anything but be so convincingly indifferent.

Case 12 came off the wall. Empty forms, sorted type in good order. The soldier tipped it, shook it, put it back.

Case 13. Wedding invitation stock. He fanned the blank cards and let them fall.

Then Case 14.

He lifted it down with both hands—it was heavy, heavier than it should have been, and I saw him register the weight, saw his shoulders adjust to it—and set it on the imposing stone. The cracked frame shifted. A divider fell out entirely. Type slid across the compartments with a sound like rain on tin.

"What is this?" the soldier said.

"Broken," my father said, from the window, without turning. "Fifteen years broken. If you can find a complete alphabet in there, boy, take the case as a reward."

The soldier grinned at his own reflection in the stone and dug his fingers into the type. I swept. The broom handle was slick under my palms. He stirred the jumble—mixed sizes, ruined sorts, orphaned characters, and beneath all of it, forty pages of oilcloth-wrapped treason that his fingertips were now within one inch of touching—

He pulled out a handful, looked at the mess of it, let it rattle back. "Basura," he said, and wiped his hand on his trousers, and reached for Case 15.

I put down the broom. Very carefully. Because if I had kept sweeping I think I would have dropped it.

* * *

They finished at noon. By ten I had learned a new thing about terror, which is that it does not peak and pass; it compresses. For those hours I lived inside a held breath so long my body forgot how it went, and when Ossorio finally closed his ledger and said, "The shop passes inspection, señor Vasquez—for today," the air in the room did not change at all. Compression does not release on command.

"For today," my father repeated.

"The city is full of rumors," Ossorio said, buttoning his coat. "Rumors need either proof or graves. I prefer proof—it makes fewer widows and better reports. But my superiors, señor, are less sentimental." He put on his cap. At the door he paused, and for the first time looked directly at me, a long flat look, the look of a man adding a column. "Your daughter sets a clean page. I noticed the shipping notice. Good kerning is rare these days."

Then they were gone, boots on the cobbles, fading toward the harbor road, and the bell above the door swung twice and was still.

* * *

Neither of us moved for a full minute. Then my father crossed the shop, lowered himself onto the stool at the stone—he lowered himself; until that morning I had never seen him seem old—and pressed both hands flat against Case 14 as if taking its pulse.

"He almost—" I started.

"Yes."

"He touched them, Papa. His fingers were right—"

"Yes, Mirabel." Not sharp. Just final. He drew the case toward him and began, with two fingers, easing the loose type away from the oilcloth edges, uncovering the plates inch by inch as if checking a wound. The top plate showed the first line of the manifesto, cast mirror-wise, Aldana's words in reverse: *When the government fears its people—*

"He wrote down our subscribers," I said. "Every name. And he looked at me, Papa. At the end. He said my kerning was good."

My father went still over the plates.

"A man like that doesn't notice kerning," he said quietly. "A man like that notices hands. He marked you, mija." He looked up at me across the stone, across forty pages of the future sitting in a broken drawer, and for the first time that morning I saw fear in my father's face—not for himself. "Which means we have less time than I told Reyes. Less time than I promised."

Outside, the patrol passed again on its round, boots keeping the city's new rhythm. Inside, my father slid the oilcloth back over Aldana's words and said the sentence I would carry through everything that came after:

"We print this week, or we don't print at all."
