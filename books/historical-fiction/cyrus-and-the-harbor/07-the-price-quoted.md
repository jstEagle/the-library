## 7. The Price Quoted

The propaganda office kept its headquarters in the old customs house, and Tomás Vasquez climbed the marble stairs at nine o'clock sharp, four minutes before his appointment, wearing his second-best coat because the best one made him look like he was trying.

He had learned that much already. Ambition, like type, was all in the setting. The same letters, arranged carelessly, read as desperation. Arranged with care, they read as destiny.

A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took his name without looking up. *Vasquez.* The word traveled down a corridor. Tomás stood beneath a portrait of some dead colonial administrator and practiced keeping his face still.

Three weeks ago he had come to this building as a supplicant. He remembered the sensation clearly—the way his stomach had knotted when the guard asked his business, the way he'd rehearsed his opening statement on the walk over and then abandoned half of it. That first meeting had lasted eleven minutes and accomplished nothing except establishing that he existed, that he was educated, that he understood printing. He'd been dismissed with a nod and a card.

But he had come back. And back again. And now there was an appointment, with a time written on it in an official hand, and a clerk who knew his name.

That was how power worked, he was beginning to understand. Not as a door you broke through but as a corridor you walked down often enough that people stopped asking why.

"Señor Vasquez." A different man this time—Captain Ibarra, broad-shouldered, civilian-dressed but military in the way he filled a doorway. "Come in. Sit. Coffee?"

The office overlooked the harbor. Tomás noted that and filed it away: the junta wanted to be seen looking at the sea. There was a message in the furniture arrangement, the visitor's chair placed so that whoever sat in it would face the windows, the water, the gunboats riding at anchor. You were meant to feel small against all that horizon. Or large. He wasn't sure which.

"I appreciate you making the time, Captain."

"You've made the time, Señor Vasquez. Four visits." Ibarra smiled. He had good teeth, which Tomás distrusted instinctively; men with bad teeth had usually earned them honestly. "You're persistent."

"Persistence is a family trait."

"So I understand." Ibarra set down his cup and opened a leather folder on the desk. The gesture was unhurried, almost gentle. "Your father is Esteban Vasquez. Proprietor of the print shop on Calle del Sol. Thirty-one years in operation. Your mother died in the fever year. Your sister works the cases—Mirabel, yes? They say her justification work is the finest in Vellamare."

Tomás kept his face still. "You've done your reading."

"It's my profession." The captain turned a page. "Your father's shop has printed parish bulletins, shipping manifests, tobacco labels, and for six years running the official city almanac. A respectable house." Another page. "And in the spring of '96, your father dined twice with Emilio Aldana."

The harbor light lay flat and silver on the desk between them. Tomás heard himself say, easily, "My father prints for many people. Printers don't choose their customers any more than taverns do."

"No," Ibarra agreed. "They don't. Which is why we're not interested in your father. We're interested in his press."

* * *

Here it was, then. Tomás had guessed at the shape of this meeting for two weeks and had been wrong about every detail except the essential one: they wanted something, and they were prepared to pay.

Ibarra laid it out with the smoothness of a man who had made this offer before, in other cities, to other sons.

"The new government values stability above all things. Stability requires trust, and trust requires that institutions continue functioning. The almanac must be printed. The proclamations, the notices, the currency vouchers. The city cannot operate without a press of quality, and yours is the finest in Vellamare." He paused. "We would like to formalize that relationship. An exclusive contract. And—" he turned the folder around, and there was a document on the letterhead of the Provisional Junta, with seals on it, actual seals, "—a commission. Propaganda Section, rank of lieutenant, salary attached. It would suit a man of your abilities. Someone will run the shop's civic operations. It may as well be someone named Vasquez."

Tomás looked at the paper for a long moment. He did not touch it. He had learned that too, somewhere—you didn't reach for the bait while the hook was still visible.

"And the conditions?"

"One condition." Ibarra leaned back. "General Aldana is an enemy of the state. Before his status changed, he was a customer of your father's shop. We have reason to believe he commissioned certain material—a manuscript, and plates to print it. Seditious material. If such material exists in the Vasquez premises, its presence constitutes a threat to your family. To your sister. To your father's legacy." He spread his hands. "Remove the threat, and everything else follows naturally. The contract, the commission, the protection of the state. It's paperwork, Señor Vasquez. Nothing more dramatic than paperwork."

*Paperwork.* Tomás almost laughed. This was what no one told you about corruption—it didn't arrive with knives in the street. It arrived politely, with coffee and seals and a captain who remembered your sister's name. It arrived with better handwriting than most love letters.

"My father," Tomás said carefully, "keeps his own accounts. His own premises. I've been away from the shop these two years—I only returned in the autumn. I can't speak to what may or may not be stored there."

"Of course not." Ibarra sounded entirely untroubled. "That's why we're offering three days."

* * *

Three days. He walked home the long way, along the seawall, because the wind helped him think and because he could not yet bear to turn up the Calle del Sol and see the shop sign—*E. VASQUEZ, HIJOS E IMPRESORES*, the paint fading, the word *hijos* doing work it had never been authorized to do.

Sons. One son, at present, selling.

No. He made himself look at it squarely, the way he'd looked at the ledger when he'd taken over the failing tobacco brokerage in the interior and turned it around in fourteen months. Look at the numbers. Name the thing.

What was being offered: survival. Not just his—his father's, his sister's. If the plates were surrendered quietly, there need be no arrest at all. No soldiers in the shop, no scene, none of the ugliness that came of making the state ask twice. Ibarra had all but said it: cooperate, and the search never happens. Refuse, or stall past the deadline, and the search happens anyway, with less courtesy. The choice wasn't between honor and betrayal. It was between betrayal performed neatly and catastrophe performed publicly.

That was the arithmetic, and it was almost convincing, and he hated how almost it was. Because underneath the arithmetic ran another current, older, that he couldn't dam: the memory of his father's hands moving through the type case like a priest at altar rail, the way the old man had said once, mildly, not even to Tomás particularly, *A press is a promise, mijo. Every job you take, you keep your word twice—once to the customer, once to the reader.* He'd been sixteen and bored and had nodded the way boys nod.

His father had promised Aldana something. Tomás didn't know exactly what—he'd been away—but he knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a debt in a house even when no one speaks of it. And now the junta was offering to forgive the debt if only the debtor's son would burn the ledger.

And Mirabel.

He thought of her at the cases, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with lead, the way she could hold a line of type up to the light and find the one crooked letter among eighty. She thought she was invisible in that shop. She had built her whole self into something the world looked through. But the captain had said her name. *They say her justification work is the finest in Vellamare.* The state had files. Files had names. Names got read aloud in rooms overlooking harbors.

If he did nothing, she would be swept into whatever came. If he did something—

He stopped at the seawall rail. Below, the tide was out, exposing the black ribs of the harbor bottom, the hulls of boats that would float again by evening. Everything depended on when you looked at it. Low tide or high tide. Scandal or contract. Betrayal or protection. The same objects either way.

Three days. He had asked for three days—or rather, Ibarra had offered three days before Tomás could ask for anything, which was its own kind of lesson. Power never waited to be petitioned; it pre-empted the petition so you'd remember who held the calendar.

He would use them anyway. Three days to go through the shop himself, before the soldiers ever could. Find whatever it was his father was hiding—because he was hiding something; Tomás had watched him not discuss the letter for a week now, burning nothing and saying less, and silence that complete was itself a confession. Find it, and decide.

Decide what? The honest answer, standing at the rail with the gulls screaming, was that he hadn't decided. He told himself he needed to know where the plates were either way—to protect the family if he cooperated, to warn the others if he didn't. Both stories were available to him. That was the terrible luxury of the uncommitted: every option still wore its best clothes.

But he noticed, walking the rest of the way home, which story he kept rehearsing. In the rehearsal, he signed the paper. In the rehearsal, the shop passed to him legally, cleanly, and his father retired with dignity, and Mirabel—Mirabel would chafe, would call it cowardice, would stand in what had become *his* shop with her arms crossed and her eyes full of a contempt that would never fully drain. He saw that scene so clearly it might have already happened. And still he kept it. Because in the rehearsal everyone was alive. Everyone ate dinner. The type stayed lead and never became evidence.

Wasn't that the whole duty of an elder son? To keep everyone eating dinner?

* * *

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and his father's supper going cold on the stove, and it struck him, entering, how much a print shop was a body—press for heart, cases for lungs, the smell of solvent for blood—and how easily it could become a corpse.

His father sat at the composing stone, sorting type he had already sorted. Mirabel was at the far wall of cases, and she looked up when he came in, and he saw her read his face the way she read a galley proof—fast, thorough, finding the error.

"Well?" she said. Not *how was your day*. Well.

"There's an offer," Tomás said. "Contract for the civic work. A commission for me."

His father's hands went still over the type. Mirabel set her composing stick down with a small deliberate click.

"An offer," his father said, "from whom?"

"The Provisional Government. Through Captain Ibarra." He kept his voice level, administrative, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "In exchange for certain considerations regarding material allegedly held on these premises."

"Allegedly." Mirabel came out from behind the case rack. "They used the word *allegedly*?"

"They used many words. Seals on paper make men generous with vocabulary."

His father had not moved. In the lamplight the old man seemed carved from something denser than flesh, and Tomás realized with a small cold shock that his father was not surprised. Whatever had been set in motion in the spring of '96, whatever the sealed letter had named, Esteban Vasquez had already priced this out behind his own closed doors. He had simply arrived at a different total.

"Three days," Tomás said. "Ibarra gives us three days."

"We," Mirabel said. "Since when are you the *we* who negotiates for this house?"

"Since the house needed negotiating for."

"By selling it."

"By keeping it standing!" The words came out harder than he intended, and he saw them land, saw his father flinch at the volume as though the press had dropped something heavy. He lowered his voice. "There are men across the harbor right now deciding whether our name goes on an arrest list or a payroll. I sat in a room today where both futures were on the table, Mirabel. Actual paper. Actual seals. Do you understand what that's worth? Most families get no table at all."

"Most families," Mirabel said, "don't have anything to sell."

Her eyes held his and he could not read them, or would not let himself read them, because he suspected that what was in them was not anger but disappointment, and disappointment from his sister weighed more than any seal-stamped document in the city.

His father spoke then, quietly, to the type in his hands. "Did they say what material?"

"You know what material."

"Did they say?"

"A manuscript and plates. Commissioned by Aldana." Tomás watched his father's thumbs press into the little lead letters until the skin whitened. "Papá. Whatever it is. Whatever you promised him. The world that made that promise doesn't exist anymore. The man it was made to is declared an enemy of everyone in this room. A promise to a ghost can't bind the living." He heard the argument as he made it and knew it was his best one and knew, in the same instant, that it was borrowed—that somewhere in a marble office a captain had said these exact ideas in better words, and Tomás had carried them home like stolen goods.

His father set the type down, each piece in its proper compartment, and looked up at his son with an expression Tomás had never seen on him before—not anger, not grief, but a kind of patient sorrow, as if marking an error so fundamental it could only be corrected by starting the whole page again.

"Go to bed, Tomás," he said. "All three days belong to you. Spend them carefully."

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in his room above the shop with the lamp low, listening to the building settle, and sometime after midnight he heard movement below—his father's tread, then Mirabel's lighter one, then the scrape of the heavy font drawers, one after another, like a man counting his own ribs.

At two in the morning he dressed and went down quietly and stood in the dark of the shop. The presses slept under their canvas. Moonlight came through the front window in long pale bars, and in those bars his father was working—lifting drawers, weighing them in his hands, shifting weight from one to another with the concentration of a man moving gold.

Moving weight toward the case of broken type. The battered drawer in the corner, the one with the shattered sorts, the mis-cast letters, the casualties of thirty-one years. Worthless lead. Everyone knew it was worthless. Even Tomás had known it was worthless, which meant—

Which meant it wasn't.

His father slid the last drawer home and stood a moment in the moonlight, an old man alone with his promise, and Tomás withdrew up the stairs with his heart going like a press drum, faster than he liked, louder than he liked.

Because now he knew. He had the location, the condition, the consideration—the whole vocabulary of the transaction. By nine o'clock he could be in Ibarra's office, and by noon the seals would be dry, and the shop would be saved, and everyone would eat dinner.

He sat on his bed in the dark and held the knowledge in his hands like a hot compositor's stick.

Three days, he reminded himself. He had asked for nothing, but he would take all three. Two days to be an honest man, trying to talk his father into surrender. One day left over.

For whichever man won.
