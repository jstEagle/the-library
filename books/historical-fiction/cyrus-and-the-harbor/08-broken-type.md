## 8. Broken Type

The font case had been bothering her for three days before she understood why.

Mirabel noticed things. It was the trade that taught her—twenty years of lead type had trained her eye to register what was missing, what was shifted, what sat a half-degree wrong in its compartment. A compositor who couldn't feel a wrongness in a case was a compositor who set lies by accident, and her father had never tolerated accidents.

So when she opened the bottom drawer of the cabinet beneath the stairs—the one they never used, the one holding the ruined fourteen-point Caslon her father had bought at an estate auction a decade ago and sworn he'd restore someday, knowing he never would—she should have felt nothing. Broken type. Dead metal. A drawer of failures.

But the dust was wrong.

She stood there in the cold morning light coming through the shutters, one hand on the drawer's edge, and let herself look at what she was looking at. The layer of gray grime that covered everything in that corner of the shop—the grime that had been undisturbed since the second year of the drought—was smeared. Not cleaned. Smeared. Four long drag-marks through it, like someone had slid the drawer out with their fingertips instead of lifting the handles, and then pushed it back in a hurry, not caring where the dust went because they were certain no one would ever open this drawer again.

Certain. That was the word that made her stomach drop.

She pulled the drawer all the way out and set it on the composing stone, and then she lifted the tray of broken type inside it, the cracked sorts and mangled slugs she'd sorted through a hundred times as a girl looking for salvageable lead, and underneath, wrapped in an oilcloth that had no right to exist in this shop without her knowledge—

Empty space.

The impression was still there in the old newspaper padding. Rectangular, heavy, the size and shape of printing plates. Two of them, side by side. The padding held their shapes the way snow holds footprints: fresh, precise, undeniable.

She put her hand into the hollow where the plates had lain. The paper beneath was still faintly warm with the memory of metal, or she imagined it was. She couldn't decide which was worse.

* * *

She did not scream. She did not run to her father. She closed the drawer, wiped her fingerprints from its face with her apron, and walked back upstairs as though she had found nothing, because until she knew *who*, finding something meant dying for it.

The shop below, the kitchen above—that was the whole architecture of her life. Her father at his desk by the window, correcting proofs he would never have the courage to print. Tomás's room empty since dawn, as it had been empty at dawn every day that week, which was itself new, which was itself a kind of evidence.

Her father looked up when she came in. "You're pale."

"It's cold."

It wasn't cold. It was October, and the stove was lit, and he knew her better than any living person and still believed every lie she told him, because he had taught her all of them.

She made coffee she didn't drink and watched him work and thought about arithmetic. Their house had three doors. The soldiers had searched once already and passed within inches of the plates; her father had hidden them in the broken type and God alone hadn't been enough to save them, only luck and a lieutenant's impatience. If the junta's men came back and the drawer was empty, they would ask why. And whoever had moved the plates had either taken them somewhere safer—and no place in Vellamare was safer than a drawer nobody opened—or somewhere more profitable.

There was only one person in the house who profited from anything anymore.

Tomás came home at dusk smelling of cologne and cigarette smoke, and Mirabel set the table herself, and watched his hands.

* * *

Dinner was salt cod, potatoes, the cheap red wine her father allowed himself one glass of since the letter came. The lamp between them threw three shadows on the wall, and Mirabel thought how strange it was that a family could be reduced to shadows so quickly, how a house could become a place where everyone performed.

"You're working late," she said to Tomás. Neutral. Almost kind.

"There's money in work." He didn't look up from his plate. He'd gotten good at not looking up lately.

"Money." Their father smiled faintly, the old joke arriving on rails. "Vasquez men and money. We print other people's fortunes and count our own pennies."

Tomás smiled too. But Mirabel saw his jaw do the small thing it did when he was calculating—a tightening at the hinge, barely visible, a tell she'd known since they were children fighting over marbles. He was calculating now. Over cod.

"The propaganda office pays its clerks," Tomás said.

The words landed on the table like a dropped knife.

Their father's fork stopped halfway to his mouth. "Its *what*?"

"Clerks, Papá. Records. Translation work." Tomás's voice was smooth, rehearsed, the voice he'd been using for weeks now, and Mirabel realized with a lurch that this dinner was not an accident—he had chosen tonight, chosen this table, chosen the softening ground before the hard thing. "Someone has to keep families fed while the city sorts itself out. I'm keeping us fed."

"By working for them." Her father said it quietly, the way he read a bad proof aloud. "For the men who sent us the letter."

"For the men who haven't arrested us. There's a difference, and the difference has a salary."

"And what does the difference cost?" Mirabel asked.

Tomás finally looked at her. His eyes in the lamplight were their father's eyes, that was the obscenity of it, the same brown, the same lashes, and behind them the same quick intelligence that had memorized type cases at seven years old—turned now toward a different trade entirely.

"Mirabel—"

"What do they pay, Tomás? What's the rate for a Vasquez these days?"

"That's enough," their father said, but softly, always softly, and softness had never stopped anything in the history of the world.

"They asked about the shop," Tomás said, and the honesty of it was so sudden it felt like violence. "They asked me about Papa's correspondence, about visitors, about whether we own a press outside the city. I answered what was safe to answer. I am *answering what is safe*, and if that disgusts you both, you're welcome to starve nobly while I—" He stopped himself. Breathed. Picked up his wine like a man resuming a negotiation. "I'm protecting this family. That's all. That's the whole of it."

"Is it." Mirabel cut her potato in half. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside she was a struck bell. "Then tell me about the bottom drawer."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Outside, somewhere down the hill, a patrol's boots kept time on the cobbles, and all three of them listened to the boots the way sailors listen to weather.

"What drawer," Tomás said.

"The one under the stairs. The one with Papa's ruined Caslon. The one whose dust has fingerprints in it that aren't mine or Papa's." She laid down her fork with great care, because throwing it would have felt too good. "I'm not accusing you of anything, Tomás. I'm asking you what happened to the plates, because if soldiers find them gone from where Papa hid them, they'll assume we sold them, or moved them to use, and neither of those things is survivable. So. What happened to the plates."

Their father had gone very still. She watched understanding cross his face—not just that the hiding place was compromised but that his son might know about it, that his secret and his son's ambition might have touched somewhere in the dark—and the look on him was worse than anger. It was the look of a man doing sums he didn't want to finish.

"I don't know anything about plates," Tomás said.

"You've been out at dawn every day this week."

"I've been at work."

"Where the junta pays you."

"Where someone pays me, yes." He set down his glass with a click. "And here's what I know, little sister, since you want to trade in knowledge: I know that a family named in a sedition letter has perhaps two options. Inform, or disappear. I know that Papa's promise to Aldana is a beautiful thing and it will kill all three of us, and I know—" his voice rose and he forced it back down, and the strain of forcing it was almost painful to watch— "I know that I sit in an office every day with men who mention our surname the way you'd mention a disease, and I smile, and I bring home my salary, and I keep us alive. If you can't honor that, honor the arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," their father repeated. The word seemed to age him in real time.

"Yes, Papá. Arithmetic."

Mirabel looked at her brother across the bread and the lamp and twenty years of shared bed and shared winters, and she understood two things at once, with terrible clarity. The first was that she could not prove anything. The dust told a story, but dust was not evidence; the empty drawer told a story, but the plates could have gone anywhere, been moved by anyone, even by her father himself in some sleepless hour she didn't know about. The second thing was that it didn't matter. Proof was for tribunals. Suspicion needed nothing. It had already moved into the house and unpacked and taken the chair across from her at every meal from now until one of them died.

"Then help me check," she said.

Tomás frowned. "Check what?"

"If you don't know anything, then help me. Tomorrow we go through the shop together, every case, every drawer, every hiding place Papa thinks we don't know about. We confirm the plates are where he left them. If they are, I'll apologize to you in front of God and the harbor." She held his gaze. "If they're not, you'll know, and I'll know, and we'll deal with what's true instead of what's comfortable. Isn't that what you want? To deal with what's true?"

She watched him weigh it. She actually watched the scales move behind his eyes—the easy lie against the risky truth, the long game against tomorrow morning—and she thought, absurdly, *he learned that weighing from Papa, we both did, we learned patience at the same stone.*

"Fine," Tomás said. "Tomorrow. Together. And when we find them exactly where they've always been, you'll stop looking at me like I'm a stranger."

"Agreed," she said, and they shook on nothing, over cod, in lamplight, while their father watched them like a man watching his two hands argue about whether to save the body or the soul.

* * *

He left before dawn, of course. She heard the door at five, heard his footsteps take the hill toward the government quarter, and lay in the dark doing the only arithmetic that mattered: he had agreed too easily. A guilty man argues. An innocent man storms around the table. Tomás had *agreed*, calm as a contract, which meant he already knew what tomorrow's search would find, which meant either the plates were gone forever—

Or he had moved them last night. Or intended to move them back. Or intended to stand beside her at the drawer wearing an innocent face while it sat there empty, having arranged matters so that whatever she found incriminated no one, or incriminated her father, or incriminated the wind.

She rose before light and went down to the shop, and she stood in front of the cabinet under the stairs with a candle in her hand, and she made herself wait until her father's tread crossed the floor above. Some things you did not learn alone.

"Papa," she said when he appeared in the doorway, gray with sleep and worry, "open the drawer."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he crossed the shop, knelt—knees cracking, fifty-nine years old and kneeling to a dead font—and drew the drawer out with the handles, careful, dignified, the way he did everything.

He lifted the tray of broken type.

The oilcloth was there.

The hollow beneath it was not.

Her father stayed kneeling for a long time. Then, very quietly, without turning around, he asked the question Mirabel had been dreading since the dust first showed her its story:

"Mirabel. Where is your brother this morning?"
