## 9. Reyes's Terms

Reyes counted the boats before he counted the money. That was the habit. Boats told you the truth about a harbor; money only told you what someone wanted you to believe.

From the seawall behind the cooperage, low tide had stripped Vellamare's harbor down to its bones. Mudflats the color of old bruises stretched out toward the channel, stitched with the ribs of hulls that should have been broken up years ago. The customs gunboats sat fat and patient in the deeper water, their searchlights sweeping the flats on some schedule known only to men who had never smelled tar.

Three of his boats were where they should be. The fourth — the *Mariposa*, small enough to lie flat in the mud and dumb enough to look worthless — was missing from its slot under the eastern pilings, which meant young Esteban had moved it, which meant the boy was learning, or showing off, or both.

The girl was late.

He didn't mind. Late was good. Punctual people frightened him; they believed the world would hold still for them. The ones who arrived late, breathless, having doubled back twice through alleys because they'd felt eyes on their necks — those were the ones who lived long enough to be useful.

She came along the waterline at last, skirts dark to the knee with harbor filth, moving with her weight forward like a woman carrying something heavy that no one could see. He'd noticed that about her the first time, at the fish stalls. She carried herself like a mule loaded past sense. Not frightened, exactly. Committed. There was a difference, and the difference was worth money, or worth more than money, depending.

"Señor Reyes." She stopped three paces off. Good distance. Close enough to talk, far enough to run.

"Señorita Vasquez." He let the name sit between them a moment longer than courtesy required, watched her not flinch. "You found me."

"You weren't hiding."

"Everyone is hiding. Some of us are just better dressed for it." He gestured at the cooperage wall, the shadowed gap beneath it where the tide came in thin and cold. They crouched there like two people waiting out rain. "You came alone?"

"I came careful."

"That's not what I asked."

"Yes," she said. "Alone."

He studied her. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three years old, with ink worked so deep into the creases of her fingers that no scrubbing would ever get it out — he'd seen that mark on typesetters before, on the old men who'd run the liberal papers back when running a liberal paper got you mocked instead of shot. Her eyes kept going to the water, cataloguing it. Currents, distances, the gunboats' rhythm. She wasn't admiring the view. She was reading it.

"So," Reyes said. "Your father wants the *Mariposa* on a Tuesday night, and a man who doesn't exist to carry whatever it is your father needs carried. That was the message."

"My father can't come to the harbor anymore." Her voice stayed level but he heard the crack run through under it, hairline-thin. "The shop is watched. His health—" She stopped, chose her words like she chose type, one at a time, rejecting most of them. "It may fall to someone else to see the work done."

"Someone else," Reyes repeated.

"Me."

He turned and looked at her fully then. Out on the flats, a heron stood on one leg in the shallows with the patience of something that had outlived every emergency it had ever witnessed.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. "Not the danger. Everyone understands the danger, they just don't believe in it. I mean the arithmetic. A smuggler moves cargo. Cargo doesn't have a face. You're asking me to move a person, and that person is the daughter of the man whose name is on a junta list. If they take you, they take you alive, and everything you know comes out of you eventually. Everything. Including my boats. Including my boys. Including the route I've spent eleven years keeping dry."

"I won't give anything up."

"Everyone says that." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The brave ones say it loudest and break fastest. It's not a question of courage, señorita. It's a question of hours. Nobody holds forever. I don't blame them for it. I'm just asking you to understand that you're not risking your life. You're risking mine, and Esteban's, and the widow Delgado's fish cart, and four other names you'll never hear."

Mirabel Vasquez looked at the mud. When she looked up, something had settled in her face, the way sediment settles in still water.

"Then tell me no," she said. "If the arithmetic says no, say no. But don't dress it up as doubt about whether I'd break. I cut type fourteen hours a day, señor Reyes. I know exactly how much pressure lead takes before it gives. So do you. The question is whether what we're carrying is worth the giving, and that's my father's question to answer, except he's dying, so I'm answering it."

* * *

They sat in the lee of the hulk — the old *Providencia*, condemned twelve years ago and left to rot at her moorings like a pensioner nobody collected. Her hull rose above them, black with tar and barnacle, big as a cathedral and twice as empty. Reyes liked meeting here because no customs launch bothered coming close; the hulk leaked a slow rainbow slick of bilge oil that fouled propellers and tempers alike.

He'd brought bread and hard sausage, because negotiations went better when people ate, and because the girl looked like she hadn't. She refused at first, then took the bread with the stiff grace of someone who'd been hungry long enough to be angry about needing it.

"Aldana," Reyes said, chewing. "Tell me what you actually know about him. Not what your father knows. What you know."

"That he wrote a manifesto. That he asked my father to print it when the time came. That the time came and the junta declared him an enemy of the state before a single sheet was set."

"Do you know what's in it? Word for word?"

"Some. I've read Father's copy."

"Then tell me why. Not the words. Why anyone should bleed for them."

She thought about it. Most people didn't think — they recited, and he could smell a recitation from across a room. But Mirabel Vasquez chewed her bread and stared at the rotten flank of the *Providencia* and thought.

"Because it says the port belongs to the people who work it," she said finally. "That the tariffs strangle the fishermen to feed men who've never baited a hook. That a press is not property, it's a voice, and a voice can't be owned by whoever holds the biggest gun." A pause. "I don't know if it's true. I know it's true that someone is willing to kill to keep it from being said. In my experience, señor Reyes, that's usually evidence."

He laughed — a short, rusty sound that surprised him coming out of his own chest. Eleven years since he'd laughed at anything said under this hulk.

"Evidence," he murmured. "Your father taught you that?"

"My father taught me that ink is cheaper than blood and lasts longer."

"And now his ink is going to cost blood anyway."

"That's the junta's choice, not ours."

Reyes pulled a piece of chalk from his coat and drew a line on the hulk's hull at the tideline. High water. He watched the harbor breathe toward it while he decided.

Here was the thing he did not say: he had known Emilio Vasquez for twenty years. Had printed — well, had arranged the printing of — customs manifests and harbor ledgers that said less than they knew, back when Emilio's shop was young and Reyes's boats were younger. He remembered Mirabel as a girl of eight sitting on a stool amid the type cases, sorting letters by candlelight while her father argued politics with men who later got exiled or worse. He remembered thinking even then: *that child sees everything and says nothing. She'll either inherit the shop or the firing squad.*

Now here she was, offering him both.

"The *Mariposa* sails Tuesday," he said. "High water, half past two in the morning. Whatever your father has ready — plates, papers, the manifesto itself — it goes in the false bottom under the salt barrels, and it goes to a press north of the point that owes me more than I can explain in the time we have. That part I'll do. For your father. For a promise I made him too, though he probably doesn't remember making me make it."

Mirabel's breath caught. He saw her file that away — another debt, another promise, another name in the ledger.

"But," Reyes said, and held up one finger, "there's a price. And it isn't money."

* * *

The tide had turned. Below them, the mudflats began their slow drowning, the channels filling first, the heron lifting off on gray wings as its footing disappeared. Reyes watched the water come in over the place where he'd drawn his chalk line and thought about all the things that rise and erase themselves.

"Name me one other smuggler the junta hasn't bought," he said. "Go on. Think."

"I can't."

"No. You can't, because there isn't one. Every man who runs cargo on this coast has a brother-in-law in the customs house or a debt the junta owns. I stay free because I am worth more to them loose than caught — for now. The day they decide I'm worth more caught, I'm caught. So when I move your manifesto, I'm not moving paper. I'm spending the last coin of my usefulness. Do you understand? I get one trip like this. One."

"I understand."

"Then hear the price." He turned to face her square. "When it's printed — and it will be printed, I'll see to that if it costs me the boats — it goes out under your name. Not your father's."

Her whole body went still. Stillness, he'd learned, was what this family did instead of screaming.

"That makes no sense," she said quietly. "My father made the promise. My father—"

"Your father is a dying man whose name is already on a junta list. Adding his name to the manifesto buys nothing. It's spent currency. The junta took it from him the day they posted it on the customs house." He tapped the hulk's hull beside him, the sound ringing hollow through twelve feet of rotten oak. "But a new name, señorita — a name they haven't priced yet, a name attached to a young woman with ink in her fingers who set every letter herself — that name is a problem they can't solve. You can't hang what's already hanging. Your father's name gets the manifesto confiscated and burned inside a week. Yours gets it read, because they won't know what to do with it. Who is Mirabel Vasquez? Where does she print? By the time they answer those questions, the words are in ten thousand heads, and you can't burn heads. Well." He smiled without humor. "You can. It's just expensive."

"They'll hunt me."

"They'll hunt your father too. They already do. The difference is that he's hunted and finished. You'd be hunted and *working*." He leaned closer. "And there's more, and you'll hate this part more. It's not strategy. It's justice, or something near it. Your father promised Aldana his press would speak. Fine. Honorable. But promises made by fathers on behalf of daughters are how women end up in graves with other men's names carved on them. If you do this, you do it as yourself. Not as Emilio Vasquez's hand. Not as the daughter honoring the dead. As Mirabel. The manifesto says: *I saw this truth and I set it in lead.* Your name. Your risk. Your act." He straightened. "That's my price. Take it or find another boat, and I'll tell you now, there is no other boat."

For a long moment she said nothing. The water climbed the hulk's side, patient as anything in this world. Somewhere across the harbor a gunboat's searchlight swept the flats, and both of them crouched lower in the hulk's shadow without discussing it.

"You want me to stop being my father's daughter," she said at last.

"No." Reyes shook his head slowly. "I want you to stop being invisible. There's a difference, and you of all people should see it — you've spent your whole life setting other men's words in type so the words could stand up and be seen while you stayed bent over the case. This is the same work, señorita. Except this once, the words are yours, and the name at the bottom is yours, and when the soldiers come — and they will come — everyone in Vellamare will know exactly who they're dragging out of the print shop. And knowing it, some of them might follow. Might shout. Might remember. That's what a name does that silence never does." He paused. "Your father gave his word to Aldana. I'm asking you to give yours to yourself. It's a harder promise. It's the only kind that holds."

She looked at her hands. Turned them over, palms up, the way a person examines a tool they've used so long they've forgotten they're holding it. Ink in every crease. Small burns from the molten lead, old and white.

"If I say yes," she said, "and they catch me — my family pays for it. My mother. Tomás."

"Tomás." Reyes let the name hang. He knew the elder son by reputation: the one who drank coffee at the café where junta clerks drank coffee, the one who'd recently started wearing better coats. He filed his own suspicion away and chose, for now, not to spend it. "Every choice costs somebody. That's what the new century is going to be about, I think. Choosing whom to cost."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then go home, and in six months the shop is seized, your father dies in a room that smells of government soap, and Aldana's manifesto gets printed after the war by some committee, with a footnote where his name should be. It happens all the time. History is mostly footnotes." He shrugged. "Or you say yes, and maybe it's the same ending — but you'll have chosen it, and the words will have gone out ahead of you, and footnotes can be fought."

Mirabel stood. Down the flats, the last of the mud islands was going under, and the *Mariposa* rode higher at her mooring, swaying on her chain like something waking up.

"Tuesday," she said. "Half past two. Under the salt barrels."

"That's the boat. Now the terms."

She was quiet a moment longer. Then, in a voice that had stopped being a girl's and become something older — a compositor's voice, measured, each word set and justified before it was spoken:

"The manifesto goes out under my name. Mirabel Vasquez. Set by my hand. I'll cut the type myself if it comes to that." She met his eyes. "But I choose the wording of the colophon. If my name goes on it, the text goes out as my father received it, unaltered — no rebel additions, no Reyes emendations, nothing trimmed for anyone's safety. Truth entire, or no deal."

Truth entire. From a smuggler's arithmetic, that was a terrible term. Whole truths got whole crews hanged.

"Done," Reyes said, before the sensible part of him could object.

She put out her hand — ink-stained, burn-scarred, steady — and he shook it, and the deal was struck there in the shadow of a rotting hulk with the tide rising around them like a slow erasure of everything said before.

"One more thing," he said as she turned to go. "Your father. Does he know you came here tonight?"

She stopped. Didn't turn around.

"He knows I'm becoming someone he can't protect," she said. "I haven't told him the details. Details frighten dying men, and I need his hands steady long enough to teach me where the plates are hidden."

She walked off along the waterline, weight forward, carrying her invisible load, and Reyes watched until the dark took her.

Then he stayed a while longer under the hulk, listening to the water climb the rotten planking above his head, doing the arithmetic he hated. One boat. One trip. One name newly minted and therefore priceless and therefore targeted. A dying printer. An ambitious son with new coats and junta coffee. And somewhere inland, General Aldana's manuscript waiting in its hiding place like a fuse laid years ago.

It occurred to him, with the flat clarity of a man who has survived by noticing, that he had just agreed to help set fire to a city — and that the match was not the manifesto, and not the general, and not himself.

The match was the girl. And she was walking back into a house where someone might already be selling the location of her father's plates.

Reyes pushed off the hulk's hull and headed for the cooperage, already composing the note he'd send Esteban: *Move everything. Trust no one with a family.*

By Tuesday, he suspected, none of it would matter — because by Tuesday, if his instinct about the harbor was right, the plates would already be gone.
