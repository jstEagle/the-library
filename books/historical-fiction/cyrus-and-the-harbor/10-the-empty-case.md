## 10. The Empty Case

She found it in the dark before dawn, the way you find a wound—by reaching for something and feeling nothing where there should have been weight.

Mirabel had come down to the shop at five, as she had every morning since the soldiers came and went and left them breathing. The habit was older than the fear: light the lamp over the composing stone, put the kettle on the back burner, stand in the smell of ink and oil until the day had a shape. Her father would come down within the hour, and they would work in silence until Tomás rose, and the silence was the only part of her life that still belonged entirely to her.

The font case sat where it always sat, third shelf on the left wall, between the case of fourteen-point Caslon and the drawer of orphaned ornaments. It was the broken case—the one her father kept for type too worn, too chipped, too cracked to trust with a customer's page. He called it his cemetery. She had never once seen him lock it.

The lid was not locked now either. That was the first wrong thing.

The second was the sound. A case of lead has a voice when you lift it—a dense, dead murmur, like sand shifting in a sack. When Mirabel pulled the shelf forward and slid her hands under the tray, the wood came up light as a bread box. Her arms, braced for thirty pounds of metal, jerked upward into the shelf above, and a shower of quoins clattered across the floor, and she stood there in the lamplight with an empty case held against her chest like a child's coffin.

She did not scream. Some instinct older than panic pressed her mouth shut and turned her ears toward the ceiling, toward the rooms above where her father slept and Tomás slept—or didn't sleep—and she listened so hard she could hear her own blood.

Tomás's bed had been empty at four. She knew because she had been awake too, staring at the water stain on her ceiling shaped like a closed hand, thinking about Reyes and the harbor and the letter that had named them all. She had heard the street door open and close below her window with that particular care Tomás used when he wanted to be unheard—which meant he wanted to be known to be careful, which was its own confession. She had told herself he was going to the market early. She had told herself a great many things.

The plates had been wrapped in oilcloth. She knew this because she had wrapped them there herself, six nights ago, kneeling on this same floor while her father stood watch at the door with a candle he wasn't supposed to be holding. Fourteen copper plates, each one etched with a column of Aldana's manifesto, each one the exact size of a lie that could hang them. They had gone into the broken case beneath a layer of ruined type, buried in the cemetery, and her father had said, *Nothing is safer than something no one wants,* and she had believed him because wanting to believe him was all she had left of certainty.

Now the cemetery was empty. Someone had even swept it. The ruined type lay in a neat pile on the stone, sorted by size, and that neatness was the thing that made her hands go cold. A soldier would have scattered it. A thief would have taken the good type, the brass, the paper stock. Only one person in the world would leave the worthless letters behind and take the priceless ones, and only one person in the world would tidy up after himself while he did it.

*Tomás.*

She said the name out loud to the empty shop and it sounded like a curse in a language she used to speak fluently.

* * *

Her father came down at half past six, and she watched him understand it in stages, the way a man drowns in shallow water—first confusion, then effort, then the terrible arithmetic.

He was moving slowly these days. Since the search, since the soldiers' boots on his floor and their hands in his drawers, something in him had slowed like a press running out of power. But his hands were still sure. He crossed to the case, lifted it the way he had lifted it ten thousand times in forty years, and felt the emptiness travel up through his arms and into his face.

He set the case down very gently. That was the worst of it—that tenderness. As if gentleness could call the plates back.

"They were here," he said.

"Yes."

"Someone moved them." Not a question. He was buying seconds, walking backward through the night the way she had.

"The case was swept clean. The junk type is stacked by size." She made herself say it plainly, because plainly was all she could manage without breaking. "Papa. Only family comes down here before dawn."

He turned away from her and looked at the press for a long moment—the old Columbian, iron eagle perched on its head, her mother's ghost in every groove of the lever. Then he looked at the stairs, and Mirabel saw him follow the same road she had followed, and arrive at the same door, and stand before it unable to open it.

"No," he said. Quietly. To himself more than to her.

"Papa—"

"He wouldn't." His voice had a crack down the middle of it, like bad type. "He is ambitious, yes. He is foolish with his pride. But he knows what these plates cost. He grew up under this press. He—" And then he stopped, because they both remembered the same thing at the same moment: three days ago, at dinner, Tomás asking casual questions about the shop's debts, about who owned the lease, about whether the junta's new licensing office had been by lately. Casual questions asked in a casual voice by a man who had learned everything he knew about concealment from watching their father set type without reading it.

"Sit down," Mirabel said.

"I need to think."

"Then think sitting down."

But he didn't sit. He began instead to move through the shop in a slow, terrible circuit—checking the good cases, checking the stone, opening the cash drawer and finding it untouched and somehow being wounded by that too, because it confirmed what the empty case already said: *this was not theft; this was trade.* He reached the composing stone last, and there he stopped, and Mirabel saw him look down at the galley where, two nights ago, they had set and torn apart and reset the first lines of the manifesto in secret rehearsal, practicing the lockup they would run when the plates came out of hiding. His hand went out toward the forme and stopped in the air, hovering, as if the metal itself might burn him now—as if everything his hands had ever touched had become evidence.

"Aldana trusted me," he said.

"I know."

"Not Aldana. Aldana is a general; generals are careful. *His messenger.* Reyes put those plates in my hands and shook them and said, *the word dies with the man unless the man has a friend with a press.* And I said—" His hand closed around nothing. "I said, *I am your friend with a press.* Forty years I have been careful. Forty years, and one sentence in peacetime, and now the sentence comes due."

"Then we collect on it ourselves. We go to Reyes today. We tell him the plates are lost and ask what can be salvaged. There may be proofs—there may be copy—"

"There is always copy." He said it with contempt, and she understood: to a printer, *copy* was the corpse. Anyone could set words. The plates were the promise made solid, the general's own text corrected in the general's own hand, the artifact that could never be denied or counterfeited. Without the plates, the manifesto became just another pamphlet. Worse—with the plates in junta hands, it could become worse than a pamphlet. It could become its own mockery, printed in his typeface, wearing his reputation like a stolen coat.

And that, she saw, was the thought that finally felled him. Not the danger. The desecration.

"Papa, sit down. Please."

He waved her off, one more turn around the shop, and then he did not so much wave as falter. His hand went to the frame of the press—the eagle's pedestal—and gripped it, and Mirabel watched the color leave his face from the edges inward, like ink retreating from a washed plate. He made a small sound. Not a cry. A sound of objection, almost—an argument cut off mid-sentence.

She caught him before his head hit the stone. Barely. His weight took them both down against the press bed, and she lowered him onto the cold iron among the scattered quoins, and his breath came in short, dragging pulls, and his left hand clawed at his chest as though the pain there were a misprint he could pull out and crumple.

"Papa. Papa, look at me."

His eyes found hers. In them was fear, yes—but underneath the fear was something else, something she recognized with a jolt, because she had seen it in the mirror every morning for weeks: the arithmetic. Even now, even with his heart failing him on the floor of his own shop, he was calculating. His lips moved.

"The case," he whispered. "Lock the case. If he comes back—if it was not—" Cough. "If it was not him, then someone must be shown the case is full. Lock it. Make them believe—"

"Stop. Stop talking."

"Mirabel." His grip found her wrist, and for a man drowning he was strong, and his eyes were furious and wet at once. "Whatever is in you when I am gone—do not let it be hate. Hate sets crooked. You cannot lock up a forme of hate. The page comes out crooked. Every time." His breath dragged again, longer this time, thinner. "You hear me?"

"You're not going anywhere. Do you hear me? You are not—"

"Tomás." The name came out of him like a stone he'd swallowed years ago. "Find out what he was paid. That is all. Don't hate him for the price. Hate is—" But the sentence never landed. His eyes rolled, his grip loosened, and his body arched once against the press bed and then settled into a heaviness that frightened her more than any struggle could have.

She screamed for Tomás then. She screamed his name up through the floorboards of the house he had emptied of honor in the night, and she screamed it into the street through the front door, and Mrs. Ferro from the chandlery came running in her apron, and the boy who swept the corner came running, and somewhere in the shouting a doctor was fetched, and none of it mattered, because by the time anyone arrived, her father's breathing had slowed to the pace of a hand-press worked by a tired man—one pull, a long pause, another pull—and then, between one pull and the next, nothing.

* * *

They laid him out on the counter where he had counted out change for forty years, because there was nowhere else, and Mirabel sat beside him holding his hand while the doctor did things she didn't watch and Mrs. Ferro wept into her apron and said the Virgin's names in a loop.

The doctor said heart. He said it kindly, with the softness men use for verdicts: *his heart, señora, the strain of these months, it was an accident of the flesh.* And Mirabel nodded and thanked him and did not argue, because the flesh had failed, that was true.

But she knew what had loaded the gun.

It was past noon when the street door opened and Tomás came in carrying a string bag of oranges—he had actually bought oranges, she would think later, he had stopped at the market on his way home from selling them all—and she watched his face perform the whole tragedy in sequence: the smile dying, the question forming, the answer arriving in the shape of a sheet over a body on a counter, and the genuine horror underneath, which was the cruelest part. He had not expected this. He had sold their father's honor and expected to collect it back later, intact, like a book borrowed from a library.

"Papá—" His voice broke on it, honestly broke, and he crossed the room fast and gripped the dead man's shoulder as if he could shake him back. "What happened? What happened, Mira?"

"What happened." She heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "The plates are gone, Tomás. Out of the broken case. Swept clean after. Sorted by size. So I know it wasn't a stranger, because strangers don't tidy."

The color left his face exactly the way it had left their father's, edge inward, and for one moment—one raw, unguarded moment—she saw the boy who used to ink rollers for her, who once took a beating from a customs officer rather than name the man who'd sold them smuggled paper. Then the moment sealed over, and the man underneath started talking, fast and low, the way he'd been talking to junta men for weeks.

"Mira, listen to me. Listen. Whatever you think—"

"I think you took them. I think you traded them. I think you walked to the propaganda office this morning with our father's soul in your bag and sold it, and you bought oranges on the way home."

"It isn't like that." His hands were shaking. Oranges rolled from the burst string bag across the floor, bright and absurd, stopping against the legs of the dead. "The commission—it was for protection, do you understand? They were going to take everything anyway. The shop, the house, all of us. I found the one door where some of us walk through. I was going to fix it. I was going to buy the plates back—"

"With what? With your commission? With the salary they pay the men who burn print shops?"

"Lower your voice."

"*Our father is on the counter.*"

For a moment neither of them could speak. Mrs. Ferro had withdrawn to the doorway with the tact of the terrified. Somewhere outside, a patrol drummed past, unhurried, master of everything.

Tomás crouched to gather the oranges, mechanically, one by one, and it was such a small, stupid, human gesture that it nearly undid her. She wanted to hit him. She wanted to grab his collar and scream the arithmetic into his face: *he died of you; the shock died in his heart but you loaded it.* Instead she stood very still, because her father's last coherent instruction had been about hate, about crooked pages, and she was not yet sure she could obey it, and saying cruel things aloud would make the hating real.

"Three days," Tomás said quietly, not looking up. "That's what I asked them for. Three days to confirm the location, and then the transfer, and then the licenses, and the shop stays Vasquez. It stays ours, Mira. Everything I've done, I've done so that something of this family survives."

"Something of this family survives." She looked at her father's face, already settling into the peace Tomás had stolen from him. "Do you hear yourself? Do you even hear it anymore?"

He stood, oranges clutched to his chest like a child's toys, and his eyes were wet and defiant at once, and she realized with a cold turning in her stomach that he believed his own story completely. That was what made him dangerous. Liars could be caught. Believers simply kept walking.

"Funeral tomorrow," he said. "Small. Quiet. I'll arrange it."

"You'll arrange it." She laughed, and the laugh frightened her with how much of her father's contempt was in it. "Get out of this room, Tomás. Get out of this room or I will say things in front of Papá that cannot be unsaid."

He went. At the door he paused, and she thought—for one heartbeat—that he might confess, might put the plates back on the counter beside the body, might be her brother again. He only said, "The doctor's bill—I'll cover it," and was gone.

* * *

She sat with her father until the light changed. The shop filled with dusk the way a chase fills with ink, evenly, everywhere at once, and she watched the shapes of the presses and cases go gray and then black, and she did not cry. There was something beyond crying, a country past the border of tears, and she had crossed into it sometime between the empty case and the oranges, and now she was simply taking inventory.

Because grief, in a printer's family, is inventory. She counted what remained: one Columbian press. Eleven serviceable cases of type. One ream of good rag paper, hidden under the floorboards where the soldiers hadn't thought to look, because paper is lighter than guilt. No plates. No father. One brother, priced and purchased, sleeping upstairs in a house that would belong to the junta within the month if she did nothing.

And one name. *Vasquez.* Now a hunted name, a poisoned name, a name the rebels would curse and the junta would use and her father had carried for forty years without a stain until this week. She thought of Reyes's rough voice at the harbor: *names are cargo too, niña, and yours is marked.*

Her hand found her father's again, cooling now, and she bent close and spoke into what remained of him, quietly, the way you speak into a forme before pulling the first proof—testing the impression.

"I'm going to finish it," she whispered. "Not for the promise anymore. For the pages coming out straight. You'd understand that."

She straightened his collar. She folded his hands over the sheet that covered him, arranging them the way he arranged fresh type—resting, ready, waiting to speak.

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the dark of the shop that had been her whole world and was now a crime scene and a inheritance and a wound, Mirabel Vasquez picked up her shawl and walked out the door toward the harbor, toward low tide, toward the only man in Vellamare who might still be crazy enough to help a girl with no plates, no father, and no name worth anything except the one she was about to make.

Behind her, upstairs, Tomás's lamp burned late.
