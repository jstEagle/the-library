## 11. What the Junta Prints

The plates rode in a fish crate, packed in sawdust like herring, and Tomás carried them the last four blocks on his shoulder because the driver refused to go further than the corner. The streets near the customs house had that emptied look they got when soldiers were about—shutters drawn, a dog trotting purposeful and alone, two boys pressed into a doorway watching him pass with the flat attention of children who had already learned what cargo mattered.

He had told himself the whole way across town: *this is protection*. He had dressed the sentence so many times it had gone soft at the seams, the way his father's old coat had, the way all lies went soft when you wore them daily. *This is what a son does when the father is too proud to live.* His father, who burned nothing and said less. His father, who would have let the whole family swing from the plaza gantries for the sake of a promise made to a man in peacetime, before Aldana was a price on a poster, before the word *manifesto* could get a woman shot in her own doorway.

His father's honor was going to kill them all. Tomás was simply removing the fuse.

That was the version he carried up the customs house steps. The crate grew heavier with every stair, or he grew weaker; he couldn't say which, and didn't want to examine too closely, because examination was the one thing he could not afford. A man who examined his own reasons the way Mirabel examined type—face down, magnifier out, hunting the flaw—would put the crate down in the street and walk away. So he did not examine. He climbed.

A lieutenant with a clipboard met him in the vestibule. That was the detail Tomás would remember afterward, more than the rifles, more than the smell of ink and gun oil in the corridors: the clipboard. Corruption had come for him politely, weeks ago, over brandy and paperwork, and now it received his betrayal the same way—a man checking boxes.

"Vasquez."

"Tomás Vasquez." He set the crate down. "You'll want to verify them before I sign anything."

The lieutenant raised an eyebrow, faintly amused. "You've done this before."

"No," Tomás said. "I've watched my father do the honest version. It looks different."

They opened the crate together. Sawdust, then cloth, then the plates—the manifesto, General Aldana's voice cut into zinc, forty pounds of sedition that had slept in a font case of broken type while soldiers stood three feet from it and saw only a dead letterpress's leavings. Tomás looked at them and felt nothing, he told himself. Felt the particular nothing of a man who has amputated something and is still under the influence of the decision.

The lieutenant ran a thumb along the raised metal, reading without reading. "The propaganda office will want these set by tonight."

"Then I'll walk them down myself," Tomás said. "If anyone stops that crate in a corridor, I'd rather be the one holding it."

*Useful*, said the lieutenant's face. *And aware of his usefulness.* Both were true, and both were the currency, and Tomás had decided some time ago that he would rather trade in currency than in promises. Promises were his father's religion, and look where the church had gotten them: named in a sealed letter, hunted by a junta, whispered about by rebels who wanted the plates destroyed as much as the colonels did. Between two guns, you chose which direction to kneel. He had chosen. The kneeling was the part no one wrote poems about.

* * *

The junta's print works occupied a former tobacco warehouse behind the customs annex, and it was running when they brought the plates in—not the rhythm Tomás knew, not the heartbeat of a shop, but something louder and dumber. Two flatbed presses, army surplus, driven hard. Compositors who moved like clerks. And the smell, God help him, the smell was exactly right: ink and solvent and the warm mineral breath of lead. It was his childhood, weaponized. He stood in the doorway a moment longer than a man with nothing to feel should have stood.

Commissioner Ossorio was waiting by the stone. Not the lieutenant's level, this one—Ossorio wore civilian clothes cut like a uniform and had the habit of looking at your hands when you spoke, as though words came from somewhere lower and less trustworthy.

"The famous Vasquez hands," Ossorio said. "Set them out."

Tomás set the plates on the composing stone. "As delivered. Unaltered."

"We'll alter them plenty." Ossorio turned one plate toward the gaslight, studying the mirror-writing of it. "Your father's work?"

"His cutting. Someone else's text."

"Aldana's."

"Aldana's."

"And now it will be Aldana's," Ossorio said pleasantly, "with certain revisions."

Tomás had known. Of course he had known—he had sat through the second meeting, the one with brandy, where the word *amendment* had been used like a knife being oiled. But knowing was abstract. Knowing was arithmetic. This was Ossorio calling over a compositor and saying, in the bored tone of a man ordering lunch: *Strike new plates from these. Same face, same measure. We change the third paragraph and the close. The general calls for negotiations. The general calls for laying down arms. The general begs his brothers in the mountains to trust the mercy of the government.*

"That isn't a revision," Tomás heard himself say. "That's forgery of a living man's voice."

"It's printing," Ossoria said—or Ossorio; the name blurred for a moment, the way names did when blood left the head—"which your family claims to know something about. Everything a press says is someone's voice wearing someone else's clothes, señor Vasquez. We are merely changing the tailoring." He smiled without warmth. "Besides. Aldana is dead to the state. Dead men consent to whatever we quote."

The compositor began to work, and Tomás made the mistake of watching.

It was his father's typeface. That was the thing he had not prepared for. Not just the plates—the *hand* in them, the slight forward lean of the serifs, the deep-cut counters his father favored because they printed clean on cheap paper, a compromise the old man had made twenty years ago for the fishermen's broadsheets and never abandoned. Every letterform on that zinc had been cut by a man who believed that poor readers deserved beautiful letters. Tomás had grown up falling asleep to the sound of that belief: the press thumping below his bedroom floor like a second heart.

Now a stranger's fingers were setting lies against it. Worse—casting new type to match it, drawing letters in wax and acid until the forgery was seamless, until no compositor in Vellamare could say where his father's hand stopped and the state's began. Paragraph three vanished. The close became a surrender. Aldana's defiance, cut in Vasquez zinc, would go out to the city tomorrow morning begging the rebels to disarm, and every man who read it would see the old man's serifs leaning forward like a bow.

*Necessary*, Tomás told himself. *They'd have printed the forgery anyway. At least this way the family's name survives to see spring.* The thought had a familiar shape, worn smooth. He kept turning it anyway, the way you keep touching a tooth that's gone bad, because the pain at least confirmed there was still a nerve.

"You'll want to see the first pull," Ossorio said. It wasn't a question. It was a lesson, and the lesson was *witness*: the junta liked its collaborators to watch, to be complicit in the room where the air changed, because a man who had seen was a man who could never again claim he hadn't.

"I don't need to," Tomás said.

"But you will," Ossorio said, "because you're a printer's son."

* * *

The first proof came off the press at half past ten, and the boy who pulled it laid it on the stone with the reverence the ignorant give machinery, and Tomás leaned over it under the gaslight and read his own doom in his father's letterforms.

*The General calls upon his brothers to lay down their arms.*

Same lean. Same deep counters. On cheap paper the lines printed slightly soft at the edges, exactly the way his father's work printed soft, and Tomás understood with a physical lurch that the forgery was perfect—that somewhere in this building was the mold, the matrix, the whole terrible competence of the state applied to one old man's conscience—and that from tomorrow morning, when people in Vellamare saw Vasquez type, they would see the government's mercy and the government's lies.

His father's name would survive. That had been the bargain. His father's *name*.

But a name is not a hand, and a hand is not a heart, and Tomás stood in the ink-smelling dark listening to the flatbeds hammer and understood, slowly, in the way of men who insist on arriving late to their own executions, that he had not sold the plates. He had sold the shop's soul while keeping its signboard. Anyone who had ever been touched by his father's press—the fishermen, the dockers' mutual aid society, the funeral notices set free of charge for widows—every one of them would read tomorrow's sheet and feel betrayed by a dead man's own letters. The junta hadn't bought zinc. They'd bought a witness and made him testify against himself.

"Necessary," he said aloud, and the word came out wrong in the pressroom din, small and tinny, and the compositor nearest him glanced up with the blank courtesy of a man used to strangers talking to themselves beside the stone.

"There's a matter of the payment schedule," Ossorio said, appearing at his elbow with papers. "The commission effective the first of the month. The deed transfer for the premises on the Calle—what is it—"

"Calle del Plomo."

"Lead Street," Ossorio said, and smiled at the coincidence. "Fitting. The deed requires your signature as surviving interested party. Your father being—" he consulted nothing, simply paused—"indisposed."

"He's alive."

"For now, the paperwork reflects the future. Sign where indicated."

Tomás signed. His signature came out steady, which surprised him; he had expected a tremor, had almost wanted one, some evidence in the record that the man signing had been dragged. But the pen moved the way his father had taught him to move a brayer—smooth, even pressure, commit to the stroke—and the line it left was confident and ugly and entirely his.

Ossorio gathered the paper, checked the signature against something, and then, as an afterthought that was not an afterthought: "There remains the question of the girl."

"Mirabel has no part in this. She cuts type. She's a girl with ink under her nails."

"She cuts type," Ossorio agreed. "Which means she knows type. Which means when the counterfeit appears tomorrow, she'll be one of perhaps fifty people in this city capable of identifying it as a fake within a minute of picking it up. Fifty people, señor Vasquez, and most of them are already accounted for." He folded the papers away. "Your sister will look at our manifesto and see her father's face on a lie. People do unpredictable things when they see their fathers' faces on lies. It would be a kindness—an administrative kindness—if she were... located. Brought in. Given the opportunity to understand the new arrangements the way you have."

"She's not a threat."

"No one is a threat," Ossorio said mildly. "Until the census says otherwise. We'll need her description. For the file."

The form slid across the stone. Name, age, height, distinguishing marks. Tomás looked at it for a long moment—at the little ruled box where a brother's knowledge was to be entered like type into a chase, locked in, ready to print—and he thought about Mirabel at the dinner table two nights ago, not eating, watching him with those eyes that examined everything the way she examined type, face down, magnifier out. She already suspected. She would suspect more tomorrow.

He picked up the pencil.

He wrote her name. Her age. Her height, which he knew from measuring himself against the doorframe marks their father had pencilled when she was nine. Under *distinguishing marks* his hand stopped, and hovered, and then he wrote: *None.*

It was true. It was also the smallest treachery available to him, and he took it, and hated that taking it felt like virtue.

"For the file," Ossorio said, satisfied, and Tomás walked out of the tobacco warehouse into the cold harbor wind carrying his commission and his deed and his sister's description, all of it lighter than the fish crate had been, and somehow heavier by tons.

* * *

The house on Lead Street was dark when he got home, except for the kitchen. He stood in the alley a while, in the smell of tar and salt, looking at that lit window, unable to go in.

Through the glass he could see his mother's silhouette at the stove—moving less these days, moving like a woman rehearsing widowhood—and beside it another figure bent over the table, working at something small with both hands. Mirabel. Even from the alley he knew the posture: shoulders low, head angled, the concentration of a person cutting type or mending it, doing fine work late because fine work was how she prayed. Their father's chair stood empty by the fire. Their father had not come down in two days.

Tomorrow morning the city would wake to Aldana's surrender in Vasquez type. Tomorrow morning Mirabel would pick up a sheet—one of the boys would bring one home, or it would be pasted to the customs house wall where everyone passed—and she would hold it to the light, and within a minute she would know, absolutely and with a craftsman's certainty, that the letters were her father's and the words were a lie, and she would know, a minute after that, who had delivered the plates.

She would not accuse him loudly. That wasn't Mirabel. She would go quiet, quieter, until the quiet was a wall he couldn't climb, and she would start doing things he couldn't see, going places he couldn't follow, and the house would become a border between them with checkpoints on both sides.

He could warn her. The thought arrived plainly, the way honest thoughts do when it's too late for them to be useful. He could tell her tonight, at the table, before the presses ran: *they've forged it, they're going to flood the city with his voice saying what he never said, and I did that, and I told myself it was to save us, and I no longer know if the us includes me.* He could put it down like a confession and let her weigh it.

But warning her meant warning her against Ossorio's office, and Ossorio had her description in a file now, and a warned Mirabel was a moving Mirabel, and a moving Mirabel was a loose end, and he had seen, tonight, with his own eyes, what the junta did with loose ends: they set them in type.

Inside, the kettle whistled. His mother's silhouette reached for it. Mirabel's silhouette did not look up from her work.

Tomás stayed in the alley a minute longer, ash collecting on his coat, telling himself the last story left in his inventory: that there was still time, that the century had days left in it, that anything could yet be unsaid, unprinted, unwound.

Then the light in the kitchen went out, and the window went dark, and the house looked like every other house on the street, and he understood that whatever he had sold today, the receipt would arrive tomorrow morning, folded inside a newspaper, set in his father's hand.
