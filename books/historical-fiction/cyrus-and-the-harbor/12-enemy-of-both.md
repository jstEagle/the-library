## 12. Enemy of Both

The counterfeit appeared on a Tuesday morning, pasted to the door of the Chapel of San Telmo where the fishermen left their offerings.

Mirabel saw it before she understood it. She was carrying a parcel of type metal wrapped in oilcloth, taking the long way to the shop because the long way passed fewer soldiers, and the broadsheet caught her eye the way printed things always did—her gaze snagging on the layout before the words could arrange themselves into meaning. Two columns. A heavy rule at the top. A familiar arrangement of white space.

Then the words arranged themselves, and she stopped walking.

*Manifesto of the Free Coast*, the headline read, in a typeface she knew the way you know your own handwriting. The Aldana text—the true one, the one that had lived folded inside her father's coat for eleven years—was there in substance, but wrong in every particular. Where the original called for free assembly, this version called for the burning of the customs house. Where it named the junta's officers as men who had forgotten their oaths, this version named them for the gallows by name, rank, and street address. Where her father had promised to print *a voice for the silenced*, this thing was a scream, ugly and inciting, the kind of document that gave soldiers an excuse and rebels a martyr and ordinary people no place to stand at all.

And at the bottom, set small and formal as a colophon: *Printed by order of the Vasquez Press, Vellamare.*

She stood on the chapel steps until a woman behind her cleared her throat, and then she walked on, fast, her hands cold around the parcel of lead.

They hadn't just stolen the plates. They had used them. Her father's typeface—his pride, the design he'd cut himself as an apprentice and refined across forty years, the slight forward lean of his capitals that printers three provinces away could recognize at a glance—had been pulled out of its case and made to speak lies with his family's name bolted underneath like a signature.

Tomás. It had to be Tomás. He had carried the plates to the propaganda office on Calle Herreros, and instead of melting them down or locking them in an evidence drawer, some clerk with a sense of humor—or a sense of strategy—had run a press night with them. Why waste good lead?

And now every wall in Vellamare would carry the Vasquez name above words that could get a man shot.

* * *

By noon she understood how completely the city had turned.

She heard it first in the market, standing in the shadow of the cheese stall while old Ferro argued prices over his shoulder at her. Two women ahead of her in the line were talking in the low, quick voices of people sharing news they shouldn't.

"—Vasquez. The printer. Can you imagine? All those years acting so respectable."

"My mother bought her wedding invitations from that shop."

"Well. Now we know what they were really printing."

Mirabel bought her cheese and said nothing. That was the discipline her father had taught her without ever naming it: the printer is in every room and of no room. You listen. You are furniture. She had been furniture her whole life, and she had thought of it as safety.

In the afternoon the denunciation came, and it came from the other direction.

She was two streets from home when the boys ran past with chalk, scrawling on the walls ahead of the evening patrol rounds. She expected the usual—junta slogans, crude caricatures of Colonel Madera with his mustaches exaggerated into tusks. Instead they wrote, in tall angry letters:

*VASQUEZ LIES IN THE JUNTA'S INK. ALDANA'S TRUE WORD IS STOLEN. DEATH TO THE FALSE PRESS.*

The rebellion had issued its own statement by sundown, copied hand to hand on scraps of paper passed along the fish carts: the manifesto bearing the Vasquez colophon was a forgery, yes—but a forgery produced from plates the Vasquez family had surrendered, which proved the family had possessed them all along, which proved the family had conspired with Aldana and then sold him out when the wind changed. Traitors to the crown first, traitors to the revolution second. There was no version of the story in which the name Vasquez meant anything but danger.

Her father sat at the kitchen table when she got home, the counterfeit spread before him under the lamp. He had not lit the stove. He was holding a magnifying glass over the bottom rule of the colophon, studying the impression in the paper the way he studied proofs, and Mirabel realized with a lurch that he was looking for flaws—that he was doing the only thing his hands knew how to do with grief, which was to examine it for defects.

"They didn't even clean the plates," he said quietly. "Look. Here, and here. Ghosting from the last legitimate job, the hymnals for Santa Cruz. Any compositor could see this sheet wasn't set fresh." He looked up at her, and his eyes were terrible. "It doesn't matter. Nobody will look that closely. Nobody wants to look closely at a thing that tells them what to be afraid of."

"Papa—"

"Forty years," he said. "Forty years I kept this press honest. Not brave—I was never brave, Mira, let us be honest with each other at least once before the end—but honest. I refused jobs. I lost contracts. I printed wedding invitations for men I knew were monsters because refusing would have closed the shop and opened nothing. And in forty years no one could point to a single sheet from my cases and call it a lie." He put the magnifying glass down very carefully, as if it might break, though it was already broken somewhere inside him. "Now the name is a lie. The name was all I had."

There was a knock at the door—not the heavy boot-knock of soldiers, which they had all learned by heart, but something softer, furtive. Her father went still. Mirabel went to the shutter and looked through the slit.

A boy from the harbor quarter, one of Reyes's runners, cap pulled low. He didn't look up at the window. He scratched twice at the door and walked on, and wedged in the jamb was a scrap of paper no bigger than a prayer card.

She waited a full minute before opening it. The message was four words, in pencil, in a hand she was learning to know:

*Both sides hunt you. Harbor.*

* * *

The next morning brought the second search.

This time there was no pretense of procedure. Six men came through the front of the shop while two more took the alley door, and the sergeant—a different man than the one from weeks before, younger, hungrier—did not ask for the proprietor. He asked for *the Vasquezes*, plural, as though the name itself were a contraband object he'd been sent to collect.

"Where is the son?" he wanted to know.

"Gone," Mirabel said, which was true in every way she couldn't afford to explain.

"And the father?"

"In bed. He's ill."

"Everyone in this city is ill," the sergeant said, without interest, and put his boot through the spine of the binding press.

They worked faster this time, and worse. They were not searching anymore; she could see that within minutes. They were inventorying. One man went down the type cases with a slate and chalk, tallying fonts like stock in a warehouse to be seized. Another pulled drawers and let the contents stay where they fell. When her father appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt, gray-faced and swaying, the sergeant looked at him the way a buyer looks at livestock too old for the yard.

"The propaganda office will post seizure notice by Friday," he told Mirabel, almost courteously. "The premises pass to state administration. If any Vasquez is found on these premises after Friday, the notice becomes an arrest warrant. Do you understand the difference between those papers, girl?"

"Yes," she said.

"Do you want me to explain it anyway?" He smiled, and it was the smile of a man enjoying his own sentence. "The warrant has your names spelled correctly."

Her father reached for the doorframe and missed. Mirabel caught his arm, and the contact steadied them both in different ways—he leaned on her, and she remembered that she had legs, and that legs were for walking.

* * *

She did not decide to flee. Decisions, she was learning, were luxuries described afterward by people who survived. What happened was simpler: she packed a satchel because her hands needed occupation—type sticks, a composing stone small enough to hide under a skirt hem, the galley proof of the true manifesto that she had slept with under her mattress since the letter came—and then she stood in the middle of the ruined shop and understood that she was not packing *for later*. She was leaving.

Her father would not come. She knew it before she asked, and she asked anyway, kneeling beside his bed in the failing light.

"The shop—" he began.

"Is gone on Friday. Papa. It's gone."

"Then I'll be here when it goes." His hand found hers, and his grip was still strong; it was everything else that had failed. "A man should stand in his own doorway, Mira. Even if the door belongs to someone else now. Especially then." He turned her hand over and pressed something into it: the small brass key to the font case, the one with the false bottom that had hidden the plates for eleven years and failed at last. "I promised Aldana my press would speak. I'm too old and too slow to make it speak myself. So I am demoting myself, do you see? From printer to keeper of keys." He almost smiled. "Go to the harbor. Find the smuggler. And Mirabel—" He waited until she met his eyes. "Whatever happens, don't let them print lies in your name. Sell the shop. Lose the shop. Burn it yourself if you have to. But the name comes from you now. Spend it carefully."

She kissed his forehead, and his skin tasted of ink and salt, and she went out through the alley with the satchel under her coat before she could find anything to say, because there was nothing, and because anything she said would have been a promise she might not live to keep, and she had learned this month what promises cost when the promiser dies.

* * *

The city at dusk was a lesson in exile.

She took the routes she knew best and found them changed. The bakery on Calle Luna where she'd bought bread every morning of her life had a new boy behind the counter, and when she stepped inside he looked at her face, then at the chalk marks on the wall outside—she hadn't noticed them, a red V smeared by rain—and his hand moved below the counter to something she couldn't see.

"We're closed," he said.

"It's ten past noon."

"We're closed."

She left. She didn't run. Running was a confession, and besides, her legs had gone strange, weightless, as though the ground had stopped meaning anything.

That was the shape of it, she thought, walking. Not expulsion. *Dissolution.* The junta had taken her future—Friday's notice. The rebels had taken her past—the denunciations, the chalked name, the story now told about her family in markets and chapels and kitchens, a story with no author and therefore no answer. Between the stolen past and the confiscated future lay only the present, narrowed to the width of the streets she could still walk, shrinking hour by hour. She was becoming a rumor in her own city. A name on walls. A shape that doors closed against.

She passed the church where she'd been baptized. She passed the schoolroom where Tomás had once won a prize for recitation and she had clapped until her palms stung. She passed the window of Duarte & Sons, Booksellers, where her father's framed trade license had hung in the glass since before she was born—it was gone now, and the empty square of unfaded paper behind it looked like a missing tooth.

At the corner of the fish market a group of dockworkers stood around a copy of the counterfeit manifesto, reading it aloud in the mocking cadence of men performing outrage for each other, and one of them said, "Vasquez," and spat, and the others laughed, and Mirabel walked past them close enough to touch, invisible again, invisible at last, and discovered that invisibility was not safety after all. Invisibility was just being nowhere, everywhere you went.

The harbor opened before her at full dark.

She came down the ramp between the salt sheds with the wind off the water pushing at her back like a hand, impatient, and the smell of tar and brine hit her the way it always did, but differently tonight. Tonight the harbor was not a place she visited. It was the only direction left.

Lamps burned on the fishing boats. Somewhere beyond the breakwater a gunboat's searchlight swept slow arcs across the water, patient as a lighthouse with bad intentions. The condemned hulk rode at anchor in the roadstead, black against blacker water, and she found her eyes going to it without deciding to.

Reyes's runner materialized out of the shadow of the net racks the way boys in this quarter did, as if darkness were a door they knew the latch of.

"You got the message," he said. Statement, not question.

"I got the message."

"Don't stand in the open." He glanced up the ramp, toward the city, toward everything she had just finished losing. "He's aboard the *Perla*. Third boat past the customs buoy. He said—" The boy hesitated, clearly weighing whether the message was his to soften. "He said, tell her the tide turns at four, and so does everything else, if she's ready to pull."

"He said that?"

"He says a lot of things." The boy shrugged. "Most of them come true, which is worse."

Mirabel looked back once, up the ramp, at the smudge of lights that was Vellamare—the city where she had cut her first letter at six years old, where her father's press still smelled of oil and lead shavings, where her brother was presumably sleeping well in whatever bed his bargain had bought him. She searched herself for grief and found it, but underneath the grief was something harder and more useful, something with edges.

They had taken her name and spent it for her—in the junta's ink, in the rebels' chalk, in the spit of strangers at a market corner. Fine. Then the name was worthless, and a worthless name cost nothing to spend, and she had a satchel full of type and four hours until the tide turned.

She pulled her collar up and went down the pier toward the third boat past the customs buoy, and behind her the searchlight swept the empty water where she had been standing, and found no one.
