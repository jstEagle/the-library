## 13. The Last Free Press

Reyes had spent twenty years learning which boats in Vellamare harbor were worth stealing and which were worth mourning, and the *Santa Elena* was both.

She lay at the far end of the breakwater moorings, past the customs cutters and the fish-processing scows, tied up among the condemned hulls that the port authority kept meaning to break up for scrap but never did, because paperwork in this country moved slower than rot. She was a coastal freighter once, ninety feet of iron, her flanks streaked orange with rust that bled down into the water like old wounds. Her masts had been stepped. Her name was half gone, the *Elena* worn away by salt until only *Santa* remained, which Reyes had always found funny in a way he never explained to anyone.

He rowed out at dusk, when the harbor police changed shifts and the customs men ate their supper with their boots off. Mirabel sat in the stern of his skiff with her arms wrapped around her knees, making herself small the way she'd been doing for six days now, ever since the rebels put her family's name on their own list of traitors and the junta's men put it on theirs. Six days of warehouses and fish cellars and one bad night under the pier among the pilings, listening to the tide come in like a slow argument.

"You could have told me where we were going," she said.

"I'm telling you now."

"You could have told me an hour ago."

"An hour ago I wasn't sure yet." He shipped the oars and let the skiff drift the last few meters, bumping gently against the *Santa Elena*'s hull. "I don't lie to people whose family has been shot at. I just make them wait until I'm certain."

"Certain of what?"

"That you're worth the risk." He caught the rusted ladder-rungs and held the skiff steady. "After you."

* * *

Below her waterline the hulk was flooded to the bilges, but above it she still had bones—a hold running most of her length, a deck house, a companionway whose hatch Reyes unlocked with a key he wore on a cord around his neck. Inside it was dark and smelled of iron and tar and the particular mustiness of things that have been left alone a long time by everyone except the rats.

He lit the lamp he'd left hanging by the door, turned the wick low, and watched Mirabel's face as the light spread.

There, against the forward bulkhead, bolted to the deck through iron plates so it couldn't have been moved with ten men and a winch, stood a press.

It was a hand-press—an old Columbian, cast in some northern foundry forty years ago and shipped south when nobody in Vellamare could afford such things new. The eagle finial on its lever was green with verdigris. Rust had climbed the frame in orange rivers, and someone had hung a fishing net over the platen at some point, and there was a gull's nest in the ink drum's housing, long abandoned, the feathers still there, gray and soft as ash.

But the platen was sound. Reyes knew because he'd checked, twice a year for eleven years, oiled it, worked the screw, kept the leather bed wrapped in waxed canvas against the damp. It would pull an impression tonight if it had to.

Mirabel walked toward it slowly, the way you'd approach something sleeping that you didn't want to wake. She put her hand flat on the frame, and Reyes saw her fingers reading the metal the way they'd read type all her life—finding the seams, the bolts, the wear points—taking its inventory without knowing she was doing it.

"This is Aldana's," she said.

"It was." Reyes leaned in the companionway, arms crossed, keeping his voice down out of habit though there was no one within four hundred meters of water. "Eleven years ago, before the colonels, before any of this. There was a reform party. You won't remember it; your father might have told you. They had a newspaper, *El Pueblo*, printed out of a shop on Calle Ferro until the first crackdown shut it down. This press belonged to them. When the soldiers came for the shop, six men carried it down to the water at night and loaded it onto a fish barge, and it came out here, and everyone who knew about it swore an oath and then got arrested anyway, one by one, over the years, until the list of people who knew was short enough to fit inside a matchbox."

"And you're the matchbox."

"I'm what's left." He shrugged. "Aldana knew. That's why his messenger was me, when he wanted your father's shop. Not because I can smuggle—anyone can smuggle, señorita, the harbor is full of smugglers. Because I know where this is."

Mirabel didn't answer right away. She had moved around the press, trailing her hand along it, and now she knelt and peeled back a corner of the waxed canvas from the bed, looking at the smooth steel underneath, and Reyes understood he was watching a woman fall in love with a machine, which was a thing he'd seen once or twice among sailors and their boats and had never expected to see here, in a drowned ship, in the worst year of the century.

"It's a relic," she said finally. "The platen alone must weigh—" She stopped. Recalculated. "It doesn't matter what it weighs. Where are the letters? There's no type. No chase, no furniture, no—"

"Type we'll find. Half the shuttered shops in this city still have their cases, and not every printer loves the junta; they're just scared, and fear is a currency like anything else. Chase and furniture I can get from a scrapyard, if the scrapyard man's price is right, and his price is always right because his brother hates the colonels too. Everyone's brother hates the colonels, señora, that is the one thing this country still manufactures in quantity."

"But no plates." She said it flatly, kneeling there with her palm still on the bed. "You know that. Whatever my father hid in the shop, Tomás took it. Even if it surfaces, even if he comes to his senses—" she said it without hope, and Reyes noticed that, filed it away— "the junta will have copied them by now, or locked them in an office safe behind three guards. My father's manifesto dies unless someone sets it again from nothing."

"Yes."

"By hand." She looked up at him. "Do you understand what that means? A manifesto is not a broadside. It's pages. Thousands of sorts per page. Setting that alone, with a partial case, hunting every letter—"

"Señorita." Reyes crouched down across the press from her, forearms on his knees, and spoke slowly, the way he spoke to customs officers he needed to persuade rather than bribe. "I want to tell you about the oath those six men swore, eleven years ago, carrying this thing down the ramp at night. They swore that the press would outlive the regime. Not beat it. Outlast it. They were romantic idiots, most of them, and they're all dead or worse now, and the regime is still standing, so you can judge how the oath has done so far." He tapped the frame. "But the press is still standing too. And the regime is eleven years older, and the men at the top of it are eleven years more frightened, and frightened men print lies because printing truth requires a machine they haven't found. They've burned every press in this city they know about. They have not burned this one. Do you understand what that makes it?"

"A target."

"The last free press in Vellamare. Maybe in the country." He let that sit. "And it needs two things before it can speak. It needs type. And it needs hands that know what they're doing. Your father's plates would have been faster. But faster isn't the only measure, Mirabel Vasquez, and I'll tell you something else your father probably taught you: type set by hand carries the hand that set it. Every letter. A machine-set page says whatever anyone paid for it. A page set letter by letter, through cold nights, by a person who could have run instead—that page says something before anyone reads a word of it."

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. Then back at the press.

"How long," she said, "to set the whole thing by hand?"

"Weeks."

"And how long before they find this ship?"

"Also weeks. If we're lucky, the same number."

She laughed then—one short breath of a laugh, no humor in it, only recognition—and stood up, wiping rust from her palms onto her skirt. "So we work in the dark, with a lamp turned low, on a condemned ship, setting thousands of letters while the harbor police change shifts, and hope arithmetic is on our side."

"Now you understand smuggling," Reyes said. "It's all arithmetic. Weight against current, cargo against patrol, hours against suspicion. Everything is arithmetic until the moment it isn't, and then it's courage, and courage is just arithmetic you've decided to stop doing."

She turned back to the press and stood before it, and Reyes watched her shoulders settle, watched something pass through her that he recognized because he'd seen it in young sailors facing their first real storm—the moment fear stops being a wall and becomes weather, something you sail into because the alternative is staying in port forever.

"My father promised a dead man's general a voice," she said quietly. "Tomás sold our name for a commission. Everyone in this city thinks the Vasquez word is finished." She laid her hand on the platen again, flat, the way you'd lay a hand on a chest to feel a heartbeat. "Good. Let them think it. We'll give them something else to read."

* * *

They stayed aboard an hour longer, while Reyes showed her the rest of it: the dry locker where type cases could be stashed, the scuttle that opened underwater for moving cargo at night, the sight line to the breakwater light that told you exactly when the patrol boat made its turn. She asked good questions. She counted patrol intervals off his answers like a compositor counting ems. By the end of the hour he had stopped thinking of her as the printer's daughter and started thinking of her as the printer, which was, he reflected, probably the entire point of whatever Aldana had seen in the Vasquez shop all those years ago.

On the row back to shore, the harbor lay black except for the customs house lamps and the low moon, and Mirabel sat in the bow with the wind pulling at her hair, and when she spoke she didn't turn around.

"Reyes. The men who hid this press. The six who carried it down. Did any of them live to see it used again?"

He pulled the oars, watching the breakwater light slide across the bow.

"No," he said.

She was quiet for a few strokes of the tide.

"Then it's overdue," she said.

Reyes grinned in the dark where no one could see it, and bent to the oars, and the skiff ran on toward the sleeping city—toward shuttered shops full of hoarded type, toward a scrapyard man with a grudge and a brother, toward cold nights and a lamp turned low and thousands upon thousands of letters waiting to be set, one by one, by hand.

Toward morning, if the arithmetic held.

If the courage did.
