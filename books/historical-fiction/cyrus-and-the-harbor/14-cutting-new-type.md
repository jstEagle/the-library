## 14. Cutting New Type

The hulk had a name once. *Corvina*, painted in flaking gold on her stern, though half the letters had surrendered to rust so that she now read *C—V—A*, which Mirabel thought was fitting: a ship reduced to consonants, holding the last free press in Vellamare.

She learned the rhythm of the tides before she learned anything else about living aboard. At low water the hull settled into the mud with a groan that traveled up through the deck planks and into her boots, and at high tide she rocked just enough to make setting type an exercise in patience. Reyes called the motion *the old woman's lullaby*. Mirabel called it an obstacle. But by the fourth night she had stopped noticing it at all, and that frightened her more than the gunboats did — how quickly a person could grow accustomed to hiding.

The press sat below decks in what had been the hold, beneath a hatch hidden under coiled rope and fish nets gone stiff with salt. It was smaller than she'd expected. Her father's flatbed press in the shop on Calle Ondina could print four hundred sheets an hour; this thing was a hand press of the older kind, iron frame pitted orange, platen scarred where a century of hands had pulled and pulled and pulled. A brass plate screwed to the bed read *IMPRENTA DEL PUERTO — EST. 1811*.

Eighty-eight years old. Older than her grandfather. Older than the century that was dying above her head.

"It printed the first harbor tariffs," Reyes said when he showed it to her, his voice carrying the odd reverence he reserved for very few things — this press, a certain knife he kept in his boot, and apparently nothing else. "Then the smuggling ledgers, when the tariffs got inconvenient. Then nothing. The rebels who hid it here are all dead or gone respectable."

"Which are you?" Mirabel asked.

"Neither, yet." He grinned. "Ask me in January."

The problem was not the press. The press worked; she had spent two days cleaning it, scraping gunk from the screw threads, oiling the rails with lamp oil cut with tallow until the carriage ran smooth. She had found a supply of paper in a sealed crate — thin stock, yellowed but sound, meant for cargo manifests no one would ever file. Ink too, hardened in a tin, brought back to life with turpentine over a spirit lamp.

The problem was type. There was almost none. Whatever case had once lived aboard had been raided decades ago for its lead and brass, and what remained was a handful of sorts scattered like teeth across the bottom of a rotted cabinet — mostly capitals, mostly worn, a dozen e's and not a single q.

"You can't print a manifesto from this," Reyes said, sifting the orphaned letters through his fingers. "You'd get maybe three words to a line."

"Then we don't print from these."

He looked at her. In the lamplight his face was all angles, the scar through his eyebrow catching yellow. "What do you propose, *tipógrafa*? We requisition new type from the junta's foundry?"

"No." She picked up one of the broken sorts, its face smashed flat, and turned it over. Lead was soft. Lead yielded. Her father had taught her that before he taught her anything else — that the whole miracle of printing rested on the fact that lead was soft enough to take an impression and hard enough to give one, ten thousand times over, and that everything worth doing in this world lived inside that contradiction. "We cast."

* * *

Her father had kept a casting pot in the back room of the shop, behind the stone sink where he washed the ink from his hands every night. Mirabel had grown up watching him melt down damaged sorts and recast them, a process he performed with the solemnity of a priest at altar and the profanity of a sailor with a hangover. She had been casting her own sorts since she was eleven. It was, in fact, the first thing he'd let her do alone — not composition, not imposition, not the press itself, but casting. *If you can make your own letters,* he'd told her, wiping sweat from his forehead with the back of his wrist, *no one can ever silence you by taking them away.*

She hadn't understood then how literally the lesson would be tested.

There was no casting pot on the *Corvina*. There was, however, in the ship's galley, a battered copper ladle and a brazier, and in the hold a coil of solder, and Reyes knew a man at the breakers' yard who sold scrap lead by the kilo without asking questions, because asking questions was bad for trade and because the man had no tongue left to ask them with anyway.

The molds were harder. Her father had used proper adjustable molds with steel jaws; here they improvised. Reyes contributed a set of jeweler's punches stolen — *liberated*, he corrected, and she let it stand — from a workshop on the waterfront, and from these, filed flat against a whetstone until their faces were true, they made matrices. It was slow, brutal work: punch a letter into soft metal, harden it, seat it in a block of hardwood carved with a channel for the lead to run, clamp, pour, pray.

Her first attempt produced something that might have been an *a* if the reader were charitable and squinting. Her second filled the galley with smoke and nearly took off her eyebrows. Her third cracked the mold block, and she sat back on the galley bench and put her head in her hands and wanted, for one long minute, to cry — not for the mold, but for everything: her father slumped at the dinner table with his heart failing between one breath and the next, the shop with its soldiers' boot prints on the composing stone, Tomás's face as he said *it was necessary, Bela, someone had to act* — and Reyes, standing in the hatchway, said nothing at all. He set a mug of coffee beside her elbow and went back on deck. That was the thing about Reyes. He knew when silence was the only courtesy left.

She fixed the mold on the fifth day.

* * *

The nights were the worst part. Or the best. She hadn't decided.

By day she slept in a hammock slung in the stern cabin while the hulk pretended to be dead — a condemned vessel awaiting breaking, flying the faded quarantine flag Reyes had run up the mast, which kept casual boarders away better than any guard. By night, when the harbor watch changed and the searchlights swept the water on their dull schedule, she climbed down to the hold and worked.

She worked by a shielded lamp, its light cut to a slit. And she cut type.

It went like this: heat the lead in the ladle until it shivered silver. Flux it with a shaving of resin. Pour into the mold in one unhesitating motion — hesitate and the metal chilled unevenly; pour twice and it foamed; pour too much and it spat. Knock the mold open with a tap of the knuckle. Extract the sort, still warm, its new-made face gleaming. Dress it — pare the fin of excess lead from the side with the knife, square the feet on the file, check the height against the gauge she had cut from a nail driven flush into a plank. If it passed, it went into the case, into its compartment, home. If it failed, back into the ladle. Nothing wasted. Everything returned.

One sort per successful pour. Two minutes, on a good night. Three, on most.

The manifesto was nine hundred words. Aldana's voice, the general's own cadence — she had copied the original text out in her fair hand onto a sheet she kept folded against her ribs, and even copied, it burned. Nine hundred words came to roughly four thousand five hundred characters, counting spaces. She needed perhaps sixty distinct characters to set the whole thing, but she needed *multiples* of each — a body text demanded twenty e's at minimum, twelve each of a, o, s, r, i, n, t, and on and on into arithmetic that made her want to lie down.

She did the sums on the back of a cargo manifest. Four thousand sorts, at two minutes each, was a hundred and fifty hours. More, allowing for failures. More, allowing for the nights when the patrol boats lingered and she had to kill the lamp and sit in absolute darkness listening to engines cross the water toward her and pass.

She had, by her best estimate, until the year ended. Seventeen days.

She stopped doing sums after that. Sums were a luxury for people with options.

* * *

On the ninth night, her hands betrayed her.

They had been aching for days — she'd ignored it, the way you ignore weather — but that night, pouring the lead, her right hand shook, just slightly, just enough, and the metal splashed, and a bead of it caught the back of her wrist above the glove.

She bit down on the sound. That was the first rule of the hulk: no sound carries. She stood there with her teeth locked and her eyes streaming and lowered her hand into the bucket of seawater they kept for exactly this, and the sea took the heat, and she counted the seconds the way her father had taught her to count when a chase was locked up wrong in the press — *one, and two, and three* — until the pain became ordinary again.

Reyes appeared at the foot of the ladder. He must have heard the hiss. He looked at the bucket, at her face, at the ruined sort congealing on the deck plates, and he didn't say *you should rest*, which would have been reasonable and unforgivable. He said:

"How many?"

"Eights," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Forty-one e's done. I need sixty."

He nodded slowly, doing his own arithmetic, and she watched him decide not to say the other thing — *it isn't enough, you'll never finish, we should smuggle you out to Cartagena instead and let the manifesto die* — and she loved him a little for the deciding, in the way you love someone for a mercy they never name.

"There's a compositor," he said instead. "Old Ferro. Worked the diocesan press till the bishop went over to the junta. He has hands and a grudge and a hideout above the ropewalk. I could bring him word—"

"No."

"Bela—"

"If he knows, then the ropewalk knows, and if the ropewalk knows then the harbor knows, and I have watched this city eat one secret after another since October." She pulled her hand from the bucket and unwound the wet cloth from the burn. It was small, a coin of angry skin. It would heal. Most things didn't. "My father promised Aldana his press would speak. His press is in Tomás's custody now, being used to print lies in his own typeface. So it has to be my press, and my hands, and my silence. That's the whole design, Reyes. It only works if it's only me."

He studied her for a long moment. Somewhere above them a gunboat's engine throttled down, and they both went still, listening to it drift, waiting for the sweep of the searchlight that came through the hatch seams and laid pale bars across the old press and moved on.

"He'd be proud of you," Reyes said quietly. "Or furious with you. With your father it was always hard to tell the difference."

"That's how I know I'm doing it right."

* * *

She fell into the rhythm the way water finds a channel.

Two minutes a sort. She learned the pours the way a musician learns intervals: this temperature for the heavy capitals, this for the thin-stemmed lowercase, the flick of the wrist that cleared the sprue hole. Her burns accumulated and mapped themselves into callus. She learned to work with the pain in her shoulders instead of against it, letting the motion come from her hips and back, conserving the wrists. She learned to hear the difference in the pour — a good sort landed in the mold with a sound like a swallowed word; a bad one hissed.

And somewhere around the twelfth night, the work changed under her hands. It stopped being endurance and became craft, and craft, she discovered, had its own anesthesia. When she was casting an *m*, there was no junta. When she was dressing the fin from a fresh *g*, there was no Tomás sitting in a government office signing papers with his good pen. There was lead and fire and the gauge-nail and the count, always the count — forty-one e's, sixty achieved; nineteen a's, target met; f's short, cast f's; the q finally solved on the fifteenth night with a mold built from a filed-down tack, and she held the first working q up to the lamp slit and laughed once, sharply, and clapped her hand over her mouth.

Patience, she understood now, was not the absence of defiance. Patience *was* the defiance. Anyone could throw a brick in a riot; the regime had guns enough for riots. What the regime could not answer, what it had no weapon for at all, was a woman in a rotting hull spending a hundred and fifty hours making four thousand small gray letters, one at a time, because a promise required it. You couldn't shoot a two-minute pour. You couldn't arrest a filing stroke. Every sort she finished was a sentence the junta had already failed to prevent, and they didn't even know it yet, and that was the sweetest part — that the defeat of General Serrano's government was accumulating, night by night, in a wooden case in a dead ship, in total silence, at two minutes per letter.

Some nights she talked to her father while she worked. Not aloud. In the language of hands — *watch this pour, Papa, see how clean* — and she could feel him somewhere at her shoulder, critical and approving, wincing at her flame management, silent with pride at the q. Other nights she talked to God, briefly and without ceremony, requesting only time. Neither answered. Both stayed.

* * *

On the seventeenth night, she set the first line.

Her fingers found the sorts by touch alone — that was the whole point of the case's geography, the California job case layout her father had drilled into her until it lived in her fingertips — and the letters came together in the composing stick, snug against the em-quads she'd cast in batches for spacing, and when the line was full she slid it onto the galley stone and read it by lamplight:

**THE VOICE THEY COULD NOT SILENCE SPEAKS NOW IN LEAD AND FIRE.**

It was not the manifesto's opening line. It was hers. She had decided, somewhere in the dark hours of the thirteenth night, that Aldana's words deserved a frame, and the frame would be her sentence, set in her type, cut by her hands — a signature in lead, claiming the work the way a painter claims a canvas. Reyes would understand why. Her father would have understood immediately. Tomás never would.

She held the line up to the light and checked it the way she'd been taught: turned it upside down, read it backward, sighted along its base for trueness. Every foot sat level. Every stem stood plumb. The impression faces were rougher than commercial type — under the loupe they showed tiny flaws, file marks, the fingerprints of desperation — but at reading distance, at arm's length, at the distance of a citizen holding a forbidden page in the square, they would resolve into something no eye could distinguish from the finest foundry work in Vellamare.

*Good enough,* her father's ghost pronounced, and she felt the words land in her chest like a medal pinned.

Four thousand sorts to go, minus one line's worth. Sixteen days gone. The year was dying around her — she could tell by the cold coming off the water, by the fireworks testing in the rich quarters across the bay, little thumps of celebration for a calendar that didn't know what it was celebrating. New Year's Eve was eleven days out. Reyes had told her the plan: fish carts, doorsteps, the customs house wall, the crowd in the Plaza del Sol reading aloud, out loud, together, so that arrest would require arresting everyone. For that to work, she needed the full run printed and dried and bundled by dawn on the thirty-first.

She banked the fire. She covered the case with canvas. She climbed the ladder into the December wind and stood at the rail looking at the lights of her city — the city that had named her family traitor, both sides of it, the regime and the rebels closing like a book with the Vasquez name pressed between its pages — and she thought about the arithmetic her father had died inside. One promise. Nine hundred words. Four thousand letters. Two minutes apiece.

It could be done. It was, in fact, already more than half done.

She was halfway down the ladder, reaching for the lamp to begin the eighteenth night, when Reyes dropped through the forward hatch ahead of schedule — hours ahead, moving fast, no grin on his face this time — and stood there breathing hard in the dark.

"They're searching the breakers' yard," he said. "Your lead seller talked. They know someone's buying casting metal." He paused, and the pause was worse than anything he'd said. "And there's a description circulating at the customs house, Bela. Of a young woman. It's a good likeness. They say it was drawn by her brother."
