## 16. The Raid on the Hulk

The type was half-set when the gulls went quiet.

I had learned to measure my nights by sound: the slap of water against the hulk's hull, the creak of the old *Providencia* settling into the mud at low tide, Reyes's boots on the deck above when he came down to check on me. And underneath it all, always, the harbor—the groan of mooring lines, the far-off clang of the customs bell, the mutter of a city that had not yet decided what it would become.

But that night the gulls lifted off the masts all at once, a white scatter against the black sky, and I stopped with a quo in my hand.

"Reyes," I said softly, to no one.

The lantern was hooded—Reyes had built the hood himself, tin and soot, so the light fell only on the composing stone and nothing escaped upward through the hatch. On the stone before me lay eleven lines of the manifesto, cut and set over six cold nights, each letter shaped by my own files from lead bar stock. Eleven lines out of four hundred. It felt like an ocean crossed by swimming with your nose above the water.

Eleven lines, and the gulls gone silent, and somewhere beyond the hull the sound I had been dreading since the first night I climbed aboard: oars. Not one boat's worth. Three.

I blew out the lantern.

* * *

You would think terror makes you fast. It does not. It makes you precise or it kills you.

My hands knew where everything was. That was the only mercy of those weeks—that craft, once driven deep enough into the body, keeps working when the mind has fled. I wrapped the set lines in the oilcloth Reyes kept for the purpose, slid them into the false bottom of his sea chest among the rusted tools, and pressed the quo and the file into the loose ballast gravel beneath the press. My fingers wanted to shake. I made them finish. Every motion I had rehearsed without admitting I was rehearsing it.

Above me, the hatch opened and let down a blade of moonlight.

"Mirabel." Reyes's voice, low, from the deck. Not alarmed. That frightened me more than shouting would have. "Stay where you are. Don't come up."

Then his boots moved away from the hatch—toward the bow, away from where I crouched—and I understood what he was doing. He was giving them something to find that wasn't me. He was making himself the destination.

I should have gone up anyway. I have turned that night over ten thousand times since, and every time I arrive at the same fork: the part of me that stayed below because he told me to, and the part that stayed because the type was not finished, and I could not let both of us be lost for eleven lines of unfinished truth. I tell myself the second reason. I know the first one is truer. I was afraid, and he was kind, and kindness is the thing I have never known how to refuse.

The oars came alongside. A voice called up—not Reyes's name; they didn't bother with names—"Boarding party! In the name of the Provisional Council!"

Boots on the deck. Many boots. The *Providencia* rocked under new weight, and dust sifted down through the hatch boards onto my face, and I did not breathe.

* * *

They had brought lanterns, good ones, junta-issue, and their light poured down through every gap in the decking like water finding cracks. I lay flat beside the old hand-press—a relic, Reyes had called it, scrap iron he'd sworn no soldier alive would recognize as anything but scrap—and I put my cheek against its cold frame as if it were a sleeping animal I could keep quiet.

Through the floorboards, their voices came broken but legible.

"—tip said printing equipment."

"This whole tub is printing equipment, Sergeant. It's a ship."

"A press, Idiot. A working press."

"Well. There's that." A pause, boots crossing overhead, right over my head, close enough that I could hear the stitching strain in a sole. "Rusted to hell. Look at it."

"It doesn't look rusted to hell."

"It looks rusted to hell enough."

I closed my eyes. The press under my hands was older than my father, older than his father maybe, salvaged off some condemned hull before I was born, and Reyes had oiled it himself, joint by joint, in the dark, with the patience of a man who believed iron could be loyal. If they dragged it into the light it would still be slick at the pivots. It would still look loved. I prayed to a God I had not spoken to since my mother died that soldiers knew rust and nothing else.

"Search the cabins," the sergeant said. "And find this smuggler. Reyes. The tip named him."

* * *

They found him on deck, waiting, which surprised them. I heard it in the change of their voices—the sharpening, the small animal pleasure men take when the thing they were sent to hunt walks out to meet them.

"Reyes."

"Who's asking?"

"You know who's asking. You've been asking about us for years, from what the records say. Running things through Vellamare that shouldn't run. Now there's a report of a press aboard this hulk. Type. Paper. Sedition."

"And what do you see?" Reyes spread it before them generously, a host showing his house. "A rotting hulk nobody would buy. Rust. Rats. Two hundred tons of scrap the harbor office has been meaning to break up since the last war. Come below if you like—I'll show you the rats personally."

Silence. The kind of silence that is a man being watched while he decides whether to believe you.

"The tip," the sergeant said slowly, "named a printer."

"Did it name a woman?"

My heart stopped.

"No," the sergeant admitted. "It named a printer."

"Then your tip is stale," Reyes said, "or your informant is selling you his debts. This is Vellamare, Sergeant. Half the city owes the other half money, and informing is cheaper than paying."

I heard one of the soldiers laugh, short and ugly, and then the sergeant again: "Search below anyway."

* * *

They came down the hatch in a clatter of gear and lantern light, and I was already moving—away from the press, away from the chest, into the stern compartment where the bilge stood knee-deep in cold black water. Reyes had shown me the hiding place the second week, saying nothing about why, just pointing, the way he showed me everything: here the bilge boards that lift, here the gap between frames wide enough for a slim body, here how to breathe shallow so the cold doesn't make you cough.

I lowered myself into the water and pulled the board across my shoulders and became cargo.

The water came to my chest. It was the cold of wells, of cellars, of things that never see the sun, and it closed around me like a fist. Through the gaps in the framing I watched their boots pass along the deck above the bilge, lantern light strobing between the boards, and I listened to them search the cabin where the sea chest sat with eleven lines of the manifesto hidden in its false bottom, and I did not breathe, and I counted. I have a compositor's habit of counting. I count ems and ens. That night I counted to four thousand and started over.

In the cabin, a latch clicked.

"Chest."

"Tools," Reyes said, from the doorway. His voice had not changed. "Line, hooks, a marlinspike older than my grandfather. Take the spike if you want it. I'll want it back."

The lid creaked. The false bottom was fitted so well I had once spent twenty minutes finding it myself. Oilcloth. Rust-stained canvas. The smell of tar and iron—

"Type," a soldier said. "Sergeant. There's type in here."

The world narrowed to a pinhole.

"Broken type," Reyes said. "Scrap. You melt that sort of thing."

"Half of it's cut fresh. Look at the edges."

"Fresh from what? A manifesto?" Reyes laughed—it was a good laugh, warm, ruined slightly by salt and cigarettes, the laugh I had come to trust more than most people's promises. "Open it. Read it aloud. Go on. If you can read me one seditious word out of that box, you can arrest me twice."

Another silence. I could picture it exactly: the soldier holding a lump of lead to a lantern, turning it, squinting. Broken sorts. Mis-cast blanks. And among them—the pieces I had ruined learning the files, the letters cut wrong, a *q* that looked like a drowned *p*, slugs of lead I had sawed too short. A week of failures. I had begged Reyes to throw them overboard and he had refused—*lead is lead, girl, we'll recast them*—and now his stubbornness stood between us and the firing squad, wearing my mistakes as a disguise.

"It's junk," the soldier said finally.

"Bring it anyway," the sergeant said. "The Colonel likes evidence. Where's this printer, Reyes?"

"There is no printer. There's me, and there's rats, and there's two hundred tons of—"

"Find him," the sergeant said, tired now, wanting to be done, wanting his bed and his pay and his little war concluded. "Turn the hulk over if you have to."

* * *

What happened next took less than a minute, and I have spent more hours of my life inside that minute than inside any other.

I heard Reyes move—not running; I never heard Reyes run—but moving with purpose, boots quick on the deck, and then his voice raised for the first time, pitched to carry, pitched deliberately wrong: "That's far enough, gentlemen."

A scuffle. A shout of *he's going over!* The crack of something—wood, a boathook—against bone. Then the real noise began, boots stamping, men cursing, the *Providencia* lurching as bodies collided above me, and through it all Reyes's voice, hard now, giving them exactly what he had decided to give them:

"You want your printer? I'm your printer. Ask the rebels who cuts their handbills. Ask them who runs paper past your gunboats. You think a smuggler wastes honest cargo space on scrap lead? Take me in. The rest of this bucket is exactly as worthless as it looks."

"Bind him," the sergeant said. "Search him. And burn the hulk—Colonel's orders. Nothing leaves this harbor we can't account for."

Burn the hulk.

The words went through the bilge boards and through my wet clothes and into my chest, where they settled next to my heart like a second, colder heart. The press. The type I hadn't hidden. The ream of paper sewn into the mattress in the forepeak. Everything.

"Tonight?" a soldier asked.

"Tomorrow, after the Colonel inspects. Tow it to the breakwater yard." A grunt. "He's not going anywhere."

Then Reyes's voice, one last time, and this was the cruelest part, because it was pitched low, almost gentle, aimed downward through the deck as precisely as a man dropping a coin through a grate:

"Whatever's in the water can stay in the water. Some things are only worth saving if they're finished."

I bit down on my own wrist until I tasted blood, and I did not answer him, and they took him up the side and into the boat, and the lantern light withdrew, and the *Providencia* rocked alone on the black tide with me shivering in her belly.

* * *

I waited until the oars faded. I waited until the gulls resettled on the masts, which took longer, because gulls know more than we do about when a thing is truly over. Then I came up out of the bilge, shaking so hard I had to grip the hatch ladder with both hands, and stood on the deck in the wind and the moonlight, dripping harbor water onto boards that were, by tomorrow night, scheduled to be ash.

Eleven lines in the chest—they had left the chest, thank God, taken only the handful of ruined sorts as proof of nothing. The press, oiled and patient under its tarpaulin of sailcloth. Four hundred lines yet unset. One night, maybe two, before they towed her to the breakwater and put flame to her.

And Reyes in a cell in the customs house, or worse, having bought my escape with the only currency he had, which was himself.

I stood at the rail and looked at the lights of Vellamare strung along the shore like a row of lit windows in a doll's house, all that ordinary life going on behind glass, and I thought about my father's hands, and about the promise I had inherited like a debt, and about the arithmetic that was beginning to frighten me more than any soldier: that everything I saved, someone else paid for. My freedom was not free. It had been purchased tonight, cash on delivery, by a man who had told me to stay below and then gone up to meet the guns.

Some things are only worth saving if they're finished.

"All right," I said aloud, to the empty deck, to the rusted press, to the man already being rowed away into the dark. My voice cracked on the harbor air and I let it. "Finished, then."

I went below, lit the hooded lantern, and unlocked the case of type I had cut with my own hands, line by line, through the cold.

Three hundred eighty-nine lines to go, and the tide would turn before dawn.
