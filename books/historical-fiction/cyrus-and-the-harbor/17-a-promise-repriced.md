## 17. A Promise Repriced

The salt warehouses at the harbor's western edge had not held salt in years. They held dust now, and rat bones, and the smell of brine soaked so deep into the timber that no decade could wash it out. Mirabel had found the one with the broken padlock on the third day, and by the fourth she had learned its rhythms: which boards creaked under a patrolman's boots on the pier outside, when the fishing boats came in and their crews shouted too loudly for anyone to hear a stranger breathing behind a wall, how the light came through the gaps in the planking and moved across the floor in slow gold bars that told her the hour better than any clock.

She was sitting against the wall with the half-set type spread out on a canvas tarp before her, counting it again. She counted it every few hours, as if the number might change, as if wishing could multiply the letters she'd carried out of the hulk in a fish crate while Reyes put himself between her and the soldiers' lanterns.

Two hundred and eleven pieces of type. Out of the thousands she would need.

*Set what you can,* Reyes had said, shoving the crate into her arms. His voice hadn't shaken. *The rest you cut. Go.*

Then he had turned and walked up the gangplank toward the boarding party, loud and drunk-sounding and entirely sober, giving them a smuggler's performance worth every year of his craft, and by the time they realized the woman with the crate had gone over the far side into a rowboat, Reyes was in irons and Mirabel was under the pier, paddling with her hands because there were no oars, the type case wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child.

That was six days ago. Or seven. She had stopped being sure.

She picked up a piece of type now—an *e*, she thought, though her fingers were so cold she had to check with her eyes—and turned it over. The brass caught the thin light. Somewhere beyond the warehouse walls, Vellamare was going about its morning: gulls screaming over the fish market, carts on the quay road, a patrol whistling some jaunty march the junta had commissioned. The city did not know she was here. The city did not know anything anymore except what the junta's presses told it, printed in her father's typeface, set by her brother's arrangement, wearing the stolen face of the Vasquez name.

The counterfeit manifesto had been circulating for two weeks now. She had seen one pasted to a wall near the customs house on her second night of hiding—a broadsheet announcing General Aldana's surrender and exile, his supposed plea for all loyal citizens to lay down their arms, the whole architecture of lies assembled from metal her father had spent thirty years keeping honest. Someone had scrawled *TRAITORS* beneath it. Someone else had written *VASQUEZ* with an arrow pointing down, as if the name needed help finding the mud.

She had stood in the dark reading it until her legs went numb, and then she had walked back to the warehouse and sat where she was sitting now and done nothing at all for a very long time.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, laid out honestly, the way her father had taught her to lay out a page—measure twice, cut once, waste nothing:

She had two hundred and eleven pieces of type. A manifesto of Aldana's ran four pages. Four pages required perhaps eight thousand sorts, sorted and set and justified, plus leads, plus furniture, plus ink, plus paper, plus a press—and the press, the rusted relic Reyes had shown her aboard the condemned hulk *La Perla*, was a hand press older than her father, seized with salt rot and a broken frisket, and Reyes was the only one who knew its temper, and Reyes was in the junta's custody on the Calle Ancha.

Even if the press worked. Even if she found ink and paper. Even if she somehow cut or borrowed or stole eight thousand letters. Even if she survived long enough to do any of it—the junta wanted her, the rebels wanted her silenced for the counterfeit, and both sides had more eyes than she had hiding places.

Against all of this, one promise. One dying man's word to another dying man's cause. Her father's voice in the last week of his life, thinned to almost nothing: *A press speaks for whoever cannot. That is the whole trade, Mira. Everything else is furniture.*

And here was the other column, the one she kept trying not to write:

If she abandoned the promise—if she found a way out of the city, north along the coast, maybe on one of the fruit boats, maybe under a new name—she would live. That was the entire argument in favor. She would live, somewhere, and the century would turn without her, and the junta's lie would stand in the historical record wearing her father's typeface, and Aldana's true words would die as rumor, and eventually as nothing. And she would be alive to know it.

She turned the *e* over in her fingers. Set it down. Picked up another.

Her father had taught her the cases when she was six years old, standing her on a stool so her chin cleared the upper trays. He had never once suggested she learn something gentler. Other fathers in Vellamare steered their daughters toward lace and ledgers; Emilio Vasquez had put a composing stick in her hand and said, *Your hands will never lie for you, Mirabel. Remember that. Everything else about you can be lied about—your face, your age, your name. But the hands know the truth.* Then he'd guided her fingers through the ritual: *e* most numerous, lower left; *a* beside it; the caps above; the rare letters in their small compartments like shy guests at a wedding. By eight she could set a line faster than his journeymen. By twelve she could read upside down and backward, the whole world of print mirrored and made plain.

He had been proud of her. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that hurt worst. Not grief exactly—grief was a country she was learning to walk through—but this specific ache: he had been *proud*, and his pride had been in her hands, and her hands were currently doing nothing but counting a dwindling pile of brass in a salt warehouse while the world's lies compounded daily with interest.

There had been a moment, after the plates vanished—after Tomás, God rot him, after Tomás took them—when her father had collapsed at the shop table, and she had thought at first it was his heart, and she had knelt by him and held his head, and he had opened his eyes and looked at her with such terrible clarity and said, *Not the heart. The arithmetic. I have been doing the arithmetic.* As if shame were simply a sum that had finally come due. He died three days later, quietly, between one dawn and the next, with the smell of ink still on his own hands, and Mirabel had closed his eyes and walked out of the shop and had not been back, because the shop was Tomás's now, bought with junta paper, and some doors you do not open twice.

*You could stop,* said a voice in her head. It sounded reasonable. It sounded, annoyingly, like Tomás—because Tomás had always known how to dress surrender in the language of sense. *You tried. Your father is dead. Reyes is captured. The plates are lost. No one could ask more. Walk away, and live, and let the dead keep their promises—they have no use for them.*

But that was the flaw in the argument, wasn't it. Promises weren't for the dead's use. They were for the living's. A promise was a load-bearing wall: remove it and see what the house became. If she walked away, she would not be a woman who had failed; she would be a woman who had *decided* that truth was optional when it grew expensive. And every morning after that decision would be built on top of it, the way floors are built on joists, and she would feel it flex under her feet for the rest of her life.

No. Despair was a compositor too, she thought, almost laughing. It set its lines neatly, in plausible type, and offered you the proof, and the proof always read the same: *It cannot be done. You are one girl. The metal does not exist.* But she had spent her whole life learning that proofs lied until the form was locked up tight. The page always looked impossible loose in the chase. You couldn't see what it was until every piece was in place and the quoins were tightened and the whole thing stood firm and suddenly—suddenly—it was real, it was printable, it could make ten thousand copies of itself and argue with the world.

She was loose in the chase right now. That was all. Loose type proves nothing. Neither does a loose woman.

* * *

In the afternoon she worked.

She had salvaged, besides the 211 sorts, three lumps of type-metal she'd pried from the hulk's wreckage, a small iron ladle from the galley stove, and—her one extravagance, purchased with the last coins in her pocket from a chandler who hadn't looked at her face—a casting mold of ancient make, adjustable, meant for bullets but close enough to the principle. Her father had cast type occasionally, when a sort wore out and no founder would sell to a man under suspicion. *Melt and mold,* he used to say. *The alphabet is immortal; only its bodies wear out.*

So she built a fire the size of a cupped hand in a corner where the smoke could find the roof gaps, and she melted the first lump of lead and alloy, and she cast her first letter.

It came out wrong. Too thick, misstruck, the stem crooked. She cut it off the sprue anyway and held it up and felt something move in her chest that was not quite hope and not quite grief but lived in the same rooms.

The second came out better. The third she actually recognized: a rough, pitted, imperfect letter *t*.

One letter down. Some hundreds of thousands to go, counting duplicates. It was absurd. She let herself laugh once, short and hard, and the laugh frightened a rat somewhere overhead, and then she got back to work, because work was the only argument against despair that ever held weight in evidence.

She set herself a schedule, the way her father had scheduled a print run: hours for melting and casting, hours for filing the casts true, hours for setting whatever words she could compose from her salvage. She began setting the manifesto from memory—she had read Aldana's original so many times during those weeks of preparation that it lived in her now, line by line, the general's plain soldier's cadence, the passage about ports and free men that she loved best: *A harbor belongs to no government. It belongs to every boat that has ever come home to it.* She set that sentence first, from the recovered type, and locked it in a makeshift chase lashed with cord, just to prove to herself that it could stand.

While her hands worked, her mind did its slower labor.

She inventoried her assets the way she'd inventory a font. Reyes: in custody, but men like Reyes were hard to kill and harder to buy; smugglers had friends in every customs house and every jailer's kitchen. There was a girl, Petra, who sold fish near the warehouses and had watched Mirabel come and go for six days without once asking why, which either meant indifference or sympathy, and in Vellamare these days sympathy hid inside indifference the way rebels hid inside crowds. There was the hulk itself—*La Perla* still rode at her mooring, unguarded as far as Mirabel could tell, because the junta had searched her and found nothing worth stealing and no reason to think a rusty press mattered. They didn't understand presses. That was the regime's whole weakness, when she thought about it clearly: they understood guns and ledgers, and they genuinely believed that whoever controlled the guns and ledgers controlled everything. They had never once set type. They didn't know what it felt like to hold a finished page and understand that it would outlive every man who ordered it suppressed.

And there was time—or almost time. The century turned in twenty-three days. Her father's promise had a deadline built into it: *before the old year dies.* Deadlines concentrated a compositor wonderfully. Her father had pulled all-nighters for less.

Twenty-three days. Eight thousand letters. One broken press. One captured smuggler. One hunted name.

She fed the little fire another shaving of alloy and poured the next letter.

* * *

It was full dark when she heard the footsteps.

Not a patrolman's boots—patrolmen moved in pairs and didn't bother with softness. These were single footsteps, unhurried, careful, testing each board before committing weight, and they stopped outside the warehouse door that hung ajar on its one hinge, and then a voice came through the gap, low, and Mirabel's whole body went cold before the voice even resolved, because she knew it, she would have known it anywhere, she had known it her entire life—

"Mira." A pause. "It's me."

Tomás stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind him, and in his arms, wrapped in oilcloth the same way she had wrapped her own salvage, heavy enough to change his posture, heavy enough to mean everything—

"Plates," he said. "All of them. We need to talk fast. I bought us until morning, and not a minute more."
