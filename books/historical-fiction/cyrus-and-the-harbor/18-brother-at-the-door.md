## 18. Brother at the Door

She heard him before she saw him—the particular way Tomás knocked, two raps and a pause, as if the door might owe him money. A habit from childhood. A habit she had thought herself past being moved by.

Mirabel went still among the salt barrels, the half-set type wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat. Three nights in the warehouses had taught her the warehouse's own language: the tick of cooling timber, the gulls arguing on the roof beams, the scrape of rats who owned the place by right of tenancy. This sound did not belong. This sound had followed her across the city like a smell.

"Mirabel." Tomás's voice, low, pitched for one listener. "I know you're here. Don't make me say it louder."

She considered the exits. There were two: the water door, where the tide slapped black against the loading sill, and the alley gap between the salt house and the chandlery, narrow enough that a soldier with shoulders would have to turn sideways. Tomás's shoulders were wide. She'd sewn the coat he wore them in.

But if they knew where she was—if the junta knew—there would already be boots in the alley, and there weren't. Only his voice, and beneath it the harbor working its endless arithmetic against the pilings.

She stepped out of the dark between the barrels with the crowbar held low at her side.

He saw it and didn't flinch. That frightened her more than anything else he could have done. The old Tomás would have laughed, made some joke about printers' daughters and their tools. This man simply watched the iron and then lifted his eyes to her face as if reading fine print.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You came to arrest me. Say it so I can decide what to do with this."

"I came alone." He raised both hands, palms out, the way you'd calm a spooked horse—or show a magistrate you carried nothing. "No escort. I paid off the man on the warehouse district watch. It cost me my mother's rings. I want that noted in the record."

"There is no record," Mirabel said. "There's just you, standing in a doorway, and me deciding whether to believe you."

"That seems fair." He lowered his hands slowly. In the thin light from the high window his face was thinner than she remembered, gray under the eyes, the collar of his good coat shiny with wear. Power was supposed to feed a man. Whatever the junta was feeding him, it wasn't much. "May I come in? Not in. May I stand inside the door? The alley has ears."

"The alley has me with a crowbar if you take another step."

He nodded, accepting the geography of it. And then he reached into his coat.

Her grip tightened on the bar. But what he drew out was heavy, wrapped in sacking, and when he peeled the cloth back the lantern-light caught it—that dull silver gleam she knew better than her own reflection. The plates. Aldana's manifesto, line upon line of cast metal, the words her father had locked into a forme eleven months ago and sworn to keep until the world was ready for them.

The original plates. The ones Tomás had carried out of the shop in a flour sack while their father's heart gave out under the weight of an empty font case.

"You took these back," she said. Her voice sounded very far away, like something shouted across water.

"I stole them twice now," Tomás said. "Once for me. Once against myself. You'll forgive me if the arithmetic doesn't balance."

* * *

She made him sit on a salt barrel because her legs had decided the matter for her. He set the plates on the barrel beside him, and neither of them touched them for a while, as though they were something recently dead.

"Tell me how Father died," Tomás said. "I've only had my version. I need yours."

"He sat down at the composing stone," Mirabel said. "After we found the case empty. He put his hand where the plates should have been, flat, like a man checking a table for dust. Then he said your name. Once. Not loud. And then he—" She stopped. Started again. "Dr. Ferro said the heart. He wrote *cardiac failure* on the certificate because the regime requires a cause and grief isn't one."

Tomás looked at the floor. The gulls went quiet overhead, and in the silence the tide filled up the whole warehouse.

"I told myself a story," he said finally. "I'm good at stories; you know that. I used to tell Father the type was damaged so he'd let me sort the broken sorts. I liked the order of it. Every wrong letter in one tray, waiting to be melted down and made right again." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "That's what I told myself you all were. Broken type. That if I arranged things correctly—gave them the plates, took the commission, kept the name Vasquez attached to the *right* side of history—the regime would melt us down and recast us as loyal citizens. Clean metal. New letters."

"And instead?"

"Instead I learned how the junta reads." He looked up. "They don't read for loyalty, Mira. They read for use. A man who betrays his father once is a tool, and tools get used until the handle breaks, and then they're thrown away with the rest of the scrap. They never intended to spare anyone named Vasquez. The commission was paper. The shop was always going to burn. People named in a sealed letter don't get pardoned; they get processed."

"The letter," Mirabel said. "You told them where we kept things. Did you write it? Was it you?"

"No." He said it fast, too fast, and she marked the speed. "I delivered information. Words, at first. Small ones. Which hours Father kept, which suppliers came to the back door. Then the plates. But the letter came before any of that—it came before I even knew there was anything to sell. Someone else named us, Mira. Someone who knew about Aldana's visits." His jaw worked. "I've spent two months trying to find out who, and I think—I think it was someone inside the propaganda office settling an old score, or someone outside it buying insurance. I don't know. What I know is that I built my whole bargain on top of a betrayal that wasn't even mine, like a man building a house on a grave."

"So you're not the monster," Mirabel said. "Just the undertaker's apprentice."

"Yes," he said simply. "That's fair."

* * *

He stood then, and took something else from inside his coat—a folded paper, office stock, stamped with the eagle of the provisional government. He held it without offering it.

"They gave me a duty roster tonight," he said. "My name is on it because I'm useful, and being useful is the only citizenship they issue. At midnight, a patrol walks the warehouse district. Two men and a corporal named Bessa, who owes me cards and hates me anyway, which makes him honest. They walk Canal Street, the customs spur, and along the salt houses." He laid the paper on a barrel between them, bridging the distance like a plank. "At one o'clock, the harbor gate changes shift, and the new watch is Captain Delgado's men, who are not bought by anyone I can afford."

Mirabel didn't touch the paper. "You're giving me their patrol routes."

"I'm giving you hours. Midnight to one. Maybe less, if Bessa decides to be diligent out of spite." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked nine years old, caught breaking the lead rules. "Reyes is taken. Everyone knows it. They took him off the hulk four days ago and he's in the naval lockup answering questions about a press nobody's supposed to know exists. Sooner or later he gives up the hulk, because everyone gives up everything eventually, and then whatever you're doing in these salt houses stops being invisible. You have days, Mira. Not weeks."

"How do you know what I'm doing in these salt houses?"

"Because I know Father taught you the cases before he taught me, and because you disappeared the same week a condemned brig in the harbor started showing lamp-light at strange hours, and because I am not stupid, whatever the family story is." He paused. "And because I asked the propaganda office for the requisition logs on seized print materials, and the manifesto plates I sold them are missing from inventory. Someone above me is having counterfeits struck from copies—cheap stereotype casts, wrong weight, wrong impression—and the originals have gone into a drawer. Which means the true plates are worth nothing to them and everything to whoever prints the real text."

He picked up the sacking, folded it, and set it over the plates again—an oddly gentle gesture, the way you'd cover a sleeping face.

"So I'm returning them," he said. "Not to Father. I understand that now. To you."

"Why?" The question came out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard. "Why now, Tomás? You had the commission. You had the office with your name on the door. Why trade all of it for a sack of metal and a guilty conscience at my door?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Out in the harbor, a gunboat's engine turned over somewhere beyond the mole, a low animal sound, and they both listened to it pass.

"Because yesterday," he said, "they asked me to identify you. A photograph, three names, point to your sister. And I realized, sitting there with my pen in my hand, that I was going to do it. That's the part I can't forgive, Mira—not the plates, not the shop. That my hand was going to move. I felt it wanting to move. Ten years of being Father's son and none of it weighed as much as wanting to be *their* man." His voice cracked and he pressed it flat again. "So I stole the plates out of the evidence drawer, which is a hanging offense, and I paid off a watchman with Mother's rings, which is a stupid one, and I came here. Too late to fix anything. Late enough that fixing isn't the word anymore."

"What is the word?"

"Answering," he said. "Father promised a man that his press would speak when the man's voice was taken. I broke that promise. I can't unbreak it. All I can do is hand the promise back to someone who won't." He stood, buttoned his coat, and became almost the old Tomás for a moment—the tilt of the chin, the merchant's ease. Almost. "Midnight to one. Canal Street, customs spur, salt houses. After that, Delgado's men at the gate."

"Where will you be?"

"Writing my confession properly, with dates. There's a notary in the Old Quarter who owes Father money and hates the junta more. If they take me—and they will, probably before the week's out—there should at least be a document with the truth in it, witnessed and stamped. The one thing this century taught us: paper outlives everyone." He smiled without any pleasure in it. "Consider it a proof copy."

He got as far as the door. Then Mirabel said his name, and he stopped but didn't turn.

"The night you took the plates," she said. "Did you know Father would die of it?"

"No." The word came out raw. "But I knew he might. I did it anyway. That's the whole of it, Mira, and I won't dress it in better cloth." Now he turned, and his eyes were wet and he let them be. "Print it under your own name. Reyes was right about that, wherever they're keeping him. Father's name is a debt. Yours is the only thing in our family that isn't owed to anyone."

Then he was gone, footsteps fading down the alley toward Canal Street, unhurried, a man walking into the record he'd written for himself.

Mirabel stood alone with the salt and the tide-sound and the sack of lead on the barrel. When she finally unwrapped it again, the plates were cold as coins left overnight in a pocket. Her father's setting—she knew every line of it, knew where his thumb had nudged a stubborn em-quad flush, knew the slightly proud letter *A* in *ALDANA* that he'd chosen deliberately, from a worn matrix, because Aldana had admired it years ago at a trade fair. All of it intact. All of it real.

Betrayal answered too late. But answered.

She looked at the folded duty roster lying on the barrel, and at the plates, and at the wrapped type she'd cut with her own hands through the frozen nights, letter by patient letter, and she understood suddenly what the next twenty-four hours demanded: not patience anymore, but speed. The hulk was watched. Reyes was in the lockup. The patrol walked at midnight and the gate changed at one.

By tomorrow, the century would be dead. Before it died, the press would speak.

She began packing the type.
