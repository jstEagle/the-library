## 19. Night Print Run

The hulk smelled of rust and old fish, and Mirabel loved it the way she had never loved her father's shop.

That was a strange thing to learn about herself at one in the morning, with gunboats somewhere out in the black water and two hundred sheets still unpulled. But there it was. The *Soberana* — a condemned coal hulk, half-sunk at her moorings, listing like a drunk leaning on a fence — held a press in her belly that no official ledger remembered owning. Reyes had found it years ago and told no one, the way he did everything: quietly, sideways, without asking permission from anyone who might say no.

Mirabel ran her hand along the press's frame. Cast iron, English-made, forty years old if it was a day, its paint gone to scab. Someone had oiled her recently — Reyes, or one of his quiet people — but the oil couldn't hide what she was: a machine that had been left for dead, waiting in the dark for someone to need her.

"You keep looking at her like that," said the woman called Pilar, "she'll start expecting compliments."

Pilar was fifty-some, broad as a barrel, a fishwife by day and something else by every other hour, with forearms that could crack a lobster and hands that were setting type now with surprising delicacy. There were four of them aboard tonight. Pilar. A boy named Anselmo, seventeen, all elbows and nerves, whose job was the ink. A silent man everyone called el Cocinero because he cooked for the resistance and apparently for nothing else, standing at the dampening tub with his sleeves rolled. And Mirabel.

And the type. Her type.

It sat in the forme now, locked into the chase, two hundred and forty square inches of hand-cut letters set line by line across eleven cold nights in the salt warehouses, in a borrowed room above Pilar's sister's kitchen, in the back of a hearse that had carried them here an hour ago under a load of ice. Every letter of it she had cut or set herself, except the ones Pilar had helped with on the last night, when Mirabel's fingers had gone numb and her eyes had begun doubling the world. *Manifiesto del Puerto de Vellamare al Pueblo.* General Aldana's words — the words he had given her father in peacetime, when promising a printer anything still cost only honor — set not in her father's typeface, which was gone, which Tomás had traded away and then stolen back and brought to her door wrapped in sacking like a corpse returned for burial, but in new type. Cut by her. Set by her. Hers.

"Read it again," Pilar said. "The last proof."

"I've read it nine times."

"Read it ten. My eyes are older than your press."

Mirabel lifted the single proof sheet they'd pulled at midnight, the trial run, and held it to the lantern. The paper was cheap — Reyes had gotten what he'd gotten, three reams of thin stock from a stationer who asked no questions — and the ink would smudge if you breathed wrong, but the letters stood up sharp and black, and the words marched down the page in their honest rows.

*To the people of Vellamare: You have been told this port is content...*

She read it aloud, low. She read the part about the grain tariffs and the men who profited from them. She read the part about the soldiers' pay, docked before it was earned. She read Aldana's sentence — the one her father had promised to print twenty years ago and never had the chance to, the promise that had killed him slow and then fast, the promise that had eaten her family like rot eats a hull: *A government that must silence printers has already confessed.*

Anselmo laughed once, sharp, at that line, then clapped his hand over his mouth like the word might escape through the porthole and swim to the gunboats.

"It's true," el Cocinero said. He almost never spoke. When he did, everyone listened. "That's why it will work. Not because of Aldana. Because it's true and everyone knows it and nobody has said it where the saying could be seen."

Mirabel folded the proof and laid it flat. "Then let's be seen."

* * *

They worked the way water works, without hurry and without stopping.

The press took two people properly. One to lay the sheet, one to pull the bar. Pilar laid; Mirabel pulled. El Cocinero kept the ink and the damping even, and Anselmo ferried blank sheets from the ice-wet stack to Pilar's hands and carried finished ones aft, laying them in fifties between boards salvaged from a crate, weighting them with a bilge pump and two ingots of lead ballast that Mirabel tried very hard not to look at, because lead was lead, and her hands still remembered the weight of it from the cases in her father's shop, and thinking of the shop tonight would undo her.

The first dozen pulls were bad. The impression came down uneven on the left — the platen needed shimming, forty years of settling had tilted her slightly, and there was no time for proper adjustment. Mirabel knelt with the lantern and folded strips of pasteboard until the pressure sat true, and her fingers, which had trembled all evening, went still the moment they had a mechanical problem to solve. That was the secret she had never told anyone: fear fell away from her hands when they had work. It was the only prayer she knew.

"Better," Pilar said after the thirteenth sheet. She held it up, tilted her head. "Look. You can read it across the hold."

"That's the point."

"No." Pilar shook her head slowly. "The junta's proclamations, you can read them too. This is different. Look how it sits on the page. Even. Honest. Like it isn't afraid of being read." She looked at Mirabel with her small, shrewd eyes. "Your father's work looked like this."

"You never saw my father's work."

"I bought a handbill from his shop in '91. The fish-market weights notice." She turned back to the stack. "I kept it. Don't ask me why. Something in the way it was printed made me trust it." She smoothed a fresh sheet onto the tympan. "Now I know why. Same hands. Same blood in the ink."

Mirabel pulled the bar. The platen came down. The sixteenth true sheet of the manifesto of the port of Vellamare was born in the belly of a ship the government had written off, and Mirabel felt nothing at all like triumph — she felt like a midwife, tired and precise, and infinitely careful.

* * *

At three o'clock, Anselmo went up top to listen and came down the ladder too fast, missing the last two rungs entirely.

"Lights. Two of them. Off the mole."

Everyone stopped. In the quiet Mirabel could hear the harbor itself: water slapping the hulk's flanks, the far creak of moored boats, wind in the rigging of the derelicts moored nearby. Then, faint, the sound underneath everything — engines. Low, patient, the idle of a boat holding position.

"They're not coming here," el Cocinero said. "Not yet. If they knew, we'd know."

"They're boxing us in," Reyes said from the companionway.

He hadn't come below all night. He'd been aloft since midnight, watching, and now he came down the ladder with the unhurried economy of a man who had decided exactly how frightened to be and refused to exceed the ration. His coat was wet at the shoulders. He crossed the hold and looked at the stack of printed sheets — nearly a hundred and forty now — and counted them with a flick of his eyes the way other men counted money.

"How long for the rest?" he asked.

"At this pace," Mirabel said, "two hours. Maybe less if the ink behaves."

"The tide turns at four-forty. The gunboats sit better on the flood; they'll drift wide on the ebb. That gives us a gap near five." He looked at her. "You understand what happens if they board before the run is done."

"I understand."

"Say it."

She met his eyes. Around her, the others went still, because everyone aboard understood that Reyes did not waste questions and did not forgive answers that flinched.

"If they board," Mirabel said, "the type gets sunk. The sheets get sunk. I am a woman printing smuggled lace for my sister in Corazón, I have papers saying so, and I do not know any of you. And if none of that holds—" her voice stayed level, and she was proud of that, later, remembering— "then it doesn't matter what I say, because a Vasquez caught with this manifesto hangs either way. Tomás learned that too late. I learned it first."

Something moved behind Reyes's face — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man checking a knot and finding it holds.

"Pilar," he said. "Faster pulls. Anselmo — the skiff, ready by half past four. Cocinero—" he paused, and for the first time all night something crossed his voice that was not command. "You'll stay aboard."

El Cocinero nodded as if Reyes had told him the weather. He would stay. If soldiers came, they would find one man, an old broken press, and a story. Everyone knew what that meant and no one said it, and el Cocinero began wiping the rollers as though he had just been complimented.

Mirabel pulled the bar again.

* * *

Somewhere around sheet two hundred, her body left her.

Not entirely — her arms kept pulling, her eyes kept checking the corners of each sheet for offset and ghosting — but some essential part of her detached and stood in the corner of the hold watching, and that part thought strange things.

It thought about her father's hands. She had been dreaming about them for weeks now, waking and sleeping: the way his right thumb rode the edge of every sheet he laid, feeling for the gripper margin by touch alone, his eyes elsewhere, already reading the next page of whatever he was setting. He had taught her that the press does not care what you print. It is the most honest instrument ever built, he said, because it cannot lie about effort. Bad type, bad impression. Lazy ink, lazy page. A press tells you exactly how much care went into a thing, forever, in every copy. People forget that. Governments count on people forgetting that.

*You cannot buy a press,* he used to say, cleaning the rollers on Sundays when the shop smelled of solvent and she was small enough to stand on a stool and help. *You can only borrow it from the truth.*

Her father had promised Aldana a press's voice and paid for the promise with everything. Tomás had sold the same promise twice and it had bought him a cell. And here was Mirabel, thirty feet from open water, pulling the promise off the bed of a stolen press one sheet at a time, and discovering that the arithmetic her father had died doing — one word against one gun, one sheet against one soldier — balanced differently than anyone had guessed. It wasn't one word against one gun. It was two hundred copies against eight soldiers. Five hundred against a garrison. A thousand against a century.

The bar came down. The bar came up. Pilar laid another sheet, smooth as making a bed.

"Your mind's gone somewhere," Pilar said, without looking up.

"My father's here."

"Good," Pilar said. "Keep him working."

* * *

At four-twenty, Reyes whistled once through his teeth, low, and the rhythm changed.

The last sixty sheets. The ink was going gray — the reservoir running thin — and el Cocinero fed it drop by drop, stretching it, because the alternative was stopping to mix more and there was no longer any alternative to anything. The paper was down to the last half-ream. Mirabel's shoulders burned; she had been pulling for three hours and the bar had grown heavy as a door. Anselmo stacked finished sheets with the concentration of a boy defusing something, because that was in fact what he was doing.

Above them the engine sounds had changed. Closer. Two, maybe three boats, moving on the ebb tide, sweeping the moorings of the derelict fleet with searchlights that slid through the hulk's rotted hatches and striped the hold in bars of white light. Each time one passed overhead everyone froze, hands on their work, faces upturned like men caught in rain. The light crawled across the stacks of manifesto, across the great black rectangle of set type, across Mirabel's ink-stained hands — and moved on. The hulk was too deep in the graveyard of dead ships. Too obvious. Gunboats hunt what runs.

Forty-one sheets left. Thirty. The tide had turned; Mirabel felt it in the floor, the hulk's groan changing pitch as the water shifted beneath her, the whole harbor exhaling and turning over in its sleep.

Twenty. Pilar was laying sheets faster now, rougher, and Mirabel corrected her once — *gripper margin* — and Pilar obeyed, because even at the end, even now, the margin mattered. The margin was the difference between a page that could be read and a page that was wasted. Everything Mirabel had ever believed lived inside that half-inch.

Twelve. The skiff bumped the hull outside; someone aboard it whispered. Anselmo started carrying the weighted bundles up, two at a time, to the deck, to hands reaching down from the skiff.

Six. Five. Four.

On the third-to-last sheet the ink gave out entirely. The impression came down and came up gray as ash, letters barely legible, and Mirabel looked at it and said, "Again," and el Cocinero bled the last of the mixed ink from the roller edges with a knife, scraping it into the well, and they pulled it again and it came up dark enough. Dark enough to read. Dark enough.

Two. One.

The last sheet. Mirabel laid it herself. Her father's thumb-position on the edge, finding the margin by touch, her eyes elsewhere, already seeing the next thing, which was the bundle, the skiff, the city, the dawn. She pulled the bar. The platen descended with its patient iron honesty and pressed two hundred and forty square inches of hand-cut truth into cheap paper, and when the sheet came up she held it a moment before passing it — the last copy of the first thing she had ever printed under her own authority, the ink still wet enough to shine.

"*Finis corona*," Pilar murmured, misquoting something, and laughed at herself.

"Not yet," Mirabel said. "Crowns later. Skiff now."

* * *

The type came apart in ninety seconds. They had rehearsed it: the quoins loosened, the chase lifted whole, wrapped in canvas, chained to a length of scrap iron. El Cocinero walked it to the rail like a pallbearer and dropped it over without ceremony. It went down with a gulp, and the harbor closed over it, and Mirabel watched the spot where it sank until the water looked like any other water. Let the bay keep it. Type is only lead. What the type said was already dry on two hundred sheets, being rowed toward the city in the bottom of a fishing skiff.

She climbed to the deck with the last bundle under her coat. The east was graying. Out across the water the gunboats sat black against the lightening sky, patient as taxes, their searchlights sweeping the moorings — and one of them, Mirabel realized with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have, was beginning, lazily, to turn toward them.

Reyes put her over the side first, into the skiff, among the bundled sheets that smelled of ink and bilge.

"Customs house first," he said quietly, crouching on the rail above her. "Before the market opens. Nailed high, where it can't be torn down fast. Everything else depends on the first hour." He glanced back at the hulk, where el Cocinero was already lighting a cook-fire, filling the condemned ship with the ordinary smell of coffee, an old man making breakfast on an empty boat. "And Mirabel."

She looked up.

"When they ask who printed these — and they will ask for years — give them the name." He tapped the foot of the sheet, where, in small honest capitals she had set herself, it read: *Printed at the free press of the port, by M. Vasquez.* "Not your father's name. Yours. Make them chase the right person for once."

The skiff pushed off. Oars muffled with sacking dipped into black water, and the hulk receded behind them, coffee-smoke rising from its deck, and the nearest gunboat completed its turn and began, at last, to move.

Mirabel put her hand flat on the topmost bundle and watched the city grow out of the dawn ahead of them — the customs house clock just visible against the pale sky, its hands crawling toward six, toward the market, toward the first readers.

"Row," she said.
