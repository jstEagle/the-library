## 20. Before the Century Turns

**MIRABEL**

The last morning of the century smelled of salt and ink.

Mirabel had not slept. None of them had—the six of them aboard the hulk, working through the black hours with the press groaning like something alive, pulling sheet after sheet while Reyes's sympathizers stacked them in oilcloth bundles and lowered them into the skiffs that came alongside in the dark, one after another, silent as fish slipping under a boat.

Her hands were ruined. Two hundred and forty hours of cutting type will do that—thumbs split, fingertips gone soft as wet paper, a crack across her right palm that kept reopening no matter how she bound it. She had learned to hold the burnisher differently. She had learned to keep pulling pages anyway. Somewhere around three in the morning she had stopped feeling her back entirely, and that had felt like grace.

The stack beside the press stood taller than her knee. The *Manifiesto del Pueblo de Vellamare*, set letter by letter by her own hands, printed under her own name at the bottom of every page: *Impreso por Mirabel Vasquez.* Not her father's imprint. Not the shop's mark. Hers.

Reyes appeared at her elbow, holding two cups of something hot and terrible that he called coffee when it suited him. He was gray-faced with exhaustion, his bad hand cradled against his chest where he'd caught it between the platen and the bed on the four-hundredth pull, and still grinning.

"How many?" he asked.

"Enough."

"Say a number. Numbers make men brave."

"Three thousand." She took the cup. It scalded her split palm and she didn't care. "Maybe more. The press stopped counting."

"The press never counts." Reyes looked out through the hulk's rotten gunport at the harbor, which was just beginning to turn from black to iron. "It only repeats."

Out on the water, a gunboat sat at anchor with its running lights burning, exactly where it had sat all night. They had watched it circle twice at dusk and then drop anchor, and everyone aboard had worked faster after that, and no one had said why.

"They know," Mirabel said.

"They suspect." Reyes drank. "There's a difference, and the difference is about an hour long. Go with the skiffs, Miri. Take the first bundle into the city yourself. You should be there when it happens."

"When what happens?"

He smiled the way he did when he was about to say something that sounded like nonsense and wasn't. "When words outrun guns. It doesn't happen often. You don't want to have missed it."

**TOMÁS**

Tomás Vásquez woke before dawn in a government office because he had stopped going home.

They had given him a desk—actually given him, with a brass plate, *T. VÁSQUEZ, SPECIAL ASSISTANT TO THE DIRECTORATE OF PUBLIC INFORMATION*—and a cot in the anteroom, and he had understood both gifts for what they were: furniture for a hostage who hadn't been told yet. But he had made his peace with that arithmetic months ago, or told himself he had, and this morning he sat at the desk with his collar stiff and his eyes gritty, drafting a notice about grain prices, when the first copy came through the door under a clerk's arm.

The clerk didn't walk so much as run slowly, the way people move when they are carrying something dangerous and want to seem casual. He put the paper on the directorate's long table without being asked and stepped back from it as if it might go off.

Tomás read the headline. Then he read the name at the bottom.

*Impreso por Mirabel Vasquez.*

Not their father's shop mark, which would have implicated the family, which was almost certainly the point. Her name alone. His sister had done the one thing he had spent eight months assuring the Directorate could not happen: she had made herself unconfiscatable. You can seize a shop. You can burn plates. You cannot arrest a name that is now on three thousand sheets of paper moving through the city faster than any soldier could follow.

Around him, the office erupted in the particular silence of bureaucrats discovering catastrophe. Someone said *customs house*. Someone else said *the fish market already has them, my cousin sent word*. The Director's door stayed shut, which meant the Director already knew, which meant someone above the Director already knew, which meant by noon there would be orders, and Tomás sat very still at his desk and felt the strangest thing rising in his chest, and it took him a full minute to identify it because he had not felt it since he was a boy watching his father work the press.

Pride. Ruinous, unforgivable pride.

And underneath it, cold and patient as harbor water: *they will come for me now, too.*

He found he no longer minded. That frightened him more than anything else that morning.

**REYES**

The fish carts went out at first light because fish carts always went out at first light, and nobody had ever stopped the sea's business to read a proclamation.

Reyes walked the market road as the sun came up over the breakwater, hands in his pockets, an honest idler among honest idlers, and watched his handiwork distribute itself. Every cart had a false bottom or a hollow rail; he'd built four of them himself out of the hulk's rotten planking. Now the fishermen lifted whole bricks of newsprint out of the bilge water and passed them up to the boys who ran deliveries, and the boys took the stairs two at a time, and by seven o'clock the manifesto was appearing the way frost appears—everywhere at once, no single place you could point to.

Under doors. Inside loaves of bread sold by two bakers who owed Reyes and three who owed Aldana. Tucked into the coils of rope at the chandlers'. Folded into newspapers already printed with the junta's tame notices, so that a man buying the government's words found the other kind inside, like a knife sewn into a coat lining.

At the customs house, two of his crew had worked fast in the gray half-light with a hammer and nine nails, and now the manifesto hung full-length on the customs house door itself, nailed through its corners, facing the square. The customs house. The regime's own front teeth, with the people's words pinned to them.

A sergeant of the municipal guard stood in front of it.

That was the moment, Reyes thought later. That was the hinge of the whole century. Because the sergeant read it. He stood there in the cold with his rifle slung and his breath steaming, and he read the whole thing top to bottom while the market came alive around him and nobody moved, and when he finished he reached up—and paused—and then simply adjusted the corner nail that had pulled loose from the damp, pressed the paper flat again, and turned his back on it and walked away down the arcade.

He left it up. He had orders not to, and everyone watching knew he had orders, and he left it up.

Reyes exhaled for what felt like the first time since dusk.

"Now," he said quietly, to no one, to the century, "we see what they're made of."

**MIRABEL**

She heard the square before she saw it.

Mirabel had come up from the harbor with the first skiff-load, carrying a bundle herself through streets gone strange and quiet, and near the cathedral arch she began to hear a sound she couldn't name at first—a low, rolling murmur, dozens of voices braided together, rising and falling like surf. She followed it into the Plaza del Reloj, where the old clock tower stood counting down the dying year, and stopped dead.

They were reading it aloud.

A crowd of hundreds filled the square—not a mob, nothing thrown or broken, just people, dockworkers and grandmothers and clerks still wearing their office hats—and at the fountain's edge a man Mirabel didn't recognize, a stevedore with rope-scarred hands, held a sheet above his head and read the manifesto in a carrying voice, line by line, while the crowd repeated each sentence after him so it could travel to the back. *That governments derive their authority from the consent of the governed—* and the square answered him, *from the consent of the governed,* and the words rolled off the cathedral stones and up the clock tower and out over the rooftops toward the gunboat at anchor in the harbor.

Someone handed Mirabel a copy. She looked down at the page her hands had made and saw her name at the bottom, and a woman next to her—a stranger, old enough to be her grandmother—touched her sleeve and said, "This Vasquez printer. There's courage, eh? To sign it."

"There is," Mirabel managed.

"My husband printed leaflets in the old troubles, God rest him. They shot him for it." The woman looked at the crowd, at the stevedore's raised sheet, at the children perched on the fountain rim mouthing along. "But look. Look how long it took them. Thirty years, and here are the same words, and now there are too many mouths to shoot."

The clock tower began to strike ten.

The crowd fell quiet for the bells, and in that silence, from the direction of the harbor, came the sound Mirabel had been dreading all night: boats putting off from the warship. Oars, engines, the flat authoritative knock of a hull against a pier. The junta had woken up.

The stevedore folded his sheet with great care, put it inside his coat over his heart, and picked up the next copy.

"Read faster," he said, and the square laughed—a real laugh, defiant and bright—and began again, louder than before, so the soldiers would hear it coming.

**TOMÁS**

They came for him at eleven.

Two men from the Directorate's security section, polite as accountants, with a folder and a car. The younger one had been at his commission ceremony in October. They stood at his desk while the office pretended to work, and the older one opened the folder and said, "Tomás Vásquez. We'll need you to accompany us and provide the current location of your sister."

"You don't know where she is." It wasn't a question. He could hear it in his own voice, the ash of it.

"We know where she isn't," the older man said pleasantly. "Which is in custody. This is regrettable, Tomás. Your file was exemplary. But the Director requires names, and yours appears—" he consulted the folder, savoring it, "—on every copy. Alongside hers. Family name, you understand. The Directorate finds family names clarifying."

Eight months of paperwork, of small betrayals sorted neatly into ledgers, of telling himself the word *necessary* until it stopped tasting like copper. And it had purchased exactly what his father could have told him it would purchase, sitting in the shop with type dust on his sleeves: nothing. A chair. A brass plate. A ride in a car with men who had never intended to keep a single promise, because men who take power by force extend credit at exactly one rate.

Tomás stood, and buttoned his coat, and surprised both escorts by walking to the car without being taken. And in the car, watching the streets slide past—streets where papers were moving hand to hand faster than the guard could confiscate them, where a boy on a corner was selling copies outright for two centavos and no one arresting anyone—he made the decision he had been circling for weeks without letting himself look at it directly.

There was one piece of information still in his possession. One thing the Directorate did not know it needed until it was too late to ask for it.

He had taken the original plates back from their vault weeks ago. They were wrapped in canvas under the floor of a rooming house on Calle Luna, and he was the only living soul who knew it.

The car turned toward headquarters. Tomás smiled at his escorts, cooperative and broken and utterly finished with them, and began to talk about grain prices.

**MIRABEL**

By noon it was over in the way that matters, and beginning in every way that doesn't.

The soldiers cleared the square at eleven-thirty, bayonets fixed, and it took them forty minutes to disperse five hundred people who simply walked away in all directions reading as they went, folding their copies into pockets, handing them to strangers. By one o'clock there were more copies in the city than there had been at eleven. Confiscation had become distribution; every sheet seized from a doorway was read by the soldier who seized it.

Mirabel stood at the harbor's edge in the afternoon light with the wind pulling at her coat, and watched the gunboat's launch return to the mother ship empty-handed, and let herself feel it—not triumph, nothing so clean, but something older and quieter. Her father's voice, from years ago, his hand over hers on the composing stick: *Type is heavier than it looks, mija. Everything true is heavy. That's how you know it will still be here when the shouting stops.*

The words were out. Three thousand copies and multiplying, in bread and bilges and pockets and mouths, and no army in the world could gather them all back. Whatever came next—arrests, reprisals, the new century's unknown weather—the manifesto would be here, still circulating, still speaking, when the men in the Directorate were dust.

The clock tower across the water struck four. Behind her, Reyes came down the pier with his coat collar up and his face doing something complicated.

"Miri." His voice was wrong. Flat, careful, the smuggler's voice for delivering bad cargo. "You need to come with me. There's a launch from the harbor master, and there's smoke coming from the direction of Calle Obispo."

The print shop. Their street.

"And Miri—" Reyes hesitated, and she realized his eyes were wet, which she had never once seen. "They took the boy. The one who ran messages for us. He gave your brother's name, and they've arrested Tomás. An hour ago."

She stood very still, the wind pushing at her, the whole harbor glittering indifferently behind her, and felt both pieces of news land—one grief and one she could not yet name—and understood that the old year was not done taking payment.

"The plates," she said.

Reyes nodded grimly. "If Tomás talked before they took him—if he ever had them—then everything depends on who reaches Calle Luna first."
