## 21. The Cost Tally

They burned the shop on the third of January, when the novelty of the new century had worn thin and the junta needed something flammable to remind Vellamare who held it.

Mirabel watched from the widow's walk above the salt warehouses, four streets back and one story up, close enough to feel the heat arrive in pulses against her face. The soldiers had cordoned off the Calle de la Tinta at both ends, and the neighbors stood behind them in their nightclothes and their coats thrown over, watching the way people watch anything they are powerless to stop — with a terrible, obedient stillness.

The Vasquez Print Shop went up like paper, because that is mostly what it was. Thirty years of job work and broadsheets and wedding invitations and shipping manifests, all of it feeding the fire in a slow roar. She could see the press itself through the front windows for a while, the old Columbian her father had bought before she was born, black iron gone orange in the light, until the roof beams came down and took even that from view.

She had expected to weep. She had braced for it the way you brace for a blow, jaw set, hands fisted in her pockets. But what came instead was stranger: she kept seeing details. The way the smoke bent leftward over the rooftops, toward the harbor, the way everything in Vellamare eventually bent toward the harbor. The way a soldier near the barricade was laughing at something another soldier said, his breath a white plume, an ordinary laugh, the kind Tomás used to make in the kitchen. The way Señora Ferrer, who had bought her funeral notices from Father for twenty years, stood with her arms crossed tight as a knot, not grieving, just cold.

Grief, it turned out, was not one thing. It was an inventory.

* * *

The ledger began, as ledgers do, with numbers she could not make balance.

The shop: thirty-one years. Her father had signed the lease before Mirabel was born, before Tomás, back when he still signed things without reading them twice. She tried to compute it in objects — the type cases, four hundred pounds of lead and oak; the composing stone worn into a shallow valley where ten thousand forms had been locked; the paper cutter whose blade her mother had once chipped slicing bread on because there was no room left cleared on the kitchen table. All of it ash or confiscation now. The junta's inventory clerk, a small precise man with ink-stained cuffs — an irony she would have found funny in another life — had posted the seizure notice on the door two days after the print run, and by then the notice itself was a joke, because everyone in the quarter knew what had come off that hulk's press.

Her father: eleven weeks dead. Or twelve. She had lost count during the worst nights in the warehouses, and now the number mattered and she couldn't recover it. He had collapsed at the dinner table with his hand half-curled around a spoon, and the last full sentence he ever spoke to her had been about type storage — *the long primes go in the second case, girl, they always drift* — and she would carry that sentence to her grave, a piece of shop instruction standing in for everything he'd never managed to say about love or fear or the promise that killed him. She had honored it. He was dead. Both facts were true and neither consoled.

Reyes: imprisoned at the customs house, awaiting transport to the naval prison at Carroña Point, which everyone called the Point and no one called anything else, because saying its full name felt like inviting it. He had been taken on the hulk with his hands raised and something like a smile on his face, covering her escape with the noise of his own surrender, buying her ninety seconds with the rest of his life, or at least some large fraction of it. She owed him a debt denominated in a currency she did not have.

Tomás: arrested yesterday morning.

She had not seen it happen. She had heard it — the boots on the stairwell of his boarding house, the muffled voice of the officer who had eaten dinner at their table twice during the negotiations, the polite paperwork voice. Two of the men he had served. The men whose lies he had set in their father's type. They took him out the front in handcuffs, past the landlady, past the fish boy who delivered to everyone and therefore told everything, and the fish boy told Mirabel within the hour, arriving at the warehouse out of breath, delivering it the way he delivered mackerel: fresh, unavoidable, wrapped in something already going bad.

"He kept saying they made a mistake," the boy reported, almost apologetic. "Kept saying his name was on the commission list."

It was. She had no doubt it was. That was precisely the point. A name on a list meant nothing when the man holding the list decided lists were suggestions. The junta had used Tomás to find the plates, used the plates to print the counterfeit manifesto, and then, when the real manifesto flooded the city anyway and made the counterfeit look like what it was, they needed someone to blame who wasn't themselves. A Vasquez was convenient. A Vasquez who had already proven himself useful and disposable — that was perfect.

She should have felt satisfaction. She searched for it honestly, sitting on a salt crate with her palms pressed flat against her knees, searching herself the way she'd search a type case for a missing character, compartment by compartment. There was anger at Tomás, yes, old and deep and cooled into something mineral. There was grief for the boy he'd been before the junta's men started coming around with their polite offers. But satisfaction — the clean hot taste of *he got what he deserved* — was absent, and its absence troubled her more than its presence would have.

Because if he got what he deserved, then the arithmetic worked, and the arithmetic did not work. Nothing balanced. That was the whole discovery of that cold afternoon: victory does not pay its debts. It simply closes the account and walks away whistling, leaving you holding receipts for things you can never get back.

* * *

She made an actual list. It was a habit of craft, not madness, whatever the warehouse watchman thought as he eyed her scribbling by candlelight. When the world becomes illegible, you set it in order yourself, line by line, and you read what you have written.

**Lost:** the shop. **Lost:** the Columbian press, unrepeatable, its like not to be found in Vellamare under any honest ownership. **Lost:** Father. **Lost:** Mother, longer ago, but the shop's burning had somehow killed her again, a second smaller death. **Lost:** Tomás — status uncertain, possibly recoverable, probably not, and she caught herself writing *probably not* with a steadiness that frightened her. **Lost:** Reyes, recoverable only by miracle or bribery, and she had no money for the first and nothing worth bribing with for the second. **Lost:** the family name, which had been a burden these three months and was now, with the arrest, a brand — though here she paused, pencil hovering, because the manifesto had carried her name at its foot, printed plain, and the same city that spat on *Vasquez* had begun saying *Mirabel* in a different tone entirely.

Names were strange currencies. One had crashed; the other appreciated daily.

**Kept:** the promise. Delivered. Twenty thousand sheets, by Reyes's smugglers' estimate, spread across the city between midnight and dawn while gunboats circled the harbor like patient dogs. Every doorstep in the old quarter. Every fish cart. Nailed to the customs house itself, which she had not authorized but which the rebels had done with evident relish, and which she chose not to disown.

**Kept:** her hands. This sounded like sentiment until you considered it literally. The junta had her brother but not her plates — the original plates, the ones Tomás stole back in his last act of whatever he'd tell himself loyalty was — those sat wrapped in oilcloth under the floor of the salt warehouse, forty pounds of lead sleeping a stone's throw from where she wrote. They had cost her everything and they were intact, every letter her father's messenger had cut, ready to speak again whenever a press existed to give them voice.

**Owed:** Reyes. **Owed:** the truth about Tomás, spoken aloud somewhere, someday, so that it stopped curdling inside her. **Owed:** the future, which had arrived eight days ago wearing the disguise of a new year, and which she had not yet figured out how to live in.

She read the list twice. Then she folded it and put it in her pocket next to the stub of pencil, and she understood, with the flat clarity of someone who has finished counting, that the list changed nothing. You could tally a loss and it stayed lost. The tally was not for the world. The tally was for her — proof she had not looked away, that she would carry the full weight rather than let any of it be quietly mislabeled *collateral* the way governments label things.

Survival, she wrote at the bottom, costs exactly what it protects. No more, no less. Then she scratched it out, because it sounded wise and wisdom was cheap, and what remained was simpler: *Finish it.*

There was still finishing to do.

* * *

On the fifth of January she went down to the harbor at gray dawn, hood up, face averted, one of forty women fetching water at that hour. The customs house squatted at the pier's end, and she walked past it slowly, the way you walk past a hospital window, and looked up.

Third floor, third window from the left. She had asked, in the careful sideways manner of the occupied city, and learned that Reyes was held there, in a room that had been an assay office once. The window was filthy but the light was on, which meant either a lamp or a guard, and she allowed herself ten seconds to stand and look at the rectangle of yellow while the water sloshed against her hip.

He had told her once, on the hulk, in the voice he used for things he meant: *A debt to a smuggler isn't like other debts. We keep accounts longer than banks.* She believed him. She believed, too, that the junta would not keep him long in ordinary custody — he knew the harbor's every hidden channel, every bribeable official, and men like that get transported quickly or quietly drowned, and either way the window would go dark.

Which meant she had days. Perhaps a week.

She turned from the window and walked on, and the plan was already forming, the way a form takes shape on a composing stone — stick by stick, character by character, upside down and backward, illegible to anyone who didn't know how to read it. She could not buy Reyes out. She could not storm the customs house. But the manifesto had done something to this city, and cities in that condition are full of unexpected openings: clerks with consciences, guards with cousins among the rebels, boats that appear at moorings where no boat was tied the night before.

And there was one more asset, unlisted, that she had been circling for days without letting herself name it. The original plates under the floorboards were her father's words, Aldana's words, already spoken. But a press needs more than one text to stay alive. And she had spent nine nights on the hulk cutting type by hand, letter by letter, and her wrists remembered the work, and there was a rusted relic of a press aboard a condemned ship that the rebels knew about and the junta did not quite believe in.

One thing at a time. First the window with the light in it.

Behind her, the smoke from the Calle de la Tinta had finally stopped rising; there was nothing left to burn. Ahead of her, the harbor lay flat and gray and patient, holding its ships and its secrets and its debts, waiting — as the whole ruined, stubborn century ahead of her was waiting — to see what she would set in type next.
