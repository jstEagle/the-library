## 22. Her Own Name

The harbor did not know it was the second of January. It went on the way harbors go on—gulls arguing over fish guts, a crane groaning under crates of tinned sardines, the tide doing its slow arithmetic with the pilings. The world had turned over and pretended nothing had happened.

Mirabel walked down to the water carrying a wooden box the size of a bread loaf, and no one looked at her twice.

That was the miracle of it. Three days ago she had been a fugitive pressed flat against a salt warehouse wall while soldiers read her description aloud from a printed sheet—*the woman Vasquez, printer's daughter, dark hair, small scar above left eyebrow*. Now the junta had bigger fires to fight. The manifesto was everywhere. It had gone out of the harbor like smoke and come back as weather, and a regime that could not control the weather could only stand in it and get wet. There were rumors that Colonel Ferrán had fled inland. There were rumors that he had not fled but had been taken by his own men. Mirabel did not much care which rumor was true. Both meant the same thing: the words had won the first skirmish.

Her scar itched where the wind found it. She touched it without thinking, an old habit, and kept walking.

The hulk still sat where it had always sat, half-sunk at its moorings beyond the breakwater, rust bleeding down its flanks into the green water like old ink. *Providencia*, some clerk had written on a ledger forty years ago, condemned and forgotten. Reyes had called it the most honest ship in Vellamare because it never pretended to sail anywhere. They had taken him off it nine days ago with a soldier's boot on his neck and the half-set type scattering across the deck like dropped seeds. She had run. She had carried what she could and jumped for the breakwater and run, and every step of that flight had been paid for out of someone else's account.

She had not seen him since. No one would tell her anything—not the fishwives, not the sympathetic customs clerk who had once looked the other way for Reyes, not the rebel boys who now swaggered along the quay claiming credit for everything the harbor had done. Reyes had gone into the military prison on the hill like a stone into a well, and wells do not answer questions.

But this morning, when she came around the fish market, the customs clerk had been waiting by the barrels, and without looking at her he had said, quietly, "They moved him yesterday. Out of solitary. He's alive." Then he'd walked away before she could thank him, as if gratitude were also contraband.

Alive. She held the word like a coin she hadn't earned.

The skiff was where she'd left it, tied under the pier among the worm-eaten hulls. She rowed out with the box between her feet, each stroke deliberate, the cold coming up through her palms. The tide was low and honest about it, showing all the hulk's scars—the barnacled waterline, the portholes blind with salt, the great black gash below the stern where the sea had been let in years ago and never fully invited back out.

She climbed the rope ladder they'd used a dozen times in the freezing nights, and stood on the deck of the last free press in Vellamare, alone for the first time since dawn of the first.

* * *

The press was exactly as she'd left it after the final pull: bed cleared, rollers wrapped in oilcloth, the forme broken down and the type distributed back into the cases. Except these cases were not proper cases. They were fish crates lined with felt, divided into compartments by strips of lath, and most of the type inside was hers.

That was the thing people did not understand about printing. Everyone imagined the press was the machine. But the press only pushed paper against ink against type, dumbly, forever. The machine that mattered was the type itself—the little leaden letters, each one carved or cast, each one a decision. You could burn a print shop to the ground, as they had burned hers, and if one tray of letters survived, the truth survived with it, patient as seed.

She had learned that from her father, though she had learned it too late for him to hear her say so.

Papa. Ten days dead, or eleven—she had lost count in the nights of setting type, when the hours blurred together like wet ink. His heart, the doctor said, when Tomás sent word through a neighbor. Or his shame; the doctor was not obliged to distinguish. She had not been able to weep properly, not with the manifesto unfinished, not with soldiers on the streets. Grief had gone into storage like a crate of stock, and she suspected it would sit there for years, surfacing at strange moments—a certain smell of lampblack, the way a man on the quay had laughed last Tuesday, the particular weight of a letter opener in the hand.

Now, standing in the hulk's hold with winter light falling through the open hatch, she opened the box she'd carried down from the warehouses.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, packed in sawdust: the original plates.

Tomás had brought them to her door two nights before New Year's Eve, pale as a man who had rowed across his own grave, and put them in her hands without a word of excuse, because he knew—I know, she thought—that no words would survive the crossing between us. He had stolen them from the junta's print office at real risk, though she suspected the risk was smaller than it looked; the junta's counterfeit manifesto had done its damage and the plates of Aldana's true text were, to them, evidence better destroyed than kept. Still. He had brought them. And then he had sat in the doorway of the warehouse and told her everything—the offer, the three days, the polite paperwork of betrayal—and she had listened the way you listen to a confession you cannot absolve.

"I'll turn myself in," he'd said finally. "Tell them whatever you want."

"They don't want you," Mirabel had said. "They wanted Father's shop and they got it. They wanted the name Vasquez and they burned it. What's left to give them?" She had looked at him a long time. "Go somewhere they've never heard of us. That's the only mercy left, and even I can't grant it. Only geography can."

He'd nodded like a man accepting a sentence. Whether he'd gone she didn't know. She hadn't asked. Some doors you close and do not check the latch.

But the plates were here, warm from her body's heat through the oilcloth, and she unwrapped the first one and saw Aldana's words standing up out of the metal in relief, mirror-wise, the way truth looks when you meet it face to face. Her father had cut these in peacetime, in the fat years, when promising a general that your press would speak was an easy kindness, the kind of promise made at dinner with wine. And he had died owing it, and she had paid it, and the debt was closed.

So why did her hands move past the plates to the type cases?

Because the debt was closed but something else was open. Because a promise kept is not the same as a name claimed. All through those cold nights, pulling sheets by lamplight while gunboats circled the harbor, the colophon at the bottom of every page had read, in her father's typeface, in her father's style: *Printed for the people by the Vasquez Press, Vellamare.* She had set it that way deliberately—let the junta hunt the family name, let the name take the arrows. It seemed right that the name should die usefully, having lived so destructively.

But this morning she had read a copy of the manifesto nailed to the customs house, one of hundreds, and a boy of maybe ten was standing in front of it, sounding out the words, and another man had said to him, "That's the traitors' press, that is," meaning hers, meaning her father's, meaning a name that belonged now to lies as much as truth, because Tomás had set the junta's counterfeit in the same typeface and the city could not tell the hands apart.

The Vasquez Press. A name that had printed both.

Well. Then it was time to sign the work herself.

* * *

She set the case on the press bed and opened the lid, and there it was, the little compartment she had cut for this purpose weeks ago without quite admitting why: empty, waiting, sized for a single line.

She took up her graver and the blank leads—soft metal, unformed, full of permission—and began.

Cutting type by hand is slow as prayer. Every letter is a room you build to hold one sound. She had spent the better part of December learning it, ruining dozens of blanks, her fingers split and stained, before the letters started coming clean. An *e* with its counter cut just so. An *a* that wouldn't fill with ink. Her father had cast type; he had never cut it. This craft was hers, learned out of necessity in a rusted ship, taught by nobody, owed to nobody. That was the point. That was the whole point.

She cut the letters of her name one at a time, breathing between them, listening to the harbor breathe around her.

M. Two valleys and the peaks between, like the hills behind Vellamare.

I. Simple, and easy to underestimate. Everything depends on it standing straight.

R. The hardest curve so far, and she spoiled two before the third came right.

A. Like the *e*, an old friend.

B. E. L.

She stopped before the last letter and looked out the porthole at the gray water. Somewhere under that water or beyond it lay the old century, drowned on schedule, taking with it her father's shop, her mother's grave-marker she'd never see again, the girl she had been who believed that invisibility was safety. Somewhere on shore, Reyes sat in a cell for the crime of helping her, and she could not reach him yet, but she would—she had already begun asking, quietly, through the clerk, through the fishwives, through anyone who owed the harbor a favor. You do not leave your people in wells. That was the other lesson of this year, the one she intended to keep.

And somewhere, maybe, Tomás was on a road out of the country with nothing but his cleverness and his shame, alive, which was more than she'd expected and less than she'd wanted. She did not forgive him. She was not sure forgiveness was hers to give; some debts are owed to the dead, and Papa couldn't collect. But she had his plates in her sawdust and his typeface in her cases, and she supposed that was a kind of answer, though not a kind one.

She cut the last letter.

V. Two lines leaning on each other. That was all it was. That was all any of it had ever been—you lean, someone leans back, and the structure stands long enough to mean something.

She set the letters into the composing stick: M-I-R-A-B-E-L, space, V-A-S-Q-U-E-Z. Then, beneath, smaller, the line she'd been turning over all night: *Prints the truth, and will again.*

She locked the line into the little chase, inked the roller, dampened a single sheet of good paper—the last of Reyes's smuggled stock, saved from the final night—and pulled the bar.

The press spoke. That groan-and-grip, iron saying what iron says. The cylinder rolled, the paper took the ink, and her name came up out of the metal and onto the page, black and exact and hers, not her father's, not the junta's, not Tomás's, not the name the rebels cursed or the junta burned. *Mirabel Vasquez.* A woman's name, a worker's name, a name that had cut its own type letter by letter because no one was going to hand it to her.

She hung the sheet from the hatch hook to dry, where the wind moved it gently, like something reading over her shoulder.

One copy. Not a manifesto this time, not a broadside for the square. Just a signature, a declaration, a keel laid. The first page of whatever came next.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon because work was how she honored things. She cleaned the rollers and oiled the ways and sorted the type back into its compartments, her father's cast letters and her own cut ones side by side, two generations of lead in one case. She pried loose the plate of Aldana's text and considered it—the general's words, her father's promise, the whole terrible arithmetic that had cost a shop and a name and nearly a sister's sanity—and she did not melt it down, though she thought about it. Words were not responsible for all their uses. Neither, she decided, were presses. She wrapped the plate and stowed it deep in the hold, under the ballast, where promises could sleep until they were needed again. The world being what it was, she did not think the wait would be long.

Toward evening the light came low and gold across the harbor, the kind of light that makes even rust look chosen, and she heard the creak of the ladder.

She turned, and there was a head and shoulders rising over the deck edge, thin now, unshaven, moving like a man whose ribs remembered boots.

"Thought I'd find you here," Reyes said. "The clerk finally took my money."

"You're out." Her voice came out strange, half air.

"Out," he confirmed, hauling himself over the rail and standing swaying, taking inventory of himself like cargo. "Bribes work faster than justice in Vellamare. Justice is still waiting for the new government to be finished not existing." He looked at her, at the sheet drying on the hatch hook, at the press gleaming faintly with fresh oil, and something moved across his ruined face that she had never seen there: surprise, almost, almost hope. "What's that say?"

She took the sheet down and handed it to him. He read it slowly—he read slowly, she had learned; the sea teaches eyes differently than books do—and then he looked up at her.

"Your own name."

"My own name."

He handed it back with the care of a man returning a knife handle-first. "So what does Mirabel Vasquez print tomorrow?"

Mirabel looked at the press. At the harbor going copper and rose, the fishing boats coming home, the customs house where ten thousand copies of her father's promise were still nailed and curling in the wind. Somewhere beyond the breakwater the new century was already arriving, indifferent and enormous, full of machines and men who would want to tell everyone what to think.

"Whatever needs it," she said. "Hand me the ink."

He grinned, and went below to fetch it, and the press waited in the last light, hungry, ready, hers.
