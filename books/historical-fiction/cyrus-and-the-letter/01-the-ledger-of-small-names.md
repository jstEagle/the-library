## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The rain came down over the dressing station like something thrown, and Edmund Sterling stood at the doorway with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and watched it hammer the duckboards into the mud. Behind her, thirty-two men lay on canvas cots in a barn that had been a dairy before the war and would be a ruin after it, and eleven of them were sleeping under chloroform's fading kindness, and three of them were dying in the slow, apologetic way that dying men did when they had made their peace with it ahead of schedule.

She went back inside. There was always more to do inside.

"Sterling." Matron Greer's voice carried over the groans without rising; it never rose. "The boy in the corner — the one from this morning's convoy. He's asking for paper."

Edmund crossed the ward, stepping between stretcher handles and the puddles that seeped up through the floorboards no matter how much straw they laid down. The boy — he was twenty at most, though his face had gone old in the way faces did out here — lay with his bandaged arm propped on a folded blanket, and when she knelt beside him he looked past her shoulder as if reading something written on the wall behind her head.

"Paper," he said again. "Please."

She found a pencil stub in her apron pocket and a page torn from an army pamphlet, and she held them while he wrote, because his right hand was the bandaged one and his left shook too badly to manage alone. He printed four words in a laborious schoolboy's hand: *Tell Ada I tried.*

"Ada," Edmund said quietly. "Your sweetheart?"

"My wife." He said it with the fierce embarrassment of a man who expected not to be believed. "Since June. We didn't tell my mother."

"I'll see it gets to her," Edmund said. "I give you my word."

He closed his eyes then, satisfied, as if the word of a nurse at a dressing station three kilometres from the front line was a currency worth anything at all. And perhaps it was. Perhaps that was all anyone had out here — the promises of strangers, made in rooms that smelled of carbolic and rot, kept by people who could not possibly keep them all and kept them anyway.

When he slept, Edmund took the scrap of paper to the small tin box beneath her cot where she stored the things that mattered: letters entrusted to her by men who might not live to post them, a photograph of her mother standing stiffly outside a house in Surrey, and — wrapped now in oilcloth against the damp — her notebook.

* * *

It was a cheap thing, the notebook. She had bought it in Boulogne during her first week in France for a few sous, black oilcloth covers, pages that took ink badly and pencil well. The stationery shop had also sold postcards of ruined cathedrals, which struck her even then as a peculiar commerce, and she had chosen the notebook over the postcards without knowing why.

She knew why now.

She waited until the evening shift change, when Greer took the ward and Sister Applegate dozed in the chair by the stove, and then Edmund sat on the edge of her cot with the notebook open across her knees and turned past the first pages — the ones she had filled early, clumsily, before she understood what she was doing — to the blank leaf at the end.

There were forty-one names in the book already. She knew them the way a priest knows his parishioners, or perhaps the way a gravedigger knows his ground.

Private Albert Finch, 2nd Battalion, Royal Fusiliers. Killed at Hooge, October the ninth. Struck from the battalion roll as *missing* because no officer wished to write home to a widow with the word *obliterated* in it, and so the word *missing* did its quiet work instead, and Mrs. Finch would wait years for a pension the paperwork said she was not owed.

Lance-Corporal Daniel Okafor, Labour Corps, listed in the official record under a number that belonged to another man entirely — a clerk's error, corrected nowhere, because correcting it required someone to admit the error existed, and Private Okafor's family in Stepney had received, in place of a death notice, a letter addressed to a stranger informing a stranger that a stranger was well.

Sapper William Pryce, whose commanding officer had struck him from the rolls as absent without leave after he wandered dazed from a dressing station and was found two days later dead in a shell crater with no wound on him large enough to explain it. Shell shock did not appear in desertion records; desertion did not appear in honourable ones. So William Pryce, who had held a trench mortar position for nine hours under fire, officially existed as a coward and unofficially as nothing at all.

Edmund dipped her pen into the ink bottle she shared with the clerks and wrote the day's name.

Private Thomas Ellery, 8th Norfolk Regiment. She had heard it spoken only once, by a delirious sergeant in the next bed who kept calling for him through the night — *Ellery, Ellery, get your head down* — long after Ellery had been brought in dead, and longer still after the body had been taken out the back door and the paperwork taken out the front. The convoy clerk, hurrying, had recorded the sergeant twice: once wounded, once killed. Two entries, one man. Which meant that somewhere in England a family named Ellery would be told their son was alive and dead in the same week, and the correction, when it came, would come from nobody.

Edmund wrote: *Pte Thomas Ellery, 8 Norfolks. Died 14 November 1917 of wounds. Witnessed by Sgt. J. Meech (convoy). Mother: Mrs. R. Ellery, 12 Chandos Row, Norwich.*

That last line was the whole point. Anyone could record that a man died. What the Army forgot, systematically, cheerfully, was that a man had a mother — that somewhere there was a kitchen where a lamp burned in the window and a woman listened for footsteps that would not come, and would go on listening, unless somebody, somewhere, wrote the address down.

"You're doing it again," said a voice above her.

Edmund looked up. Sister Ruth Applegate stood over her, sixty years old and grey as November, holding two cups of the chicory substitute they drank instead of coffee. Her eyes went to the notebook, and Edmund felt the old flinch, the reflex of concealment, but Ruth had known about the book since the spring and had never once asked to read it.

"Doing what?" Edmund said, though she closed it.

"Carrying other people's dead." Ruth handed her a cup and lowered herself onto the adjacent cot with a sound like a rope settling. "You'll wear yourself thin, girl. Grief is heavier than kit."

"It isn't grief," Edmund said. It was the nearest thing to a lie she told, and they both knew it. "It's bookkeeping. Someone has to balance the account."

"The Army keeps accounts."

"The Army keeps *convenience*." Edmund heard the edge in her own voice and did not soften it. "Ruth, we've got men in this barn who will vanish. Not die — vanish. Their names will go into the ground with them and stay there, and in ten years their mothers will still be writing to the War Office, and some clerk will write back *regret to inform you no further information available*, and that will be the entire sum of a human life. A sentence. A wrong sentence."

Ruth drank her chicory and looked at the stove for a while. Outside, the guns had settled into their evening rhythm, distant and patient, like a heart that would beat forever.

"My husband," Ruth said at last, "went down with the *Hampshire* two years ago. Or the records say he did. They also say his brother did, and his brother is farming in Canada, so you may judge the records how you like." She set the cup down. "Keep your book, Sterling. But keep it where Vane can't find it."

Edmund went cold in a small, specific way. "Captain Vane doesn't inspect us for another fortnight."

"He's coming Thursday." Ruth rose, joints cracking. "Moved up. Nobody says why." At the curtain she paused. "And Sterling — the boy who asked for the paper. His pulse is poor. If you mean to promise things, promise them tonight."

* * *

Thursday came the way Thursdays came: mud first, then grey light, then the smell of the cooks' tea, then Captain Vane's motorcar grinding up the track with its engine complaining louder than the guns.

Edmund had met officers by the dozen in eighteen months, and she had sorted them the way she sorted wounds — superficial, serious, mortal. Vane she had never managed to sort. He was not cruel in any way that could be reported. He complimented the cleanliness of the wards in a voice like warmed butter. He remembered names — hers, Greer's, the orderlies' — and used them precisely, and somehow being remembered by him felt less like courtesy than like inventory. He looked at men the way a quartermaster looked at boots: assessing wear.

Today he walked the ward with Greer at his shoulder, pausing at certain cots, and Edmund followed at a distance with her hands folded, watching him watch her patients. When he reached the boy with the paper — Private Carter, Ada's husband — Vane stopped.

"This one's not on yesterday's intake list," he said pleasantly.

"He came in with Tuesday's convoy, sir," Greer said. "Clerical error at the railhead. I've sent it back for correction."

"Twice this month." Vane smiled at the ward in general, at the rows of bandaged faces pretending to sleep. "The war runs on paper, Matron, and every error is a widow waiting. I do wish your clerks would be careful." He bent slightly over Carter, studying him the way one studied a ledger entry, and moved on.

At the door he turned, and his eyes found Edmund with unerring accuracy, as though he had known where she stood the whole time.

"Sister Sterling." A nod. "They speak well of you."

"They're kind, sir."

"They say you remember everything." Still smiling. "A useful quality. In nursing, and in other matters." Then he was gone, and the motorcar was gone, and Edmund discovered her hands had gone numb inside her gloves.

Greer exhaled slowly through her nose. "Well," she said. "That was almost pleasant."

"Yes," Edmund said. "Almost."

* * *

That night the bombardment to the north changed its tune.

She had learned the guns the way one learned weather, and she knew the difference between the routine drumming of a harassment barrage and this — a deeper note underneath, a rolling of distant thunder that meant something heavy was moving. By ten o'clock the sky above Pilckem Ridge glowed a dirty orange, and the stretchers began arriving before midnight, and the barn filled past capacity until men lay on straw in the aisle and the operating table worked without pause and Edmund's world narrowed to the circumference of a wound: clean it, pack it, bind it, move on, next.

She worked until her arms stopped belonging to her. Somewhere in the small hours she knelt beside a boy of nineteen with his abdomen opened, and held his hand, and he asked her whether it was morning yet, and she said nearly, nearly, and it was not quite a lie when he stopped hearing the answer. She entered his name in the ledger later — *Pte James Wheldon, 11th Borderers* — along with his mother's address in Wigan, written in handwriting that shook harder than her hands had shaken.

Dawn came up the colour of dishwater. The barrage slackened, then stopped altogether, and the silence felt bruised. Edmund stepped out into the yard to breathe air that was not carbolic and blood, and found Greer already there, holding a signal flimsy, reading it a second time as if hoping the words would rearrange themselves.

"What is it?" Edmund asked.

"Evacuation orders." Greer's voice was flat with exhaustion. "The whole forward chain — casualty clearing stations included. They're repositioning the line of evacuation west." She looked up, and her face in the grey light looked older than Ruth's. "I'd expected weeks to prepare. The flimsy says seventy-two hours."

"Seventy-two." Edmund did the arithmetic automatically, the arithmetic one always did: thirty-two patients, eleven stretcher cases, supplies to sort, records to copy, trains to beg space on. It was a great deal, but it was manageable. It was the kind of bad news the war dealt daily, and she had learned to fold it away and begin.

Then Greer added, quietly, "That's not all. Read it yourself."

Edmund took the paper. The type was standard, the language standard, the bureaucratic cadence she knew as well as her own breathing. But the second paragraph contained one word out of place, one instruction that had never appeared in an evacuation order she had seen:

*All non-essential personnel and unattached persons to proceed with the first convoy.*

Unattached persons. Edmund read it three times. There were no unattached persons at a dressing station. Everyone was attached — to a unit, a roster, a paybook, a number. Unless —

Unless someone had decided that a certain category of person should be moved early, and quietly, and had invented a phrase broad enough to sweep for them.

"Matron," she said carefully. "Who counts as unattached?"

Greer took the flimsy back and folded it along its crease, once, twice, precise as surgery. "That," she said, "is exactly the question I put to the signals clerk. And do you know what he told me? He wouldn't say. He just looked at me as though I'd asked him to translate Hebrew, and said the order came from division, and the signature at the bottom—" she tapped the flimsy, "—isn't the usual one."

Edmund looked north, toward the ridge and its dying glow, and thought of Captain Vane's warm voice saying *they say you remember everything*, and thought of her notebook sleeping in its oilcloth beneath her cot, forty-one names deep, and of the forty-second entry she had not yet had time to make.

Seventy-two hours. It had sounded like enough.

It sounded like nothing at all now.
