## 2. What Can Be Carried

The order came at four in the afternoon, typed on a sheet of paper so thin that the words showed through from the back like bruises. *Evacuate within seventy-two hours.* Edmund Sterling read it twice, standing in the doorway of the matron's hut with rain dripping off her cap brim and down the back of her collar, and felt nothing so much as relief — and then, immediately behind the relief, shame at feeling it.

Seventy-two hours. The dressing station near Ypres had stood for eleven months, its sandbags furred green with damp, its roof of corrugated iron singing whenever the guns spoke too near. Eleven months of blood soaked into floorboards that would never come clean, of names learned and lost, and now the front had shifted and the war required the ground more than it required them. They were to pack what they could carry and burn what they could not.

She folded the order into quarters and put it in her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip all evening like a small cold coin, and she went about the rest of her shift with the peculiar lightness of a woman already halfway gone. It was strange, she thought, how quickly a place became provisional the moment you were told to leave it. She had slept in that hut for nearly a year and had complained about the draught under the door every night of those months; now she caught herself looking at the door almost fondly, the way one looks at a fault in a person one is about to bury.

At midnight her shift ended and she walked back through the duckboards to the nurses' quarters, past the tents where the wounded breathed in chorus — some snoring, some moaning, one man near the far end counting aloud to ten, over and over, in a voice worn smooth as river stone. She had stopped asking why he counted. Some arithmetic was private.

Her quarters were half of a requisitioned farmhouse room shared with two other nurses, both asleep, both breathing the slow breaths of women who had learned to sleep through anything. Edmund lit the stub of candle on the windowsill, shielded it with an enamel bowl, and took her kit bag down from the nail behind the door.

It was an army kit bag, olive drab, stained at one corner with something she preferred not to identify, and it held — when emptied onto her cot — the whole portable sum of her life at twenty-nine years of age. She sat cross-legged on the blanket and looked at the pile the way a merchant looks at stock he must appraise honestly: two spare uniforms, both patched at the elbows; four sets of underthings, laundered to a grey so pale they were nearly the colour of surrender; stockings darned until the darning was more stocking than original wool; her boots, which went on her feet and therefore did not count among the choices; a tin cup; a folding knife her father had put in her hand at the dock in Southampton three years ago, saying only, *You'll want this more than you'll want a photograph*; a photograph anyway, of her mother and father outside their house in Cheltenham, squinting into a sun that no longer existed anywhere in the world except inside that piece of card; a Bible she never read but could not imagine abandoning, for reasons she had never examined; a bar of soap, hoarded, wrapped in a handkerchief; a hairbrush missing four teeth; needles and thread; a small tin of aspirin; and, at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, the notebook.

The ledger. Though it was not really a ledger. It was a cheap notebook, five inches by seven, its cover soft with handling, bought in a stationer's in Poperinghe in the first week of her posting because she had needed somewhere to copy drug dosages and had found, within a month, that it wanted to hold other things.

She unwrapped it now, out of habit rather than need. She knew every page.

The first name was Corporal Alfred Wren, Royal Fusiliers, admitted with a chest wound on the ninth of March, dead by morning — and struck from the regimental roll, she later discovered, as *wounded, missing*, because the clerk who processed him had made an error of date that could not be corrected without paperwork, and paperwork, in that season, flowed in one direction only. Alfred Wren had a mother in Deptford who would receive a form saying *missing* — a word that meant hope, that meant years of waiting by a door — when Edmund had held his hand while he went. So she wrote his name, his rank, his wound, the hour of his death, and beneath it: *He asked whether the cherries were ripe yet in Kent. I told him yes.*

After Wren there came others. Thirty-one names by now, thirty-one men erased by clerical error, by administrative convenience, by the great grinding indifference of an army that had more dead than it had ink. Some entries were long. Some were only a name and a sentence, because there had been no time or because the man himself had left her nothing else. Private Daniel Okafor, who sang *Abide With Me* in Igbo while she cleaned shrapnel from his shoulder, and who survived, though his name vanished from his battalion's records as if the earth had opened under his paperwork instead of under his feet. Sergeant Bell, who died believing his paybook had been found and returned to his wife, and which had in fact been burned with the rest of the tent's contents after the shell came through. She had written them all. She had written them because someone must, because a person unrecorded was not merely lost but *unmade*, and she had come to believe — quietly, fiercely, in the small hours between shifts — that the universe kept its own accounts only if human beings kept them first.

She wrapped the ledger again and set it apart from the pile, the way one sets aside the one true thing on a table of trinkets.

Then she began the sorting.

There was a discipline to it that she found oddly comforting, the same comfort she took in setting a fracture: a problem with clear rules and a definite end. The kit bag would hold perhaps twenty pounds comfortably, thirty if she was willing to break her own back over miles of duckboard, and she had learned long ago that a nurse who arrived exhausted helped no one. Twenty pounds, then. Everything weighed against everything else. Two uniforms or one? One, and wear the other layered. The Bible or the photograph? Both — she measured them together against the soap and chose faith and family over cleanliness, and smiled at herself for framing it so grandly when what she had actually chosen was six ounces of paper over four ounces of fat.

This was what the war taught, if you let it: that a life reduced to its essentials was shockingly small. That most of what a person accumulated was scaffolding around a few load-bearing things. That love, whatever the poets said, weighed almost nothing and occupied no space at all, and yet was the heaviest item in any bag ever packed.

She heard one of the other nurses turn in her sleep and paused, candle-shielded, a still figure in a dark room, until the breathing evened again.

* * *

The letters lived in the bottom of her footlocker, and it was for them that she had saved the last hour of the night.

There were forty-one of them. She counted them first, as she always counted them, because counting was a kind of tending. Letters from home: her mother's looping hand filling both sides of thin paper with parish news, recipes adapted for rationing, reports of neighbours' sons — *poor Mrs. Ashworth's boy is posted missing* — each line a small application of pressure to a bruise Edmund had long since stopped letting show. Her father had written only twice since she sailed, both times briefly, both times signing off *Your loving Father*, the words set down with the careful formality of a man handling something explosive. There was a bundle from her sister Alice, married now these six years, mother of two, whose letters arrived monthly and described a world — jam-making, school fees, a dog with a bad leg — so remote from this place that reading them was like pressing an ear to a wall and hearing a party on the other side.

And beneath all of them, tied with the ribbon off a Christmas box from 1914, the letters she never answered. From Thomas.

Thomas Ellery, who had proposed to her on the platform at Cheltenham Spa station with the train already steaming, so that his proposal had been partly drowned in engine noise and she had been able to pretend, boarding, that she hadn't entirely heard it. Thomas, who wrote faithfully every fortnight, whose letters were kind and steady and full of a future laid out like a table: the house on Bath Road, the position at his uncle's firm, the garden, the children sketched in prospect with such specificity that she sometimes felt she had already failed them. Thomas, whom she did not love — not in the way the word demanded — and who deserved better than a wife who had discovered, out here amid the unmaking of the world, that she was built of stranger timber than the house on Bath Road required.

She had known for months that she would have to answer him. She had drafted the answer in her head a dozen times, on night shifts, walking between cots: *Dear Thomas, you deserve a woman who can be carried in a photograph...*

Now the order to evacuate sat in her pocket, and the arithmetic changed.

Seventy-two hours. A move to a forward station, the adjutant had said, closer to the new line. Forward. Everyone said *forward* as though it were a direction one could approve of. And forward meant shells that arrived with less warning, meant the possibility — spoken of by no one, considered by everyone — that a kit bag might one day be all that reached England, forwarded by a stranger with a form and a rubber stamp.

She could not carry forty-one letters forward. She could not risk Thomas's future, her mother's grief, Alice's children's faces falling into the hands of some clerk's bonfire. And more than that — she admitted it now, sitting on the cold floor with the footlocker open and the candle guttering — more than that, she could not carry them because carrying them meant remaining the woman they had been written to. The woman who would come home. The woman who might, if pressed, have said yes to Bath Road out of sheer exhaustion.

The stove in the corner of the room had been lit at dusk and still held a bed of embers. She fed two of them carefully from the scuttle, coaxed the fire awake, and knelt before it with the bundles in her lap like a penitent with her sins laid out for confession.

She burned Alice's letters last, and wept while they curled, and could not have said precisely why — only that Alice's jam and Alice's dog and Alice's bad-legged ordinary life were the last proof that such a life existed anywhere, and burning them felt less like travelling light than like closing a door on a lit room and walking into the dark.

Her mother's letters went next, each one read a final time, fast, the way one looks at a face before a train leaves. *Poor Mrs. Ashworth's boy is posted missing.* Edmund thought of Alfred Wren, first name in the ledger, missing on a form in some War Office drawer while his mother in Deptford waited by a window, and thought: *I am the only record of you, Alf. You are safe. You live in oilcloth.*

When the letters were ash she stirred the fire and scattered what remained, and washed her hands in the basin although there was nothing on them, and felt — not lighter, exactly. Emptied. The way a field looks after harvest: stripped, but ready for whatever comes next.

She packed the kit bag by candlelight with a surgeon's economy. Uniform, underthings, stockings, needle and thread, knife, cup, soap, aspirin, brush. The photograph went into the breast pocket of her uniform, over her heart, where she could feel its small stiff rectangle when she bent. The Bible went in last but one.

The ledger went in last, wrapped in its oilcloth, tucked into the corner where the bag's seam gave it a kind of shelter, and she pressed her palm flat against the canvas over it for a moment, the way one presses a hand to the flank of a horse before a hard ride.

Thirty-one names. Whatever else the war took from her in the days ahead, it would not take those.

* * *

Dawn came up the colour of dishwater, and with it the sound of the station packing itself for flight.

All that day the dressing station dismantled itself like an animal shedding a skin. Stretcher-bearers carried crates to the lorries in the lane; the dispensary was inventoried and boxed; the operating theatre's instruments went into oiled canvas roll after oiled canvas roll, each with a sound like a sword being drawn. The chaplain burned his sermons. Matron Greer moved through it all with her watch in her hand and her voice pitched at exactly the level that permitted no argument, directing the demolition of eleven months' work with the unsentimental efficiency that Edmund had privately decided was the truest form of love the army had to offer. You did not weep over a hospital. You packed it.

Edmund worked alongside the others, and worked well, and no one looking at her would have guessed that she was saying goodbye to a doorframe here, a hook on a wall there, the particular view from the particular window where she had stood at two in the morning on the worst night of last June and watched the flares go up over the ridgeline like a city burning on the horizon. Grief, she had found, was mostly conducted in silence and in passing. Nobody organized it. It simply attended you while you lifted boxes.

By late afternoon the wounded who could travel had gone ahead by ambulance convoy, and those who could not had been made ready for the morning train. The station stood half-gutted, its rooms suddenly enormous with emptiness, echoing in a way rooms should not echo. Rain had begun again around three o'clock, a thin persistent rain that turned the duckboards greasy and made the lamplight hazy, and beyond it, far off to the northeast, the guns kept up their irregular conversation — not the urgent stutter of an assault, but the idle, terrible murmur of a war that continued regardless of timetables.

Supper was bread, cheese, tea brewed from leaves used so often they gave the water little more than a memory of flavour. Edmund ate standing in the doorway of what had been the mess, watching the lories load by lamplight, and felt the strange double exposure of a person present in a moment and already remembering it.

Tomorrow they marched to the rail head. Tomorrow this place stopped being a hospital and became only a place.

She finished her tea, rinsed her cup in the rain barrel, and went down the passage toward the store room to check the final inventory — her last task, assigned by Matron, and she was glad of it, glad of any reason to walk once more through every room and look at everything.

That was when she realized how quiet it had become. The convalescent tents emptied, the walking wounded gone, the theatre dark. Just the skeleton staff for one more night, a dozen souls rattling around in a building built for two hundred. The rain on the corrugated roof. The far guns.

She was halfway along the passage, lamp raised, when the guns stopped.

Not diminished. *Stopped.* The sudden silence was so complete it had texture, a pressure against the ears, and she stood motionless in the corridor with the lamp trembling very slightly in her hand and understood, in the animal way one understands such things, that silence at the front was never peace. Silence was an inhalation.

Then the first shell fell close enough to make the walls lean, and the plaster came down in white ropes from the ceiling, and in the confusion of dust and shouting and the second impact and the third, above the crack of timber and the ring of the corrugated roof being struck like some vast gong — above all of it, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the main entrance —

a knocking. Small. Persistent. The knock of a fist no bigger than a pear, beating on a door at the edge of the war.
