## 3. A Knock at the Door of the War

The shelling began an hour before dusk, and by the time the light had gone the colour of old linen, Edmund Sterling had stopped counting the guns.

She had learned, in eleven months of dressing stations and casualty clearing posts, that there were two kinds of bombardment. The first was the kind you counted — shells arriving with their own terrible punctuation, each report a full stop in a sentence nobody would ever finish. The second was the kind that simply became weather, constant as rain, so that the mind surrendered its arithmetic and gave itself over to work. Tonight was the second kind. She preferred it, in the way one prefers any misery that allows for efficiency.

The marquee above her breathed. That was the only word for it — the canvas walls swelling inward under pressure waves and exhaling again, the guy-ropes singing their thin high note like a woman humming to keep herself calm. Beneath the breathing tent, thirty men lay on stretchers laid over trestles, and Edmund moved among them with her basin and her forceps and her small, unsentimental hands.

"Sterling." Sister Calloway's voice came from the far end, raised not in panic but in that flat carrying tone nurses developed, a voice pitched to ride over shellfire without ever seeming to shout. "The abdominal case is going. I want you ready."

Edmund was already ready. She had been ready since four o'clock. She bent over Private Netherby, whose abdomen had been opened by a piece of shell casing no larger than a thumbnail, and watched his eyes while she worked — because his eyes were all he had left to speak with, and they were asking her the question every dying man asked, which was not *am I dying* but *will someone know I was here.*

"Yes," she said quietly, answering what he hadn't asked aloud. "I've got your name."

His lips moved around the tube. His hand found her sleeve and gripped it with the last of a soldier's strength, and Edmund held still and let him hold on, because there was nothing else in her kit that could do him more good.

He died at seventeen minutes past six, between one breathless moment of the barrage and the next, so that even the guns seemed to pause out of courtesy. Edmund closed his eyes and stood for a moment with her palm resting over his chest — not prayer exactly, though she supposed it might look like prayer to anyone watching. It was registration. It was saying: *you existed, Private Alfred Netherby, 2nd Battalion, aged twenty-two, and someone wrote it down.*

Later, when the worst of the intake had passed through, she would take out the notebook.

* * *

The ledger lived inside her left boot-top during the day and beneath her rolled blanket at night — a cheap cloth-covered notebook bought in Folkestone before embarkation, its pages ruled for accounts but filled instead with names. Not official names. Official names belonged to the Army; the Army kept them in ledgers of its own, in offices far from the mud, and sometimes — this was the thing Edmund had discovered early and never quite forgiven — the Army lost them. A man killed outright and unidentifiable went into a ledger marked *Unknown*. A man who wandered off concussed and was shot as a deserter went into no ledger at all. A man whose papers burned with the rest of him became, administratively speaking, a person who had never existed.

Edmund refused to permit it. It was not defiance in any shape anyone would recognise; she did not argue with officers or deface documents. She simply wrote things down. *Netherby, Alfred, 2nd Bn., d. 14 Nov., six o'clock, holding my sleeve.* Small names, small facts, entered in a hand so neat it looked almost obedient.

Tonight, after the intake had thinned and the lamps had been trimmed low, she drew the notebook out and sat on an ammunition crate near the entrance flap, writing by the light that leaked from the operating tent. Around her the barrage had settled into its long grumbling conversation, close enough now that the ground flinched with each arrival and fine grit sifted down through the canvas like black snow.

She had written three entries when she heard the knock.

It was such an absurd sound that at first she took it for a shell fragment striking a stretcher pole. Three taps, spaced and deliberate, at the wooden post beside the entrance flap — the way a person knocks at a door when they have manners and are frightened. Nobody knocked at a dressing station. They arrived screaming, or carried, or crawling, or not at all.

Edmund put the notebook away and went to the flap.

The wind outside carried smoke and the smell of churned earth, and against the flare-light from the ridge she saw first something small, then smaller still — a figure standing at the edge of the duckboards, barely higher than the sandbags stacked along the entrance, motionless in the way that only children and animals are motionless, as though stillness itself were a hiding place.

"Hello?" Her voice came out wrong — too loud, too much the nurse's voice, and she softened it. "Hello. Are you hurt?"

The figure did not move. Flares rose and fell behind it, so that it was lit and darkened, lit and darkened, like something flickering at the edge of a bad dream. Edmund stepped out onto the boards, crouching to make herself smaller, less uniform, more human. The cold bit through her skirt. Somewhere to the east a shell found something soft and the ground shuddered.

Then the child walked toward her.

He came carefully, placing his feet on the boards as if the boards might be water, and Edmund saw him whole in the flare-light: a boy, perhaps five years old, maybe younger — war-aged children were hard to measure, hunger made them young and gravity made them old. He wore a coat too large for him, wool once good, now stiff with dried mud. His face was pale and perfectly clean, which struck her as stranger than dirt would have — someone had washed this child, recently, deliberately. Under one arm he clutched a leather satchel with both arms wrapped around it the way other children clutch blankets or dolls, and his knuckles were white where they gripped the strap.

His eyes went past her shoulder, into the glowing mouth of the marquee, then back up to her face. He did not cry. That was the thing that undid her, later, thinking of it: he did not cry. Children lost in shelling cried. This one had gone past crying into some country Edmund recognised from the faces of men brought in with their minds broken — the country of people who had seen the worst thing and were now waiting to see what came after.

"You're alright," she said, and heard how useless it was, and said it anyway. "You're alright. Come inside, out of the cold."

When she extended her hand he looked at it, considered it, and did not take it. Instead he walked past her into the tent, small boots ringing on the boards, and stopped just beyond the entrance where the lamplight reached, and stood there gripping his satchel and looking at the rows of wounded men with an expression no five-year-old should own.

* * *

Regulations, Edmund knew, were unambiguous on the subject of children. Field hospitals admitted military personnel and, under specific emergency provisions, civilian casualties requiring immediate treatment. They did not admit stray children. A child found wandering near the line was to be reported to the provost, logged, and passed along the chain toward whatever refugee machinery existed two and three echelons back — machinery that, everyone knew, ran slow and rough and lost things.

She thought about regulations for approximately four seconds, standing in the entrance with the cold pouring in behind her.

"Matron's going to have my hide," she murmured, and dropped the flap.

Inside, the boy had not moved. Sister Calloway glanced over from across the aisle, eyebrows rising above her mask. "Sterling? What in God's name—"

"He came to the door." Edmund was already kneeling, unwinding her own scarf, winding it around the boy's neck where the too-large coat gaped. Up close he smelled of soap and woodsmoke and underneath both something faintly chemical that she chose not to identify. "Anyone claim him? Did anyone come through the intake with a child?"

"Nobody. Sterling, you can't—"

"I know what I can't do." She looked up at Calloway over the boy's head, and something in her face must have carried, because Calloway exhaled through her nose, muttered something about the end of the world arriving one absurdity at a time, and turned back to her patient. That was the thing about Calloway: her objections were real but never fatal.

Edmund guided the boy to the relative quiet behind the screen at the end of the ward, where the supply crates stood, and sat him down on a folded blanket. He permitted all of it — the sitting, the scarf, her fingers checking his ears and throat and the quick professional inventory of limbs for injury. He was unhurt. That fact, simple as it was, felt enormous. In a place where everything that arrived was damaged, here was one intact thing.

"Hungry?"

At that his eyes came alive, briefly, and he nodded — one sharp dip of the chin, a soldier's nod, and Edmund's chest tightened at it. She fetched condensed milk and a hard biscuit, crumbled the biscuit into the milk to soften it, and watched him eat with a control that was worse than greed: careful spoonfuls, no spilling, the eating of a child who had learned that food could be taken away.

When the bowl was empty he wiped his own mouth with the back of his wrist and returned immediately to his satchel, gathering it into his lap.

"That yours, is it?" she asked gently. "What's in the bag?"

Nothing. Not a shake of the head, not a shrug. Nothing at all. And Edmund realised, with a small cold settling in her stomach, that he had not made a single sound since she'd found him. No whimper at the shellfire that was loud enough to make grown orderlies flinch. No cry when a stretcher-bearer jostled past. The barrage rolled overhead and the tent breathed and the boy sat in its noise with his mouth closed like a purse drawn tight.

"Mute," she whispered, mostly to herself. Or struck dumb. Or simply done with speaking, the way some people were done with walking after what walking had cost them.

It was when she leaned in to check his collar, looking for a name-tape, a label, anything, that she felt the stiffness in the coat's inner pocket. She should not have looked. She told herself afterwards that any nurse would have looked — that identifying effects was procedure — but she knew, even as her fingers drew out the folded paper, that this was something else. This was the beginning.

It was a note, written in French, in a hand that had been elegant once and was now hurried, the ink blotted where the writer had pressed too hard:

*I cannot protect him longer. Those who want what he carries will kill us both for it. Please — get him from the country, anywhere, England, anywhere. Forget this face. Do not try to find me. FORGET THIS FACE.*

Below the words, a name and address had been started — *Madame* — and then scratched out so violently the paper had torn.

Edmund read it twice. The barrage went on talking its terrible talk beyond the canvas. The boy watched her read, and she saw his small hands tighten on the satchel until the leather creaked, and understood that he knew what the paper was. Whatever was in that bag, whoever had washed his face and dressed him in a good coat and walked him through a bombardment to a hospital door — the boy knew the price of it, and had chosen, at five, to keep holding on.

Edmund folded the note and slid it back into the pocket. Then she did something she could not afterward account for: she reached out and smoothed the hair back from his forehead, this child delivered to the door of the war like a parcel, and said, softly, in English he almost certainly could not understand:

"Nobody's forgetting anything. Not tonight."

He stared up at her. And in the flare-lit dark of his eyes there was something that was not trust yet, but might have been its first faint sketch.

She sat down beside him on the blanket, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and together, in silence, they listened to the guns.
