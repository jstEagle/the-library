## 4. The Boy Who Would Not Speak

He did not cry. That was the first thing Edmund noticed about him in the daylight, and it frightened her more than weeping would have.

She had known men to come off the stretchers silent — the ones with the faraway eyes who looked through her as though she were glass — and she had learned to fear that silence more than any scream. Screams could be soothed. Silence was a country with no roads into it. The boy sat on the end of an empty cot near the stove, his legs too short to reach the floor, the leather satchel crushed against his chest with both arms, and he watched the ward the way a sparrow watches a field it must someday cross.

The note was still folded in her apron pocket. *Forget this face.* She had read it eleven times by morning, turning it over as though the back of the paper might confess something the front withheld. It had not. It was ordinary paper, torn from a cheap memorandum book, written in a hurried hand that had tried to disguise itself and half succeeded — the letters of someone educated pretending to be unlettered, or unlettered pretending composure. She could not decide which troubled her more.

"Matron will want to know what we're doing with it," said Nurse Prewitt at her elbow, low. "The authorities, I mean. There's a procedure for found children."

"There is," Edmund agreed, and did not move toward any procedure.

"You know what happens to found children, Miss Sterling. They go to the gendarmerie at Poperinghe, and from there to—" Prewitt waved a hand that took in Belgium, the war, the whole indifferent machinery of Europe. "Whoever claims them. If anyone does."

Edmund looked at the boy. He had a fine-boned face under the grime, dark smudges beneath his eyes like thumbprints of exhaustion, and a small cut healing along his jaw that someone had, at some point, bathed and dressed — the plaster was clean, hospital-issue, applied with care. Someone had tended this child recently. Someone had then walked him through a bombardment and left him at a door with instructions to forget him.

"He's not staying," Edmund said, which was true, and meant nothing, and bought her an hour.

* * *

She named him at noon, though she told no one but herself.

It happened the way these things happen — absurdly, in the middle of something else. She had brought him bread softened in milk, and he would not take it from her hand, so she set it on the cot beside him and turned to answer Corporal Finch about a dressing, and when she turned back the bread was gone and the boy was chewing, watching her all the while, wary as a thief. A crumb had lodged at the corner of his mouth. She almost smiled, and the smile needed somewhere to go, and she heard herself say, "Well, Cyrus, you're hungrier than you let on."

Cyrus. She had no idea where it came from. Not a family name; hers was Sterling, her mother's had been Ashdown, and no cousin or uncle or story carried anything like it. Perhaps a book. Perhaps something read years ago and forgotten until this child's stubborn silence called it up out of her. It didn't matter. A nameless thing could be handed over without ceremony; a named thing could not, quite. She understood what she had done even as she did it, and did it anyway.

He did not respond to the name. But he swallowed, and blinked at her once, slowly, the way a cat grants absolution, and she counted it.

* * *

Winning the trust of a mute five-year-old, Edmund discovered over the following two days, was a discipline not unlike nursing: it ran on patience, observation, and the strict suppression of one's own urgency.

She learned his geography first. The satchel never left his reach — not for washing, not for sleeping, not when he finally permitted himself to be lifted onto the cot at night and lay rigid until exhaustion took him. If she came within arm's length of it, his whole body tightened like a fist. Once, when Prewitt, meaning kindly, reached to move it aside so she could change his cot linen, the boy made a sound — not a word, not a cry, a single raw exhalation, the ghost of a scream strangled at birth — and every head in the ward turned. After that they worked around the satchel as sailors work around a live shell.

She learned his fears. The whistle of incoming shells made him flatten himself against whatever was nearest, eyes shut, hands over his ears but the satchel dragged protectively under his chin even so, which struck Edmund as the most terrible arithmetic she had ever witnessed: a child dividing his small strength between terror and guardianship. She learned that he feared boots most of all — the heavy tread of officers crossing the ward made him go still and pale in a way shells did not — and that he feared having his coat removed, though the coat itself was filthy past salvaging. She did not insist. There were battles worth choosing, and she had begun keeping a private tally of which ones were hers.

And she learned what drew him. Not toys; the ward had few enough of those and he ignored them. Not songs; Prewitt hummed to her patients and it made the boy retreat behind his eyes. What drew him was work. Hands doing useful things. He watched her fold bandages with an attention that bordered on devotion, and on the second evening, when she sat near the stove rolling lint in the last grey light, she felt rather than saw him slide from his cot and cross the floor and settle himself a careful foot away, close enough to see, far enough to flee.

She did not look at him. She rolled lint, and tore it, and rolled more, and after ten minutes a small hand appeared at the edge of her vision and took a strip of cloth and began, clumsily, furiously seriously, to imitate her.

They worked side by side until the lamp had to be trimmed. It was, she would remember afterward, the first whole hour since Ypres began in which she had not been afraid.

* * *

On the third day she washed his hair.

It required strategy worthy of a staff officer. She could not touch his head without his permission and could not ask for it in words he trusted, so instead she knelt at the basin herself, in view of his cot, and washed her own hair with the flat yellow soap the army issued, making a production of it, grimacing at the taste of soap-smell, wringing it out with theatrical complaint. He watched with an expression she was learning to read — the faintest loosening around the mouth that served him for amusement.

Then she heated a second basin and set it on a stool beside her own chair, and waited.

It took him the better part of an afternoon. He circled the arrangement twice, retreated, returned, stood at a distance studying her face for the trick in it. She kept her eyes on her mending. At last, in the way of all children, want overcame caution: he climbed into the chair, gripping the satchel strap across his chest, and bowed his head over the basin like a very small old man at prayer.

His hair, once the mud was coaxed out of it, was brown, and curled at the nape when wet. His scalp, when her fingers worked the water through, bore a healed scratch, and behind his left ear a faded bruise gone the colour of old butter. Neither recent. Both noted, and filed in the ledger behind her ribs where such things went.

"There," she said quietly, wrapping his head in the warm towel. "That's the worst of it done, Cyrus."

At the name, this time, his eyes came up and met hers through the towel's frame. Not understanding — she did not flatter herself — but recognition. The sound belonged to her voice and to him. It was his now, whether he had asked for it or not.

He held her gaze for three full seconds. Then he looked away, and it was over, and it was enormous.

* * *

That night the bombardment came close again.

The dressing station was a requisitioned schoolhouse, and its walls had opinions about artillery; each distant concussion sifted a fine rain of plaster-dust from the ceiling, and the nearer ones made the window shutters shudder in their frames. The wounded moaned in their sleep or slept with the terrible stillness of the deeply exhausted. Edmund made her rounds with a shaded lamp, checking pulses, easing dressings, and on her way back down the ward she passed the boy's cot and found it empty.

The fear went through her like cold water. She found him in thirty seconds — he had not gone far — wedged into the gap between the stove and the wall, knees drawn up, satchel locked in his lap, eyes enormous in the lamplight. He had not run because running would have meant leaving the satchel's weight behind or carrying it, and he could not do either. So he had simply removed himself to the smallest space available and become part of the architecture.

Edmund lowered herself to the floor beside him. Her knees cracked; she was twenty-six and felt sixty. The shells walked their slow line across the darkness, closer, then further, the way weather moves, impersonal and vast.

"When I was small," she said, conversationally, to the stove, "I was frightened of thunder. Properly frightened. My mother used to say the angels were moving furniture." She pulled the blanket she'd brought across both their laps, including him in it without touching him. "I never believed her about the angels. But I liked being told. That was the real trick of it, I think. Not the explanation. Being told."

The boy said nothing, of course. But slowly, in increments, the rigidity went out of his shoulders, and by degrees he tipped sideways until his shoulder rested against her arm — the lightest pressure, the weight of a bird — and stayed.

She did not move until the shelling moved elsewhere, and not much even then.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Prewitt came to find her at the washstand, wringing out a cloth, and spoke the sentence Edmund had been waiting four days to hear and dreading all the same.

"The gendarmerie sent word. About found persons. They'll do a collection run through the forward stations Thursday week."

Thursday week. Eight days. Eight days in which a child could become a file number, a photograph, a line in a register, transferred to some institution in Rouen or forgotten entirely in a warehouse of displaced lives — or worse, if whoever had written *forget this face* came looking, if the collection run contained not only gendarmes but a man in civilian clothes with a memorandum-book hand and questions. Edmund wrung the cloth until her knuckles whitened.

"We'll see," she said.

Prewitt gave her a long look, the look of a woman who had shared a dugout with someone long enough to smell a decision before it was spoken. "Miss Sterling. That satchel he won't put down. You've wondered what's in it."

"I haven't," said Edmund, which was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and Prewitt went back to her patients and let it stand.

But the truth was that she had wondered from the first hour, and the wondering had grown teeth. A note instructing her to forget a child's face was not the debris of war; it was a transaction, and transactions have parties, and parties have reasons. The satchel was old leather, good quality once, worn soft at the straps — not an orphan's scavenged thing but a kept thing, maintained, loaded. The boy guarded it the way messengers guard dispatches. The way people guard evidence.

She thought of the clean plaster on his jaw. Of the disguised handwriting. Of a house somewhere with a nursery and a basin and careful hands, and then a road, and a door, and *forget.*

* * *

That evening, the offering came.

She found it when she returned from evening rounds: laid on her cot with a precision that undid her — squared to the blanket's edge, centered, deliberate. A square of paper, folded once. On it, in charcoal stolen no doubt from the stove-blackened fingers of some orderly, was a drawing.

A woman's face. Young, drawn with the wobbly earnestness of a small hand and yet unmistakable — high cheekbones, dark hair swept back, eyes rendered as two careful circles with darker centres, watching. Around the edges of the paper the charcoal had been pressed hard and rubbed outward, so that the face floated in a border of black, as though it emerged from smoke. As though it were burning.

Edmund sat down slowly on the edge of her cot.

The boy stood at the end of the ward by the stove, not watching her, ostentatiously not watching her, the studied indifference of a child who has given a gift and cannot bear to learn its fate. His arms were crossed. The satchel hung from his shoulder as always.

She looked at the face again. At the smoke-dark margins. At the two careful eyes that had watched someone, sometime, and remembered.

"Well then," Edmund said softly, to the drawing, to the boy, to the ledger behind her ribs where names and faces were kept against the world's forgetting. "You needn't speak. We'll manage. Between us we've got enough words."

She folded the paper with great care and put it inside the front of her uniform, over her heart, and looked up, and the boy was watching after all, and something in his posture had changed — a settling, a loosening, the first degree of a trust that might, given time and luck and eight days, become everything.

Across the ward, the door opened, and Matron Greer came in out of the cold with the wind still on her cheeks and an expression that meant business, and her eyes went — as everyone's eyes eventually did — straight to the boy, and then to the satchel, and stayed there.

"Miss Sterling," she said. "My office. Now. And bring whatever it is everyone in this station is pretending not to see."
