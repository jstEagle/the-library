## 6. Captain Vane's Inspection

The warning came an hour before he did, carried by a dispatch rider who did not dismount — a captain from the Lines of Communication, inspecting casualty evacuation procedures, would arrive before noon. Sister Archambault repeated the message twice in her flat Belgian English, and then, as if the sentence itself were insufficiently alarming, added: *He is one of theirs. Not army medicine. Army administration.*

Edmund understood the distinction at once, and felt it settle in her stomach like swallowed river water. Army medicine asked whether you had enough carbolic and whether your stretcher-bearers slept. Army administration asked what you had that you should not.

She was folding lint in the ward tent when she thought of Cyrus, and her hands stopped mid-fold.

The boy had been with them four days now. Four days of learning that he ate slowly and thoroughly, wasting nothing; that he feared raised voices but not shells, which told its own terrible story about where he had lived; that he would not let the satchel out of arm's reach even in sleep, curling around it in the cot behind the supply crates as though it were a warm animal. Matron Greer knew of him — Greer, whose knowledge was a locked drawer and whose discretion was the lock — but the other nurses believed he was a Belgian refugee child awaiting transfer, a story Greer had distributed with the efficiency of rations. Transfer, however, required paperwork, and paperwork required names on lists, and lists invited reading, and reading was precisely what Edmund now feared most in the world.

Because she had opened the satchel. She was not proud of it; she had done it at two in the morning by candle stub, telling herself she needed to know if the boy carried anything that could identify him, anything that might reunite him with family. That much had even been true. But beneath the small shirt folded with heartbreaking neatness, beneath the wooden horse missing an ear, she had found the letters. Seven of them. Sealed, though the seals had been broken and re-pressed so carefully that only the faintest shine of new wax betrayed them. And she had read three before her hands began to shake too badly to hold the paper — read of shipping contracts and demurrage charges, of holds listed as carrying medical stores that manifest after manifest showed empty, of sums paid to a name that recurred like a refrain: *Ashcombe*. Read, on the last sheet, in a smaller and more frightened hand, of a housemaid dismissed without character for asking questions, of wages withheld, of a woman who knew where ledgers were kept.

The housemaid's name had been Lise. The boy's satchel smelled faintly of lavender, and Edmund had sat in the dark with her hand over her mouth for a long time.

Now a captain of administration was coming, and the boy curled behind the supply crates was worth more to dangerous men than every wounded man in the ward.

* * *

Captain Vane arrived at eleven forty, ten minutes early, which Edmund would later understand to be a tactic rather than a habit. He came in a staff car polished to the brightness of a chapel, and he stepped out of it into the mud of the dressing station as though the mud were a social inferior who would know better next time.

She watched him from the doorway of the receiving tent and made her first assessment the way she had learned to assess wounds: quickly, completely, without flinching. He was perhaps thirty-five, tall without being heavy, in a uniform whose tailoring whispered rather than shouted money. His cap sat at an angle no regulation specified but every well-born man seemed born knowing. He was handsome in the way of portraits — symmetrical, composed, finished — and his eyes were the pale grey of winter light on water, moving constantly, resting nowhere, warming nothing.

"Sister Sterling?" He consulted a leather notebook before she answered, as though confirming her existence against some private inventory. "Captain Julian Vane, Deputy Assistant Director — well." A smile, brief and precise as a scalpel. "You needn't trouble yourself with my full style. I'm here to help, I assure you."

"Help with what, sir?"

"Efficiency." He pronounced the word the way other men said *God save the King*. "Your station handles four hundred transit cases a month, I'm told, and your paperwork reaches base three days late on average. Three days, Sister. Do you know what three days means in the arithmetic of this war?"

Edmund did not say that she knew exactly what three days meant, having watched men die of six hours. She said, "We're understaffed, sir."

"You're all understaffed. That is the war's excuse, not yours." He walked past her into the receiving tent without waiting to be invited, and she fell into step beside him because the alternative was letting him walk unaccompanied among men who might say anything. "I'll want to see your admission registers, your disposal returns, your stores ledger. And your personnel roll, of course."

"Of course, sir."

There it was already, the word *personnel*, laid down like a card turned deliberately face-up. She watched his grey eyes and saw nothing in them but courtesy, and understood that the nothing was the point.

He conducted his inspection beautifully. That was the horror of it, she thought, following him through the wards for the better part of two hours — how *good* he was at this. He knelt beside a gunshot wound to the femur and asked the boy — the man, twenty-two years old, from Salford — how he'd earned his Blighty, and laughed at the answer in exactly the right measure, and left kind words that cost nothing. He examined the stores ledger with genuine fluency, catching a discrepancy in the chloroform returns that even Greer had missed, and praised rather than punished it. He complimented the cleanliness of the wards, the discipline of the orderlies, the coffee, which was vile, and he did all of it so warmly that Edmund caught herself twice on the edge of liking him, and both times pulled back as one pulls a hand from a flame seen at the last moment.

Because all the while, like a needle moving through cloth, his questions ran beneath the seam of the inspection toward something else.

"Large catchment area, yours," he observed, at the tally board. "Do many civilians come through? Refugees, displaced persons? Belgium is unfortunately generous with them."

"Occasionally, sir. We treat whoever the bearers bring."

"And the ones the bearers don't bring?" Still smiling. Still writing nothing. "Strays. One hears that strays find their way to soft-hearted women. Children especially."

"The front moves faster than families do, sir. We've sent two unaccompanied children to the refugee bureau at Poperinghe this month." Both sentences true. She was surprised by how steady they came out, and more surprised by how little effort the steadiness cost. That was the thing she would remember afterward: not fear, but the smoothness. The words had simply been there, ready, like instruments laid out on a trolley.

"Were they documented?"

"Bureau handled it, sir."

"Naturally." He closed his notebook. "I'd like to see the whole establishment, Sister. Including the rear stores. Matron's quarters, kitchens, ordnance." A pause, measured to the half-second. "Everything."

* * *

She took him through the rear of the station because refusing would have been a confession. Her heart was a trapped bird and her face was a painted mask, and between the two of them she walked Captain Vane past the supply crates behind which her whole world currently breathed in its sleep.

Cyrus was awake. She knew it before she rounded the last tent line — knew it by some transmission older than reason, the way one knows rain through a wall. The boy had heard the car, or the voice, or simply the change in her footsteps, and he would be sitting up in his nest of blankets with his arms around the satchel, watching the canvas wall as though it might dissolve.

Vane slowed at the ordnance stack. He put out one gloved finger and ran it along the edge of a crate, considering the dust on it, and Edmund heard, from behind the canvas six feet away, the smallest sound — a breath caught and held.

"This your cold store?" Vane asked.

"Yes, sir. Nothing perishable but the men's own rations."

"Mm." He did not move toward the crates. He stood precisely where he was, and Edmund realized with a clarity that felt like cold water that he had heard something, or sensed something, or was conducting the entire inspection toward this single square yard of Flanders mud. "Funny thing about stations like yours," he said conversationally. "So close to the line. Things drift through. Deserters. Civilians. Papers." He turned the word over as though tasting it. "Papers drift worst of all. A man can lose everything in this war except his papers, and yet somehow the wrong papers are always finding their way into the wrong hands."

"I wouldn't know about papers, sir. Ours are all accounted for."

"Aren't they always." He smiled at her again, and for the first time the smile went all the way to the grey eyes, and what lived there when it arrived was worse than the coldness. Interest. Appetite. "Well. You've been very patient with a fussy inspector, Sister Sterling. I'll see your registers now, and then I shan't keep you from your wounded."

Behind the canvas, very softly, something shifted.

Vane's head turned toward the sound — barely a movement, a bird's motion — and Edmund did the first genuinely reckless thing of her life. She dropped a crate of dressings.

It was in her way, plausibly, carelessly placed; she knocked it with her hip as she stepped to follow him, and it came apart on the ground with a glorious clatter, bandages unspooling across the mud like entrails. "Bother," she said — *bother*, of all the words in the world — and knelt in the muck gathering them, and Vane, courteous to the marrow, bent to help her, and whatever small noise a five-year-old boy made behind a canvas wall was buried under fifty yards of rolling gauze and her murmured apologies and his amused assurance that no harm done, Sister, really.

They left the crates behind them. She did not look back. Looking back, she understood, was a language spoken fluently by men like Vane, and she had resolved somewhere in the last ninety seconds — she could not have said when — never to speak it in his presence again.

* * *

He read her registers for forty minutes in the matron's office, and Greer sat across from him the whole time with the immovable calm of a woman who had buried three predecessors' worth of chaos and expected to bury his. He found nothing, because there was nothing to find; the ledger Edmund truly kept lived flat against her ribs, inside her uniform, and weighed less than a Bible and considerably more.

At last he closed the final register, squared it with its fellows, and stood.

"You run a clean station, Sister. I shall say so in my report." He buckled his notebook into its leather case. "One piece of advice, gratis, from a man whose trade is information. In war, the things that matter are almost never written where they ought to be. When you find such a thing—" the grey eyes rested on her, gently, the way a hand rests on a door it intends to open later— "the wise course is to bring it to someone equipped to appreciate it. An amateur attempt at disposal can be very... costly. To everyone involved."

"Is that a regulation, sir?"

"It's an observation." He put on his cap. "I'm at the advanced base through the week. People talk to me. It's remarkable what people talk to me, given how easy I am to talk to." Another scalpel-smile. "Sister Greer. Sister Sterling. My compliments on the coffee."

Then he was gone, engine noise fading down the duckboard road into the grey afternoon, and the station exhaled around them like a body resuming breathing.

* * *

Edmund went directly to the supply crates and drew back the canvas.

Cyrus looked up at her, arms locked around the satchel, eyes enormous and dry — he did not cry, she had learned; whatever had taught him silence had taught him that too — and she sat down on the mud floor beside his cot without caring what it did to her skirt, and pulled him against her side. He came stiffly at first, then all at once, the way small things surrender, and pressed his face into her sleeve.

"He's gone," she said. "Do you hear me? He's gone."

He didn't answer. He never answered. But his grip on the satchel eased by one degree, and she felt his heartbeat slow against her arm, and she thought: *I lied today. Smoothly, repeatedly, without practice, and I dropped a crate on purpose, and I watched a man's eyes and calculated his appetites like a card player.* She searched herself for shame and found instead a strange, cool competence, unfamiliar as a stranger's coat on her shoulders. This was what the war was doing. Not hardening her — *equipping* her. Every quality she had ever despised in others was revealing itself, fully formed, in her own hands.

She held the boy tighter and looked at the canvas wall, beyond which the winter light was failing.

Because there was one more thing, and she had understood it in the moment she knelt among the spilled bandages: Captain Vane had not come to inspect casualty evacuation. He had come because somewhere in the machinery of the war, a report existed — a housemaid dismissed, a satchel dispatched, an instruction to forget a face — and reports traveled faster than children. He knew something was moving through Flanders. He did not yet know it was wearing her spare shirt and sleeping behind her stores.

But he would come back. Men like Vane always came back, and each time they would look at slightly more of the station.

And the following morning, while she mended dressings and Cyrus stacked his wooden horse beside the satchel with the grave ceremony of a boy building a life out of almost nothing, an orderly brought word from the railhead that set the whole station murmuring: a civilian vessel had been requisitioned for England. Wounded, displaced persons, essential transports.

Limited places. A list forming at the base camp office.

Edmund set down her needle and looked at the boy who did not speak, and began, for the first time, to count.
