## 7. The Berth on the Calais Boat

The news came with the rain, which was fitting. Everything of consequence arrived at the dressing station soaked, delayed, and slightly wrong.

Edmund was folding bandages when Corporal Finch picked his way through the mud of the entrance with a telegram flimsy in his fist, holding it before him like something that might bite. She had learned to read the posture of messengers before she read their messages: the ones who brought death came quickly and left quickly, heads down, wanting the transaction finished. Finch was dawdling. Dawdling meant possibility, and possibility was rarer than morphine.

"From the Adjutant's office, Sister," he said. "They're saying—" He stopped himself, glanced at the wounded men dozing in their cots along the far wall, and lowered his voice as though the news might frighten them. "There's a ship, Sister. A civilian boat out of Boulogne. Cross-Channel paddle steamer, requisitioned for evacuations. They're calling it the *Calais Boat* because that's what she used to be, before the war. She'll take wounded and displaced persons to England. Direct passage. No stops."

England. The word arrived in Edmund's chest like a struck bell, humming through her ribs. She had not permitted herself to think the word in months — had kept it folded away with the other uncarryable things, in the same inner pocket where she kept her mother's kitchen, the smell of her father's pipe, the green door in Cheltenham. England was a country that existed for other people now. Her country had contracted to the dimensions of this tent: thirty-two cots, a stove that smoked, a ledger hidden in the false bottom of her kit.

"When?" she asked.

"Thursday. Weather permitting." Finch handed over the flimsy and stood there shifting his weight, clearly hoping to be told what it meant, wanting someone senior to convert the paper into fate.

The message itself was terse even by the standards of an army that had made terseness a moral discipline. EVACUATION PRIORITY SHIP DEPARTING BOULOGNE THURSDAY 0800. CIVILIAN AND MEDICAL PERSONNEL ELIGIBLE BY APPLICATION. BERTHS LIMITED. LISTS TO BE POSTED AT PORT AUTHORITIES. NO EXCEPTIONS WILL BE ENTERTAINED.

She read it three times. The first time, she saw only England. The second time, she saw *berths limited*. The third time, she saw the arithmetic hiding underneath the words like shrapnel under skin, and the bell in her chest went quiet.

"How many berths?" she asked.

Finch shrugged, unhappy at being asked to carry numbers as well as paper. "Nobody knows yet, Sister. Depends how many stretcher cases they take aboard first. Could be fifty places after the wounded. Could be ten." He hesitated. "Could be none."

"Thank you, Corporal."

He saluted, slopped back into the rain, and Edmund stood holding the flimsy while around her the station continued its ordinary business of saving and losing men — a moan from cot eleven, the rasp of a saw being wiped, Matron Greer's voice raised somewhere near the dispensary in the flat, exhausted tone of a woman who had been arguing with the quartermaster about chloroform since dawn.

Berths limited. Lists posted at port authorities. Applications required.

She thought of the wounded men in the ward behind her — of Private Ancel who had no family left to write to, of young Whitcombe who was seventeen and lied about it badly, of the shell-shocked boys who sat against the tent wall watching things no one else could see. Every one of them would be listed ahead of her. Every one of them should be.

And then there was Cyrus.

* * *

She found him where she always found him now, in the narrow space behind the supply tent that he had annexed for himself sometime in the past fortnight without anyone's formal consent. He was sitting on an upturned crate, knees drawn up, the satchel strapped across his chest the way another child might wear a shield. He was watching a line of ants conduct their incomprehensible wars across the boards.

Edmund had learned his silences the way one learns weather. There was the frightened silence, tight and watchful. There was the sleeping silence, boneless and complete. And there was this one — the working silence, when he sat very still and observed the world with the grave attention of a small magistrate taking evidence. It was in these moments that he most unnerved her, because in these moments he did not seem like a child at all. He seemed like a witness.

"Cyrus," she said softly, and crouched so they were level. "I have news."

His eyes came to hers. Brown, dark, older than five. He didn't reach for her hand anymore — he had stopped needing to, mostly — but he leaned forward a fraction, and that lean contained more trust than she felt she deserved.

"There is going to be a boat," she said. "A big boat. It will go across the water to England. To safety."

Something moved behind his face — not hope exactly; hope was perhaps a word he had not been taught, or had been taught too well to use. But his fingers found the strap of the satchel and gripped it, and he looked over his shoulder toward the east, toward the direction from which he had come on that bombardment night, and then back at her. The question was unmistakable even without language: *the bad thing follows?*

"No," she said. "Not here. Across the sea. Do you remember the sea?"

He shook his head slowly. Then he paused, and lifted one hand, palm flat, and moved it in a slow rolling motion — waves. Not remembering. Asking. *Is it like this?*

"Yes," she said, her throat tightening. "Like that. Only bigger than you can imagine. Big enough to hold everyone."

It was a lie, and he seemed to know it, because he looked at her for a long moment with those magisterial eyes and then simply nodded, accepting the lie as children accept the lies of adults who are kind — filing it away, perhaps, against some future reckoning.

She stayed with him until the ants marched off the edge of the world, and all the while the arithmetic ran beneath her thoughts like water under ice.

* * *

That evening the station's little universe assembled itself in the mess tent to hear Matron Greer read the telegram aloud and translate it into consequence.

Greer had aged years in the past month — Edmund could mark the war's progress on her not by wrinkles but by postures, the slow collapse from upright authority into something more like endurance. She stood now with the flimsy in her hand and her reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, and she looked at the gathered nurses the way a captain looks at a crew before weather.

"You've heard, most of you. There's a ship Thursday out of Boulogne. Some of you have family in England who will be sending telegrams tonight, and I don't blame you, and God knows I won't stand in the way of any nurse whose name makes the list." She paused, folding the paper with great precision. "I want to be honest with you, because you've earned honesty and we haven't time for anything softer. The lists will be drawn up at the port. Wounded first, always. Then civilian displaced persons with documentation. Then medical personnel — by application, by seniority, and by however much influence anyone happens to possess, which is to say: by chaos. I cannot promise any of you a place. I can promise you that I will put every name forward, including my own, and that if I get a berth and any of you don't, I will consider it the great failure of my life."

Someone laughed nervously. Nobody else joined them.

"What about the boy?" asked Nurse Applegate, and Edmund's heart clenched like a fist.

Greer's gaze crossed the tent and settled on Edmund — not hostile, merely tired, carrying the weight of regulations the way mules carry shells. "The boy is not on any roll. He is not registered, documented, or officially present. You all know this. Officially, we are sheltering a ghost." She let that sit. "Unofficially — I will see what can be done. Displaced persons includes children, and children include orphans, and orphans include him, if anyone asks. Whether anyone asks kindly is another matter."

The meeting broke up into murmurs, and the murmur became, within minutes, the sound Edmund had dreaded: calculation. Two nurses by the stove comparing seniority dates. Someone wondering aloud whether the RMO would sign travel recommendations for staff with dependents. Hope, arriving in the station like an infection, and spreading along the same channels as fear.

Edmund washed her hands longer than necessary that night. In the dark water she saw reflected fragments of lamplight, and in the fragments she tried to count.

Thirty-two wounded, of whom perhaps fourteen were fit enough to be classed walking. Nurses and orderlies, twenty-three souls, plus Greer. Refugees already crowding the roads toward the coast — she had seen them passing all week, carts and prams and pushchairs, whole villages folded onto handcarts. And one ship. One old paddle steamer with a civilian name and a military master, and berths that would be counted the way shells were counted, jealously, by men whose careers depended on scarcity.

Her name was not on any list. She had not been asked to apply. And between her and the port lay forty miles of road and Captain Vane's inspection, scheduled — she had seen the chit — for Tuesday.

* * *

She wrote the application that night by candle stub, on the back of a redundant stores form, because good paper was for officers and the dead.

APPLICATION FOR PASSAGE — STAFF NURSE E. STERLING. She looked at the words a long time. Then, beneath, in smaller letters: ONE CHILD IN MY CARE. MALE. AGE FIVE. STATELESS. She stared at *stateless*, which was both true and insufficient, and finally added: SURVIVOR OF [illegible]. She left the illegibility standing. Let the port authorities make of it what they liked; she had no better answer herself.

Where had he come from? Who had written *forget this face*? What lived in the satchel he would not surrender even to sleep?

She had stopped asking the questions aloud weeks ago, but the ship made them urgent again. If a berth could be found for him, someone would want to know whose child was crossing to England. Papers would be wanted. Papers were the currency of survival now, and Cyrus had been delivered to her with nothing but a note instructing erasure.

She blew out the candle and lay listening to the distant artillery, which had become a kind of silence, and did sums against her own heartbeat instead.

One ship. Perhaps two hundred berths after the wounded. Thousands of claimants. Against that: one small boy, one mute, undocumented, nameless except for the name she had given him for no reason she could explain, asleep ten feet away in the space behind the supply tent with his satchel buckled to his chest like a second heart.

The mathematics did not work. She had known it since Finch handed her the flimsy, and she knew it now, and still her mind returned to the problem the tongue returns to a broken tooth. Unless. Unless the wounded took fewer berths than feared. Unless Greer worked a miracle of advocacy. Unless Vane never came at all, or came and saw nothing, which he would not, because Vane saw everything, and what he saw he collected.

Unless she gave up her own place.

The thought arrived quietly, without drama, the way the worst thoughts do — dressed in plain clothes, reasonable, almost relieved. If there was one berth and two of them, the sum solved itself. She was twenty-eight years old and healthy and resourceful and she had survived eleven months of this war by becoming useful and invisible. Cyrus was five and voiceless and carried evidence that powerful people might kill to bury. Weighing them on any scale that mattered, the needle did not tremble.

But she lay in the dark and felt the full animal weight of her own terror anyway — the body's honest veto over the soul's arithmetic — and hated herself for feeling it, and felt it.

* * *

Wednesday morning brought the roads' testimony. Refugees had been passing for days, but today they came in a river: families from the flooded valleys, Belgian farmers with their cattle gone and their sons gone, an old woman carrying a birdcage with the bird still in it, singing. They flowed toward the coast the way water finds the sea, and every one of them was bound for the same port, the same ship, the same arithmetic.

Edmund stood at the road's edge with a bucket of water, ladling it out to the passing columns, and counted again, helplessly. A mother with four children. A man pushing his father in a wheelbarrow. Twin girls in matching coats, holding hands so tightly their knuckles whitened. Each face a claimant. Each mouth an arithmetic.

A woman stopped to drink, grey-faced, her coat held shut with wire. "The English boat," she said. "You know it? They say it takes the children first. They say there's priority for the little ones."

"They say that," Edmund agreed, because it was kind.

"And is it true?"

The question hung in the cold air. Behind Edmund, back at the station, a five-year-old boy was drawing in the dirt with a stick, guarding a satchel of letters that could bring down a family, wearing the only name anyone had ever given him. Ahead of Edmund lay Boulogne, and lists, and Captain Vane somewhere between here and there like a closed hand.

"I don't know," Edmund said honestly, and gave the woman's cup back full.

That afternoon she began, quietly and without telling anyone, to learn everything she could. She interviewed the quartermaster about the port procedures at Boulogne — who stamped the chits, where the lists were posted, whether the naval transport officer could be bribed with cigarettes or conscience. She wrote out a fair copy of her application and Cyrus's case for inclusion in the careful copperplate hand that had recorded three hundred erased names and had not yet learned forgery but possessed, she suspected, the right foundation. She checked the satchel's straps, sewed a loosened seam, and considered — not for the first time — the locked mystery of its contents, weighing ignorance against the possibility that knowledge might be the boy's best passport.

And in the evening, when the light went long and gold over the ruined trees, she sat with Cyrus between her knees and brushed his hair with the cracked ivory brush that was the only beautiful thing she owned, and felt the small skull under her hand, warm and alive and entrusted to her by nobody, by accident, by the war itself.

"Tomorrow," she told him gently, brushing, "we may go on a journey. To the sea."

He reached up and caught a lock of her hair — his rare gesture, his single sentence of touch — and held it, looking at her with absolute seriousness, as though memorizing her for a journey she might not be making.

"Don't worry about me," she said, and heard how the sentence trembled, a bridge under weight. "I am very hard to get rid of."

He smiled. He had begun smiling in the past week — small, brief, like sun through moving cloud — and each one went through her like a bayonet goes through silk. Then he pointed at her, and at himself, and brought his two fists together. Together. He had invented the sign himself; she had never taught it. She wondered sometimes where children learned to make promises when no one had taught them the words.

"Together," she agreed, and lied again, and kissed the top of his head.

Later, after he slept, Matron Greer found her at the washing line in the dark, pinning up sheets that would dry to grey in the cold.

"The adjutant's clerk owes me," Greer said without preamble. "The provisional lists for Boulogne go out tomorrow with the mail convoy. I've put your name forward. First position I could manage."

Edmund's hands stilled on the wet sheet. "And Cyrus?"

A pause long enough to measure. "Children without papers go on a separate list, if there's room after the documented cases. I've argued his case. I don't know if argument travels forty miles." Greer looked out at the dark, at the horizon where the guns muttered. "There's one more thing. The porter tells me a soldier came in on today's convoy asking for you by name. Shell case, they said. Struck off the rolls months ago. He's in cot nine." Her voice dropped. "Sister — he keeps saying his name isn't what the chart says. He keeps insisting there's been a mistake. He wouldn't stop till they threatened to sedate him."

The cold went through Edmund like the flap of a great door opening. Cot nine. A nameless man, insisting on a name.

"He's asking for you," Greer said again, and turned away into the dark.
