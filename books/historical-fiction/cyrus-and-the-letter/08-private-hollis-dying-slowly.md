## 8. Private Hollis, Dying Slowly

The man in the far cot had no name.

That was the official position, at least. The board over his bed read *Unknown, Private, regiment unknown*, the words written in Matron Greer's careful hand because nobody else could be bothered, and beneath them the number they had given him when he'd been carried in from a communication trench outside Zillebeke five weeks ago: a string of digits that meant nothing to anyone, least of all to him. He lay on his back with his eyes open most of the day, watching the canvas ceiling of the ward as though it were a sky worth studying, and he did not speak, and he did not sleep so much as he switched off, like a lamp with a faulty filament, and when he ate it was only if you put the spoon into his hand and folded his fingers around it and told him, gently, twice.

Edmund knew his name. She had known it since the second week of October, when she'd found it in the margin of a casualty return she had no business reading — a clerk's error, a line half-struck and abandoned: *Pte. A. Hollis, 9th Btn., missing.* And then, three days later, in a different document entirely, a second line that had made her go cold all over: *Pte. A. Hollis, 9th Btn., struck off strength — desertion.*

Desertion. As if a man could desert a war by having his mind blown out through the back of his skull. As if he had chosen the silence he now lived in, chosen the way his hands lay open on the blanket like two surrendered things.

She had copied the name into her ledger that night, in the blue pencil she reserved for the living — for the ones who might still be argued back into existence — and she had written beside it: *Found. Not lost. Never deserted.* And then she had buttoned the ledger into her tunic, hung her watch above the cot, and gone back to work, and in six weeks she had done precisely nothing else about him at all.

She thought about that every time she bathed his face.

* * *

The ward in November was a country of its own. Rain came through the roof seams and dripped into buckets set out like offerings; the stove ate coal and gave back grudging heat that reached the first two cots and gave up. Beyond the sandbags the guns kept their argument going day and night, a low continuous grinding punctuated by the flat cough of something nearer, and the men had learned to sleep inside the sound the way fish live inside water. Edmund moved down the rows with her basin and her carbolic, washing faces that were mostly boys' faces under the dirt, and at the last cot she set the basin down on the crate and said, in the voice she used for him alone,

"Good morning, Private Hollis."

Nothing. His eyes moved from the ceiling to her face and stayed there, which was more than some days gave her, and she counted it and went on.

"I've brought warm water today. Real warmth, not the usual dishwater. Tomas bribed someone. I don't ask questions."

She wrung the flannel and laid it across his brow, and his breath changed — not much, a fraction of an easing, the way a knot loosens one thread at a time — and she worked the cloth along his temples, his jaw, the hollows under his eyes where the skin was nearly translucent. He was twenty-six years old; she had decided this from the photograph in his paybook, which she kept in the ledger now because the paybook itself had been taken from him on admission and lost, another small erasure, and she had rescued the photograph from a waste bin behind the adjutant's hut. In it he stood squinting beside a brick wall with a woman's arm hooked through his, both of them laughing at whatever was off-camera, and on the back, in pencil gone soft with handling: *Alfie & May, Whitstable, Aug. 1914.* Before. Everyone in every photograph she had ever seen was before.

"Your sister writes very properly," she told him, as she told him most mornings. "I haven't got a letter — I'm sorry, I shouldn't have said *writes*, I only mean I'm sure she does. People like your sister write properly. My brother writes like a man throwing stones at paper." She talked to him the way one talks to the sleeping, or to God: without expectation, and therefore honestly. "I've started three letters to her and burned all three. You see how it is. What would I say? *Dear Miss Hollis, your brother is alive, which is more than the Army will confirm, and mad, which the Army has resolved by declaring him never to have existed.* It reads like an accusation, doesn't it. It isn't meant as one. It's meant against them."

His fingers twitched on the blanket. She took his hand. It was a habit now, hers and his — the one transaction between them that required nothing of his broken machinery — and his grip closed around hers with a slow, enormous pressure, as though he were holding on to a rope thrown across water.

"There you are," she said softly. "There you are."

* * *

He spoke sometimes. That was what nobody understood, the officers least of all, who had looked at him once, pronounced him *shell shock — severe, non-effective*, and signed him off the strength of the human race with the same pen they used for requisitions. But he spoke, in fragments, mostly at night, mostly to the ceiling, and Edmund had learned to keep still and listen the way one listens to weather coming.

It came on her third night tending the fever tent next door, when the lamps were turned low and the drip-buckets kept their small percussion and the guns had settled into their long grind. She was mending a tear in her own sleeve by lamplight, sitting on the crate beside his cot because the alternative was her cot and her thoughts, and suddenly, out of the dark:

"—told him. Told him plain."

Her needle stopped.

"Told him. Don't go up. Don't— it's not—" A sound that was not quite a word. Then, with terrible clarity, in the voice of a man giving evidence at his own trial: "I went after him. That's all. I went *after* him."

Edmund set down the sleeve. She did not lean closer; leaning was a kind of demand, and demands closed him like a fist. She sat exactly as she was and let the silence hold its shape.

"Sergeant Blake," he said to the ceiling. "Went up with the wire party and didn't come back and they said leave it, wait for light, and I—" His breathing quickened. His hands rose off the blanket and made small searching movements in the air, patting at nothing, the gestures of a man feeling his way along a trench parapet in the dark five weeks ago and five minutes ago simultaneously. "Found him. Halfway. Called out and the flare went up and they all saw me, everyone saw me standing out there like a — like a fool, like a—" The hands dropped. The voice flattened into the dead level tone she dreaded more than any scream. "They wrote me down for it. Running. They wrote me down running."

"You weren't running," Edmund said quietly. "You were going *toward*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and one day somebody is going to write that down."

He turned his head. It was the first time in five weeks he had turned his head toward a sound she made, and his eyes in the lamplight were wet and present and appallingly ordinary, the eyes of the man in the photograph at Whitstable, and he looked at her for four seconds — she counted — and then he was gone again, withdrawn behind whatever wall the shells had built, and the ceiling received him back.

But those four seconds. She sat unmoving until the candle guttered, and what she felt was not triumph. It was shame arriving on a delay, like the thunder after lightning, like the guns' echo rolling up from Passchendaele an hour after the flash — because she understood, sitting there, exactly what she had just been shown. She had recorded him. She had inked his name in blue pencil and written *never deserted* in her small upright hand and felt — she admitted it now, in the dark, where honesty lived — she had felt the *satisfaction* of it. The collector's satisfaction. Another name saved into her book, another small rebellion completed, filed, done.

Recorded was not restored. Written was not fought for. She had built herself a quiet archive of the erased and called it resistance, and meanwhile Alfred Hollis lay in an unlabeled cot being slowly buried alive under the Army's version of him, and not once — not once in six weeks — had she stood in front of an officer and said *this man's name is Hollis, he went out under fire for a sergeant, and you will write it down.*

She had sent Cyrus's name into the world in a ledger. She could not even get one private soldier's name onto a hospital board.

* * *

In the morning she went looking for the medical officer and found instead Captain Vane.

He was in the corridor outside the ward, immaculate despite the mud of the whole world, examining the noticeboard with the mild interest of a man at a exhibition, and her heart performed a small cold maneuver before settling.

"Nurse Sterling." He inclined his head. "Early hours, your ward."

"We're early people, Captain."

"So I hear." He smiled the smile she had begun cataloguing the way she catalogued everything — symptoms, shells, names — the smile that involved the mouth and arrived nowhere near the eyes. "I understand you keep excellent records here. Matron speaks highly. Order in a place like this depends on good records, wouldn't you say?"

"Order," Edmund said, "depends on knowing who people are."

"Mm." He considered her a moment too long, the way he considered everything, pricing it. Then he gestured at the ward door. "The silent fellow. The unknown private. No progress identifying him?"

"He has a concussion, Captain, not amnesia. He'll speak when his mind lets him. Until then his name is whatever we write on his board, and I should think we'd want to write it correctly."

"Should we?" said Vane pleasantly, and moved on down the corridor, and Edmund stood with her hands folded at her waist and hated him with a cleanliness that surprised her, and understood, in the same moment, that she had just lied by implication to an officer — implied she intended no formal identification, implied compliance — and that lying to men like Vane was becoming easy, and that easy was a road she did not remember starting down.

* * *

That afternoon she did the thing she should have done in October.

She found Lieutenant-Surgeon Wray in the operating hut between cases, cleaning his instruments with the grim economy of a man who sharpened everything in his life including his opinions, and she stood at the end of the table and said, "Sir, the unknown private in bed fourteen. I believe I can identify him."

Wray did not look up from a forceps. "Can you."

"Private Alfred Hollis, ninth battalion. Struck off the rolls as a deserter in October. He isn't one." She laid it out plainly, the facts she had assembled in the ledger's margins: the casualty return, the half-struck line, the date discrepancy between the deserter entry and the night of the wire-party flare, which she had extracted piecemeal from a lance-corporal in the convalescent tent who remembered the whole affair and thought it a disgrace. "He went out after a sergeant under fire. Witnesses exist, sir. Two of them are in this station."

Now Wray looked up. He had a long grey face that seemed assembled for disappointment and occasionally, at great cost, rearranged itself for something else. "Nurse Sterling. Do you know what happens when you make an official question of a man marked *deserter*?"

"Yes, sir. A court of inquiry. Which is what he needs, and hasn't had."

"What he *needs*," Wray said, setting down the forceps with a click, "is what every man in this ward needs, which is eight hundred calories and a war that ends. What he will *get*, if you put his name in front of the provost marshal, is a tribunal convened at base, a train journey he cannot survive in his present state, and three officers who have never been within a mile of a wire party deciding whether he is a coward or a lunatic. And while they decide, he stays exactly where he is, unnamed, because no one will move him either way." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "I know why you're doing this. It does you credit. But listen to me — there is a way to help that man and it is not the loud way. Get him fit. Get him speaking. A man who can state his own name to a board is a different animal from a name on a nurse's piece of paper. Paper gets lost. Men testify."

"Paper is all he has, sir. Paper is all any of them have. The Army wrote him out with paper; it seems to me paper might write him back in."

For a moment the surgeon's tired face held something almost like respect. "And when your paper crosses the wrong desk," he said, "and some staff captain asks who's been assembling evidence against a ratified disciplinary entry — what then? Who burns? Not me. Not you, probably; they don't burn nurses, they post them. Him. They'll move him to a base hospital where nobody talks to ceilings, and he'll finish dying slowly in a corridor somewhere, and his board will say what boards always say." He picked the forceps back up. "Do it properly or don't do it. Through channels. With the matron. And God help you, choose your moment."

She carried Wray's refusal out into the grey afternoon and found, turning it over, that it was not entirely a refusal. *Choose your moment.* It was advice. It was even, possibly, permission wearing armour.

* * *

She sat with Hollis through the evening change, propping him for his feed, spooning the thin soup into his hand and folding his fingers round it as always, and tonight she added a new ritual. She tore a sheet of paper from the back of the ledger — the ledger itself, her own secret scripture, opened on her knee in the ward where anyone might see, which was the point, which was the whole new terrifying point — and she printed in large clear letters:

*A. HOLLIS. PTE. WHITSTABLE.*

and pinned it to the board above his bed, over the words *Unknown, Private*, so that the old label hung loose behind it like a shed skin.

"There," she said. "Small mutiny. Don't tell the King."

His spoon paused halfway through the mechanics of eating. His lips moved. What came out was barely a sound, more the memory of a sound, the shape of a word worn thin: "*...May.*"

Edmund went absolutely still.

"May," he said again, clearer, and his eyes came off the ceiling and found her face and held it, wet and burning and present, and she realized he was not answering the board at all. He was answering the photograph, which she had taken out and propped against the water jug without thinking, Alfie and May laughing at Whitstable in the last summer of the world.

"She's waiting for you," Edmund said, and her voice cracked on it, which embarrassed her and she let it. "So we're going to get you well enough to be ashamed of yourself. That's the treatment now. I've decided."

Something happened to his mouth that had not happened in five weeks. It was, unmistakably, at four seconds' range and under lamplight, the ghost of the grin in the photograph.

She wrote it in the ledger that night — *Hollis, A.: SPOKE. Twice. Name of sister.* — and sat a long while afterward with the pencil idle, listening to the guns and to the rain finding its way into the buckets, thinking about moments, about choosing them. Wray was right that the loud way could kill a man. But the ship was coming — the whole station hummed with it, the civilian boat out of England, berths for the wounded and the displaced, lists forming, hopes forming faster than berths. Lists, she thought, were only ledgers with authority. Whoever controlled the list decided whose existence continued past the gangway.

And a man whose name was not on any official roll — a man struck off strength, marked *non-effective*, boarded over like a grave — would not be on anyone's list at all unless somebody put him there.

She buttoned the ledger into her tunic, and lay down, and did not sleep, and toward dawn heard boots in the corridor and a knock at the ward door, and Matron Greer's voice, flat with a fatigue that had gone past exhaustion into mineral: "Sister Sterling. Dress quickly. There's a telegram, and we're counting heads."
