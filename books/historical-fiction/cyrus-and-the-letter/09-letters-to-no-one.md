## 9. Letters to No One

The first offering appeared on a Tuesday, though Edmund would not have known it was Tuesday except that Sister Beaumont changed the dressing-store inventory on Tuesdays and the smell of carbolic was sharper in the air, the way it always was when the cupboards stood open. Edmund came off a fourteen-hour stretch of splinting and lancing and holding men's heads while they were sick into basins, and she wanted nothing in the world so much as her cot and the ten minutes of darkness before sleep took her whether she invited it or not.

She pulled back the grey blanket and stopped.

A square of paper lay folded at the foot of the bed. It had been folded with enormous care — four corners turned in, then folded once more, the way an envelope is made when one has no envelope, and the creases were pressed flat as if small hands had run over them again and again to make them obedient. She stood looking at it for a long moment, too tired to understand, unwilling to touch it in case touching broke whatever ceremony had brought it there.

The ward behind her breathed and murmured. Somewhere down the row of beds a man talked to his mother in a voice gone soft with morphine, telling her the war was nearly over, telling her he'd been careful.

Edmund sat down on the edge of the cot and unfolded the paper.

It was a drawing. Charcoal, or the burnt end of a stick from the brazier — she could see the char still crumbling faintly at the strokes. A face. A woman's face, rendered in the unsteady but determined lines of a very young hand: two eyes drawn too large, a mouth a single grave curve, hair falling past the frame of the picture as though the child had not known where to stop it. The proportions were wrong the way children's drawings are wrong, and yet the face was unmistakably *someone* — not generic, not a symbol of Woman, but a particular woman with a high forehead and a small mole, or perhaps only a smudge of charcoal, beneath the left eye.

Edmund held it in both hands and felt the temperature of the tent drop around her.

She looked up, scanning the ward. Cyrus was nowhere in sight. He had a habit now of making himself invisible when he wished to — folding himself into corners, sitting under the washstand, becoming part of the general clutter of a field hospital the way a cat becomes part of a drawing room. He had been with them eleven days. He had spoken no word, wept no tears, and followed Edmund with his eyes the way a compass needle follows north: without comment, without choice.

She refolded the paper along its creases, carefully, matching corner to corner, and set it inside her ledger where it lay like something pressed between the pages of a Bible.

That night she dreamed of a house burning, and in the dream she knew whose house it was, and in the morning she could not remember, and was grateful.

* * *

The second offering came three days later, tucked into her boot. She found it when she rose before dawn to take the midnight dressings, and had to strike a match and hold the flame close to see what the stiff paper was. Another face. The same face — she was certain now; the smudge below the left eye had returned, deliberate this time, drawn twice over. But this drawing was different in one terrible particular: the edges of the paper were scorched. Not cut, not torn. Burned, browned and brittle, curling inward at the corners as though the picture had been rescued from a fire — or as though the child wanted it to look that way, which frightened her more.

She carried it to the lean-to where they boiled water, where Corporal Wicks kept a fire going through all hours, and she asked him — casually, she hoped — whether anyone had burned letters lately. Papers. Anything.

"Burn everything we're told to burn," Wicks said, stirring the great dented kettle. "Matron's orders. Letters from home get read out to the boys who can't read, and then in the stove they go, mostly. Why?"

"No reason."

But she thought of the brazier at the end of the ward, the one used for the personal effects of men who died — how the orderlies were meant to destroy anything unsalvageable, and often did, standing papers over the coals because it was quicker than sorting, and how a five-year-old with nothing to do but watch might have watched that too. Except Cyrus had come to them with those drawings already forming somewhere behind his silence. He had seen fire before he ever saw their brazier.

She found him that afternoon in the place he liked best, the gap between the water cart and the tent wall, where the canvas made a dim green light like the bottom of a pond. He was sitting with his knees up, arranging pebbles. She crouched down at a distance she had learned by trial — near enough to be company, far enough to be weather, present but not pressing.

"I found your letter," she said.

He did not look up. His hands went still on the pebbles.

"It's a very good likeness," she said, though she had no idea whom it resembled and said it only because speaking seemed kinder than silence, and because she had begun, without noticing when it started, to talk to him the way one talks to a person rather than a circumstance. "I've put it somewhere safe. With my important things."

His thumb moved over the largest pebble. Back and forth. Back and forth.

"Was she kind to you?" Edmund asked softly. "The lady in the picture?"

Stillness. Then, so small she almost missed it: he drew the pebble in against his chest and bowed his head over it, the way a man bows over a wound.

Edmund did not ask anything else. She stayed crouched in the green light until the water cart needed turning, and when she rose, he did not flinch, and she understood that this, somehow, had been the right thing: to be asked nothing of, and to leave slowly.

* * *

By the third week there were seven drawings. They arrived without pattern — in her kit bag, under her pillow, once balanced improbably on top of the ledger itself as though the boy knew precisely what that book was and was posting letters to its address. Each was the same woman. Each had burned edges. Some were hurried, done in three strokes, almost rude in their economy; some were labored, shaded for what must have been an hour, the charcoal worked and reworked until the paper wore thin. In one, drawn on the back of a ration label, the woman stood beside a smaller figure — a child, stick-armed, handless — and the two figures were drawn inside a rectangle, and the rectangle was drawn inside another rectangle, and Edmund stared at it a long time before she understood: a door frame. He had drawn them standing in a doorway. Or he had drawn a photograph. A framed photograph of the two of them, such as a housemaid might keep on a mantel in a servant's room, and such as a child might remember last of all.

She began keeping the drawings interleaved with the names in her ledger, and she was aware, doing it, that she was committing a kind of category error. The ledger was for soldiers, for men struck off rolls by officers who found paperwork inconvenient. It was a book of corrections, of small restorations. It was not for civilians. It was certainly not for women who existed nowhere in any record she could name — women who had been maids, who had borne children, who had vanished from houses and villages and histories without ever appearing on a roll to be struck from in the first place.

And yet where else was she to keep her? The heart was not a filing system. She had tried.

* * *

"You've got to teach him to play like a normal child," Sister Beaumont said, watching Cyrus from the doorway of the store tent. He was sitting on an ammunition crate, solemnly folding a piece of paper. "He folds things. All day. He folds things and gives them to you and stands there like a little clerk waiting for a receipt. It's not natural in a child that age."

"He's five and he's watched things no one should watch," Edmund said. "There's no natural way to do it."

Beaumont sniffed, but without conviction. She was forty, iron-grey, and had buried her softness somewhere around Passchendaele, though it surfaced at odd moments — extra jam on a shell-shock case's plate, a hand lingering on a fevered brow when she thought no one saw. Now she lowered her voice.

"Matron asked after him again yesterday. Asked if arrangements were being made." Beaumont picked at a loose thread on her cuff. "There are arrangements, Edmund. There are societies. Children's refugee committees. He ought to go on a list."

"He's not going on a list."

"Every person in this war is on a list. That's what the war *is*. Lists."

Edmund looked across at the boy, bent over his paper, his tongue-tip caught between his teeth in concentration. "Then let someone make a list worth being on," she said, and heard in her own voice a hardness that surprised them both. Beaumont raised her eyebrows and withdrew, and Edmund stood among the crates of gauze feeling the shape of what she'd just said — feeling that she had drawn a line somewhere and planted a flag on it, and that flags, once planted, have consequences.

* * *

The ninth drawing was different, and it undid her.

It came in the hour before dawn, when the ward was at its stillest and the lamps burned low and yellow, and she found it because she woke — the nurse's curse, waking at every sound — and saw a small shape retreating from her cot on quick bare feet, silent as smoke. She lay still until he had folded himself back into his corner, and then she reached under her pillow.

He had drawn the woman lying down.

Not sleeping — he had drawn sleep before, in one earlier picture where the face was horizontal and peaceful. This was not sleep. The eyes were open and drawn upward, fixed on something above the frame, and the mouth was open too, and around the head he had made marks — fast, jagged, repeated strokes, dozens of them, radiating outward like the rays of a furious sun, and Edmund sat in the lamplight with the paper trembling in her hands because she had seen that exact image before, not in charcoal but in flesh: the faces of men in the moment a shell burst near enough, the mouths open, the eyes fixed upward at the noise coming out of the sky. He had drawn terror. He had drawn it competently, from memory, with the fluency of repetition.

And at the bottom of the page, in the corner, small and careful as a signature, he had drawn a window. A window with a cross-hatched pane, and outside the window, flames — simple triangular flames, the way children draw flames — climbing up the side of the picture as though the fire were trying to reach in through the glass.

He had watched. From a window, or a doorway, or the dark of a cupboard with a door ajar. He had watched her die, and he had no words — whether none at all, or none for this, Edmund could not know — and so he had done the only thing left to a creature made of speech who cannot speak. He had written. In the only language that survived whatever had burned away his voice, he wrote her a letter every few days: *this is she, this is she, look at her, do not forget her, I saw.*

Edmund sat until the lamp gutted low, and then she did something she could not afterwards entirely account for. She took her own pencil, and on the back of one of the simpler drawings — the one with the doorway, the mother and the small handless child — she drew, clumsily, a reply. She drew herself. Stick-legged, big-nosed, her cap drawn as best she could manage, and beside her figure she drew the boy's, small, hand-in-hand, and above the pair of them a crude sun, because it seemed wrong to answer grief with an empty sky.

In the morning she left it where the offerings came, under her pillow, and pretended to sleep, and watched through her lashes as he crept near, took it, looked at it, looked at her, looked at it again — and pressed it, briefly, against his chest, exactly the way he had pressed the pebble.

He did not smile. But that evening, for the first time, he sat within arm's reach of her while she wrote in her ledger, and did not flinch when her elbow brushed his shoulder, and when she finished the entry and closed the book he put one small finger on the cover and left it there, resting, as though taking an oath upon it.

* * *

She told no one about the correspondence. It belonged to the green half-light between the water cart and the tent wall; it would not have survived translation into the ward's language of banter and briskness. But it changed her working days in ways she could feel. She had spent months believing that her ledger was her whole rebellion — that recording was the same as rescuing, that a name kept safe in a notebook was a soul kept safe in heaven. Now she watched a five-year-old post pictures into the dark and understood the difference between them. Her book said *remember.* His letters said *witness.* Remembering was archival. Witnessing demanded something next — a next sentence, a next act, an answer to the question the letters kept asking, the question she had been declining to hear: *what will you do about it?*

On the last night of the month she sat on her cot after lights-out with the ledger open on her knees, and by the stub of candle she permitted herself, she turned past the pages of struck-off soldiers to the space where the drawings lived, and beneath the latest one — the woman's face again, calm now, the burned edges trimmed neatly with tiny scissors someone had lent him — she wrote, in her small record-keeper's hand:

*Unrecorded. Name unknown. Known to God and to me and to one child. Watched from a window. Died badly. Loved well — the evidence survives.*

She read it back and found it insufficient and true, which was, she supposed, the most one could say of any document in this war.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, from his corner, came the small dry sound of paper being folded — corner to corner, crease pressed flat, another letter made ready for the morning post to no address at all — and Edmund lay listening to it, and thought: *he is practicing. Whatever he is going to say someday, he is practicing.*

Three days later, Matron Greer sent for her, and when Edmund reached the office there was a man standing by the window with polished boots and an easy smile, and Greer's face said, before any word was spoken, *be careful.*
