## 10. Vane Makes an Offer

The rain had stopped by evening, and the dressing station smelled of wet canvas and carbolic and the sweetish rot that clung to everything within ten miles of the front. Edmund Sterling stood at the window of the supply hut — she had learned to think of any quiet room as hers for exactly as long as it took to be needed elsewhere — and watched the light fail over the duckboards. Somewhere beyond the tree line that was no longer a tree line, the guns had settled into their nightly conversation, distant and arrhythmic, like a heart that had forgotten what it was keeping a man alive for.

Behind her, in the dispensary, Cyrus slept. She could hear him breathing if she held still: the small, even respiration of a child who had finally, after four nights, stopped sleeping with his eyes half open. He lay curled on a stretcher between crates of bandage rolls, the satchel tucked against his chest with both arms wrapped around it, the way other children embraced a cat or a mother's skirt. Even unconscious, he did not let go.

Edmund had told herself the arrangement was temporary. A day, perhaps two, until transport came, until some authority could be found who dealt in found children rather than found wounds. Four nights now. She had already begun to know him — the way he flinched at the smell of burning but not at gunfire, the way he ate bread crust-first as though sweetness must be saved, the way he watched her hands when she worked, as though hands were the most important thing about a person.

She heard boots on the duckboard outside. Measured ones. Not the hurried slap of orderlies or the dragging tread of the walking wounded.

"Matron said I might find you here, Miss Sterling."

Captain Vane filled the doorway without appearing to crowd it. He had removed his greatcoat somewhere drier, and his uniform beneath carried that particular sharpness of a man whose tailoring survived the army rather than being defeated by it. His face was pleasant. It was always pleasant. That was the thing about Captain Vane that Edmund had been turning over since his inspection three days ago, the way one turns a stone to see what lives underneath: nothing about him was threatening, and everything about him was.

"I hope my patient isn't bleeding out while you stand admiring the view," he said.

"The view is free, Captain. The patients are not." She turned back to the window, deliberately unhurried, and then, because rudeness would be remembered and she could not afford to be remembered wrong, she added, "The rain's broken. We'll have mud instead of water. An improvement, in this country."

"An optimist." He stepped inside, closing the door behind him with a soft click that she felt in her spine more than heard. "I'm told that's rare in a dressing station."

"We're rare in most respects, according to the Army Council."

He smiled at that, genuinely enough, and moved along the shelves with the air of a man inspecting an estate he was considering purchasing. His fingers drifted over a jar of iodine crystals, a roll of splint wood, the scale they used for weighing morphine tablets. He touched nothing carelessly. He touched nothing at all, really; his hand simply traveled near things, close enough that she understood he *could* touch them, that everything in this room existed at his discretion the way everything in France existed at the discretion of men like him.

"You've done well here," he said. "Sister Ashworth speaks highly of your triage. Says you have a head for arithmetic under fire."

"Sister Ashworth is generous."

"She's precise. There's a difference, and it matters." He turned, and the lamplight caught his eyes, which were pale and dry and gave back almost nothing. "Which is why I want to speak plainly with you, Miss Sterling. You strike me as a woman who prefers plain speech to the usual dance."

*No one opens with a compliment to plainness unless what follows is crooked.* Edmund folded her hands at her waist — a nurse's habit, a nun's habit, a woman buying three seconds — and said, "Plainly, then."

He glanced toward the dispensary door. It was shut. Through it, faintly, the boy's breathing continued, steady as a metronome.

"The child," Vane said.

"What of him?"

"He has no papers."

"He has no voice, either. He has a name we've given him until someone comes to correct it."

"No one will come." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse — the flat administrative certainty of a man reading a column of figures. "I made inquiries. A village east of here, shelled twice in October. The survivors are accounted for in refugee columns at Calais. No missing children reported. No mothers searching. Whatever family he had, the war has finished its paperwork." He let that sit a moment, then: "Unregistered persons are a complication in an active sector. They cannot be evacuated through official channels. They cannot be provisioned. Strictly speaking, they cannot be harbored."

"Strictly speaking," Edmund agreed, "neither can half the things we do here every day. Morphine rations above schedule. Letters written for men too burned to hold a pencil. I wasn't aware the Army had developed a conscience about regulations."

Something flickered across his face — appreciation, perhaps, or the predator's patience when prey shows spirit. "Quite. Which brings me to why I'm here at this hour, alone, rather than through channels."

He crossed to the table where her kit bag sat and, uninvited, sat himself on its edge, arranging his long frame with the ease of a man who had never once been asked to leave a room.

"There is a ship," he said. "Out of Boulogne, within the fortnight. Civilian hull, Admiralty-chartered, carrying wounded and displaced persons to England. One berth has come available through — administrative adjustment. A priority passage, with protection attached. Protection meaning that no questions are asked at the dock, no records are consulted, no inconvenient histories follow the passenger home." He paused, letting each word find its place, the way a man lays cards he already knows the value of. "Such a berth would be of interest, I imagine, to a nurse who has spent two years in the salient. I'm told your file mentions exhaustion. Twice."

Her file. Of course there was a file. She thought of the two occasions — the shaking hands after Passchendaele's first weeks, the afternoon she had wept over a boy from Lancashire and been sent to sit in the sun for an hour like a piece of equipment gone temporarily wrong — and felt the peculiar violation of knowing strangers had weighed her worst days and filed them.

"That berth," she said carefully, "would be for whom?"

"For you."

"And?"

"And the boy travels separately. There are church organizations. Quakers, Catholics — they run homes for war orphans. Very efficient. The satchel he carries would remain in military custody, as recovered property." Vane smiled again, gently, as though offering her sweets. "You'd have your passage. Your health. Your future. And the child would be fed, clothed, and raised by people whose entire vocation is mercy. Honestly, Miss Sterling, it is the better outcome for everyone. You cannot keep him. You must see that. A dressing station is not a nursery, and the next place you're posted will be worse than this one. You're delaying an inevitability at cost to yourself, and I am offering to remove the cost."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the guns kept their slow time, and beneath it all, faint and precious, the sound of Cyrus breathing.

Edmund made herself consider it. That was the discipline she trusted most — to actually weigh the thing offered before refusing it, so that her refusal would be a judgment and not merely a reflex. And the offer *was* beautiful, in the way that well-made lies are beautiful. Safe passage. An end to the mud and the smell and the ledger of names she kept because no one else would. A kitchen in England. A garden. Sleep.

And the boy, handed to institutions by a man who wanted his satchel, and who had looked at Cyrus during his inspection with the particular attention one gives a problem to be solved — not a child to be placed.

"Recovered property," she repeated. "Those are interesting words for a five-year-old's school bag."

"It's an interesting satchel." The gentleness left his voice entirely, just for a moment, the way a floorboard reveals the joist beneath when stepped on wrong. "Contents of unknown provenance. Papers, we believe. In wartime, papers of unknown provenance are a security matter. You'll understand that I can't be more specific, and you'll understand also why specificity wouldn't change anything."

*I don't know what's in it.* The lie arranged itself in her mouth and she found, with a small cold shock, that she could deliver it. Three days ago she could not have. "I haven't looked through the child's belongings, Captain. It seemed indecent. He won't set it down even to sleep."

"I know you haven't. If you had, you'd have come to me in a fright, and we'd be having a different conversation." He rose from the kit bag, smoothing his tunic, and for the first time he came close — close enough that she could smell bay rum and something under it, wool and damp and the iron tang the whole country wore. "Here is the shape of it, plainly, as promised. Tomorrow evening I return, and I will require the satchel. In exchange, your name goes on the manifest tonight, before the list closes. Passage, protection, and my personal word that no shadow from this business touches you or your colleagues. Matron Greer keeps her record clean. Sister Ashworth keeps her posting. Everyone rises."

"And if I decline?"

He was already moving toward the door. He paused with his hand on the latch, and when he turned, his face had resumed its pleasantness so completely that she might have imagined the moment beneath — except that she knew now it was always there, the joist under every floorboard.

"Then tomorrow evening I return anyway," he said, "and the satchel comes into custody regardless, and I'm afraid the question of how unregistered persons came to be harbored in a military dressing station becomes a matter for channels. Channels involving Matron Greer." He opened the door onto the blue dark and the smell of rain-soaked earth. "You've been very good to the boy. Nobody disputes it. Let the people whose work this is finish it properly. Think on it, Miss Sterling. But do think quickly — manifests close, and fortunes change."

The door closed behind him. His footsteps crossed the duckboard, unhurried as ever, and faded.

* * *

She did not go into the dispensary at once. She stood at the window with her arms wrapped around herself, doing the thing she had learned in two years of war: feeling everything, and filing it.

Fear first, honestly labeled. He knew about the letters. He had said *papers* with the confidence of a man who knew there were papers, and he had known the boy's village before Edmund had. This was not an officer stumbling upon an inconvenience; this was a net, drawn tight around a specific point, and she and Cyrus stood at the center of it.

Anger next. *Recovered property.* As if the child were a rifle to be logged. As if the woman whose face he drew on scraps of paper — the face with the burned edges, drawn over and over in careful childish strokes — were a line item.

And then, last and most shameful, temptation itself. She examined it without mercy, because it deserved examination. He had offered her everything she had quietly starved for: safety, rest, a future. All she had to do was surrender a child she had known for four days to an institution chosen by a man who wanted what he carried more than he wanted him. It was not even a monstrous bargain, by the standards of this war. Thousands of children had been institutionalized by well-meaning hands. Perhaps the Quakers would raise him kindly. Perhaps.

But *perhaps* was not a word she could afford to build on. She had seen what happened to people who became lines in other people's ledgers. She kept her own book precisely because of it — the names of men struck off by officers with pleasant faces, erased with a stroke and a shrug. Private Hollis, struck off as a deserter when what he was was broken. If she surrendered Cyrus to Vane's channels, the boy would become exactly that: unwritten. And whatever was in that satchel would die with him, and whatever had been done to the woman in the drawings would stay buried under the mud with everything else.

She lit the lamp in the dispensary and stood looking down at him. He had turned in his sleep, cheek pressed to the cracked leather of the satchel, lashes dark against skin still faintly bruised with exhaustion. His fist was closed around the buckle. Even dreaming, he guarded it.

"Cyrus," she whispered, though he could not hear and would not have answered. "What did they give you to carry, little man? What is worth all this?"

He stirred. For a moment his eyes opened — that flat, old, watchful look she had learned to read — and found her face above him. Something in it softened, or she imagined it did. Then his eyes closed again, and he slept.

She sat down on the crate beside him and pulled her private notebook from her apron pocket. By habit now, by ritual, she opened it to the current page and added the night's names — two men from the ward who had died at dusk, recorded with their proper spellings, their regiments, the details officers omitted: *one liked bees, kept hives at home in Sussex; one had a laugh like a cart on gravel.*

At the bottom of the page, below the names, her pen hovered. Then she wrote:

*Cyrus — age approx. 5 — arrived 14 Nov, no papers, no voice. Satchel contains papers wanted by Capt. Vane. Offered me my own passage for him. Refusal pending. Note: he does not ask twice.*

She read it back and understood, with the clarity that sometimes arrives after danger has passed through a room and left, that she had already decided. The refusal pending was a courtesy to the woman she had been yesterday. Tomorrow evening Vane would return, and she would have to be cleverer than she had ever been, because she could not give him the satchel, and she could not give him the boy, and she could no longer pretend those two refusals would cost nothing.

Outside, the guns resumed. She snuffed the lamp, sat in the dark listening to the child breathe, and began — methodically, fearfully, the way one plans a trench raid — to think about ships.

She had heard the orderlies speaking that morning, low voices over tea: a civilian evacuation out of the coast, berths limited, lists already forming. Hope had passed through the station like weather. Now it returned with teeth in it, and she held it up to the dark and looked at it plainly.

One berth. One child. One satchel full of somebody's crimes.

And somewhere between here and Boulogne, a captain who never asked twice.
