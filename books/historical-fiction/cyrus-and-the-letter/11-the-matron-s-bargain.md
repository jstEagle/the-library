## 11. The Matron's Bargain

The lamps in the sisters' quarters burned low after ten o'clock — Matron Greer's economy extended even to light, as if darkness itself could be rationed by the ounce and entered into a ledger. Edmund found her there at the small table beneath the one permitted candle, mending a stretcher strap with waxed thread, her fingers moving with the blind competence of a woman who had done the same work so many thousands of times that her hands no longer required the company of her mind.

"You wanted to see me, Matron."

Greer did not look up. "Sit down, Sterling. And close the door properly. The wind in this place has better hearing than any German."

Edmund closed the door. She sat on the edge of the cot opposite, her knees together, her hands folded in her lap like a girl called before a headmistress, though she was twenty-six years old and had held men's arteries closed with her thumbs while they screamed for their mothers. There was something about Greer's quiet that reduced everyone to a child awaiting judgment. It was not cruelty. It was simply the weight of authority worn so long it had shaped the body around it, the way water shapes stone without ever once raising its voice.

For a while there was only the sound of thread pulling through canvas, and beyond the shuttered window, far off to the east, the dull percussion of guns working through the night shift of their trade.

"Three years," Greer said at last, still not looking up. "Three years I have run this station, and in three years I have never once asked you what you keep in that notebook you think nobody has seen."

Edmund's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept falling.

"I've seen everything in this place," Greer went on, drawing the thread tight with her teeth and biting it off. "I see which nurses steal morphine for the pain they won't admit to. I see which orderlies cry in the linen store where they think the smell covers them. I see which doctors drink, and how much, and whether it is grief or appetite. You learn, in my position, or you go under." Now she looked up, and in the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, gray hair coming loose from its pins, eyes the flat pale blue of winter sea. "So don't sit there looking like a schoolgirl caught with contraband. I'm telling you I know about your book. What I'm asking is why you thought you had to hide it from me of all people."

Edmund took a breath. The truth came out quieter than she intended. "Because it isn't regulation, Matron. Because if anyone official ever saw it—"

"Anyone official." Greer set down the strap. "You mean Vane."

"I mean anyone."

"Mm." Greer leaned back, and Edmund watched exhaustion settle over her like snowfall, weightless and relentless. Up close she was older than her fifty years suggested; the war had been eating her alive from the inside, one rationed hour of sleep at a time, and she wore the evidence without apology. "I'll tell you something, Sterling, since we're being honest tonight. In the spring I struck four names off a casualty return myself. Not because the men were deserters. Because the returns were due, and the colonel wanted his numbers clean before the divisional review, and four clerical casualties would have made his afternoon inconvenient. I signed the paper. Their names went nowhere. They are nowhere now, officially. Do you understand what I'm confessing?"

Edmund understood, and the understanding sat in her chest like swallowed ice. "You wrote them out of existence."

"I wrote them out of *paper*," Greer corrected sharply. "There's a difference, though I grant you the difference matters less every year of this war. My point—" she pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose, and for a moment she looked as though sleep might claim her right there, mid-sentence, sitting upright at her table — "my point is that I know precisely what kind of quiet rebellion your notebook is, and I have let it be, because it harms nothing and because God knows somebody should remember those men. But what you have done in the past week is not the same animal. What you have done in the past week can end us all."

"Matron—"

"Don't." One word, low and absolute, and Edmund fell silent. "Don't defend him yet. Hear me first. Then you may say whatever you like, and I promise you I will listen, because you have earned that much in three years of the best work I have seen from any nurse under my command. But you will hear me first."

* * *

Greer rose, poured two inches of cold tea from a pot that must have been brewed before supper, and drank it standing at the window, looking through the crack in the shutters toward the dark.

"A boy appears at our door during a bombardment," she said. "Five years old, perhaps six. No name, no papers, no voice. A note pinned to his coat instructing whoever finds him to forget his face. You take him in — against every regulation governing this station, against the specific written instruction of that note, and without reporting him through any channel whatsoever. He lives among us for six days. He eats our rations, which are counted to the ounce. He sleeps in the linen store, which means every nurse who draws sheets from that store becomes, knowingly or not, party to concealing him. And then Captain Vane inspects the station, walks its entire length, and stops — of all the beds and all the wounded and all the women in white — stops exactly where he might catch sight of a child's coat drying on a stove rail."

"How do you know he saw the coat?"

"Because I saw him see it," Greer said. "And I saw his face when he did, which was worse. A man who finds something unexpected looks surprised, Sterling. Vane looked *satisfied*. Like an auditor finding the entry he hoped was wrong." She turned from the window. "He didn't ask me about it. That frightened me more than a question would have. A question can be answered, misdirected, laughed off. Silence means he already knows whatever he needs to know, and is deciding what to do with it."

"He hasn't reported us," Edmund said. "Six days, and nothing. If he meant to make trouble over Cyrus, he'd have done it by now."

"Cyrus." Greer repeated the name slowly, tasting it. "You named him. Of course you did. After the Persian king who freed the captives." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, gone as fast as it came. "You always were a romantic under all that starch. Very well. As to Vane: officers like Vane do not report things, Sterling. They *collect* them. A nurse harboring an undocumented child is not, to such a man, a scandal. It is leverage. He files it away in that beautiful polished head of his, next to whatever else he has gathered in this corner of Flanders, and he waits until the moment arrives when having it is worth more than spending it. And when that moment comes, it will not be you alone who pays the price. It will be every woman under this roof."

She let that stand. Outside, a shell landed somewhere distant enough to be only a rumor, a soft thud felt in the floorboards rather than heard.

"Think about who we are here," Greer continued, quieter now, and this was somehow worse than her severity — this was the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Forty-one patients tonight. Nine of them cannot be moved. Sisters Ashworth and Delacour, who volunteered within a month of the war starting and have never once asked to leave. Orderlies — Tomas, who works until he drops, and young Prewitt who still writes home to his mother every Sunday as though this were a lark he'd soon be finished with. Every one of them is here because I signed papers saying this station would be run according to regulations. Regulations are not ceremony, Sterling. Regulations are the wall between us and the firing squad. The day some staff officer decides we are running an unregistered safe house for children carrying documents of whose contents we claim ignorance — that is the day the wall comes down, and it does not come down on you alone. It comes down on Delacour. On Prewitt. On all forty-one patients lying helpless under canvas while we are marched off to answer questions in a rear area where nobody has ever heard of mercy."

"They're not just papers," Edmund said quietly. "Whatever's in that satchel. You've guessed that much."

"I've guessed." Greer returned to the table and sat, folding her hands. "A woman doesn't pin a note to her child telling the world to forget his face unless she is afraid of being remembered herself. And she doesn't get a boy to the front-line area of a dressing station near Ypres without help — money, at least, probably papers, certainly a plan. Somebody wanted that child delivered to somebody, and failing delivery, wanted him erased. And now he sits in my linen store eating my bread while a captain with friends in high places smiles at me like a man holding a winning hand." She shook her head slowly. "No. I don't need to open the satchel to know it's dangerous. Danger announces itself these days. It has a smell."

Edmund looked at her hands. She thought of the letters she had read by lamplight three nights ago — the shipping manifests, the tonnage figures, the prices paid for shells that arrived empty and boots that arrived rotted, and above it all the elegant printed name of Ashcombe & Sons, Contractors to His Majesty's Government. She thought of the third letter, the one not about contracts at all but about a housemaid named Ruth who had *asked too many questions* and been given, in the cold phrase of the writer, *an arrangement on the coast*. She thought of the burn-scarred drawings of a woman's face that Cyrus left folded outside her cot each morning like offerings at a shrine.

She could tell none of it to Greer. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

"The boy stays," she said.

"Edmund—"

"No." Her own voice startled her — steady, low, certain in a way she had not authorized. "You've heard me out, Matron, as promised, and I thank you for it. Now hear this. I will not pretend I haven't weighed what you're weighing. I've lain awake doing the same arithmetic every night since he came. Forty-one patients. Two sisters. An orderly who covered for me without being asked. I know the cost of keeping him, or I know its shape. But I also know what happens if we put him out. This is a road with military traffic on it day and night, and a silent five-year-old alone on it has a life expectancy I decline to calculate. If we report him to the authorities, he goes into the system — and whatever system receives a child with no name and no voice and enemies rich enough to arrange his mother's death will hand him straight back to those enemies, wrapped and ribboned. There is no version of giving him up that I can live inside. So he stays. And if that endangers this station, then I am the danger, Matron, not him — and you may send me away for it, and I will go, and I will take him with me when I go."

The candle guttered. Greer studied her across the little table for a long, long moment, and Edmund felt herself being measured — not as a subordinate measures another, but the way one soldier measures another across a parapet, estimating what the other can withstand.

"You love him," Greer said. Not a question. Not quite an accusation either.

"I've known him six days."

"That's not what I asked."

Edmund swallowed. "Yes, Matron. I love him. God help me, I do. He follows me on rounds and holds the dressings like a tiny surgeon. He takes my hand when the shelling starts and doesn't tremble, and I tremble enough for both of us. He leaves me drawings of his dead mother because he has decided I am the person safe enough to show them to. Six days, and I would walk into fire for him. I know how it sounds. I know it is madness and sentiment and everything we're supposed to have drilled out of us. But I have spent three years writing down names of men the army decided didn't exist, and I have learned one thing absolutely: the moment you start calculating which lives are affordable, you have already lost whatever it was that made you worth saving."

Silence. Greer turned her wedding ring — Edmund had never asked about it, had never dared — once around her finger, twice, then stopped.

"Do you know what I wanted, when you came in?" she said at last. "I wanted to give you an ultimatum. The boy goes to the church orphanage committee in Poperinghe by Friday, or I write to the DMS and let them sort us all out. I rehearsed it. I had the sentences ready." She gave a short, terrible laugh. "And then you sat there and told me you'd take him into the road yourself rather than give him up, and I found I couldn't say the sentences. Because you sounded like me, thirty years ago, before I learned what things cost."

"Then—"

"*Listen.*" The sharpness returned, but it was different now — strained, like rope under load. "I am not giving you permission, Sterling. I want that understood in every particular. I am not sanctioning the child's presence. If Vane asks me directly, I will lie for this station, because lying for my people is the one regulation I have never broken. But I will not build the lie around your private crusade forever, and I will not watch good women go down in flames because you have fallen in love with a satchel full of trouble. So here is my bargain, and it is the only one I will offer. You have until the station moves — and it will move soon; I've smelled it coming for a week, the way one smells weather. Until then, the boy stays hidden, no one speaks of him outside this room, and you begin — tonight — making arrangements of your own for what happens to him when the orders come. Because they will come, Edmund, sooner than you think and at the worst possible hour, and when they do I will have forty-one patients to move and no time and no room for sentiment. Whatever you mean to do for that child, do it *before* the war makes the decision for you."

Edmund stood. Her legs felt strange, too light, as if a fever had broken. "Thank you, Matron."

"Don't thank me." Greer picked up the stretcher strap again, bent her gray head to the work. "Thank me by being gone from this station with that boy somewhere safe before I'm forced to choose between my conscience and my people. I've made enough of those choices to last several lifetimes, and I find I'm nearly out of the stomach for them." Her needle paused. Without looking up, in a voice suddenly very old: "Is he well? Truly?"

"He slept through the shelling last night," Edmund said. "Held my hand the whole time. Didn't tremble once."

"Hm." The needle resumed. "Stronger than the rest of us, then. Go on. Get out. And Sterling — the lamp behind the third bunk in the linen store. It burns oil faster than the others. Mind it doesn't set something alight."

It was, Edmund understood as she closed the door softly behind her, as close to blessing as either of them would ever come.

* * *

The corridor back to the ward ran dark and cold, lit at intervals by shaded lamps that turned the duckboards blue. Edmund walked it slowly, turning the bargain over like a coin she hadn't yet decided to spend. Greer knew. Greer would cover — for a while, for a price, for a deadline. The bridge behind her was burning; she could feel the heat of it already. Which meant there was no longer any version of tomorrow in which she waited for circumstances to resolve themselves. The station would move. Vane would move. The boy needed to move first.

She pushed open the door to the ward, into the familiar amber gloom and the chorus of breathing — rattling, whistling, peaceful — that she knew better than any music. And there, at the foot of Private Hollis's bed, small and solemn in the lamplight, sat Cyrus, cross-legged on the floor, keeping watch over the sleeping man with the fierce diligence of a soldier twice his age. He looked up when she came in. He did not smile. He simply reached out and took the hem of her apron in his fist, the way he did, anchoring himself, and waited to see where she would lead.

Edmund looked down at him, and at Hollis's sunken sleeping face, and felt the arithmetic of the coming days assemble itself in her chest, cold and clear as morning frost. Three lives. One road. And somewhere ahead of them all, she did not doubt, Captain Vane already walking it.

She took Cyrus's hand. "Come along, little one," she whispered. "We have arrangements to make."
