## 12. Night Train to the Coast

The orders came at half past two in the morning, carried by a despatch rider who did not dismount but thrust a folded paper through the doorway like a man passing a live coal.

Edmund read it twice by the guttering light of a storm lantern. *Station to be evacuated. All personnel and transportable wounded to proceed by rail to the coast. Departure 05:00. Nothing of value to remain.*

Nothing of value to remain. She looked across the ward at the small shape curled beneath a stretcher-bench, hidden by a folded blanket and an ambulance cushion, and felt the words settle over her like snow.

Cyrus slept with his satchel strapped across his chest now — he had insisted on it in his wordless way, tugging the straps until she buckled them herself, and even in sleep his hands lay over it the way other children's hands lay over a mother's neck.

*Nothing of value to remain.* She almost laughed. The army had no idea what it was carrying out of here.

* * *

The next three hours passed in the particular madness of a dressing station packing itself into its own kit bags. Stretchers were lashed into stacks; the stove was left to die; stores were counted and recounted by lantern light while the wounded — those who could travel — were lifted, wrapped, and carried out into the freezing dark where the ambulance lorries idled, exhaust steaming like cattle breath.

Edmund worked with her sleeves rolled despite the cold, because a nurse with rolled sleeves was simply busy, and a nurse who kept glancing at a blanket-covered bench was something else entirely.

Matron Greer passed her once, close, and said without turning her head, "The train has no children on its manifest, Sister Sterling."

"I know, Matron."

"I know that you know." Greer's voice was flat as a filed report. "I have not decided yet whether I know anything else."

She moved on. Edmund stood very still for a moment, listening to her own heart, then went back to folding bandages that did not need folding.

It was Tomas who solved it — or who thought he had, which in the end amounted to nearly the same thing. He appeared at her elbow in the doorway, a great gentle shadow of a man with a crate of dressings on his shoulder and frost in his eyebrows, and murmured in his careful English, "The boy. You mean to bring him."

"He doesn't stay," Edmund said. It was not a plan. It was the whole of her position, stated as geography.

"The train will have forty lying cases, sixty sitting, and orderlies enough to lose count of both." Tomas shifted the crate. "There is a man on the second coach, Duval, wounded through the shoulder, who sleeps like the dead and owes me for his brother's letters. A boy under a blanket between two sleeping men is invisible if no one is looking for him."

"And if someone looks?"

Tomas's face, in the lantern light, did something complicated. "Then someone must have been looking after him instead of counting stretchers. That someone should not be you. You they know. You they watch." He said it lightly, but not carelessly; nothing about Tomas was careless. "Give me the boy at the siding. Walk behind me with your hands full. That is all."

Edmund looked at him — this orderly she had known four months, who wrote letters for men who could not write, who had never once asked her why she kept a private notebook — and felt the peculiar vertigo of accepting kindness she could not repay.

"Tomas," she began.

"Later," he said. "The lorries are loading."

* * *

They moved Cyrus out wrapped in a grey army blanket, carried flat in Tomas's arms as though he were a casualty — which, Edmund thought, following with a basket of dressings pressed to her chest, was not even a lie, only a category error the war had taught everyone to make.

The siding was chaos. The hospital train stood hissing along the embankment, its carriages painted the greenish white that from the air was supposed to read as mercy, and along its length orderlies shouted, stretcher parties jostled, and a medical officer moved down the line checking manifests by torchlight with the grim economy of a man counting souls against tonnage.

"Second coach," Tomas murmured. "Walk slow."

Walking slow was the hardest thing she had done in months. Every instinct screamed at her to run, to gather the boy up and bolt into the dark fields, to do anything but amble toward a lit doorway where an officer held a clipboard. She fixed her eyes on the middle distance, on nothing, on the ledger-keeper's trick of being unremarkable, and counted her own footsteps like rosary beads.

At the carriage steps, a sergeant with a list looked up. "Wounded?"

"Lying case," Tomas said, tilting the blanket bundle so that the shape suggested a man rather than a boy. "Morphine. Sergeant-major says straight up to the second tier, he's holding a place."

"Straight up, then. No stopping in the corridor."

"No stopping in the corridor," Tomas agreed gravely, and carried Cyrus past him and up the steps, and Edmund followed, and no one shot at them, and no one arrested them, and her legs felt strangely foreign beneath her, as though courage were a borrowed uniform she was wearing inside out.

Inside, the coach was a corridor of suffering arranged with terrible neatness: two tiers of stretcher racks down each side, men stacked like books whose spines were torn, the air thick with carbolic, cigarette smoke, and the sweetish smell she would never again be able to separate from the smell of trains. Somewhere forward a man moaned in a rhythm, in and out, like tide.

Tomas slid the bundle onto the lower tier between Private Duval — who indeed slept like the dead, mouth open, one arm hanging — and a boy of nineteen with a bandaged jaw who glanced once, registered a child's face among the wreckage, and then deliberately closed his eyes, turning his head away. An act of witness performed by refusing to witness.

Cyrus's eyes were open in the dark, enormous, fixed on Edmund.

She knelt at the rack's edge and put her lips near the blanket. "You are safest here," she whispered. "You must not come out, even when it is light. Even if you are frightened. Especially then." His hand crept out and gripped her sleeve, two fingers pinching the wool, and she covered them with her own. "I will be in the third coach. If you need me, whistle — can you whistle?" He couldn't, or wouldn't; he shook his head once. "Then cough. Cough twice, loudly, and I will hear you if God permits and probably even if He doesn't."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A beginning of the machinery of one, stored somewhere safe.

Then boots in the corridor, voices, the check moving coach to coach, and Tomas touched her shoulder — *go* — and she went, walking the length of that groaning corridor with her back straight and her heart left behind her on a lower bunk under a grey blanket.

* * *

The third coach was sitting cases: men propped upright along benches who would ride to the coast watching the dark windows and seeing things that were not the dark. Edmund found a place near the far end beside a gunner with a splinted wrist who made room for her without a word, and settled her kit bag at her feet, and became once more Sister Sterling, E., Royal Army Nursing Corps, transportable, of no particular interest to anyone.

The train pulled out at seven minutes past five, three hours late, which in the arithmetic of that winter counted as punctual.

She had expected the journey to be the worst of it — hours of stillness with a secret thirty feet behind her — but she had not accounted for the way a hospital train at night flattens time. The wheels set the rhythm and everything else fell into it: the sway, the clatter, the moaning man forward keeping his tide. Men slept or pretended to. Someone sang very quietly, two lines of a music-hall song, and stopped as if embarrassed. The lamp at the corridor's end swung on its gimbal and threw shadows that climbed the walls and slid down again, climbing and sliding, climbing and sliding.

Edmund took out her ledger, out of habit more than purpose, and found she could not write. Her pencil hovered over the page and produced nothing. What entry belonged here? There was no name to record. There was only a boy under a blanket, a man who owed somebody letters, a nineteen-year-old who chose blindness as charity. The official record would say: *One hospital train, forty lying, sixty sitting, departed 05:07.* It would be accurate. It would contain nothing true.

She closed the book and held it in her lap like a sleeping bird.

It was nearly midnight when the checks came through properly. Not the sergeant from the siding — a captain, RAMC, red tabs on a staff captain's coat, moving down the coach with a torch and an adjutant behind him, waking men gently, checking tags, ticking lists. Efficient. Tired. Harmless-looking, which was how harm mostly travelled.

He reached her row. Torchlight crossed her face.

"Sister Sterling? Third coach?" He consulted a paper. "You're listed as attending officer for the lying cases. Second coach."

Her stomach performed a long, slow revolution, and her face did absolutely nothing. This was the strangest gift of the war: she had discovered, too late for it to be comfortable, that she was a natural liar. "There was some shuffling at the siding, sir. Orderlies short. They asked me to sit with the sitting cases instead."

"Mm." He shone the torch along the bench, over the gunner, over her kit bag, over the ledger in her lap. "Any of these men likely to deteriorate before Boulogne?"

"One or two, sir. Nothing sudden."

"Good." He ticked something. "We've had a report of a stowaway — a child — reported at the clearing stations south of here. If anyone's seen—" The torch swept the coach again, casually, resting nowhere. "Unlikely. But orders."

"No child here, sir," said the gunner, waking with soldierly promptness.

"No child here," Edmund agreed, and the words came out level and clear and cost her something physical, a small tearing under the ribs, because she had just lied to the wrong coat and knew it, and knew also that she would do it again in every coach of this train and every hour of whatever came after.

The captain moved on. The adjutant's boots receded. The wheels kept their rhythm, and Edmund sat with her hands folded over the ledger and understood that she had crossed a line so quietly she hadn't heard herself cross it. On the other side of it there was no longer Edmund Sterling who recorded names, and Edmund Sterling who invented them; there was only whichever one the moment required.

She did not recognize the woman who had just spoken. She suspected she would have to learn to.

* * *

Somewhere past two in the morning she gave in to the pull of the second coach and walked back along the swaying corridor, past tiers of sleepers, telling herself it was to check on Private Duval's shoulder, which was even technically true.

Tomas was sitting on the floor below the lower tier, his back against the carriage wall, Cyrus's head heavy against his shoulder. The boy had come out of the blanket after all — of course he had; he was five, and five years old does not fit under blankets forever — and Tomas had simply absorbed him, arranged himself around the child like furniture built for the purpose. To anyone glancing down the aisle it would read as an orderly resting, holding a sleeping... something. A kit roll. Nothing.

Tomas looked up at her and did not speak, because speaking would wake the boy, and instead he smiled — slowly, tiredly, with a sadness in it that she had never seen in daylight.

She sat down on the floor beside them, skirts against the cold boards, shoulder touching the rack above, and for a while none of them said anything, and the train carried them through the frozen fields of Flanders toward the sea.

"You knew," she whispered at last. "At the station. You knew what he was carrying before I told you. Or that it didn't matter what he was carrying."

Tomas was quiet so long she thought he might not answer. When he did, his voice was low, pitched under the wheel-noise, meant for her alone.

"I carried a satchel once," he said. "Not so different. For a family with a name people were afraid to say. I carried it from a house to a train, and I gave it to a man I was told to trust, and afterward—" He stopped. Adjusted the boy on his shoulder with infinite gentleness. "Afterward my brother was transferred to a battalion that was going where battalions go to disappear. They sent me his effects in a box the size of a hatbox. They did not send me the satchel."

Edmund's throat closed. In the dimness she could see the lines of his face, deepened beyond his years, and the boy's dark hair against his collar, and the unbearable arrangement of the two.

"Tomas—"

"Don't thank me," he said, mildly, firmly. "And don't apologize either, because you were going to do one or the other and neither helps." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes held not bitterness but something worse: instruction. "Listen to me, Sister. Men like Vane — because it will be a man like Vane, if it isn't Vane himself — they don't punish the child first. They punish whoever helped. So when the time comes, and it comes for everyone, you make sure the price lands on you and not on the ones standing near. That is the whole of what I know. My brother is what happens when you learn it late."

The train rocked. Forward, the moaning man breathed in, and out, in and out, the tide of him steady against the dark.

"I won't let it land on you," Edmund said.

Tomas smiled again, that tired, sad smile. "You don't get to choose where it lands," he said. "You only get to choose what you carry."

Cyrus stirred, murmured — a small sound, half-asleep, meaningless — and burrowed deeper against the orderly's shoulder. Edmund sat on the cold floor of the hurtling train, between the boy she would not give up and the man already paying for her, and watched the black windows, and thought about the coast coming toward them through the night like a verdict.

Near dawn, the sky ahead turned the colour of old tin, and far off she saw the first thin lights of the port, strung along the dark water — and, drawn up outside the harbour station, a staff car flying the pennant of the Army Service Corps, its headlamps burning through the mist.

Someone was waiting for the train.
