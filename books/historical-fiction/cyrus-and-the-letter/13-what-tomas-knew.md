## 13. What Tomas Knew

The train moved through the dark like a wounded animal finding its way home by instinct alone, pausing at each siding as though listening for pursuit. Edmund had not slept. Sleep on a hospital train was a luxury distributed by the same cruel arithmetic that governed everything else in this war — given to those who could not bear to be awake, denied to those who could not bear it either but had no choice.

She sat wedged into the corner of a carriage built for forty men and holding sixty, among stretchers racked three high like loaves on a baker's shelf, and beneath her cot — hidden by a folded blanket and the fiction of a sleeping nurse — Cyrus breathed in the small space she had made for him. Every few minutes her hand drifted down, found the warmth of him through the blanket, and returned. A ritual now. She did not remember deciding on it.

The carriage lamps were hooded, throwing cones of amber light onto the floor and leaving everything above shoulder height in shadow. Moans rose and fell. Someone was reciting the Twenty-Third Psalm in a voice worn down to thread. The smell was carbolic and iodine and old blood and the particular sourness of men who had been wet for days and knew they might stay that way until England.

Tomas appeared at her elbow with two cups of something that called itself coffee the way a crow calls itself a nightingale. He handed one over without ceremony and lowered himself onto an ammunition crate that had been repurposed as a stool, his long legs folded up like a folding rule.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I'll sleep when we're at the coast."

"You said that at Amiens."

"I lied at Amiens."

He smiled at that — the quick, crooked smile that had become, over eighteen hours of shared conspiracy, something she looked for the way one looks for a lamp in a dark house. Tomas Verhoeven, orderlies' corporal, twenty-eight years old or thereabouts, with a face too angular for handsomeness and hands so gentle that the worst-off men quieted when he touched them. He had covered for her twice since the station: once when a sergeant had wanted to search under the cots for deserters, and once — the greater sin, the one that could not be undone — when he had told Captain Vane's adjutant, with a perfectly straight face, that the child Nurse Sterling was seen carrying was the colonel's nephew, evacuated under family arrangement, papers with the quartermaster, terribly busy man, wouldn't want to be bothered.

It was the second lie that had bought them this compartment of darkness, this moving hour of safety. And Edmund had been turning over a question ever since, feeling its edges like a stone in a boot.

"Tomas," she said, keeping her voice below the psalm, "why did you do it? You didn't have to. If Vane had pressed you—"

"He would have pressed me and I would have bled." He shrugged, blowing on the coffee. "It is nothing."

"It is not nothing. Men have been shot for less than lying to an officer about a child." She heard her own voice sharpen and made herself soften it. "You put yourself between me and a firing squad. I'd like to know why."

For a while he only looked into his cup, and the train swayed, and somewhere behind them a man cried out in his sleep and was hushed by his neighbour. Then Tomas set the cup down on the floor beside his boots, precisely, as though it were an instrument on a tray, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — flattened out, emptied of the lightness he wore like a uniform.

"Because I have done this before," he said. "And last time I did not do enough."

* * *

Edmund waited. She had learned waiting the way nuns learn silence: not as absence but as discipline, a thing practiced daily until it became a form of prayer. She waited, and after a moment Tomas reached inside his tunic and drew out something on a cord around his neck — a small tin photograph case, the kind soldiers carried, dented at one corner as if it had stopped a splinter, which perhaps it had.

He opened it and turned it toward her without comment.

In the lamplight's cone she saw a young man in civilian clothes, stiff with the self-consciousness of anyone photographed before the war taught men how to stand without flinching. He had Tomas's jaw, Tomas's crooked smile worn straighter, and hair combed flat with what must have been water and great effort. Under the photograph, in faded pencil: *Jan & Tomas, Antwerp, 1913.*

"My brother," Tomas said. "Two years older and forever certain of it."

"You don't look alike."

"Everyone says this. It was our mother's favourite grievance." He closed the case but did not put it away; he held it in his palm like a coin he was deciding whether to spend. "In 1914, when the Germans came through Belgium, I was working at the hospital in Ghent. Orderly, same as now. Jan was — Jan was clever in ways I am not. He could talk to anyone. He could talk himself into rooms where he should not have been." A pause. "He got work driving for a family. English family, business people, who had stayed too long in Brussels because their business was too good to leave. Shipping people."

Edmund felt something shift under her ribs, small and cold, like ice cracking in a bucket.

"They were doing very well out of the war already," Tomas went on. "Coal contracts, transport contracts — moving things for whoever paid, army or occupation, it made no difference to them. And there was a woman who worked in their house. A maid. She had seen things in that house. Papers, letters, conversations she was not meant to hear. And she had a son." His eyes came up and met Edmund's, and in them was something so level and so terrible that she understood, before he said another word, exactly where this story ended.

"The boy was five years old," Tomas said.

The carriage swayed. Behind them the psalm had finished and someone had begun to snore, gently, blessedly, the sound of a man alive enough to sleep.

"The woman came to the hospital," Tomas continued. "January of 1915. She was frightened in a way I had not seen before — and I had seen the German army come down the Gentsesteenweg, so I knew the catalogue of fear. She asked for help getting letters out. Letters to England, to a lawyer, or a newspaper, she did not say precisely and we did not ask. My brother agreed to carry them. He said it was easy money and easy virtue together, which for Jan was irresistible. I said—" He stopped. His thumb moved across the dented corner of the photograph case, back and forth, a small tide. "I said it was dangerous and I wanted no part of it. That is my contribution to history. I said I wanted no part of it."

"Tomas—"

"No. Let me finish, because I have never finished it, and your captain's adjutant nearly killed us both tonight, and it seems the night has decided I will say these things aloud whether I wish to or not." He took a breath. "Jan carried the satchel to the coast twice. Twice it went well. The third time, someone talked. A servant, a clerk, someone in the house whose fear was bigger than their loyalty — it does not matter who, though I have spent nights making lists. They were waiting for him at the checkpoint outside Bruges."

Edmund's hand had gone, without her instructing it, to rest on the blanket over Cyrus's hiding place.

"They shot him by the canal," Tomas said. "Not as a soldier. Not even properly as a spy, with the forms and the blindfold. They shot him like a dog stops a rat, and they left him for the tide, and they sent a bill to my mother for the bullets. Sixty centimes. I still have the paper. I keep it because rage keeps better than grief, and I need one of them to keep the other company."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

"And the woman?" Edmund asked, though she knew. Some part of her had known since the note at the hospital door, since *forget this face*, since the burned edges of a child's drawings.

"She lasted eleven more months. They watched her after Jan. She could not run — running confirms guilt, and she had the boy to think of, and a boy slows you down worse than any wound. In November she was gone. Illness, the house said. An accident, some said. Nothing was ever written down, because the family who owned that house owned the town as well, in every way that matters." Tomas finally tucked the photograph back inside his tunic, against his chest, where it would sit over his heart through whatever came. "The boy vanished. Children vanish in war like coins down a drain. I looked for him. For two years, whenever I was near that country, I looked. I thought — if I find him, perhaps the ledger of my own account balances. Perhaps one rescue pays for one failure."

He turned to look at her fully then, and Edmund saw that his eyes were wet and that he was not ashamed of it, and that this, more than anything, undid her.

"And then a mute child appears at your dressing station," he said, "with a satchel, and a note saying forget his face, and I watch you take him in against every regulation the British Army has produced, and I know — I know the way you know your own name — whose satchel that is. Whose son. There cannot be two such boys in one war. God is not inventive, only cruel in repetition."

* * *

Edmund sat with it. The train slowed for a signal and the whole carriage leaned like grass in wind, and stretchers creaked, and a man murmured *mother* into his bandages, and Edmund sat with the truth settling over her the way snow settles — silently, weightlessly, until suddenly the branches break.

"Cyrus," she said at last. "I named him that. For no reason I could explain."

"Names chosen for no reason are the ones that hold," Tomas said. "Reason is thin. Instinct is rope."

She almost smiled. Then the weight of what he had told her arrived in full, and she looked down at her hands — a nurse's hands, cracked and carbolic-stained, hands that had held the ledger and believed that writing names down was a kind of fighting — and heard herself ask the question she did not want answered.

"The family. The shipping family. What was their name?"

"You know their name," Tomas said quietly. "You have read it in the satchel. Ashcombe."

"Yes." The word tasted of iron. "Yes, I supposed I did."

They were silent again, but it was a different silence now — not the silence of two tired people resting, but the silence of conspirators discovering the true size of their conspiracy. Edmund found herself thinking of the letters in the satchel, the seals she had broken by lamplight three nights ago with her heart hammering like a girl reading a love letter, except these were death warrants wearing the grammar of commerce. *Shipment schedules amended per instruction. Dependents' claims refused per house policy.* Cold phrases. Phrases that had a woman dead in November 1915 and a boy struck dumb with grief in a ditch somewhere between Ghent and Ypres until the war dumped him, like all its other debris, at a dressing station door.

"He saw it," Edmund said. "Whatever happened to her. He was there. That's why he doesn't speak. That's why the drawings are burned at the edges — he took them from a fire, or—" She stopped, unable to complete it.

"He watched," Tomas said simply. "That is enough. Watching is its own wound. I have watched, and I know."

Outside, the train gathered speed again. The hooded lamps swung slightly on their hooks, and the cones of light rocked across the floorboards like searchlights in miniature, and Edmund thought: *this is what comes next. This is the shape of my future, sitting on an ammunition crate telling me how it ends.*

Because she could see it now, plain as a map. She could see the road Tomas had walked and was still walking — the looking, the ledger-keeping of the soul, the decades-long arithmetic of *one saved does not balance one lost*. She could see what she would become if she failed: a woman who served out the war and then served out her life, competent and haunted, checking faces in crowds for a boy she had let be taken. And she could see, equally plainly, the other road, the one where she did not fail — and it was not a gentle road either. It led through Vane's courtesy and Vane's threats, through forged chits and dockside searches, through choices with prices attached that other people would pay first. Tomas had just named the price of survival aloud, and the price was always, *always*, someone else's blood on the ledger.

"There is something else," Tomas said, as though reading the direction of her thoughts. "Something you must understand about the Ashcombes, since you are going to Boulogne and they are going to try to stop you."

"We're going to try to get past them, you mean."

"I mean what I say." He leaned closer, into the cone of lamplight, and lowered his voice beneath even the snoring. "My brother thought he was carrying letters. Letters can be intercepted, and interception ends the matter — one execution, one burial, everyone satisfied. But the Ashcombes do not behave like men stopping letters. After Jan died, the letters kept being expected. Agents came asking at the ports for a satchel, describing it. Two years, they have been described. Do you understand? They are not cleaning up an incident. They are retrieving evidence, continuously, as a policy, because the evidence is never entirely theirs until it is ash — and there is a copy somewhere, always a copy, in the hand of the maid or the brother or the nurse, and they know it." His eyes held hers. "Captain Vane is not a man who stumbled into this. Whatever he has told himself, whatever uniform story he wears, he is their instrument, and instruments of that family do not stop at the water's edge. They have friends in Whitehall. They have friends everywhere."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I made it my business to know," Tomas said, "the way you make names yours. Everyone keeps a ledger of some kind, Nurse Sterling. Mine simply has fewer names and longer sums."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment — this Belgian orderly with his dead brother against his chest and his two-year search delivered to her lap like a gift he had no right to feel hopeful about — and she understood that she had been naive. She had thought the danger was Vane. She had thought the danger was regulations, courts-martial, the machinery of the army catching her with a child under a cot. But the danger was older and quieter and richer than any of that. The danger was a family in London who had already buried one carrier of that satchel and billed his mother for the bullets.

The train began to slow. Through the blackout curtains' seams, a faint grey was leaking into the world — dawn coming up over flat fields, or perhaps the sea approaching; at this hour and in this country she could not tell them apart. Somewhere ahead an engine whistled, long and low, and men stirred on their stretchers, and the day's arithmetic began again.

Tomas stood, joints cracking, and retrieved his coffee cup from the floor. At the carriage door he paused, half in shadow.

"For what it is worth," he said, "when I covered for you with the adjutant, it was not only for the boy. Though mostly it was for the boy." The crooked smile came and went. "It was also because you lie badly, Nurse Sterling, and someone must look after you."

Then he was gone up the corridor, boots soft on the boards, and Edmund knelt and lifted the blanket's edge, and Cyrus looked up at her from his nest — awake, she realized, he had been awake for all of it, silent as ever, his eyes enormous in the half-light and fixed on her face with an attention that felt less like a child's and more like a witness's.

"It's all right," she whispered, though nothing in the world supported the claim.

He reached up from the cot-space and caught her sleeve, and tugged it, twice, the way he did when he needed her to understand something he could not say — and then he pointed, small finger steady, toward the satchel he had never once let leave his body.

Edmund froze. Because the satchel was not against his chest where she had left it.

It was open. And inside it, on top of the letters, lay a folded piece of paper she had not seen before — a paper that had not come from the burning woman's house, a paper in fresh pencil, folded in quarters, addressed in a careful childish hand to no one and everyone:

*Nurse. If they take me, give this to someone who writes things down.*

Beyond the curtained window, the whistle sounded again — closer now, and lower, and wrong somehow, a note with urgency in it — and the brakes began to sing, and Edmund, with the paper in her hand and the dawn rising grey over the coast road to Boulogne, understood that whatever was written inside had been written for a moment exactly like this one.
