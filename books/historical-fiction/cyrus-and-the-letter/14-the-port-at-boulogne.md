## 14. The Port at Boulogne

The sea announced itself before they saw it — a grey exhalation rolling up the streets of Boulogne, carrying the smell of tar and cold salt and something beneath both, something burnt and animal that Edmund had learned to recognize and never learned to stomach. The lorry ground to a halt at the top of the hill above the port, and through the canvas flap she caught her first sight of the docks, and wished she hadn't.

They were full of people.

Not soldiers, or not only soldiers — but families, whole villages apparently decanted onto the quay: women in black shawls sitting on bundles tied with string, old men with faces like crumpled paper, children asleep across their mothers' knees in the open air as though sleep could be anywhere and it made no difference. Between them wound barrows and handcarts, crates of chickens, a pram with one wheel, a man selling bread from a basket who was surrounded by hands reaching before he had even stopped walking. Beyond the crowd the water lay flat and pewter-coloured, and on it rode the ship — or the shape of a ship, grey and high-sided, funnels breathing faint smoke, lying at anchor well out beyond the harbour mouth as if even the vessel hesitated to come closer.

"She's delayed," said the driver, spitting over the side. "Been told since yesterday evening. Tide, they say. There's always a tide."

Cyrus stirred against Edmund's side. He had slept most of the journey wedged between her and a stack of stretcher frames, his cheek pressed to her coat, the satchel's strap wrapped twice around his small wrist even in sleep. Now he woke the way he always woke — without sound, without movement, his eyes simply opening as though he had been awake behind them all along and had only now decided to admit it.

Edmund lifted him down from the lorry and stood him on the cobbles, and kept her hand closed around his.

The hospital party was directed down toward the eastern quay, where a requisitioned shed served as a clearing station for embarkation. The walk took them along the edge of the refugee camp, close enough that Edmund could see individual faces in the crowd, and she found herself doing what she always did with crowds now — reading them like casualty lists. A woman with a burn dressing gone grey at the edges. A boy of perhaps ten with his arm splinted to his chest by someone who had used a fence paling. An old man whose feet were wrapped in sacking against the cold, sitting perfectly still, staring at the water with an expression Edmund recognised because she had worn it herself in the dressing station at Ypres on the nights when the wounded came faster than the hands: the expression of a person who has stopped expecting anything from the world and is merely waiting to see what it does next.

She tightened her grip on Cyrus's hand and walked faster.

The shed smelled of disinfectant applied too sparingly and wet wool applied everywhere. Inside, orderlies moved among rows of stretchers; a sergeant with a ledger sat behind a trestle table at the far end, and beside him a young subaltern who looked as though he had been awake for three days and expected to be awake for three more. Edmund presented her papers. They were good papers — Tomas had seen to that much, God keep him wherever the train had taken him — naming her as nursing sister Sterling, attached, with one dependent child, category C evacuation, pending transport.

*Pending.* The word did its quiet work in her chest.

"Ship's anchored off the mole," the sergeant said, not looking up. "Tenders when she comes in. Could be tonight, could be tomorrow, could be the day after. Don't go far."

"We have nowhere to go."

"Then you're no different from anyone else." He stamped nothing, signed nothing, and turned to the next person in line.

Outside again, Edmund found a place against the shed wall, out of the wind's worst edge, and set Cyrus between her knees so that her body made a wall around him on three sides. From here she could see the length of the quay, the crowd, the gangways stacked with crates waiting for a ship that would not come, and — she counted them twice to be sure — two men in officer's caps moving through the refugees, stopping people, examining papers. Not checking for deserters. Not checking for contraband. Stopping *families*. Stopping women with children.

One of them wore the pale blue armband of the shipping control office. The other wore nothing so official, and that was what frightened her about him.

* *

She fed Cyrus bread saved from the train and half a tin of milk scraped thin with water, holding the tin while he drank because his hands were too cold to hold it himself. He ate the way he did everything — carefully, watchfully, taking nothing for granted — and when he finished he leaned back against her chest and watched the crowd with her, two sentries sharing a post.

It was while they sat thus that she did it.

She had thought about it through the last hour of the train journey, turning the problem over the way one turns a stone in the pocket, feeling its weight, learning its shape. The ledger could not stay in her kit bag. Kit bags were searched; hers already had been once, at the transfer point, by a corporal who had flipped through her spare shirts with bored fingers and would have gone deeper had a stretcher not arrived at that exact moment. And the ledger — the ledger was the only true thing she owned. Forty-one names. Forty-two, counting the entry she had made on the train for a gunner called Pell who had died somewhere between Béthune and the coast with his hand in Tomas's. If anything happened to her on this dock — and the word *anything* had lately grown very wide shoulders — the book must not fall into an officer's hands, must not become evidence against the living, must not vanish into some file drawer marked *recovered property* and never be seen again.

And there was another reason, one she admitted only in the privacy of her own skull: if she could carry only one thing off this dock, she had begun to suspect which thing it would not be.

"Cyrus." She kept her voice level, conversational, the voice of a woman remarking on the weather. "Turn round a moment. Face the sea."

He turned. She drew him back against her so that his body hid her hands from casual view, and she opened her kit bag beneath the fold of her coat.

The ledger came out warm from lying against her. It was a cheap notebook, boards softened by handling, string-tied, its edges furred. She had covered it in oilcloth against the wet, and beneath the oilcloth it had survived eleven months of mud and lice and shell-fire that had destroyed things built of stone. Forty-two names in pencil, each with whatever she knew of them written after: regiment, wound, date struck from the rolls, sometimes a mother's name, sometimes only *he sang, on the bad nights*. Private Hollis's entry was third from the front, the ink of his real name gone slightly brown with age and rain that had got at it once in a dugout.

She unbuttoned Cyrus's coat. Beneath it, next to his shirt, he had worn a small canvas money-belt since Ypres — empty, she believed, or nearly so; she had never asked what might be in it, and he had never offered. She loosened the belt now, working by touch, her eyes on the crowd, and slid the ledger flat against his spine where the belt met skin, and cinched it snug.

He did not flinch. He understood immediately, the way he seemed to understand everything that mattered — that this was not affection but armour — and he reached back and pulled his coat straight and buttoned it himself, all the buttons, even the small ones at the bottom that he usually left undone.

"There," Edmund said, and her voice betrayed her only slightly. "Now you're carrying something worth more than the ship."

He looked up at her then, and for a moment she saw reflected in his face the question she could not answer: *why you, why not me.* She smoothed his hair under his cap.

"Because," she said, to the question or to herself, "if they take me, they take a nurse, and nurses are replaceable. If they take that book, they take forty-two men who were already killed once by paperwork. I won't let them do it twice."

A woman passing with a bundle of bedding glanced at her, and Edmund realised she had spoken aloud. She bent her head and became ordinary again.

* *

The adjutant came at dusk.

She noticed him first as a disturbance in the crowd — people parting slightly ahead of him the way water parts ahead of a hull, not fleeing him, merely declining to be near him. He was young, perhaps twenty-five, tall and narrow in a greatcoat too good for the mud he was walking through, gloves of soft leather, a folded paper held in one hand the way a clergyman holds a service sheet. He did not look at the refugees. He looked *through* them, scanning, sorting, and Edmund understood before he came within thirty yards that he was looking for a particular kind of person, and that the kind of person was her, or was with her.

She rose, drawing Cyrus up with her, and began to move along the shed wall — not quickly, nothing that would draw the eye, just a woman stretching her legs, a woman with a tired child, entirely unremarkable — and it was of course impossible, because there was nowhere to go. The quay ended in water on three sides. She had known that when she started moving. She moved anyway, because standing still felt like surrender, and because those extra twenty yards might matter later in ways she could not yet foresee.

"Sister Sterling?"

The voice behind her was pleasant. That was the worst of it. Vane's people were all pleasant; the Captain himself had shaken her hand at the inspection in Ypres and complimented the cleanliness of her dressings while his eyes rested on a five-year-old boy.

She turned. Up close the adjutant was younger than he'd seemed, with a fair moustache grown in recently and unevenly, like something planted late in the season. His paper was a list. Her name was on it, near the top, in someone's careful hand.

"You're attached to the clearing party from the railhead," he said. "Embarkation list, category C. I'm required to verify dependants travelling with medical personnel." He smiled. It reached his mouth and went no further, stopping at the border like a traveller short of papers. "You have a child listed. Your son?"

"My ward," Edmund said. "Evacuated from the Ypres sector. I have the documentation."

"I'm sure you do." He consulted the list with a small show of reluctance, as though the paper bored him and he had read it many times anyway. "There's also a question of certain property reported missing in transit. A document case. Perhaps you've seen something — a leather satchel, brass fittings, army pattern? It would have come through the railhead with your convoy."

"No," Edmund said.

The word hung between them, complete, unhurried. She heard her own voice deliver it and marvelled, distantly, at how easily it came now — the lie smooth as a stone from a riverbed, dry and exactly the right weight. Six months ago she had been a woman who could not lie to a matron about a broken thermometer. She wondered what that woman would make of the one standing here, and decided the two of them would not have much to say.

"You're certain?"

"The convoy carried wounded, stretchers, and kit," Edmund said. "If an officer has lost property, the RTO at the railhead would hold a record of it. You might wire him." She paused, and added, with the mild helpfulness of a tired nurse, "Though the wires are backed up terribly since the offensive. We waited nine hours at Béthune for a signal that had gone astray."

Something shifted behind the adjutant's eyes — recalculation, perhaps, or simple irritation at being instructed in logistics by a woman. He wrote nothing down. That frightened her more than writing would have; men who write things down are collecting information, but men who write nothing down are confirming it.

"The ship," he said instead, folding his list away, "will board tomorrow morning, weather permitting. Tenders from the east quay, beginning at seven. Category C parties will be called by number." He glanced down at Cyrus, who had pressed himself against Edmund's skirts and gone still in the way of small animals when a hawk crosses the sky. "What number are you, lad?"

Cyrus said nothing. Cyrus, who had never said anything.

"He's deaf in the left ear and shy in both," Edmund said, before the silence could curdle into suspicion. "Shell shock. He hasn't spoken since the spring. The doctors call it mutism of affect; I call it Tuesday." Her voice was light, almost gay, and underneath it she was screaming, because the adjutant had knelt — actually knelt, on the wet cobbles in his good greatcoat — to bring his face level with her son's.

"Can't speak, eh?" he said kindly. "Well. Sometimes the ones who can't talk remember best of all. Isn't that so?"

He straightened, brushed his knees fastidiously, wished her a safe crossing, and walked away into the crowd, which parted for him and closed behind him, and Edmund stood watching until the dark took him, her heart going like a bird in a closed room.

*Remember best of all.*

They knew. Not everything — or he would not have needed to kneel — but enough. Enough to send a polite young man with a list through ten thousand refugees to find one mute child and look into his face and ask him, softly, tenderly, whether he remembered.

She picked Cyrus up. He was five and getting heavy, but she lifted him as she had not permitted herself to do since Ypres, and he put his arms around her neck and his cold nose into her collar, and inside his coat, flat against his small spine, the ledger lay like a keel.

"Tomorrow at seven," she whispered into his hair, to him, to the book, to whoever governed arithmetic on docks. "We only need tomorrow at seven. One boat. One berth. One morning."

Out past the mole the grey ship rode at anchor, her riding lights just beginning to show, small and patient in the gathering dark — and along the quay behind Edmund, unseen, someone struck a match, and she heard, very distinctly, footsteps beginning to follow.
