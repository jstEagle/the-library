## 15. A Name on a List

The shipping office at Boulogne had been a café once. Edmund could see it in the bones of the place — the mirrored wall behind the clerk's desk, cracked diagonally now, still throwing back fractured reflections of everyone who came to beg for passage; the brass rail along the counter worn bright by the forearms of men who had leaned there waiting for absinthe and now waited for salvation. Someone had hung a chalkboard behind the desk on which someone else had written, in a careful schoolroom hand, *NO PASSAGE TO ENGLAND WITHOUT STAMP*, and beneath that, as if the writer had relented halfway through the sentence, *unless priority class*.

The clerk was a Frenchman of about fifty with a grey moustache and the eyes of a man who had spent three days telling desperate people no in two languages. His stamp rose and fell like a slow heart. Thump. No. Thump. No. Thump — a pause — yes, and a woman on the other side of the counter made a sound Edmund had never heard from an adult, something between a sob and a hymn.

Edmund joined the queue at half past seven in the morning, with Cyrus's hand folded inside her own and his satchel slung across her body where no pickpocket and no casual search would find it. He had slept against her on the train with his cheek over her heartbeat, and she had not slept at all, and she found that the sleeplessness had scoured her down to something clean and hard and useful. Fear did that, if you let it finish its work. It burned away everything that was not necessary to the next hour.

The queue was long and quiet. That was the worst of it — the quietness. Refugees who shout are still fighting; refugees who wait in silence have already begun to say goodbye to things. In front of her stood a family from Lille, the grandmother holding a hatbox as though it held ashes, which perhaps it did. Behind her a Belgian fisherman with a bandaged hand explained to no one that he had a boat once, a good boat, and the sea had never done to him what the army had.

Cyrus tugged her sleeve. She looked down. He held up four fingers, then folded one down, then another, watching her face with the grave attention he gave to everything — the arithmetic of their small party, performed in silence. Two of us, his fingers said, and then, hesitantly, two fingers more, and his eyes went toward the wounded on their stretchers along the far wall, and she understood that he was counting the ones who could not count for themselves.

"Three," she said softly. "Maybe three."

He frowned, unconvinced by maybe, and put his hand back into hers.

* * *

It took two hours to reach the counter. The clerk looked at her papers — her nursing credentials, her orders, the letter from the Quaker liaison office in London that Matron Greer had somehow conjured before the station fell — and then at Cyrus, who stood very straight and looked at the mirror and did not look at the mirror and did not speak, because he never spoke, and the clerk's pen hovered over the manifest.

"The child has no document," the clerk said.

"He has me." Edmund laid her hand flat on the counter, beside the papers. "Sister E. Sterling, Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service Reserve. I am his sponsor. I will sign whatever you require."

"Sponsorship is a form." The clerk said it without heat, wearily, the way one says the sun rises. "For a child travelling alone with a nurse, provisional status, subject to confirmation at embarkation. One berth beside yours, if your berth exists." He flipped through his ledger — another ledger, Edmund thought, the world was nothing but ledgers, names written and struck and written again — and ran his finger down a column. "Priority medical and civilian. You are civilian-adjacent, sister. There is a supplementary list."

"I know my category," she said, though she had learned it twenty minutes ago from a Red Cross corporal who spoke out of the side of his mouth like a man betraying a secret to a friend rather than stating a regulation. "Provisional places, confirmed at the gangway. I'll take them."

The pen scratched. *Sterling, E., plus un enfant mineur, non identifié.* She watched him write the words and felt the strange vertigo of seeing her life reduced to ink — herself reduced to three letters and a rank, Cyrus reduced to a phrase in French meaning less than nothing, a minor unidentified, a blank where a boy should be.

But blanks could be filled. That was the whole of her religion, learned in a dressing station near Ypres with a notebook full of struck-through names: a blank is not an ending. A blank is only a name that hasn't been restored yet.

"Berth numbers assigned at boarding," the clerk said, and brought his stamp down, and the sound it made when it fell on her papers was the sweetest mechanical noise Edmund had ever heard. "Thursday morning. The boat comes Thursday, if the minesweepers clear the channel Wednesday night." He raised his eyes to hers for the first time, and they were kind under all that exhaustion, kinder than his voice. "If they don't clear, nothing sails, and we start again. Don't tell the others that part."

She gathered the papers. Cyrus reached up and took the corner of her coat in his fist, his anchor-hold, the grip she had learned meant *I am frightened but I am staying with you*, and they walked out of the café-that-was into the light.

Outside, the harbour spread away from them under a sky the colour of dishwater, grey water, grey boats, grey gulls hanging motionless in the wind like scraps of paper that had refused to fall. Somewhere beyond the breakwater the Channel lay between them and England, twenty-three miles of winter sea, and somewhere on the far shore of it, if the letters in the satchel were read by the right hands, there were powerful people who would prefer that neither of them arrived.

Two berths. Provisional. Subject to confirmation.

It was more than she had dared hope for at midnight, and less than she needed by daylight, because daylight brought back the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had grown a third term overnight.

* * *

She found him on the second day, or rather she heard him — heard his voice before she understood whose it was, a flat repetitive muttering rising from among the stretchers on the east quay where the wounded waited in rows under canvas, and her whole body knew the cadence before her mind caught up. Numbers. A man reciting numbers over and over, the way Hollis used to recite them in the ward at Ypres, the numbers of his company, the numbers of the dead, as though the list would come apart if he stopped saying it.

She came around the end of the canvas shelter and stopped.

Private Hollis lay on a stretcher with a grey blanket to his chin. He was thinner than she remembered — she had thought she remembered thin, but the war had kept working on him after she left, planing him down further, hollowing his cheeks until the skin lay close over the bone like paper over a frame. His eyes were open and fixed on the canvas roof. His lips moved. And beside the stretcher, where every man had some token of himself — photographs, letters, a pipe, a rosary — Hollis had nothing. No kit bag. No paybook. No tag.

No name.

"Sister says you're going out," said the orderly crouched beside him, a young Frenchman filling in forms on a clipboard. "You understand? England. You're going to England."

"Forty-one," Hollis said. "Forty-two. Forty-three."

"He won't answer to anything," the orderly said, glancing up, apologetic. "We have him as unknown, male, regimental details unverified. Transferred from Second Casualty Clearing with a consignment Tuesday. No accompanying record." He shrugged the shrug of a system that had misplaced a man the way a tide misplaces driftwood. "They cannot board anyone without verified identity. Ship's regulations. If nobody claims him—"

"I'm claiming him," Edmund said.

The words came out faster than thought, before policy, before prudence, and the orderly blinked at her tone. She knelt by the stretcher, and up close she could see how the cold had got into Hollis, blue at the fingertips, shivering in waves that travelled down the blanket.

"Hollis," she said. "Private Thomas Hollis. Ninth Battalion. You are forty-four years old and you have a sister in Sheffield named Ada, and her husband keeps bees, and you told me once that you meant to keep bees too, after."

His lips went on moving. Forty-five. Forty-six. But his eyes came down off the canvas roof, slowly, like something being lowered on a rope, and found her face, and for a moment — a moment only — there was a flicker in them, a recognition arriving from very far away, a man looking up through deep water at a light.

"Sister," he whispered. Not a question. Just the word, the old word, the one he had used a hundred times in her ward, and then the numbers took him again, but slower now, gentler, as if he were saying them for her benefit, showing her he still had the list, he hadn't lost the list, the list was all he had.

Edmund stayed kneeling a long time. The wind came off the water and snapped the canvas overhead. Around her the quay filled and filled — wounded carried up from the trains, families with their bundles, children asleep standing up against their mothers' coats — and she sat in the middle of the swelling crowd and did the sum she had been refusing to do since the clerk's stamp fell.

Three lives. Two provisional berths.

Herself, who had a sponsor's letter and a service record and a name that appeared in files in London. Cyrus, who had nothing but her, and could not travel at all without her, and whom Vane wanted, and whom she would die before surrendering — that was not arithmetic, that was bedrock, she built on it whether she wished to or not. And Hollis, who had nothing but her memory of him, who was unfit to travel without a sponsor, who had already been struck from the rolls once as a deserter while shells fell on men who were only trying to stop hearing the sounds of dying — struck off by officers who had never knelt by his cot, erased for a wound no X-ray could show.

She had written his name in her notebook months ago. *Hollis, Pte T., 9th Btn. Struck. Not lost.* Six words in pencil. She had believed, then, that writing a thing down was itself the act — that remembrance was enough, that keeping faith was a matter of ink. She had recorded the forgotten and called it fighting for them, and it had cost her nothing, not one meal, not one night's sleep, not one berth on a boat.

Now the ledger wanted paying.

She rose, stiff-kneed, and pressed her hand briefly to Hollis's forehead — warm, fever coming, or perhaps just the cold — and walked back along the quay toward the customs sheds where the manifests were kept, and Cyrus fell into step beside her without being asked, matching her stride, watching her face with those dark serious eyes that missed nothing.

He tugged her sleeve. Held up two fingers. Then, slowly, deliberately, a third — and pointed back the way they had come, toward the canvas shelter, toward the muttering voice.

"Yes," Edmund said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, since everything under it was shaking. "Three. We're going to find a way to make it three."

Cyrus studied her for a moment, unconvinced by conviction. Then he reached into his coat — into the inner lining where, since Boulogne, she had sewn the flat leather cover of the ledger against his chest, next to the letters, the whole weight of the evidence riding over his small heartbeat — and drew out a scrap of paper, torn from the margin of some form, and a stub of pencil, and offered them up to her without ceremony, the way he offered everything: folded paper, drawings, burnt-edged faces, trust.

She took the pencil. On her knee, with the wind pulling at her skirt and the gulls crying over the grey water, she wrote:

*Sterling. Cyrus. Hollis.*

And stared at the third name, and at the space above it where the ship's name should go, and the space below where the berth number should go, both of them blank, both of them waiting, and understood with perfect clarity that tomorrow morning Captain Vane would learn exactly which boat they meant to sail on — because a man like Vane always learned — and that between now and the gangway she had perhaps thirty hours to turn a pencilled name into a living man on English soil.

Behind her, faint under the wind, from the direction of the east quay, she could hear him still counting.

"Forty-nine," said Private Hollis. "Fifty. Fifty-one."

* * *

That evening she presented herself at the embarkation office — a shed of salt-whitened boards near the head of the gangway, lamplit, crowded — and asked to add a dependent to her sponsorship. The naval lieutenant behind the desk was younger than her, pink-cheeked, and kind in the brisk way of the very young, who have not yet learned that kindness costs.

"Dependent, Sister?"

"A patient. Private Thomas Hollis, Ninth Battalion, currently on the quay, listed as unidentified due to loss of documents in transfer. Shell shock. Unfit to travel unsponsored. I am willing to sign as sponsor and assume full responsibility for his conduct and care on arrival."

The lieutenant pulled a form toward him, and hope rose in her chest like water finding its level — and then his pen stopped. He consulted a paper-clipped list, running his finger down it the way the French clerk had run his finger down his ledger, and Edmund had time to think *not lists again, anything but lists,* before he looked up with genuine regret rearranging his young face.

"There's the difficulty, you see. Sponsorship requires a berth. Each sponsored person occupies a berth on the manifest, same as anyone. Your own place is provisional, priority class, one plus one dependent." He turned the form around so she could see the boxes. "I can't issue a third berth against your sponsorship. There aren't third berths. There are barely second berths — half the provisional places will be cut at the gangway if the wounded convoy runs heavy, and it always runs heavy." He softened. "Bring me a discharged man with his papers and his own entitlement, and I'll wave him aboard myself. But a nameless patient needs a berth attached to a sponsor, and—" he tapped the box, the little empty box, "—there's only this."

One box. Two names in it already, in her own handwriting: *Sterling, E., plus un enfant mineur.*

"Thank you, Lieutenant," Edmund said, and her voice was perfectly level, perfectly courteous, the voice of a woman who had learned to lie smoothly and had not yet recovered from not recognizing herself afterwards. "You've been very clear."

She walked out of the shed into the dark. The lamps along the quay burned in a ragged line out to the gate, and beyond them the black water breathed against the stones, and somewhere out in that darkness the minesweepers were either clearing the channel or they were not, and on Thursday morning the whistle would either sound or it would not, and three people stood on this dock, and the ship had room for two.

Cyrus was waiting where she had left him, in the lee of the customs shed, wrapped in a soldier's blanket Tomas had procured before the train — dear Tomas, arrested at the gate, and she had not even been allowed to say goodbye properly — and he looked up at her approach and read her face the way he read everything, instantly, completely.

He held up two fingers. Looked at them. Looked at her.

Then, with great deliberation, he folded one finger down, and pointed at himself.

"No," Edmund said.

He folded the other one down, and pointed at her.

"*No,*" she said again, sharper than she meant, and knelt and took his shoulders, and the lie assembled itself in her mouth out of materials she did not remember acquiring — the smoothness, the warmth, the nurse's trick of making the worst sound survivable. "Nobody is being left. We are going to sit down and eat something, and sleep, and tomorrow I am going to fix this, do you hear me? I fix things. Names especially."

He considered her for a long moment, five years old and entirely unconvinced. Then he leaned forward and put his arms around her neck, briefly, the way he did perhaps once a week, an economy of affection that made each instance weigh a tonne — and over his shoulder, across the dark water, Edmund saw a lantern coming along the quay toward them, carried by an adjutant's aide with a clipboard, and behind the lantern, at the edge of the lamplight, a tall figure in a polished greatcoat, hands clasped behind his back, strolling unhurried, as a man strolls when he owns the ground he walks on.

Captain Vane had found out which boat they meant to sail on.

And he was coming, at his leisure, to talk about the arithmetic.
