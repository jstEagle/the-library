## 16. Hollis Remembers His Name

The shed at the end of Quay Number Three had been a fish store once. Edmund could still smell it beneath the disinfectant and the wet wool of a hundred men — brine and old salt and something iron-dark underneath, the smell of the sea doing what the sea had always done at Boulogne, taking in and giving back. Now it was overflow ward space: trestles laid shoulder to shoulder, blankets that had gone stiff with cold, and along the far wall, in the corner where the lamplight thinned to nothing, Private Hollis sat with his knees drawn up like a man trying to make himself small enough to be overlooked.

He had been overlooked very successfully for eleven months.

She found him there on her second circuit of the dock, after she had argued with the embarkation clerk until her voice went hoarse and been told, with the flat courtesy of men who move numbers rather than people, that unattached civilian women could register provisionally at best, that displaced children were the concern of the Refugees Committee, and that no list existed yet because no ship had yet been assigned its final complement. Provisional. The word had followed her out of the office like a dog she couldn't shake off. Everything now was provisional — her place, Cyrus's place, the sailing itself, the whole fragile arithmetic of three lives balanced against berths measured in feet and inches.

But it was Hollis the arithmetic kept returning to, and so she had come to the fish-store shed, and there he was, exactly where the transfer manifest said he would be, filed under *unknown, male*, which was how the Army had learned to speak of him.

"Hollis," she said, and then, before the word finished landing, corrected herself — as she had wanted to correct herself for months, in the dressing station at Ypres, in the train, on the dock, every time the false name came out of her mouth smooth as any other lie. "No. That's not it, is it. Not yours."

His head came up slowly. He had that way of moving, Hollis, as though motion were something being asked of him and he was considering whether to grant it. Shell shock had done that — taken a man who had been, by all accounts before Arras, quick and laughing, and left someone who moved like the last man out of a burning house, checking every room behind him. His eyes found her face and held it with an attention that was almost painful, the way starving men look at bread.

"Sister." His voice was rusty. At the port they called him nothing at all; there was no one to call him anything. "You shouldn't be here. They'll ask you questions."

"They've been asking me questions since 1914. I've gotten fond of it."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one used to live. She lowered herself onto the trestle beside him, careful of the man sleeping two feet away with his bandages rising and falling, and put her hands in her lap so he would see they were empty. She had learned that about Hollis early. Hands first. A man who had been struck off the rolls by an officer's pen, declared a deserter while he lay under a stretcher twenty yards from the trench he'd never left, watches hands the way other men watch weather.

"I brought something," she said.

* * *

The ledger lived inside her coat these days, against her ribs, where it had lived since Ypres. It was a cheap thing — a stationer's notebook bought in Poperinghe for forty centimes, oilcloth cover gone soft as chamois from handling, corners furred, spine cracked in three places and mended twice with surgical tape. To anyone else it would have looked like nothing. To Edmund it was heavier than her kit bag had ever been, because everything in it weighed more than paper should.

She did not open it at first. She sat beside him in the half-dark and listened to the port breathe — the slap of water against pilings, the far whistle of a tug, a wounded man somewhere calling out for a mother in a dialect she couldn't place, and someone else answering in the same dialect, *here, here, I'm here*, which was most of nursing when she was honest about it. All of medicine, maybe. All of everything.

"Do you remember," she said, "the night of the twelfth of March?"

Hollis went very still. Stillness was his native country now; he could inhabit it the way other men inhabited pubs and music halls.

"The dressing station was full past sense. We had thirty-six men on eighteen trestles and I was working by candle because the generator had died again, and they brought you in at some hour I don't remember, and your uniform was soaked through and you were shivering so hard I could hear your teeth from across the room. And Sergeant Mowbray came through with the day sheet and read out the intake and when he reached you he paused, and then he said —" she kept her voice level, kept it gentle, though saying it aloud again after all these months still made something in her chest twist — "he said, 'This one doesn't go on the roll.' Just like that. As if you were a delivery refused at the door."

"I ran," Hollis whispered. It seemed to come out of him without permission. "I know they say I deserted. But I didn't run *away*, Sister. I ran *out*. There's a difference. Nobody ever—" His breath caught. "Nobody would hear the difference."

"I heard it," Edmund said. "That night. You were delirious and I was sitting up with you because your fever spiked and you talked. You talked for two hours straight, into my ear, quiet as a prayer, and most of it I couldn't follow, but some of it I could. Your mother's kitchen. The smell of apples in the loft. A girl named Ada whom you'd meant to marry at Michaelmas." She felt him flinch and went on steadily. "And your name. You said your name over and over, like you were afraid of losing it, like a man counting his money in the dark."

The lamplight down the shed guttered and steadied. Out on the water a siren sounded, long and mournful, and was silent.

"And when dawn came you didn't remember saying any of it. But I did. So I wrote it down."

Slowly, with the ceremony she had performed perhaps sixty times in this war and never once grown casual about, Edmund drew the ledger out and opened it on her knee. Her thumb knew the pages; it found the right one without hunting. March. Thirty-six names crowded above yours, because March had been like that, and below, squeezed in where the margin allowed, in her small upright hand — the hand of a woman who had been taught copperplate by a schoolmistress mother and had never let the war take it from her — the words she had written by candlelight while he shivered.

"'Private Albert Edward Hollis,'" she read aloud, quietly, "'2nd Battalion, Duke of Wellington's. Struck from the rolls the fourteenth of March by order of Captain R. Vane, cited absent without leave. Present at duty station the entire morning of the eleventh. Witnessed by this writer. Mother: Ellen Hollis, née Craven. Apples in the loft. Ada.'" She closed the book gently, keeping her finger in the page. "'Ada,' it says. I didn't know her surname. You never said it. I'm sorry. I would have written that too."

For a moment Hollis did not move at all. Then Edmund saw the shaking start — not the fever-shaking she remembered, but something coming up from deeper, from the cellar of him, and she put her hand over his where it gripped the blanket and held on.

* * *

He wept the way soldiers wept, which was to say silently and with visible shame, his jaw clenched against it, tears cutting channels down a face that hadn't expected them. She had seen strong men cry like this over a letter from home, over a dead friend, over nothing at all at three in the morning. But she had never seen a man cry over his own name before, and she found she could not look away, because it was not grief exactly. It was something else. It was the sound a man makes when something he believed lost forever is set back in his hands, still warm.

"You kept it," he said at last, when he could speak. "Eleven months. You kept my name."

"I keep all of them."

"Why?" And then, quickly, as if the question were insolent: "Begging your pardon, Sister. But why? I'm nobody. There's hundreds like me. Thousands. What difference does a book make?"

Edmund looked down at the ledger on her knee, at the oilcloth cover worn shiny where her fingers traveled, and considered how to answer honestly, because he deserved honesty and had received almost none of it from anyone in uniform.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my grandmother died, and the family gathered to sort her things, and there was a Bible with the births written in the front — my grandmother, her sisters, her mother before her, all in fading ink. And my uncle said burn it, we have our own books now, and they burned it, and I stood in the yard and watched the smoke and I was twelve years old and I understood something I didn't have words for yet." She turned the ledger over in her hands. "A name is the only thing that can't be replaced. Not properly. Money comes back, houses get rebuilt, even children — God forgive me for saying it — children get born again. But a name struck off is a hole. And the world walks around holes as if they weren't there. It's very good at that. Walking around holes."

"My mother thinks I'm dead," Hollis said. It came out flat, factual, the way men stated the worst things, because stating them flatly was the only way to state them at all. "Or worse than dead. Deserter's family gets the white feather through the post. Gets spat at in the village. If I went home—" He stopped. Started again. "If I went home they'd know me for what the record says I am. There's no coming back from what the record says."

"Yes," Edmund said. "There is. That's what this is."

She opened the ledger again, to his page, and laid it flat between them on the blanket, and for the first time since she had begun it — since that first terrible week at Ypres when a sergeant had torn a dead private's name from the day sheet because paperwork was easier than truth — she heard herself explain it aloud, and heard, in the explaining, what it had become.

"This started as remembering. That's all. Men vanished and I couldn't bear it, so I wrote them down the way you'd press flowers — not to do anything, just so they wouldn't be nowhere. But it isn't flowers anymore, Albert." His whole body jerked at the name, as though a current had passed through him; she gave him a moment, then went on. "It's evidence. Every entry has dates, witnesses, what actually happened versus what the roll says happened. Eleven months of the truth, in ink, in my handwriting, witnessed by God and whoever reads it. You are in this book as what you are: a soldier who stood his ground and was erased by an officer who found your absence convenient. And books travel. Books cross water. Books end up in front of boards of inquiry and lawyers and, if it comes to it, judges. This page can go home ahead of you and tell your mother her son is not what they say. This page can stand in a courtroom one day and say *I was there*."

Hollis stared at the ledger as if it might ignite. Then he reached out one finger — carefully, the way men touch relics, or babies — and touched his own name.

"That's really me," he said wonderingly. "Albert. Nobody's said it since—" His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "Sister. Miss Sterling. Make it mean something."

"What?"

"Make my name mean something." He turned to her fully for the first time, and his eyes were wet and blazing and for a moment he was not a shell-shocked ghost in a fish store at all but a soldier, a man who had marched and dug and frozen and bled for a country that had deleted him. "You said it yourself — the book travels. Well, I can't. Look at me." He spread his hands, and she saw the tremor in them, the fine uncontrollable tremor that had ended his service and would follow him all his life. "I'll never stand in front of a board. I can't even stand in front of a clerk without my teeth going. But you can. You're going where the book goes. So make it count. Don't just remember us — *use* us. Make every name in there a witness. Make them sorry they ever picked up a pen."

The ferocity in it startled her — and then it didn't, because she had known all along that stillness like Hollis's wasn't emptiness. It was pressure. Eleven months of a man holding his own erasure inside his chest, and now, given one page of proof that he existed, the pressure had found a direction.

"I will," Edmund said. And discovered, saying it, that it was no longer a comfort offered to a dying man or a promise made to soothe. It was a commission. She took the pencil from her pocket, and beneath his entry, in the space she always left at the bottom of each page — space she had never known why she left until tonight — she wrote: *Witness statement pending. Name restored.* Then she showed it to him, and watched him read it twice, moving his lips, and watched his shoulders come down half an inch from around his ears, the first lowering she had seen in eleven months.

"There's a boat," she told him then, because he deserved the whole truth and there was no time left for anything less. "Civilian evacuation, England, maybe two days, maybe tomorrow — nothing here is certain but the cold. Places are short. I've registered myself and the boy, provisionally, and I will fight the clerk, the committee, and the Admiralty itself if I must to add your name. But Albert — listen. There's an officer. Vane. He signed the order that struck you off, and he's here, on this quay, and he is looking for something I have, and if he finds it before that boat sails, no ledger and no testimony will save any of us. So I need you to do the hardest thing a soldier ever does. I need you to be patient one more day, and invisible one more night, and trust me with the fighting part."

Hollis looked at her for a long moment. Outside, across the grey water, the evacuation ship lay at anchor, riding the swell, her funnel smoking lazily as if the whole business were routine — and Edmund's eye was caught, snagged like cloth on a nail, by a figure standing at the foot of the gangway two hundred yards off, hands clasped behind his back, polished and unhurried, turning slowly to survey the sheds along the quay.

Captain Vane had begun to walk toward the fish store.

"He's coming," she said, very low, closing the ledger and sliding it back against her ribs. "Albert — whatever happens, whatever he asks — you never saw me. Tonight, at dark, the third warehouse door, there's a woman named Greer's contact with hot soup and a place behind the crates. Trust the soup. Trust nothing else."

She was already moving down the shed, becoming one more nurse among nurses, when Hollis's voice stopped her — clear, steady, the voice of a man whose name had been handed back to him that afternoon and was learning to be spoken in.

"Sister. When you write it — the next entry, when it's over — spell it right. Hollis. Two L's. Everyone always forgets the second one."

"Not me," Edmund said. "I forget nothing."

And walked out into the wind, toward the sound of Vane's boots on the wet stones.
