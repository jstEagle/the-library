## 17. Vane Closes the Net

The dock at Boulogne smelled of tar and wet wool and the particular sourness of too many human beings pressed into too small a space. Edmund moved through the crowd with Cyrus's hand gripped in hers, feeling the small bones of his fingers and thinking, not for the first time, how strange it was that the whole architecture of her conscience had come to rest on something so slight. He was heavier than he looked, this child. He carried weight the way ballast carries a ship — invisibly, low down, keeping everything upright.

The ship — the *Dover Queen*, some clerk had called her, though she looked to Edmund like any other grey-hulled thing that had been worked too hard — sat at the far berth with steam already breathing from her funnels. The sight of that steam did something to Edmund's chest. Steam meant schedules. Schedules meant arithmetic, and she had grown to hate arithmetic.

She found a place against the sea wall where a crane's shadow fell across the stones, and set Cyrus down on her kit bag so that he could watch the gulls without being jostled. He watched them the way he watched everything: completely, silently, with the whole of himself gathered behind his eyes. She had learned to read him now. The tilt of his head meant curiosity. The pressing-together of his lips meant fear held in strict discipline, a discipline no five-year-old should have had cause to learn.

"Stay here," she said. "Watch the birds. Count them for me."

He held up four fingers. There were six gulls. She smiled as though he had answered correctly, because in a sense he had; numbers were whatever the war said they were, and the war said most things were fewer than they appeared.

"I'll be ten steps away. Not more. If anything frightens you, you come to me."

He nodded once, gravely, and took out the stub of pencil she had given him and a folded square of paper. She watched him begin to draw — the quick, certain strokes of a child who drew the same thing over and over, a woman's face rendered again and again in lines that grew steadier each week, though never less haunted — and then she turned toward the embarkation office, and the crowd closed between them like water closing over a stone.

She had gone perhaps thirty steps when she felt it.

It was not a sound. It was not even a sight, at first. It was a change in the texture of the crowd, the way men part subtly around authority the way water parts around a keel. And then she saw him, standing beside the customs shed with his hands clasped behind his back in an attitude of perfect, patient ownership, and her heart performed a slow, deliberate drop, the way a lift drops when the cable first groans.

Captain Vane.

He was dressed immaculately, which seemed to Edmund a kind of obscenity here at the end of the world, where every other coat bore salt stains or mud or the grey film of other people's suffering. His greatcoat had a collar that sat exactly where a collar should sit. His boots gleamed dully under the lamplight that was just beginning to matter in the thickening afternoon. He was looking at the crowd the way an auditor looks at a ledger — not hostilely, merely checking the sums.

And then his eyes found her, and he smiled, and Edmund understood that the sums had been checked and found interesting.

"Sister Sterling." He inclined his head. "What a providential crowd. I've been looking for a needle all afternoon, and here you are, walking out of the haystack of your own accord."

"Captain." Her voice came out level. She was distantly amazed by this. In the last month she had discovered reserves of composure she had never known she possessed, and each time she drew on them she felt a small grief, as though the woman she had been before the war — the one who blushed and stammered and told the truth badly, out of inexperience rather than virtue — was being spent coin by coin. "Are you travelling?"

"I'm inspecting." He fell into step beside her, uninvited, and matched her pace with an ease that was itself a form of pressure. "The port is chaos. Someone must take an interest in order. You'd be astonished what drifts through a place like this when nobody takes an interest. Deserters. Stowaways." A pause, measured to the inch. "Children."

Edmund kept walking. Ten steps, she thought. Cyrus was ten steps behind her, or he had been; she could not look. Looking would tell Vane everything.

"You seem troubled," Vane said pleasantly. "Have I said something wrong? It's a fault of mine. I'm told I say the quiet part aloud."

"You haven't said anything at all," Edmund said. "You've arranged words in a suggestive order. That's different."

He laughed — a genuine laugh, brief and warm on its surface, and all the more chilling for its warmth. "Oh, they warned me about you. Matron Greer especially. *Sister Sterling doesn't lie*, they said. And yet." He spread one gloved hand, gesturing at nothing, at everything. "Yet here we both are at a dockyard gate, and neither of us is quite what our paperwork says we are. Isn't that so?"

They had reached the end of the warehouse, where a lamp burned above a door marked in fading paint: **CUSTOMS & MILITARY CONTROL**. Vane stopped, and Edmund stopped, because stopping was safer than walking on alone with him at her shoulder.

"Let me describe my afternoon," Vane said, and his voice dropped into something almost conversational, almost kind, which was how she knew the real conversation had begun. "I spent it with a coat. A small coat. Belonging to a small boy who was seen leaving the customs shed rather quickly yesterday when my men came to ask routine questions. The coat was recovered from a fishmonger's cart where it had been abandoned — left behind, presumably, in the haste of flight. A fine coat for a refugee child. Too fine. And sewn into its lining—" he paused, savouring it, a man arriving at the main course— "papers. Correspondence. Letters of a personal nature, addressed to parties whose names would mean nothing to you."

*They didn't get the satchel,* Edmund thought, and the relief hit her so hard she nearly swayed. *They got the coat. The lining. But the letters went into the satchel — no. No.* Her mind raced backward through the dock, through the shed, through Tomas's hands. She had hidden the ledger in the coat. She had hidden the letters—

She had hidden the letters in the coat.

Her face must have done something, because Vane's smile deepened by a fraction, the way ice deepens before it cracks.

"Ah," he said softly. "There it is. Yes, Sister Sterling. We have them."

For a moment the dock noise receded entirely — the gulls, the cranes, the endless shunting of lorries — and there was only the lamp above the customs door, guttering in the sea wind, and Vane's voice continuing with the smoothness of oil poured over still water.

"Now. Let me tell you what those letters are not. They are not, in law, evidence of anything. They are correspondence from a distressed young woman — a housemaid, formerly in service at a respectable household — written to her family, expressing grievances of an emotional character. A girl given to exaggeration. A girl, moreover, who is deceased, and cannot be cross-examined. Dead girls make poor witnesses, Sister. Their handwriting can be explained away. Their motives can be reassigned. Their names—" he examined his glove, adjusted it— "can be struck from records. It happens more often than you would credit. People vanish from files all the time. Whole lives, quietly removed, like a stain sponged off a tablecloth."

Edmund thought of her ledger, wrapped now against Cyrus's chest where the letters had been, and said nothing.

"But here is what they *are*," Vane went on. "They are embarrassment. Considerable embarrassment, to considerable people. People who fund hospitals. People who dine with undersecretaries. People whose goodwill keeps ships like the *Dover Queen* sailing and field stations like yours supplied with chloroform and catgut. Embarrassment, in wartime, is a currency. And I am in a position either to spend it—" he turned the flat of his hand upward, an open palm, a gift offered— "or to bury it. With your assistance, I will bury it, and no one will ever need to know it existed. Least of all you."

"And if I don't assist?"

Vane's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted — a depth sounding, a darkness finding its true measurement.

"Then the letters become something else. They become *exhibits*. They become the foundation of a very dull official inquiry into the handling of irregular correspondence discovered in the possession of an unregistered alien minor found at a forward medical station — harboured, I should add, contrary to every regulation, by a nursing sister who falsified no fewer than three documents to effect his evacuation." He sighed, with genuine-sounding regret. "Matron Greer signed one of those documents. Did you know that? Her name is on it. And the orderly — Tomas Brandt, is it? A foreign national, working under permit. Permits are so easily reviewed."

The wind came off the harbour and cut through Edmund's coat, and she thought of Greer's tired eyes across the desk at the station, the night she had said *harbouring that child endangers every woman under my roof*, and had let Edmund stay anyway. She thought of Tomas lifting Cyrus over the tailboard of the ambulance with those enormous gentle hands, saying *go, go*, as the searchers' lamps swung toward the shed.

"You would destroy them," Edmund said. "For a dead girl's reputation. For a family's dinner-table comfort."

"I would preserve *order*," Vane said. "Which is what the dead girl's reputation and the family's comfort both are, when you scrape off the sentiment. Order is the only thing standing between that crowd"— he nodding toward the docks, the heaving mass of refugees and wounded and lost humanity— "and what the crowd becomes when nobody takes an interest. I take an interest, Sister Sterling. You take an interest in names. I take an interest in consequences. Between us we might understand each other perfectly."

"And the boy?"

"The boy," Vane said, "is a detail. Details are absorbable. Give me the remaining papers — you have more, I know you do; the coat contained copies, incomplete, and incomplete things irritate me — and the boy travels. Today, tomorrow, I don't much care. First class, if you like. A guardian will be appointed. A good school. He'll forget the war entirely. Children do." He smiled again. "Forget faces, forget voices. It's practically a mercy. Someone once advised you along those lines, I believe. Whoever left him at your door knew what they were doing."

The note. *Forget this face.* Edmund felt the old coldness of those words move through her again, and beneath the coldness the fierce heat that had answered it every day since. Forget this face. As though a face were a document to be destroyed rather than a person to be remembered. As though forgetting were safety, when in her experience forgetting was only the war's second-favourite weapon, after shells.

"No," she said.

Vane raised his eyebrows, genuinely interested. "No to which part?"

"To all of it. To the arithmetic where a dead girl stays dead twice — once in body and once in record — so that living men can keep their seats at dinner. To trading a child's history for his ticket. To making Greer and Tomas pay for my decisions." She heard her own voice steady itself further with each clause, and wondered at the strangeness of it: fear and certainty using the same throat. "You're right that I keep names, Captain. So let me tell you one of mine: I don't negotiate with men who threaten nurses."

"A curious distinction," Vane said mildly. "Since I've threatened no one. I've merely described consequences. Threats are crude. Consequences are simply physics." He stepped closer, and up close she could see that his eyes were not cold after all, but warm — a banked, domestic warmth, like a stove in a comfortable parlour, and somehow that was infinitely worse. "But let me offer you a courtesy, since you extend me such frankness. Dawn. The ship sails at nine. My men board at eight to examine the passenger lists against my instructions. Until dawn, Sister Sterling, you may consider. Speak to whoever you speak to. Pray, if you do that. Count your arithmetic. And at eight o'clock, you may find me in the customs office, and tell me whether you are a sensible woman or a sentimental one. Sentimental women," he added, almost gently, "leave such interesting wreckage behind them."

He touched the brim of his cap — a gesture of flawless courtesy, a courtesy with a knife folded inside it — and turned away, and was absorbed by the crowd within seconds, a grey coat among grey coats, gone as though he had been invented solely for this conversation and now been decommissioned.

Edmund stood beneath the customs lamp for a long moment. Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them with a kind of clinical detachment, the detachment she used on wounds, assessing tremor, pallor, shock. Then she made them still, because stillness was a skill like any other, and walked back along the sea wall.

Cyrus was exactly where she had left him, cross-legged on the kit bag, drawing. Six gulls now wheeled above the masts. He held up his paper to show her: the woman's face, rendered in careful strokes, and beneath it — new, this week — a smaller figure holding its hand.

Edmund knelt and gathered him against her coat and breathed in the smell of him — woodsmoke and chalk and childhood — while the ship's steam rose white against the darkening sky, and somewhere behind her a bell rang the half hour, counting down the hours until eight o'clock, until dawn, until nine.

Eight hours. One berth. Three souls.

And Captain Vane, warming his hands over the ruin he intended to make of everyone she loved, waiting politely in a lamplit office for her answer — as though mercy were a thing that could be scheduled, filed, and struck from the record if it failed to appear.

She stood, took Cyrus's hand, and turned toward the refugees' fires at the end of the dock, where Private Hollis sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the water, waiting for a name to be spoken aloud.

She had until dawn to find a lie big enough to save them all.
