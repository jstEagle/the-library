## 18. Cyrus Speaks

The customs shed behind the quay had been given over to refugee families, and in its long dark belly a dozen small fires of breath rose and fell — mothers and children and old men laid out on straw that smelled of salt and cattle, sleeping the way people sleep when sleep is the only country left to them. Edmund lay awake among them, her back against a crate stenciled *MUNITIONS — KEEP DRY*, and listened to the ship's rigging tick against its masts somewhere out beyond the breakwater, a sound like a slow clock counting down a night she did not know how to spend.

Dawn was the hour Vane had given her. *Think until morning,* he had said, with the courtesy of a man laying a card face-down on a table, and she had carried his courtesy away from the dock like something sticky on her shoe.

Cyrus slept against her side. He had slept badly since Boulogne — since the whistle, since the crowds, since the moment on the train when Tomas had covered for them with a lie so smooth Edmund still marveled at it — and tonight he had finally gone under the way children go under, all at once, as if falling into a well. His head was heavy against her shoulder. His satchel was no longer his to carry; Vane's men had it, had taken it off the dock with the efficiency of men who knew exactly what they were looking for, and Edmund understood with a cold settled certainty that the loss of it should have been the end of everything. The letters were gone. The proof was gone.

And yet the boy remained. That was the strange arithmetic she kept arriving at and could not make balance: take away every document, every name, every scrap of paper that made him matter to powerful men — and he was still here, breathing against her arm, warm through two layers of wool, a weight more real than any ledger.

She had hidden one thing after all. Her hand rested inside her coat, over the notebook sewn flat against her ribs, and she counted its existence the way misers count coins in the dark. Names. Forty-one names now, if you counted Hollis twice — once as the man struck off the rolls, once as the man who had wept on the dock this afternoon because a nurse he barely remembered had spoken his true name back to him like a psalm.

*Make it mean something,* Hollis had said. She could still feel the grip of his hand, too strong for a man so hollowed.

The lamp at the end of the shed guttered. A child whimpered in its mother's arms and was hushed. Rain had begun again on the corrugated roof, soft and persistent, a sound like someone writing quickly.

Edmund closed her eyes and tried to draft tomorrow the way she drafted letters in her head before writing them — but every version began with a name written down and ended with someone left standing on the dock as the gangway drew up, and she could not finish a single one.

It was near the blackest part of the night — the hour she had learned to dread in the dressing stations, when the wounded who would die tended to choose it — that Cyrus stirred.

He did not wake the way children usually woke, with a start or a cry. He surfaced slowly, and he did not open his eyes, and his small hands found the front of her uniform and held on, the way he had held on since the first night, as though she were a rail fixed between him and deep water. She felt his breathing change. Felt the tremor move through him like weather.

"Shh," she murmured, not even properly awake herself, her voice worn down to its last thread. "You're here. You're with me."

And then, into the dark under her chin, muffled and cracked and rusted with disuse — the voice of a boy who had not spoken a word in all the weeks she had known him, five years old and already out of practice at being heard —

*"Marguerite."*

Edmund went very still.

She thought at first she had dreamed it, the way one dreams the sound of one's own name called across water. But his hands were trembling against her chest, and his breath came faster, and he said it again, louder this time, the word torn out of him rather than spoken — *"Marguerite"* — and then he was awake and crying, really crying for the first time since the night at the hospital door, great silent gulping sobs that shook his whole small body without making much sound at all, as though even his grief had learned to keep itself quiet.

"Cyrus." She gathered him up, pulled him half into her lap, rocked him the way she had rocked wounded men through the worst hours, one hand cradling the back of his head. "I'm here. I'm here. You're safe."

But he was not crying about safety. She knew that within seconds, with the terrible clarity that sometimes came to her in the small hours, the same clarity that had told her which men would live and which would not. He was crying the way people cry when something has been locked up inside them so long that the unlocking itself is the wound.

"Marguerite," he said again, and this time she heard what he was doing: he was not speaking to her. He was calling. Calling the way you call in the dark for someone who should be there and is not, the way a child calls down a stairwell, the way you call when you do not yet understand — or have only just, in this moment, alone in a shed full of strangers in a foreign port — understood.

His mother's name.

* * *

She held him until the storm inside him slowed. She had nothing to give him but warmth and rhythm, so she gave those, rocking slightly, humming some tuneless thing under her breath, and gradually the gulping eased and he lay against her, spent, his eyes open now and fixed on nothing, wet lashes catching what little light the lamp threw.

"You're all right," she said softly. "You're all right."

He turned his face up to her. And in the dimness he looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him before — not the wary blankness of the boy who would not speak, but something older, something that had been waiting behind his silence all this time like a letter behind a door.

"Marguerite," he said a third time. Quietly now. Deliberately. Testing whether the word could exist outside himself. Whether saying it aloud meant it would be taken from him, or whether — and this, Edmund thought later, was the thing that undid her — whether saying it might mean someone else would finally carry it too.

"Yes," Edmund said, though she did not know yet what she was agreeing to. Children say names; children grieve; she had seen it before, in Belgian farmhouses, in the mouths of girls younger than he was. But her heart was already moving ahead of her understanding, the way it always did, running toward whatever needed holding.

"Marguerite," he said, and then his small hand came up and touched her cheek, the way he had touched the drawings — those folded paper offerings left at her cot, the woman's face rendered over and over in careful childish lines, always burned at the edges, always the same face. He touched her cheek and then drew his finger down, tracing, and Edmund understood with a lurch that he was drawing her. That in the dark, with one finger, he was sketching the line of a jaw, the shape of lips, mapping the face from his papers onto hers.

Or finding the difference.

"She had brown hair," he said.

The words came haltingly, each one dredged up from somewhere deep, and his voice was strange to hear — high and thin and formal, the voice of a child who had perhaps once been spoken to carefully, by someone who loved language enough to give it to him whole. English, but with the faint rounded vowels of a household where French had also lived.

"She brushed it a hundred times. She showed me. In the glass." His brow furrowed, reaching. "A hundred. She counted."

"Oh," Edmund said. It was all she could manage. Her throat had closed.

"She sang when she thought I was asleep." His eyes moved over Edmund's face, searching it. "At the window. At the big house she went to work. She wore grey. Grey with white." His small fist pressed against his mouth. "I waited for her."

The shed seemed to contract around them. The rain wrote on. Somewhere out on the water the ship groaned against its anchor chain, and Edmund heard it as if from very far away, because she had gone somewhere else entirely — she had gone to a grey dress with a white apron, going out through gates of a large house before dawn, a girl with a hundred brushed strokes of brown hair singing to a boy who pretended to sleep so the singing would continue.

The housemaid. The Ashcombes' housemaid. For weeks Edmund had known those words, had read them in stolen letters naming names and contracts and sums, and she had thought she understood what they meant. Evidence. Leverage. A name attached to a function attached to a family that shipped men's boots across the Channel stitched by women paid in nothing.

She had not understood anything.

Marguerite. Not *the maid.* Not *the woman in the drawings.* Marguerite, who brushed her hair a hundred strokes and sang at windows and wore grey with white and had a son who waited for her and waited and waited, and who had watched — Edmund was sure of it now, sure with a sick drop of the stomach — watched whatever had happened to her. Watched it, and been carried away from it, and handed to a stranger at a hospital door with a note instructing the world to forget his face, because his face was connected to hers, and hers was connected to numbers in ledgers, and numbers in ledgers were the only things men like the Ashcombes believed could truly be killed.

"How old are you, Cyrus?" she asked gently, though she had asked before and received silence.

He held up fingers. Five. Then, after a moment, he folded two of them down and held up three, and pointed at himself, and then held the five up again, and Edmund worked it through the fog of her own grief: five years old, and three of them — or some portion of them, some season, some eternity measured in a child's arithmetic — spent at the big house, in the grey-and-white world of servants' stairs, while his mother worked.

"And then?" she asked, barely above a whisper. "Then what happened, my darling?"

He did not answer with words. He answered with his hands: he raised both of them before his face, palms out, and pushed at the air, and his breath went short, and Edmund saw the memory hit him like cold water — smoke, she thought, or shouting, or hands — and she caught his wrists gently and brought his fists against her heart and held them there.

"Don't," she said. "Not if it hurts. Not tonight. You've told me the most important part."

He looked up at her, questioning, the way he had looked at her the first time she'd cut bread for him into the shape of a bird.

"Her name," Edmund said, and her voice broke on it, and she let it break, because there was no one in this shed who mattered, because some things deserved to cost something. "You gave me her name. Now I know her. Marguerite. Now she isn't lost."

Something happened in his face then that she would remember for the rest of her life. The fear did not leave it — she suspected nothing would ever fully remove the fear from this child's face — but beside the fear something else appeared, something like the first crack of light under a door: the look of a person discovering that testimony works. That a name spoken aloud does not vanish into the air but lands somewhere, in someone, and stays.

"I forgot," he whispered. "I tried to forget. The note said."

"I know."

"To forget my face." His brow creased with the effort of a child parsing an instruction built for adults. "So they couldn't find me. Because I look like her."

"Yes," Edmund said. "Yes, sweetheart. You look like her."

She reached into the pocket of her coat — slowly, so as not to alarm him — and took out the stub of pencil she carried everywhere, and one of the folded papers, one of his drawings that she had kept, the most recent, the woman's face with its burned edges. She smoothed it on her knee.

"What was her other name?" she asked. "Marguerite — and then? Did she have another name?"

He thought hard, lips moving. Then: "Bellamy." He pronounced it carefully, two syllables, learned by rote. "Marguerite Bellamy. She signed the letters. To her sister. In Rouen."

Edmund's hand stopped moving.

*A sister. In Rouen.*

All these weeks she had read those letters as evidence — dates, sums, the word *contract*, the word *Ashcombe* — skimming past the human freight they must have carried, because reading them closely had felt like a violation and she had permitted herself only what she needed. She had never asked what a housemaid wrote home about, in letters that a family like the Ashcombes would burn or bury or kill to recover. Letters to a sister. In Rouen. Which meant somewhere in Rouen there might be a woman still living who had received them, who might hold duplicates, who might —

Who might be the second copy of everything Vane's men had seized tonight on the dock.

Edmund sat very still in the dark, holding a sleeping-weight child and a piece of paper with a dead girl's face on it, and felt the whole shape of the war shift around her the way ice shifts on a river — silently, and then all at once.

"Marguerite Bellamy," she said aloud, and took the pencil, and beneath the drawn face, in her small precise nurse's hand — the hand that had recorded forty-one erased men — she wrote the name. Date unknown. Place unknown. Mother of Cyrus. Sister in Rouen.

"There," she whispered. "Recorded."

Cyrus watched her write it, and when she finished he reached out and touched the wet ink, and got a grey smudge on his fingertip, and looked at it, and then — impossibly, horribly, wonderfully — he smiled. Just briefly. Just the ghost of a smile, exhausted and tear-streaked. But it was aimed at the page, at the name, at the fact of it existing in someone's handwriting besides his own memory.

Then his eyes closed, and he was asleep almost instantly, the way children are, grief spent and done with in a way adult bodies never permit. Edmund eased him down onto the straw, arranged her coat over him, and sat back against the crate with her heart hammering and the drawing trembling in her hands.

* * *

She did not sleep. Of course she did not sleep.

Through the last hours of the dark she sat with the notebook open on her knee — the real one, the sewn one, the one whose existence Vane suspected and could not yet prove — and she read it not as she had ever read it before. She had always read it as a record of the dead and the disappeared. Tonight she read it as evidence. Tonight she read it the way a lawyer would read it, or a court, or a sister in Rouen: forty-one names, dates, regiments, the struck-off men, and now, newly entered, a woman's name with a city attached to it, a thread leading out of this shed, across this country, to a door she had never considered knocking on.

The letters Vane had seized were copies of nothing — originals, singular, gone. But letters get answers. Letters come from somewhere. Marguerite Bellamy had written to her sister, which meant the sister had written back, which meant there was paper in Rouen with the Ashcombe name on it, and a living witness who had wondered, all this time, why the letters stopped.

Vane had the satchel. Vane did not have this. Vane did not have a boy who could speak, after all — a boy everyone believed was mute, a boy Vane had dismissed precisely because he was small and silent, and who had opened his mouth in the night and produced the single most dangerous word on the coast of France.

*Marguerite.*

The rain thinned toward grey. Through the shed's broken doorway Edmund could see the harbor emerging from the dark: the ship at anchor, the cranes, the wet stones of the quay shining under the early lamps, and — posted at the corner of the customs office like a nail driven into the morning — the silhouette of one of Vane's men, stamping his feet against the cold, waiting for dawn on her behalf.

She had until morning. Morning was here.

Edmund put away the notebook, tucked the drawing back into her pocket, and bent to wake Cyrus with a kiss on his hair — and paused, and stayed bent over him a moment longer, breathing him in, because she could feel it already, the way one feels a coming fever: the day assembling itself around her, the berths and the lists and the lies, the three lives and the shrinking room, and she understood that before the tide turned she would have to decide, finally and irrevocably, who stood on this dock when the gangway went up.

The ship's whistle sounded across the water, long and low, and the boy slept on, and the soldier Hollis turned over in his straw across the shed, and Edmund Sterling rose to do the coldest arithmetic of her life.
