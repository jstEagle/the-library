## 19. The Arithmetic of Mercy

The customs shed at the end of Pier Four had been requisitioned as a counting house, and Edmund had come to think of it as a temple of a particularly cruel god. Inside, behind a trestle table scarred by salt and boot-heels, a Royal Army Service Corps clerk named Whitcombe added and subtracted human beings with the flat diligence of a man tallying sacks of flour. Wounded: forty-one. Nurses: six. Civilian displaced: eleven, provisional, subject to confirmation. Berths remaining after the walking wounded boarded: one.

One.

Edmund stood at the counter with her hands folded so that no one could see them shake, and asked him to repeat the figure, as though arithmetic were a thing that might repent of itself if spoken twice.

"One," Whitcombe said again, not unkindly. He was perhaps fifty, with a moustache the colour of wet ash and eyes that had stopped registering individual faces sometime in 1914. "After your wounded, Sister, and the matron's allocation for the children already listed, there is one civilian berth outstanding. The Harbour Master's office confirms it. One." He wrote the numeral in his ledger — an official ledger, she thought, a ledger with no conscience attached to it — and blotted it with a small rubber stamp, and that was that. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the Admiralty, three lives had been divided by one berth and the remainder was expected to simply disappear, the way remainders always did in war.

She walked back out onto the dock because the walls of the shed had begun to lean toward her, or she toward them. Night was coming down over Boulogne in layers — first the grey of the water, then the grey of the smoke lying on the water, then a bruised violet above the cliffs that might once have been called beautiful by someone who had never watched a ship's searchlight swing across a crowd of refugees and light them up like startled animals. The *Maidstone* — no, the *Invicta*, she corrected herself; she had learned its name that afternoon and would not let herself confuse it, because names mattered, names were the whole point — rode at her mooring with her lights doused except for one green lamp at the gangway, burning like a very small promise.

Three people who needed that promise. One berth.

She found a sheltered corner between two stacks of crated ammunition — an absurd place to sit, but the crates broke the wind off the harbour, and tonight she would take absurdity wherever it offered warmth. She opened her kit bag, took out the small tin that had once held throat lozenges, and inside it found paper, pencil stub, and the ledger itself, which she had taken back from Cyrus's coat only that morning when the adjutant's questions began. The ledger sat in her lap like something alive. Forty-one pages of names. She did not open it. Tonight it was not the ledger she needed. Tonight she needed paper.

She laid three sheets on a crate, weighted them with pebbles from the dock edge, and began to draft tomorrow.

* * *

*Version the first,* she wrote at the top of the first sheet, because she had learned in four years of dressing stations that horror survives better when it is labelled.

*Version the first: I board.*

In this version, she put herself on the *Invicta*. She told herself the story the way a sensible woman would tell it: I am his guardian, his sponsor, the only adult he will follow onto a strange ship in a strange language; without me he will not go up the gangway at all; therefore my passage is his passage, and the logic of it is unimpeachable.

It was a good story. It was even mostly true. Cyrus clung to her coat hem in crowds; he had slept every night since the dressing station curled against her side with the satchel strapped over his chest like a second heart; the one time a Red Cross volunteer had tried to take his hand and lead him to the children's mess, he had gone rigid and silent and looked at Edmund over the woman's shoulder with eyes that contained, quite precisely, the whole argument against the plan.

But the story had a hole in it large enough to sail the *Invicta* through, and the hole was named Hollis.

Private Elias Hollis lay in Ward Tent C on the hill above the port, transferred down from the clearing station two days ago with a label tied to his buttonhole that read, in someone's efficient hand, *NYD — nervous, unfit to travel, no next of kin.* Not yet diagnosed. No kin. No name, really — because the name the army had for him was *deserter struck off*, and the name he had for himself, when he could find himself at all beneath the shelling that still went on perpetually behind his eyes, was a name he had confessed to Edmund at three o'clock one grey morning: *Elias*, he had said, *my mother called me Elias, Sister, please remember, they took it off the books like I was never whelped.* And she had written it down, because writing things down was what she did instead of weeping, and because a person unrecorded was never truly lost — she had built her whole quiet rebellion on that article of faith, and here now was the faith come due, presented for payment.

If she boarded, Hollis stayed. And Hollis, unfit to travel, sponsorless, nameless in the eyes of the RASC, would be returned to a convalescent depot and then to a discharge process that had already mislaid him once, and the shell shock that made him flinch at the slam of a lorry door would finish what the front had started, slowly, in some ward where nobody knew his mother had called him Elias.

Edmund looked at the words *I board* and found she could not make her pencil write the sentence that followed them. She turned the sheet over.

*Version the second.*

*Version the second: Hollis boards.*

This was the version that virtue argued for, and Edmund distrusted it immediately, because virtue in her experience arrived most insistently precisely when it was asking someone else to pay.

In this version she marched to Ward Tent C and told Elias Hollis that there was a berth for him on the England boat, that his name meant something, that the ledger had kept him and now the ledger would deliver him home to whatever remained of his mother's calling. In this version his face — that grey, hollowed face, twenty-six years old and carved like sixty — cracked open into something she had never seen on it, something like sunrise over a flooded field. In this version she gave away her own place with a clean hand and stood on the dock waving until the green lamp disappeared, and felt the particular warm ache of having done the right thing.

And Cyrus?

In this version Cyrus went into the care of the Quaker relief workers, kind people, competent people, who would list him as *unknown child, male, approx. 5 years, non-verbal* in yet another ledger, another book of names not his, and wait for relatives to claim him. Except no relatives would come. She knew that now. She had read the letters in the satchel; she knew whose household he had lived in and why nothing about that household would ever surface to claim a housemaid's bastard son. Unknown children without families did not stay in kind hands. They went into industrial schools; they went into institutions; they vanished into the same administrative fog that had swallowed Hollis's name, and somewhere in ten years' time a boy who would not speak became a file closed for want of further enquiries.

And worse — far worse, and she made herself look at it — Vane wanted the boy. Captain Alistair Vane, with his polished vowels and his cold patient eyes, wanted Cyrus and the satchel, and if the satchel left the country under a nurse's escort the evidence died in transit or was seized; but if the boy stayed, alone, unclaimed, in a French port town where Vane had staff and influence and time —

No. She pressed the pencil so hard against the crate that the point snapped. She sharpened it with her dressing scissors against her knee, breathing carefully.

Version the second was a lie dressed in virtue's clothes. It bought Elias's redemption with Cyrus's life, and called the transaction mercy. She had seen enough transactions dressed as mercy to last her a lifetime.

She turned the sheet over.

* * *

The third sheet she saved for last not because it was hardest but because she already knew what it said, the way you know the diagnosis before the doctor speaks. Some part of her had known since the counting shed, since the word *one* fell out of Whitcombe's mouth like a coin dropped down a drain.

*Version the third: the boy boards alone.*

She wrote it and then sat with it while the searchlight swept the docks again, whitewashing the crowd, the cranes, the tents, her own hands, and moving on.

There was a way to make it work. There was, God help her, always a way; four years of war had taught her that the machinery of any army could be made to carry almost anything if you greased it with paperwork. The travel chit for a displaced minor required a sponsoring adult or an institution at the port of arrival. She could not sponsor him from Boulogne — but she could send the sponsorship ahead. She had friends from training days in London; she had Matron Greer, who had allies she had hinted at and never named; she had the letters themselves, copied — she was nearly certain Greer had copied them before mailing, the woman missed nothing — which meant leverage existed in the world beyond this dock. A letter carried by the ship's purser, wired ahead, could put a Quaker family or a matron's connection at Euston or Charing Cross with papers in hand when the *Invicta* docked. It was fragile. It depended on telegrams arriving, on strangers keeping promises, on a five-year-old holding together for thirty hours among strangers without the one fixed point of his small universe.

And it required the satchel to travel with him, and the satchel was the single most dangerous object on this coast. If Vane's men searched the boy at the gangway — and they would, they were already searching, she had seen the adjutant's corporals working the queues with their little lists — the letters would be taken, and then everything Cyrus was carrying would become a reason to pull him back off the deck.

So the letters could not go in the satchel. Which meant — and here her mind did the thing it did at three in the morning, the cold clear thing, the surgeon's thing — the letters should go nowhere near the ship at all. Let Vane find them, or not find them, in some safe or some ditch far from the gangway. Let the boy carry only what could not incriminate him. Let him carry —

Her eyes went down to her lap. To the ledger.

Forty-one names. Elias Hollis among them, written in her careful hand the morning after his confession, with the notation *struck off as deserter; witness states he never left his post; recommend restoration.* Names of men from Ypres and Passchendaele, from the dressing stations at Brandhoek and Dozinghem. Dates. Units. The particulars of men whom the army had decided out of existence — desertions falsified to save officers' statistics, deaths recorded under wrong numbers, a whole quiet country of the erased. It was proof of nothing about the Ashcombes. But it was proof of *her* — of her method, her witness, four years of her testimony in her own handwriting — and if anything happened to her, if Vane's threat against the nurses she'd left behind came true, if the inquiry that must someday come needed a witness who could no longer speak —

Then the ledger should go with the boy. The ledger, and the memory of her face. Two things to carry across the water, neither of them heavier than a coat pocket.

She looked at what she had drafted and understood, with a grief that arrived not like a wave but like the slow tide coming in over flats, that she believed none of it. That was the discovery of the night, the real one, underneath all the logistics: she had written three versions of tomorrow and believed none of them.

Version one was selfishness wearing duty's coat — no, not even that, it was simpler than that and more forgivable: it was love refusing to do sums. Version two was cruelty wearing virtue's coat. Version three was the truest and the most terrible, and it rested entirely on hope, and hope, she had learned at a dressing station within earshot of the guns, was not a strategy; hope was what you felt while the strategy failed.

She thought of Cyrus asleep just now in the relief tent, wrapped in a Grey blanket much too big for him, the satchel straps still over his shoulders because she had learned not to try to loosen them. She thought of how he had begun, in the last week, to bring her things — folded paper, a drawing, once a button polished bright — offerings, courtships, the wordless diplomacy of a small creature deciding whom to trust. He trusted her. That was the fact from which all three versions hung like laundry on a line, and the wind coming off the harbour was going to take one of them, whichever one she failed to hold.

She thought of Hollis saying *please remember* and of her own answer, given without hesitation because hesitation would have been obscene: *I have remembered. It's written down.* She had made a promise with the whole weight of four years behind it. Promises made to the forgotten are the only promises that cannot be renegotiated, because the forgotten have nothing else to trade.

And she thought of herself, and was honest, which was the hardest arithmetic of all. Edmund Sterling, thirty-one years old, no family living, no sweetheart waiting, a woman the war had hollowed and filled again with other people's blood until she was mostly a container for service. If anyone's life could be spent here without waste, hers was the obvious candidate. The sum was simple. The sum had been simple for days.

But oh, she wanted to live. That was the hot thing under the cold clarity, the thing no ledger line could hold. She wanted spring. She wanted to drink coffee in a room that smelled of nothing but coffee. She wanted to see whether Cyrus's voice, whenever it came back, would be high or low, and what he would choose to say first. She wanted — and here the tide came all the way in, quiet and total — she wanted to be the one at the other end of the platform at Charing Cross, the fixed point, the person the boy crossed the water to find. Every version of tomorrow in which she was not on that ship was a version in which she gambled a child's entire future on the competence of strangers, and staked against it the only currency she had left, which was herself.

A bell buoy clanked somewhere out past the mole. The tide turned in her, too.

*Not one of us vanishes,* she wrote at the bottom of the third sheet, though she did not know yet where the sentence came from or what it promised. *Not Hollis, not the boy, not — * and there the pencil stopped, because she could not write *not me* and mean it, and she could not strike it out without meaning something else.

She gathered the three sheets, and would have burned them in the brazier by the shed door — there was no room in the world for drafts of a choice not yet made — but her matches were soaked through from the harbour spray, and the paper went back into the tin, folded small, three tomorrows folded like letters to no one.

She was standing to leave, stiff with cold, when she saw the orderly picking his way along the pier toward her with a lantern — not one of theirs; a French civilian, a *facteur*, a postman, of all impossible things, still doing his rounds through a war. He held out an envelope grimed with coal dust and mud, and said something apologetic in French, and the address, in a hand she knew as well as her own — tight, upright, unsentimental, the hand of a woman who had run three hospitals and buried two matrons younger than herself — was her own name.

Matron Greer. From the dressing station, hundreds of miles and a whole collapsed army away.

Edmund tore it open with fingers gone suddenly clumsy, and the lantern-light caught the first line, and the first line was enough to stop her breath:

*Sterling — trust no officer, the family has friends everywhere. Hold the line. You are not alone.*
