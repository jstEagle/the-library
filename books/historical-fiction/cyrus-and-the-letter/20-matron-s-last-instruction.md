## 20. Matron's Last Instruction

The letter came on a Tuesday, though at Boulogne the days had stopped meaning anything. Edmund learned later that it had traveled by three hands — a Red Cross van, a quartermaster's bicycle, and finally a boy of perhaps twelve who wove through the dock crowds selling newspapers and carrying, tucked inside his cap, whatever else people would pay him to deliver. He found her because she had made herself findable: every morning she stood for ten minutes beside the customs shed where the queue for passes began, in plain view, holding Cyrus by the hand and looking as though she were waiting for no one.

It was a discipline of hope disguised as boredom. She had been performing it for six days when the boy tugged her sleeve and held up the envelope without a word, took her coin, and vanished into the grey press of bodies as if he had never existed.

Edmund knew the hand at once. Matron Greer wrote the way she ran a ward — upright, economical, every stroke doing its duty without complaint. The letters were small and even, pressed hard enough into the paper that Edmund could feel them with her thumb, as though Greer had wanted the words to survive anything short of fire. The envelope bore no return address. The postmark had been smudged by rain somewhere along the way, but the smell of it — cold paper, damp wool, a ghost of carbolic — was the smell of the dressing station they had left behind on the night train, and for a moment Edmund could not open it. She stood there with the envelope in her gloved hands and let herself be, briefly, back in that long hut with its rows of cots and its lamps turned low, before she remembered where she was and what waiting did to people who lingered.

She read it standing against the customs shed, with one eye always moving across the quay.

*Sister Sterling —*

*I will be brief, as brief things travel better.*

*You have reached the coast, I know, because Tomas's sister has heard from Tomas, and Tomas does not write unless he has something worth risking the post for. I will not ask what you carry or whom. I asked enough questions when you were under my roof and I asked too few, which is worse, and we will neither of us speak of it again.*

*Understand this, and understand it plainly: the family you have crossed has friends everywhere. Not merely in Whitehall — in shipping offices, in provost marshal's tents, in the comfortable clubs where men decide which ships sail and which lists are consulted. Captain Vane is not an anomaly. He is an instrument, and instruments are replaced when they break. Do not comfort yourself with the thought that his removal ends your danger. If he falls, another will be sent who may be cleverer.*

*Therefore: hold the line. By which I mean do not surrender what you carry, do not sign anything, do not accept any officer's protection, and do not believe any promise made after dark. Trust no officer — none, however courteous, however decorated, however tired. Courtesy is their native language and they speak it best when they mean you harm.*

*What I can offer is this: I have copied certain papers and sent them where they will do the most good, slowly and through channels no one watches, because women's channels are invisible — that is our advantage and it is nearly our only one. If all goes as it should, you will not need to know what I have done. You need only hold until the ship sails, and put what matters aboard it.*

*The nurses here ask after you. I have told them you were transferred. It is not entirely a lie; everything is a transfer, in the end.*

*Do not reply to this address. Burn this letter tonight. And Edmund —* (here the pen had pressed harder, so hard the paper had nearly given way) *— you were never insubordinate under my command. Whatever anyone says afterward, remember that I say otherwise.*

*E. Greer*

Edmund read it twice, folded it small, and did not burn it — not yet, not there, with no private place and the wind off the harbour carrying voices further than anyone intended. Instead she slid it inside her glove, against the palm, where its small stiff rectangle beat against her pulse like a second heart.

Hunted together. That was the phrase that assembled itself in her mind as she walked back along the quay, past the tarpaulin shelters where refugees sat in families like bundles someone had dropped, past the wounded lying on stretchers in the rain with oilcloth over their faces because oilcloth was what there was. She had thought of herself, these six days, as alone — alone with Cyrus, alone with the satchel's contents sewn now into places Vane's searchers had not yet thought to look, alone with the arithmetic that woke her each morning before the gulls did. Greer's letter dismantled that solitude in four paragraphs. There was Greer, copying papers by lamplight in a station still smelling of the evacuation. There was Tomas, detained — *held for questioning*, said the note he had contrived to send his sister, as if he had been caught at a border crossing rather than at a gate he had opened on purpose. There was Tomas's sister, passing words through the post like a woman passing water buckets along a chain in a fire. And there was whoever received what Greer had copied, somewhere in London, in rooms where the Ashcombe name did not open doors.

They were a chain, then. A poor, improvised, mostly female chain, stretched from a mud-flat in Flanders to a dock in Picardy, and every link of it was holding.

* * *

Cyrus was where she had left him, sitting on an overturned crate behind the aid tent with Private Hollis, and the sight of them together did what the letter alone could not quite do: it steadied her.

They made an unlikely pair, the shell-shocked soldier and the silent child. Hollis had deteriorated on the journey south — the trains undid him, the whistles especially, and at Boulogne the whistles never stopped — but near Cyrus some knot in him loosened. He talked to the boy in a low, unhurried murmur, not expecting answers, describing the clouds, the ships, the shapes of cranes against the sky, as though narrating the world on the boy's behalf so the boy should not have to fear it unexplained. Cyrus, for his part, sat close enough to touch and drew. He drew constantly now, on scraps of wrapping paper Edmund salvaged, and the drawings had changed since the night he had spoken his mother's name. The face still came — the same oval, the same careful mouth — but lately it appeared whole, unburned, sometimes smiling, once wearing a hat Cyrus had invented from memory or from wish. Grief, Edmund thought, does not leave. It changes its handwriting.

"Sister." Hollis rose when he saw her, the old reflex of a soldier greeting an officer, and then seemed to remember he was no longer anything official and sat back down, embarrassed. His true name — Corporal Alfred Hollis, struck from the rolls as a deserter on a day he had been, in fact, dug out of a collapsed trench half-dead — rested in her ledger and, since Boulogne, in a fold of paper sewn into his own jacket lining, because she had decided a man should carry his own name into whatever came next. "There's a man been round twice asking after a woman and child travelling together. Civilian coat. Not uniform."

Edmund kept her voice level. "Did he see the boy?"

"He saw the boy's back." Hollis's hands worked in his lap, the tremor that never fully left him. "I moved the crate so his back was to the tent flap. Seemed the thing to do."

It was more than the thing to do; it was strategy from a man whose file said he had fled his post, and Edmund felt the old guilt shift its weight again. She had recorded Hollis's name. She had preserved it. But it was Hollis, trembling, positioning crates between a child and a stranger's eyes, who was fighting for something at last — fighting the only way his broken nerve allowed, quietly, from a seated position, guarding a boy not his own.

"Thank you, Corporal," she said, and gave him the rank deliberately, and watched it land in him like warmth.

She told them both about the letter, or as much of it as served. That Greer was safe and working. That friends existed in places they couldn't see. That the instruction was to hold. Cyrus listened with his whole body, the way he did, eyes fixed on her mouth as though language were something he was relearning by watching it formed. When she finished he reached up and touched the lapel of her coat, where the ledger no longer was — she had moved it days ago, redistributed its weight into the satchel's hidden seams — and looked a question at her.

"It's safe," she said. "It's exactly where it needs to be."

He considered this, nodded once, and returned to his drawing. Later she would find that he had drawn the two of them, herself and him, standing on a grey line that might have been a dock or might have been the edge of the world, and above them a shape with funnels, and beyond the shape, scribbled in his fierce child's crayon, nothing at all.

* * *

She burned the letter that night.

Not wholly — she could not bring herself to feed all of it to the small spirit stove in the room over the chandler's shop where she and Cyrus slept among the smell of rope and tar. The final page, the page with Greer's last lines, she cut free with scissors and sewed flat beneath the lining of her coat, over her ribs, where a letter could do less harm than a promise. The rest went into the stove in strips, and she watched Greer's careful economy curl and blacken and go up, and thought about how strange it was that the truest things she possessed now existed only in fire-resistant forms: memory, and the stitching of other women's hands.

Cyrus watched the burning from the bed, knees drawn up, and when the last strip flared he said — his voice still a rare event, spent like coins hoarded for necessity — "Greer."

"Yes," Edmund said. "Matron Greer."

"Kind," he said. Then, after a silence in which the stove ticked and the harbour muttered below the window: "Everyone."

She looked at him. Five years old, minus whatever those months had taken from him, sitting in a borrowed nightshirt in a strange port with his mother dead and his name — his real name, whatever it was — locked behind his teeth. *Everyone,* he had said, and meant it: the nurses who had smuggled him milk, the matron who had pretended blindness, the orderly now sitting in some detention shed because a gate had opened when it shouldn't, the soldier upstairs who angled crates. Everyone. The child had conducted, without a word, a survey of the human race more generous than any she had managed in thirty years, and his finding was: kind.

"Everyone," she agreed, her throat tight. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we board."

Tomorrow. The word sat in the room with them after his eyes closed, heavy as cargo.

* * *

Sleep did not come for her. It rarely did, but this night the arithmetic was louder than usual. One berth. She had known it since the manifests went up at the shipping office — known it with the cold clarity that had come to her sometime between Ypres and the coast, the clarity she neither welcomed nor resisted, because resisting it changed nothing. The *Delphine*-something — the ship's name kept sliding away from her, which she suspected was her mind's small mercy — would take two hundred and forty wounded and forty civilians. The wounded list filled itself; the civilian list had been cut twice, and cut again, and when the final revision had gone up on the office door that afternoon, hers and Cyrus's names had appeared together provisionally, subject to space, subject to the harbourmaster's discretion, subject to everything. Subject, in truth, to the fact that she had not yet decided.

Three lives. Hollis, who could not legally travel without a sponsor and whose sponsor, if he had one, would have to give up his own place. Cyrus, who carried evidence in his coat and murder in his memory and no claim on anyone except the claims she had chosen to honor. Herself, who had no claim at all, really — a nurse without a station, a woman whose war would end whenever the paperwork remembered her, and whose absence from England would inconvenience precisely no one.

Except one small person asleep in the next bed, who had said *everyone* and meant her.

She got up in the dark and stood at the window. Below, the harbour lay under lamplight and drizzle, the ship a black mass with a few working lights, loading already, hours early, cranes swinging their slow pendulum arcs. Somewhere down there Vane's adjutant would be making his rounds, and Vane himself — she had seen him at evening, on the far quay, motionless under an umbrella held by a soldier, watching the crowd the way a man watches water for something he has dropped. He had until dawn, he'd said. She had said she would think. Both statements had been true, and both had been lies.

Her hand went to her ribs, to the stitched rectangle of Greer's last page. *Hold the line.* Well. She had held lines before — the line of a dressing station at third light, the line of a tally in a forbidden notebook, the line between the truth and the version of it that kept people alive. Lines were what she knew.

And it occurred to her then, standing in the dark above the harbour with the child breathing evenly behind her, that Greer's instruction contained more than caution. *Put what matters aboard it.* Greer had not written *save yourself.* She had written *put what matters aboard* — and Edmund understood, with the sudden quiet click of a thing long looked-at finally seen, that the decision had already been made for her, weeks ago, by every choice that had brought her here. The ledger had gone into the satchel. The satchel had gone into the coat. The coat would go onto the ship, worn by a boy who would grow up and one day read, in her own cramped hand, every name she had refused to let vanish — and would understand why the last entry was going to be blank, waiting, because she had given her berth not to a dying man nor to herself but to the future, which was the only thing, in the end, worth the passage.

Down on the quay, a whistle sounded — not the ship's, shorter, sharper, a police whistle — and a knot of figures detached itself from the shadows near the customs shed and began, with terrible orderliness, to move along the line of shelters where the refugees slept. Lanterns swung. A voice called out names, checking them against a list.

Edmund dressed quickly, and shook Cyrus gently awake.

"Get up, my darling," she whispered. "We're boarding early."

Below and to the left, moving against the flow of lanterns, she saw the familiar uneven silhouette of Private Hollis coming toward the chandler's stairs at a pace she had never seen him manage — and behind him, unhurried, umbrella furled now like a sword at rest, came Captain Vane.
