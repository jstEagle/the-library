## 21. Boarding Call

The whistle came at half past nine, a long iron cry that rolled across the harbour and broke over the crowds on the dock like weather. Edmund felt it in her teeth before she heard it properly — a vibration in the fillings of her back molars, in the bones behind her ears — and then the sound arrived, enormous and indifferent, and four thousand people turned their heads as one toward the grey hull waiting beyond the mole.

The *Duchess of Argyll*, they called her, though she had been a cross-channel ferry in another life and was now something stranger: a ship with one berth left in the world's arithmetic, or three, or none, depending on how the morning went.

Edmund stood very still and let the crowd move around her.

She had learned that trick in the dressing station at Ypres — when the shells came close and the wounded began to scream, you could either run about doing ten things badly or stand still for five seconds and choose the one thing that mattered. She stood still now. Cyrus's hand was folded inside hers, small and cold, his fingers curled around two of hers the way ivy curls around a fence post. He did not look at the ship. He looked at the coats between the bodies ahead of them, at the gaps, at the routes of escape he seemed to map without any face to show he was doing it. Five years old. He had learned to think like a hunted thing already.

*Not today,* she wanted to tell him. *Today we simply wait in a queue.*

But she had learned not to promise anything out loud in this country. Promises were the first casualties.

* * *

The boarding proceeded with the terrible beauty of bureaucracy. That was what struck her, standing in the press of refugees and stretcher-bearers and men from the Transport Corps who shouted numbers at each other across a barricade of rope: how orderly it all was. How calm. A war had chewed up a continent and spat its remnants onto this dock, and here was a clerk with a clipboard under an oilcloth awning, ticking names off a list with a pencil stub, as though a pencil stub could sort the century into those who would cross and those who would not.

"Stretcher cases first," the clerk called, again and again, in English and in broken French. "Stretchers to the head of the gangway. Ambulance men to your marks."

They came up the plank in slow procession. Edmund watched them because watching them was the only useful thing she could do, and also because every stretcher was a name she might have written down once, in another notebook, in another life. A gunner with both eyes bandaged, his jaw working silently under the gauze. A boy of perhaps nineteen whose left leg ended at the knee in a neat cone of fresh lint, who kept trying to salute the orderlies carrying him and kept knocking his own cap off. A sergeant so wrapped in blankets that only his moustache showed, moving with the tide of him toward the ship like a parcel rather than a man.

The gangway groaned under each load. The sailors took the stretchers with hands that knew their work, tilting them level at the steepest part of the plank, passing them inward to where the ship's lights made a warm yellow rectangle of her open hatchway. Into that light they went, one after another, swallowed by the hull. The hatch swallowed and swallowed and never filled.

Behind Edmund the crowd pressed forward a few inches whenever a gap opened, the way water finds the hole in a dyke, and the military police along the rope let them advance and then pushed them back with flat palms and flat voices. *Back. Back there. You'll be called.* Everyone knew the words meant nothing and everyone obeyed them anyway, because obedience to nothing was better than chaos, and because hope, in a queue, has nowhere else to put itself.

Cyrus tugged her hand. She bent.

He pointed — discreetly, with his chin, the way she had seen grown men point in occupied villages — toward the far end of the dock, near the customs shed.

Captain Vane was standing beneath the lamp by the shed door, and he was not looking at the stretchers, and he was not looking at the list, and he was not looking at the sea.

He was looking at the crowd. Methodically. Left to right, front rank to rear, the way a man reads a page.

* * *

Edmund turned her body a quarter-turn so that her shoulder shielded Cyrus from that patient gaze, an instinctive movement, a hen with a wing, and hated herself even as she made it, because it told her something: she had stopped thinking of herself as a nurse guarding a child and started thinking of herself as an animal guarding young. There was no romance in it. There was only teeth and fear and arithmetic.

Vane wore his greatcoat unbelted, which somehow made it worse. A belted coat is a soldier; an unbelted coat is a man who has decided the cold does not apply to him. His adjutant — the pale lieutenant with the moustache like a smudge, the one who had come asking questions at the refugee canteen two days ago, *have you seen a boy, madam, travelling alone or perhaps not alone* — stood a pace behind him with a leather document case held against his chest like a hymn book.

They were searching. Not frantically, not with whistles and shouts, but the way one searches a house one already owns: room by room, certain of finding.

Edmund counted her assets the way she had counted berths the night before, lying awake in the loft above the canteen with Cyrus breathing against her back.

The ledger was gone from her — sewn now into the lining of the satchel strap, riding against Cyrus's chest where the letters used to be, so that the record of forty-one struck-off men lay over the boy's heart. If Vane took the satchel he would take everything, and she had weighed that risk in the dark and decided that a burden split between two people is harder to confiscate than a burden carried by one. Or she had told herself so. At four in the morning, all reasoning feels sound.

The forged chit was in her bodice, folded to the size of a stamp, carrying a name she had built letter by letter with a pen warmed over a candle: *Sterling, E., civilian nursing sister, with dependent child.* Two places. One woman, one boy. Hollis's true name was written nowhere that anyone official could read it, and Hollis himself sat twenty yards away on a bench by the fish sheds, upright out of habit, his kit between his knees, watching the gangway with the terrible patience of a man who has been told by everyone, including his own mind, that he does not exist.

Three lives. One chit. And Captain Vane reading the crowd like a page.

* * *

"Stretchers clear!" someone shouted from the ship. "Walking wounded to the second rope! Refugees remain behind the line until called!"

The walking wounded began to move. They shuffled and limped and were wheeled toward the second rope in a long ragged column, and the clerk ticked them through one by one, checking tags, and the MPs checked the tags against the clerk's ticks, and somewhere on the ship a purser checked the checks. Four layers of paper between each man and the deck he would sleep on tonight. Edmund thought of Private Hollis, struck from the rolls eleven months ago as a deserter though he had never deserted anything but his own senses, and wondered which layer would fail him. All of them. Every layer would fail him. Paper had been failing Hollis since the summer of 1916.

She crossed to him during the shuffle, when the crowd's attention was on the column of wounded.

"Hollis." She said his number-name quietly, the false one, because even now she would not spend his true name in the open air. "When the civilians are called, I want you near me. Not beside me — behind me, half a step, as if you're my porter. Carry something. Here." She took the empty canvas holdall from her shoulder and gave it to him, and his hands closed around the straps and steadied, and she saw what a gift weight can be to a shaking man.

"Sterling," he said. His voice had the flat calm of shell shock, which sounds like peace and is not. "If it comes to choosing—"

"It won't come to choosing."

"You said last night it comes to choosing. You counted us out loud."

"I counted possibilities," she said. "Possibilities are not decisions." Which was a lie, and he looked at her with his washed-out eyes and let her see that he knew it was a lie, and thanked her anyway, in the old soldiers' fashion, by saying nothing more about it.

She returned to Cyrus. He had not moved from where she'd left him, wedged against a bollard between two refugee families who had adopted him with glances and half-smiles — a Breton grandmother who kept pressing bits of bread into his hands, and her daughter, and a baby who cried with a thin furious persistence that Edmund found, perversely, comforting. Babies could still be furious. The world could still be worth shouting at.

Cyrus looked up at her and touched two fingers to his own chest, where the satchel hung beneath his coat. Their private signal. *Still here? Still safe?*

She nodded. *Still here.*

Then his gaze slid past her shoulder and fixed, and every trace of childhood drained out of his face, leaving something ancient and watchful, and she turned because she already knew.

Vane was walking toward them along the rope line.

* * *

He did not come directly. That was his method, she had learned. He came the way frost comes — everywhere at once, touching everything lightly. He paused at the second rope to watch a stretcher go up. He exchanged a word with the clerk under the awning and glanced at the clipboard and said something that made the clerk laugh nervously. He inspected a stack of mail sacks as if mail might conceal a five-year-old. He let the whole dock see him being unhurried, and the whole dock relaxed slightly, because unhurried men in immaculate greatcoats must surely be harmless, and meanwhile his pale adjutant peeled away from him and drifted along the back of the crowd, alone, unremarkable, working the far rows the way a man works a field.

Working toward the bollard. Toward the Breton grandmother. Toward the baby and the bread and the small silent boy.

"Civilians will board at eleven o'clock," the clerk announced, and a moan went through the crowd — relief and dread braided together, because eleven o'clock was almost here and eleven o'clock might mean everything or nothing. "Have your documents ready. Families board together. Families. Together." He repeated it twice more, and Edmund understood the emphasis: no unaccompanied children. No unexplained adults. The word *together* was a net, and it had been strung there deliberately, and she would have given a great deal to know whether the clerk had strung it or whether the instruction had come down from a polished voice in a customs office yesterday evening.

Her chit said *with dependent child.* It would pass. She had made it pass in her mind fifty times. But making a forgery in your mind and offering it to daylight under the eye of a man who already suspects you are two different disciplines entirely.

The adjutant was three families away. Then two.

Edmund knelt, as if to tie Cyrus's bootlace, and put her mouth close to his ear.

"When I say *now,*" she breathed, "you take Grandmother's hand. You are her grandson. Her grandson's name is Pierre. Can you be Pierre?" He nodded against her cheek, quick, once. "Only until we reach the rope. Then you are yourself again. Do you understand why?"

He didn't answer, of course. But he lifted his small chin and set his jaw in a way she recognised with a jolt — it was her own expression, the one she wore for matrons and medical officers, the blank competent mask of the person who cannot afford to feel anything yet — and her heart cracked neatly down the middle, painless at first, the way big breaks are.

The adjutant reached the family directly behind theirs. He spoke to the Breton daughter. She shook her head, frightened. He moved to the grandmother, bending courteously, hat in hand — *madame, forgive me, have you seen*—

"Now," Edmund whispered, and Cyrus's cold little hand shot out and gripped the grandmother's apron string, and the old woman — bless her, bless her forever — gathered him in against her hip with the automatic ferocity of a woman who had raised nine children through two wars, and glared at the adjutant as if he were the weather.

"Your grandson, madame?" the adjutant asked.

"Mien petit," the grandmother said, in the tone of a woman closing a shop door. "And tired. And cold. Go elsewhere."

The adjutant hesitated. His eyes went over Cyrus's face the way Vane's eyes went over crowds — thoroughly, patiently. Cyrus stared back at nothing, slack and bored, a tired Breton child, Pierre, whoever Pierre was. Ten seconds passed. Fifteen. Somewhere on the ship the engines woke with a deep subterranean shudder, and the whole dock felt the deck plates begin to hum, and the adjutant straightened, murmured some apology, and withdrew.

Edmund did not exhale until he had rejoined Vane fifty yards away and shaken his head.

* * *

At eleven exactly — bureaucratic calm in its purest form, as though the century's dead had queued politely to make the appointment possible — the clerk called the civilians.

What followed was a kind of controlled flood. Documents raised above heads like tickets at a theatre. Families linking arms so as not to be divided in the crush. The MPs swinging the rope wide and the crowd pouring through the gap toward the second checkpoint, where a naval officer and two WRENs examined papers by lamplight with the brisk tenderness of people who had done this for eighteen hours and would do it for eighteen more.

Edmund joined the flow with Cyrus's hand in hers and Hollis half a step behind, carrying the empty holdall, invisible in his porter's role. The queue advanced in jerks. A step, a stop. A step, a stop. Each jerk brought the lamplight closer, and the lamplight was judgment, and her bodice held a forged chit warm against her skin, and behind them somewhere in the crowd Vane was no longer drifting — she could feel it, the change in pressure, the way one feels a storm front — he was converging.

A step. A stop.

Ahead of her a woman was refused. Papers wrong, or insufficient, or merely unlucky; the argument rose in French and English, the husband's voice cracking, the WREN's voice gentle and immovable, and the queue absorbed the tragedy without slowing, stepping around it as water steps around a stone, and Edmund thought: *that will be us, within the hour, and the crowd will step around us too.*

A step. A stop. The lamp now close enough that she could see the officer's chapped hands, the ink pad, the stamp coming down on each accepted document with a soft decisive thump — thump, thump — the heartbeat of passage.

Then, cutting across the queue from outside the rope, unhurried, courteous, carrying the certainty of a man who has stopped searching because he has finished it:

"Nurse Sterling. What extraordinary luck. I was hoping to say goodbye."

Captain Vane stood at the rope with the lamplight full on his face, and he was smiling, and his eyes went from her to Cyrus to the empty holdall in Hollis's hands and back to her — and he said, pleasantly, for the benefit of the WREN and the officer and everyone within earshot:

"I insist on seeing you aboard myself."
