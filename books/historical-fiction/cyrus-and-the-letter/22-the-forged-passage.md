## 22. The Forged Passage

The customs shed at Boulogne had a smell Edmund would carry to her grave: tar and wet wool and lamp oil, with something underneath it all — old fish, perhaps, or old fear. She had come in with the last light, when the lamplighters were still working their way down the quay and the crowd of refugees had thinned enough that a woman could stand at a counter without three people pressing at her elbows asking questions she could not answer.

She had a travel chit in her pocket. It was not hers.

It belonged, on paper, to a Madame Berthe Fournier of Amiens, forty-one years of age, travelling to England under the auspices of the Friends' Relief Committee, one berth, no dependents. Tomas had pressed it into her hands an hour ago behind the gatehouse, folding it small, folding it fast, glancing up and down the dock as though the paper itself might cry out. *Her papers came through yesterday. She won't use them now.* He had not said what had happened to Berthe Fournier. He had only looked at Edmund with those tired, patient eyes and said, quietly, *You'll need two names where there is one,* and then he had gone back to his stretcher cases as if he had handed over nothing more remarkable than a match.

Two names where there is one. Edmund stood at the counter now with the chit flattened before her and understood that everything she had done until this moment — every name entered in the ledger, every careful record kept against the forgetting — had been practice. Apprenticeship. She had spent three years teaching herself the shape of a name, the weight of it, the way ink settles into cheap paper and becomes a fact. She had made men legible who the army wanted erased. She had done it in secret, in candlelight, in the margins of a dressing station, believing herself a quiet rebel, a keeper of small flames.

What she was about to do was not quiet. What she was about to do was a crime.

*Good,* said some voice inside her, a voice she almost didn't recognize, low and steady and entirely without remorse. *Let it be a crime. Let it be the right one.*

* * *

She had stolen the pen. That was the first thing. Not stolen exactly — borrowed without asking, from the customs officer's own desk while he argued with a shipping clerk about tonnage — but theft all the same, and she noted it in herself the way she noted a new symptom in a patient: pulse quickening, palms damp, pupils wide. Fear. Useful fear, if you did not let it rule you. She had watched men walk into shellfire on fear alone; she could certainly walk through a forgery on it.

The chit lay under the lamp. The paper was thin, war-cheap, fibrous, and the original entries had been written in a violet ink that had faded toward grey at the edges — which was a mercy, because faded ink forgave. Madame Berthe Fournier, *quarante et un ans*, Amiens. One berth. No dependents.

Edmund needed: one berth for C. Sterling, nurse, and one dependent child. She needed the word *dependents* to appear where it had been struck through or left blank. She needed a second name added to the first without the addition looking like an addition. And she needed it all done in the next four minutes, because the customs officer was still arguing about tonnage but arguments end, and arguments always end faster than you want them to.

She breathed once, slow, the way she breathed before lancing a wound, and began.

First: the age. Forty-one was wrong for her and would invite questions; thirty-two invited none. The figure *4* yielded to *3* under a wetted thumb and a careful nail better than she had hoped, the fibre softening just enough, the stroke shortening so that a casual eye read *34* or *31* depending on how tired the eye was. Tired eyes. That was the whole art. Everyone at this port was tired — the clerks, the gendarmes, the officers, the ship's masters. She was not forging documents; she was forging documents for tired eyes. There was a moral distinction there, she told herself, thin as cigarette paper, and she elected to believe in it.

Second: the name. This was the part her hands knew. Three years of the ledger had taught her that a name is not letters; it is rhythm. *Fournier* sat on the page in a certain slant, a certain pressure, the capital *F* hooked the way the original clerk had hooked all his capitals — she had studied him for a full minute before touching the pen, the way she'd study a surgeon's technique before assisting. Now she wrote *Cyrus* above the surname line in that same hook and slant, matching pressure, matching speed, letting her hand tremble very slightly because the original hand trembled too, everywhere, the small honest tremor of a man writing his ten thousandth document of a war that produced documents the way the earth produced mud. A perfect hand would have betrayed her. Her imperfection was her authenticity.

*Berthe Fournier and child,* the line now read, if the tired eye agreed to read it so. Or *C. Fournier-Sterling, avec enfant.* She had chosen the hyphenated form in the end, half-English half-French, plausible as rain — mixed marriages filled these docks, displaced families arrived hourly with papers in three languages, nobody expected elegance. The truth of the world's chaos was the forger's greatest ally. She was not inventing an improbable document. She was nudging an ordinary one half an inch out of true.

Third: the berth count. *Un* becoming *deux* was the hardest surgery, because numbers do not fade gracefully and the space was tight. She solved it not by altering the numeral but by adding a line beneath it in the clerk's own hand — *enfants de moins de cinq ans ne comptent pas séparément, voir circular 114/bis* — a regulation citation that did not exist, delivered with bureaucratic confidence, in ink she had aged between two damp cloths to match the page. Anyone who checked would find nothing. But nobody checks a citation. Nobody has time. The war had seen to it that nobody had time.

She set down the pen. The chit dried under the lamp, and the violet-grey ink settled into the cheap fibre, and within a minute it looked as though it had always said what it now said. Within five minutes she could not see the joins herself, and she was looking. Within ten, it was simply a paper belonging to a simply a woman with a child, and the woman it had belonged to before was nowhere on it at all, and that was the part that frightened her — not that she had made Cyrus legible, but how easily she had made Berthe Fournier vanish. The ledger worked both directions. Every act of writing-on was also an act of writing-over. She had known this abstractly for three years. Now she knew it in her hands.

The customs officer finished his argument about tonnage, turned back to the counter, and held out his hand for her chit with the flat courtesy of a man who had stamped four thousand of these and would stamp four thousand more.

Edmund gave it to him.

He looked at it for perhaps three seconds. She stood in the lamplight and let him look, and she thought, with a strange floating clarity, *I am holding my face perfectly still. I am a woman waiting for stamps. There are thousands of us. I am invisible among the tired.* He glanced at her — one flick of the eyes, face, papers, face, the portico ritual of suspicion — and stamped it, twice, and slid it back across the counter, and called *Suivant*, and Edmund Sterling walked away from that desk carrying the most dangerous piece of paper on the Boulogne docks, and her legs held, and she did not exhale until she reached the shadow of the warehouse wall.

Then her knees informed her they had opinions, and she leaned against the cold timber and shook for the better part of a minute, silently, like a horse after a long gallop.

* * *

She found Cyrus where she had left him, in the lee of a beached lifeboat among a family of Belgian refugees who had adopted him for the evening without being asked — feeding him bread and dripping, tucking a blanket around him, speaking over his head in Flemish about people and places he was not part of. He tolerated this with the grave patience of a child who had learned that adults needed to fuss and that fussing was survivable. When he saw Edmund he detached himself from the Belgians with unhurried dignity and came to her side and took hold of her coat hem, and the small weight of that grip went through the wool and the linen and the skin and settled somewhere behind her ribs, where it had been settling for weeks now, accumulating.

"Hello," she said, in English, their private language, useless to spies and gendarmes alike. "We're nearly ready."

He looked up at her. He did not smile — he rarely smiled — but he did the thing that was better than smiling: he tilted his head and studied her face with total attention, the way he studied everything, cataloguing it, keeping it. She wondered sometimes what archive existed behind those dark eyes, what shelves, what order. She knew the satchel was on him. She had sewn the letters into its lining herself, and the ledger — God help her, the ledger — rode flat against his chest inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with the string from a Red Cross parcel. All her evidence, all her dead, all her small struck-off names, riding on the body of a five-year-old boy because it was the only body she trusted.

That thought had seemed sane at midnight. In the grey of late afternoon, with the ship's masts visible past the cranes, it seemed insane, and she knelt down in the shadow of the lifeboat and put both hands on his shoulders.

"Cyrus." His name — her name for him, the name given for no reason she could explain, except that perhaps some part of her had already known she was naming a story, not a child. "Listen to me. Tonight you're going to go somewhere I can't come. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. Do you understand *not yet*?"

He blinked. Once. He understood everything; that was the trouble.

"You'll have your satchel. You must never let go of the satchel. Not for food, not for sleep, not for kindness. There are people on the ship who will help you — a lady with grey hair and a red cross on her sleeve, you find the red cross, do you hear me? Grey hair, red cross." She drew it on his palm with her finger: a cross. He watched her finger. "And when you get to England, you give the satchel to whoever takes you in, and you say—" she stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, made it a plan, made it a thing that could fail. She said it anyway. "You tell them the letters are true, and the book has the names."

His mouth moved. No sound. It rarely did anymore — the words had gone underground since the night he spoke his mother's name to her in the dark, *Éloise, Éloise*, over and over like a bell until she had gathered him against her chest and held the sound in. Since then he had returned mostly to silence, but a different silence now: not empty. Full. A held breath rather than an absence.

Now his mouth shaped something again, barely, and she leaned close, and what he shaped — no sound, only the lips' ghost of it — was *your face.*

"I know," Edmund whispered, and her throat closed around it. "Remember mine instead. That's the bargain. I forget nothing, you remember me. That's how we don't lose each other, even across water. Even across years." She pressed her forehead briefly to his cold little forehead, one second, no longer — sentiment was rationed here as strictly as coal — and stood, and became practical again, because practical was the armor and if she took it off even for a minute the whole structure would come down.

* * *

The cost arrived on schedule. It always did.

She had got the arithmetic wrong, and she found it out at dusk, standing at the foot of the gangway with the forged chit burning in her pocket like a coal. The chit was good — the chit was, she believed, genuinely good, tired-eye-proof, stamped twice — but a chit was not a berth. The berths were allocated at the ship's rail by a purser with a master list, and the master list had been amended at six o'clock, and the amendment was this: the walking wounded had come aboard ahead of schedule, forty of them, and the civilian allocations had shrunk accordingly. Two places had become one-and-a-half. One-and-a-half had become, effectively, one.

One adult and one child counted as one-and-a-half. She had built her whole plan on that regulation, circular 114/bis, invented out of whole cloth and desperate hope. The purser's amended list did not recognize inventions. The purser's list recognized numbers.

So the geometry she had been dreading for a week — the shrinking room, the three lives, the single door — closed another notch, and this time it closed on her own fingers. She had forged passage for herself and the boy, and the passage existed now only as paper, and paper was worth precisely what the man at the rail decided it was worth, and somewhere along that rail, she could see him: Captain Vane, immaculate even at the dockside, gloved hands clasped behind his back, surveying the embarkation with the mild satisfaction of a landowner watching harvest come in. Looking, she was quite certain, for a small boy. Looking, when his gaze swept the refugee families and the stretcher parties and the huddled knots by the warehouses, with the thoroughness of a man who intended to find.

And near the rail, wrapped in a blanket he refused to surrender, sat Private Hollis — transferred to the port that morning, nameless on every list, unfit to travel without a sponsor, sitting where the orderlies had parked him like luggage, his eyes fixed on the water with the terrible calm of a man who has been struck off the world and knows it.

Edmund stood between them in the failing light — the boy at her hem, the soldier at the rail, the ship breathing smoke into the darkening sky — and understood, with the cold clarity of the fully committed, that the forged chit in her pocket was not the end of her night's work. It was the beginning. Paper could be made to say anything; she had proved that tonight. The question was no longer whether she could write a lie well enough.

The question was whose tomorrow she would write it for.

Down the quay, past the arc-lights, a search party in Vane's colours began moving through the crowds with lanterns, turning faces toward the light one by one — and among them, unmistakable in his stillness, a small boy with a satchel, exactly the boy Vane was looking for, and Cyrus was no longer at her hem.

He had followed the lanterns.
