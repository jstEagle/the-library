## 23. Vane Finds the Satchel

The morning came in grey strips, torn open by gulls and the groan of cranes along the Boulogne quays. Edmund had not slept so much as she had lain down beside her fear for a few hours and risen before it did. Cyrus slept curled against the wall of the customs shed where Tomas had stowed them among bales of straw, his small hands even in sleep curved around nothing — reaching always for the satchel strap that was no longer there, because Edmund had moved it, because she had sewn the letters into the lining of his coat three nights ago with thread pulled from a blanket hem, working by the light of a shielded candle while he watched her with those dark unblinking eyes that asked questions his mouth would not.

She checked the stitches again now, in the thin light, her fingers cold as coins. The seams sat flat. The coat hung on him the way coats hang on children everywhere: too big at the shoulders, worn like a promise of the man to come. No one looking at him would see anything but a boy. That was the whole of her plan, and it was a terrible plan, and it was the only one.

"Hungry?" she whispered.

He shook his head, then changed his mind halfway through the gesture and nodded instead, and something in Edmund's chest turned over. It was the closest thing to speech he offered most days, this half-and-half language of the body, and she had learned to read it the way sailors read weather — quickly, and with respect.

Tomas appeared at the shed door with two rolls and a wedge of cheese wrapped in newspaper, and behind him the dock unfolded in noise: boots on planking, the complaint of winches, a bugle somewhere inland sounding a call nobody answered anymore. Refugees had camped along the sea wall in families shaped like question marks, huddled over suitcases that held everything a life had been reduced to. A woman was combing another woman's hair with long patient strokes, picking through it for lice, and the intimacy of it made Edmund look away as though she had intruded on a prayer.

"Ship's in the roads," Tomas said, low. "They'll board the walking wounded by nine. Your chit gets you to the gangway." He handed over the bread. "After that—" He didn't finish. There was no finishing. After that was arithmetic, and they all knew what numbers were waiting.

"Where is Vane?" she asked, because she could not stop herself asking, because not knowing where the wolf was was worse than knowing.

"His adjutant was at the gate an hour ago. Taking names." Tomas's jaw worked. "He has a list, Sister. A short one."

Edmund ate the bread without tasting it. A short list. She was almost certainly on it. So, then, was a boy who had never been on any list at all — that was the bitter joke, the boy the officers had tried to erase from existence was now the one name in Boulogne that mattered, hunted precisely because someone had decided he should never have been recorded.

*Forget this face.* She looked at Cyrus chewing steadily, crumbs at the corner of his mouth, and thought: *I will do many things today, but I will not forget it.*

* * *

The boarding began the way catastrophes often begin: with paperwork.

A naval lieutenant stood at the foot of the gangway with a clipboard and a voice trained to carry over artillery, calling categories of wounded in a bored liturgy — *stretcher cases first, then sitting wounded, then women and minors with documentation* — and up the plank they went in ones and twos, bandaged heads bowed against the wind off the Channel. The ship sat low in the water, grey as the sky, funnels breathing smoke that leaned landward as though reluctant to leave France either.

Edmund kept Cyrus at her hip, her hand closed around his smaller one hard enough that she worried about the bones, and moved them through the crowd with the studied aimlessness of a pickpocket. Every soldier's greatcoat became, briefly, a hiding place she considered. Every officer's silhouette made her pulse jump and settle, jump and settle.

It was the adjutant she saw first — Lieutenant Prasse, sandy-haired, meticulous, moving through the refugees with a folded paper in his hand and two military policemen trailing him like gulls after a plough. He stopped people at random. He looked at faces the way an auditor looks at figures, seeking only errors.

Edmund turned, drawing Cyrus back toward the customs sheds, walking at the pace of a woman with nowhere particular to be, and it was then — she would replay this moment ten thousand times in the years after, searching for the hinge, the exact second the door swung — it was then that she heard the whistle.

Two short blasts, one long. The signal of Vane's searchers, established at the station inspections, at the train, at every checkpoint since Ypres: *something found.*

She turned in time to see them emerging from between two sheds near the eastern gate, four men in the blue-grey of the Army Service Corps, and in the centre of them, held aloft by its collar like a specimen, was a child's coat.

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. She could hear the gulls and the winches and the lieutenant's litany of wounded categories, and beneath it all a single high tone like the residue of a shell burst, and her own heart saying, absurdly, *no, no, no,* as though repetition were an argument the universe might accept.

They had searched the sheds. They had gone through the straw where she and Cyrus had slept, and found the coat she had left there — deliberately, God help her, she had left it there as a decoy, stuffed lightly with the satchel's shape suggested by rolled blankets, because she had reasoned that men searching for hidden papers would be satisfied finding hidden papers, would carry their prize to Vane and trouble her no further. She had put the true letters in the coat Cyrus wore, sewn flat against his ribs, and built a false treasure to satisfy the hunters.

But the coat in the searcher's raised fist was not her decoy. She knew it instantly, the way a mother knows her own child's cry among a hundred cries. The buttons were wrong. The tear at the cuff — the one she'd meant to mend — was absent. It was Cyrus's coat, the real one, lifted from his shoulders while he slept or taken in some moment she could not account for, and her beautiful clever plan had been read like a large-print book, and the hunters had simply waited until the boy walked out from under his own concealment and taken the coat off his back.

Or — her mind raced, sickeningly — they had not known which coat was which at all. They had searched both. And if they had searched both—

Her hand went to Cyrus's shoulder and found him wearing it. Still wearing it. The grey coat, the mended cuff, the weight of paper flat against his small ribs. He was looking at the men across the quay, and his face had gone the colour of the sky.

They hadn't found the letters yet, then. Or they had taken the wrong coat in error. Either way, the prize was in their hands and the truth was still against her son's — *the boy's*, she corrected herself, fiercely, *not your son, you have no right to that word* — against the boy's ribs, and every second he stood on this quay he stood inside the evidence that could kill them both.

"Edmund," Cyrus said.

She heard it as one hears a bell in another room — faint, impossible, unmistakable. Her name. His second word ever spoken, given not in tenderness but in alarm, and there was no time to weep over it because the searchers had seen them.

"There!" One of them pointed. "The woman and the boy!"

What happened next happened in the space of perhaps forty seconds, and afterward Edmund could never assemble it into order, only into impressions, the way a shell-shocked man remembers a bombardment as light and noise and the smell of earth rather than as events.

She ran. She pulled Cyrus with her and she ran, into the refugee camps along the sea wall, into the maze of tarpaulins and cooking fires and stacked luggage, and the crowd, bless it, the enormous anonymous suffering crowd, closed behind them like water. A woman thrust out a laundry basket of wet washing as the first searcher passed; he swore and shoved it aside and lost three steps. A man selling cigarettes knocked his tray over on purpose, cards and packets scattering across the planks, and the soldiers slowed to pick through them or not, cursing. Somewhere a child began to wail and other children took up the wail in sympathy until the air itself seemed to cry, and in that crying Edmund bent low and spoke into Cyrus's ear with the terrible clarity of desperation:

"The fish crates. By the harbourmaster's steps. You know the ones — where we hid yesterday. Go inside them, behind the stack, and whatever happens, whatever you hear, you do not move until I come. Do you understand? Until *I* come."

His eyes were enormous. His hand gripped hers so hard his nails bit her palm.

"Cyrus." She knelt, making herself small, making herself the only thing in the world. "You carried the satchel when I could not. Now let me carry this. Run."

And he ran. He ran the way small animals run, low and silent and swift, threading between legs and cartwheels and sleeping families, and within ten seconds the crowd had swallowed him whole, and Edmund Sterling stood alone on the quay of Boulogne with her empty hands and her hammering heart and four soldiers bearing down on her.

She did not run further. Running further would draw them deeper into the crowd, toward the fish crates, toward him. Instead she turned to face them, lifted her chin, and summoned the voice that had soothed dying men through the worst nights of 1916.

"That will be quite enough shouting," she said. "I am a nursing sister of His Majesty's service, and you will conduct yourselves accordingly."

It stopped them, as authority stops tired men, for exactly the breath she needed. Then the tallest seized her arm — actually seized it, fingers closing above her elbow, and the outrage of it, the sheer unprecedented *liberty*, nearly blinded her.

"Sister Sterling," the man said, and it was not a question. "Captain Vane wants a word."

"Then Captain Vane may make an appointment," she said, "like any other gentleman."

But they were already marching her, none too gently, past the cigarette seller (who would not meet her eye), past the woman with the laundry (who looked at her with an expression of frank apology, as though the whole crowd had failed her personally), toward the customs office where the lamplight burned even in daylight, toward the door where Lieutenant Prasse stood holding the small coat across his arm like a valet, and beyond the doorway, visible through the glass in the grey morning, Captain Vane sat at a desk with the coat laid before him and his pocketknife in his hand, already at work on the seams.

* * *

They did not put her in the office. They put her outside it, on a wooden chair against the wall of the passage, where she could hear everything.

She heard the knife part thread. She heard Vane's voice, unhurried, courteous, describing what he found as though narrating a surgical procedure for students: "Six envelopes. Sealed. Watermarked stock — Ashfield and Sons, London, stationers to the gentry. How very well-bred our little courier's correspondence is." The sound of an envelope being slit. Paper unfolding. Silence, long silence, and Edmund sat with her hands folded in her lap and stared at a knot in the floorboard and breathed the way she taught the wounded to breathe, in for four, hold, out for four, because panic is a luxury purchased with other people's lives.

Then Vane laughed.

It was the worst sound of the entire war. Not triumph — amusement. Genuine, warm, delighted amusement, the laugh of a man finding a ten-pound note in an old coat. "Well, well. Well, well, well." Papers riffling. "Sister Sterling, you may come in now. I believe we've passed the point of pretence."

The escort opened the door and she walked in on her own feet, because they would not carry her, and stood before the desk.

Vane looked better than he had any right to. Rested. Shaved to a shine. He wore his cap on the desk edge like a man at home, and the six letters lay in a row before him, and beside them, folded neatly, the small coat, its lining hanging loose as a wound.

"You took a coat off a five-year-old," Edmund said. Her voice was steady. She was distantly proud of it. "I hope the Army appreciates the calibre of enemy it's sent you."

"I took a coat containing documents relevant to the security of the realm." He tapped the letters without touching them, a conjurer's gesture. "Do you know what these are, Sister?"

"They're letters."

"Do you know *what they say*?"

"I couldn't read them. They were sealed."

"Ah." He smiled. "Sealed. Yes. Which raises the interesting question of why a battlefield nurse was smuggling sealed correspondence from the family solicitors of a gentleman she has never met, sewn into the clothing of a child whose name does not appear on any muster roll, hospital register, or civilian manifest anywhere in France." He leaned back. "You see my difficulty. So many questions. And you, famously, keep such excellent records."

Cold moved through her then, slow as floodwater. *Any muster roll.* He had looked. He had searched the registries for Cyrus and found nothing, and the nothing had told him everything — that the boy was a blank space, and blank spaces, in Vane's world, were made deliberately.

"The child is a war orphan under my care," she said. "The coat was mine to mend. If there are letters in it, someone used me to move them. I'm a nurse, Captain, not a courier. Search my kit if you like — you'll find lint, morphia, and a notebook of dead men's names, which I'll thank you to treat with respect."

"A notebook." His eyes sharpened. "You still have it."

She said nothing, and the silence confessed more than speech would have, and Vane received it like a gift.

"Here is where we find ourselves," he said, rising, coming round the desk, and she noted that he placed himself between her and the door with the ease of long habit. "Those letters go to London tonight, under seal, to the parties most qualified to assess them. Certain persons will be extremely interested to learn how close a nurse and a mute child came to delivering them into the wrong hands — by which I mean, of course, the right ones. The newspapers." He smiled at his own wit. "Now. The child. Where is the child?"

"I don't know."

"Edmund." Her Christian name, soft as a lullaby, and she flinched before she could stop herself. "We both know that's not true. Here is what happens next. In one hour I walk to the gangway of that ship, and I have already sent Prasse to review the passenger list against my short list. Wounded men travel unexamined — regulations, you understand, the Red Cross is very touchy. But *minors travelling with sponsors* require documentation, and documentation requires signatures, and signatures can be queried. Delayed. For days." He let that hang. "You will produce the boy before that ship sails, or you will watch him become what his family's friends have already decided he should be: nothing. Unrecorded. A blank space in a French port. You, of all people, know what becomes of the unrecorded."

The words went into her like a bayonet goes in — cleanly, professionally, with a twist at the end.

"He's a child," she said, and hated the crack in it.

"He's a liability who became a parcel and was addressed to the wrong recipient. As, I regret to say, are you." Vane gathered the letters, squared their edges, slid them into a leather dispatch case, and buckled it with two efficient movements. "You have one hour, Sister. Bring me the boy and the notebook, and I give you my word — my word of honour — that you'll both be on that ship, papers in order, and the matter closed."

"And if I don't."

"Then Prasse begins at the gangway with the passenger list," Vane said pleasantly, "and works alphabetically through the nurses of the 44th Field Ambulance currently awaiting repatriation. Greer. Okafor. Whitlock. Such good women. It would be a shame." He opened the door for her, the perfect host. "One hour."

* * *

The passage swallowed her and gave her back to the quay, to the wind and the gulls and the great grey ship breathing smoke at anchor in the roads, and Edmund stood in the crowd holding herself upright by an act of pure will.

Everything was gone. The letters — the proof, the whole case, the murdered maid's testimony in six sealed envelopes — sat buckled in Vane's dispatch case, bound for whoever paid him. The coat. The plan. All of it taken in a morning, undone by four soldiers and a pocketknife, and the hour ticking against her like something with teeth.

And yet.

She pressed her hand flat against her stomach, against the place where fear lived, and made herself think the way she had thought over operating tables when the surgeon's voice went flat and quiet, the way you think when everyone else is permitted to panic except you.

Vane had the letters. Vane did *not* have the boy. Whatever the letters proved, they proved it on paper — names, dates, contracts, payments — and paper could be denied, discounted, explained away by the very family it named. What could not be explained away was a living witness. A boy who had seen a house, a mother, a death. Vane knew that. It was why he wanted the child more than he wanted the letters, why his voice had tightened on the single question he cared about: *where is the child.*

Which meant the boy in the fish crates was still the most dangerous person in France, and the letters in the dispatch case were worth nothing without him — and worth everything, if he ever reached a courtroom, if anyone ever asked him to say aloud what he had seen.

Testimony. Memory. Nerve. That was what remained. Hers, and — God help them both — his.

Edmund looked at the clock on the harbourmaster's wall. Forty-five minutes left on Vane's hour, and the ship's whistle would sound before that, and somewhere behind the fish crates a small boy was keeping faith with a promise she had made him: *until I come.*

She began to walk toward the water, slowly, an ordinary nurse in an ordinary coat, and in her mind she was already composing three lies and choosing which one could survive an hour in a lamplit room across from Captain Vane.

Because he would ask again — he would ask where the boy was, and this time there would be threats with names attached — and Edmund Sterling, keeper of forgotten names, liar for the first time in her life and getting better at it daily, would have to give him an answer.

She just had to decide whether it would be the truth, a lie, or something worse than either.
