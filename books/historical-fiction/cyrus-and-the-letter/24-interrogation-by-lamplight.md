## 24. Interrogation by Lamplight

The customs office at Boulogne smelled of tar, cold tea, and the particular staleness of rooms where men sat waiting to decide other men's futures. A single lamp burned on the desk between them, its shade cracked so that a seam of light fell across the table like a blade laid flat. Beyond the window the dock noises came muffled — gulls, winches, a ship's bell somewhere counting itself — but inside there was only the tick of a clock that had lost its minute hand, and Captain Vane's voice, which was worse than silence because it was so very pleasant.

"Sister Sterling. Or is it Miss? I never can remember how the nursing services like to be addressed."

"Either does for me, Captain." Edmund sat with her hands folded in her lap, the way she had been taught at Guy's twenty years ago — hands quiet, spine straight, face arranged into the mild attentiveness of a woman who had seen everything and been alarmed by none of it. It was a mask she had worn through amputations without anaesthetic and bombardments without warning, and she had believed it impenetrable until tonight.

Tonight it felt thin as the paper Cyrus drew on.

"You'll forgive the setting," Vane said. He did not sound as though he expected forgiveness; he sounded as though he were offering her a gift by noticing the inconvenience at all. "The military police commandeered it from the customs men, who commandeered it from a shipping clerk, who probably stole it from someone else in the first place. Everything in this war is second-hand, don't you find? Even our crimes."

"I wouldn't know about crimes, sir."

"No," he agreed softly. "I don't imagine you would."

He opened a leather folder on the desk — unhurried, the gesture of a man laying out cards he already knew the value of — and turned it so she could see. Her breath did not catch. She had trained it not to. But something behind her ribs went very still, the way a ward goes still when the chaplain's boots are heard on the gravel.

The folder contained photographs. Not of letters — of *things*. Her cot at the station, its blankets thrown back. The shelf above it where her Bible had stood. And a satchel — no, not the satchel itself, but an inventory written in a clerk's careful hand: *One leather school-satchel, brown, strap worn. Contents: correspondence, sealed, eleven items.*

Eleven items. She had counted them herself on the train, in the dark, with her thumb, while Cyrus slept against her shoulder with his mouth open and his fists curled. Eleven envelopes, each sealed with grey wax, each bearing the crest she now knew better than her own name.

"They took his coat," Vane said, conversationally, watching her the way she had watched men come out of ether — waiting to see what they would say when they thought no one could hear. "The boy ran, which was clever of him, or lucky, or both. But coats are heavy things for small shoulders, and children drop things when they run. You'd know that. You're good with children, they tell me. Good with all of them, in fact. The wounded. The dying. The forgotten." He let the last word sit on the table between them like a coin he suspected was counterfeit.

Edmund looked at the lamp instead of at him. She counted the seconds in her head, the way she counted a pulse — one, two, three — because counting kept the fear in a box with holes punched in it, and fear in such a box grew quieter rather than louder.

*He has the letters. He does not have me. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole game.*

"You're wondering why you're here," Vane went on, "rather than, say, your friend Tomas. The orderly. He's here too, as it happens. Different room. Rather less comfortable, I'm afraid; the military police aren't gentle with orderlies who obstruct searches." He paused, delicately. "And Matron Greer — Greer, yes? — is back at the coast hospital at Étaples by now, or perhaps not yet arrived. Roads being what they are. One hopes she travels safely. War zones are dangerous places for women of a certain age with a certain amount of conscience."

There it was. He had not raised his voice. He had barely moved his hands. But the threat unfolded itself in the room like smoke, filling every corner: *Greer. Tomas. The nurses left behind at the station, forty miles back down the road you came by in the dark.*

"You have questions for me, Captain," Edmund said, and heard her own voice arrive steady, which surprised her. "I'd be grateful if you'd ask them. It's late, and I've a boy somewhere on these docks who doesn't speak and doesn't know anyone's face but mine, and whatever you intend to do to me, I'd like it done before he starts looking."

Something flickered at the corner of Vane's mouth. Respect, perhaps. Or appetite.

* * *

He began, as she might have predicted, with kindness. The dangerous kind.

"You've served since '14. Three years at the front without a single reprimand. Your matrons' reports read like love letters — *unflappable*, *tireless*, *a tower.*" He turned pages he did not need to read; he had memorized them. "A remarkable record. Which makes this business all the more puzzling, doesn't it? A woman of your standing, concealing a child against regulations. Harboring stolen correspondence. Forging—" he touched the inventory sheet, "—well. We'll come to the forging."

"None of that is puzzling to me, Captain."

"No?"

"No. I found a child at my door in the middle of a bombardment. What was I meant to do with him — post him back?"

Vane laughed. It was a real laugh, brief and warm, and it frightened her more than anything he had said yet, because it proved he could be genuine, and genuine men were harder to outlast than cruel ones. "You see, this is why I wanted to talk to you myself rather than hand you to the provosts. You're sensible. Sensible women understand arrangements." He leaned back, and the lamplight slid off his collar badge. "So let me be sensible in return. The letters in that satchel concern certain commercial contracts. Shipping contracts. Wartime business conducted — imperfectly, perhaps — by parties who prefer their affairs private. They are not evidence of anything a nurse needs to worry about. They are paper. Wet paper, by the time the lawyers finish with them. And the boy—" he spread one hand, a small, reasonable shrug, "—is a five-year-old mute of unknown parentage, probably Belgian, possibly French, certainly nobody. If he vanished into the system, into an orphanage, into the general forgetting of this war, no one would ever miss him. No one except you."

"And if I said nothing at all?"

"Then we would go on having a pleasant conversation, and eventually I would believe you, or believe enough of you, and everyone would sleep in their own beds tomorrow." He smiled. "That's the offer, Sister. That's the whole of it. Give me the truth shaped conveniently, and I will shape my report the same way. Nobody need be hurt. Not you. Not Greer, whose health, I'm told, is not robust. Not your orderly friend, whose brother — ah, but you know about the brother, don't you. He told you. On the train."

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, a ship's whistle sounded, long and low, and Edmund felt it pass through her body like grief finding a resonant chamber.

*Tomas told you on the train,* he had said. Except Tomas had told her nothing on the train; he had told her in the cattle-truck dark outside Amiens, three nights ago. Which meant either Vane was guessing — fishing with a fine line and hoping her face would tell him when the hook caught — or someone else had talked, and she could not know which, and he knew she could not know, and that was the true instrument he was playing her with. Not threats. Uncertainty, tuned note by note.

"His brother died at Loos," Edmund said carefully. "Tomas carries that the way we all carry things. I don't see what it has to do with shipping contracts."

"Don't you?" Vane studied her for a long moment, and then, apparently satisfied, closed the photographs away. "No. Perhaps not. Perhaps I'm confusing my files." He rose, poured tea from a pot on the side table — actual tea, hot, the first she had smelled in days — and handed her a cup as though they were two travelers sharing a waiting room. His courtesy was flawless. It was also, she understood now, the mechanism of the machine. Every kindness was a lever. Every courtesy assumed a relationship that did not exist and invited her to make it exist.

She held the cup and did not drink. Poison was unlikely; sedatives were not impossible; and besides, drinking would feel like agreement.

"The boy's mother," Vane said, sitting again, folding his hands. "You know who she was."

It was not a question, but she treated it as one. "I know what the drawings show. A woman's face."

"A housemaid," Vane said. "At a house in Kent. Who left her position in circumstances the family found embarrassing, and who subsequently died — of fever, the record says — leaving behind a child no one claimed." His eyes came up, flat and bright as coins. "You see how tidy it all is. A fever. A record. A dead girl with no name worth saying. The world has already ruled on this matter, Sister Sterling. All you're holding is some sentimental correspondence that changes nothing and costs everyone who touches it."

*Her name,* Edmund thought, with a clarity that felt almost like violence, *is Ada. Ada Wren. He said it in the night, in his sleep-voice, the first words I ever heard from him — Ada, Ada — and you will not fold her into a file and rule on her.*

What she said was: "I couldn't say, sir. I never saw any papers about her."

"No," Vane agreed. "You saw drawings. Burned at the edges." He watched her. "You flinched."

"I didn't."

"You moved your little finger. Left hand. It's quite all right — I've interrogated men who lost entire composure over less." He said it almost gently. "Do you know what I admire about you, Sister? You lie the way good soldiers dig. Quietly, thoroughly, and expecting to be shelled at any moment. Most people lie badly because they want to be forgiven for lying. You don't want forgiveness. You want the trench to hold." He drank his tea. "Unfortunately for us both, I have artillery."

* * *

They went around again. The boy — where had he come from, who brought him, what had the note said. The note, she realized with a lurch, must have been taken with the coat; it had lived in the satchel's inner pocket. *Forget this face.* Had she ever actually destroyed it? She turned the memory over, hunting for comfort, and found none. Let him have the note. The note was ash already; it named no one but the boy's unknown deliverer.

She gave him truths. That was her strategy, settled upon somewhere in the fourth hour without her ever quite deciding it: give him truth, but truth cut to pattern like cloth cut to a dress — accurate in every piece, arranged to a different silhouette. Yes, she had found the child at her door during the barrage. Yes, she had kept him, against regulations, and would do it again. No, he had never spoken to her — that much was even true enough, after a fashion, though the memory of *Ada, Ada* rose hot in her throat whenever she said it. No, she had not read the letters; she had seen seals, crests, nothing more. (A half-lie, and she felt it sit in her stomach like a swallowed stone. She had read one. Only one. Enough.)

And the ledger — ah, the ledger. He asked about it twice, in two different keys, once casual and once not. The book she carried, the notebook, the names. Someone had talked; there had been too many searches, too much precision. She denied it with the calm of long practice: a nurse keeps notes on patients, everyone knows nurses keep notes, ask Matron Greer, ask any sister in any clearing station in France.

But when he asked the third time — leaning forward into the lamplight, all the pleasantness set aside like a coat removed — she understood that the ledger mattered to him more than the letters, or as much, and she filed that away in the place where she was keeping things for morning: *he fears the record. He fears the list. A man who fears lists is a man who can be listed.*

"Where is it?" Vane asked.

"Where is what, Captain?"

"Sister Sterling." He said her name the way one taps a wall to hear if it is hollow. "I have been patient, and courteous, and I hope we have established that I am not a fool. You have lied to me beautifully all evening, and I have enjoyed it more than I can say, because it is rare to meet a worthy opponent in this mud-farm of a war. But dawn is coming, the boat sails at nine, and I am going to ask you plainly. Where is the book of names?"

Edmund looked at him across the lamp. Her hands were steady in her lap. Three years of holding dying men's hands had taught her steadiness as a native language; fear had become merely a dialect she could speak when required.

"It's in England," she said.

It was not what he expected. She saw it land — a fraction of widening at his eyes, quickly mastered, but enough. She pressed the advantage the way she had learned to press a dressing: firmly, quickly, before the bleeding started.

"You searched my kit. You searched my cot. You have my photographs to prove both. So you know I don't carry it. And you should consider, Captain, before you decide what that means, that a woman who spends three years writing down names the Army strikes off — a woman who has watched men be made into nobodies by clerks with better handwriting than conscience — does not keep that book where it can be burned. I sent it home. Months ago. With copies." She let the word *copies* rest exactly where it fell. "If I don't send word by Thursday, more copies go further. I learned that from the war, Captain. Everything important should be in two places, because everything important gets destroyed in one."

Silence. The broken clock ticked. Out on the water, faintly, another ship sounded — the tide turning, or a tug, or the great delayed transport herself shifting at anchor in the roads, carrying toward morning all those berths and all that arithmetic.

"That," Vane said at last, quietly, "was well done."

"It wasn't done for your approval."

"No. Still. Well done." He stood, buttoned his tunic with small precise movements, and for a moment the lamplight made him ordinary — just a tired staff officer with ink on his cuff and a war to administrate. "Here is what will happen. You will be escorted to the Sisters' quarters at the port annex and kept there — comfortably; I insist on that — until morning. At eight, you and I will speak again. By then I will have verified some things, spoken to some people, and determined whether the story you've told me tonight is the truth or merely excellent tailoring." He gathered the folder, tucked it under his arm, and paused at the door. "You've bought yourself until morning, Sister. I want you to appreciate that's all you've bought. Morning comes regardless. What it brings depends entirely on whether you spend the night deciding to be sensible."

"And the boy?" The question escaped before she could cage it, and hated herself for the hunger in it. "Where is he now?"

Vane's hand rested on the door handle. "Somewhere on the docks, presumably, being looked for by people paid to look. Small boys are remarkably difficult to lose in a port town — and remarkably easy to lose in one. It depends entirely on who finds him first." He opened the door onto the corridor's colder dark. "Good night, Sister Sterling. Sleep well. One of us should."

The door closed. The lock did not turn — she noted that; they wanted her mobile, manageable, unbruised for morning — and she heard his boots recede along the boards, unhurried, metronomic, fading into the dock sounds beyond.

* * *

Edmund sat for a long time in the pool of lamplight, doing what she had done every night of the war: taking stock.

Her body: intact, exhausted, hands steady enough to work. Her position: detained until eight, watched, but not charged, not yet. Her assets: one bluff about copies that was nine parts invention and one part desperate hope — there were no copies, there was only the ledger itself, and the ledger was sewn into a coat lining or hidden in a satchel or carried in a small boy's grip somewhere out in the black labyrinth of Boulogne docks. Her liabilities: Vane had the letters, Vane had Tomas, Vane had leverage over Greer, and Vane had until eight o'clock to verify that her midnight fiction was tissue.

And Cyrus. Five years old, mute, alone, in a port town full of military police and Vane's hired searchers, with nothing of the evidence left but the thing that could not be confiscated — a face, remembered; a name, *Ada*, spoken once into the dark.

She rose, crossed to the window, and put her forehead against the cold glass. Below, the docks lay under searchlights, white cones sweeping the crowds of refugees who slept in rows like the dead of some earlier battle, and among those blankets, somewhere, was a small boy who would not speak to strangers, who clung to satchels, who trusted one face in all the world.

*Hold the line,* Matron Greer had written. *Trust no officer.* Well. She had held it, and trusted none, and given Vane truth shaped like surrender — and it had bought her the night, no more. Eight o'clock. Nine hours. In nine hours Vane would learn there were no copies unless she made some, and the only way to make copies was testimony, paper, ink, and a lawyer's address in London, and the only person who could carry any of it out of France was already asleep on these docks or lost in them.

Edmund turned from the window and began, methodically, to plan — counting doors, counting guards, counting hours the way she had once counted pulses.

Outside, the tide was turning. At low water, she knew from the charts pinned in the passage, one could walk the harbor wall's seaward rocks all the way from the fish gate to the eastern mole — past the patrols, past the lights, out to where the crowds thinned and the ships waited.

Nine hours. Somewhere out there, a berth was sailing at nine, and she intended to be standing under it when the gangway went down.
