## 25. Tomas Pays Again

The customs office still smelled of lamplight and cold tea when they let her go.

Edmund stepped out into the Boulogne night as though stepping out of a bath too hot — the whole world a shock of cold after the close, careful cruelty of that little room. Her legs carried her because they had learned during three years of war to carry her whether she willed it or not. Her mind lagged behind, still sitting in the chair across from Captain Vane, still hearing the mild click of his pocket watch being closed, the way he said *Matron Greer* and *Orderly Tomas* as though reading names from a menu, letting her fill in the prices herself.

She had given him truth shaped like surrender. She had told him enough — that the boy was in the dock crowds somewhere, that she would find him before morning — to buy hours, and she had kept back the one thing that mattered: that the satchel Vane's men had seized was only half the war. The ledger rode inside Edmund's own coat now, against her ribs, where it had ridden since the docks; she had lied about that too, smoothly, easily, and the ease frightened her more than anything he had said.

The quay stretched away under electric lamps strung on temporary poles, their light shivering in the wind off the water. The crowd had not thinned with the hour. Refugees lay in heaps of blankets along the warehouse walls, children sleeping sitting upright, soldiers who could not sleep standing at the rail and staring at the black water as if it might explain itself. Somewhere out in the roads, invisible past the harbour arm, the evacuation ship rode at anchor — a shape with lights, patient as a promise. The whistle would sound at dawn. Not before.

And Cyrus was out here alone in all of this, five years old, and silent, and quick as a sparrow, and she had let him run.

Not let him, she corrected herself, walking faster. He had run when the searchers grabbed at his coat — had twisted out of it like a snake leaving its skin, leaving the letters in their hands and himself free, and God, the terrible cleverness of it, the boy had *planned* it, or some animal part of him had, the part that had already watched one person die and decided it would not be him. He had bolted into the crowd and she had been held by two military policemen at the cordon while Vane's voice said pleasantly behind her, *Do give the child our regards when you find him, Miss Sterling,* and then they had walked her to the customs office instead of letting her run after him.

Three hours. It had cost her three hours and every ounce of composure she owned.

Now she walked the quay edge, scanning the sleeping heaps, the doorways, the gaps between crated shells — crates of shells stacked ten feet high within reach of a hospital pier, the war's idea of humour — looking for a small figure with a coat too big for him and eyes that had learned to be doors rather than windows. She did not call his name. Calling his name in this crowd would be like striking a match in a powder store; Vane's men were not far, she could feel them the way one feels weather coming, and half of Boulogne would sell a found child for the price of a meal.

*Cyrus.* She had named him for no reason she could explain. Now the name sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She worked the length of the fish market first — empty but for cats and the reek of the day's catch — then the alley behind the Hôtel de la Marine, where the refugees had built a corridor of tarpaulins and hope. Nothing. Then back toward the gate.

The gate.

It stopped her the way a hand stops a clock. The gate to the inner basin, where the gangways were, where the wounded would be brought at first light — a double leaf of green-painted iron, and through its bars, in the lee of the gatehouse, a single lamp burning over a single figure. An orderly's grey uniform, sleeves rolled to the elbow even in the cold, dark hair falling across a forehead she knew.

Tomas.

He was standing at the bars talking to the sentries — not arguing, nothing so alarming as arguing, just talking, his hands moving, his face wearing the easy grin she had seen him use on dying men and frightened sisters alike. One of the sentries was laughing. The other was scowling, and the scowl was the thing that turned Edmund's stomach over, because she knew what it meant to watch a man spend himself and not yet know what he was buying.

She moved closer, into the shadow of a shell crate, and listened.

"—so my grandmother says, madame, not the priest, not the mayor, my grandmother, and she is four foot nothing —" Tomas was saying, in French pitched to carry, and the laughing sentry wiped his eyes. "You cannot refuse an old woman's cure for chilblains. It involves goose fat and insults. The insults are the important part."

*Tomas.* What are you doing here? He should have been with the ambulance column at the far quay; she had seen him waved toward it hours ago, before the customs office, before everything. His presence at this gate at this hour was not accident. And the sentries were looking at him, both of them now, the scowler included, their backs to the basin, their attention gathered up like spilled coins into his open hands —

— and Edmund understood.

Beyond them, through the bars, in the black seam of shadow between the gatehouse wall and a stack of dunnage, something small shifted. A coat too big. Eyes catching the lamp for half a heartbeat and going dark again.

Her breath left her slowly, so slowly it made no sound at all.

He was inside the basin. He had been inside the basin — how long? How had a five-year-old got through a guarded gate into the one place in Boulogne he might actually survive until dawn, warm-ish, hidden, steps from the gangway? And the answer stood at the bars telling a story about his grandmother's goose fat, spending his charm like a man scattering bread before birds so that no one would look at what the birds were doing behind them.

Tomas was covering for the boy. Had probably been covering for him since the crowd took him — had found him, she would learn later, huddled under a tarpaulin near the ambulance shed, and had done the arithmetic of gates and guards in the time it took to roll his sleeves. And now he was holding two armed men exactly where Edmund needed them not to look.

Which meant there was one thing left in the world that Tomas needed from her, and it was for her to stop hesitating in the shadows like a woman writing her memoirs, and go and get her child.

Edmund went to the small side wicket set into the larger gate — the one Tomas had angled his shoulders to screen, the one whose latch she had noticed an hour ago and dismissed as impossible. Her hands knew latches. They knew buckles and knots and the clasps of kit bags packed by men who would not live to unpack them; her fingers had spent a war learning the grammar of small fastenings, and the wicket gave to her the way such things give to calm hands, with a soft iron click that the laughter covered entirely.

She slipped through. Crossed eight yards of cobbles in the dead angle of the lamp. Knelt.

Cyrus looked at her out of the dark, and did not run to her — he never ran to anyone, that was one of the thousand things the war had taken from him before he could miss it — but his hand came out and gripped her sleeve, hard, five fingers and a fistful of wool, and that was his running, that was his shouting, that was the whole of his vocabulary tonight. His face was grey with cold and grime and the particular exhaustion of children who have learned to sleep with their ears working. There was blood on his knuckles, a scrape, shallow. She put her lips against his hair.

"I have you," she breathed. "I have you, I have you."

Behind her, at the bars, the story ended.

She heard it end the way one hears a floor give way in another room — a change in the quality of the silence. The laughter stopped. The scowling sentry's voice said something short and official, and then a different voice said something else, an officer's voice, unhurried and faintly bored, a voice she knew the way you know a toothache, and Edmund, still kneeling with her arms full of a shaking boy in the shadow of the dunnage, closed her eyes.

*Adjutant Brandt.* Vane's man, working the quay since noon, asking questions in a voice like wet gravel. Of course he would come to the gate. Of course he would come now.

"Name and unit," Brandt said.

"Orderly Tomas Ferreira, sir, Ambulance Column Fourteen, and I am profoundly sorry to say I have lost my pass, which is why I am standing out here like a beggar instead of—" 

"Turn out your pockets."

A pause. Rustling. Edmund eased her head around the corner of the crate, one inch, and saw Tomas's hands, palms up, offering the contents of his pockets to the lamp: a pencil stub, a folded map gone soft as cloth, a tin of condensed milk with a hole punched in the lid. And something else. A second paper. Cream-coloured, official-sized, and Edmund's heart performed a slow, sick somersault, because she knew that size, that cream, that watermark — it was a travel chit, the same stock as the one folded in her own coat with Cyrus's name inked where Private Ellison's name had been.

Brandt took it. Held it to the lamp. Turned it over.

"Where did an ambulance orderly get a civilian passage chit?"

"Bought it, sir. Off a sailor. Probably shouldn't have." The grin was audible. "My sister's in England. I thought — well. You know how it is."

"You thought."

"It's blank, sir. Look at it. It's worth nothing."

It was blank. She could see that from eight yards; Brandt could see it from one. But the adjutant held it anyway, turning it in the light, and Edmund watched his face do the thing faces did when a man suspected a shape beneath a surface — the slight forward tilt, the narrowing, the hunter's stillness. Blank chits were stock. Blank chits were forgery waiting to happen. She had made one herself twelve hours ago with a stolen pen nib and Tomas's borrowed steadiness of hand, and here was Tomas holding its twin like a man holding a lit fuse and calling it a cigarette.

"Detain him," Brandt said. "Check the column manifests. If his name isn't down where it should be, we'll find out why he's carrying passage papers he can't account for."

"Sergeant." Tomas's voice changed then. Not pleading — she would have expected pleading, would perhaps have forgiven pleading — but flat and clear, pitched low, pitched *past* the sentries, pitched across eight yards of darkness to the shadow where he knew she knelt. "Sergeant, mind the boy's coat, he gets cold."

As if Cyrus were standing beside him. As if any boy were there at all. It was a sentence with no meaning except one: *I know you're there. I know you heard. Go.*

Two military policemen took his arms. He went without resistance, without drama, turning once at the gatehouse door to give the laughing sentry — the young one, the one who had enjoyed the grandmother — a small salute and a smile, and the door closed on him, and the lamp outside it burned on with nobody under it at all.

Edmund stayed in the shadow a long moment, Cyrus's grip cutting circulation out of her wrist, the cold coming up through her knees from the cobbles, and she made herself count, because counting was what she had instead of weeping. One berth. That was the arithmetic waiting at dawn, the sum she had been refusing to do: one berth remaining after the wounded boarded, and three claimants — Hollis, who had asked her to make his name mean something; Cyrus, whom she would trade the whole world for; and herself, who had no one at all to speak for her except her own tired voice. Three lives, one plank.

And now the plank had a fourth price attached, written in another currency entirely: whatever it cost to get Tomas out of that gatehouse before morning, because a detained orderly with a blank chit and a known connection to her would unravel everything — the forged passage, the testimony, all of it — unless someone intervened, and the only someone was her.

*The price of survival is always paid by someone else first.* Tomas had told her that on the train, in the dark between Arras and Amiens, telling her about the other satchel, the other family, his brother. He had said it as warning. He had apparently also meant it as instruction, because he had just lived it again, deliberately, on purpose, choosing the cell over the safe road with a joke about goose fat on his lips.

She got Cyrus up. She wrapped her own scarf around his neck, tucked his scraped hand into her pocket, and walked him away from the gate at the pace of a woman taking the air, past the sleeping heaps, past the shell crates, until the gatehouse lamp was a star behind them and the ship's riding lights hung ahead above the harbour arm like a constellation with no name.

Only when they reached the shelter of an overturned lifeboat on the beach side of the mole did she let herself stop, and kneel, and hold him with both arms and no dignity at all, her face pressed to his cold hair, feeling his heart go quick and stubborn against her own like two clocks disagreeing about the hour.

"You clever, brave boy," she whispered. "You clever, impossible boy."

He patted her back twice, the way one comforts a patient, and she laughed despite everything, a cracked sound, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

Then she took out the ledger — because it was the ledger, always, that did her thinking for her — and opened it against her knee in the thin moonlight, and turned past the small names, past Hollis's page with its struck-off line restored in her careful hand, to the blank leaf at the back, and wrote, in pencil, because pencil could be denied:

*Tomas Ferreira, orderly, Ambulance Column Fourteen. Detained at the basin gate, 11 February, for my sake. Owed: everything.*

She looked at the entry a long time. Then she added one line more, the line she had been avoiding since the customs office released her, the line that was really the whole chapter of tomorrow compressed into six words:

*One berth. Dawn. Decide.*

Cyrus leaned against her shoulder and studied the page upside down with the grave attention he gave all her marks on paper, as if names were things that could be taught to behave. Perhaps they could. Hers had not behaved yet.

Out in the roads, the ship's anchor light burned steady over black water, and behind them, in a lit room at the basin gate, a man who had already paid once with his brother sat paying again — and dawn was five hours off, and Captain Vane would want his answer by then, and somewhere between the cell door and the gangway Edmund was going to have to find a way to be three people's worth of brave with only one berth to give.

She closed the ledger. She took Cyrus's hand.

"Hold on to me," she said, "and don't let go, whatever happens."

He didn't answer. He never answered. But his grip tightened, five fingers and a fistful of her sleeve, and together they turned toward the long black hours left before the whistle.
