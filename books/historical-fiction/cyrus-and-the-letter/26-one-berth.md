## 26. One Berth

The whistle came at half past four in the morning, a long low moan that rolled across the harbour like something alive and grieving. Edmund had heard sirens, shells, the scream of stretcher wheels on cobbles, and she thought she had catalogued every sound the war could make. She was wrong. This one went through her ribs and settled somewhere beneath her heart, and it said: *now*. All your counting is finished. Now you must choose.

The gangway had been lowered while it was still dark, and already the wounded were going up it — a slow procession of blankets and bandages and bare feet, orderlies steadying men who wept without noise because they had used up all their noise months ago. The ship sat low in the water, grey as the sky, her funnel breathing smoke into a wind that smelled of coal oil and salt and the cold iron tang of the docks. On her hull, someone had painted a white cross large enough to be seen from the air, or from God, whichever came looking first.

Edmund stood with a hand on each of them.

Cyrus was at her left side, his small fingers hooked through two of hers, his coat hanging heavy with what was sewn into its lining — though she no longer knew whether the letters still rode there or had already been found. Hollis stood at her right, swaying slightly, upright by will rather than strength, his cap held in both hands like a schoolboy at a funeral. He had shaved that morning. She had done it for him, the razor trembling in her own grip more than in his, and when she finished he had looked at himself in the little mirror and said, quietly, "Well. There he is," as though greeting a man he had been introduced to once, long ago.

"Edmund." His voice was rough gravel. "You've gone the colour of the harbour."

"I've always been this colour."

"You haven't. You've been the colour of someone who knows her own mind, these five months, and now you're the colour of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want to finish."

She almost laughed. That was the cruelty of Hollis — the shell had taken so much from him and left the seeing intact, sharpened it even, the way fire tempers a blade. He watched everything now because he could trust nothing else to watch itself.

"The list says three hundred souls," she said. "They're calling it two hundred and ninety-nine berths after the walking wounded. Someone made an error. Someone always makes an error, and the error is always a person."

"And the person this time is standing between you and the water."

* * *

She had done the arithmetic a dozen times since the customs office, and every time it came out the same way, which was the trouble with arithmetic — it was honest even when you hated it.

One berth. The purser's manifest, corrected twice overnight by clerks who worked with the grim efficiency of men who knew their numbers were lives, had closed around her like a hand. The wounded took the hold and the lower decks. The displaced civilians — widows, orphans, the shattered residue of three villages — took the tween decks. By midnight the provisional list bore two names she had fought for: E. Sterling, nurse, and child (male, approx. 5 yrs), unregistered. And then at six o'clock the adjutant had come to the shelter where the port refugees huddled, carrying a transfer paper, and read out a name that did not exist.

"Private —" he'd squinted — "Hollis. Struck off the rolls. Desertion, pending review. No sponsor, no papers, no entitlement." He'd said it without malice, which was somehow worse. Malice at least acknowledged you existed. "He can't board. Regulations are regulations."

And Edmund, who had lied smoothly to Captain Vane and forged a travel chit with a steady hand, had heard herself say: "He has a sponsor."

The adjutant had looked up. "Who?"

"A registered nurse of good standing. Myself. I'll sign for him."

"Then you take responsibility for his conduct, and your own place is forfeit if he's not on the rolls within—" he'd consulted his watch— "the ship sails at five. You can't sign him aboard and yourself aboard both, sister. One signature, one berth. That's how paper works."

That was how paper worked. She had spent five years learning exactly how paper worked — how it erased men like Hollis, how it could be unmade and remade in lamplight, how a name written small in a private ledger could outlast regiments. Paper was never neutral. Paper was a weapon that decided who was real.

So: one berth, three people. Cyrus, who carried evidence that could hang powerful men and whose survival was worth more than any single life including her own — she had accepted that weeks ago, before she loved him, back when he was only strategy. Hollis, whose name she had promised to make mean something, whose body would not survive another winter in France, whose redemption was a debt she had incurred the first time she wrote his name down and then did nothing with it. And herself — and here the arithmetic turned ugly, because her own claim was the weakest and the strongest at once. Weakest, because a healthy woman of twenty-nine could walk back up the hill to the hospitals and probably live. Strongest, because Cyrus would not let go of her hand, and because some part of her — the part she did not permit to speak aloud — wanted desperately to believe that wanting to survive was reason enough.

* * *

The queue moved. It always moved, indifferent as weather.

Cyrus tugged her sleeve. He had not spoken since the night he said his mother's name — *Élise, Élise, Élise*, three times, like a bell rung until it cracked — and Edmund understood now that he spent words the way misers spent gold, keeping them against some future need too terrible to imagine. But he communicated constantly anyway. He pulled her sleeve and pointed at the gangway, then at Hollis, then at himself, and then he did the thing that broke her heart in a new way each time: he pointed at her, and then shook his head, and pressed his palm flat against her chest over her heart.

*I know,* she told him with her eyes. *I know, my darling. I'm still working.*

He had grown since Ypres. Not taller, exactly — there had not been enough food for tall — but filled-out, awake, a boy instead of a ghost. He let Matron Greer's letter ride folded in his pocket next to his drawings, and sometimes he took the drawings out and looked at his mother's face and did not burn at the edges anymore, because Edmund had given him new paper and taught him that remembering did not require suffering, only attention. Last week he had drawn her — Edmund herself — standing at a cot with her hands full of bandage, and the likeness was terrible and true, and she had kept it inside her copybook where she kept the things she could not afford to lose.

Now he stood in the grey light with his satchel over his shoulder like a small soldier reporting for embarkation, and she thought: *five years old*. Five years old, and he had already buried a mother, crossed a war, outwaited Captain Vane, and learned to read the moods of adults the way sailors read the sea. Children paid the war's bills too. Nobody ever put them on the manifest.

"Sister Sterling?"

She turned. The purser's assistant stood at the foot of the gangway with a clipboard and a pencil stub, harried, human, forty seconds from being someone else's problem. "We're boarding family groups now. You're on the list — you and the boy. Where's the third of you?"

"He's here," Edmund said. "Private Hollis. I'm signing as his sponsor."

The assistant frowned at his clipboard. "Sponsorship requires a responsible-party declaration, countersigned by the port medical officer. The MO boarded an hour ago."

"Then give me the form and a pencil, and I'll stand here and be responsible until the ship sails."

"That's not—"

"I know what it isn't. Give me the form."

He gave her the form. Perhaps it was the hour, perhaps it was that everyone at this dock had stopped believing in rules and started believing only in whatever got the next person up the plank, but he tore a sheet from his pad and handed it over, and Edmund wrote her name in the space provided with a hand that did not shake, and felt the pen make the choice before her heart was ready to ratify it.

There. Signed. If Hollis boarded under her sponsorship, her berth was consumed — the arithmetic completed itself with bureaucratic elegance, requiring nothing from her now but the courage to stand still while it happened.

She stared at her own name on the line and thought, absurdly, of Chapter Two of her own life — *What Can Be Carried* — the night in Ypres when she had sorted her possessions into kit bag and bonfire and kept only the ledger. She had thought then that she had made the hard choice, burning the letters, keeping the names. She had not known anything yet. Choosing what to carry was easy. Choosing whom to carry was the whole of it, the entire war distilled into a signature on a scrap of requisition paper.

* * *

"Hollis," she said. "Look at me."

He turned. Up close she could see the tremor in him, the fine vibration of a man whose nerves had been burned down to the wiring, and beneath it the face she had first recorded eleven months ago in a dressing station lit by hurricane lamps: *Hollis, J., private, struck from rolls, present, afraid, alive.* Present. Afraid. Alive. Her ledger had never needed more than those three words for anyone.

"When you get to England," she said, "there will be people who tell you that you deserted. Some of them will have forms."

"I know."

"There is a book," she said, "that says otherwise."

His eyes went bright. "Your book."

"My book. Every name in it is a man the army pretended never existed. Yours is the fourth entry. I wrote you down the night they brought you in, and I have never once considered striking you out." She took his hands — they were cold, they were always cold — and folded them around nothing, around air, around the weight of a promise. "James Hollis. Fourth of the Small Names. You are going to step onto that deck, and when they ask you who you are, you are going to say your whole name, loudly, as though it has never once been taken from you."

"Sister." His voice failed and restarted. "You can't do this. The boy needs—"

"The boy needs what I'm giving him, which is everything I have. And what I have is the knowledge of how to stay alive in France. I've done it five years. I'm rather good at it." She smiled, and was astonished to find the smile real. "Besides, someone has to stay behind and haunt Captain Vane."

"Don't joke about that man."

"I'm not joking. Haunting is the family business now. Ask anyone in my ledger."

A shell of laughter escaped him, ragged, half sob. Then his face crumpled all at once, the way faces did, and he gripped her hands hard enough to hurt and said, "Make it mean something. You promised. You tell me, every day of your life, what my name means, because I can't carry it myself yet — do you understand? I'll try. But at first you'll have to carry it for me."

"Then we'll both be carriers," she said. "It's an respectable trade. Look around."

The whistle sounded again, shorter this time, imperative. The assistant was waving them forward. Behind them the crowd pressed — a woman with twins, an old man carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, a girl of maybe twelve leading her blind grandmother by a scarf — and Edmund looked at all of them and understood that the berth she was surrendering would be taken by one of these, that mercy was not a clean transaction but a stone dropped in a pond, ripples touching strangers she would never meet. Let it ripple. Let it reach the birdcage man. Let it reach everyone it could.

* * *

The gangway rose before them, narrow and wet, planked and roped, sloping up into the smoke-dark morning like the beginning of a sentence no one would let her finish. At its head a sailor stood with his arms out — a big man, red-faced, wind-burnt, reaching down the slope for the smallest passengers because the smallest passengers always faltered halfway.

Edmund lifted Cyrus.

It was not the plan; the plan had been words, explanations, a last conversation she had rehearsed forty times. But his arms came up to her without being asked, the way they had the first night at the hospital door, trusting her before he knew her name, and she gathered him up and felt his heart hammering against her collarbone, quick as a bird's, and knew there would never be a better moment than this one, which was the worst moment of her life.

"Hollis — take my bag. Everything in it is yours now. The copybook too."

He took it without understanding, and then understood, and his mouth opened on protest, and she shook her head once, hard, the field-hospital gesture that ended arguments before they began.

She walked to the foot of the gangway with the boy in her arms and spoke into his ear, fast and quiet, under the whistle and the engines and the crying of the gulls, a message meant to survive the crossing of a channel and the crossing of years:

"Cyrus. Listen to me. Listen once, and keep it always. In your satchel, under your drawings, there is a notebook. It is full of names — soldiers, forgotten, and one woman called Élise, and now one boy called Cyrus, whom I named for no reason except that he deserved a name that nobody could strike out. You will guard that book. You will grow tall. You will learn to read every word in it. And when you are grown — when you are safe, and educated, and angry in useful ways — you will find out why your mother died, and you will say her name in rooms where it has never been allowed. That is your work, my love. That is what I am sending you to do."

He was crying silently, the way he did everything, his fists knotted in her collar.

"And one thing more." She pulled back so she could see his face, so he could see hers, and she held it there, willing her features into his memory grain by grain: the grey eyes, the scar at the eyebrow from shrapnel at Passchendaele, the small crooked tooth when she smiled, and she was smiling now, God help her, smiling through it. "The note they pinned to you said *forget this face*. Don't. Forget the war, forget Vane, forget the dock and the smell and the fear — forget all of it, every bit, you have my permission and my blessing. But not this face. This face is going to remember you every day of its life, and it would like very much to be remembered back."

The sailor's hands appeared at the edge of her vision, outstretched, patient, enormous.

She kissed his forehead — the warm, salt-smelling centre of him, the whole world concentrated into one small boy — and lifted him upward into the waiting arms, higher than she had ever thrown anything, higher than hope, and his fingers trailed from her collar and caught air, and then he was up, he was held, he was on the gangway above her turning back with his mouth open in a soundless cry, and Edmund stood at the bottom with empty arms and did not fall, because falling was not among the permitted options.

Hollis went up ahead of him, slow, gripping the rail, pausing once to look back. She raised her hand. He raised his. Between them passed everything that had ever been written in a small notebook by hurricane light.

The whistle screamed. The gap between ship and dock widened by inches, then feet, then a darkness of green water no hand could span. Cyrus stood at the stern rail between a sailor's knees, small and straight, one hand raised, and he did not look away and neither did she, and Edmund thought: *this is what it is to be a ledger.* To hold names. To hold one above all others, and to keep it, always, in a hand that does not shake.

Then a voice at her shoulder, silk over steel, said:

"Well, Sister. How very moving. And now that your boat has sailed — I wonder if we might discuss what remains."

Captain Vane stepped out of the shadow of the customs shed, and behind him, flanking the gate, stood two military policemen who were not watching Vane at all.
