## 27. The Girl Who Forgot the Face

The whistle had not yet sounded its last warning, but the dock had already begun to empty itself of everything that mattered. Wounded men went up the gangway two at a time, stretchers swaying between their bearers like hammocks in a swell that hadn't reached the harbor yet. Edmund stood at the foot of the plank with Cyrus's hand in hers and felt the smallness of his fingers, the particular way his thumb pressed against her palm — a pressure she had learned to read over these weeks the way sailors read weather, and which now she understood she would have to survive on for the rest of her life.

One berth.

She had done the arithmetic a dozen times since midnight, as though the numbers might change if she interrogated them often enough. They never did. Private Hollis stood a few feet away, upright by an act of will so tremendous it seemed to bend the air around him, his kit bag hanging from a shoulder that had forgotten how to carry anything lighter than a rifle and then nothing at all. He was watching her with those eyes that had been hollow once and were hollow no longer — eyes that had remembered, three days ago, their own name, and had wept at the reunion.

And Cyrus. Five years old, mute since before she'd known him, carrying a satchel heavier than any child should be asked to bear — though the letters were gone now, seized by Vane's men along with the coat they'd been sewn into, and what remained in the satchel was only what Edmund meant to add to it.

She knelt down on the wet stone of the dock. The cold came up through her skirt and settled into her knees, and she let it, because she wanted to remember this — every detail, even the discomforts. Especially the discomforts. She had spent four years recording the names of men the army had decided to forget, and she knew now that memory was not a passive thing, not a photograph fading on a shelf. Memory was work. Memory was a decision made over and over, hour after hour, until it became a habit, and habits became a life.

"Cyrus," she said quietly. "Look at me."

He looked. He had grown thinner since Boulogne, or perhaps she had only just begun to see it; worry carved children faster than adults, because there was less of them to carve. His hair needed cutting. There was a smudge of coal dust along his jaw where he had leaned against the dock bollard that morning, watching the tugs nose the great gray hull of the ship into position. She wanted to wipe it away with her thumb and could not make her hand do it, because if she touched his face she would come apart here on the dock in front of God and the Royal Navy and Captain Vane's adjutant, who was still circling the crowd like a gull with opinions.

"I have something for you," she said.

* * *

She had wrapped it in the oilcloth herself, in the customs shed, an hour ago, while Vane's questions still rang in her ears and Tomas sat somewhere behind wire, detained, paying again. The ledger — her ledger, the one she had kept through Ypres and Passchendaele and all the nameless months between, the one with its soft leather cover cracked from handling and its pages swollen with pencil entries in a nurse's cramped hand — lay inside a wrapping she had tied with surgical knots, because some things deserved the best of whatever skill you had.

Two hundred and eleven names. That was the count as of last night, when she had made her final additions: Hollis's true name, written out whole and restored, dated and witnessed in the margin with her own declaration. *Struck off in error. Remembered here.* And beneath it, in fresh ink, one more entry — the first in the book's history that was not a recovery but a bequest.

*Cyrus. Age five. Son of Alice Marlowe, housemaid, late of the Ashcombe household, London. His mother's name is true even if no record says so. He is entrusted to the future.*

She had written *entrusted* rather than *sent*, because *sent* implied she was letting go, and she was not. She was placing him somewhere she could not follow, which is a different thing entirely, and requires a different vocabulary — stiffer, more formal, like the language of wills and commissions and other documents in which people describe acts too enormous for ordinary speech.

Now she drew the satchel onto her lap and unclasped it. Inside: a folded shirt too big for him, a tin cup, half a loaf wrapped in newspaper, the little wooden horse he had carried since the night he arrived at her door without a voice. She laid the oilcloth parcel among these humble treasures, settling it against his horse as though introducing them.

"This," she said, "is very important. More important than anything else you will ever carry, until someday it isn't — and I'll explain what I mean by that when you're older, or better, you'll understand it yourself." Her hands were steady. She marveled at them. "There are names in this book. Men whom the war tried to lose. As long as someone carries their names, they are not lost. Do you understand? A person can be erased from paper. But paper isn't the only place a person lives."

Cyrus watched her mouth the way he always did, reading her words in the movement of her lips before they reached his ears — a habit she had noticed early and never commented on, because some intelligences announce themselves and simply wait to be honored. Now he reached out and put both hands flat on the oilcloth, and looked up at her with a question he could not ask aloud.

"It's yours now," she said. "I'm giving it to you. Not lending it. Giving."

His chin trembled. He was too young to hide it, and she loved him ferociously for not hiding it, and she hated herself for the gift she was about to complete, and she did it anyway, because love that stops at the edge of its own pain is not love but its rehearsal.

"The ship," she said, "is going to England. England is where the people are who can keep you safe, and finish what your mother started, and make sure the men who hurt her are made to answer for it. You cannot do that here. I cannot do it there — not as fast, not as well. This is not a choice between loving you and not loving you." Her voice caught on the word *loving*, snagged like cloth on wire, and she forced it smooth again. "It is a choice about who goes where, and I have done the sums more times than there are stars, and every time the answer is the same. It has to be you."

He shook his head. Once, twice, small violent movements, and then his face crumpled and the sound came out of him — not words, never words, but a cry like a hinge that had rusted shut, and he threw himself against her chest and gripped her coat with both fists as though the gangway were already pulling them apart, as though he knew, with the terrible clairvoyance of children, exactly what was happening and had decided to register his objection with his whole body.

Edmund held him. She held him the way she had held dying men, which is to say completely, leaving nothing in reserve, her cheek against his hair, her hand moving over his back in slow circles. Around them the dock continued its business — boots, orders, the groan of the ship's horn testing itself, a chaplain's voice somewhere reciting the Twenty-Third Psalm to a man on a stretcher who was either asleep or past needing it. The world was under no obligation to pause for her heartbreak, and it didn't, and there was a strange mercy in that, because it meant she could break privately, inside the noise, unseen.

"I know," she whispered into his hair. "I know. I know."

* * *

Later — minutes later, or years; grief keeps its own clock — she held him at arm's length and wiped his face with her sleeve, the gesture she had denied herself earlier, spending it now like the last coin of a currency about to be withdrawn.

"Now listen to me," she said. "This is the important part. This is the part you must keep."

He sniffed and nodded, obedient, wrecked, brave.

"There was a note pinned to your coat the night you came to us," she said. "It said, *forget this face.* Whoever wrote it wanted you to carry nothing — no names, no faces, no proof. They wanted you to be nobody, going nowhere, so that no one would ever look for you and no one would ever find you." She took his face in both hands. His jaw fit in her palms almost exactly, as though her hands had been measured for this and had waited thirty years for the commission. "You are not going to obey that note. Do you hear me? You are going to do the opposite. You are going to remember everything. Your mother's face — the drawings you made me, I've seen them, the woman with the kind eyes — you'll remember her. And Tomas, who helped you. And Matron Greer, who wrote the letter that saved you. And Hollis, standing right there—" she tipped her chin toward him, and Hollis, understanding himself summoned into the boy's inheritance, straightened further and gave a nod of such gravity that it might have been a sacrament— "and me."

"Me most of all," she said, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back. "Because there is one more thing in your satchel besides the book. It doesn't weigh anything and it won't show up in any search. I put it there just now, while I was holding you. It's my face. I want you to keep it. Not the note's version — not forgetting. Keeping. Every morning when you wash your face in England, I want you to look at the water and check whether you still have mine. When you learn to write properly, you'll write to me — Matron Greer will give you the address, she has arranged it all — and you will tell me you still have it. And if ever you forget, you'll open the ledger and read my handwriting, because a person's handwriting is a kind of face too, and mine is all over that book like footprints in snow."

Cyrus's mouth moved. No sound came, but she saw the shape of it, saw him practicing the architecture of a sentence, and whatever it was, it died in his throat and came out instead as a single nod, fierce and total, the nod of a soldier accepting terms.

"That's my boy," she said, and the words broke her in half and rebuilt her in the same breath.

* * *

The last call came as a blast from the ship that scattered gulls off the warehouse roofs. A sailor at the gangway head was shouting the final boarding, and the queue of remaining civilians — a handful, only a handful, the berth arithmetic made suddenly, brutally visible — began to move. Hollis stepped forward, then stopped and turned back, and for a moment the three of them stood in a loose triangle on the wet stones, a family assembled by accident and war and choice, about to be dispersed by a gangway.

"Sterling," Hollis said. His voice was rough; shell shock had taken the polish off it long ago. "The name you kept. You made it mean something." He swallowed. "Whatever happens on this dock after that whistle — it's already meant something. Don't let anyone tell you different. Including you."

"Hollis—" she began.

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You don't get to thank me and I don't get to thank you, there isn't time, and anyway it would take all day and the navy hasn't got all day." Something flickered across his ruined face that was almost, astonishingly, a smile. "Look after the lad's book. Read him the names when he can't sleep. Works better than laudanum."

Then he picked up his kit bag, took Cyrus's hand in his free one — the boy went to him readily, trusted him, another thread in the net she had spent months weaving without knowing it — and walked toward the gangway, and Edmund stood alone on the dock and watched her whole reason for existing move away from her in the arms of a man whose name she had rescued from oblivion and who was now, in the only currency that mattered, repaying the debt.

At the head of the plank the sailor held out his arms. Hollis paused, set Cyrus down, and bent to say something into his ear — she would wonder forever what it was — and then stepped aside, and Cyrus turned around at the top of the gangway, a small figure against the gray sky and the ship's superstructure, and looked down at her.

Everything in her surged upward. Every muscle she owned voted to run up that plank, to seize him, to present herself to the sailor and lie and beg and produce papers she didn't have. She had to lock her knees to keep her feet on the stone. This, she thought, this is what faith actually is — not belief in the comfortable sense, not hope with cushions, but the moment when you send the thing you cannot afford to lose into hands you cannot control, and stay where you are, and trust.

Cyrus raised one hand. Not waving — holding it flat, palm out, the way she had taught him to signal *I see you, I am here* during the train journey, when words were impossible and noise made touch dangerous.

*I see you,* his hand said. *I am here.*

She raised her own palm, and held it there, and did not wave, and did not look away, and saw him turn at last and disappear into the ship's dark doorway like a coin dropped into a pocket.

* * *

The mooring lines came off at four minutes past eleven. Edmund had looked at her watch; she would always know it was four minutes past eleven. The great hull shifted, sighed, gathered itself, and the gap of dark green water opened between the dock and the ship — six inches, then a yard, then a gulf — and she stood at the very edge of the stones with her toes nearly overhanging, the way mourners stand at gravesides, close enough to fall, declining to.

She found him. It took her a moment — the rail was lined with soldiers and refugees, a hedge of faces — and then she saw him, third pillar from the stern, small hands hooked over the rail, his face pale above the gray water. Beside him, Hollis's taller shape, one arm around the boy's shoulders. Good. Good. She sent the word at them with her whole mind, silently, uselessly, the prayers of the non-religious.

The ship's horn sounded, long and low, a sound like the earth grieving, and the tugboats leaned into their cables, and the *City of Rouen* — she had made herself learn the name, she refused to send her reason into the world aboard an unnamed vessel — began to move.

Cyrus's face did not crumple. She had braced for that, prepared to hold the image of his tears for the rest of her life as a kind of penance. Instead he did something worse, and better. He kept his eyes on her the entire way, unblinking, deliberate, a sentinel's watchfulness, as though he had accepted her instruction literally and completely and was already at work at the task she had given him: keeping her face. Checking the water of the receding harbor to confirm it was still there. And when the distance grew too great for detail, when she became merely a figure among figures on the dock, a woman in a nurse's grays with the wind pulling at her hair, he lifted his hand again — palm out, flat, steady — and held it there until the ship swung toward the harbor mouth and the angle took him from her.

The gulls followed the ship out. The crowd on the dock dissolved into its separate griefs. And Edmund Sterling stood at the edge of France in the thin winter light with empty hands and a full inventory — every entry in a book she no longer owned, every fold of a small coat, the exact pressure of a thumb against her palm — and understood that she had not given the boy the ledger at all.

She had given him the war's opposition. Everything the war did — subtract, erase, forget — the book did the reverse, and now the book was at sea, sailing toward Whitehall in the keeping of a five-year-old with a wooden horse, and whatever happened next, whatever Vane attempted, whatever the Ashcombes' money bought, there existed in the world a portable, waterproofed, surgically knotted argument that the forgotten had been remembered, and it was out of her hands, and it was safe, and it was gone.

The harbor mouth emptied. The sea resumed its horizon.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, to the water, to the boy growing smaller by the second somewhere beyond the breakwater. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "That's done, then."

She turned from the wall.

Behind her, at the mouth of the customs shed, a figure detached itself from the shadows where he had been waiting with the patience of a man who has never once in his life missed an appointment: Captain Vane, hatless, gloved, smiling the smile of a chess player contemplating the board after his opponent's most confident move.

"Nurse Sterling," he said pleasantly. "I believe we have unfinished business — and I'm afraid you're about to learn what it costs to send my cargo to England."
