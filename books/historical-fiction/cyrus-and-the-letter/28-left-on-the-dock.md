## 28. Left on the Dock

The ship went out the way grief goes — slowly at first, so slowly that Edmund could pretend it was not happening, and then all at once, irretrievably.

She stood at the edge of the quay with Hollis beside her, both of them motionless while the crowd around them surged and wept and waved. The morning was the colour of unwashed wool, a low grey sky pressing down on the harbour, and the funnel smoke of the vessel unspooled behind it like a thread being drawn out of the world. Edmund did not wave. Her arms hung at her sides, emptied of their weight and therefore heavier than they had ever been.

*Remember my face,* she had told him. *Forget nothing else, but remember this.*

He had pressed his small palm flat against the porthole glass as they carried him below — she had seen him for one last instant there, a pale smudge of a face in a dark round frame, the satchel strap crossed over his chest like a bandolier, like a sash of office. Then a sailor's shoulder had blocked the glass, and he was gone, and now only the ship remained, and then not even that.

"She's turning," Hollis said. His voice was still rough from the shell-shock, from months of silence, but he used it now carefully, as a man uses a limb long splinted. "Look. She's making for the channel."

"I see it."

"He'll be all right." Hollis said it in the tone of a man building something brick by brick in front of him, testing each word before he set it down. "They'll feed him. There'll be a woman on board — there's always a woman on boats like that, some volunteer with biscuits in her pockets. He likes women with biscuits."

Edmund made a sound that was meant to be a laugh. It came out as something else, and she let it.

The berth was empty now. The arithmetic was finished: one place, three claimants, and she had spent hers the way she had decided in the small hours, lying on a bench in the customs shed with Cyrus asleep against her ribs — spent it on the boy and the ledger both, the child and the proof, sent them off together into the grey water like a message in a bottle addressed to a country she could no longer quite imagine. Hollis had his passage revoked anyway; the medical board had re-listed him an hour before boarding, some clerk's pen-stroke undoing everything, and Edmund had felt, absurdly, relieved — as though the decision had been taken out of her hands, though it had not been, though it never had been.

And so they stood, the two of them. The forgotten woman and the forgotten man, watching the remembered boy go.

The wake reached the sea wall and broke against it in small exhausted waves. Gulls wheeled screaming over the mooring posts. Somewhere behind them a lorry reversed, its warning bell shrill and idiotic in all that vastness.

"Miss Sterling." Hollis's hand found her sleeve — he rarely touched anyone, but when he did it was always with the delicacy of a bird landing. "You did the right thing."

"The right thing," Edmund repeated. The words tasted like dock water. "Do you know what I gave him, Private Hollis? A notebook full of dead men and a satchel full of evidence that will get him questioned by the first constable who looks inside it. I gave a five-year-old child a job too heavy for any soldier, and I called it safety, and I put him on a boat alone."

"You gave him the truth to carry instead of a lie," Hollis said. "There are men in that hospital who'd have traded twenty years of their lives for someone to do that for them."

Edmund looked at him. He was thinner than when she had first found his name struck from the rolls — they were all thinner — but his eyes had come back to themselves somehow, since she had spoken his true name aloud to him on the pallet by the coal store, since he had wept and asked her to make it mean something. It meant something now. It meant standing on this dock, left behind, alive.

The ship cleared the breakwater. For a moment its hull flashed pale against the open sea, and then it was only a shape again, and then a suggestion, and then Edmund closed her eyes and could not say whether she still saw it or only remembered it.

When she opened her eyes, Captain Vane was crossing the quay toward them.

* * *

He came the way he always came, unhurried, gloved, his greatcoat brushed to a soft military perfection that seemed almost insolent amid the mud and the refugees and the piled stretchers. Two of his men trailed him at a respectful distance, which was how Vane liked his power — visible but deniable, like perfume. The crowd parted for him without being asked to; there was something in the cut of a man like that which taught strangers to move.

Edmund felt Hollis stiffen beside her. She did not. She found, examining herself with the detached curiosity of a nurse reading another patient's chart, that fear had been burned out of her somewhere between midnight and dawn, and what remained was only a kind of clean exhaustion, and under it, unexpectedly, anger — cold, clear, sustainable anger, the kind that keeps a dressing station running through a bombardment.

"Sister Sterling." Vane stopped three paces away and removed one glove finger by finger. "You are looking remarkably composed for a woman who has just committed a serious offence against the war effort."

"Captain Vane." She did not salute; she was a civilian matron's assistant and owed him none, and they both knew it, and the knowledge sat between them like a coin on a table. "You've been up early."

"The ship sailed four minutes ago." He turned his head and watched the last smear of smoke dissolving on the horizon. His profile was very fine; it had always been very fine; that had never once deceived her. "I gave orders that no unaccompanied minor was to board without documentation. Yet a small boy went up the gangway in the arms of a deckhand, carrying a leather satchel, in the company of no adult whose name appears on any manifest I have seen. Curious, wouldn't you say?"

"Children are always going where they shouldn't," Edmund said. "It's the nature of children. You were one yourself once, presumably."

One of Vane's men shifted his weight. Vane himself smiled — the thin courteous smile she had learned to read the way sailors read weather.

"The satchel, Sister. My searchers took a coat from a stowaway child last night near Shed Fourteen. In the lining of that coat were letters. Sealed letters. Correspondence of a private and sensitive nature, concerning persons of considerable standing." He said it lightly, conversationally, a man remarking on the tide. "Perhaps you know something of those letters."

"I know that you took a coat," Edmund said, "from a child who ran from your men in the dark. I know that a frightened boy carries many things. Bread. A knife, if he's clever. I had a brother once who carried frogs in his pockets until my mother put a stop to it."

"This is not a jesting matter."

"No," Edmund agreed. "It isn't. You've made certain of that, these past weeks. So let us not jest, Captain. Let us speak plainly, since you've come all this way through the fish-guts and the mud to find me. You want the boy. You wanted him before you knew what he carried, because you knew *who* he was, and what his mother was, and whose bed she died in or beside or underneath — whichever version your masters prefer. The letters only made it urgent. Am I wrong?"

Vane's face did not change. That was the terrible thing about him, Edmund thought — that his face never changed, that composure like his must have cost years of practice, must have been built like a sea wall against exactly the kind of wave she was sending at him now.

"You are speaking treason," he said quietly.

"I am speaking *arithmetic*. You taught me the value of that, aboard this dock, with your berths and your lists and your chits. Three lives, one place. Well. Here is more arithmetic." Her heart was slamming against her ribs now, but her voice came out level, and she marvelled at herself even as she spoke. "The letters you seized are copies. Or they are not copies — I won't tell you which, and you can't make me, and every hour you spend trying is an hour you don't spend finding the ones that matter. Somewhere beyond your reach there exists correspondence naming the Ashcombe Shipping Company, its contracts, its premiums on hulls that sank suspiciously conveniently, its dependents abandoned to French ditches. And somewhere in England there are people who already know it, because I made sure of that before I ever set foot on this quay."

It was perhaps two-thirds bluff. Greer had written: *the family has friends everywhere, trust no officer* — but also, in the same cramped hand, *hold the line; you are not alone in this*, and Edmund had chosen, in the black hours, to believe that the second sentence was not comfort but fact. Belief was all she had left in her kit bag. She might as well spend it.

Something moved behind Vane's eyes. Not doubt — she would not be granted doubt so cheaply — but recalculation. He was a man who had never once in his life been interrupted mid-sentence by reality, and he was being interrupted now, and she watched him decide how to answer it.

"Sister Sterling," he said, "you are a woman of no family, no influence, and no position. You hold no commission. When this war ends — and it is ending, whatever the papers say; even you must smell it on the wind — there will be an accounting of irregular persons. Nurses who kept improper records. Orderlies who forged passes. Soldiers listed as deserters." His gaze slid, deliberate and contemptuous, to Hollis. "Men who vanished from their units and turned up in the baggage trains of field hospitals, telling stories. The peace will sweep all of that into order, and order will require examples. I would take it kindly if you would think about which side of the examples you wish to stand on."

"There she is," Edmund said softly.

"What?"

"The offer. Third time you've made it, Captain. Passage. Protection. Now examples. You always dress it in courtesy first and threats second — it's your uniform, in a way. But do you notice what's different this time?" She stepped closer, close enough to see the fine dry skin at the corners of his eyes, the small human failures that grooming could not conquer. "This time you're making the offer *after* the ship has gone. After the boy is out of your reach and the satchel may be anywhere from Calais to Canada. If you had the letters that mattered, you wouldn't need me. And if you needed me, truly, you wouldn't threaten me — you'd flatter me, the way you did in the dressing station with your polished boots and your questions about Matron Greer's correspondence. Threats are what a man reaches for when his hands have started to shake."

For one moment — she would remember this moment for the rest of her life, would turn it over in the dark like a stone found on a path — for one moment Captain Vane's composure cracked. Not much. A hairline. The jaw tightened a half-degree past elegance; the gloved hand closed, very slightly, on itself. He looked, for the space of a breath, like a man standing in a room that had begun, very quietly, to tilt.

Then his adjutant was at his elbow, murmuring, and the moment was paved over with routine.

* * *

The military police came up the quay at the double.

They came six strong, in the flat blue-grey of the MP, led by a red-faced sergeant with a warrant case chained to his wrist, and they came *past* Edmund and Hollis without so much as slowing — past them, around them, folding themselves with drilled economy around Captain Vane and his two men like a net closing on fish.

Edmund did not understand it at first. She heard the sergeant's voice — clipped London vowels, the accent of a man who had learned his authority in rooms harder than any officers' mess — saying words like *Whitehall* and *requisition of contracts* and *to accompany us, sir, at once, sir*, and she understood the words individually but not together, not aimed in that direction. Power, she had learned in three years of war, flowed in one direction only: downhill, onto the powerless. Watching it flow upward was like watching water run the wrong way. It made the world feel briefly unsprung.

"Captain Vane?" The sergeant consulted a paper. "Reginald Arthur Vane, Royal Army Service Corps? You are to accompany us for questioning in connection with an inquiry into defence contracts held by Ashcombe Shipping Limited. Sir."

Vane did not move. That was the extraordinary thing — he stood absolutely still on the wet stones, gloveless now, one hand bare at his side, and for a long moment he simply looked at the sergeant the way a man looks at weather that has changed without warning. His two escorts had already been separated from him, already been drawn aside, already become irrelevant. The machinery that had carried him all his life had shifted gear, and he had not been told, and he was — Edmund saw it plainly now, and it did not satisfy her the way she had imagined anything would — he was *afraid*.

"Sergeant," Vane said, and his voice was still perfectly level, God help her, still perfectly level, "there has been a mistake. I will require—"

"You'll require nothing, sir, except to come along quiet." The sergeant folded his paper away. "Begging pardon, sir."

And then — and this was the part she had not prepared for, the part that ambushed her between the ribs — Vane turned and looked at *her*.

Not at Hollis. Not at his captors. At her, across the three paces of wet stone, with the gulls screaming and the crowd pressing in to see an officer taken like a common thief, and his face was stripped of everything it had been built from, all those years of polish and courtesy and cold arithmetic, and what lay underneath was young. Underneath, he was only a man discovering that the ledger had been kept on him all along, by people he had never thought to count.

"Sister," he said. Just that. *Sister.* As if she might yet be appealed to, as if she were the tribunal now, as if mercy were a thing dispensed from dressing stations by women in bloodstained aprons and he had finally, far too late, identified the actual seat of judgment in this war.

Edmund looked back at him and said nothing. There was nothing she needed to say. She had sent her whole testimony across the Channel in a child's satchel, bound for a country where names still meant something if you fought hard enough for them, and it was already doing its work — here it was, working, in front of her, wearing handcuffs.

They walked him past her. Close enough that his sleeve nearly brushed hers. He smelled of bay rum and wet wool, and his stride held its length all the way to the gate, and she gave him that much — she noted the courage of the stride, because she was a keeper of records and recorded even her enemies fairly — and then he was through the gate, and the lorry door slammed, and the quay exhaled.

* * *

The crowd, disappointed of a hanging, dispersed back into its business: tarpaulins, tea-urns, lists pinned to boards, the eternal shuffling arithmetic of displaced people. The light was lifting. Somewhere inland, muffled by warehouses, a church bell began to ring eleven o'clock, and Edmund counted the strokes without meaning to, the way she counted pulses.

Hollis touched her sleeve again.

"That was him," he said. "In your notebook. Wasn't it? One of the names?"

"No," Edmund said. "His name was never struck off anything. He'll be in every roll call the army ever takes, right to the end of his life. That's the trouble with men like him — they're *recorded*." She looked out at the empty channel, at the seam of paler grey where sea met sky and the ship had long since vanished. "But the inquiry will need witnesses. Contracts leave paper trails, and paper trails leave names — clerks, stevedores, harbourmasters, wives. Someone wrote those letters in the first place, Hollis. A woman with a housemaid's hands and enough nerve to seal an envelope. They'll need to find *her*, now, or find what became of her, and give her name back to the record."

Hollis considered this in his slow careful way. "And the boy? When they question Vane, they'll ask about the boy. Someone will come looking."

"Yes," Edmund said. "Someone will."

She should have felt triumphant. She searched for it in herself, honestly, the way she palpated a wound — and found instead only the ache of the empty berth, and the memory of a palm against porthole glass, and beneath all of it, faint but persistent as a pulse under a bandage, a single stubborn strand of hope: because if the inquiry was real, if Whitehall had moved, if Greer's friends in London had teeth — then the letters had arrived. The satchel had been opened. Which meant someone in England had read every page, including the pages that were not evidence at all, including the last entry, in a tired woman's hand, describing a boy of five with grey eyes and a habit of holding on to sleeves.

Which meant that somewhere across that grey water, a stranger now knew her son existed.

"Come on," Edmund said, and picked up her kit bag. "We've a war to finish and a name of yours to make mean something. And then—" she looked once more at the channel, and let the look go where the ship had gone— "then I intend to write some letters of my own."
