## 30. Winter of Waiting

The war did not end when the guns moved east; it only changed its address. Edmund followed it through the spring of 1918 — a clearing station at Étaples that smelled of carbolic and tar paper, then a casualty hospital at Rouen with wards built of salvaged theatre scenery, painted flats of Italian gardens standing in for walls, so that men drifted off to sleep under papier-mâché cypresses and woke to the sound of the amputation trolley. By June she was at Le Tréport, where the sea came up grey and patient against the seawall, and the hospital ships stood out at anchor like patient animals waiting to be fed.

She learned the new arithmetic quickly. A nurse's year was measured not in months but in things: number of chests drained, number of boys who kept both legs, number who kept their faces. She kept no second ledger now. The one she had was gone — given away, deliberately, gloriously, on a dock at Boulogne with a child's arms closing around it — and though she had made copies of certain pages in her own careful hand before the end, the original had sailed for England inside a satchel that also carried letters naming the dead.

What she wrote now were letters. One every month, on the first Sunday, after chapel if there was chapel and regardless if there was not. The same address each time:

*Mr. Cyrus Sterling, c/o The Cottage, Harrietsham, Kent, England.*

She had chosen the surname herself, sitting in the customs office at Boulogne with her hands still shaking from Vane's lamplight, because a boy travelling alone needed a name that explained him, and *Sterling* sounded like someone's grandson. It had seemed a small forgery beside all the others. Now it was the truest thing she owned.

*Dear Cyrus,* the January letter began. *I hope by the time this reaches you the winter has been kind to Harrietsham and that you are warm. I am well. The sea here is the colour of gun metal but there are gulls, and gulls are the same in every country, which I find comforting.*

She told him about the ward, carefully filtered. About the boy in Bed 9 who had lost his sight at Passchendaele and could identify every nurse by her footsteps, and had identified hers as *the tall one who walks like she is late*, which had stung precisely because it was true. About Sister Ardent, who could make a thermometer appear from nowhere like a stage magician. About the cat that lived in the laundry boiler room and answered to nothing.

She did not tell him about the gas cases, or the sounds they made at night, or the way she had begun to dream in the smell of gangrene. He was seven years old, or perhaps eight; she had never learned his birthday, and had privately appointed it as the fourth of May, the day he had first eaten bread out of her hand without flinching.

*Your friend Private Hollis is in my thoughts often,* she wrote. *I have had no word of him since we parted, but I choose to believe the sea air did him good.* This was not entirely a lie. It was a belief, maintained the way one maintains a fire in wet weather, with constant small attention and a great deal of smoke.

*Write to me when you can,* she finished. *Even three lines. Even your name only. I will know what it means.*

She addressed it, stamped it, and set it on the pile with its eleven predecessors.

* * *

No answer came.

This was, she told herself, entirely reasonable. He was a child. Children did not write letters; the Quaker family who had taken him in — she had invented them too, in her imagination, gave them a kind aunt named Mrs. Pym and an orchard and a dog — would write for him when it suited them, when the war made postal service something better than a lottery, when there was news worth sending. There was a war on. Half the letters in England were going into the same silence.

But reason is a poor lodger in the heart's house. It pays its rent and keeps to its room, and all night the heart walks past its door and hears it pacing.

By March she had constructed explanations the way other women knitted socks — compulsively, endlessly, one after another. The letters were being intercepted. The Ashcombes, though Vane sat in custody awaiting whatever justice the War Office served cold, had friends everywhere; Matron Greer's last note from Boulogne had said so, *trust no officer*, and perhaps the warning should have extended further, to postmen and parish councils and the quiet men in clubs who moved names from one column to another. Perhaps the cottage in Harrietsham had been watched. Perhaps the family had been warned away and moved, taking the boy and the ledger and her whole testimony into some safer dark.

Or — and this explanation arrived always last, and always largest — he had simply forgotten her. He had been five. Five-year-olds forgot faces the way tide forgets footprints; she had told him once, on the dock, to remember her face instead of forgetting it, and perhaps the instruction had been beyond his strength. Perhaps somewhere in Kent a small fair boy was growing up English and unburdened, remembering nothing of Ypres or the smell of ether or the woman who walked like she was late, and this was not a tragedy at all. This was the mission accomplished. She had bought his forgetting with everything she had. To mourn it would be like a soldier complaining the armistice was too quiet.

She believed this version for almost four days in April.

* * *

The hospital at Le Tréport had been a casino before the war, and it retained the habits of pleasure-seeking. The long ward windows still faced the sea as if expecting dancers; the parquet floors squeaked under stretcher wheels in a way that suggested waltzes. Edmund found it unbearable in a low, chronic way, like a stone in a boot, and loved it anyway.

Her days acquired the shape of liturgy. Six o'clock: temperatures, the glass thermometers shivering in armpits like small anxious birds. Seven: dressings, the slow unwinding of puttees and bandages, the revealing of what high explosive did to the architecture of a human leg. Ten: the walking wounded went down to the convalescent marquee, and the serious cases came up from the operating theatre, and the beds rearranged themselves around her like weather.

She measured time in saved men now, as she had promised herself she would. Not in victories — there were none of those left that anyone could describe without laughing — but in specific faces discharged to Blighty with pulses that would hold. Sergeant Doyle, whose lung she had watched collapse and reinflate like a bellows, gone home to Wigan in May. The Canadian with the jaw rebuilt from his own hip bone, who wrote her a thank-you note that consisted entirely of the words *I CAN EAT PIE*, underlined twice, and which she kept folded in her paybook until it disintegrated from handling.

And she measured it in losses, because a nurse who counted only saves was lying to herself, and lying to yourself, she had learned at Boulogne, was how Captain Vanes were made. Bed 14, gone in forty minutes on a Tuesday. The boy from Beds 22 and 23 — brothers, wounded together, separated by a screen that everyone pretended was invisible — the younger one lasting two hours longer and asking for his mother in a voice that hadn't broken, and Edmund holding his hand and saying *yes, yes, she's coming* in the great tradition of merciful lies, and hating herself, and doing it anyway, and understanding for the first time with her whole body why a maid might seal letters naming powerful men and give them to her son to carry.

That thought arrived unbidden and left slowly. She had stopped trying to control it. Grief, she discovered, does not visit; it tenanted.

* * *

In June the Germans fired their last great gun at the front and the wounded came in such numbers that the casino's ballroom became a ward, its mirrored ceiling intact above sixty iron beds, so that every man lay doubled — himself below, his reflection above — and the dying looked up into a sky full of other dying men. Edmund worked eighteen-hour shifts among the mirrors. She caught herself searching the upper world for faces she knew. Once she saw, unmistakably, a small fair boy reflected in the ceiling glass, and turned so fast she dropped a kidney dish, and found behind her only an orderly carrying water, five foot ten, moustached, apologetic.

After that she wrote two letters in one month. The second said only:

*I do not need you to remember me. I need only to know that you are somewhere, eating breakfast, being ordinary. Ordinary is the whole victory. Write your name and I will count myself paid in full.*

She read it over before sealing and thought: this is what I have become, a woman begging a seven-year-old for evidence of life. Then she thought: yes. And sealed it.

* * *

July brought the matron, Miss Haversham — not Greer; Greer had been posted to Abbeville and then, in the reshuffle of early summer, demobilised home to London with a lung the gas had touched — a tall, silent woman who ran the ward like a ship and noticed everything and commented on almost none of it. Almost. On the first Sunday of July, finding Edmund at the writing table in the corridor with her thirteenth unanswered letter open before her, Miss Haversham paused.

"You write to the same address every month," she said. It was not a question. Matrons did not ask questions; they conducted audits aloud.

"Yes, Matron."

"No reply."

"Not yet."

Miss Haversham regarded the letter, the neat stack of its predecessors, the envelope addressed in Edmund's upright drafting hand — the hand of a woman who had spent two years making erased names legible and could not stop. Something moved behind the matron's face, some memory of her own silences, filed and unretrieved.

"My sister's boy is missing since Messines," she said at last. "A year in May. I write to his battalion. They write back that enquiries continue." She straightened the stack of envelopes a half-inch, an unnecessary kindness. "Enquiries continue. That is the army's whole soul in one phrase." And she moved on down the corridor, squeaking faintly on the parquet like everything else in that building.

Edmund sat a long while afterward, not writing. *Missing* was a word with a trapdoor in it. She had spent a war pulling men out of that category and into ledgers, giving them back the dignity of a recorded fate. But there was no ledger for Cyrus. There was only a cottage in Kent and an orchard she had invented and a silence that grew one month deeper every thirty days, and she began to understand that the uncertainty was worse than any entry, even the worst entry, even *died of wounds*. A name in a ledger could be grieved. Silence could only be survived.

* * *

August: the papers began using a new word, *breakthrough*, cautiously, the way one mentions rain during a drought. The wounded coming down the line were German as often as British now, and strange to see — young, lice-ridden, apologising. One of them, a boy of nineteen with a shattered elbow, tried to thank her in fragments of English learned from sailors' songs, and ended by singing four bars of *Tipperary* to the entire ward, badly, and the British patients groaned and laughed and someone threw a pillow, and Edmund stood in the middle of it with her hands full of saline and understood that something enormous had come loose in the world.

The war was ending. Everyone felt it in the teeth, the way one feels a storm.

Which meant the next thing was coming too. Demobilisation. Return. The channel boat to England, the train, the county of Kent, and a lane outside Harrietsham that she had drawn in her mind so many times it had acquired texture — the hedge height, the state of the gate, whether the orchard (invented, hers, absurd) would be in blossom.

She could go and look. After the Armistice, when the trains ran, she could stand at a garden gate and ask a neighbour, *does a boy live here, fair-haired, about seven, called Sterling?* and receive either a door opening or a face closing, and either answer would be an entry in a ledger at last.

She was frightened of both answers equally. That was the thing she could not have explained to Miss Haversham or anyone: that hope and dread had become indistinguishable, twin currents in the same tide, and she no longer knew which one was pushing her toward England and which was holding her back.

* * *

September's letter she began six times.

*Dear Cyrus. Dear Cyrus. Dear Cyrus —*

On the seventh attempt she abandoned the fiction of correspondence and simply talked to him, the way she had talked to him once across a dressing-station cot, winning trust with small things: a heel of bread, a paper boat, the patient silence that says *I will not demand what you cannot give*.

*I met a boy today who reminded me of you,* she wrote. *He wouldn't speak either. They never do, at first. Speaking is a muscle and grief is a weight set on top of it, and some weights take years to lift. If you have not written, I understand. I want you to know that I understand completely, and that no answer is itself an answer, and I have received it thirteen times with respect.*

*But here is what I never said on the dock, because there was no time and because saying it would have been a cruelty dressed as a gift:*

*I think of you at odd hours. I set a place for you in my mind the way some women set places at table. I taught you nothing except how to eat bread slowly and trust one adult, and I do not even know if that survived the crossing. You owe me nothing. You never owed me anything. You arrived at the door of the war with a satchel and instructions to be forgotten, and instead you became the fixed point I navigated by, and I would do every hour of it again, the dock included, the lamplit office included, all of it, to arrive at the same result: you, somewhere, eating breakfast.*

*One day, if the war ever truly finishes, I will come to Harrietsham and knock on a door. If it is the wrong door, tell me so kindly. If it is the right one —*

She stopped there. Her pen had been hovering over the page so long that the ink dried in the nib and she had to re-wet it, and the pause let honesty in through a gap she had not guarded.

*If it is the right one,* she continued, more quietly, as though ink could carry tone, *then I am afraid you will look at me the way one looks at a photograph of a place one visited in infancy: politely, without recognition, wondering why the stranger is crying.*

*I am crying now. There. You have made a grown woman cry, and you have done it by not existing loudly enough, which is the least criminal method available.*

*Yours, in the old style,*

*E.S.*

*P.S. — Hollis, if you are anywhere in England and reading over his shoulder: your name is Thomas Edwin Hollis, 2nd Battalion, and I never struck it out. Ask anyone. Ask the records office. Ask me.*

She sealed it on the first Sunday of October and put it in the mail sack with hands that smelt permanently now of iodine, and went back onto the ward, where the mirrors doubled every bed and the sea outside had turned to pewter, and took the pulse of a boy from Cardiff who was going to live, and entered it silently in the only ledger she had left, which was herself.

* * *

November came in cold and clear. On the eleventh, at eleven, the guns stopped — all of them, along fifty miles, a silence so total that men in the ward sat up in bed listening to it as though to music — and France went mad gently, church bells and foghorns and strangers kissing strangers, and Edmund walked out to the seawall at Le Tréport with the crowd flowing around her like water around a stone.

She did not celebrate. She listened. Somewhere under the bells, she thought, was the frequency of every name she had ever written down, the living and the struck-off and the restored, Tomas detained at the gate and Greer coughing in London and Hollis somewhere on the far side of a channel with his true name in his pocket if he could only remember where he'd put it — and Cyrus, Cyrus most of all, seven or eight years old now, alive or not alive, remembering or not remembering, in a cottage with an orchard that existed only because she had needed it to.

The bells rang. The sea did what the sea does.

She stood at the rail of the seawall — the same pale stone, she realised with a small shock, the same wall at Boulogne's cousin, the geography of departures repeated along the whole coast — and made herself a promise with the full formality of a ledger entry: *When the boats run again, I go. Whatever the answer is, I will have it, and enter it, and close the line.*

It took four more months for demobilisation to release her — the machinery of gratitude moving, as always, at the speed of enquiries continuing — and it was a raw morning in March 1919 when she finally stood on the deck of the channel steamer with a kit bag containing one change of uniform, a discharge paper, and fourteen unsent drafts she had not had the heart to burn.

England rose out of the haze white and green and impossibly quiet. At the station she bought a paper, and a cup of tea she could not drink, and studied a timetable with the concentration of a woman deciphering codes, because there was one more journey to make, and she had rehearsed it so many times that her heartbeat had worn a groove.

Harrietsham. Change at — she traced the line with her finger. Change at Maidstone. The train left in twenty minutes.

She picked up her bag. Her hands, which had held arteries closed and forged travel chits and a living boy lifted into a sailor's arms, were shaking like a recruit's.
