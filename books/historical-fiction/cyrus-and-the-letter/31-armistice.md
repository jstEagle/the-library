## 31. Armistice

The bells woke her.

Edmund Sterling had been sleeping the sleep of the overworked — a shallow, salt-thin sleep that broke at every moan from the ward and never quite returned — when the sound came through the grey morning of the eleventh of November like water through a cracked wall. Not the church bell of her childhood in Surrey, which had struck the hours with dull English reliability. This was every bell in Rouen at once, all of them loosed from their towers, tumbling over the rooftops and into the hospital courtyard where they pooled and rang and would not stop.

She sat up on her cot in the nurses' annex and did not move for a long moment. Her first thought, absurd and unforgivable, was: *something has gone wrong*. Four years had taught her that loud noises meant catastrophe, that crowds gathering meant bad news being read aloud, that sudden joy in wartime was almost always the prelude to some new accounting. Her second thought arrived more slowly, walking up behind the first like a nurse behind a doctor on rounds, and it put its hand gently on her shoulder:

*It is finished.*

She dressed in the dark because there was no light worth lighting a lamp for, and she went out into the corridor, and the whole building was already stirring. Orderlies who had not smiled since spring were grinning at each other across trolleys of dressings. A physiotherapy sergeant was weeping openly by the stairwell, wiping his face with his sleeve as though annoyed at it. Somewhere below, someone was attempting to play *La Marseillaise* on a mouth organ and getting most of the notes wrong, which somehow made it better.

Edmund stood in the corridor with her hand flat against the cold plaster wall and let it all wash over her, and discovered that she could not feel it. Not properly. It reached her the way sunlight reaches the bottom of a well — visible, undeniable, but landing somewhere short of the place where feeling is kept.

* * *

The ward was quieter than the streets. That was the first cruelty of Armistice Day, and she learned it within minutes of beginning her rounds: the men who had been broken making this day possible heard the bells too, and for many of them the bells changed nothing. The guns had stopped; their bodies had not. Private Aubrey in bed twelve, both legs gone above the knee since August, asked her in a careful voice whether it was true or whether the French got excited easily. She told him it was true. He nodded, looked at the ceiling, and said, "Then what am I supposed to do now?" and she had no answer that would survive daylight, so she gave him her hand instead and he held it briefly, hard, and let go.

At ten o'clock — eleven by the clocks in England, but France kept her own hour even at the end of the world — the guns along the front ceased. Everyone said you could hear the silence travel, like weather moving west to east, though of course what they heard was only their own ears adjusting after four years. In the ward, the moment passed without ceremony. A man with bandaged eyes turned his face toward the window. An officer with a shattered shoulder said, "Thank God," in a voice that suggested he was thanking no one in particular and trusting no one to have heard.

Edmund went on with her rounds. That was her armistice: morphine at the prescribed hours, dressings changed, water jugs filled, the small liturgies of tending that had carried her from Ypres to the coast and back again and would carry her still, because nobody had yet told her body how to stop.

But between tasks — in the gaps, the little dead airs of time while solutions dripped or a patient slept — she took out the ledger.

She had carried it through everything. The oilcloth wrapping was worn soft as chamois now, darkened at the corners where her hands had held it in every kind of weather. The notebook inside had begun as an ordinary penny exercise book and was now something else entirely: sixty-odd pages of names in her careful, upright hand, each one a man struck from the official rolls, erased as a deserter or a malingerer or a clerical error, unrecorded and therefore — in the cold arithmetic of the War Office — never truly lost. Except that they had been lost. That was the whole wound of it. She had spent four years writing down the forgotten, and now, on the day of peace, she found herself doing the thing she had promised herself she would not do.

She was reading the casualty lists.

They came to the hospital in bundles, these lists — printed sheets, smudged, columns of names in type so small it seemed designed to discourage reading — and the corridors filled all that week with men and sisters bent over them, running fingers down the columns, hunting. Most hunted for the living: a brother, a friend from the old battalion, anyone who could be written to. Edmund hunted for the dead. Or rather, for confirmation of them, which was a different thing and worse.

Because her ledger held names that would never appear anywhere else. If Thomas Renwick, struck off as AWOL outside Passchendaele though she had personally held his hand while he died of his wounds, appeared on no list at all — then he had never existed, and her entry was the only evidence in the world that a man named Renwick had lived and laughed and been afraid of thunder even before the war gave him cause. And if he *did* appear — if his name surfaced among the killed, restored by some clerk's tardy diligence — then she felt a grief so peculiar it had no name: relief and loss arriving in the same instant, indistinguishable, like two voices singing the same wrong note.

She found three of her names in the November lists. She copied their details into the ledger beside her own entries — date, unit, circumstances as officially recorded — and then, because the ledger deserved it, she added what the lists could never hold. *Renwick, T., Pte., 2nd/7th. Killed 4 October 1917, Zonnebeke. He was twenty-six. He had a habit of whistling through his teeth when nervous. His mother lives in Leeds; he wrote to her every Sunday and missed only twice.*

The third addition took longer. Private Hollis.

Not dead. Hollis's name was not on any list, dead or wounded or missing, and that absence had been eating at her since Boulogne. He should have boarded. He should have been standing beside her on the dock when the ship pulled away with Cyrus in it, small and straight-backed in the crowd, one hand raised. Instead Hollis had been left behind — transferred inland again in the administrative shuffle that followed Vane's arrest, shunted from one convalescent depot to another like a parcel with a damaged address, because a man whose records say *deserted* cannot be discharged home no matter how thoroughly the desertion is disproved in the heart. His shell shock was documented nowhere. His service existed nowhere. Officially, Private Samuel Hollis had never gone to France at all.

She had written to the War Office four times. She had received two replies, both courteous, neither useful, both signed by different subalterns with the same regretful phrasing. She had written to Matron Greer, who replied in her cramped iron hand: *Keep at them. I have friends here who owe me. The family is finished — the inquiry sits in January — but paperwork dies slower than men. Do not despair. G.*

So on Armistice Day, while strangers kissed in the street and church bells ruined themselves with joy, Edmund Sterling sat in the linen room with the door shut and wrote her fifth letter to the War Office, and this one she began differently. *Gentlemen. On the fourth of June 1918 your office struck the following name from the rolls of the 9th Battalion...* And then she laid it out, fact by fact, date by date, the way she had laid out names for four years — quietly, precisely, without flourish — and ended with a sentence she wrote slowly, pressing hard enough that the nib nearly went through the paper: *I was present at every event described. I will testify to them under oath if required. A man cannot be made to have never existed simply because it is convenient.*

She sealed it, addressed it, and sat holding it for a moment before setting it with the outgoing post. Then she opened the ledger one more time and turned past all the names to the last page — the page she had never written on, kept blank for reasons she had not examined until now — and looked at it. Blank. Waiting.

*One more,* she thought. *Hollis gets his name back. And then —*

She did not finish the thought. Finishing it meant thinking about Kent, and thinking about Kent meant thinking about a boy of five going alone aboard a ship with a satchel full of letters and a notebook full of names and instructions given through tears: *remember my face. Remember it instead of forgetting.* Three years ago. He would be eight now. Eight, and speaking English by now surely, growing like a weed, forgetting her face a little more each winter the way children must forget things in order to grow.

She closed the ledger and wrapped it in its oilcloth and put it away, and went back to the ward, because there was still a war's worth of tending to do and the tending did not stop because the shooting had.

* * *

That evening the town went mad, gently.

Rouen at dusk was candlelight in every window and strangers embracing in the cobbled streets, soldiers and civilians and the widows in black who stood at the edges of the rejoicing like figures in someone else's photograph. Edmund walked out after supper with Sister Marguerite, a Frenchwoman of fifty who had worked the same wards since 1914 and who had taken Edmund's hand that afternoon and pressed it and said only, *"C'est fini, ma fille,"* in a voice that broke on the last syllable, and that had done more than all the bells.

They walked to the square by the clock. There was music — several musics, competing, none of them in agreement — and dancing, and a great bonfire being fed with anything burnable, and above it all the cathedral tower catching the firelight so that the stone looked warm, which stone never is.

"Four years," Marguerite said, watching the dancers. "And it stops like a tap turned off. One does not know where to put oneself."

"No," Edmund said. "One doesn't."

A young soldier — nineteen perhaps, unwounded, whole, laughing — caught Marguerite's hands and swung her into a dance before she could protest, and she protested, and then she danced, badly and wonderfully, her cap askew, shrieking with laughter. Edmund watched from the edge of the crowd and found herself smiling, really smiling, for the first time in longer than she could measure. Around her the city kissed and sang and drank and wept, and grief and relief moved through the crowd so mingled that no one could have separated them with all the chemistry in the world. A woman embraced a stranger in uniform and then collapsed against him sobbing; he held her, having no idea whose son he resembled. Two old men toasted each other with rough red wine and then simply stood together, silent, glasses raised, unable to complete the gesture. Joy and mourning wore the same clothes tonight.

Edmund bought a twist of sugared almonds from a stall because a child near her wanted some and had no money, and gave them to him, and the boy grinned at her with sugar-blackened teeth and ran off, and she stood very still in the firelight with her empty hand.

*A boy of five*, she thought. *He would run like that.*

She had sent him away with her whole reason for everything — the ledger, the evidence, the testimony that might someday hang the men who killed his mother — and kept for herself only the memory of his weight in her arms and the smell of his hair and the terrible gravity of his silence. She had told herself it was arithmetic: three lives, one berth, and his was the life that could be saved most completely. She had believed it. Standing in the square at Rouen on the night the world ended its worst madness, she believed it still — and understood at the same time that belief had nothing to do with it. You did not send the child away because the sums favoured it. You sent him away because you loved him, and love is not an argument, and it never balances.

She walked back to the hospital late, alone, past windows going dark one by one. In her pocket, her fingers found the oilcloth corner of the ledger where it rode in her coat now always, and rested there, and were comforted.

* * *

Demobilization came for her in stages, the way the tide leaves a beach — not all at once but in retreats, each withdrawal revealing another stretch of what had been submerged.

First the wards emptied, slowly, of those who could be moved home. Then the hospital itself contracted, wards closed and shuttered, sisters posted onward or homeward according to rules no one could fully explain. Marguerite left in December for her family in Normandy, and wept at the gate, and made Edmund promise to write. In January came the news that the Ashcombe inquiry had opened at Westminster and that Captain Vane — formerly Captain, currently something rather lower — had been committed for court-martial on charges arising from the shipping contracts, and that certain letters had entered evidence, copies thereof, lodged by a Mrs. Greer, matron, retired, of whom the newspapers spoke with a respect that made Edmund laugh out loud alone in the mess.

In February, a letter came from the War Office. It was not one of hers; it was from Greer, forwarding an official enclosure, and Edmund knew what it was before she broke the seal — knew by the weight, by the shape of it, by three years of learning how bad news travels in envelopes.

It was good news. That was the shock of it.

*The Army Council have directed that the records of No. 7914, Pte. S. Hollis, 9th Bn., be restored... discharge by reason of disablement attributable to service... authority for issue of medals...* Paperwork, rising from the dead like Lazarus, shaving and putting on clean clothes. Hollis's name, restored. Somewhere in a depot in Étaples or Shorncliffe, a hollow-eyed man was about to be informed that he existed.

Edmund read it twice, then set it down and put her face in her hands and stayed that way for some minutes, and no one disturbed her, because the mess was empty and because women had learned in this war when to look away.

She wrote to Hollis that evening, care of the depot, addressing him properly — *Dear Private Hollis* — and telling him what had been done, and adding: *Your name was never lost. I kept it. I am keeping it still, and I will keep it until you tell me yourself that you are Samuel Hollis and always were, and then I will cross out my entry and write beside it: found.*

* * *

By March she had her own papers. Demobilization, they called it, as though she were an army of one being decommissioned, ordnance returned, accounts squared. They gave her a railway warrant, a testimonial of service with her name spelled correctly for once, and a handshake from the commanding officer, and that was the thanks of a grateful nation, delivered in under a minute on a platform at Rouen while a porter wheeled past with a trolley of unclaimed kit bags, each one tagged with a name, some of them belonging to men who would never come to claim them.

She sailed from Le Havre on a grey morning with one kit bag and the ledger in her coat, and stood at the rail as France fell away behind the haze, and thought of another ship, another crossing, three years gone — a small figure lifted into a sailor's arms, a satchel clutched to his chest, a face at the rail that grew smaller and smaller and then was lost among other faces, and the sea taking all of it south toward England while she stood on the dock with Hollis beside her and the law closing on Vane behind her, and her arms empty in a way they have never entirely stopped being.

England, when she reached it, was a country she did not recognize wearing the clothes of one she did. The station at Waterloo was crowded with returning men, and there were bands, and flags, and women with flowers, and also — everywhere, threaded through the celebration like wire through cloth — a strange hush, a national embarrassment, as though the whole island had woken from a long illness and could not remember how to speak to itself. Nobody knew what to say. Welcome home was easy enough for the crowds to shout at strangers in uniform; but the country seemed to have mislaid the larger sentence, the one that came after. *Thank you* was somehow too small and *forgive us* too large, and so it said neither, and hung bunting instead.

Edmund stood in the crowd at Waterloo with her kit bag at her feet and watched mothers find sons and wives find husbands and one elderly gentleman in a black armband move through the throng handing out printed cards, and she took one from him out of kindness. It read: *For the Fallen. They shall grow not old...* She folded it carefully and put it in the pocket where the ledger rode, next to the oilcloth.

No one met her. No one, she reflected, knew where she was going except Matron Greer, who had written in her last letter a single line of instruction, unpunctuated as all her best sentences were: *Come and see me when you land I have an address for you.*

Greer received her the following day in a small house in Camberwell that smelled of tea and carbolic soap, and greeted her at the door with a look that was half appraisal, half embrace, and said, "You're thinner. Sit down." Tea was produced. For a quarter of an hour they talked of the inquiry — Vane's trial concluding, sentence pending, the Ashcombes' name in ruins, contracts voided, a maid's death finally spoken aloud in a courtroom by a barrister who had never known her — and then Greer set down her cup, folded her hands, and looked at Edmund with the expression she used to wear before issuing orders no one wanted to hear.

"I've kept something for you," she said. "From the port. From Boulogne, the day after the ship sailed. The Quaker family's people sent word through their meeting — they'd taken the boy in, you understand, as arranged. There was a note."

Edmund's heart performed a manoeuvre she had not permitted it in three years.

"He was well," Greer said. "Fed, sleeping, unhurt. They wrote to say he'd arrived safe and was — " she hesitated, choosing the words with unusual care — "was asking for something. Asking, mind. First words anyone reported from him." She rose, went to the mantelpiece, and took down an envelope that had been kept somewhere flat and safe, its edges softened by handling. "They enclosed it. Said it was found sewn into the lining of his satchel, and that it was plainly not meant for them."

She held it out. On the front, in a careful child's hand — the hand of a five-year-old taught urgently, in secret, in the margins of a war — was written a single word.

*Sterling.*

Edmund took the envelope. Her hands were steady; she noted that remotely, the way she noted a pulse. Inside was a folded piece of paper, and inside that, when she opened it with the deliberation of someone defusing something, she found a single line in the same painstaking hand, and beneath it a drawing — small, burned at one corner, unmistakable.

The line said: *I remembur your face.*

The drawing was of a woman in a nurse's cap, drawn with the fierce economy of a child who has decided what matters and will waste no crayons on anything else.

Edmund sat very still in the little Camberwell parlour while the tea went cold, and Greer, who had commanded dressing stations through bombardments, had the wisdom to say nothing at all. On the back of the drawing, in adult handwriting Edmund did not recognize, someone had added an address in Kent and a single explanatory sentence: *He keeps this under his pillow. We thought you should know where he sleeps.*

She read the address three times. Then she looked up at Matron Greer, and Greer looked back, and something passed between them that needed no rank and no uniform — the old understanding, soldier to soldier, that a mission is not concluded merely because the guns stop.

"How far is it to Kent from here?" Edmund asked.

Greer refilled her cup. "Drink your tea first," she said. "And take the morning train. Some things want arriving in daylight."

Edmund folded the drawing along its original creases and put it in her pocket beside the ledger, where it fit as though the pocket had been waiting for it all along, and outside the little house the bells of Camberwell began to ring for no occasion at all — ringing, perhaps, for everything, for everyone, for all the names in all the ledgers in all the pockets of all the women who had kept count when no one else would — and she sat with her hands around the hot cup and did not taste one drop of it, already gone, already on the platform, already halfway to Kent.
