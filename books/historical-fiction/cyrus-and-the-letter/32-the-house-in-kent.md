## 32. The House in Kent

The war released Edmund Sterling the way a hand releases a bird it has held too long — not with triumph but with a numbness in the fingers, an uncertainty about what she was meant to do next.

Demobilization came in stages, each one smaller and more administrative than the last. There was a final medical review at a requisitioned hotel in Étaples where a doctor half her age looked into her ears and down her throat and pronounced her, with mild surprise, entirely sound. There was the surrender of her uniform — she kept the apron against regulations, folding it into the bottom of her kit bag where the smell of carbolic still lived — and the signing of papers that returned to her, on thin War Office stock, a name that had belonged to someone else for four years. *Edmund Ruth Sterling, V.A.D., discharged.* She read it twice and felt nothing so much as the peculiar grief of being handed back to herself.

There was the crossing — white cliffs rising out of a grey March sea while soldiers around her sang something vulgar and wonderful — and there was London, which had gone on without her, which had grown fat on armistice and thin on memory, its streets full of women in black and shop windows full of things nobody could afford. And then, eleven days after setting foot in England, there was a third-class carriage rattling south through the Weald of Kent with Edmund alone in it, her kit bag on the seat beside her like a companion, watching hop fields and oast houses slide past the fogged window, and in her coat pocket, worn soft as cloth from handling, a bundle of thirty-one unanswered letters.

She had written them all to the same address. *Mr. Cyrus Sterling, c/o Mrs. H. Faircloth, Rose Cottage, Aldington Frith, Kent.* The surname was her own invention, given in the first weeks after Boulogne when a matron at Calais demanded to know the boy's family name and Edmund heard herself say *mine* before any wiser part of her could intervene. It had seemed then like a small thing, a thread thrown across a chasm. For five years she had paid the postage on that thread, month after month, through two changes of hospital and one bad winter in Picardy, writing to a child who had been six when she last saw him, who would be eleven or twelve now, who might by now have forgotten her face entirely — the face she had told him, on a dock in France, to remember.

None of the letters had come back. That was the thing she clung to on the train, turning it over like a coin worn smooth. Returned post would have meant a closed door, a house gone dark, a name struck off another list. But the letters had simply vanished into Kent the way letters do, accepted and unacknowledged, and silence, Edmund had learned in a dressing station near Ypres, was not the same as absence. Silence was only an answer she hadn't learned to read.

The guard called Aldington. She gathered her bag and stepped down onto a platform so small it hardly deserved the word — a wooden edifice, a flower tub gone leggy with neglect, a single lamp already lit against the early dusk. The air smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke and, faintly, of sea. After four years of the western front's particular atmosphere — mud and chloride and the sweet-rotten undertow that no amount of spring could ever quite scour — the clean cold smell of an English countryside in March nearly undid her. She stood a moment longer than necessary on the empty platform, breathing.

The stationmaster was also the porter and also, he explained, the keeper of the only telephone for three miles. He was an old man with a walrus moustache and the unhurried manner of someone whose war had consisted chiefly of keeping trains running past troop movements, and when Edmund asked the way to Rose Cottage he looked at her a moment too long before answering.

"Past the church, left at the forge, and it's the last house before the orchard starts." He hesitated. "You'll be wanting the Faircloths, then?"

"I'm hoping to."

"Ah." He said it the way men said *ah* when they knew something and were deciding how much of it to give away. Then he seemed to think better of discretion, or perhaps simply of the walk ahead of her in the gathering dark. "You'll find the cottage empty, miss. Been empty since — oh, must be 'twenty now. The old lady died. Faircloth, that was." He tugged his cap. "I'm sorry. You've come a long way, by the look of you."

Edmund's hands, she noticed distantly, had gone very still on the strap of her kit bag. "There was a child," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she had spent years learning to make her voice come out level over worse news than this. "A little boy. He would have lived with Mrs. Faircloth."

"Ah," the stationmaster said again, and this time the sound carried a whole paragraph with it. He glanced up the platform as though checking for witnesses, then lowered his voice into the confidential register of small places. "The little gentleman. I remember him well enough — used to come down on a Saturday sometimes to watch the trains, stood right where you're standing, quiet as a church mouse, never said a word. Mrs. Faircloth used to buy him a penny bun at the shop in the village and he'd hold it in both hands like it might get away from him." A faraway smile moved under the moustache and faded. "Nice child. Odd child. They took him in during the war — Quaker connection of the Faircloths', some society that placed the refugee children out. Then the old lady passed, and the cottage went to a nephew in Leeds who didn't want it, and the boy—" He spread his hands. "Gone. Placed out again, I'd suppose. There was a gentleman came from the society. London man. Collected him one morning in a motor car."

"You don't know where?"

"I couldn't say. The Society of Friends, it'd be — they keep their own books, I shouldn't wonder. Very thorough people, Quakers." He studied her face, and whatever he found there softened him further. "You'll want somewhere to sleep tonight regardless, and the Bell is the only house that takes lodgers. You tell them Tom the stationmaster sent you and Mrs. Pargiter won't charge you for the hot water." He paused, then added, kindly and carefully: "It's a hard thing, miss, coming all this way to an empty door. But if the lad watched the trains like he did, week after week — well. A child that watches trains is waiting for somebody. You bear that in mind."

She thanked him. She did not tell him that she had spent five years bearing precisely that in mind, that it was the only cargo she had carried out of the war intact.

* * *

Rose Cottage sat where the road gave out into orchard, exactly as promised: low and stone-built, its roses long ago surrendered to a tangle of dead-looking climbers, its windows blind with dust or board. The gate hung open. The garden had gone to couch grass and the skeletal stalks of last summer's weeds, but someone — the nephew in Leeds, perhaps, or simple habit — had kept the path swept, and the front door, when Edmund tried it, yielded to a lifted latch, because doors in country places had not yet learned to fear anything worse than weather.

Inside it was cold and dry and utterly still. A flagstoned passage, a smell of apples gone over and beeswax gone stale. She set down her kit bag in the passage of a stranger's empty house — a thing no version of herself from four years ago would have countenanced — and walked through the rooms as though wading upstream.

A kitchen with a black leaded range and a kettle on the hob, rusted in place, mid-pour forever. A parlour with a fireplace and, above the mantel, a photograph of a severe couple neither of whom could be Mrs. Faircloth, left behind like furniture nobody wanted. A small back room with a loom or some apparatus of country industry folded under a dust sheet. And up the narrow stair, two bedrooms: one plainly the old woman's, bed stripped, wardrobe open and hollow as a ribcage; and one other.

The other room stopped her on the threshold.

It was small, with a sloped ceiling and a dormer window that looked out over the orchard, and it had been a child's room — was perhaps still, in the way of houses that cannot bring themselves to finish the argument. A cot against the wall. On the sill of the dormer, arranged with a deliberateness that closed her throat, a regiment of objects: three smooth stones, a brass button, the wing case of a stag beetle pinned to a card, and a train — a tin locomotive with its tender, the paint rubbed silver along the top edges where small hands had carried it, over and over, to the window and back.

Edmund crossed the floor and picked up the engine. It weighed almost nothing. The rubbing of the paint told her everything: a boy standing at this window hour after hour, looking down the line of the orchard toward the distant sound of Saturday trains, holding the only train he was allowed. Waiting. *A child that watches trains is waiting for somebody.*

She sat down on the cot, which groaned under her, and let herself, for the length of sixty seconds by the small clock of her own pulse, feel the whole weight of the five years. The letters she had written — thirty-one of them, and what had become of them? Burned with the old woman, most likely, or bundled into the loft with everything else the nephew hadn't wanted, or sent on to wherever the Society sent its orphaned threads. The face she had made him promise to remember. *Remember mine instead,* she had said, kneeling on the dock at Boulogne with the ship's whistle tearing the air, pressing the ledger into his satchel. And he had looked at her with those enormous grave eyes and nodded once, and she had lifted him into a sailor's arms, and the ship had pulled away carrying every reason she had.

She had imagined finding him here. She had imagined it on hospital nights for five years — the cottage door opening, the boy grown tall, saying her name in the voice she'd given him. She had not, until this moment, allowed herself to imagine the alternative: the empty room, the rubbed-silver toy, the silence where the answer should have been.

When the sixty seconds were done, she stood, put the tin engine precisely back among its stones and button and beetle, and went downstairs to see what else an empty house could be made to tell.

* * *

Mrs. Faircloth's parlour gave up its secrets grudgingly but not completely. Behind the mantel photograph — Edmund moved it with a housekeeper's guilt and a detective's resolve — the wallpaper showed a clean rectangle, and in the dust on the mantelpiece itself, a ring where something round had stood for years: not a vase, by the size of it. A tin. Biscuit tin or tobacco tin, the kind of container in which country women kept the documents of their lives. Gone now, taken or thrown out, but it had stood there, and whatever it held had stood with it.

In the kitchen, wedged behind the range where a draught would find it, she found better. An envelope, foxed with damp, addressed in a spidery hand to *Mrs. E. Faircloth, Rose Cottage*, franked with a stamp two years old, and inside — she eased it out with the care due to a relic — a single sheet.

*Dear Missus Faircloth,* it began, *We write further to your enquiry of last quarter regarding the welfare of Master Cyrus, placed in your care 1919 under our Children's Placement Scheme. We are glad to say the boy continues well in his new situation. His foster family are members of our Meeting at — *here the damp had eaten the place-name, leaving a brown archipelago in the middle of the sentence — *and report him to be a quiet and dutiful child, much improved. He keeps his effects about him very careful, which we understand from your letter. You may assure yourself he has not forgot.*

*Yours in service,
J. Bramley, Hon. Sec.*

Edmund read it four times. *He has not forgot.* Written by a clerk who could not have known what the words weighed, who had set them down between enquiries of last quarter and stamps two years old, and gone to his tea. The letter was dated March 1921 — after the old woman's death, then; Mrs. Faircloth had written to the Society on her deathbed or near it, anxious for the boy she had sheltered, and the Society had answered her, and somehow the answer had arrived too late or been laid aside, and ended up behind a stove for the nephew's workmen to miss.

*His new situation.* A Meeting somewhere whose name the damp had stolen. A foster family, Quakers. Friends.

She sat down at the dead woman's kitchen table, in the dead woman's kitchen chair, and laughed once — a short, cracked sound in the empty room that startled her more than the knock at the hospital door ever had. Not found. Not lost, either. Moved, the way children in the care of societies are always being moved, from book to book, list to list — struck off one roll and entered on another, invisible at every step unless someone, somewhere, refused to stop reading.

She thought of her ledger, five years older now and living in the bottom of her kit bag between the apron and a photograph of a dressing station she had never taken. She had written in it last on the boat home: *Cyrus Sterling, age 6, embarked England, 12 August 1917, carrying the record. Purpose: to be found.* It was the only entry in the whole book she had never been able to close.

The light was failing altogether when she rose. She left the cottage as she had found it — door latched, photograph squared on its hook, tin engine at its post in the window of the room where a boy had watched for trains — and stood a moment in the weedy garden while the first star came out over the orchard. The trail was cold. Cold as the trail of every man whose name she had ever copied into a notebook at midnight while officers slept. But she had spent a war learning what cold trails yielded to: patience, records, the slow stubborn art of making the unrecorded legible. Somewhere in Kent or beyond it there was a Meeting of the Society of Friends with a placement register, and somewhere in that register a surname she had invented, and attached to that surname, a boy who kept his effects about him very careful.

She walked back through the dark to the village and the Bell, where Mrs. Pargiter gave her a room over the tap and, on hearing the magic words *Tom the stationmaster*, declined to charge for the hot water. Edmund ate bread and cheese and drank one glass of the local cider and slept the dreamless sleep of the finally decided.

In the morning she would go to the parish church and ask after burial records and the nephew in Leeds. In the afternoon, the vicar would know, or Tom would know, or someone would know, the name of the Meeting. And if Kent would not yield it, then London would — London with its registries and its offices and its acres of paper, where every erased name waited, patient as seed, for someone willing to go looking.

She wrote in the ledger that night by candlelight, beneath the entry she had never closed, a single new line:

*Cyrus Sterling — trail cold, not dead. Following.*

Then she blew out the candle, and lay in the strange good bed listening to the wind move in the orchard, and planned her journey to London.
