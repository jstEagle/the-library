## 34. A Letter, Finally

The rain had followed Edmund Sterling home from work that evening in March of 1925, and she had not bothered to outrun it. Seven years of French winters, of mud that ate boots and men alike, had taught her a certain indifference to weather. Rain was only water doing what water did. She let it soak into her coat as she climbed the three flights to her rooms above the chandler's shop in Bermondsey, let it drip from the brim of her hat onto the landing, let it darken the doormat she had never gotten around to replacing.

She had learned, over the long years, to stop waiting.

Not all at once — that was the thing no one understood about hope. It did not die in a single blow, clean and merciful. It died the way men died in the wards she had worked: by inches, by small surrenders, each one reasonable on its own. First she stopped expecting an answer by Christmas of 1919. Then by the following spring. Then she stopped writing *he will answer* in her letters at all and wrote instead simply, as though into the wind — accounts of her days, small jokes, descriptions of the chestnut trees along the Embankment, anything that might, if he were alive somewhere and reading, make him feel less alone even if he could not reply.

Thirty-one letters in all. She had kept the drafts of none of them. What would have been the use? A woman does not keep receipts for prayers.

Her rooms were cold and smelled faintly of lamp oil and old paper — the paper being her own fault, for she had never quite conquered the habit of rescuing documents from destruction. It was the records office that had done it, or perhaps the records office had merely given shape to something that had been in her since Ypres. Twice a week she sat in a grey room in Somerset House with a greyer pen and moved names from one column to another, and twice a week, in defiance of every regulation she had read and several she had helped draft, she restored the erased to the record. There was no glory in it. There was barely any notice. But there was a satisfaction so deep it bordered on the physical, like setting a bone — Private Hollis's service record corrected two years past; a Canadian lance-corporal struck off as a deserter who had in fact died at Passchendaele, now properly mourned on paper if nowhere else; forty-one names and counting, each one entered in her own hand, which was steady, which did not tremble, because trembling was a luxury and she had never been able to afford it.

She hung her dripping hat on its peg. She knelt to light the small grate, matches failing twice before catching, and was still crouched there with her palms out when she saw the envelope on the mantel.

It had come with the afternoon post, pushed beneath the door by the boy who helped the chandler, and it lay propped against the clock where he left everything — but Edmund's eye went to it the way a compass goes north, before thought, before permission. Not because of the handwriting, though the handwriting stopped her heart within the first second. Not because of the Kent postmark, though she saw that too. It was the address itself. Her name — *Miss E. Sterling* — written in two distinct hands, layered one over the other like geological strata. The first hand was careful and childish, the letters built rather than written, an architecture of pencil-strokes gone over twice. The second was grown. Fluent. Confident. The same hand, seven years apart, one laid directly atop the memory of the other.

He must have copied his childhood address of her, she thought absurdly. He must have written her name once as the boy and once as the man, deliberately, so she would see both of them on the envelope. So she would know that both of them had survived.

Edmund did not open it standing. She found she could not. She sat down slowly on the edge of her armchair, still in her wet coat, rain darkening the upholstery beneath her, and held the envelope on her knees for a long moment while the fire ticked and spat and the rain worked at the window glass with patient fingers.

Then she opened it.

*Dear Miss Sterling* — and here the childish hand began, large and deliberate —

*I am writing this part first and the rest after, because I want you to hear from the boy you put on the ship before you hear from anyone else. This is Cyrus. I am eleven years old and we are learning about business arithmetic and I am bad at it. I have your book. I have not shown it to anyone except Father Ainsley who is our meeting elder and he cried, which surprised me because I did not think grown men did. He said it was the most Christian document he had ever held and also the least forgivable and I did not understand him then but I do now.*

*I remember your face. You told me to remember it instead of forgetting the other one and I have done as you said. It has not worn away. When I try to describe it I see it exactly: your eyes and the way your mouth went crooked when you were deciding whether to tell me a lie or the truth, which was usually the truth shaped carefully.*

*We are in Sissinghurst. My family are Friends. They do not say family, they say household, and they mean everyone in it. I have a sister named Prudence who was already here and resents me mildly and a brother named Amos who came after and adores me completely, which is the correct arrangement. Mother Wren makes bread on Wednesdays and lets me turn the millstone and I am going to enclose a drawing of the mill because you liked drawings.*

*Please write back if it is allowed. I do not know the rules for this sort of thing.*

And then, below a gap of some inches, the grown hand took up the page — smaller now, swifter, slanting slightly rightward as though always in motion:

*Miss Sterling — forgive the theatre of it. I wanted my eleven-year-old self to speak to you first, since he is the one who owed you everything, and I am only the interest his debt accrued. I am twenty-two. I am reading law at the Inns of Court, which will surprise neither of us, and I spend more time than is strictly wise in archives. You will understand why.*

*I have the ledger still. Every name. Hollis among them — T. Hollis, struck off 14th June 1917, restored by your hand, and I have wondered for years whether he lived, and whether he knows what you did for him. I have carried it through four houses and one flood and I sleep better for knowing where it is, which I suspect is the whole point of books.*

*I looked for you, before you look for me and find nothing. I wrote to the nursing registry; they said their records of overseas stations were incomplete, which is the bureaucratic way of saying burned. I wrote to the Quaker relief committees in Boulogne. Nothing. I concluded you had chosen to be unfindable, or that the war had chosen it for you, and either way I resolved not to grieve you twice. But I never once forgot you. There is a difference between losing someone and losing sight of them, and I have spent seven years keeping faith with the difference.*

*You told me to remember your face. Here is what I remember: a woman standing on a dock in the rain — it was raining then too, I have learned that rain features largely in the important moments, I don't know what that says about England — holding a book out to a sailor and watching me over his shoulder with an expression I could not name until I studied law and learned the word for it. Testimony. You were testifying. You were making me the deposition and the court and the verdict all at once.*

*I am coming to London. There is business I must discuss — the book, and what is in it, and what ought now to be done about it, because the people in those pages are not finished with us. But that letter can wait for a proper conversation, and this one cannot, so I will end it plainly.*

*You gave me a name when I had none worth keeping. You fed me soup I still think about. You lied to an officer with the smooth face of an angel, which I witnessed, and which taught me that courage often looks like composure. And when the berth was one and the claimants were three, you did the arithmetic no one else in Europe was brave enough to do.*

*I will be at Charing Cross Station on Saturday next, the twenty-eighth, beneath the clock, at half past two in the afternoon. If you cannot come, send no word — I will simply wait until the last train and understand. But I think you will come. You were never any good at leaving things unfinished.*

*Yours, in gratitude that does not fit on a page,*

*Cyrus*

Beneath the signature, in the childish hand once more, a single line had been added:

*I remembered your face. See?*

Edmund read the letter four times.

The first time she read it as a whole, greedily, the way a starving person eats. The second time she read it slowly, hunting for the flaw, the cruel joke, the mistake — because seven years had trained her thoroughly in disappointment and she did not trust anything that arrived without it. The third time she read it aloud, quietly, to the fire, and heard in the grown voice an echo she recognized: the cadence of someone who had learned to speak carefully because speech had once been impossible. And the fourth time she read only the last line, the child's line, over and over, until the words lost their meaning and became only shapes, and then recovered their meaning again, unbearably.

*I remembered your face. See?*

She became aware, distantly, that she was crying — not the way she had cried at Armistice, ugly and helpless in a street full of strangers, but something quieter and stranger, tears arriving without convulsion, without sound, as if they had been standing in queue for seven years and had finally reached the counter. She did not wipe them away. She sat in her wet coat in front of a small fire in Bermondsey and let the letter rest on her knee and wept with perfect dignity, which was, she supposed, the only kind she had ever managed.

The rain against the window had thickened. Through the glass the lamps of the street wore haloes of wet gold, and the river beyond the rooftops was invisible but present, the way certain presences are — felt rather than seen, vast and moving in the dark. Edmund thought of Boulogne. She thought of the dock, the gangway, the sailor's outstretched arms, the terrible arithmetic of that morning reduced at last to its final term: one berth, one child, one book of names pressed against a small beating heart. She had watched the ship shrink into the grey Channel and had believed, with a certainty that felt almost holy, that she was watching the best part of herself sail out of reach forever.

And now this. Now a letter in two hands. Now a boy who had become a man who had become a law student who kept her ledger through floods and house-moves the way other men kept photographs of their mothers.

Joy, she discovered, arriving after such a drought, behaves like weather. It does not ask permission. It does not arrive proportionate to the waiting. It simply breaks over you — sudden, drenching, indifferent to your preparations — and you stand in it soaked and laughing and unable to explain yourself to anyone.

She laughed. Out loud, alone, at nearly ten o'clock at night in a cold room in Bermondsey, Edmund Sterling laughed until she had to press the letter flat against her chest to keep it safe from her own shaking.

Then she rose, and moved, because grief had taught her that the body must be given tasks or it will invent its own torments. She took off the ruined coat at last and hung it properly. She put the kettle on the ring. She fetched the shoebox from under her bed — the box that held thirty-one unsent drafts' worth of silence, a pressed chestnut leaf from the Embankment, and a single folded paper with burnt edges that she had never been able to destroy and never been able to look at — and she set the new letter inside it, on top, where it belonged.

Saturday. The twenty-eighth. Half past two, beneath the clock.

Three days. Seventy-odd hours between this moment and a train station crowded with strangers, where a young man she had last seen at five years old would look at a woman of — she calculated, wincing — forty-one, grey at the temples now, lined about the eyes, carrying seven years of patients and ledgers and unanswered letters in every crease of her face. Would he know her? That was the fear that arrived now, punctual as ever, taking its seat beside the joy as it always did. She had memorized his face; he had memorized hers; but memory was a portraitist who flattered, who froze, who refused to age its subjects. He remembered a nurse of thirty-four. He would find a woman the war had continued to work on long after the war ended.

*You were never any good at leaving things unfinished.*

No. She was not. It was the whole of her biography, really — the ledger, the letters, the names moved from column to column, the stubborn refusal to accept that erased meant gone. She had spent her life insisting that people could be made legible again. Very well. She would go to Charing Cross and present herself for reading.

But it was the middle of his letter that kept returning to her, the paragraph she had skimmed in her first greedy pass and now turned over slowly, like a stone found in a field: *There is business I must discuss — the book, and what is in it, and what ought now to be done about it, because the people in those pages are not finished with us.*

Not finished with us.

She set down her untasted tea and read the sentence again, and something shifted in her chest — not dread, exactly, and not hope, but that third thing, that wartime thing, the feeling of terrain changing underfoot before the orders came. Ashcombe. The name surfaced unbidden, the way it always did, in the space between one breath and the next. She had assumed the letters burned, or buried, or locked in some solicitor's strongbox until everyone involved was dead. She had assumed the war had swallowed its own evidence, as wars do. But Cyrus had the ledger. Cyrus had studied law. Cyrus wrote *the people in those pages are not finished with us* the way other young men wrote *I have fallen in love* — with the same helplessness, the same bright inevitability.

Whatever was coming, it would not be a quiet reunion over tea. She understood that much. The boy at her door had never been random debris; he had been evidence, and evidence does not stop being dangerous because seven years pass. Somewhere in those pages of hers — in her small careful hand, in the names she had rescued from oblivion — lay testimony that powerful people had spent money and blood to suppress, and the boy she had saved had grown into precisely the kind of man who would carry such testimony into daylight.

Edmund stood at her window a long time, the letter's warmth still in her hand, and watched the rain make silver rivers of the street below. Three days. She would need a new coat — no, she decided, touching the sleeve of the old one, still damp, still smelling faintly of salt and channel fog if she imagined hard enough; she would wear the old coat, the one she'd worn on the dock, the one he might somehow, impossibly, recognize. She would need to write to Hollis. She would need to sleep, which she would not manage.

She looked down at the shoebox, at the two-handed letter resting on seven years of silence, and she spoke aloud to the empty room, to the fire, to the rain, to a boy on a ship and a man beneath a station clock — to everyone she had ever loved across a distance.

"See?" she said. "I remembered yours too."

Outside, over the black shoulder of the river, the rain began at last to ease. And in three days' time — beneath the great clock at Charing Cross, amid the smoke and the crowds and the thunder of arriving trains — a young man would remove his hat, look into the face of a woman who had forgotten nothing, and say her name.
