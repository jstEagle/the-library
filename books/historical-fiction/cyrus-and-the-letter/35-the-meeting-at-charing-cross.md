## 35. The Meeting at Charing Cross

The clock at Charing Cross said twenty past two, and Edmund Sterling had been watching it say something different for the better part of an hour.

She had arrived too early — of course she had; she had been arriving too early to everything since 1917, since the winter she learned that lateness was measured in lives — and now there was nothing left to do but stand beneath the great iron lattice of the station with her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a train she had no intention of catching. The letter was in her coat pocket. She had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth, the paper worn thin along its folds, and she could have recited it entire: *Dear Miss Sterling — I hope you will forgive the presumption of a stranger's handwriting after so long — I will be under the clock at Charing Cross on the twelfth of March at three o'clock — I will know you — Cyrus.*

*I will know you.*

That was the sentence that had undone her, the night the letter came. Not the apology, not the careful grown-up grammar of a young man who had learned English twice, once as a child and once as a inheritance. Those words. *I will know you.* As though knowing her were the simple part. As though seven years were a thing one could simply step over, the way one stepped over a puddle in a polished corridor.

She looked up at the clock again. Twenty-three past.

Around her, London performed its indifferent miracle. The station roared and sighed, steam coiling up into the glass roof like breath against a window, porters wheeling luggage in rattling processions, soldiers home on leave moving through the crowd in their ill-fitting demobilization coats, looking everywhere and nowhere with those eyes she knew better than any doctor's textbook eyes, the eyes of men who had come back from somewhere the rest of the city could not pronounce. A woman laughed near the bookstall, high and sudden, and Edmund's heart lurched before she could tell it why. She had not heard laughter like that since Boulogne, since the docks, since a whistle blew and a ship pulled away from the wall carrying everything she had.

Seven years. She had measured them the way she measured everything now — in names. The men restored to the rolls through her quiet, clerical rebellion at the records office: forty-one of them, forty-one small resurrections accomplished with pen and patience and forms filed in triplicate. Private Hollis, who wrote to her monthly from his brother's farm in Kent, letters full of weather and sheep and never once of the war, which was his way of saying thank you. Matron Greer, retired to Scarborough, who signed her notes *your old adversary* and meant *my dear girl*. Tomas, whose sister Edmund visited every Christmas with shortbread nobody ate, because grief needed a table more than it needed appetite.

And Cyrus. Cyrus, who had been a silence with a satchel, a five-year-old boy who would not speak, whom she had named for no reason she could explain and had never once, in seven years, stopped explaining to herself. She had lifted him into a sailor's arms with the ledger sewn into his coat and told him to remember her face instead of forgetting it, and then she had stood on the dock at Boulogne and watched the ship diminish into the grey until the grey took it entirely, and she had thought — she had thought with a clarity that still frightened her — *well. That is done. Whatever comes next, that is finished, and it was the only thing I ever did that mattered.*

Now the thing that came next was due at three o'clock, under the clock, and Edmund discovered she had no idea how to stand.

* * *

She had rehearsed this, God help her. On the train up from her lodgings she had rehearsed it, catching the alarmed glance of the woman opposite as Edmund moved her lips silently through openings. *Hello. You've grown.* Absurd — of course he had grown; he had been five, he must be twelve or thirteen now, tall as her shoulder perhaps, a schoolboy's cap, a Quaker family in the country, a life she had built for him out of nothing but a berth on a boat and the ferocious arithmetic of mercy. *Do you remember—* No. Never ask that. If he remembered, he would say so. If he did not, she would not put the weight of her whole history into his small hands and watch him stagger under it. She had decided all of this on the train, sensibly, firmly, a list of permitted sentences like the permitted quantities of a ration book.

But rehearsal dissolved the moment she actually stood beneath the clock, because rehearsal assumed she would recognize him, and it had only just occurred her — standing there in the iron-smelling cold of the concourse, buffeted by strangers — that recognition might be the whole of the problem, and it might run in both directions.

What if she did not know him?

It was a terrible thought and she made herself think it through, the way she had once made herself count berths on a dock: if you look at the arithmetic honestly you can at least choose your lie with open eyes. He had been five. Five-year-olds changed completely; they grew new faces the way they grew new teeth. He might walk straight up to her, this grown boy, this almost-man, and say her name, and she would look into a stranger's face and feel nothing rise to meet it — no echo of the child who left paper offerings at her cot, drawings of a woman's face burned at the edges, grief rendered in a mute child's careful pencil. She would have to pretend. She had pretended before — she had lied smoothly to Captain Vane and hadn't recognized herself afterward — but this pretense would be worse, because it would be kind, and kindness practiced as deception had always struck her as the loneliest of the sins.

Or — and here her mind turned the knife over, examining its edge with the methodical cruelty of a mind that had spent years among wounds — what if she recognized him instantly, wholly, the shape of his ears and the way he held his head, and he looked at her and saw nobody at all? A middle-aged woman in a good grey coat, a civil servant's face, a stranger. What if *I will know you* had been written by a boy who remembered a uniform and a smell of carbolic and a voice saying *remember my face*, and the face itself had gone the way of the drawings, burned at the edges?

She had kept every face. That was her curse and her vocation, wasn't it? The ledger of small names, the memory that forgot nothing, the nurse who could tell you the exact tone of Private Hollis's weeping on the worst night of his life and the precise angle of light through the tent flaps the morning the order came to evacuate. She had carried them all, faithfully, obsessively, a woman made of other people's faces — and the one face she most needed to be remembered by belonged to a child who had been instructed, in a note pinned to his own small coat, to forget.

*Forget this face*, the note had said, meaning the mother's, meaning spare the boy. And Edmund had reversed it at the gangway — *remember mine* — and had lived seven years inside the space between those two instructions, not knowing which one had taken root.

Twenty-nine past. Her heart was going like a metronome set to a march.

She made herself look at practical things. The flower stall by the barrier, daffodils bundled in brown paper — March flowers, out-of-season optimism, and she thought absurdly of buying some and just as absurdly dismissed it, because what message could daffodils possibly carry that wouldn't collapse into sentiment the moment he read it? The steam. The pigeons dropping from the girders in sudden slate commas. The clock again — she forbade herself the clock, then looked anyway. Thirty-one past.

He might not come.

There it was. The last fear, the one under all the others, the one she had not let herself say even in the privacy of her own skull on the train. Seven years of unanswered letters to an address in Kent — she knew about unanswered letters; she had written monthly into that silence until the war ended and some months beyond, out of sheer momentum of hope, like a clock that keeps ticking after the spring is spent. Perhaps the Quaker family had moved. Perhaps the boy had read her letters and felt the terrible obligation of them and chosen, gently, finally, not to answer. Perhaps remembering her hurt. She had considered this possibility many times, in the long grey evenings, and had always concluded she could bear it — she had sent him away to save his life, not to collect a debt — but conclusions made in an evening are cheap, and conclusions tested beneath a station clock at half past three are another currency altogether.

A young man detached himself from the crowd by the ticket barrier.

Later, she would try to reconstruct the moment and find that memory had refused to cooperate; it had given her fragments instead of a scene. The grey coat, too big in the shoulders, as though cut for the man he would be rather than the boy he was. Dark hair, unruly where the wind had come through the doors with him. The way he stopped ten yards away — stopped as though the floor had informed him of something — and stood with his hands at his sides, looking at her.

She did not recognize him.

That was the first fact, and it went through her like cold water through cloth. She searched his face the way she had once searched casualty lists, line by line, desperate and systematic, and found nothing of the round-cheeked solemn child who had clutched a satchel against regulations. This was a long-faced, serious youth of perhaps thirteen, with a healing scrape along his jaw and shoes that had been mended well by someone who loved him.

Then he tilted his head — a small tilt, leftward, questioning — and seven years collapsed.

Because she knew that tilt. She had seen it across a dressing station in Flanders, the boy watching her work, head angled exactly so, filing her away with the terrible thoroughness of children. It was the same tilt. It was the same boy. It was — oh, it was *Cyrus*, her Cyrus, wearing a taller body the way one wore a borrowed coat, and her eyes were scalding and her throat had closed and every rehearsed sentence she owned evaporated at once, leaving her standing under the clock with nothing but her face, which was doing, she was certain, something unrestrained and unbecoming.

He walked toward her. Ten yards became eight became five, and she watched him watching her, and she understood suddenly that he was searching too — that behind his composure the same terror was conducting the same inventory, checking her jawline against a memory five years deep, comparing this grey-haired woman to a face kept safe through two countries and a Quaker childhood and a hundred nights of choosing whether to remember.

Five feet. Close enough that she could see his eyes were brown, had always been brown, that some things survived even growth.

"Edmund," he said.

Not *Miss Sterling*. Not *Nurse*. Not any of the formal salutations a careful orphan might construct. Just her name — the name she had given him, the name she had spoken over his cot in a freezing tent in Flanders when he was a silence who needed owning — *Cyrus, this is Edmund, Edmund is here* — repeated so often in those weeks that the word had stopped being a label and become a place, a shelter, a small lit room the boy could enter whenever the guns made the world too large.

He had carried it across the Channel. He had carried it seven years, the way she had carried his face, and now he set it down between them like a returned key.

"Cyrus," she said, and her voice broke on the second syllable, and she did not care, because she had held herself together through dockside catastrophe and lamplit interrogation and a war's worth of dying and she would be damned if she'd spend her composure on this, of all moments, this one unambiguous grace.

He smiled. And there — *there*, surfacing at last through the strangeness of the grown face like a coin rising through bathwater — was the child. The same smile, guarded and then not guarded, arriving slowly and then all at once, the smile of a boy deciding to trust. She had won that smile once through small kindnesses: warm milk, a folded blanket, patience without demand. It appeared she had never lost it. It had merely been in storage.

"You're smaller than I remembered," he said gravely, and then, horrified at himself, "I mean — I've grown — I didn't mean—" 

"I know exactly what you meant." She was laughing now, or crying, or some union of the two that had no respectable name. "You were four feet tall and furious about a satchel. Everyone is smaller than that."

"It's not in the satchel anymore." He said it quickly, the way one says a thing prepared in advance, rehearsed on a train as she had rehearsed her sentences, and his hand went to his breast pocket — to a shape there, rectangular, hard-cornered, familiar to her fingers before her eyes confirmed it. "I kept it. All this time. I kept everything."

The ledger. Her ledger, her book of small names, the record of every soldier the officers had struck from the rolls, every erased man she had refused to let become unwritten — sewn into a child's coat on a dock at Boulogne, sent to England as evidence, as inheritance, as the only letter she had ever truly written. He had kept it. Through a Quaker upbringing and a new surname and whatever slow business of growing up a rescued child performs, he had carried her rebellion the way she had once carried his whole survival.

"The train was late," he added, apropos of nothing, because thirteen-year-olds are owed conversational mercy and rarely know how to claim it, and because neither of them, she suspected, could bear the weight of the important things for one more second without a rest stop in the ordinary. "From Ashford. There was a cow on the line outside Pluckley. An actual cow. The guard made an announcement about it in a very dignified voice, as though the cow had purchased a ticket."

"A cow," Edmund said. "On the line."

"A very large cow." His gravity was total. "I thought you should know the caliber of obstacle I overcame."

And she laughed — really laughed, helplessly, standing under the clock at Charing Cross with tears running freely down her face while commuters flowed around them like water around two stones — and the sound of it startled her, because she could not remember the last time she had made that noise, and she suspected, from the way he grinned, that he had made her do it on purpose, that the cow was tactical, that this solemn child had already learned what she had needed a war to teach her: that love arrives most safely disguised as ordinary conversation.

* * *

They sat afterward in the station tea room, at a marble-topped table by the window, with tea going cold between them because neither drank it, because drinking would have required letting go of the looking.

He told her about Ashford. About the Aldous family — *Friends*, he said, capitalizing it without irony — who had taken him in off the evacuation register with the satchel and no history and had asked no questions for a full year, and then had asked only gentle ones. About learning to speak again, haltingly, at six, his first word in England being *please*, which he reported with the embarrassment of a boy who has been told this is charming and disagrees. About school, where he was middling at games and alarming at history, because he kept asking what had happened to the people the textbooks rounded off.

"They always stop at the treaties," he said, frowning into his cold tea. "As though the men are just — figures. Sums. But somebody was each of them. Somebody kept their boots oiled. Somebody waited." He glanced up, suddenly self-conscious. "I sound like you."

"I should hope so," Edmund managed.

She asked carefully about the letters — hers, the monthly ones to Kent — and watched something complicated move across his face.

"I have all of them," he said. "In a biscuit tin. Aunt Prudence — Mrs. Aldous — kept them for me until I could read them myself, and then I read them so many times the folds wore out, and I copied the early ones into the back of—" He touched his pocket again. "Yours were the only ones addressed to me. Everything else was addressed to 'the child.' You wrote to *Cyrus*."

"You were Cyrus," she said simply. "I named you. It seemed only fair I should use it."

"Why that name?" And there it was, the question she had dodged for seven years, asked with a young man's directness, and she found — sitting in the steam-fogged tea room with the afternoon light coming down through the Victorian glass — that she could finally answer it, or begin to.

"I don't know," she said. "I've tried to make it mean something clever. An old king, a Persian, a man who freed captives. I've told myself I chose it for the history. But the truth is you were lying asleep on my cot with your fist closed around that satchel like the whole world was in it, and I needed to call you something, and it arrived, whole, the way names do." She paused. "Perhaps I chose it because it sounded like someone who would survive."

He considered this with the tilt of the head she had known him by, and then he said, quietly, "It worked," and she had to look out the window at the trains for a while.

It was he who turned, eventually, to the pocket, and laid the shape on the marble table between them, unwrapping the handkerchief around it with the unhurried reverence of an acolyte handling relics. The ledger. Smaller than she remembered — everything was — the oilcloth cover cracked along the spine, the corners soft with handling. Far more handling than seven years of storage would account for.

"I have to show you something," Cyrus said, and opened it, and turned past her entries — Hollis's true name, the men of the 14th, all her small insurrectionary ink — to pages she had never written.

Pages in a child's careful pencil that had matured, entry by entry, year by year, into a young man's confident hand. Names. Dates. Places. Dozens of names she did not know, each one followed by notes in a tidy column: *found*, *confirmed*, *written to*, *restored*.

"What is this?" she whispered, though she already knew, though she had known since he touched his pocket on the platform, and knowing did nothing at all to protect her from it.

"Your book," Cyrus said. "I kept it going." He turned another page, and another, and she saw the years unfold in graphite — a record begun by a traumatized child and continued with what she now recognized, with a shock that went through her like a bell being struck, as vocation. "There are more of them than you'd think. Men struck off. Families scattered. Papers lost in the shelling. Nobody keeps track, so I—" He stopped, and looked up at her, and asked the question that would reorganize the remainder of her life: "Will you help me? There's one I've been trying to trace for a year. A woman. A housemaid. I think — Edmund, I think I'm ready to find out what happened to her. Properly. With names and courts and everything. And I don't want to do it alone."

Her mother's face in burned-edge drawings. A murdered girl who had never, in any official record anywhere in England, been given back her name.

Edmund put her hand flat on the open ledger, on her handwriting flowing into his, one continuous line across seven silent years.

"Tell me everything," she said.
