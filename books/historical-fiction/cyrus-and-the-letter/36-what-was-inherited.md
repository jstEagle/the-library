## 36. What Was Inherited

The cottage at Charing Cross Road had been Cyrus's idea of a joke, he told her — a solicitor's clerk lodging above a tailor, three flights up and thin as a hymn book — before the Quakers moved him somewhere with light. The rooms in Bloomsbury where he actually lived were nothing like what Edmund had imagined during seven years of unanswered letters. She had pictured something austere, monastic, a boy grown into a man who kept his possessions like a soldier on retreat: one kit bag, everything essential, nothing that could not be carried. Instead there were books everywhere, stacked in columns against the walls like masonry, and maps pinned above the hearth — France, Belgium, the coast of Kent — and on the table by the window, beneath a green-shaded lamp that had been lit for her arrival, a single object laid out square and waiting.

Her ledger.

She had not seen it since a February morning on the dock at Boulogne, when she had slipped it into the satchel along with the last of the letters, buttoned the coat to the boy's chin, and said, *Remember my face instead.* Eleven years. Long enough for the notebook to have passed from being hers to being history; long enough that she had made her peace with never seeing it again, the way one makes peace with a grave.

"May I?" she asked, though her hand was already halfway across the table.

Cyrus laughed — he laughed easily now, a low sound with gravel in it, the laugh of a man who had decided at some point that joy was a discipline worth keeping — and pulled out the chair beside his. "It was never mine to permit," he said. "But yes."

She sat. She opened the cover.

The oilcloth she had wrapped it in at the dressing station near Ypres — she remembered cutting it from a ration crate with Thomas's penknife — had been replaced with proper binding cloth, dark blue, stitched by someone patient. But inside, unchanged, was the paper she knew: cheap, grey-edged, ruled faintly, water-stained where rain had come through the tent seam in March of 1918. Her own handwriting looked back at her, small and upright, each entry composed like a headstone because she had never allowed herself more room than a headstone's worth of grief.

*Pvt. E. Hollis, 2nd Btn., struck from rolls 14 Oct. 1917 as deserter. Not a deserter. Found at Poperinghe, shell-shocked, wandering. Name: Eli Hollis. Mother: Ada. Ledbury, Herefordshire.*

And below it, in ink that had faded to the colour of weak tea:

*Sgt. A. Mbala? No — Mabon. Sgt. D. Mabon...*

She stopped. That was not her hand.

"Cyrus," she said quietly. "What is this?"

He came to stand behind her shoulder, and she felt rather than saw him lean in, the way an apprentice leans toward a master's bench — except that the apprenticeship had run backwards, she realized, the master work done first by the boy and only understood afterward by the woman. "Read further," he said. "There are forty-one of them."

Forty-one. She turned pages. Her original entries — thirty-two souls, recorded in pencil between bandage changes and bombardments, each name a small act of treason against the machinery that had erased them — marched down the left-hand margins of the notebook as she had left them. But filling the right-hand margins, crowding into the footers, spilling onto pages she had never written on because the war ended before she could, was another script entirely. It began childish: round letters, unevenly spaced, the careful labour of a six-year-old taught to write by Quaker schoolmistresses who believed that a child's hand should be legible if it killed them. And it grew, page by page, year by year, into the strong slanted hand of the man beside her — the hand that had addressed the letter she'd received in the rain outside the records office, the hand that had signed himself *Cyrus Sterling*.

Sterling. Her name. He had taken it without asking, or perhaps he had asked and she had forgotten through the noise of reunion; either way it sat in her chest now like swallowed sunlight.

"The first ones I copied out of your book," he said. "I didn't understand them yet. I thought they were a prayer list. Mrs. Penhale — she was the one in Kent, you remember from the letters — she found me copying them instead of scripture and instead of scolding me, she sat down and helped." He reached past her and turned to a page near the middle. "She said, *if these are men God forgot to write down, then someone must hold the pen for Him.* She was not, strictly speaking, a conventional Quaker."

Edmund traced the entries with one finger. Mabon, David, sergeant, Royal Welsh Fusiliers, struck off after Cambric Copse — *restored*, the boy's later annotation read, *per regimental archive, 1924, witnessed*. Below him, a woman: *Constance Ferris, V.A.D., struck from hospital register St. Omer after refusing matron's order to discharge infectious cases to duty. Restored to register 1926. Alive. Living in Cardiff. I wrote to her. She wrote back.* And below that, in red ink, a different notation entirely: *Not found. Searched 1927, 1929, 1931. Continue.*

"There are eleven marked in red," Cyrus said. "I keep them at the front of the drawer so I don't forget they're open cases."

*Open cases.* As though the dead and disappeared of the Great War were correspondence awaiting reply. Edmund looked up at him — this tall man with his mother's dark eyes and his unknown father's jaw, whom she had lifted into a sailor's arms while a ship's whistle screamed — and felt something turn over in her that she could not immediately name. It was not pride, or not only pride. Pride was too simple a coin for it. It was closer to what she had felt standing in the ruins of the dressing station at Ypres, holding her little book, understanding for the first time that the empire's ledgers would always be shorter than its mercy, and that somebody, somewhere, would have to keep writing.

"You built a life out of my notebook," she said.

"I built a life out of your *practice*." He pulled his chair around so they sat cornerwise, knee to knee, the way they had sat in the hospital train among the wounded while Tomas stood guard at the corridor door. "Do you know what I used to think about, in Kent, when I couldn't sleep? Not my mother — I'd made my peace with her early, or as early as anyone does. I thought about you on that dock. I thought: she gave me the berth. She gave me the *book*. And I spent years trying to work out which gift was larger, and then one day I understood they were the same gift, and that the book was never the point. Anyone can own a notebook. You *wrote in it*. Every day. Against orders. Against sense. That was the inheritance — not the object. The habit."

"The habit," Edmund repeated, and laughed, a short wet sound. "Matron Greer called it insubordination."

"Matron Greer," said Cyrus, "is getting a very long letter this Christmas, whether she likes it or not." He opened the drawer beneath the table and lifted out a second volume — thicker, newer, bound in the same blue cloth. "This is the continuation. Volume two, if you like. Begun 1922, when I went up to Cambridge and discovered the university library keeps records the War Office has never heard of."

She took it. It was heavier than hers had been, dense with entries in his adult hand, cross-referenced, indexed by regiment and by year, some entries carrying small pencilled checkmarks and others carrying dates — *visited*, *written*, *confirmed*. Page after page of names. Soldiers, nurses, labourers, a chaplain, two Chinese members of the Labour Corps whose company had been struck from a battalion diary with a stroke of administrative pen so casual it might have been correcting a misprint. In the back, folded into a pocket, she found newspaper clippings, copies of pension board decisions, and a photograph of a middle-aged man standing awkwardly in a Hereford garden — Eli Hollis, restored to the rolls through Edmund's records office work two years ago, alive and growing roses.

"He sends me runner beans every autumn," Cyrus said. "I've never told him why I care. Should I?"

"He knows why," Edmund said softly. "Hollis remembers everything. He only needed reminding that it happened to a man with a name."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon the way old soldiers clean rifles: companionably, without hurry, each motion worn smooth by repetition. Cyrus showed her his system — the archive numbers, the correspondence files, the map pins, red for unresolved and brass for found. He introduced her to his landlady's cat, who supervised from atop a stack of *Regimental Histories, 1914–18* with the air of a colonel reviewing troops. He made tea in a pot that had seen better decades and confessed, with the ghost of the mute boy flickering under his composure, that he still sometimes could not speak in the mornings — that the words simply would not come until noon, on bad days, and that he had learned to write instead, and that the writing had become, over the years, more fluent than the speech ever was.

"That's why the law suits me," he said. "Everything important is already on paper."

Edmund understood. She had spent seven years in a records office restoring names with a pen, and had never once spoken aloud, even to Matron Greer, the full contents of her heart about those years at the front. Some inheritances were better practised than pronounced.

Toward dusk he grew quiet in a particular way she recognized from the boy he had been — a settling, a gathering, the look of someone arranging words in a satchel before showing them. He went to the bureau in the corner and returned with a leather folder, untied it, and spread its contents across the clear end of the table where the lamp fell strongest.

Photographs. A house — she knew it instantly from the letters' descriptions, even before he said it: the Ashcombe estate in Surrey, all honeyed brick and confident chimneys. An invoice, photographed in flat grey, bearing a shipping company's letterhead. A crew manifest. And a single sheet, yellowed and brittle, that made Edmund's breath stop in her throat though she had known it was coming — the copy of a copy, Greer's precaution made real, of a letter naming prices, tonnages, contracts fulfilled while ships sailed deliberately short-handed into the U-boat lanes.

"They're reopening the inquiry," Cyrus said. "Quietly. A parliamentary committee, next spring. The Ashcombes' friends are not what they were — Vane's arrest cracked the wall, and the men who hid behind it are old now, and frightened, and frightened men talk." He laid his hand flat on the manifest. "I've been asked to give evidence. As a witness. As — " he hesitated over the word, and she watched him choose it — "as the surviving dependent. The maid's son. The boy the family tried to make disappear along with the paperwork."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, a bus changed gear on the Tottenham Court Road, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, indifferent to the fact that a murdered housemaid's name was about to be spoken in a room where power sat listening.

"They want my account," Cyrus said. "The satchel, the note, the dock, all of it. But there is a part of the record that exists nowhere but in you, Edie. My mother's face. The letters before they were copied. What Vane said on that dock with his voice like warm oil. What you did with the chit, and why." He met her eyes, and for a moment he was five years old again, clutching a bag that weighed more than he did, and she was the woman who had lied smoothly for the first time and hadn't recognized herself after. "I can testify to what survived. Only you can testify to what was risked. Will you come with me? Will you say it — in the open, on the record, where no officer can strike it from any roll?"

Edmund looked down at the table: her ledger and his, side by side under the lamplight, the rebellion and the vocation, the mother's handwriting and the son's. Thirty-two names in one book. Forty-one in the other. And beyond them, unnumbered, a girl with burned-edge eyes in a child's drawings, waiting eleven years for a courtroom to learn what she had been called.

She had drafted three versions of tomorrow once before, on a dock in France, and believed none of them. This time there was only one version, and she believed it completely.

"Yes," she said. "I'll say it. Every word."

Cyrus let out a breath that seemed to have been held for a decade, and then he did something he had not done since Boulogne: he put his head down on the table beside the ledger, the way the boy had once slept against her cot, and laughed, and then, briefly and without ceremony, wept — and Edmund Sterling, keeper of names, rested her hand on his dark hair and began, silently, to compose her testimony.
