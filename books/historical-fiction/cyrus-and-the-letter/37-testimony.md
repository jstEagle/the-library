## 37. Testimony

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope so official that Edmund held it at arm's length for a full minute before opening it, as though the heavy cream paper and the embossed crest might bite. *His Majesty's Commission of Inquiry into War Contracts and Supply Arrangements, 1914–1918. Reopened Session.* And beneath the formal salutation: *Your attendance is required to give evidence concerning documents recovered from the estate of Captain Alistair Vane.*

She read it twice at the kitchen table while her tea went cold, and then she carried it upstairs to where Cyrus kept his books, because some news was not meant to be held alone.

He was at his desk — he was always at his desk now, bent over his law texts like a man praying over them — and when she laid the envelope beside his inkwell he read it once, quickly, the way he read everything, and then again slowly, and then he looked up at her with an expression she had learned to recognize over the years but had never grown used to: the look of a boy standing very still at a doorway, deciding whether to come in.

"They've copied the letters," he said. "The ones Greer sent ahead. They've had them all this time."

"Then they've had everything but the witness."

"You're the witness." He said it carefully, testing the weight of it.

"I'm the witness," she agreed, and found that her voice did not shake, though something behind her ribs did — not fear, exactly, or not only fear. Something more like a door that had been locked since 1917 beginning, very slowly, to swing open.

* * *

In the week before the hearing, they rehearsed.

Not formally — neither of them would have called it that. Cyrus simply began, over supper, over the washing-up, over the last pipe of the evening, asking her questions in the flat, courteous tone he had been practicing at his chambers, and she answered as best she could across twenty-two years of distance.

"State your name and your service for the record, please, Miss Sterling."

"Edmund Sterling. Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service. I served at casualty clearing stations near Ypres from the spring of 1917 until demobilization."

"And how did the documents in question come into your possession?"

And there she always faltered. Not because she did not remember — she remembered everything, that was her curse and her calling both, she had built a life out of remembering what others were paid to forget — but because the shape of the story sounded absurd when spoken aloud in a warm kitchen in Kent with a copper kettle ticking on the stove. *A child appeared at the door during a bombardment. He carried a satchel. Inside the satchel were letters naming one of the great shipping families of England.* It sounded like something from one of Cyrus's novels, if he still wrote novels, which he did not; he had traded fiction for law on the theory, as he put it once, that the truth needed better advocates than stories could give it.

"It's the dates they'll go after," Cyrus said, pacing the length of the sitting room with his hands clasped behind his back, looking, at twenty-seven, alarmingly like someone's barrister already. "They'll say you cannot swear to when you opened the satchel, or that the letters could have been planted, or that a nurse under bombardment could not reliably recall—"

"Cyrus."

"—or that your political sympathies—"

"Cyrus." He stopped pacing. "I have carried those names in my head for twenty-two years. I have written them down, filed them, restored them, defended them before records boards and pension committees. The Ashcombes' lawyers can ask me any question they like about dates. Dates are the easiest thing I know."

He looked at her then with an expression she could not immediately name, and after a moment she understood it was awe — plain, undecorated awe — and she turned back to her mending because she had never in her life known what to do with being looked at that way.

But later that night, lying awake in the narrow room she still thought of as the spare room even though she had lived in this house nearly two years now, she heard him downstairs in his study, the scratch of his pen going past midnight, and she understood that he was afraid. He would never say it. He was the boy who would not speak, grown into a man who spoke carefully and completely and never about himself. But she knew the sound of a person building walls out of paper at three in the morning, because she had done it herself, in dressing stations, in ledgers, in monthly letters to an address that never answered.

She got up and made tea and brought it down, and he looked up at her over his briefs with eyes shadowed like his father might have had, whoever his father was, no one had ever known, and he said, without preamble:

"If I do this badly, the inquiry closes again. The contracts stand. Her name goes back in the ground."

Her name. The maid's. The woman in the drawings with the burned edges, whose face a five-year-old had carried through a war and across a sea and into the keeping of a stranger.

"She has a name, Cyrus," Edmund said. "That's the first thing we'll win, whatever else happens. She'll be spoken aloud in a courtroom. After twenty-two years of being *the housemaid*, *the deceased female servant*, *the unidentified woman*. They will have to say her name into the record, and the record keeps things. I know something about that."

He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "You know everything about that."

"No," she said. "I know one thing about it. I know that a person unrecorded is not unloved, and a person erased is not gone. Everything else is clerical work."

* * *

London in March was rain and coal smoke and buses grinding through the wet streets, and the Royal Courts of Justice rose out of the murk like a cathedral built by someone who worshipped procedure. Cyrus walked her up the steps himself, though he was called to the bar only in aspiration and had no formal standing in the inquiry; the Ashcombe family's counsel had retained men whose wigs predated the war, and the commission sat five in number beneath a carved ceiling of trumpeting angels, and every bench was full.

Edmund wore her service medal and the grey dress she had bought for Hollis's funeral two years before, and she sat with her hands folded in her lap and discovered, with distant surprise, that they were steady. Twenty-two years ago her hands had shaken over a notebook in a dugout. Now they lay quiet as church stones. Perhaps, she thought, that was what age was for: it taught the hands what the heart refused to learn.

The letters were produced first, laid out in evidence bags on the long table like relics — and she supposed that was precisely what they were. She recognized them at once, the water-stained seals, the careful slant of a hand that had learned penmanship in some great house's servants' hall. *Margaret Ashling,* the record said now. *Housemaid, Ashcombe Hall, Norfolk.* Someone had found her name. Someone had gone digging through parish registers and employment books, and had pulled one small name out of the mud, and Edmund felt her throat tighten at the sight of it printed on the exhibit list. Margaret Ashling. Born 1889. Died 1916, cause recorded as *fever*, which was a lie so tidy it might have been typed.

Cyrus sat two rows behind her. She did not look at him. She did not need to.

They questioned her for four hours.

The Ashcombes' lead counsel was a silver-haired gentleman named Sir Roderick Fane — *Fane*, she noted, and wondered if the universe was amusing itself — and he was very good. He was courteous, unhurried, faintly regretful, the way a man is regretful when he must disturb a nice old lady's memories for legal purposes. He established that she had been a nurse under bombardment. He established that shell shock affected memory. He established, gently, remorselessly, that she had forged a travel chit in 1917 — they had found that too, in Vane's papers, the vindictive little man had kept a copy of everything — and that therefore her respect for official documentation was, shall we say, situational.

A stir went through the gallery. Edmund waited for it to settle.

"Sir Roderick," she said, when he paused, "may I answer?"

"That is rather the commission's privilege, madam."

"Then I'd be grateful if the commission would let me answer, because you've asked whether my memory can be trusted, and I should like to tell you what my memory holds."

The chairman, a dry stick of a man named Justice Weller, inclined his head a fraction. She took it as permission.

"In October of 1917," she said, "I held a boy in my arms on a dock at Boulogne while a ship pulled away from the wall carrying everything I loved. I can tell you the colour of the paint on the ship's funnel. I can tell you the sailor who took him had a tattoo of an anchor on his right wrist, and that his arms were shaking, though whether from cold or from lifting a child I couldn't say. I can tell you that Private William Hollis, struck from the rolls as a deserter, stood beside me and that his coat smelled of creosote and that he wept without noise, the way men learn to. That is what my memory holds, sir. Not dates. Faces. Names. The weight of a child against my shoulder. You may take it or leave it, but you will not shake it, because I have spent twenty-two years checking it against itself and it has never once been wrong."

The silence afterward had a texture to it, like the pause after a shell lands and everyone waits to learn if they are among the living.

Sir Roderick consulted his papers. When he looked up, the courtesy had thinned.

"These letters," he said. "You claim the boy delivered them. Can you account for the boy? Can you produce him?"

"He's sitting behind me."

Another stir. Cyrus rose. He did not speak — he had told her he would not, that his testimony was scheduled separately, that the sight of him standing would be testimony enough — but he stood, and he turned, and he faced the commissioners, and Edmund watched five elderly men understand, all at once and visibly, that the abstract document in the evidence bag had arrived in the world inside a living body that was now wearing a good suit and looking at them with steady dark eyes.

"The commission will hear from Mr. Ashling tomorrow," Justice Weller said, and there was something in his voice that had not been there before. Something with an edge on it.

Ashling. Cyrus had taken the name years ago, quietly, by deed poll — the surname of the mother he could barely remember, spelled out at last in a parish register by a researcher's patience. Margaret Ashling's son. Edmund had wept when he told her, alone, in the garden, and he had pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of tenderness.

* * *

That evening they stayed in a small hotel near Lincoln's Inn, because Cyrus said the trains would be miserable and because, Edmund suspected, neither of them wanted to be alone. They ate dinner in the dining room almost entirely in silence, and afterward he walked her through the Inns of Temple in the drizzle, past the dark chestnut trees, and she saw him composing, composing, his lips moving slightly over tomorrow's words.

"Tell me about her," she said suddenly.

He stopped under a lamp. Rain beaded on his shoulders.

"You've told me pieces," she went on. "The drawings. The smell of starch. That she sang. But tomorrow they'll ask you about her as evidence, and you shouldn't have to meet her for the first time in that room. Meet her here first. With me. Tell me about Margaret Ashling."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he came and sat beside her on a wet stone bench, not caring about the damp, the way he had never cared about damp since the docks at Boulogne, and he looked out at the black lawn and the falling rain as though reading something written there.

"She hummed when she worked," he said. "I don't remember songs. I remember the humming. Low, mostly, under her breath. And I remember that she was frightened in the last weeks — not of the loud things, I was used to the loud things by then, the shouting below stairs, the motorcars arriving at night with men who didn't wipe their boots. She was frightened quietly. She packed the satchel in front of me like it was a game. *This is your treasure, Cyrus, you guard the treasure.* I thought it was sweets, at first." His mouth twisted. "I guarded it for a year thinking there might be sweets."

"There were," Edmund said softly. "There were exactly the sweets that mattered."

He looked at her then, and his eyes were bright and terrible, and he was five years old and twenty-seven all at once.

"They're going to say she was unreliable," he said. "Unbalanced. That she made trouble and was let go and died of fever somewhere, naturally, regrettably. They've hired men to make a dead servant girl into nothing, in front of a commission, in front of me, in front of—" he gestured, helplessly, at the whole rainy world "—and I am supposed to sit there and be *measured*. Measured and reasonable. And all I will want to say is her name. Over and over. Until it fills the room."

"Then fill the room," Edmund said. "That's what testimony is, Cyrus. It isn't winning. I learned that late, thank God, but I learned it. You don't testify to defeat anyone. You testify so that the record holds what actually happened. Let Sir Roderick have his dates and his doubts. You put her in the record. Humming, and frightened quietly, and packing treasure. The record keeps things. I told you — I know something about that."

He laughed then, a short broken sound, and put his arm around her shoulders, and they sat together on the wet bench in the dark like two refugees from the same drowned country, which is what they were, and always would be.

* * *

The second day, Edmund gave the remainder of her evidence: the ledger itself, produced from her bag like scripture, its pages soft as cloth from handling. She watched the chairman turn the entries with fingers that slowed despite themselves. *Private William Hollis, 2nd Buffs. Struck as deserter, 12 June 1917. Shell shock, not cowardice. Witness: E. Sterling.* Page after page after page. Small names. Kept names.

"How many?" Justice Weller asked at last, and his dryness had cracked somewhere along its length.

"Three hundred and eleven," Edmund said. "In that book. My colleague Mr. Ashling has found and restored several hundred more since. It became rather a habit."

"I see," said the justice, and wrote something down, and Edmund had been alive long enough to know that sometimes the most important sentences are the ones that never get read aloud.

Then it was Cyrus's turn, and she had to leave the room — witnesses were excluded until after giving evidence, and besides, she could not have sat through it, she knew that about herself now. She waited in the corridor outside the courtroom on a bench worn smooth by a century of waiting relatives, with her hands folded and her medal catching the weak light from a high window, and through the heavy oak door she could hear nothing but a murmur, rising and falling, questions and answers, the old machinery of proof turning over.

But once — only once — the murmuring stopped entirely, and in the silence she heard, faintly, muffled by oak and velvet and twenty-two years, a single voice speaking low and level, saying something that lasted perhaps ten seconds, and she could not make out a word of it, and she did not need to.

Afterward, when it was done, Cyrus came out into the corridor white as paper and found her, and sat down heavily beside her on the bench, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. Then:

"They asked me to describe her," he said. "For the record. So I did. The humming. How she folded things, corners first. That she called me her treasure." He passed a hand over his face. "And then Sir Roderick asked whether a housemaid's word could be trusted against a family of national importance, and the chairman stopped him. Stopped him, Edmund. Said the commission had read the shipping manifests and would prefer not to insult its own intelligence."

"Vindication tastes like ash," Edmund said. "Someone wise told me that once."

"You told me that. In the garden, when I changed my name." He smiled faintly. "It doesn't taste like ash today."

"No," she agreed, looking down the long corridor toward the doors through which justice arrived, imperfectly and late, smelling of rain and floor polish and hope. "Today it tastes like tea. Like something ordinary. Which is the whole point of it, I think."

They rose to go. And they had reached the end of the corridor, nearly to the doors and the waiting rain, when a clerk came hurrying after them — a young man, breathless, holding a folder — saying that the commission apologized, that one further matter had arisen, one document recovered late from the Vane papers, and that Mr. Ashling's presence was respectfully requested tomorrow morning, because the document appeared to concern the disposition of certain monies, trust arrangements made secretly in 1916, in the name of —

Cyrus took the paper. Read it. Went very still.

"Edmund," he said quietly. "It's a trust. In my mother's name. Funded the month before she died. It was never cancelled." He looked up, and his eyes were wide with something that was not yet joy, could not afford to be, but was circling it like a bird over land. "Someone in that family knew about me. Someone has been paying for me — or trying to — for twenty-two years."
