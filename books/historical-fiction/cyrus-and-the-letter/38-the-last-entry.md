## 38. The Last Entry

The house in Kent smelled of beeswax and coal smoke and the particular sweetness of old paper, and on the last evening of October 1925 Edmund Sterling sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open before her and did not touch it for a long time.

She had carried it through two wars — the declared one and the long unnumbered one that had followed inside her — across a dock at Boulogne, into a records office in London, out to this cottage with its crooked chimney and its garden going over to frost. She had carried it when carrying it meant prison or worse. Now it lay under the lamp like any ordinary notebook, its cloth cover worn soft at the corners, its spine cracked in the places a book cracks when it has been opened too often by hands that loved it.

Cyrus was in the parlour. She could hear him through the wall, the scratch of his pen, the small clearing of his throat he made when a sentence would not come — a habit he could not have learned from her, since he had been five years old and silent when they parted, and yet there it was, as though some grammar of concentration had passed between them on that gangway along with the ledger. He was writing up his testimony for the inquiry. The Ashcombe papers had been reopened in September; counsel had requested depositions; and Cyrus Ashworth-Sterling — he used both names now, wearing them the way one wears a coat tailored long ago for a body still growing into it — had spent three evenings at the kitchen table turning memory into evidence.

Edmund turned to the back of the ledger.

The pages were crowded. Twenty-two years of entries, first in her field pencil, later in her fountain pen, finally in Cyrus's careful adolescent hand after he had taken up the practice as a boy might take up a father's trade. Names. Dates. Wards and trains and hospitals where men had been struck from the rolls — deserters who had not deserted, dead men who were not dead, living men erased by clerical convenience or an officer's irritation or the simple arithmetic of paperwork that had run short. Some of the entries ended with *found*. A few ended with *dead — confirmed*. Most ended with nothing at all, an open bracket of ink that waited, sometimes for decades, to be closed.

She had closed many of them now. That was the work of the records office, her quiet empire of ink: restoring names, correcting registers, sending letters to parish councils and pension boards until the forgotten became, on paper at least, remembered again. But one entry had remained open all these years, not because she lacked information but because closing it required something she had not known how to write.

*Private Elias Hollis, 2nd Battalion, struck off as deserter, March 1917.*

She found the line where she had written it, in the dressing station near Ypres, by lamplight, while the man himself lay three cots away shaking with a shaking no one could name. She had recorded him because no one else would. And then she had put him on no ship, given him no berth, watched him wave from the dock at Boulogne beside her while the boat carrying her whole reason pulled away — and afterward she had lost track of him, the way one loses track of everyone in those years, and had spent a decade quietly ashamed of it.

Until the Christmas letter came, and then the dinners, and then the Tuesday visits. Hollis lived now in a halfway house in Deptford run by a Methodist charity, worked three days a week in a timber yard, and had, six weeks ago, done a thing so ordinary it had taken Edmund's breath entirely away: he had walked into a recruiting office — the same chain of offices that had once processed him into the army and then processed him out of existence — and asked for his discharge papers corrected.

They had refused him twice. They had lost his file once, which he told her with a laugh that still had a crack running through it. But Cyrus had written to the War Office on solicitor's paper, and Greer — Matron Greer, retired now to Broadstairs, formidable as ever — had supplied sworn statements from two officers who remembered Hollis being carried from the line rather than running from it, and yesterday morning a buff envelope had arrived at Deptford containing a single typed sheet:

*The record of Private Elias Hollis is amended. His service is acknowledged. He is discharged honourably, to date retrospectively.*

Honourably. Edmund read the word again in the newspaper clipping Cyrus had pinned above the parlour desk. To date retrospectively. As though eight years of erasure could be folded backward and smoothed away by a clerk's stamp. It could not, of course. Nothing could be folded backward. Elias Hollis had lived eight years as a ghost, and the ghost-years were real, and no amendment would return them. But the stamp existed. The name stood in the book again. The war, in this one narrow place, had been made to give back what it took.

Tomorrow they would go together — all three of them — to the timber yard, where Hollis intended to show the paper to Mr. Padgett the foreman, who had never believed his story of desertion but had hired him anyway out of a decency he expressed mainly by grumbling. Then there would be tea somewhere, probably, and Hollis would cry, because Hollis cried easily now, having spent so many years unable to feel anything at all, and neither Edmund nor Cyrus would pretend not to notice.

But tonight was hers. Tonight the ledger.

Through the wall, Cyrus's pen stopped. She heard him stretch, chair legs groaning, and then footsteps, and then he was in the doorway with his shirtsleeves rolled and ink on the heel of his right hand — always the heel of the right hand, the way she had inked hers for twenty years, and she had stopped wondering whether he copied her consciously and accepted it as simply true: some inheritances are property, some are practice, and some are just the shape your hand falls into.

"You've been sitting there half an hour," he said. "You haven't written anything."

"No."

He came and looked over her shoulder, as he had done since he was tall enough, and read the open entry. He was twenty-six now, or nearly — they had settled on a birthday in April, chosen by the Quaker family in York who had raised him, chosen arbitrarily out of kindness, because a child should have a day — and he had the habit of reading silently with his lips moving, which he had never grown out of either.

"Hollis," he said.

"Hollis," she agreed.

"Are you going to close it?"

"I don't know what to write."

This was not strictly true. She knew exactly what to write; the words had been assembling themselves all evening behind her eyes, in the queue of her blood, waiting. What she did not know was whether writing it would be a door closing or a door opening, and she had learned to be careful about doors.

Cyrus pulled out the other chair and sat down across from her. The lamp between them threw their shadows up the wall, two heads leaning toward one page.

"When I was small," he said, "I thought this book was full of dead people. I used to count them. There were a hundred and forty-one when you gave it to me."

"A hundred and forty-three."

"A hundred and forty-one." He smiled. "You missed two in the dark of the boat. I found them later, in the margin, sideways. You'd written them lying down."

She had. She remembered — the deck of the Calais boat, or rather the memory of hearing about it, since she had not been on it; Cyrus had described it to her once, at Charing Cross, in the first ten minutes of their reunion, before either of them had finished weeping: how he had sat below with the satchel clutched to his chest and opened the ledger by the light of a storm lantern because he could not sleep, and how he had understood immediately, even at nine, even wordless with grief, that this was not a list of dead people at all.

"It's full of lost people," he said now, finishing the thought he had begun at nine. "And lost isn't the same. Lost can be undone. That's the whole point of it, isn't it? That's why you kept it. Not to mourn them. To find them."

"Yes," Edmund said. "That's why."

"And Hollis is found."

"Hollis is found."

"Then it's not hard, Mum." He said it without ceremony, the way he said everything that mattered — *Mum*, though she was not his mother, though his mother was a burned face on folded paper and a name spoken once in the dark at Boulogne; he had decided the word at fourteen and would not be argued out of it, and she had long since stopped trying. "It's the easiest entry in the whole book. Everything else you wrote blind, hoping. This one you know."

*Everything else you wrote blind, hoping.* Yes. That was it exactly. A hundred and forty-one names entered in darkness against no evidence but faith — faith that records could be fought, that erasure was not destiny, that somewhere a man erased was eating bread and being somebody. She had written them the way one plants bulbs in November: an act of pure credit against a spring not guaranteed.

But Hollis. Hollis was spring itself, standing in a timber yard in Deptford with sawdust in his hair and a buff envelope in his pocket.

Edmund uncapped her pen.

* * *

The nib moved across the page the way it had moved across a hundred pages before it, and yet the sound was different tonight — or she only noticed it tonight, which amounted to the same thing. That small dry whisper, steel on rag paper. In the dressing station it had sounded like defiance. At Boulogne, like prayer. Here, in a warm kitchen in Kent with her son across the table, it sounded like nothing more dramatic than what it was: a woman putting down the truth while the light was good.

Beneath the old entry — *struck off as deserter, March 1917* — she wrote:

*Restored. Discharge amended, honourable, by order of the War Office, 26th October 1925. Witnessed by E. M. Sterling, formerly QAIMNS, and C. Ashworth-Sterling.*

Then she sat back, and looked at it, and found it insufficient.

It was accurate. Accuracy had been the whole religion of the ledger from the first night — no embellishment, no sentiment, only what could be sworn to. But accuracy alone had never been the point. The point had come to her slowly, over years, in the records office among the grey boxes of misfiled lives: that a restored name is a beginning and not an end, that the state can stamp a word like *honourable* on a page and still leave the man himself standing outside the sentence, holding his cap. The ledger had never been only a corrective instrument. Somewhere in the keeping of it — she could not say when — it had become a home. Not the record of men coming home, but the thing itself: the place where they were known, greeted, counted, kept. The hearth at which every struck-off name had a chair.

So she dipped the pen again, and beneath the witnessed facts she added one line more, breaking her own rule of twenty-two years, permitting herself at last the sentiment she had always refused:

*Private Elias Hollis came home. He is alive, and he is known, and his name will be in this book for as long as anyone keeps it — not as the lost man of March 1917, but as himself: a good soldier, a kind patient, a friend who waved from the dock and stayed to see the end of the story.*

She read it over. Her hand was steady. Her eyes were not, entirely, but that had stopped embarrassing her sometime around the Armistice.

"There," Cyrus said softly.

"There," she agreed.

He reached across and touched the page — not the wet ink but the margin beside it, gently, the way one touches the frame of a picture rather than the paint. Then he said, in the voice he used when he was about to say something he had rehearsed: "When the inquiry closes. When the Ashcombe business is finished and the maid's name is in the record and Vane's people can't appeal it anymore—" he paused, started again. "I've been thinking about the ledger. Where it goes. After."

"After," Edmund said, and the word tasted like the future, which she had learned to chew slowly.

"It should go to an archive eventually. Properly catalogued, available to historians — it's evidence, and it's history, and it shouldn't die with us." He looked up at her, suddenly very young. "But not yet. And not before—" He stopped, and tried a third time, and she watched him fail, and loved him for failing, and reached over and closed her hand over his ink-stained one.

"Not before it's finished," she said.

He exhaled. "Yes. Is it? Finished?"

Edmund looked down the long column of names, upward through the years, past Hollis to the beginning of the book: the first entry, made at a folding table within sound of the guns, a name copied from an erased form by lamplight while a boy slept under a cot nearby and did not yet know he would inherit everything she owned, which was nothing but this.

A hundred and forty-one names. She counted them now in her head, the way Cyrus had once counted them on a boat. One hundred and thirty-seven closed — found, or confirmed dead, or restored, each bracket of ink finally shut. Three remained open, and those three were not failures but work: a lance-corporal last traced to Liverpool, a Canadian private whose file had surfaced in Ottawa only last month, a stretcher-bearer whose family Cyrus had located in Cardiff and who might, if the letters were answered, be home by spring.

Work. Not grief. Work.

"Not quite," she said. "There are three left. But they're not lost, Cy. They're just still walking."

Cyrus laughed — a short, startled laugh, the kind that comes when a weight lifts faster than expected — and then grew serious again, and leaned forward, and turned the ledger gently around to face himself.

"Then leave room," he said, and took out his own pen, and at the bottom of the final page, beneath her entry for Hollis, beneath twenty-two years of her hand, he wrote in his careful upright script the words she would think of, later, as the true close of the book and also its opening:

*Continued in Volume Two.*

* * *

They sat a while longer without speaking, which was a language they had never lost. The fire settled. Somewhere outside a late owl called across the frozen garden, and the frost ticked in the window glass, and Edmund thought of a dressing station eleven years and a lifetime ago, of a knock at the door of the war, of a note in a stranger's hand instructing her to forget a small face — and of how thoroughly, how gloriously, she had disobeyed.

"Right," Cyrus said at last, standing, stretching until his shoulders cracked. "If we're taking Hollis his paper tomorrow, I'm making sandwiches tonight, because you'll insist on leaving at dawn and he'll insist on feeding us anyway, and someone has to hold the line against Mrs. Kettle's seed cake."

"The seed cake is a kindness," Edmund said.

"The seed cake is a weapon." He was already at the pantry, already talking with his hands, and she watched him the way she had watched him all his life: as evidence. Proof of the thing she had staked the ledger on — that what is carried when the orders come is not what fits in a kit bag.

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the table, at the ledger lying open under the lamp with its last page drying.

"Mum," he said. "Read me the first one. Before bed. You always used to."

"You're twenty-six."

"You always used to," he said again, and it was not childishness; she understood that. It was liturgy. It was the ritual by which a family of two survivors and their gathered dead reminded itself what it was.

So when the sandwiches were made and the fire was low and the lamp was turned down to a thumb of gold, Edmund drew the ledger onto her knee, and turned to the first page, and read aloud the first name she had ever saved — the way she had read it to a silent boy on a boat, the way he had read it to himself in orphaned years in Yorkshire, the way they would read it tomorrow to Elias Hollis with his buff envelope and his tears and his seed cake — and the book of the forgotten, nearly closed, breathed on.
