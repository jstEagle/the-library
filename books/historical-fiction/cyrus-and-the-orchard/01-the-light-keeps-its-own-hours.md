## 1. The Light Keeps Its Own Hours

The trouble with a lighthouse, Bram Montrose had long ago decided, was that it made an excellent excuse. Nobody could accuse a keeper of idleness when there were wicks to trim and brass to polish and a lens to wipe down twice daily whether it needed it or not. And so nobody did accuse her, and she went on being industriously alone, which suited her down to the ground—or down to the rock, rather, since the tower stood on exactly eleven acres of it, most of which the sea took back whenever it felt like.

It was the fourth of December, 1893, and the wind had come up from the northwest with the particular grievance of a creditor who has waited too long already. Bram heard it before she opened her eyes: a low moan in the gallery railings, the rattle of the storm shutters she'd meant to oil in October. The light itself had gone out at dawn, as lights do, and lay upstairs sleeping its daylight sleep.

"Up," she told the ceiling. "The world won't drown itself."

She put the kettle on first. This was doctrine. Ewan had taught her—no, Ewan's mother had taught Ewan, who had taught Bram with the solemnity of scripture—that no keeper touched the lamp before tea. A cold hand on cold brass led to cracked lenses or worse, and besides, there was no hurry that a cup of tea wouldn't survive.

The kettle was Ewan's. That is to say, it was a perfectly ordinary kettle of tinned copper, dented at one hip where he'd dropped it in 1887 and sworn, colorfully and at length, that the dent had improved the pour. She had not replaced it. She had not replaced his oilskins hanging behind the door either, nor the stub of pencil he'd left wedged in the logbook's spine, sharpened down to the length of a thumbnail. There was a school of thought—her sister-in-law Verity's school, chiefly—that held a widow ought to clear such things away and make a fresh start. Bram's own school held that a person could make a great many fresh starts without touching a pencil stub, thank you kindly, and that Verity could take her opinions back to Torquay where they presumably kept them warm.

She poured the tea and drank it standing at the north window, watching the sea assemble its argument. Grey on grey, with a seam of white where the reef showed its teeth. In summer the water off Carrick Light went green as bottle glass and you could count the fish. In winter it was a ledger of debts, all of them owed by somebody else.

That was the thing about this coast: history happened elsewhere. It happened in London, where men blew each other up over Irish questions; it happened in Paris, where her cousin's letters spoke of anarchists and motorcars; it even happened in Falmouth, twelve miles south, where the assizes came twice a year trailing scandal like a wet dog. Here there was only weather, and weather was honest. Weather told you what it intended to do to you and then did it. Bram had signed on for that bargain and had never once regretted it—not in nine years of keeping the light with Ewan, and not in the fourteen months since the light had become hers alone.

Fourteen months. She caught herself counting again and stopped, the way you'd stop yourself from pressing a bruise. Grief, in her experience, was less a wound than a lodger: it paid its rent in silence, mostly, and only occasionally rearranged the furniture when she wasn't looking. This morning it had moved the kettle. The pour sounded different without him pretending the dent mattered.

* * *

At half past eight she climbed. One hundred and six steps, worn concave in the middle by a century of feet, hers included. She counted them out of habit rather than necessity; the body knew the way the way a hymn knows itself. At the top, the lantern room sat waiting under its dust sheet of morning, glass fogged with the breath of the night just ended.

She worked while the light slept. Wiped the lens panel by panel with soft linen, checked the clockwork that turned it—the great crystal beehive of it, five hundred pounds of ground glass that weighed nothing at all when it caught the sun. People came sometimes in the summer season and asked if it wasn't lonely work. Bram always said no, because it was true in a way she couldn't explain: the work was company. Brass wanted polishing the way a dog wanted walking. The mechanism wanted winding the way a cat wanted feeding. Everything in the tower asked after her in its small mechanical voice, and she answered, and the days went round like the lens itself, steady, circular, lit from within.

At noon the supply boat *Gannet* came round the headland with flour, oil, coal, and the post. Young Tam Pellow brought it up the winding stair because he liked the exercise, or claimed to, though he paused on step eighty-four every time to get his breath and inspect the masonry.

"Wind's making," Tam said, handing over the parcel. "Harbor master says a gale by Thursday."

"The harbor master says a gale by Thursday every week. He's right often enough that nobody minds."

"And this." Tam produced a letter with the air of a man producing something slightly unpleasant he'd found on his boot. "From Torquay."

Bram looked at it. Her sister-in-law's hand, upright and disapproving even in address. "You may leave it on the table, Tam, and we shall both pretend I haven't seen it until tomorrow."

"That's how I handle my aunt's letters," Tam said seriously. "Only my aunt writes about money."

"Verity writes about money too. She merely dresses it up as concern for my immortal soul." Bram set it face down beside the flour, where it lay like a card played in a losing hand. "Anything from town?"

"Not for you. There's talk, mind." He leaned against the doorframe, enjoying being the bearer of talk. "They're saying Aldous Montrose has been down to Falmouth twice this month, seeing lawyers."

"Aldous sees lawyers the way other men see doctors—for complaints they haven't got yet."

"Well, he's complaining loudly enough now. Something about the family papers. Old ones."

Something turned over in Bram's chest, small and cold as a fish. She ignored it, the way one ignores weather reports: interesting, but far away, and certain to change. "Aldous has been fighting with the family papers since before I married into this family. Tell your harbor master I said Thursday's gale will arrive Wednesday, out of spite."

Tam grinned and clattered down the stairs, leaving Bram alone with the flour, the oil, the coal—and the letter, which she did not look at, and the fish-cold feeling, which she also did not look at, being by now a great expert at not looking at things.

* * *

Here is what Bram believed, and believed firmly, the way some people believe in tides: history belonged to other people.

Her own history was a modest affair. A childhood in a customs house at Penzance, a father who smelled of tar and arithmetic. A marriage conducted mostly at the top of a staircase, in lamplight, with gulls for witnesses. Ewan Montrose had been a tall, kind, ridiculous man who named the gulls (the one he called Admiral lived longest) and who talked to the clockwork and once, memorably, attempted to teach her to waltz on the gallery walk in a flat calm, both of them nearly ending their story three years early against the railing. He had died in October of last year, quietly, in his chair, with the log open on his knee and the pencil stub in his hand, mid-sentence apparently, though the sentence was only a routine entry about wind speed. She had finished the entry herself. She had written *wind moderate* in her own hand beneath his, and then she had closed the book, and in some sense she had never entirely opened anything again.

His family was another matter. The Montroses were old on this coast—old enough to have accumulated the two things old families accumulate: money and things best not discussed. There had been Montrose shipowners, Montrose magistrates, a Montrose mayor of a town that had since declined to remember him. Aldous ran the chandlery and the family's small investments and the family's smaller silences, of which he was head custodian. Verity managed propriety. A third sibling, Clemency, had done the sensible thing years ago and moved to Madeira, where silences presumably kept better in the warmth.

Bram had married into all this with her eyes open and had learned the household grammar quickly: there were things one mentioned and things one did not. Ewan's father's name was a thing one did not mention, though Bram had never learned precisely why, and Ewan, asked directly, would go pleasant and vague as sea fog and begin talking about the weather—which, on that coast, was itself a way of changing the subject. She had let it lie. You did not dig under a working lighthouse. Everyone knew the ground there was hollow.

Since Ewan's death, the siblings had sent the usual cards and the unusual letter—Verity's, twice yearly, proposing that Bram sell the light and come live somewhere *civilized*, a word Verity used the way other people used *dry*. Bram answered politely and did nothing, and the estrangement, if you could call it that—it felt more like two ships passing with lamps showing green to green, all clear, no intention of closing—continued without effort on either side.

And so her days. Trim, wind, wipe, log. Tea at four. Supper at seven. The *Admiral's* descendants squabbling on the rocks below. On clear nights the coast showed her its lights in order—Carrick's sisters strung down the dark like beads on a wire—and she would stand in the gallery and feel what she called peace, though a sharper observer might have called it something with more edges. Quiet isolation, worn smooth by habit, mistaken at last for contentment. A woman can do that for years, mistaking still water for deep rest, when all the while the tide is simply waiting.

* * *

Wednesday came early, as she'd promised Tam, and with it the gale's advance guard. By dusk the sea had gone from grey to the color of wet slate, and the swell was coming in long and deliberate, like a man rolling up his sleeves.

Bram made her rounds with extra care. Storm shutters pinned. Oil topped. The spare wicks laid out on the shelf like soldiers at attention. She ate supper standing up, listening to the building of it, that deepening note underneath the wind that old keepers swore they could hear through the rock itself.

By full dark the light was burning and turning, and she sat below with the log on her knee and wrote: *Wind increasing NNW. Barometer falling fast. Sea high.* Then she added, because the log was the one place her whole self was permitted, *A bad night coming.*

She was wrong only in the direction of her worry. She sat up past midnight watching the beam swing its slow white arc across chaos—out, around, back—and thinking of nothing much, of the dent in the kettle, of whether she'd oiled step ninety's hinge, of ordinary things. History, she believed, happened elsewhere.

Around two in the morning, the storm reached down with one grey finger and began, patiently, to undo the coast. Somewhere out in that black water, something broke loose and started traveling toward her rocks, carrying a sealed letter and thirty-two years of silence, addressed—to her astonishment, when she finally read it—to *the Montrose keepers, whoever keeps the light.*

But that was hours yet. For now there was only the wind, the turning light, and Bram Montrose alone in the tower, keeping her watch over a history that was, she thought, safely elsewhere.
