## 2. What the Tide Brought In

The gale came in on a Tuesday, as the worst of them tended to do, and it stayed through Wednesday out of what Bram Montrose could only assume was spite.

She had weathered eleven years of storms at Harrow Point, and she had a system. Shutters latched before dusk. Lamp trimmed and filled. The logbook open on its chain so the wind couldn't take it—though God knew the wind tried every time, the way a cat tries a door handle, not from need but from principle. By midnight the tower was shuddering like a dog shaking off rain, and Bram sat in the lamp room with her feet propped on the stove, tea gone cold beside her, reading the same paragraph of *Waverley* four times because the light kept flickering and her eyes kept drifting to it anyway.

"You'll not have my flame tonight," she told the glass. "I've nothing else worth taking, but you'll not have that."

The storm, being a storm, did not answer, which was one of the more companionable conversations she'd had lately.

Ewan used to talk through gales. He'd narrate them like races at a fair—"there she goes, that's a big one, mind the south pane"—as though the tower were a horse they owned together and he were its trainer. In the first year after his death, the silence during storms had been unbearable enough that Bram had taken up narrating herself, just to fill the shape of him. Now she mostly didn't. Now the wind was simply the wind, loud and impersonal and honest about wanting to break things, which put it ahead of several people she could name.

By Thursday dawn the sea had spent itself. The sky came up scrubbed and pale, the kind of washed light that made everything look newly invented, and Bram went down the hundred and four steps with her coat buttoned to the throat to see what the night had left her.

Usually it left wreckage. That was the arrangement: the sea took whatever it liked and dumped the receipts on her rocks. Driftwood by the cordful. Half a rowboat once, painted a green so cheerful it seemed rude given the circumstances. Baskets, barrels, one complete ship's figurehead whose serene blank face had watched her haul coal for three years until she'd finally turned it to face the wall. A dead whale in '88, which she did not count among her fond memories, and which had taught the whole coast that even a lighthouse keeper's solitude has limits.

She walked the tide line with her hands in her pockets, boots finding the old rhythm of the stones. Gulls argued over something unmentionable. Far out, the water lay flat and gleaming, wearing an expression of perfect innocence.

"There's no fooling me," she told it. "I know where you were last night."

It was the glint that caught her first—a dull brass buckle, half-buried in wrack at the base of the black rocks below the north gallery. She dug it out with two fingers, expecting the usual: a soldiering kit, a coin purse long since emptied by salt and luck.

Instead it was a satchel. Oilskin, strapped and buckled, the brass fittings crusted green but sound. Waterproof, clearly, and deliberately so—not the accidental survival of some sailor's kit, but a thing built to cross water and mean it. It was heavier than it looked. Bram stood there holding it, and the strangest sensation moved through her, cold entering at the wrist and traveling up past the elbow, past the shoulder, settling somewhere behind the breastbone.

Because it wasn't addressed to nobody. Things that wash ashore are usually addressed to nobody—that's the whole contract of flotsam. This had writing on the flap, ink under wax, legible as a sermon:

*For the keepers of Harrow Point. To be opened only by a Montrose.*

Bram read it three times. The gulls screamed. The sea glittered away, minding its own business, if the sea could be said to have any.

"Well," she said finally. "That's flattering."

She carried it up the cliff path without opening it, which cost her something. Curiosity was not a vice Bram had ever managed to renounce; she'd merely learned, in thirty-odd years of being a woman with opinions in rooms full of men, to fold hers small and put them in a pocket. But this felt different. This felt less like curiosity and more like standing outside a sickroom, hand raised to knock, knowing that whatever waited behind the door had been waiting a long while already and would still be waiting whether she knocked today or next year.

At the top of the tower she set the satchel on the kitchen table, made tea she then forgot to drink, and studied the thing as though patience might change its contents.

The oilskin was good work. Sailor's work, or smuggler's—she supposed those hands overlapped more than anyone in town cared to admit over their Sunday dinners. Someone had wrapped this against the sea on purpose. Someone had believed it would reach the light, or had wanted very much for it to seem that way. And someone had decided that the Montrose name was the key to it, which was a curious choice, because as far as Bram knew, the Montroses held exactly one office on this coast, and she held it alone. Ewan's people were town people now. Aldous ran the chandlery and his sister Prudence ran her late husband's accounts and between them they produced a quantity of opinion about Bram's housekeeping that could have filled a warehouse.

*To be opened only by a Montrose.* She was a Montrose by marriage, which might or might not count. The letter hadn't specified, and neither, apparently, had the sea.

"Oh, stop stalling," she said aloud, and cut the cords with the bread knife.

Inside, wrapped in tarred canvas gone soft as chamois, lay papers. A bill of lading, brittle with age, its dates faded to brown ghosts. A page torn from a larger book, columns of figures in a cramped clerk's hand. And beneath these, folded twice around a lump of hardened wax for sealing, a letter in a strong, slanted script—the handwriting of a man who had learned penmanship as discipline and never afterward as pleasure.

The seal had been pressed with a signet she didn't recognize: a bird, wings up, over crossed lines. She broke it because it was already broken history; the wax crumbled at the touch.

The letter began without greeting.

*If you are reading this, then I am past helping, and the sea has finally done the decent thing by me. My name is Josiah Kell. On the ninth of October, 1861, I piloted a cargo into Harrow Cove for men who paid me well and trusted me poorly. What was in the crates is no matter now. What matters is what happened after.*

*I write to you because your family knows what happened after, and has known for thirty years, and has said nothing.*

Bram's hands went still on the paper. Below the lamp, the wick ticked softly as it burned, the only sound in the tower besides the gulls and her own blood.

*There was a second boat that night. There was always meant to be a second boat—it's how such cargos are done, one vessel to draw the eye of any patrol and another to do the honest dishonest work. Your people arranged it. Your people were paid from it. And when the revenue cutter came faster than expected, when the plan went wrong in the dark, the men your family served chose to save the cargo and let the pilot drown. They took my boat's plug. They watched me go down in the cove with the light of your tower above me—your light, keeper, burning bright enough to read by, while I drowned beneath it.*

*I did not drown. I will spare you the story of how; it is not a kind story. But a man who has been left for dead is owed an accounting, and I have spent thirty years collecting debts, and this is the last of them. Names are in this packet. Dates. Figures. Enough to hang the guilt where it belongs, which is higher up the hill than my poor head ever reached.*

*I am an old man and dying slow, so I am sending the truth to sea instead, addressed to the only address on this coast that cannot move: the light. Let the Montrose keepers read it and reckon with it. Let them decide, as I never got to decide, whether their name is worth more than a man's life.*

*It wasn't. That is my finding, after thirty years. Yours may differ.*

There was no signature beyond the initial *J.K.*, worked into the wax like a brand, and no date newer than the faded bill of lading. Bram laid the pages out on the table in order, smoothing each flat with both hands, and read them through once more, slowly, the way she checked a mechanism—every gear, every tooth.

1861. Nine years before Ewan had come to the light; twenty before she had. The names meant little to her individually—Carrow, Fenwick, a *Montrose* appearing twice in the columns of figures like a bad penny—and yet collectively they meant everything, because the letter did not accuse strangers. It accused the family. Her family, insofar as she had one left. The people whose Christmas tables had seats for everyone but her.

She looked out the lamp room glass at the pale morning sea, flat and bright and innocent as a Sunday school pupil.

All winter she had thought her solitude was the worst thing the tide could bring her. Something to be endured, kept like a candle behind glass, private and small. She had believed history belonged to other people—to magistrates and ministers and men who wrote books—with the same faith she'd once believed Ewan would always come up the steps whistling.

She gathered the papers, folded them back along their old creases, and sat for a long moment with her palms flat on the table.

Then she put on her good coat, took her stick, and started down the cliff path toward town, because there was exactly one person alive who could tell her whether Josiah Kell's letter was madness or memory, and that person had a chandlery on Front Street and a face that had never once managed to lie to her without blinking first.

Aldous Montrose was going to earn his family name this morning, whether he liked it or not.
