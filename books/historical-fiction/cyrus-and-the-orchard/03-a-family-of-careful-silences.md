## 3. A Family of Careful Silences

Bram had not wanted to go to town. Town was a place of opinions, and she had arranged her life carefully to contain as few of them as possible. But the letter had sat on her table for three days, patient as a cat, and she had not slept. A person could forgive a great deal of the sea—drowning, thievery, weather—but she could not forgive it for delivering an argument she could not win alone.

So on Thursday morning she rowed across the harbor, tied up at the stone steps with hands that would not entirely steady, and walked up the hill to Montrose & Sons, Shipping Agents, which was a grand name for what was mostly Aldous, two clerks, and a stove that smoked when it sulked.

Aldous looked up from his ledger when she came in, and his face did something complicated. Bram had married into this family eighteen years ago and had learned to read its faces the way sailors read clouds. What crossed Aldous's now was joy, then guilt at the joy, then fear of the guilt.

"Bram." He stood too quickly and knocked his inkwell, which he caught with a fisherman's reflexes and a merchant's expression of nothing-happened. "What a surprise. Sit down, sit. There's tea—or there will be, if Mrs. Petch hasn't let the kettle die again."

"I don't want tea, Aldous."

"Nobody ever wants tea," he said, pouring it anyway. "That's never stopped anyone from being offered it."

She took the cup because refusing twice felt like cruelty, and sat, and laid the oilskin packet on the desk between them as gently as if it were a dead bird.

The color went out of Aldous's face in stages, like a tide going out over sand.

"Where," he said, and then again, quieter, "where did you get that?"

"It got where I did. On the rocks below the light, after the gale. Addressed—" she turned it so he could read the inscription through the oilskin's fogged window, "—to *the Montrose keepers*."

He didn't touch it. That was the thing she noticed first, and it frightened her more than anything else he might have done. A man confronted with a forgery reaches for it, turns it over, laughs. A man confronted with the truth sits very still and lets his tea go cold.

"You've opened it," he said.

"Of course I've opened it."

"Of course," he agreed faintly. "You always were thorough. Ewan used to say—"

"Don't." She heard her own voice crack on the word and hated it. "Don't tell me what Ewan used to say. Not yet. First you'll tell me whether this is true. The cargo in 'sixty-one. The ship that went aground at Cutter's Point. The man who drowned."

Aldous looked at his window, where the harbor lay gray and innocent, doing business as usual with the horizon.

"Close the door, Bram."

"Aldous—"

"Please. Close the door."

* * *

From the outside, Montrose & Sons appeared to be conducting ordinary commerce. Inside, Aldous Montrose was discovering that a man can keep a secret for thirty years and still not have practiced, even once, how to say it out loud.

He watched Bram close the door and thought, absurdly, of their father teaching them both to sail: *a lie is like a jibe, children—you must commit to it fully or it will take your head off.* Father had been full of such wisdom. Father had also, it developed, been full of contraband.

"Tell me," Bram said, sitting opposite him with her hands folded, patient as stone. Patience was her weapon; it always had been. Ewan had been the quick one, laughing and gone before you noticed your pockets were lighter of worry. Bram stayed. Bram waited. Bram won.

"The firm was founded in fifty-eight," Aldous began, because beginnings were safe. "Father started with two boats and a good reputation, which is the correct order of things. By sixty-one we had four boats and a better reputation, and the war across the water had made some cargoes... valuable."

"Smuggling."

"If you insist on the word. Everyone insisted on the word eventually. It was cloth, mostly, and brandy, and once a shipment of rifles that Father swore he took only because the alternative was letting Halloran's people take it, and Halloran's people were worse men than we were." He heard himself and stopped. "Listen to me. Thirty years and I still say it in Father's voice, with Father's excuses, as though excuses were ballast and kept us upright."

Bram said nothing. The stove sulked. Somewhere below, one of the clerks whistled tunelessly, a young man who did not yet know what his employer's desk contained.

"There was a keeper at the light before Ewan," Aldous said. "Old Tam Osgood. And there was a night in November of sixty-one when a revenue cutter was patrolling, and the shipment was worth more than the boat that carried it, and Father paid Tam to let the light go dark for one hour. Just one hour. The ship would stand off, wait for the dark, run in dark, and be gone by dawn."

"But someone else went dark instead," Bram said quietly.

"No. The light went dark exactly as planned. That was the horror of it. Everything worked." His own voice surprised him—how steady it was, how much practice silence gives a man for speaking. "Except the pilot that night was a man named Josef Kell. Good sailor. Better than any of us. He ran the ship in toward the point in the dark, as arranged—but the arrangement was that a boat would meet him with lamps and guide him over the bar. And the boat wasn't there. Whether Father forgot, or the money ran short, or the men simply lost their nerve—I've never known. The ship struck the outer rocks. Kell went down trying to get clear. The cargo floated ashore in pieces for a week, and every piece said *Montrose* to anyone who cared to read it."

"And no one cared to read it."

"One man did. A revenue officer named Struan. He wrote letters. Asked questions. Father bought the questions off, one by one—it cost nearly everything, Bram, that's the part no one tells you about crime, it's ruinously expensive—and when the money was spent, Struan went away, and we told ourselves the story was over." He rubbed his eyes. "It wasn't over. Stories like that don't end. They just wait for the tide."

Bram set down her untouched tea. "And Ewan knew."

There it was. He had been circling it the whole hour like a gull over a fish, and now there was nowhere left to land but on it.

"Ewan found Father's papers after he died. Eighty-six. We were going through the strongbox together and there they were—the payments to Osgood, the letters from Struan, all of it. Ewan read everything twice. Then he put it back in the box and locked it and said—" Aldous's throat closed briefly around the memory, Ewan at twenty-nine, looking older than his father ever had, "—he said, *the light is ours now, Aldous. It stays lit. Every night. No matter what.* And it has. You've kept it lit. I know what that's been worth."

"Don't." Her voice was low and terrible. "Do not turn my keeping of the light into your family's penance. I kept it because I loved him, and because it was mine, and because it was honest work—not to burn off the sins of a man dead thirty years."

"No," Aldous agreed miserably. "I suppose that doesn't work, does it. It never has."

"He *knew*." She was standing now, though he couldn't remember her rising. "All those years. He knew what his father did, and he married me, and he put me in that lighthouse, and he never said one word."

"He was protecting you."

"He was protecting *you*." 

"Yes," Aldous said, because lying to Bram Yarrow had never once worked, "both things. Mostly that. Bram—listen to me." He came around the desk, and the letter sat between them on the wood like a third person at a funeral. "What names are in it? Whose hand?"

"I don't know whose hand. It names the Montrose firm. It names the night. It says a man was left to drown and that the ones responsible are known, and it asks whoever finds it to see justice done." She paused. "It calls itself a confession, Aldous. Someone wrote this intending it to be found."

"Then it's old paper and old grief, and it can stay lost. Burn it." He heard the plea come out of him naked and undignified and did not care. "Bram. Please. There's nothing in it but ruin. The men who did it are dead or dying. Burning it hurts no living soul."

"Doesn't it?" she asked, and he could not answer.

Because here was the arithmetic he had done a thousand nights and never solved: the secret had already cost one life and two reputations and every easy night's sleep he'd had since eighty-six. But telling it might cost more—might reach past the guilty to the merely related, might strip his sons of a name, might make Bram's home a scandal and her widowhood a courtroom. Loyalty said burn it. Truth said burn it and be damned either way. He was fifty-eight years old and he discovered he had no more idea what was right than he'd had at nineteen, standing on a dark quay while his father counted crates.

"Bram," he said, "I'm asking you. As what's left of Ewan's family. As the only family you have. Don't do anything with that letter. Not today. Not this week. Let an old man sleep a few more nights before the world ends."

Something moved behind her eyes—pity, maybe, which was worse than anger—and she picked up the packet and put it back inside her coat.

"I'll keep it safe," she said. "That's all I'll promise. Keeping things safe is what I do."

"That's what frightens me," Aldous said, and managed something like a smile. "You're the only Montrose-adjacent person alive who actually does it."

She went to the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"One more question. The drowned man. Kell. Did he have family?"

Aldous closed his ledger, though there was nothing in it for this anymore.

"A wife," he said. "And a daughter."

"How old was the daughter?"

"Born in fifty-nine." He did the arithmetic she was already doing, and felt the floor tilt under his chair. "She'd be thirty-four now."

Bram nodded once, the way she acknowledged weather, and was gone. Aldous stood a long moment in the smoky warmth of his office, listening to her boots fade down the stair, and then he took a fresh sheet of paper, and dipped his pen, and for the first time in thirty years began to write down the truth—because if it was coming for his family anyway, someone in this cursed firm ought to have the story straight.

He had written three lines when the clerk knocked to say there was a woman downstairs asking about supply contracts for the winter, new to the district, name of Kell.
