## 4. The Drowned Man's Daughter

Maren Kell had three rules for lying, learned over eleven years of asking questions that made men reach for their coats and their consciences at once.

The first rule: never lie about anything you could avoid. A liar's burden lightens when most of what she says is true. The second: give people a version of yourself they can be useful to, because vanity is the softest door in any house. And the third—learned the hard way, in a chandler's office in Hull with a man's hand around her wrist—never let them see you counting.

She stepped off the mail coach at Harrow Point with a sample case of lamp oil and tallow, a forged letter of introduction from *Kell & Sons, Marine Suppliers, Whitby*, and a face arranged into mild commercial interest. Kell & Sons did not exist. Her father had been a fisherman, not a merchant, and he had been dead for thirty-two years, drowned within sight of the Montrose light on a night the record books insisted was clear as glass.

She had come to see whether the light itself remembered otherwise.

The town was small enough to cross twice before breakfast. She bought a meat pie from a woman who talked the entire time without once drawing breath, and by the end of it Maren knew three things: that the winter had been wicked, that the lighthouse keeper was "the widow Montrose, poor thing, though she never says so," and that the Montrose family of Ashcombe House had money the way a drowned man has lungs—technically present, doing no one any good.

"They don't mix," the pie woman confided, pressing a second pie on her unasked. "Not even at Christmas. Old Aldous comes down for the market like clockwork and looks through everyone as if we were shop windows. And the keeper—" she lowered her voice with the tenderness of gossip, "—the keeper only comes for flour and lamp wick, and she thanks you for your trouble every time, which I find unsettling in a Montrose."

*So do I,* Maren thought, and ate her pie.

* * *

Her first sight of Bram Montrose was at the harbor stairs, two days later, in a wind that tried its best to take her hat and nearly succeeded with both of theirs.

Maren had been warned to expect a grim woman. What she found instead was someone tall and weather-colored, hair escaping its pins in silver streaks, arguing gently with a boatman about the price of rope as if the argument were a game they'd been playing for years and neither wished to win. The boatman won. Bram paid him anyway, and smiled—not widely, but truly—and something in Maren's chest shifted like ballast.

*Careful,* she told herself. Rule three. But there was no rule for this, the sudden stupid ache of watching a stranger be kind to a boatman.

"You'll be the oil woman." Bram turned and looked at her directly, which took some doing; most people looked near her. "Mrs.—"

"Kell. Miss Maren Kell, of Kell & Sons." True name, false trade. It was the arrangement that kept her honest-ish. "I'm told the Point light burns colza."

"Burns whatever I feed it." Bram held out a hand rough as wharf timber. Her grip was brief and exact. "Bram Montrose. If you've come all this way to sell me oil, you've chosen an ugly month for commerce."

"I chose the month the rates were best."

Something flickered across the keeper's face—amusement, quickly banked. "A woman after my own arithmetic. Walk up with me, then. The path's foul but short, and I'll make you tea that tastes of regret and salt, and you can tell me why your prices are better than the Whitby firm's, which I doubt but am willing to hear."

The tower stood white against a sky the color of dishwater. Inside it smelled of brass polish and paraffin and, beneath everything, the deep cold mineral smell of stone that has stood in one place being hammered by the sea for a century. Maren had been inside a dozen lighthouses in her years of hunting. This was the first that felt inhabited rather than maintained—there were books on the stair landings, a chipped blue mug on the watch desk, a cardigan hung over a chair as though a chair could catch cold.

And everywhere, quietly, Ewan. Not in photographs—she saw none—but in the way two mugs sat by the kettle, and the way one side of the bed in the little room below was turned back and never slept in.

"So," Bram said, pouring tea that did indeed taste of regret and salt. "Your prices."

They dickered for an hour. It was, Maren reflected with some alarm, the most pleasant hour she'd spent in a year. Bram haggled like a person who had nothing else to do and found the exercise restful, and between rounds of it she asked questions that seemed idle and were not: Where was Kell & Sons based? How long in trade? Had Maren known the coast long?

"My father worked these waters," Maren said, and stopped, because the truth was a cliff edge and she had walked to the lip of it. "Years ago. I don't remember much."

"No," Bram said, looking into her cup. "One forgets."

But her voice had gone quiet in a way that said she forgot nothing, and Maren understood, with the small cold click of a lock turning, that the keeper of the light was also keeping something else. Two lonely women, circling. One wound, perhaps, or wounds shaped alike.

* * *

Over the following fortnight, Maren invented reasons to stay.

It wasn't difficult. The supply order needed confirming; the weather closed the roads; a crate arrived damaged and must be inspected. Bram accepted each extension with a dry look that suggested she knew exactly how thin the excuses were and had decided not to mind them. On the fourth visit she said, "You may as well leave your sample case here. You carry it about like a penance," and Maren left it, and felt oddly lighter.

They fell into a pattern. Maren would climb the path in the afternoon; they would drink tea; Bram would show her some small machinery of the light—the clockwork that turned the lens, the weights that ran down like patience and were cranked up again. Once, teaching her to trim a wick, Bram put her hands over Maren's for a moment to correct the angle, and neither of them mentioned it afterward, and neither of them forgot it.

"You're good at this," Maren said.

"I'm good at sitting still and minding fire. It's not a talent, it's a temperament." Bram trimmed the second wick herself. "My husband loved this place more than he loved me, I think. Or loved me in the same way, which amounts to it. He used to say the light didn't belong to us. We belonged to it."

"And do you?"

Bram considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, as if the question might be settled by evidence. "I mind the fire," she said at last. "That's different from belonging."

Maren wanted to say: *your husband's ledger, if he kept one, would show a payment in the spring of 1861.* She wanted to say: *my father's name was Jonas Kell, and there is a grave with no body in it at Fennwick, and a coroner's verdict of accidental drowning on a night so calm the fishermen still curse it.* She had rehearsed the speech on the mail coach, on the pier, in front of inn mirrors. Eleven years of rehearsal.

Instead she asked about the garden, and Bram described her losing war with the rabbits, and laughed—an unguarded sound that transformed her whole face—and Maren went home that evening furious at herself, and came back anyway two days later.

Because here was the thing no rule covered: she liked her. Liked the dry humor and the careful courtesies, liked the way Bram thanked the kettle. Every day of friendship was another day of fraud, and every day of friendship made the eventual confession heavier, until Maren carried it up the cliff path like contraband.

*Soon,* she promised herself. *When I have proof. When I know which Montrose gave the order and which ones merely kept quiet.*

She should have known better. Truth, in her experience, never waited to be convenient. It arrived the way weather arrived at Harrow Point—all at once, and sideways.

* * *

On a grey Thursday in late February, Maren climbed the path and found the tower's door standing open to the wind and Bram at the foot of the stairs, very still, holding a leather satchel as though it were something recently dead.

"There you are," Bram said, and her voice was wrong—flat and distant, a voice speaking from the far end of a long room. "I half expected you today. Isn't that strange? The tide brought something in after the gale, and my first thought was to send for you."

Maren's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth. "Send for me? I'm an oil merchant, Mrs. Montrose."

"Yes," Bram said. "Of course." She looked down at the satchel, then up, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman doing sums she did not like. "It's a letter. Sealed, waterproofed, thirty years old if it's a day. Addressed to *the Montrose keepers*. Do you know, in fourteen years here I have never once received a letter worth the paper."

She said it lightly. Nothing else about her was light. She climbed one step, paused, and looked back at Maren with an expression caught somewhere between courtesy and appeal—as if she wanted, badly, to hand the dread to someone else for an hour and did not know yet whom she could trust.

"The kettle's on," Bram said. "Stay while I open it? I find I don't want to read it alone, and I can't think why I'm telling you that, except that you've the same look about you I see in my own mirror. Someone who's waited a long time for bad news."

The wind pushed through the open door, cold and salt and inevitable. Maren stood on the threshold with her lies in her mouth and her father's name in her chest, and understood that whatever came next, the circling was over.

"Yes," she said, closing the door behind her. "I'll stay."
