## 5. Ewan's Handwriting

The letter had lived three days in the bread tin, and by the fourth morning Bram could no longer pretend it wasn't watching her.

She had moved it twice—first to the drawer with the good candles, then to the cupboard above the stove where she kept the medicines and the mustard plasters—and both times she had found herself standing before its hiding place like a woman checking on a sleeping child she didn't entirely trust. The tin was tin. The letter was paper. Neither of them could see her. And yet.

"This is absurd," she told the kettle, which agreed with a small, civilized whistle. "I am a grown woman of forty-one years. I have survived two shipwrecks, one epidemic of typhoid, and eleven winters in this house without once being murdered by my own correspondence."

The kettle had no rebuttal. It never did. That was rather why she liked it.

She poured her tea and sat at the kitchen table where the morning light came through salt-hazed glass in long grey panels, and she admitted to the room what she had been refusing to admit since the tide gave up its secret: she could not burn a letter she had not properly read, and she could not properly read it until she knew whether Ewan had left anything behind that would confirm or condemn it. A letter, after all, was only a stranger's voice. But a ledger—that was arithmetic. Arithmetic did not weep and it did not lie, or if it lied, it lied in columns you could catch.

Ewan's effects were in the room at the top of the tower stairs that they had never formally named, because naming it *his study* would have meant admitting he needed one, and naming it *the spare room* would have meant admitting nobody would ever sleep in it. So it remained *the room upstairs*, which is what they had both called it for nineteen years, and which is what she called it still, out loud, to keep the habit of him alive in her mouth.

It was also, she realized now, a room she had not truly searched. She had dusted it. She had washed his windows and folded his spare jumpers into cedar chests and mourned over his pipe rack with the undignified thoroughness of any widow worth her weeds. But mourning and searching are different trades. Mourning touches everything and sees nothing. Searching sees everything and touches it twice.

She took her tea upstairs.

* * *

The room smelled of him—or of what she had decided was him: tobacco, lamp oil, the particular wool of Shetland jumpers. She opened the desk first, on the theory that honest men keep their papers where their wives can find them, and Ewan had been the most honest man on this coast, or so she had told the vicar, the magistrate's clerk, and at least four visiting inspectors of lights.

The desk yielded nothing but tide tables, a broken magnifying glass, receipts for lamp oil going back nine years (filed, God help her, alphabetically), and a letter from the Board of Trade thanking him for his punctuality. Bram read the Board's thanks twice and felt her throat tighten in a way that annoyed her. Nineteen years, and the man could still make her cry with someone else's stationery.

"Right," she said. "You were too honest to file things honestly. Where would you put something you wanted lost?"

She knew the answer before she finished asking it, because she had known the man. Ewan Montrose hid things the way the sea hides things: not by burying them deep, but by putting them somewhere so obvious and so dull that no one bothered to look. His spare key had hung on a nail labeled *rope*. Their emergency sovereigns had lived for years in a biscuit tin marked *BOLTS*. Once, when the excise men had come asking questions about a Danish brig that had stood too long off the point, Ewan had handed over his logbook with such cheerful readiness that Bram had teased him about it for a week—"You showed them the book like a proud father, love"—and he had laughed and said, "Best place to hide a page is inside a book nobody reads twice."

She had thought he meant the logbook itself.

She went to the shelf. His logbooks ran back twenty-three years, spines cracked, covers salt-stained, arranged by year with the loving pedantry that had made him insufferable at inspection time. She pulled 1861.

She had read that volume before, of course. In the first raw months after his death she had read all of them, hungrily, looking for his handwriting the way other widows looked for photographs. October 1861 said what every keeper's log in the country said: weather, wind, lamps trimmed, wicks dressed, nothing unusual. His hand marched across the pages, neat and upright as fence posts.

But now she held the book to the window light.

There—a page slightly thicker than its neighbors. A faint ghosting where something had been pasted down and pressed flat. She had found the same trick in a smuggler's ballad once, in a penny dreadful she'd bought at a fair: pages glued face to face to smuggle a whole chapter past the eyes of customs officers' wives. Ewan had known it too. He had probably learned it from the same sort of literature, the kind he'd pretended to disapprove of while reading over her shoulder.

She fetched his penknife—the one with the whalebone handle, the one she'd been unable to give away—and eased the blade along the seam.

The page came free in two halves, and between them lay a folded sheet, thin as onion skin, covered in his writing.

Not log-writing. Not fence-post neatness. This was his other hand, the quick slanted script of his letters to her during their courtship, the hand that had written *I count the days badly but I count them constantly.* Her heart did something complicated and stupid at the sight of it, and then she began to read, and her heart shut up and went cold.

It was a ledger. Small entries, dates, sums.

*Oct. 9, 1861 — rec'd from A.M., £12 — trim wicks short, note fog.*

*Oct. 11 — rec'd £8 — oil 'spoiled,' report delay.*

*Oct. 14 — rec'd £25 — DARK, full night. No flare. Done quiet.*

And beneath, in smaller letters, almost apologetic, as though the pen itself had tried to shrink:

*A.M. says cargo only. Says no souls aboard. God forgive me if he lies.*

The ninth of October, 1861. The same date the drowned-letter had given. The night the *Cormorant's Pride* went onto the Black Teeth with all hands, and a man named Jonas Kell was never recovered, and the inquiry concluded—she remembered the old men still repeating it in the taproom—that the light had failed through no fault of the keeper, these things happened, terrible luck, poor souls.

No fault of the keeper. Twenty-five pounds' worth of no fault.

Bram set the ledger down very carefully on the desk, the way one sets down a thing that has become suddenly heavy with meaning rather than weight, and she stood at Ewan's window and looked out at the sea, which went on glittering and heaving and behaving exactly as if nothing had happened, the great unconcerned brute.

He had turned the light off. Her Ewan, who had once gone out in a January gale with a rope around his waist to rescue a fisherman everyone else had given up on; who had taught her the trade with the solemnity of scripture—"the light is a promise, Bram, and we are the people who keep promises"; who had died in the snow climbing the tower stairs to relieve her watch because he wouldn't let her stand a double shift. That man had taken twenty-five pounds to darken a promise and let thirty souls drive onto rocks in the dark.

And then—this was the part that made her sit down slowly on his chair, the part she would turn over for the rest of her life—he had written *God forgive me if he lies.* He had known. Some part of him had known, even that night, and he had done it anyway, and then he had kept the proof. He hadn't burned it. For thirty years he had carried the evidence of his own guilt folded inside a book he touched every day of his working life. Perhaps that was penance. Perhaps it was cowardice. Probably, she thought, with the terrible fairness she could never quite abandon, it was both, worn smooth together like sea glass.

"You fool," she said softly, to the empty room, to the pipe rack, to the jumpers in their cedar chest. "You dear, brave, stupid fool."

Because here was the thing she could not stop seeing: the letter in the bread tin accused *the Montrose keepers*, plural, generations. But this ledger accused only one. Whatever Aldous's family had done—and she believed now that Aldous had begged her to burn the letter for reasons far older than grief—the hand on this page was Ewan's alone. A.M. might be Aldous; might be Aldous's father; but the dark night, the twenty-five pounds, the shame—that was hers now. Hers by marriage, hers by inheritance, hers the way the lighthouse was hers: kept whether she wanted it or not.

She should burn it. That was what a loyal widow did. Aldous had all but knelt in the street begging her to burn the letter; how much more would he beg for this? And who would ever know? The sea keeps no records. The dead cannot testify.

Except that they can, she thought. They testify constantly. They testify in handwriting.

She wrapped the ledger page around its hiding page, folded them together, and sat holding them, hearing the gulls argue outside and the clock downstairs clearing its throat, and she understood with sudden clarity that she could not decide this alone—not because she lacked courage, but because the page mentioned a name she couldn't read alone. A.M. Aldous? Aldous's father? And there had been another name in the satchel letter, half legible through water damage, a name belonging to whoever had organized the cargo and the payment and the drowning—

A knock at the door below. Firm, unfamiliar, twice.

Bram slid the pages into her bodice, blew out the candle she had not needed to light, and went down, her heart keeping time with her boots on the spiral stair.

Through the small window beside the door she could see a young woman on her step, travel-worn, dark-eyed, with a supply merchant's sample case held like a shield—and behind her, far down the lane, a gentleman's carriage that no supply merchant could afford, standing empty in the wind.
