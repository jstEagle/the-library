## 6. The Magistrate's Patience

Aldous Montrose had three great talents in life: he could appraise a horse at forty paces, he could tell a convincing lie to a customs man, and he could always, always find the exact moment a thing began to go wrong. It was the third talent that woke him at half past four on a Tuesday morning in February, sitting bolt upright in bed with the certain knowledge that the world had shifted an inch to the left while he slept.

He lay still and listened. Rain on the shutters. His wife Cressida breathing the slow, dignified breaths of a woman who would deny having heard anything even while awake. The clock downstairs clearing its throat.

Nothing was wrong yet. But something *would* be, because Aldous had spent thirty years cultivating the particular peace of a man with nothing in writing, and last week he had watched his sister-in-law walk out of his office with a sealed letter in her coat pocket like a woman carrying lit gunpowder through a dry hayfield.

"She's going to read it," he told Cressida's sleeping shoulder. "Of course she's going to read it. Bram reads everything. She reads the shipping notices for pleasure."

Cressida breathed on, unbothered. This was why the marriage worked.

* * *

The trouble with being a Montrose, Aldous had long ago concluded, was that everyone assumed you were either a villain or a saint, when in fact most of you were simply tired men with ledgers. He had not drowned anyone. He wanted this understood, by history, by any future magistrate, and especially by himself: Aldous Montrose had never drowned so much as a kitten. What he had done was smaller and sadder — he had signed things. He had looked away from other men signing things. He had accepted a share of profits that arrived with the particular silence of money that does not wish to be asked about, and he had told himself, at nineteen, at twenty-five, at thirty-eight, and again only last autumn, that the past was a country one could renounce citizenship in.

His father had believed otherwise. Old Corin Montrose had gone to his grave still keeping accounts of a single night in 1861 as though God were auditing them personally. "There is a debt," the old man used to say, tapping the table with two fingers, tap-tap, until Aldous wanted to scream. "Not in coin. In *attention*. Someone must keep watch over it."

Well. Aldous had kept no watch. And now the tide had delivered the whole rotten account to a lighthouse keeper who thought history belonged to other people, and it turned out history had merely been waiting politely on the rocks like a parcel.

* * *

Magistrate Harrow arrived at eleven, which was early for Harrow, who considered mornings a rumor spread by farmers. Aldous found him in the front parlor of the Montrose offices, examining the family portrait above the mantel with the expression of a man pricing a horse he intended to buy cheaply.

"Aldous." Harrow did not turn around. "Your father had a good face. Honest, I'd have said."

"You'd have been the first." Aldous poured two glasses of the decent sherry, kept for lawyers and clergy, and offered one over. "What brings you out before noon? Has the sun reversed?"

Harrow took the glass but didn't drink. That was the first bad sign. The second was that he sat down without being asked. Magistrates who sit down uninvited are settling in; magistrates who remain standing are performing. Harrow was settling in.

"I've had a letter," Harrow said.

"Congratulations. I get several a week. Mostly from a widow in Penzance who believes her late husband communicates through the price of mackerel."

"A letter concerning the wreck of the *Kestrel*."

Aldous set down his own glass, carefully, because his hand had developed opinions. The *Kestrel*. Even the name after all these years had a taste — brine and lamp oil and something copper underneath. "That was sixty years ago if it was a day. Before my time entirely."

"Mm," said Harrow. "Before your time. And yet." He drew from his breast pocket a paper, unfolded it with ceremony, and laid it on the low table between them like a card played in a slow game. "A petition. From a woman named Kell. Daughter of Tomas Kell, boatswain, lost off the Montrose light in November of 1861. She wants the file reopened. She has, and I quote her solicitor, 'correspondence in the possession of the Montrose family confirming the same.'"

The room went very quiet. Rain, clock, the small internal roar of a man's blood deciding where to be useful.

"That can't be right," Aldous said. "No such correspondence exists."

"Then she's invented it," Harrow said comfortably, "which would make her a liar rather than a threat, except that liars don't usually know the ship's name, the cargo manifest discrepancies, or — and this bit I enjoyed — the fact that the lighthouse log for the relevant night shows a gap of four hours, later amended in different ink. Somebody has done her reading, Aldous. Somebody with access to Montrose papers." He finally drank. "Or Montrose *keepers*, as our late correspondent appears to have phrased it."

"You've seen the letter." Aldous's voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "You've seen it yourself."

"I've seen a copy." Harrow smiled, and there it was, the thing beneath the thing, surfacing at last like a body after three days. Harrow owed money. Everyone knew Harrow owed money — cards, mostly, and a mortgage dressed up as an investment in a tin mine that existed mainly in the mine promoter's enthusiasm. A magistrate with debts is a door left unlatched. "I've seen a copy, Aldous, and I will confess the copy found its way to me by channels I couldn't properly document, which means I am currently the only officer of the court aware of its contents. Currently. A situation with a natural tendency to change."

Aldous looked at his father's portrait. Honest face. Tap-tap on the table. *Someone must keep watch.*

"How much?" he said.

"Now, that's an ugly word between friends."

"It's the only word you came for. How much?"

* * *

They settled, in the way of such settlements, on a figure that was both ruinous and insufficient — ruinous now, insufficient eventually, since men like Harrow do not eat once and depart. Aldous wrote the draft with a steady hand and felt the steadiness itself become evidence against him: *there*, said his own calm signature, *a man signing away money to a magistrate is a man purchasing something, and men purchase only what they fear to lose.*

He walked Harrow out into the rain and stood under the awning watching him go, umbrella bobbing down the street like a self-satisfied jellyfish, and it was there, in the wet doorway of his own respectable offices, that Aldous felt the old fear finish its slow work and curdle into something harder and colder. Fear makes a man freeze. This was not freezing. This was arithmetic.

If the Kell woman's petition reached the assizes, the file would open, and the file contained his grandfather's initials beside a payment dated the week after the drowning. If the letter in Bram's pocket surfaced, it named the family plainly. And if Harrow came back hungry — and Harrow would come back hungrier each time, debts are patient that way — then Aldous would spend the rest of his life feeding him, quarter by quarter, until the Montrose name was eaten down to the bone anyway.

Whereas if the letter burned, and if a certain widow's testimony never quite materialized, and if a certain petitioner's proof stayed lost at sea where it had so nearly stayed these thirty years —

He caught the thought and held it up to the light like a suspect coin. He was surprised how little it weighed. All his life he'd imagined himself incapable of the family sins, a mild man in a fierce line, and here he stood in the rain doing sums in which living women figured as costs.

"Not murder," he said aloud, to no one, needing the distinction recorded somewhere. "Just... obstruction. Silence management."

The rain declined to comment.

* * *

Cressida was sorting invoices when he came back in. She looked up, took one look at his face, and set down her pen — which from Cressida was the equivalent of another woman fainting.

"What have you done?"

"Paid a man to be patient."

"Those payments never end, Aldous. Papa-in-law taught us that much." She pushed a telegram across the desk toward him, her movement oddly careful. "This came an hour ago. From your sister Verity, in Falmouth. About Bram."

He opened it. Nine words. He read them twice, because the first time they refused to be true:

*MAREN KELL IN THE BAY. SHE KNOWS YOUR NAME.*

"Well," Aldous said, and sat down slowly in his own chair like a man learning the chair's true purpose. "She doesn't know my name. Nobody knows my name. My name is the least guilty name in this entire county—"

"Aldous."

"—which is precisely," he continued, with the dawning clarity of a man watching his last exit close, "why I must go and see her. Today. Before the tide, before the magistrate's second appetite, before—" He stopped. Straightened his cuffs. Discovered his hands were shaking anyway.

Cressida rose and fetched his good coat from the peg, holding it out behind him like a valet, like an accomplice, like a wife. "Say something true to her," she said quietly. "For once in the family's history. Just one true thing. See what it buys."

He kissed her cheek, took the coat, and went out into the rain toward the harbor, where the morning boat was preparing to make for the light, and where — though he could not have said how he knew it — the hour for buying silence had just closed forever, and a far dearer market was opening in its place.

Behind him, in the office safe, the second copy of the letter he swore he had destroyed lay exactly where he had hidden it from himself, because some part of Aldous Montrose, even now, even terrified, could not bring itself to burn the only honest paper his family had produced in thirty years.
