## 7. Storm Glass

Maren Kell had lied to a great many people in her thirty-one years—harbormasters, landladies, one unfortunate curate in Hull—but she had never yet managed to lie to herself, and so she knew, climbing the lighthouse path with the wind shoving at her back like an impatient hand, that the storm was only partly the reason she had come.

The real reason was standing at the top of the tower.

Bram Montrose had taken to leaving the door unlatched for her. Maren had noticed it two visits ago: the iron latch hanging open, no bolt thrown. A small carelessness, most people would call it. But Bram Montrose did nothing carelessly. She trimmed her wicks to the millimeter and wound her clockwork on the hour and scrubbed her lens until it could have shaved by. An unlatched door was not carelessness. It was an invitation written in the only language a solitary woman trusted herself to speak.

"Supply buyer calls twice in one week," Bram said when Maren came into the lamp room, cheeks scoured red, hair escaping its pins in ropes. "People will talk."

"People will talk regardless. I'd rather give them something accurate." Maren set down the crate of lamp oil she'd carried up as pretext—it was good oil; she would hate for the lie to be entirely wasted—and warmed her hands at the little stove. "Your glass is falling, Mrs. Montrose."

"It's been falling since Tuesday. It can fall all it likes; the light goes up either way." Bram glanced out the seaward panes, where the horizon had gone the color of wet pewter. "You shouldn't be on the road when it breaks."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "I suppose I'll have to stay."

Bram looked at her then—a long look, steady as the beam itself—and something in her face suggested she had known precisely how this conversation would go from the moment the door had failed to lock.

"There's tea," Bram said. "And there's a chair that doesn't wobble, if you're quick about it. Sorcha claims both otherwise."

Sorcha, the enormous gray cat currently occupying the non-wobbling chair, opened one eye to confirm this policy.

* * *

They sat below, in the round stone room that served Bram for everything—kitchen, parlor, study—with the wind arriving in great shoulder-shoves against the tower and the stove ticking like a patient heart. Maren wrapped her hands around her cup and understood that she had perhaps twenty minutes of cowardice left in her, and that after that the storm would make the decision for her, because storms have always been on the side of truth. Ask any drowned man.

"Mrs. Montrose," she said. "I need to tell you who I am, and I need you to let me finish before you decide what to do about it."

Bram set down her cup. She didn't spill a drop. Maren would later think that was the thing that steadied her—the sheer competence of the woman, the way she met even bad news with the posture of someone prepared to trim its wick.

"I'm not a supply buyer," Maren said. "I've never sold a tin of anything in my life. My name is Maren Kell. My father was Jonas Kell, second mate of the *Merrow's Fortune*, out of Penhallow, and on the night of the ninth of November, 1861, he went into the water off this coast and did not come out." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "He was thirty-four years old. I was born four months after they buried the empty box."

The wind filled the silence, and then Bram filled it instead.

"You said your father was a cooper."

"My father was whatever the story required that week. He was a schoolmaster in Whitby. He was a widower with a nice line in melancholy. I've been inventing him for years—it seemed only fair. Everyone else got to invent him too." She heard the edge come into her voice and did not entirely try to file it off. "Except whoever left that letter on your rocks. They told the truth about him. Someone finally told the truth."

Bram rose, crossed to the narrow shelf where she kept her accounts, and took down the satchel. She set it on the table between them without ceremony, the way one sets down a debt.

"I've read it eleven times," Bram said. "I know it by heart now, every word. And I have spent three weeks telling myself it was some crank's fancy, some old grievance dressed up as confession, because the alternative—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully. "Because the alternative has my family's name on it. Has my husband's name near it."

"May I ask you something?" Maren said. "When you read it. When you read the part about the light."

"The part where the light was dark when it should have burned." Bram's jaw tightened. "Yes. That part."

"What did you feel? Truly."

Bram considered the question with the seriousness she seemed to bring to everything, as though feelings were cargo to be inventoried before acceptance.

"Grief," she said at last. "But not the clean kind. The kind that's been sitting in a barrel too long and comes out tasting of something else entirely." She looked at Maren across the table, and Maren watched her arrive at the understanding she must have been circling for weeks. "You've felt it too. Thirty years of it. You came all this way carrying yours."

"I came," Maren said, "because everyone else who might tell me the truth is dead or lying, and I couldn't bear another winter of being the drowned man's daughter in a town that had decided to forget him. I came because I wanted one person to say his name back to me."

"Jonas Kell," Bram said quietly. Simply. As though it were a fact that deserved daylight.

Maren had braced for many things on this long errand. She had not braced for that, and it undid her entirely. She wept the way one does when a fever breaks—ugly, grateful, unceremonious—and Bram Montrose did not fuss or flutter, merely pushed the teapot closer and sat with her in the lamplight while the storm arrived overhead like a judgment, and Maren thought: *oh*. This was the thing she had actually crossed the country for. Not proof. Witness.

* * *

It was Maren who asked about the ledger, once she could speak again.

"Your letter says the keeper was paid. If my father's death was arranged—if that light was dark on purpose—someone kept books. Men like that always do. Sin loves a column of figures; it makes it look like commerce."

Something shifted in Bram's face. Maren recognized it because she'd seen it in her own mirror for years: the expression of a person who has found a thing and is deciding whether to show it.

"Ewan's ledger," Bram said. "I found it in his sea chest. Hidden under the false bottom, which Ewan used for—" She stopped, and her mouth did something complicated. "Which I had always assumed was for love letters. I preferred it as love letters." She fetched it from the bedroom: a plain marbled book, salt-stained, the sort any keeper might use for stores and hours. "It looks ordinary. That's rather the point of it, I think."

Maren turned the pages by stove-light while the wind tried the shutters. At first it was innocent enough—oil, rope, flour, the mundane liturgy of a lighthouse household. Then, interleaved among the honest entries, dates. Single dates, standing alone, each followed by a small sum and a symbol like a fishhook.

"Ninth of November 1861," Maren breathed. "Here. Forty pounds. And the hook mark."

"And here—" Bram leaned over her shoulder, close enough that Maren could smell lamp oil and wool, "—the same hook mark, 1863, and again in '67, and '71. Payments, not payments out. Money coming *in*, on those dates, regular as rent."

"Not rent," Maren said slowly, the shape of it assembling itself. "Retainers. You pay a retainer to keep a service available." She looked up. "Mrs. Montrose. Your husband wasn't paid once, in 1861, for one dark night. He was paid for thirty years. He was the family's insurance policy. Any time a ship needed to go missing—or a light needed to go blind—there was a man on this coast who could arrange it, and a ledger proving he had."

The cat, sensing weather in the room, relocated to Bram's lap. Bram's hands rested on the gray fur, perfectly still.

"He kept the accounts," Bram said. Her voice was level in the way of ice on a pond. "That means he knew the names above him. Whoever paid him. Whoever gave the orders. A man that careful wouldn't record sums without recording sources." She drew the ledger back and began turning pages toward the front, faster now, hunting. And there—folded between two leaves—a slip of paper, older, in a different hand. Maren leaned in.

Five names. Four struck through with single decisive lines, like headsman's strokes. One remaining.

"Aldous Montrose," Bram read aloud, and laughed—one short, terrible note. "His own brother kept a list, and Aldous's name is the last one living on it." She traced the struck names with her finger. "These others. Dead, all of them?"

"If your letter's author told the truth, yes. Dead or transported or vanished." Maren's mind was moving fast now, cold and clear, the way it did when grief put on its working clothes. "But a name alone isn't flight, Mrs. Montrose. Why would Aldous run? Unless—"

"Unless he knows the letter survived." Bram was already rising, crossing to the window, staring out at the black roaring world. "Unless someone told him. Or unless he's found out the magistrate's been asking questions—which he has; half the town knows it—and he's done the arithmetic men like Aldous always do." She turned back. "Aldous can't outrun a trial. But a ship can outrun a magistrate. And there's a brigantine loading at the quay this very week—the *Cormorant*, out of Falmouth, taking on late cargo. My neighbor's boy works the customs house; he mentioned it Sunday. Odd hours. Night loading. Bound for Lisbon, no manifest published."

"Storm's breaking tonight," Maren said. "She'll want to sail on the morning tide, before the roads are watched. Before anyone thinks to look."

They stood facing each other across the small warm room, two women and a cat and a ledger full of sins, and outside the storm threw the whole weight of the sea against the tower as though demanding they mind their business.

"Maren." Bram said her name—her true name—for the second time, and it sounded different now: not a kindness but an enlistment. "If we're right, we have until dawn, and one road into town, and it will be waist-deep in places. I can't leave the light unlit. Not tonight of all nights. Whatever else I am, I am still the keeper."

"Then I'll go," Maren said. "I'll take the ledger's dates copied out and find the magistrate before the tide does."

"No." Bram was already pulling her oilskins from the peg, movements brisk, almost cheerful in the way of a woman whose life had just acquired a purpose with sharp edges. "We both know you'll be arrested as a trespasser or worse before you reach the customs house. No—we go together. I'll light the lamp first. The light keeps its hours; it always has. And then—" she paused at the stair, hand on the rail, rain already loud on the gallery above, and smiled a smile with real teeth in it, "—then the Montrose family is going to learn what happens when you leave a lighthouse keeper alone with the truth for three weeks."

Above them, the storm tore at the tower like something impatient to be introduced.

The morning tide, Maren thought, following her up into the dark. We have until the morning tide.

She only hoped the magistrate kept earlier hours than smugglers did.
