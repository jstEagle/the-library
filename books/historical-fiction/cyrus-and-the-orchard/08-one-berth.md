## 8. One Berth

The knock came at dusk, which was the first suspicious thing about it. Nobody climbed the ninety-one steps to the lighthouse after dark unless they wanted something, and nobody wanted something from Bram Montrose except light, shelter, or silence.

She opened the door with the poker in her left hand, mostly out of habit and partly out of hope for a dramatic gesture.

Aldous stood on the threshold, hat in hand, looking like a man who had rehearsed a speech on the walk up and drowned it twice on the way. Behind him waited a stranger—tall, silver-haired, with the sun-weathered look of someone who had spent decades making money in places where money required discretion.

"Sister Bram," Aldous began.

"Brother Aldous," she returned pleasantly. "How unusual. You've come all the way from town after sundown, and you've brought company. If this is about the harvest festival subscription, I gave at the church."

"It isn't the festival." He swallowed. "May we come in?"

Bram considered the two of them against the wind, Aldous's coat flapping like a nervous bird, the stranger perfectly still as though weather were a thing that happened to lesser men. She stepped aside. Curiosity, she had learned in her fifty-three years, was cheaper than entertainment and considerably more reliable.

* * *

The stranger introduced himself as Mr. Callum Vane, and he did it the way a man lays a card face-up on a table. He admired the lamp room. He admired the view. He admired, at some length, the tea, which was ordinary tea, and Bram began to understand that everything this man praised was a thing he intended to take or use.

"You'll have read the letter," Vane said at last.

"I've read the letter."

"Then you know my family's name is in it. And you know what happens to names, Mrs. Montrose, when they're dragged through a courtroom. They don't come clean. They come ruined."

"The man in the letter came drowned," Bram said. "I find I mind less about the name."

Vane smiled as though she'd made an amusing remark at dinner. "Your husband thought otherwise."

And there it was—the small cruelty done neatly, without raising the voice. Bram set down her cup so that it wouldn't rattle. It rattled anyway, faintly, and she pretended it hadn't.

"Ewan kept your family's secret for thirty years," she said. "Whatever else he was, he wasn't a coward, whatever you say of him."

"No," Vane agreed softly. "He was paid well not to be. Which brings me to why we've climbed your stairs." He produced no papers, which was somehow worse; men with papers wanted witnesses, and men without them wanted only agreement. "There's a ship. The *Merrow*, out of Harrow Point, sailing on the morning tide. Myself, my daughter, my household. There is one berth remaining."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the rocks with the patience of an old creditor.

"One berth," Bram repeated.

"Passage to Amsterdam, then anywhere. Two hundred pounds in hand, and the letter's twin—the copy you haven't found yet—burned before the anchor lifts. No magistrate, no trial, no questions about what your husband wrote in his ledger. You'd keep your light. You'd keep his memory whole. You'd keep—" he gestured gently at the round window, the beam swinging its slow arm over the dark water, "—all of this."

Bram looked at the lamp overhead, unlit yet, waiting for its hour. She thought: *he thinks he's offering me the world.* What he was actually offering was smaller than her kitchen.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Vane's expression didn't change, but the temperature of the room did. "Then there will be an inquiry, and inquiries are untidy things. A dead man's ledger in a widow's keeping. A widow who corresponded with strangers. Questions about whether Ewan Montrose acted alone, or whether his wife signed her name to things she preferred not to remember signing." He rose, buttoned his coat. "I don't threaten, Mrs. Montrose. I merely describe tomorrow both ways and let a sensible woman choose."

Aldous lingered at the door. His face was gray. "Bram," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle like old ice. "Two hundred pounds. You could go anywhere. Be anyone. Nobody here would—"

"Aldous." She touched his sleeve. "Go home to your wife. Tell her nothing tonight; let her sleep." Then, because gentle humor was the only coin she had never counterfeited: "And eat something. You look like a man being chased by his own conscience, and conscience always wins on an empty stomach."

He almost smiled. Then he went down into the dark, and Vane went after him like a shadow collecting a debt, and Bram stood alone with one berth on the morning tide and the longest night of her life ahead of her.

* * *

She lit the lamp first. Whatever she decided, the ships would need their light, and there was a comfort in that—one duty in the world that had never lied to her.

Then she sat at the kitchen table with Ewan's ledger open under the oil lamp, though by now she knew every line of it by heart. *Received, ten pounds, for the keeping of the light.* Thirty years of careful entries in her husband's tidy hand, each one a small brick in a wall of silence, and she had lived happily inside that wall without once asking what it was built around.

She had loved a myth, she thought. The kind, steady Ewan who mended nets for neighbors and never raised his voice. The myth was true as far as it went. It simply hadn't gone far enough. Beneath the kindness there had been this ledger, and beneath the ledger a drowned man named Kell, and beneath the drowned man—she turned the page—a night in October 1861 when the great lamp had been allowed, deliberately, quietly, to go dark for eleven minutes while a cargo came in that was not herring.

Eleven minutes. She had spent longer than that choosing curtains.

Toward midnight there came a scratching at the door, and she found Maren half-frozen on the step, hood plastered to her face, eyes bright with cold and purpose.

"He came," Maren said. Not a question. She'd watched Vane's party ride out from the village and known exactly what they carried in their pockets.

"He came. He offered me Amsterdam and a fortune, and he offered to burn the truth like rubbish." Bram stood back to let her in. "You're soaked through. Sit by the fire before you argue with me, or I'll be answering to your conscience and your pneumonia."

Maren sat, dripping, hands stretched to the flames, and for a while neither spoke. The wind found some gap in the stonework and played a thin note on it, like a fiddle tuning.

"There's one berth," Maren said finally. "One. They mean it as a gift and a leash both."

"They mean it as a mirror," Bram said. "They want me to see myself in it and like the reflection."

"Will you take it?"

Bram laughed—a short, surprised sound, because the answer had been sitting in her chest all evening and had only just chosen to introduce itself. "Maren. He offered to keep Ewan's memory whole if I'd help bury your father a second time. Deeper, this time, where nobody could dig." She shook her head slowly. "I was married to that man for nineteen years and I can't tell you what he'd have wanted, because I apparently never met him. But I know what *I* want. I want to stop being a woman things happen to."

The fire settled. Maren looked at her across it, and there was something in her face that wasn't gratitude—gratitude was too small—but recognition, the look of one swimmer sighting another.

"They sail at morning tide," Maren said. "Half past six. The patrol cutter sits at Harrow Point, but it won't stir without word."

"Then word it shall have." Bram rose and took down the signal flags she hadn't flown since the Queen's jubilee, when she'd hung them half in loyalty and half in irony. "Aldous will hate me. Half the county will hate me. The Montrose name will come apart like wet rope, and I'll stand in a courtroom and read from my dead husband's ledger while his sisters glare holes in my back."

"Yes," Maren said. "Probably."

"Well." Bram smoothed the flag against her knee. "At least the seating will be interesting."

* * *

The rest of the night she spent the way she'd spent a thousand nights—keeping watch. She brewed tea neither of them drank. She wound the clock. She wrote three letters: one to the magistrate, short and terrible; one to Aldous that began *By the time you read this* and ended, after much scratching-out, simply *I'm sorry, and also you should eat more*; and one to Ewan that said nothing at all, because he was beyond post, and because some letters you write only to know what you think.

What she thought was this: ruin was a door as well as a wall. Vane had described tomorrow both ways and called it a choice, but he'd forgotten to count the third morning—the one where the tide went out carrying a full ship and an empty berth, and Bram Montrose stood on her gallery with the lamp burning behind her and her name in splinters around her feet, and found, to her astonishment, that she could breathe.

Near five o'clock, Maren slept at last in the chair, chin dropped to her chest. Bram covered her with the good blanket and climbed the tower.

East was going gray at the edges. Out in the harbor roads, riding lights showed where the *Merrow* lay waiting, patient as a spider. One berth. Two hundred pounds and a burned letter and a whole quiet life, sailing away without her.

Bram put her hand flat against the cold glass of the lantern room, over the great lens with its hundreds of prisms, each one small and useless alone and together capable of throwing a beam twelve miles out to sea.

"That's the trick of it, Ewan," she told him. "You'd have known that, if you'd ever let yourself look."

Below, the harbor began, very faintly, to wake. And at Harrow Point, if the wind served, a cutter was about to receive word that the morning tide would carry more than one ship's worth of consequences.

She went down to dress for court.
