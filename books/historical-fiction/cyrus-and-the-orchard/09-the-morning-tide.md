## 9. The Morning Tide

Bram had lit the lamp a thousand times before, and it had never once felt like an accusation.

She rose at four, as she always did, into the blue-black hour when the world seemed to have been drawn in charcoal and forgotten. The stairs of the tower were worn smooth in the centers by thirty years of Montrose feet—Ewan's father, Ewan, and now her own—and she climbed them the way one climbs any habit: without thinking, hand on the rail, kettle already imagined steaming on the stove below.

But tonight her mind was not on wicks or oil levels. Tonight it was full of names.

Aldous Montrose. Silas Renner. The magistrate, Mr. Corvane, whose debts she had learned of from Maren's careful questions in town. Old Mrs. Tavish, who was perhaps only a fool and not a criminal, though Bram suspected fools and criminals were frequently confused for one another. And Ewan. Ewan, whose handwriting she knew better than her own reflection. *Paid to keep dark, 14 October 1861*, in his neat, patient script—a script that had written her love letters, their marriage lines, the grocery lists with little drawings of turnips in the margins when he was feeling playful.

The same hand. That was the unbearable part. Not two hands, one wicked and one kind. One hand, doing both.

"Right," Bram said aloud to the lamp room, because the lamp room did not judge, which was most of why she liked it. "Right."

She trimmed the wick. She filled the reservoir. She wound the clockwork that turned the great lenses with the patience of a thing that had never once lied to anybody. The flame caught, swelled, steadied, and the beam swung out over the water like a long arm pointing at nothing and everything.

Out in the dark, low and heavy with cargo and shame, the *Cormorant's Pride* rode at anchor beyond the point, waiting for morning tide and one last passenger. They had come to her door at dusk—Aldous pale as whey, Renner smooth as river stone—and laid it all out with the terrible gentleness of men delivering bad news they'd rehearsed.

Passage on the ship. Money enough to live out her days somewhere the name Montrose meant nothing. All she had to do was nothing. A lifetime salary for silence; she had only to keep doing what she'd apparently been doing for years without knowing it.

"You'd be cared for," Aldous had said, and his voice had cracked on *cared*, because he had been her brother once, in every way but blood's paperwork, and he knew exactly how much that offer insulted them both.

"And my light?" she'd asked. "Who tends it?"

Renner had smiled. "There are keepers for hire."

"No," Bram said now, to the empty lamp room, to the whole gray beginning of the world. "There aren't."

* * *

At half past five, Maren Kell came up the tower stairs with two cups of tea and the expression of a woman who had slept in her coat, which she had.

"You're up early," Maren said.

"I'm always up early. I'm a lighthouse keeper. We keep our own hours and everyone else's too." Bram took the cup. Their fingers brushed; Maren's were cold as the railing. "You didn't sleep."

"I slept some." Maren stood at the glass, looking down at the harbor where the patrol boat lay dark at its mooring, and beyond it, past the point, the faint riding lights of the *Cormorant's Pride*. "I kept thinking I'd wake up and it would all be ledger paper and my father would be alive and you'd just be the woman who sold me lamp oil and complained about the price of flour."

"I do complain about the price of flour."

"You do. It was very comforting." Maren turned. In the lamplight her face was young and old at once—the way grief ages people and honesty makes them young again. "It's not too late to take the berth, you know. They'd give it to you still. You could be on that ship by six and free by noon."

"Free," Bram repeated, tasting the word like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. "Do you know, my husband used to say the tide forgives everything. Comes in, goes out, wipes the sand clean twice a day, holds no grudges." She set down her tea. "He was wrong. The tide remembers. It carried your father's letter to me across thirty years and a hundred storms just to prove the point. The tide has been more patient than any of us deserve."

"So what do we do?"

Bram looked at the signal locker in the corner—the flags, the lanterns, the code book Ewan's father had bought before either of their fathers had likely ever spoken to a Kell. And beneath the flags, wrapped in oilcloth because Ewan had been a careful man even in his sins, the ledger.

"We do our jobs," Bram said. "We keep the light. And then we tell the truth, in order, loudly, to anyone with the authority to be inconvenienced by it."

Maren laughed—one short, startled sound, half sob. "That's the plan?"

"It's the best plan I've ever had. Most of my plans involve flour."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, then oyster, then a thin brave gold. The tide began its long pull out of the harbor, and with it the *Cormorant's Pride* would go, and whoever stood on her deck at six o'clock.

Bram signaled first.

Not with flags—with the light itself. She shuttered the beam, three quick occlusions, pause, three more, the old harbor-patrol call that meant *come at once, bring authority*. She had practiced it in drills so long ago she'd thought the knowledge had washed out of her, but her hands remembered, hands being loyal creatures that way.

Then she took the small boat across the harbor in a chop that soaked her to the knees and made the crossing feel appropriately ceremonial. Some things ought to cost a wet hem.

Magistrate Corvane met her on the quay, buttoned and pompous and visibly annoyed at being summoned by semaphore before breakfast—which lasted right up until she put the letter, the ledger, and a written account in his hands, and watched his face perform the complicated gymnastics of a man realizing his own name was in the document.

"Mrs. Montrose," he said carefully, "this is a serious accusation."

"It is a serious document," Bram said. "I've counted the entries three times. My husband had beautiful penmanship; it makes it worse, somehow. You'll want witnesses. There's a woman waiting on the point—Miss Kell. Her father was Josiah Kell, boatswain's mate, drowned the fourteenth of October, 1861, while the Montrose light sat conveniently dark. She can tell you what a daughter knows. I can tell you what a wife found. Between us we should fill your morning nicely."

"Bram." Corvane lowered his voice. "If this goes forward, the Montrose name—"

"The Montrose name," said Bram, "has been standing on a drowned man's chest for thirty years to keep its boots dry. It can stand in court instead. It may find the exercise bracing."

And there—there it was—not peace exactly, not yet, but the sudden strange lightness of a woman setting down something she had carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine.

* * *

They came for Aldous at half past six.

By then the *Cormorant's Pride* had weighed anchor, and the single berth meant for a fleeing heir sat empty as a broken promise, because Aldous Montrose had stood on the quay at dawn with his ticket in his fist and seen his sister-in-law rowing toward the patrol boat with a satchel under her arm, and understood, in the way of men who have spent lifetimes reading weather, that the wind had changed.

He didn't run. That was the thing Bram couldn't have predicted and would think about for years. He handed his ticket to a stunned dockhand, sat down on a bollard like a man forty years older than himself, and waited for the constables to come, because running would have made it real, and sitting still let him pretend, one last hour, that he was only a man waiting for a ship.

Bram watched from the lighthouse gallery, and Maren stood beside her, and neither of them spoke while Aldous was walked up the hill between two officers, his coat collar up against the wind.

"He could have gone," Maren said quietly.

"Yes."

"Why didn't he?"

Bram considered the horizon, where the *Cormorant's Pride* was shrinking to a smudge, taking Renner and money and silence off to wherever such things retire. "Because Ewan taught him to read weather too," she said at last. "And because some part of him, God help him, wanted to be caught. Guilt is patient, Miss Kell. More patient than tides."

* * *

The testimony took place that afternoon in the harbor office, before Corvane and two clerks and a stove that smoked resentfully. Bram stood with her hands folded and named every name.

Silas Renner, who arranged the cargo. Aldous Montrose, who carried messages and took payment. Mrs. Tavish, who kept her mouth shut for a pension of eight pounds a year and probably deserved pity more than prosecution, though Bram left that to the law. Mr. Corvane, whose debts had made him curious in the wrong direction—she named him too, watching his face go the color of porridge, and rather enjoyed it, in a way she would later confess was unchristian.

And last, in the same clear voice she used against gales:

"Ewan Montrose, keeper of this light, my husband. Paid to darken the lamp on the night of the fourteenth of October, 1861, that a man might drown unwitnessed. He was twenty-six years old and frightened, and he spent the rest of his life being kind, and I will not weigh those things against each other because they are not mine to weigh. But I will not hide his hand behind my grief. His name goes in the record same as the rest. Write it down, sir. Write all of it down."

The clerk wrote it down.

When it was done, Bram walked home along the seawall in the last of the light, salt wind pulling at her shawl, and discovered she was crying and could not have said for the life of her whether it was mourning or relief, and decided, around the third bench along the wall, that it was both, and that both at once was simply what freedom smelled like when you'd been holding your breath for years without noticing.

The lighthouse rose ahead of her, white and straight and honest, its lamp already lit against the coming dark because Maren had climbed the stairs and done it without being asked.

Bram stood in the doorway a long moment, watching her—this drowned man's daughter trimming the wick with competent hands, humming something low and northern, at home in a tower that had been built on a lie and was, from tonight, going to have to make its own way to truthfulness.

"Well," Bram said. "It seems I've acquired a keeper."

Maren looked down through the lamplight, and grinned, and something in Bram's chest—something rusted shut since the day they buried Ewan—swung open like a door.

"There's a crate in the cellar," Bram called up the stairs. "Been sealed since before I came here. I've a mind to open it tomorrow, if you're staying."

Maren's hum stopped. From above, thoughtful, warm: "What's in it?"

"That," said Bram Yarrow Montrose, closing the door behind her at last, "is precisely what I intend to find out."
