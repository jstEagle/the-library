## 10. Keeper of the Record

The assizes came to Harrowgate in the first week of April, and with them a smell of damp wool and civic self-importance that Maren Kell had learned to associate with other people's justice.

She sat in the gallery on the third morning, in a borrowed coat because her own had been ruined scrambling over the Montrose rocks in February, and watched the machinery do its slow, uneven work. Aldous Montrose was fined two hundred pounds and bound over to keep the peace, which everyone in the room understood to mean *pay up and stop being interesting*. The magistrate's own debts, it turned out, had been quietly settled by an uncle in Edinburgh shortly after the trial began — a coincidence no newspaper remarked upon, because newspapers knew which coincidences kept them welcome at dinner parties. The younger conspirator, Fenn, got eighteen months for conspiracy to defraud and not one day for anything that touched her father's name, because there was no living witness to the drowning and the ledger only proved what had been paid, never what had been done.

Uneven, then. But real. Her father had a name again, spoken aloud in a courtroom, entered into a record that could not be burned or bought: *Joren Kell, mate of the* Merrow's Gift, *deceased 14 November 1861, death unlawfully occasioned.*

Unlawfully occasioned. She turned the phrase over during the recesses like a coin she kept finding new faces on. It was not *murdered*. The law had declined to say *murdered*, because saying so would have meant admitting that thirty years of respectable silence had been an accessory. But it was more than nothing. Thirty years of nothing, and now this small hard something.

Bram Montrose sat through every session in the second row, hands folded, grey head erect, answering questions in a voice so level that the barristers twice asked her to repeat herself as though clarity itself must be suspect. When the verdicts were read she did not smile. She did not weep either. She simply stood, when the court rose, and stood a moment longer than courtesy required, as if letting the room understand that she would be leaving on her own schedule.

Ewan Montrose's name had been read into the record too. Maren had watched Bram's face when it happened — watched the flinch arrive and be met, the way a keeper meets a wave against the seawall. Not deflected. Met.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, Aldous Montrose passed them without looking at either woman, his fine paid by draft on the seized estate, his hat held very straight. His sister-in-law did not watch him go. "He'll write to me by autumn," she said instead, mild as milk. "Aldous always writes when he's run out of audience. It'll be three pages about the weather and then one sentence about how none of this was entirely his fault."

"And you'll answer?"

"I'll send him a tide table." A pause, and then, drier: "With the wrong dates marked."

Maren laughed — actually laughed, out loud, on the steps of a courthouse — and was surprised by it, the way you're surprised by warm water in a cold month. That was the thing nobody warned you about grief's long tail: joy came back rude and unannounced, elbowing its way in at odd hours.

* * *

She went back out to the point in May, when the gorse was doing its best to set the cliffs on fire and the sea had remembered how to be blue.

The lighthouse looked smaller in sunlight than it ever had in storm, which Maren suspected was true of most formidable things. Bram met her at the door with flour on her sleeves and an expression of elaborate innocence that meant she'd been baking, badly, and hoped to disguise the evidence in a loaf.

"There's tea," Bram said. "And there's what I made, which is technically bread. I'd start with the tea."

"You've been keeping a light and a household alone for six years and you still can't make bread?"

"The light doesn't care if I overproof. It's very forgiving company." She stood aside to let Maren in. "Also I have something for you, and I wanted bread to be ready, in case it's terrible news, in which case bread helps, or wonderful news, in which case bread also helps. Bread is equal-opportunity."

In the kitchen — scrubbed table, brass fittings polished to a shine that suggested they were the conversation partners of long winter evenings — Bram laid a oilcloth packet on the table and unwrapped it with the ceremony of a woman who had rehearsed this moment and decided, characteristically, not to let rehearsal show.

It was a logbook. Small, salt-stained, the spine repaired with sail twine in a hand Maren recognized before she could stop her eyes from stinging. Her father's hand. The cramped, forward-leaning script of a man who wrote standing up on a moving deck and never once waited for calm weather.

"Where—" she started, and couldn't finish.

"Under the floor of the old store room. Where Ewan hid the ledger, there was a space beneath, older, someone else's hiding place first. Your father's kit was in it. The *Merrow's* carried two logs, same as any vessel — the fair copy for the owners, and the working book." Bram pushed it gently across the table. "The fair copy drowned with him. This stayed. Which means the truth stayed, all these years, three feet under the feet of men who thought they'd buried it."

Maren didn't touch it at first. She looked at it the way you look at a bird that has landed on your hand by mistake and might leave if you breathe.

"Read the last entry," Bram said softly. "November the thirteenth."

She opened it. The pages smelled of brine and lamp oil and thirty years of dark. And there it was, in her father's tidy deck-hand script:

*Wind veering NW, making for the coast under short canvas. Cargo sits wrong in the hold and Montrose's man says run dark past the point tonight. Told him a dark light is no light at all and a man who asks it should think what he's asking. We will see who keeps their nerve.*

"He refused," Maren said. Her voice came out strange. "He told them he wouldn't—"

"He refused," Bram said. "That's why they drowned him. Not for silence. He wouldn't give it." She refilled Maren's untouched cup, her own hand steady, though her eyes were bright. "I spent weeks thinking my husband's name in that record was the worst thing I'd ever learn. Then I read your father's last page, and I understood the shape of it properly. There were two men in this family's story, Maren. One paid to put the light out. One said no. You come from the one who said no. I married into the other line. I've decided we're both allowed to claim whichever ancestor we can live beside."

"That's generous to Ewan."

"It's accurate about Joren," Bram said. "The generosity is incidental." And then, with the small crooked smile that Maren had learned meant the armor was coming down for exactly four seconds: "Though I'll admit the bread is an apology. For the whole family. On behalf of the name. Eat it and consider us partially square."

* * *

They walked out to the rocks after, because Bram claimed the light needed inspecting, and both of them knew the light needed nothing, and neither said so.

The inheritance was gone — seized into receivership, the big house at Harrowgate already stripped of its portraits, sold to a shipping magnate whose solicitors were said to be less sentimental than the magistrate. Aldous would land somewhere soft; men like Aldous always did. Fenn would do his eighteen months and come out to find his friends had rearranged themselves. Justice, Maren thought, watching the water work at the stones below, was a tide table of its own. It reached some marks and missed others. But it moved, and you could read it, and it came from an ocean that did not care who it inconvenienced.

"Will you stay?" she asked. "Out here. Alone. After everything — you could sell the light. There'd be interest, now the name's in the papers."

Bram considered this with the seriousness she gave to navigation. "Do you know what happens to lighthouses when they're decommissioned?" she said. "They become cottages. They get dormer windows and geraniums and people pay enormous sums to feel romantic next to a dead flame." She shook her head slowly. "This light was dark for one night in thirty years, and a man died because of it. As long as I'm alive, it stays lit and it stays honest. Besides—" she squinted out at the horizon, where a smudge of smoke announced the Harrowgate steamer, right on her schedule, "—the Board has offered me the appointment formally. Keeper, in my own name. First time they've put a widow's name on the roll rather than hunting up some nephew. I suspect I scandalized a clerk somewhere into doing the right thing."

"An accidental reformer."

"Don't tell the papers. I have a reputation for minding my own business." She turned. "You could stay too, you know. There's work here for a person who reads ledgers and asks uncomfortable questions. The coast is full of cargoes that sit wrong in the hold. Somebody ought to keep the record."

Maren looked out at the water, at the particular stretch of grey-green where, thirty-one Novembers ago, a man had swum until he couldn't, while a light he trusted failed him on purpose. She had come to this coast with one purpose, wound tight as a spring: find who killed my father, ruin them. She had found them. Some were ruined. Some were fined. All were named. And the spring, unwound, had left behind a strange empty space that turned out not to be emptiness at all but room — room for tea and terrible bread and a woman who kept truth the way other people kept fires, fed and banked and stubborn against the dark.

"Someone ought," she agreed. "I'll want it in writing, mind. I've seen what this family does with verbal arrangements."

"My dear," Bram said, already turning toward the tower, "I'm going to write down everything. Dates, names, tides, mistakes, mine included. It's going to be the most uncomfortable document on this coast." Over her shoulder, mild as milk: "Chapter one is called *What the Tide Brought In*. You're in it."

Maren stood a moment longer at the edge of the rocks, her father's logbook dry and safe under her arm, and watched the steamer come in on the morning tide — and high above her, in the lantern room, the great lens caught the sun and threw it back out across the water, one flash, then another, steady as a promise.

The light, she thought, keeps its own hours. And at last, so did she.
