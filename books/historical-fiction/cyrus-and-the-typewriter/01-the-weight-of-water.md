## 1. The Weight of Water

The first thing you learn under the water is that the sea does not know your name.

It doesn't care that you're cold. It doesn't care that your lungs are burning or that the weight belt cinched around your waist belongs to a company that owns eleven years of your life. The sea takes what it wants and gives back what it pleases, and if you go down and don't come up, it will not pause. It will not even notice.

I found this comforting, once. I have been trying to remember why.

My name is Gideon Winterbourne. I am nineteen years old, and I am very good at being forgotten.

* * *

The Meridian Trading Company's warehouse smelled of brine, tar, and the particular sweetness of copra going soft in the heat. By seven o'clock I was at my station at the sorting table, hands already moving through the morning's haul — shell, mostly, mother-of-pearl still crusted with the sea, along with whatever else the divers had brought up tangled in their nets. A ship's bell gone green with age. A brass fitting someone would swear was solid gold until we tested it. Three bottles, sealed, their paper labels dissolved into gray pulp.

I cataloged all of it. Item, quantity, condition, estimated value. My figures were neat. My figures were always neat. Mr. Pratchett, the warehouse foreman, had said once — in front of the others, which was unusual — that Winterbourne's girl had the best hand for figures on the whole waterfront. He'd said it the way you'd praise a mule for steady footing. Nobody looked up. That was the point. Praise a mule and you don't promote it; you just work it harder.

"Winterbourne." Pratchett's voice from the far end of the shed. "The Kestrel's manifest is short forty pounds of shell."

"The Kestrel's manifest has been short since March," I said, without looking up. "The boat leaks. The shell sits in bilgewater and loses weight before it's weighed."

A pause. Pratchett chewed on that. He was not a man who enjoyed being corrected by an indentured diver's daughter, but he enjoyed being wrong even less, and there it was: the Kestrel *had* been short every month since the spring, and everyone knew why, and nobody wanted to say it near the company clerk who took his cut off the top.

"Log it as evaporation," he said finally.

Evaporation. As if the sea reached into the hold of a moored boat and drank. I wrote it down in my neat hand because that was what my hand was for. Eleven years ago my father had signed — made his mark, rather; my father could not write — a contract binding the Winterbourne family to the Company for the sum advanced to buy our boat, our gear, and our silence about the terms. The debt accrued interest the way a wound accrues infection. At nineteen, working six days a week diving and cataloging both, I calculated each quarter that we owed more than the quarter before. It was arithmetic as a form of drowning. I did it anyway. Numbers were the one place where things added up honestly, even when nothing else did.

At noon the bell rang and the divers came in off the luggers, and I went out with them, because cataloging cargo you've never touched is a kind of lie, and because the water paid better than the table.

* * *

Nineteen feet down, the harbor turned green-gold and slow, and the noise of the world above went away entirely.

I worked the bed off Marker Rock, fingers walking the seabed, reading it the way Pratchett read a ledger. Shell here, shell there, the empty scorched circles where someone had already stripped a patch bare. The other divers worked fast and shallow. I worked deep and patient, because deep shell was bigger, and because down there, alone, no one could assign me anywhere else.

There is a moment in every dive when the body begins to complain and the mind has to decide whether to listen. Most people never learn to sit in that moment. They surface early, gasping, and they spend their whole lives like that — surfacing early from everything. My father taught me otherwise. *Breathe out slow,* he used to say, crouched at the gunwale, his face weathered down to something like teak. *The fear lives in the air you're holding. Let it go and there's just you and the water, and the water's honest.*

He had been dead four years, and I still dove to his voice.

I filled my basket, checked my line, and hung a moment longer than I should have, knees in the sand, looking at the pale slope of the bottom fading down into blue dark. Fish moved through the light in loose silver grammar. Somewhere below the shelf, deeper than any of us went, the old wrecks lay with their timbers silted over — the ones the Company filed reports on, the ones with names I wasn't supposed to ask after. My father had died ashore, officially. A fever, the record said. But men who dive for a living do not die of fevers ashore; they die in the water or they die of what the water pays, and either way the record is written by someone who wasn't there.

I let the thought go, the way my father taught me. I surfaced early that day anyway.

* * *

Evening in the divers' quarter meant smoke and salt and forty kinds of quiet argument. Our row of company houses leaned against the seawall like tired old men holding each other up. My mother kept a kettle going and didn't ask questions, which between us was a form of tenderness. My younger brother, Tam, mended nets by the door and narrated his day to anyone who'd listen, which was no one, which stopped him not at all.

"You cataloged a ship's bell today," he said. "I heard. Was it big?"

"Medium."

"Was it from a wreck?"

"Everything in that warehouse is from a wreck, if you wait long enough."

He considered this with the seriousness of a boy who is nine and believes the world will eventually make sense. I envied him the belief. I had held it myself once, briefly, the way you hold a shell too long and find out it's empty.

After Tam slept, my mother sat by the window with her mending, and I sat across from her with the debt book, though there was nothing new to enter. We didn't speak. We had a whole language of not-speaking, she and I, built over eleven years and four years of grief. She had loved my father loudly, once — I had the stories, though not the memory — but the Company had a way of teaching people to fold themselves smaller each year, like letters passed hand to hand until the creases cut the words in half.

"Gideon." She said it quietly, to the window. "Pratchett came by while you were at the water."

"What did he want?"

"To say the Company was reviewing its ledgers. All of them. Top to bottom." Her needle paused. "An audit, he called it. From the governor's office, he said. New inspector coming to look at the books."

"Let him look. Our figures are neat."

"That's what frightens me," my mother said. "Neat figures are easy to read."

She went back to her mending. I pretended to study the debt book, and instead I looked at my father's mark on the last page — a small crossed X, pressed so hard the paper had dented, as if he'd been trying to push his whole self through the pen he couldn't use. Beside it, in some clerk's tidy script: *Winterbourne, G., diver, indenture term 20 yrs., heirs bound.*

*Heirs bound.* Two words. My whole life, itemized.

I closed the book. Outside, the harbor lay flat and black under a moon doing its best, and somewhere out on the water a lugger's lamp swung at anchor, patient as everything else the Company owned. I thought, as I did most nights, that tomorrow would be the same as today — shell, figures, water, sleep, and nothing worth dreaming about. I had gotten so good at wanting nothing that I'd begun to suspect I felt nothing, and I had decided this was safety, and I had told myself the story so many times I almost believed it.

I blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark and dreamed of nothing, exactly as planned.

Which is why it seems, in retrospect, like the sea itself arranged the joke: that in the very last week of my life as nobody, the letter that would ruin everything arrived in the hands of a dying man — and he gave it to me.
