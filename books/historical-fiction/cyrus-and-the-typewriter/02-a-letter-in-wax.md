## 2. A Letter in Wax

The morning Old Tam drowned, the harbor lay flat as poured tin, and I remember thinking—before anyone knew, before the boats went out—that the water was too calm to trust.

Calm water is a liar. It shows you everything on top and nothing underneath, which is exactly how the company likes it. I'd been diving since before dawn, hauling up oyster shell for the Meridian Trading Company's beds, and by seven o'clock my fingers had gone pruney white inside my gloves and the tally clerk had already shouted at me twice for sorting slow. That was normal. Being shouted at was a kind of weather. You learned to stand in it without getting wet.

Old Tam was sixty if he was a day, and he dove anyway because his debt had outlived his lungs. He'd been down four minutes when Kestrel's boy saw the air line go slack. Then it was all shouting and rope and men hauling hand over hand, and I stood at the edge of the barge with the rest of the divers, dripping, watching them pull him up like a sodden sack of grain.

He was still breathing. Barely. The company doctor was two islands away, so they laid him on the deck of the sorting shed, wrapped in canvas that smelled of mildew and diesel, and someone had the sense to send for Marisol, though she didn't dive anymore and hadn't for years.

I didn't know why they sent me into the shed. I told myself later it was chance—that the tally clerk needed someone to fetch dry blankets and I was nearest and invisible, which has always been my condition. But there are no chances on a company pier. Everything is counted.

Tam's eyes found me before I'd crossed half the room.

You have to understand what a dying man's gaze does. It isn't like being looked at. It's like being picked up and carried somewhere you didn't agree to go. His mouth worked under the gray of his mustache, and I knelt because there was nowhere else in the world to be.

"Gideon Winterbourne," he rasped. Not a question. He knew my name. Nobody knew my name. On the books I was *diver #114, Winterbourne, G.—indentured, debt outstanding*, and the clerks called me by my number more often than not.

"Don't talk," I said. "Marisol's coming."

"Past talking." His hand came out from under the canvas, and it was nothing but wire and leather, and pressed into that palm was something flat and heavy that he pushed against my chest with the last of his strength. "Your father. He kept his promise. Now it's yours."

The thing was an envelope, sealed so many times in wax that the wax had built up like coral around a mooring post. Dark red. Cracked with age at one corner. It was warm from his body, which felt obscene somehow, like holding someone's heartbeat in your hand.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"Peace is ending, girl." And here he did the terrible thing—he smiled. "Vane wrote it. Your father swore. Swore on the water, on the salt, on your mother's name. Half the old harbor heard him do it." His breath caught and rattled. "Deliver it."

"To who?"

But his eyes had gone past me, up through the shed's corrugated roof, past the sky, to whatever a man sees when the company finally can't collect from him anymore. He died between one wave slap against the pilings and the next. I know because I counted them. Counting is what I do.

* * *

They took the body away and they took the morning away and they gave me back my sorting table, because grief is not a line item and the shell doesn't tally itself. I worked with the envelope sewn into the lining of my coat, which sounds brave and was actually just the only hiding place a person owns when she owns nothing.

Here is what I knew about Elias Vane, as of that morning:

I knew the name the way every diver in the harbor knew it—from the old men's stories, mostly. Vane had been the company's golden man twenty years ago, a merchant prince of the pearling routes, the one who negotiated fair prices for the divers' catch back when fair was a word anyone bothered with. My father had spoken of him once, maybe twice, in the careful voice he saved for things that mattered. *Vane stands good for us, Gid. Remember that.* Then Vane had left the harbor, and then, eight months ago, the government gazette had printed his name beside the words *enemy agent*—a traitor to the crown, wanted, his properties seized, his portrait grainy and sharp-eyed on every customs post from here to the capital.

And here is what I knew about my father: he had been dead nine years, drowned in the wreck of the *Corvane*, and the company had written a report about it that satisfied everyone who mattered, which is to say nobody who loved him.

By afternoon my hands were shaking on the shells, and the tally clerk shouted at me a third time, and I didn't hear a word of it.

* * *

I opened the letter that night in the drying loft above the cooperage, where the family quarters were—one room, two hammocks, my mother asleep in hers with her cough folded small beneath the blanket so it wouldn't cost us anything. The oil lamp had perhaps ten minutes of fuel left. Good, I thought. A short light. Whatever this was, I could look at it briefly and put it away and go on being unremarkable until I died of it.

The wax seal had been applied in layers, year over year—someone had resealed it each time it cracked, or perhaps several someones had, passing it hand to hand down a chain of promises I'd never known existed. My thumb found the oldest edge, where the red had gone brown and brittle as blood on old cloth.

I want to say I hesitated. I didn't. Dread had been sitting on my chest since the shed, cold and patient as ballast stone, and the only way out from under it was through.

The wax cracked under my thumb with a sound like a knuckle popping, and I flinched as though I'd broken something in the world's architecture.

Inside: two sheets, folded in thirds, and a smaller paper wrapped around them like a band. The outer sheets were in a hand I didn't know—quick, slanted, confident, the writing of a man used to being believed. The band was in my father's hand, and I would have known it anywhere, would have known it blind, because he'd written our names on the inside of my mother's sewing box lid with that same nib-dragging pressure, and I had traced those letters a thousand times in the dark.

The band said: *To Winterbourne's wife and child. Deliver when peace ends. — E.V., witnessed.*

*When peace ends.* What peace? I'd grown up under the company flag; I'd never seen peace, only the long armistice between debts. Unless he meant something else—unless peace, for men like my father and Old Tam, was just the interval before the powerful needed something from the powerless again. In which case peace had ended eight months ago, when the gazette printed Vane's name in black.

I unfolded the letter.

*To the family of Josiah Winterbourne,*

*I write this knowing Josiah will keep it better than any strongroom, because he keeps his word the same way he dives—without coming up until the work is done.*

*What follows must reach you only if I cannot deliver it myself. If you are reading it, then either I am dead, disgraced, or both, and the men I write of below have decided the truth of the Corvane should stay at the bottom of the sea.*

I stopped. The lamp guttered. My mother turned in her sleep, and I sat absolutely still in the dark of my own body.

*Your husband died because he refused to lie for men whose signatures are worth more than his life. They required a wreck report saying what they needed said, and he refused, and they arranged the refusal out of him. I hold papers proving it. If ever the crown turns on me—and it will, because I mean to end what they are building—then these pages become the only shield your family has. Keep them hidden. Trust none of the company's officers. Trust Marisol. Trust the promise.*

*When peace ends, deliver the truth. It is worth more than safety. I am sorry to make that arithmetic yours.*

*—Elias Vane*

There was more—the second sheet, dense with dates, initials, cargo numbers, a wreck report annotated in red—but my eyes had stopped working as eyes. They were working as doors, and behind every door was my father walking down a gangway for the last time, and behind that was the report the company had filed, stamped and tidy, *accidental foundering, no irregularities*, the report I had read once at fourteen and wept over because it was so clean, so complete, so finished with him.

No irregularities.

He had refused to falsify it. He had refused, and then he had drowned, and the report had been falsified anyway, by other hands, and I had spent nine years believing the sea took him honestly.

The lamp died. I sat in the dark holding treason in my lap, and downstairs my mother slept on, and outside the harbor lights burned in their neat, counted rows—all the property of the Meridian Trading Company, including the water, including the darkness, including, now, me.

Because here was the arithmetic Vane had apologized for: destroy the letter, and my father stayed a drowned accident forever, and I stayed safe, and safe was the whole of my ambition, the entire architecture of my life. Deliver it—and I delivered myself into the same gazette that carried Vane's face, into the same ledgers, under the same word: *traitor*. The company held my family's debt. The company owned the pier, the boats, the shells, the air we breathed on the barge. The company could not be betrayed casually. It would notice.

And yet my father had sworn on the water, on the salt, on my mother's name. And Old Tam had died pressing the promise into my hands like a man handing off a live coal, glad to set it down.

I folded the sheets along their old creases, wrapped the band around them, and sat in the pitch dark of the loft with the letter against my sternum, feeling my own heart knock against it like a fist on a locked door.

Half the old harbor heard him swear it, Tam had said. Which meant half the old harbor knew. Had known for years. All those faces at the sorting tables, all those men who nodded to my father's memory and said *good diver, honest man*—they had watched me grow up indentured and invisible, and not one of them had ever said *your father left you something, girl*.

Or—no. Perhaps they had watched and waited, the way divers watch the surface from below, and the promise simply hadn't been mine to carry until tonight.

Either way, the choice was in my hands now, sealed and cracking.

I didn't sleep. Somewhere past midnight, I heard boots on the cooperage stairs—company boots, measured, unhurried—and I slid the letter into the coat lining with fingers that had finally stopped shaking, because terror, it turns out, burns off like fog and leaves something harder underneath.

The boots passed on. But the next morning, when I reported to the sorting barge, a clerk I'd never seen before was waiting by the tally desk with an open ledger and a polite expression, and he asked me my name.

Not my number. My name.

Nobody had asked me my name in nine years.
