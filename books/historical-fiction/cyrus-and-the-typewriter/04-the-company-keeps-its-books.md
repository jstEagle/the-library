## 4. The Company Keeps Its Books

Inspector Halloway did not like the archive.

It sat below the waterline of the company house, three rooms of shelving sunk into the harbor's stone, and the air in it was cold and mineral and old. Paper rotted faster here. The clerks complained of the damp; the junior men invented excuses to be assigned elsewhere. Halloway had taken the room for himself on his second day, because a man who wanted to know what the Meridian Trading Company truly was could not learn it upstairs, where the ledgers were clean and the ink was fresh. He had to come down where the company kept its history — which is to say, where it kept its mistakes.

He worked by lamplight with his coat on. Four days now he had been pulling records, and the shape of the thing he hunted was beginning to show itself the way a wreck shows itself to a diver: first a shadow, then an outline, then, if you were patient and your hands were steady, the whole hull of it, sitting where it sank.

Elias Vane. Enemy agent, by order of the Crown. Ten years the company's friend, six months its traitor. His accounts had been seized, his letters burned by men who should have copied them first, his name struck from the rolls with a violence that Halloway found almost theatrical. Someone had been very eager to make Vane disappear from the record. In Halloway's experience, eagerness was never innocent. A man who destroyed evidence was hiding something. A man who destroyed evidence *quickly* was hiding something for someone else.

So Halloway went sideways. If Vane had been erased, then Vane's world remained — every transaction he had touched, every name that appeared beside his own in the margins of ten years of shipping manifests. Conspirators left trails like snails left slime. You couldn't see it until you tilted the light.

He found the first thread on the fourth evening, in a bound ledger dated seven years back. A wreck report — the *Coriander*, lost off the shoals with a full cargo of company silk — and beside it, in Vane's unmistakable hand, a note initialed by two colonial officials whose signatures Halloway knew from a hundred other documents. The report settled the insurance. The company ate the loss quietly. Everyone signed. Everyone moved on.

Except that the *Coriander* had never sunk.

Halloway knew this because he had spent three weeks in the divers' quarter buying rum and asking nothing, and a drowned man's mate had told him, for the price of two cups, that the *Coriander* sat in forty feet of water off a reef where no company diver was ever licensed to go — and that she had been sitting there, cargo intact, for seven years, while certain gentlemen grew rich on her disappearance.

The report was false. And Elias Vane had written it.

Halloway sat back and let the lamp hiss. This was the conspiracy, then, or the spine of it: officials who falsified losses, merchants who profited from them, a wreck that never happened pretending to be a wreck that had. And Vane — who must have been inside it from the start, or had turned against it at some point Halloway could not yet see — was now the enemy of the crown because he knew too much, or because he had threatened to tell.

But someone else had refused to help falsify the original report. The mate's story had a second thread in it: a diver, a company man, who had been asked to swear the *Coriander* was beyond salvage and had said no. Who had gone out one morning and not come back. Drowned, they said. The harbor said drowned about a great many things.

Halloway turned to the index of personnel. His finger went down the columns.

**WINTERBOURNE, T.** — diver, indentured, Meridian Trading Company. Deceased, 1908.
Beneath it, in a later hand: **WINTERBOURNE, G.** — inherited debt, indentured, age twelve at assumption.

Halloway looked at those two lines for a long time.

Then he pulled the Winterbourne debt file, and began to read.

Gideon felt it before she understood it, the way you feel a change in current before you see the water move.

She had come up from the morning dive with her quota and her numb fingers, and the dock clerk had called her name — her actual name, aloud, across the yard — "Winterbourne! Office. Now."

Nobody was called to the office. The office called people the way weather called rain: something that happened to other ships. She stood dripping on the boards while the other divers watched her with the flat careful expressions of people glad it wasn't them, and she walked up the hill to the company house feeling as though she were carrying her own body for the first time in years, heavy and conspicuous, taking up space.

The junior clerk wouldn't meet her eye. He told her to wait in the passage, and she waited, and through the wall she heard paper being handled — that dry whisper of files being opened, pages turned, a ledger's spine cracking. Someone was reading about her. She could not have said how she knew. She simply knew, the way she knew the weight of a shell before she pried it open.

After an hour they told her to go back to work. No explanation. No charge. Just her name spoken into the open air of her life, and then silence again.

That night she lay awake in the divers' quarter and did the arithmetic she had spent seven years refusing to do.

She had believed herself invisible. She had built the whole architecture of her survival on it — the bowed head, the unremarkable quotas, the habit of finishing her work five minutes before anyone thought to look. She had thought the company's records held her the way the sea holds a grain of sand: incidentally, without interest, one among millions. Her father had died and become a line in a ledger. She had inherited his debt the way you inherit the tide — impersonal, vast, unanswerable. She had told herself there was safety in that. That no one watched what no one valued.

But the letter in its oilcloth wrap, buried under the floor stones where she slept, said otherwise. Her father had not drowned by accident, if the letter was true. Her father had refused men who needed a signature, and the harbor had said drowned, and the ledger downstairs in the cold rooms under the company house still carried his name in a neat column — deceased, 1908 — as though death were simply an entry, balanced and closed.

And now someone was pulling that file.

She thought of Inspector Halloway, whom she had seen twice from a distance: a still man, a patient man, the kind who stood at the end of piers watching the water as though it owed him testimony. He had arrived six weeks ago asking quiet questions about Elias Vane, and everyone had assumed he would fail the way investigators always failed, because the harbor kept its secrets the way the sea kept its dead. But Gideon had read the letter now. She knew what was in it. She knew that her father's name sat in the middle of it like a stone in a net, and that if Halloway pulled hard enough on any thread connected to Vane, sooner or later he would feel the weight of the Winterbournes at the end of the line.

Her invisibility had never been real. That was the vertigo of it, lying in the dark with the letter beneath her and the harbor slapping the pilings. It had never been hers at all. She had not made herself unseen; she had been *filed*. Entered into a system by men who measured her worth in shells gathered and debt accrued, who had recorded her mother's illness, her father's death, her own indenture at twelve years old with the same unhurried hand that recorded cargo. Somewhere downstairs in the cold, some clerk had written *WINTERBOURNE, G.* in a fair round script when she was still a child crying at a funeral, and had thought nothing of it, because she was nothing to think of. A number that breathed. An entry that dove.

All those years of being careful, and the care had never mattered. The ledger had known her name since before she knew how to spell it.

She got up quietly, knelt, and lifted the floor stone. The oilcloth bundle was cool and dry. She held it against her chest and felt her own heart going hard against it, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head, that she and the letter were already in the same file. That whatever happened next — surrender, destruction, delivery — would be written down somewhere by somebody. There was no version of this where she stayed unrecorded.

The only question left was what the record would say.

Far up the hill, past the locked gate, a light still burned in the company house. She could see it from the doorway of the quarters, one yellow square in the black face of the building. The archive room. Someone working late among the dead names.

Gideon wrapped the letter back in its oilcloth, tied it, and put it away — but differently now, deeper, shifted to a place she'd have to reach with both arms. Then she stood at the door a moment longer, looking at that lit window.

Down in the dark under the company house, Inspector Halloway turned a page, and ran his finger down a column of names, and stopped.
