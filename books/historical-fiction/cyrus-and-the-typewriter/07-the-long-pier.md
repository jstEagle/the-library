## 7. The Long Pier

The tide turned at half past eleven, and I was in the water before it finished.

Not diving tonight. Swimming—out along the pilings of the long pier, where the harbor lanterns thinned to a smudge of amber and the company's watchmen grew bored around midnight. I had spent nineteen years learning the underside of this harbor: which beams were rotten, which ladders were greased with weed, which shadows pooled deep enough to drown a girl in if she stood still long enough.

The letter rode against my ribs in an oilskin pouch, tied twice, warm as a second heart. Three nights I had kept it under the floorboard by my cot and three nights I had lain awake listening for boots on the stairs. Tonight it moved or I did something stupid trying to make it move. Marisol said that was how mistakes got made. She'd agreed to come anyway.

*The courier comes at slack low water,* her note had said, written in charcoal on the back of a fishmonger's receipt. *Wears a red sash. You do not speak first. You do not speak last.*

I pulled myself up onto the ladder at pier forty-one and crouched in the dark between two moored lighters, wringing saltwater from my hair. The harbor breathed around me—hull timbers ticking, rope groaning against cleats, somewhere far off a party still going aboard one of the company steamers, laughter spilling thin across the water like oil.

Marisol appeared out of the black without a sound, as she always had. Sixty years old and she moved through water quieter than I moved through air.

"He'll be here," she murmured, settling beside me. Her knees cracked as she crouched. "Elias trusted this man with more than letters."

"You've met him?"

"Once. Years ago." She was quiet a moment. In the distance the party laughed again. "Your father stood where you're standing now, Gideon. The night he gave Elias the letter to keep. He said, *when peace ends.* Those exact words. Half the harbor heard him say it."

"And half the harbor has spent six years pretending they didn't."

"Fear is a good listener," Marisol said. "It hears everything and repeats nothing." She put one hand over mine where it gripped the piling. Hers was rough as wharf timber. "Whatever happens tonight, you did not make this promise. He did. You are only keeping his arithmetic."

That was the thing about Marisol. She made treason sound like bookkeeping.

A boat came around the end of the breakwater—oars muffled with rags, riding low. A shape stood in the bow, and even at fifty yards I could see the dull gleam of cloth at its waist.

Red sash.

My mouth went dry. Six years of being nobody, of walking past inspectors with my eyes down and my hands empty, and now every step I took toward that boat was a step someone could hang me for. I thought of Halloway's ledger, his clean columns, my family's debt written there in his tidy hand. *The Winterbournes. Indentured. Reliable.* I had been a line in his book since before I could write my own name. The only question left was what he would enter beside it.

Marisol touched my shoulder. "Steady."

The boat came alongside the ladder below us. The man in the red sash looked up.

And then the pier lit up.

Lanterns—four of them, maybe five, flaring all at once down the length of the planks above. Boots. The crash of a whistle. A voice I knew by now the way you know a toothache, level and unhurried, saying, "There's no hurry, gentlemen. Take them all."

Halloway.

He hadn't followed me. He had simply read the same harbor I had. Slack low water, pier forty-one, the divers' quarter awake and restless—he had known where the courier would come before I did, because Halloway was a man who understood that people are most legible at night, when they think no one is reading.

"Under," Marisol said, and shoved me.

I went into the black water with barely a splash and hung beneath the surface, hands locked on a piling root, the cold pressing in on my ears. Above me the lantern-light broke into coins on the underside of the swell. Through the hull-timber gaps I saw shapes: watchmen swarming down the ladder, the courier's boat pinned against the pilings by grappling hooks, oars splintering.

And Marisol.

She had not gone under. She had gone *up*—up the ladder, straight into the lanterns, loud on purpose, shouting something I couldn't hear through the water, drawing every boot and every eye toward her small grey figure while the courier slipped his sash and slid over the far gunwale into the dark.

They took her. I watched it happen through the gaps in the wood—watched them fold her arms back, watched her go without struggle, upright and almost dignified, like a woman boarding a ferry. One of the watchmen laughed. Another said something to the man beside him and they both looked toward the shadowed stretch of pier where I should have been standing, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find. Just water. Just another diver's daughter who knew the underside of her harbor better than any inspector ever would.

I stayed under until my lungs burned and my vision sparked, and then I came up inside the shadow of a grounded barge, breathing through my teeth.

*You did not make this promise,* Marisol had said. But she had been making it for six years herself, silent in her smoke-filled kitchen, and now she was in a cell for a promise she'd only ever witnessed.

The letter against my ribs felt less like a heart now. It felt like a stone.

* * *

I cut through the ropewalk behind the cooperage, moving fast with my head down, and that was where Tobias found me.

"Tobias—" My hand was already fisted, ready.

"It's just me." He stepped out of the dark by the salt shed with a lantern shuttered to a slit, and God help me, my first feeling wasn't fear or guilt. It was relief. That's the part I can't put down even now—that in the worst hour of my life, seeing him made things lighter.

"You shouldn't be out here."

"Neither should you." He looked at me the way he always did, like he was checking whether I was hurt before anything else, and then past me, toward the glow of the pier. "Gideon. They took the old woman."

"I know. I was there."

"They'll have her in the company lockup by morning. Halloway will want her talked about in front of witnesses before anyone can ask why she was arrested instead of you."

"How do you know what Halloway wants?"

Something crossed his face. "Because I file his papers. Because I've been filing them for three years, and lately every third page has your family's name on it." He swallowed. "Because when the duty roster went up tonight, your name was already flagged. Before the pier. Hours before. He didn't follow you, Gideon. He *waited* for you."

The cold I felt then had nothing to do with the water. Flagged. Entered. My name sitting in his ledger with an annotation next to it, dated hours before I'd ever decided anything—as if I'd never had a choice at all, only a line item waiting for its figure.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "You could be dismissed for it. Worse."

Tobias was quiet for a moment. The shuttered lantern made a bar of light across his boots.

"You know why," he said, finally, very evenly. "You've known for two years. I've never once made you look at it, and I won't start tonight. But I'll tell you what I told myself when I copied the flag order into my coat pocket instead of the file." He looked up. "Some books you keep. Some books you burn. And some books you'd walk into a fire for, and I don't mean ledgers."

I couldn't answer him. There was Marisol in a cell because of me. There was my father six years drowned, refusing a falsified report, and here was a clerk with ink-stained fingers offering to lie to an inspector's face for no reason I could calculate, no debt, no promise, nothing but the plain unreasonable fact of me.

Every plan I had made tonight had been arithmetic. Keep the letter, save the name, trade the risk. Now the sums wouldn't hold. Marisol's face at the top of the ladder. Tobias's bar of lamplight. Love—his, hers, my father's—it got into every column and spoiled the figures, and I understood suddenly that this was exactly how Halloway caught people. Not by outsmarting them. By counting on the fact that they counted.

"Go home," Tobias said. "Divers' quarter, not your room. Your room will be watched by dawn. I'll come find you tomorrow with—" He stopped himself. "With whatever I can carry."

"Tobias."

"Don't thank me." He was already backing into the dark, lifting the lantern so its slit of light fell away from my face. "Just be somebody worth doing this for."

Then he was gone, and I was alone in the ropewalk with the letter against my ribs and the whole harbor holding my name in its teeth.

* * *

The divers' quarter took me in without questions, which is the custom of people who have learned that questions get answered badly. Old Rennick propped a mirror of tin by his door so I could see the lane behind me. Someone brought tea. Nobody asked about the pier, though everyone knew; news travels faster than watchmen in a place built on holding its breath.

I sat by Marisol's cold stove until the small hours, turning it over and over. Halloway had waited for me and taken her instead. Which meant he believed I still had the letter—which meant Marisol, in a cell, was worth nothing to him except bait. He would let her sit. He would let me hear about it. He would count on exactly the arithmetic that was already grinding through me: *one old woman against your father's truth against your family's freedom.*

And underneath the fear, something else was setting like concrete.

Because there was one other thing Tobias had said, half-finished, walking backward into the dark. *Whatever I can carry.*

I thought about what a clerk carries. Papers. Copies. Access.

I thought about the annual audit, three weeks away, when every record in the company's keeping would be opened before witnesses—and about Inspector Halloway, who had waited for me at pier forty-one because he was certain, absolutely certain, that a girl who survived by being overlooked would stay overlooked forever.

Somewhere across the harbor, in the company lockup, Marisol was proving him right or wrong at that very moment, and I couldn't reach her, and the not-reaching was going to kill me faster than any tribunal.

So I did the only thing left. I stopped running the sums.

I put my hand flat on the floorboards over the letter's hiding place and made the decision I would spend the rest of my life explaining: I was done delivering promises made by dead men through darkened piers. If Halloway wanted to read my name in his ledger, he could read it in front of the entire colony.

I just needed to know what Tobias could carry.
