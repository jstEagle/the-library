## 8. Enemy of the Crown

The notice went up on the harbor board at dawn, and by seven it had been read aloud in every language spoken south of the customs house.

BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY AND HIS MAJESTY'S COLONIAL SECRETARY: GIDEON WINTERBOURNE, DIVER, INDENTURE NO. 4471, IS DECLARED A COLLABORATOR WITH THE ENEMY AGENT ELIAS VANE. SHE IS TO BE DETAINED ON SIGHT. REWARD: FORTY POUNDS.

Forty pounds. Gideon read the number twice before she could feel anything about it at all. Forty pounds was three years of a diver's share. Forty pounds was what a person was worth to her neighbors now, the exact price of her arms and legs and the wet weight of her lungs. She had spent nineteen years learning to be unremarkable, and some clerk had priced her in an afternoon.

"Inside," Marisol said, and pulled her off the street by the elbow, into the smoke-dark kitchen where the divers' quarter kept its secrets. The shutters came down. Someone moved the cooking pot over the fire so the smell of fish stew would cover everything else.

The quarter hid her the way water hides a stone. Word passed door to door without paper, without names: *the girl is here, say nothing.* Old divers who had never spoken two words to her father while he lived now stood in their doorways at odd hours, watching the lanes. The silence Marisol had described—the half-harbor silence that had held the promise safe for years—had turned itself inside out and become a wall.

It was not comfort. It was siege. And Gideon knew the difference even as she was grateful for it.

By noon the harbor was locked. She heard it before she saw it from the gap between shutter slats: the chain going across the channel mouth, the company launch running its slow patrol, the Customs cutter riding heavy with marines. No boats out. No mail. The lascar crews confined to their decks. The whole bright machine of the port seized up like a fist closing.

They were not looking for Vane. Not really. They were looking for one woman with a letter.

She sat on Marisol's floor that first night with the letter wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, under her shirt, against skin still salted from a childhood of diving. She had not let it out of arm's reach since the pier. Since Marisol—Marisol, who had gone to stand in for her, who had been taken in her place, whose name Gideon could not even say aloud without her throat closing.

*I'm sorry* was useless. *I'm sorry* was a coin so debased it wouldn't buy bread. So Gideon did the only thing left. She stayed alive, and she kept the letter dry, and she waited for fear to finish with her.

It took two days. On the third day, somewhere between midnight and the grey hour when even the gulls quit, the fear burned down to its coals and something else was sitting there when the smoke cleared.

Not courage. She would not insult herself with that word. It was colder than courage and simpler. It was arithmetic. Halloway had made her invisible all her life and then erased her entirely in one printed notice, and if she was already nothing—if she had already lost indenture, wages, name, safety, all of it itemized away—then she had nothing left to protect except the truth. And the truth, unlike her, could not be detained on sight unless someone chose to speak it.

Someone was going to have to choose.

* * *

Tobias Fenwick kept the ledgers of the Meridian Trading Company in a hand so fine his superiors called it feminine, which he permitted because it made them underestimate him, and underestimation was the only currency a junior clerk ever accumulated.

He also kept, folded inside his waistcoat, a copy of the 1907 wreck report of the *Cormorant*, transcribed in that same fine hand over four stolen nights, initialed where the original bore initials, because Tobias had understood something early about paper: that institutions do not run on truth, they run on records, and whoever holds the record holds the institution's throat. He had copied the report years ago for reasons he could not then have explained—instinct, boredom, the small rebellions of an unnoticed man—and he had told no one, not even Gideon Winterbourne, whom he loved with the patient, hopeless discipline of a man who catalogued things for a living and knew better than to misfile his own heart.

Now he sat across from Inspector Halloway and watched the inspector ruin himself, and understood that the hour of his hoarded paper had arrived.

"You understand the difficulty," Halloway was saying. He did not look up from the file on his desk. He never looked up; it was how he made men confess to things just to see his eyes. "Vane's network is scattered, but the letter is the proof of conspiracy. Without the letter, we have testimony. With it, we have evidence. The Winterbourne woman has it. Therefore the Winterbourne woman must be found."

"And when she is found, sir."

Halloway looked up then, which meant something. "Then the matter resolves itself." He closed the file. Tobias saw, in the half-second before it shut, the draft pages inside—a new report, already begun, dated forward. *Recovered from the collaborator Winterbourne upon arrest: correspondence confirming the family's long complicity with enemy agent Vane...*

Complicity. Long complicity. Tobias felt the words go through him like cold water through cloth. Halloway was not planning to destroy the letter, or not only that. He was planning to make it mean the opposite of what it meant—to turn Elias Vane's dying testimony into a confession of Winterbourne guilt, to bury the falsified wreck report and the dead father under the same shovelful, and to hang the whole rotten business on a family that could not answer back. One dead already. One declared traitor before any court convened.

"The governor's audit is in nine days," Tobias said carefully. "All ledgers will be open. All files produced."

"All files *required*," Halloway corrected mildly. "Whether all files are produced depends on the diligence of the producing clerks." He smiled without warmth. "You're diligent, Fenwick. That's why I keep you close. Now. The debt registers. Bring me the Winterbourne ledger entry. Original only. There are irregularities I wish to review privately."

There were no irregularities. Tobias knew the entry by heart—knew the whole sad architecture of it, interest compounded on a dead man's dive gear, the debt designed never to shrink. But he said, "At once, sir," and went to fetch it, and walked the corridor with his face arranged into clerkly blankness while his pulse hammered out a different arithmetic entirely.

If the letter vanished into Halloway's private review, it would reappear as evidence against the Winterbournes, and no one—not the governor, not the courts, not God Himself reading the manifest—would ever know otherwise. Unless someone stood in a room full of witnesses and said otherwise, with the true papers in hand.

Nine days. He had nine days, a copied wreck report sewn into his coat lining, and one road into the divers' quarter that the patrols hadn't thought to watch: the old eel-catcher's drain beneath the sea wall, which flooded at half tide and which he had used exactly once before, at seventeen, for reasons involving a girl and a stolen orange. His stomach turned over at the memory of the water. He went anyway. Some debts you paid forward.

* * *

He came to her at low tide, in the dark, smelling of brine and lamp oil, and Marisol's neighbors nearly killed him before Gideon recognized the shape of him in the doorway—Tobias, soaked to the thighs, hat crushed, holding a parcel wrapped in waxed paper as though it were the most natural thing in the world to bring a fugitive her supper.

"Bread," he said. "And cheese that has committed no crime against anyone. Also—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do, dropped its careful clerical evenness— "also bad news, which I'm afraid travels faster."

"Sit down before you fall down," Marisol said, and put tea in front of him, and Gideon realized the old woman had known he would come this way eventually. Had perhaps been leaving the door unlatched.

Tobias ate half the bread, because Gideon made him, and then he told it plainly, the way he entered figures: the draft report, the phrase *long complicity*, the order for the Winterbourne debt ledger, Halloway's plan laid out in clean handwriting like everything else that man touched.

"They'll destroy the letter," he finished. "Or worse—they'll keep it, and produce it rewritten. Either way, your father dies again, officially, as a guilty man. And you hang for his sins instead of his honesty." He set down the crust he'd been holding. "I'm sorry. I wanted to bring better news."

Gideon found she wasn't afraid anymore. That was the strange thing. She listened to the whole architecture of her own destruction and felt only a rising heat, steady as a furnace drawing, the fury of a woman who has finally stopped checking whether anyone is watching.

"He wants the letter," she said. "Fine. He'll get it. In front of the governor, in front of the audit, in front of every clerk and official and diver he drags in to witness his clean little ledger." She looked at Tobias. "You said all files are required. Required means witnesses. Witnesses mean a room I can walk into."

"A room guarded by marines."

"I've held my breath longer than their patience will last."

Marisol laughed once, low, from the chest. Tobias did not laugh. He reached instead into his coat lining and drew out folded papers, and laid them on the table between the bread and the lamp: the *Cormorant* wreck report, page after page in his fine clerk's hand, initialed true.

Gideon stared at it. "How long have you had this?"

"Since 1908." He would not meet her eyes. "I don't know why. I didn't know why until tonight."

Outside, the patrol boat's lamp swept the shutter slats, crossed the floor, and slid away. Three people sat absolutely still around a table with treason on it, and none of them moved, and when the light was gone Gideon put her hand flat over the copied pages as if over a wound that had finally stopped bleeding.

"The audit," she said. "Nine days."

"Eight," said Tobias. "They moved it up."
