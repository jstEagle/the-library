## 9. The Reading Room

The governor's audit fell on the third Tuesday of October, as it had every year since before Gideon could read. By tradition older than anyone in the harbor could name, the Meridian Trading Company opened its ledgers to colonial inspection on that day—every book balanced, every debt displayed, every indenture witnessed aloud in the reading room where clerks in pressed collars recited numbers like scripture.

By law, the audit was public. Anyone whose name appeared in those books had the right to stand before the assembled officials and hear it spoken.

Gideon Winterbourne's name appeared in those books.

She had spent eleven days in the divers' quarter since Tobias brought her word of Halloway's plan. Eleven days of salt fish and whispered strategy, of Marisol's kitchen filling each night with faces she'd never noticed before—the harbor's silent half, surfacing one by one. The old harpooner Deen who had watched her father sign his mark. The cooper's widow who kept a duplicate manifest from 1907 because she distrusted company arithmetic. A boy of sixteen who could recite the tide tables for three years running and had memorized which clerk altered which column.

They had given her something no one had ever given her: an audience.

"You don't have to do it yourself," Marisol said on the last night, working a needle through Gideon's torn dive sleeve as though mending were prayer. "Half these people would carry the letter for you. Put it in front of the governor themselves."

"That's exactly why I have to do it myself." Gideon turned the letter over in her hands. The wax was broken now—had been broken since the night she first cracked it—but she had learned its contents the way divers learn a reef: by touch, by repetition, by lamplight until she could recite Elias Vane's testimony with her eyes shut. Her father's refusal. Halloway's predecessor's falsified wreck report. The payments routed through Meridian accounts. The names, all of them. "If someone else speaks for me, then it's their story. Their risk. And Halloway will find a way to make it theirs alone to answer for." She looked up. "My father made a promise. I'm the only Winterbourne left to keep it."

Marisol bit her thread and tied it off. "Then we make sure you live long enough to finish."

* * *

The reading room smelled of lamp oil and floor polish, of paper and the particular staleness of power that has sat too long in one place. Gideon entered through the public door at nine o'clock precisely, wearing her good dress—the gray one, unremarkable—and carrying the letter inside her bodice, flat against her heart, where it had lived so long she sometimes forgot it wasn't part of her body.

The room was fuller than she had ever seen it. Governor Ashcombe himself presided from the raised desk, a dry man with dry hands who turned ledger pages as if he were shuffling cards. Beside him sat the colonial auditor, two magistrates, and Inspector Halloway.

Halloway saw her at once. Of course he did. That was the point.

She watched the recognition move across his face like weather—not surprise, she noted, and that frightened her more than anything. His eyes flicked to the door where four company constables stood, then back to her, and something almost gentle came into his expression. *Come here, then,* that look said. *Finish your mistake.*

"Names called in order of entry," droned the head clerk, a reedy man named Pell who had held his post for thirty years. "Ledger citations follow. Public witnesses may speak after citation."

Gideon took her place at the back, among the standing crowd. Behind her she felt them—the divers who had come off night boats to be present, still smelling of brine, taking up space in a room they had never been welcome in. Deen. The cooper's widow. Sixteen-year-old Nami with her tide tables. They did not look at her. They had agreed not to look at her. Let the room believe she was alone.

The morning crawled. Debts were cited. Indentures renewed. A shipping dispute consumed forty minutes over the price of copra. Gideon counted her own heartbeats and found them steady, which surprised her; she had expected to be shaking. Instead she felt strangely clear, the way the water went clear at thirty feet when everything above you stopped mattering.

At half past eleven, Pell's voice cracked on a name.

"Winterbourne. Gideon. Indentured diver, entered into service of the Meridian Trading Company, debt of inheritance, original sum—" He paused. Squinted at the page. "Inspector, this entry is flagged."

Halloway rose without hurry. "Flagged for review under security provisions," he said smoothly. "The Winterbourne account is connected to an ongoing investigation into enemy correspondence. I'd ask the court to seal it."

"I haven't heard any evidence yet," said the junior magistrate.

"You're about to," said Gideon.

Her voice didn't carry the way she wanted it to—it came out thinner than in Marisol's kitchen—but the room went quiet anyway, the way rooms go quiet when someone who has never spoken finally does. Every face turned. Governor Ashcombe lowered his spectacles.

"Name?" he said.

"Gideon Winterbourne. And I have the right to be heard before my name is sealed. That's the law. Your law." She walked forward, past the clerk's desks, past the constables—one of them reached out and Halloway, very slightly, shook his head—and stopped at the open floor beneath the governor's desk. She drew the letter from her bodice.

Halloway moved first. He crossed the floor in four strides, hand extended, voice pitched for reasonableness. "Miss Winterbourne. Whatever you've been told that paper contains, you've been used. Vane's network recruits the desperate and disposes of them. Give it to me, testify to how it came into your possession, and I give you my word—"

"Your word." She held the letter against her chest. "Your word is in this letter, Inspector. Yours and your predecessors'. Shall I read the parts?"

"Governor," Halloway said, turning, "this document may contain material harmful to ongoing negotiations with foreign powers. Under the Emergency Correspondence Act—"

"Under the Audit Act of 1898," Gideon said, louder now, finding the bottom of her voice the way she'd found the bottom of the lagoon a thousand times, "any document touching a company ledger entry must be produced and read before the audit is closed. My father's name is in your books, Inspector. So are the payments. So is the falsified wreck report of the *Cormorant*, filed in 1907, signed by a company surveyor who was dead six months before the ink dried." She looked at Ashcombe. "I am invoking my right of citation. Read me, or read it. But the audit doesn't close today with my family's name sealed in it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water.

Ashcombe looked at Halloway. Halloway looked back, and for one suspended moment Gideon understood that everything would be decided not by law or letters but by whichever of these two men blinked first.

The governor blinked. "Read," he said.

* * *

She had rehearsed it until the words wore grooves in her. But nothing had prepared her for the sound of her own voice filling that room—Vane's careful testimony in her dockworker's accent, the incongruity of it, treason dressed in salt-stained cloth. She read the date. She read the promise: *Gideon, when peace ends, your family owes the truth to no one more than to itself.* She read the wreck report's true contents—the *Cormorant* had gone down carrying contraband manifests approved, stamped, and paid for through Meridian account fourteen; her father had refused to certify the false version; her father had been transferred to deep-water work the following month, work that killed him, work assigned by—

"Enough." Halloway's voice cut like a cleaver. "Governor, this is fabrication. This letter is a forgery planted by a convicted enemy agent. I have sworn statements—"

"Produce the original wreck report," Gideon said. "File reference C-1907-44. It's in your own archive, Inspector. Or is it?"

Halloway smiled. It was the worst thing she had seen him do. "The archive suffered water damage last spring. Many files were lost." He spread his hands, a man regretting fate. "How unfortunate. Nothing to compare it against."

"There's something to compare it against," said a voice from the doorway.

Tobias came down the center aisle holding a leather folio the way a man carries something that might detonate. His collar was crooked. His hands were shaking. He had never looked braver. He stopped beside Gideon, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him, and laid the folio on the clerk's table.

"Full copy of file C-1907-44," he said. His voice cracked; he steadied it. "Copied over four months, in sections, during archive inventory. Certified against the original by three separate clerks who will attest if asked." He swallowed. "I am one of them. Clerk Second Class Tobias Merrick. Employee number 2211."

Halloway stared at him. Something behind his eyes recalculated—Gideon watched it happen, watched a man discover that the quiet ones had been keeping count all along, that invisibility ran both directions, that the boy he had never once bothered to learn the name of had spent a season copying his crimes in secret.

"You'll hang for theft of company property," Halloway said softly.

"Maybe," said Tobias. "Open it."

Ashcombe opened it. He read. His dry hands, Gideon noticed, were not entirely steady either. He read for a long time while the room held its breath—officials, clerks, divers, everyone pressed into the same terrible stillness—and then he set the folio down and looked at Halloway with an expression that contained no anger at all, which was somehow more terrifying than rage.

"Inspector Halloway," the governor said. "You'll want to sit down."

Halloway did not sit. He lunged instead—for the letter in Gideon's hands, one last grab at the thing that had undone him—and he got no further than an arm's length before Deen's fist, seventeen years of hauling lines behind it, caught him across the jaw and put him on the floor between the desks.

The constables hesitated. Then, slowly, they stepped away from him.

In the ringing quiet, Gideon realized she was still holding the letter, and that she had not stopped reading—that somewhere in the chaos her voice had continued, steady as ballast, finishing the sentence her father had died rather than falsify:

*"—and I sign this not with a mark but with my name, because a name is the one thing they cannot confiscate."*

She looked down at the page. Then at Tobias, bleeding lip, crooked collar, watching her as though she were the only solid thing in the room. Then at the doors, where the harbor's silent half stood shoulder to shoulder, waiting to see what the world would do next.

The governor cleared his throat.

"The audit," he said, "is not closed."
