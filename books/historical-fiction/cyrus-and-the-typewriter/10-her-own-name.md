## 10. Her Own Name

The monsoon came early that year, and with it the inquiry.

They set up the tribunal in the customs house, the same building where Gideon had once stood for six hours on her fourteenth birthday, waiting to be counted and catalogued and entered into the company's books as property. Now the ledgers were stacked on long tables like evidence at a drowning trial—because that was what they were, she supposed. Evidence that people had been weighed, priced, and spent.

Halloway had fallen quietly, which surprised no one who understood how empires worked. No spectacle. No trial by mob. One morning he simply wasn't in his office anymore, and his name was struck from the manifests as cleanly as a diver's name was once struck from the rolls when the sea took him. Inspector Halloway, erased by the same machinery he'd served so faithfully. She found she couldn't hate him the way she'd expected to. He had been, in the end, only another man who believed the books were truer than the bodies they described.

Vane's exoneration had come in April, printed on cheap paper in language so careful it barely admitted there had ever been anything to exonerate. *The Crown withdraws all charges. The Crown regrets.* The Crown, it turned out, could regret a great many things without ever apologizing.

Gideon read the notice standing in Marisol's kitchen, where the smoke still curled the same way it had a year ago, and Marisol poured tea the same way, and outside the harbor still breathed its slow salt breath as though nothing had changed at all.

"Read it again," Marisol said. "Loud. Some things you have to hear out loud to believe."

So Gideon read it aloud, and her voice didn't shake, and afterward they sat in silence while the rain came down on the corrugated roof like applause from an indifferent god.

* * *

The letter itself lived now in the colonial archive, under glass, behind a brass plaque that spelled her father's name correctly for the first time in eleven years. WINTERBOURNE. Not Winterburne, as the company manifests had it. Not *diver, deceased, debt outstanding*. His whole name, his letters, his refusal preserved like a fly in amber.

She went to see it once, in the first week of May, and stood before the case until a clerk asked if she required assistance.

"No," she said. "I'm just visiting."

It was strange, visiting your own history in a government building. Strange to think that all those years she'd spent being unremarkable—the careful flatness of her walk, the way she'd learned to pass through rooms like weather—the whole time, the most remarkable thing about her had already been written down. Filed away. Waiting. Her father had died refusing to lie, and Elias Vane had kept the proof of it for a decade, and half the harbor had held the secret in their cupped hands like water, passing it from kitchen to kitchen, diver to diver, until it reached a nineteen-year-old girl who thought she had no story at all.

History hadn't been something she made. It had happened to her—invisible, patient, accumulating interest in someone else's ledger—until the day she cracked the wax and let it happen through her instead.

That distinction mattered. She was still learning why.

* * *

Vane came to the harbor himself in June, which caused a sensation among the younger divers, who had heard his name spoken half in fear and half in reverence for so long that seeing him in the flesh—a spare man in his fifties with sun-damaged hands and a limp he tried to hide—was like seeing a figure step off a coin.

He found Gideon mending nets on the long pier, because some work you did even when the company no longer owned your hours. Habit was its own kind of tide.

"Miss Winterbourne." He said the name deliberately, the way you'd test ice before stepping onto it.

"Mr. Vane."

"I owe you my life. Or at least my good name, which for a merchant is nearly the same thing." He sat down on the pier beside her, uninvited, unhurried, and looked out at the water. "I've arranged passage. Singapore first, then London. There are people there who should hear what you know firsthand—the registry men, the reformers. They'd give you a position. Protection. A future with walls around it."

"Away," she said.

"Away," he agreed. "Which is what I promised your father I'd offer, when the time came. He asked me once—if it ever ended, if peace ever came—see that my girl gets somewhere the company can't reach."

Gideon folded the netting in her lap and considered the water below, where a school of silver fish scattered and reformed, scattered and reformed, each one safe only inside the crowd.

"He also signed a registry petition," she said. "In 'ninety-nine. Did you know that? Marisol showed me. His mark's on it, third page, right between old Chen and Father Anselm. He wanted the divers counted. Named. On the books as men, not as line items." She looked at Vane. "You're offering me somewhere the company can't reach. But the company is being reached. Right now. In the customs house, every day, by people with my father's temper and worse handwriting. The inquiry doesn't end when the officials are disgraced. It ends when the system that let them do it can't be rebuilt."

Vane was quiet a long moment. Then he smiled—not the merchant's smile she'd seen in photographs, but something older and more tired and more real.

"You could come back in ten years," he offered. "When it's safe."

"It'll never be safe," Gideon said. "That's not what any of this was for."

He left three days later on the *Meridian Star*, ironic name and all, but before he sailed he pressed an envelope into her hands—unsealed, this time, nothing hidden—and inside was a letter of introduction, addressed To Whom It May Concern, describing one Gideon Winterbourne in terms so generous she laughed out loud alone in her room.

*She is the reason there is anything left to introduce.*

* * *

The hearing for the Divers' Registry Act fell on the last hot Tuesday of summer.

The customs house chamber was full in a way it had never been full for anything concerning divers: clerks in the gallery, newspapermen with their stub pencils, a scatter of officials wearing the particular blank faces of men calculating which way to jump. And along one wall, sitting together in an untidy row, the divers themselves—old Chen, Marisol, a dozen others who had spent lifetimes being counted and never once consulted.

Tobias saved her a seat near the front. He wore his best coat and his clerk's face, which fooled nobody anymore, least of all her. Since the night he'd looked away on the long pier—since the original wreck report had emerged from his copying desk like a rabbit from a hat—he had stopped pretending to be invisible too. It suited him badly and wonderfully, like a boy wearing his father's coat.

"Nervous?" he murmured.

"Terrified," she said. "But it's a useful terror. I know where it lives now."

The testimony ran all day. Economists with figures. Doctors with lungs in jars, blackened by years of pressure, passed hand to hand like relics. A company solicitor who argued, with a straight face, that the indenture rolls were *voluntary instruments freely executed*, and had to pause while the entire gallery of divers laughed at once.

Then the chairman called her name.

"Gideon Winterbourne."

Walking to the stand took eleven steps. She counted them, an old habit, measuring distances in case she needed to run. But she didn't run. She stood, and she looked out at the room—at the clerks and the reporters and the row of salt-cured faces against the wall—and she thought about her father, who had put his mark on a petition twenty years ago believing someone would read it someday. History had happened to him. It had happened to all of them, silently, whether or not anyone wrote it down.

The difference was what you did when it finally called on you.

"State your name for the record," the chairman said.

"Gideon Winterbourne. Daughter of Tomas Winterbourne, diver, deceased. Formerly indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, account number 4471."

"And you carry testimony relevant to the wreck report of the *Cormorant*?"

"I carry testimony relevant to everything the wreck report was designed to hide." She laid her papers on the rail, each one squared. "For eleven years, my family's death and my family's debt were the same document. I'm here to separate them."

She spoke for forty minutes. Her voice held.

* * *

Afterward, outside on the customs house steps, the evening light came gold across the harbor, and Tobias waited at the bottom with two cups of sugared tea bought from the widow Chen's cart.

"They'll vote within the month," he said. "And it'll pass. Even the ones who hate it will pass it—no one wants to be the man who voted for drowning."

"That's how most things pass," Gideon said. "Nobody wanting to be the man who voted wrong."

He handed her a cup. Steam rose between them. Down the pier, the evening boats came home low in the water, heavy with shell, and the divers moved among the crates—counted now, named now, their names going into new registers with clean pages and honest columns.

"There's something else," Tobias said, and produced a paper from his coat. The registry enrollment form. Official stock, cream-colored, with its blank lines waiting. "Marisol said you'd want to do it tonight. Before the vote. Before anyone makes it ceremonial." He hesitated, then smiled. "Your father signed with a mark. They wouldn't let a diver write, back then. Didn't trust the hands."

Gideon took the form and read down its questions. Name. Parentage. Occupation. All the small facts of a life, requested at last as though the life mattered.

She borrowed Tobias's pen.

Where it asked for her name, she wrote *Gideon Winterbourne* in full, in her own careful hand, and watched the ink dry in the sea air. Eleven strokes for her given name, and beneath it the surname that had been misspelled on manifests, misfiled in ledgers, spoken aloud in a reading room at the risk of everything—and was now simply hers, on a public page, in daylight, where anyone might read it and know that a diver's daughter had stood up in front of the crown's own witnesses and told the truth all the way to the end.

Her father had signed a mark because they never taught him otherwise. She signed her whole name because he made sure, in dying, that there would be something left to sign for.

History had happened to her all along—silent, patient, written in other people's ink. But this line, this name, these eleven strokes drying in the harbor wind: this was the first sentence she had written herself.

Below it, the next question waited in crisp official type:

*Witnessed by:*

Tobias held out his hand for the pen.
