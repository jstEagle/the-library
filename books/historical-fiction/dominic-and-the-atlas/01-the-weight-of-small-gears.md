## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The last gear of my apprenticeship piece was the smallest thing I had ever been asked to make, and it was also the heaviest.

It weighed, by my brass scale, four grains. A moth's whisper. A crumb of a crumb. And yet when I set it on the soft leather of my palm and looked at it under the lamp, I felt the whole weight of six years pressing down on that little wheel — every scolded filing, every ruined blank, every night Dominic had stood behind my shoulder and said in his dry gravel voice, *Again,* until the word wore a groove in me like a pivot wears a jewel.

Four grains. I weighed everything. It was a habit I'd taught myself at seven years old, back when I arrived at the workshop door with nothing but the coat I stood in and a name nobody could quite pronounce. If you know what a thing weighs, you know whether you can carry it when the time comes to go. Everything I owned fit into one cedar box under my cot: two combs, a book of poems with a water-stained cover, a spool of blue silk thread I had never found a use for, my father's penknife, and now — though it would not stay there long — this.

I turned the gear under the lamp. The teeth were even. The wheel was true. Sixty-four teeth, each filed by hand because the cutting engine was Dominic's and Dominic's alone, and he had said, only three days ago, "An apprentice finishes by hand or doesn't finish at all." My thumb ached from the graver. There was a bright red callus on my middle finger that I suspected would be with me until I died, like a freckle or a sin.

"Stop looking at it," said Marisol from the doorway, wiping her hands on an apron that had once been white in some other century. "You'll jinx it. Come and eat."

"It needs to be fitted."

"It needed fitting an hour ago. Now it needs soup. Gears don't get cold, Wren, but soup does."

Marisol had opinions about the proper order of all things — soup before gears, prayers before sleep, gossip before breakfast — and she enforced them the way a harbormaster enforces the tide tables: absolutely, and with a wooden spoon if necessary. She was broad as a barrel and twice as warm, and she had been feeding this household since before I could reach the stove without a stool. When I came to Brightwater Court I was so thin she had cried, which frightened me more than anything else about that first terrible week, and then she had fed me eggs until I understood that crying and feeding were, for her, the same language.

I set the gear down on a square of chamois, put the chamois in the cedar box, closed the lid, and latched it. Then I let her herd me out of the workroom with the spoon, because there is no arguing with Marisol, only surviving her.

* * *

The kitchen of the Brightwater workshop was the heart of the building the way the balance wheel is the heart of a watch — small, warm, and governing everything else without appearing to move at all. The room held a scrubbed table, six chairs that did not match and never had, a stove that Marisol spoke to as if it were a horse she was training, and a smell of onion and thyme that had soaked so deep into the plaster I believe you could strip the walls to brick and still find it there.

Tobias was already at the table, as he always was at half past six exactly, with his ledgers squared in front of him like a fortification. Tobias kept the household accounts and did it beautifully, in a hand so fine and regular it looked printed, and he did everything else in his life the same way: precisely, punctually, and as though someone might come through the door at any moment and catch him at it. He had been with us eleven years and still sat with his back to no wall that had a window in it, and still flinched — just slightly, just a tightening around the eyes — when anyone knocked.

"Evening, Wren," he said, not looking up. Then he did look up, and his whole narrow face rearranged itself into something almost relaxed. "Finished, then?"

"The last gear." I took my chair, the one nearest the stove, which was mine by ancient treaty. "It ran true on the test stand. Dominic hasn't seen it yet."

"He'll see it," said Ezra from his corner.

Ezra occupied the corner chair the way a lighthouse occupies a headland: massively, permanently, and with the air of having witnessed worse weather. He was our retired sailor, seventy if he was a day, with forearms like mooring cable and a white beard he trimmed himself, unevenly, with the same scissors he used on the twine. He had come off the sea twenty years ago with bad lungs and a chest of stories, and he had installed himself in the workshop's corner on the grounds — as he put it — that somebody had to sit near the door and judge visitors before they got comfortable. He judged them all. He judged the milkman, the knife-grinder, the tax assessor (harshly), and me, when I was seven, for a full month, before deciding I would do.

"He'll see it tomorrow," Ezra said now, "and he'll say the pallets are a hair proud, because he says that to everyone, and then he'll take it round to the Guild himself in that coat of his, and he'll tell the whole Bench his apprentice finished early. Which you did."

"By four days," I admitted.

Tobias made a note of it. He genuinely did; I saw him write *Wren — piece complete* in the margin of something, as though my apprenticeship were a shipment due and he its manifest clerk. That was Tobias. He believed that anything written down became true, and anything true should be written down before it could change its mind.

Marisol landed the tureen on the table hard enough to rattle the salt cellar. "Ezra, wash your hands, I can see the tar from here. Wren, elbows. Tobias, put those away or wear them, I don't care which, but the soup is not going to audit itself."

"They're audited," Tobias murmured, but he closed the ledger.

We ate the way we always ate, which is to say we ate while talking, and talked while eating, and none of it was important. Marisol reported that the greengrocer on Carter Lane had tried to sell her wilting parsley and had received, in her words, "an education." Ezra gave his opinion of a steamship he'd seen towed upriver that morning — too much beam, too little soul, "a floating ledger, that one, all tonnage and no poetry." Tobias asked whether anyone had seen his good pen, and we all lied and said no, because we had all seen his good pen, because I had borrowed his good pen three days ago and it was at this moment in my apron pocket, and returning it tonight would mean admitting I'd had it, and there are debts you pay and debts you delay, and a pen between friends belongs to the second kind.

Ordinary. All of it ordinary. The stove ticking as it cooled, the rain starting against the dark window, the smell of thyme and lamp oil, Ezra's laugh like a keel scraping gravel. I sat in my chair with my bowl going empty and felt the contentment settle over me like a coat that fits — and underneath the contentment, as always, faint as a hairline crack in a mainspring, the other feeling. The one I never said aloud.

The feeling that said: *borrowed.*

I don't know how else to describe it. Everything I loved in that room was real — I tested it daily, the way I test a train of gears, tooth by tooth — but somewhere under all of it ran the knowledge that households like ours were built on sufferance. Dominic Vale had collected us. That was the joke Ezra told strangers: *Vale collects strays.* An orphan off the Fenchurch steps, a cook with no references and no surname she'd claim, a clerk who would not discuss the eight years before Brightwater Court, a sailor washed ashore. None of us related by blood. None of us with any paper saying we belonged to each other or to him. And paper, I knew even then — I knew it the way you know winter is coming, without wanting to — was the only thing the world outside our door believed in.

So I measured things. Small things, portable things. If the worst ever happened, if Dominic died or tired of us or the rent came due one final time unpaid, I would be able to carry what mattered: my tools, my box, my two combs and my water-stained book. I had rehearsed leaving so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone turned over in the pocket.

I just never wanted to do it. That was the cruelty of the habit. You prepare your escape from the place you love most, and preparing becomes its own way of staying.

"Wren." Marisol snapped her fingers under my nose. "You've gone away again. Where do you go?"

"Nowhere," I said. "Just counting."

"You count like other people brood." She pushed the bread at me. "Eat."

* * *

After supper the household drifted apart into its evening orbits, the way it had for as long as I had known it, reliable as escapement and dial. Tobias to the front office to close the books; Marisol to the scullery with Ezra drying beside her, their argument about whether nutmeg belonged in rice pudding conducted at the low, well-worn register of a debate entering its fifteenth year. I went back to the workroom, because I had promised myself I would present the apprenticeship piece to Dominic properly mounted, and a proper mounting takes an hour, and an hour spent making a thing right is never wasted. That last part is Dominic's. He has a hundred sayings and they are all the same saying: *do it true.*

The workroom at night was my favorite version of anywhere. Lamplight pooled on the bench; the great regulator clock on the wall breathed its slow tick-tock over everything, the loudest silence in the world. Dominic's own bench sat at the far end under its dust sheet — he had not worked at it in weeks — and mine sat near the window where I could watch the court below, the lamps coming on across the roofs, the city settling itself like a machine finding its beat.

I was fitting the movement into its case — a small carriage clock, brass, with a silvered dial I had engraved myself — when the floorboard in the corridor creaked, and Dominic looked in.

He did that, sometimes: appeared at the edge of lamplight without announcing himself, quiet as a man checking on a fire he wasn't sure he trusted. He was thinner than he used to be. I had noticed it the way you notice a clock running slow — a few seconds a day, deniable, cumulative. His collar hung looser. His hands, those great precise instruments, had developed a tremor he hid by resting them on things.

"Still at it," he said.

"Mounting the movement. It goes to the Guild Thursday."

"Mm." He came and stood over the bench, and I felt him look at the case, the dial, the tiny polished clicks and screws laid out in ranks on the baize like soldiers at inspection. For a long moment he didn't speak, and I learned later that when Dominic Vale is silent longest, it is because he has the most to say and no safe way to begin it.

But that night I only thought: *he's proud, and he can't find the words, and that's fine, I'll carry the words for both of us.*

"You measured the escapement?" he said at last.

"To a tenth of a millimetre. Twice."

"And the beat?"

"Even. Nine thousand an hour." I hesitated, then said the rest, because apprenticeship pieces are supposed to be defended: "I let it run fast by half a second over the first hour and corrected it with the rating nut rather than re-pinning. It seemed more honest to leave the correction visible."

Dominic was quiet. Then he reached out and touched the case, one fingertip, the way he touched nothing else in the world.

"Honesty you can adjust," he said. "There's a lesson in that worth more than the Guild will know." And he looked at me — really looked, that strange searching look he sometimes gave me, as though comparing my face to some document only he could see — and said, "You've grown into yourself, Wren Brightwater."

Nobody had given me the name Brightwater. I had chosen it myself at nine, out of a book, because it sounded like a place where water ran clean, and Dominic had entered it into the household ledger without comment, in Tobias's beautiful hand, as though I had always been so. Hearing it in his mouth always did something to my chest that I refused to examine.

"Thank you," I said, which was inadequate, and he nodded, which forgave it, and he turned toward the door.

Then stopped.

For a moment he stood with his hand on the frame, and the lamplight put shadows in the hollows of his face, and I saw how tired he was — not evening-tired but something deeper, a fatigue with ballast in it. "Go to bed soon," he said. "Tomorrow may be—" He stopped again, chose a different word entirely. "The post comes early."

And he was gone, his tread fading down the corridor, slower than it used to be.

I stood at my bench with the little clock half-cased in front of me and its even beat filling the room, and I felt the hairline crack under the contentment shift, very slightly, wider. *Tomorrow may be—* May be what? Dominic left sentences unfinished perhaps once a year. A man who teaches you to file a truth clean does not leave burrs on his own.

I finished the mounting. I couldn't have said why I hurried.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when I heard the side door of the yard open and close — softly, softly, the latch lifted rather than dropped — and I knew it was not Ezra, who slams doors the way other men shake hands, and not Tobias, who locks things thrice and tests the lock after. So I did something I am not proud of and have never once regretted: I put out my lamp, opened the corridor door a finger's width, and watched.

Dominic was in the passage by the coat pegs, dressed again, coat collared, hat in hand. On the hall table lay the iron cashbox that lived locked in his room, and he was standing over a small fire burning in the grate — a fire, at midnight, in October, in a house heated by one stove and stubbornness.

He was feeding papers to it. One at a time. Carefully, watching each catch, holding pages by the corner until the flame took them completely. He burned maybe five letters, and I could read nothing of them, only the shapes of handwriting curling black. And then he took up the last envelope, and paused over it longer than all the others together, and the light from the grate came up between us and showed me the seal.

Black wax. A seal stamped in black wax — not the plain disc of a merchant, but a device, some crest or cipher pressed deep — and I remember thinking, absurdly, professionally: *that die must be fine work, whoever cut it knew their graver.*

Dominic looked at the seal for the length of three ticks of the hall clock. His shoulders, beneath the good coat, might have been carrying rigging.

Then he cracked the black wax with his thumb, unfolded whatever the letter held, read it once through, and fed it to the fire — envelope, wax, and all — and stood watching until the last of it curled, and stirred the ashes with the poker until they were indistinguishable from any winter's leavings, and took up his hat, and let himself out into the rain.

I closed the door. I stood in the dark of my workroom with my heart going like a flywheel gone loose, and behind me the little carriage clock ticked evenly on, honest to a tenth of a millimetre, keeping the beat of a night that had, without warning, changed time altogether.

Borrowed, whispered the old voice under my ribs. Borrowed, borrowed.

The courier came at half past seven the next morning, knocking loud enough to wake the whole court.
