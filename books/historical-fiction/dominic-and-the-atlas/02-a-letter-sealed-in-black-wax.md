## 2. A Letter Sealed in Black Wax

The knock came at half past four, which was itself an event, because nobody knocked at the Brightwater workshop door who had any business there. Marisol knocked twice with the flat of her hand when supper was ready, but that was a different sort of knock entirely — a knock you could set a clock by, and often did. Tobias never knocked; he had a key and the manners of a man who had once been turned away from too many doors to risk it. Ezra came and went through the yard like weather.

This was a courier's knock: three sharp raps, brass-tipped cane against the oak, the sound of somebody being paid by the delivery rather than the conversation.

Wren heard it from the bench, where she was fitting the last wheel of her apprenticeship piece — a small regulator clock, eight-day movement, cased in walnut she had spent three months making herself hate. She did not look up. Looking up cost time, and the escapement was balanced on the edge of cooperation in a way that demanded superstition. Every clockmaker she had ever met was superstitious about something. Dominic's superstition was that a movement should be assembled without swearing; Wren's was that a nearly-finished clock should not be abandoned mid-thought, even for kings, archbishops, or supper.

Marisol answered the door. Wren heard the exchange in fragments over the tick of forty-odd clocks: the courier's flat official voice, Marisol's rising one, the word *Observatory* spoken twice, and then a silence with a particular quality to it — the silence of a household holding its breath all at once without coordinating the effort.

She set down her tweezers. Some thoughts could not be finished.

The man in the doorway wore the grey-and-gold of the Royal Observatory service and held out an envelope as though it were hot. It was heavy paper, cream-coloured, sealed with the observatory's star-and-armillary stamp in red wax, and addressed in a secretary's careful copperplate:

*To the Master Clockmaker, Brightwater & Vale, Clockmakers, Fetter's Yard.*

Dominic's name wasn't on it. Neither was Wren's. Just *the Master Clockmaker*, as if the workshop were a person and persons were interchangeable parts.

"He can't sign for it," Marisol was saying as Wren came to the door, wiping her hands on her apron out of habit rather than need. "He's abed. He's been abed since Tuesday. I've told you he's abed."

"I only require a signature of receipt, madam."

"Then you'll have hers." Marisol jerked her chin at Wren, and something passed across her face — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. Not worry exactly. Worry wearing the costume of its own denial.

Wren signed. The courier tipped his hat, mounted his bicycle, and was gone into the fog with a squeak of tyres, leaving the envelope in her hands and the whole of the evening changed shape around it.

* * *

The Royal Observatory. Wren turned the envelope over three times before Tobias got home, and once more while he read it over her shoulder, smelling of ink and the damp walk from the solicitor's offices.

"*His Excellency's Astronomer requests the attendance of the Master Clockmaker*," Tobias read, in the voice he used for reading things aloud, which was halfway between a churchwarden and a music-hall comedian, "*to examine and repair the great regulator clock, whose failure threatens the accuracy of the standard time service. The matter is urgent. Expenses will be borne by the Observatory.*" He lowered the page. "Well. That's either the finest thing that's ever happened to this shop or a summons to the gallows."

"How do you mean?"

"Nobody writes *urgent* and *standard time service* in the same sentence unless something has gone badly wrong somewhere expensive." He handed it back. "Still. The Observatory. Finch's tower. They say the master clock there keeps better time than Greenwich itself, or used to, before—" He stopped, because Dominic had come down the stairs.

He came down slowly, one hand on the rail, which was in itself alarming. Dominic Vale did not use rails. He descended staircases the way he did everything else, briskly and with the air of a man who had somewhere better to be in eleven minutes. Tonight he looked like a coat hung on a frame, grey-faced above his dressing gown, and when he reached the bottom step he stood a moment as if the rest of the journey required negotiation.

"Let me see it," he said.

"Dominic—" Marisol began.

"Let me *see* it, Marisol. I'm ill, not blind." Though he took his spectacles from his pocket and put them on, which undermined the claim somewhat.

Wren put the letter in his hands. He read it once fast, then again slowly, and Wren watched his thumb move along the edge of the envelope — not reading, she realised later, but feeling, the way she herself felt the balance of a wheel between finger and thumb. Checking for something. She couldn't have said what made her notice it, except that she had spent nine years learning every motion of his hands, because they were the hands that taught her hers.

"Well," Dominic said at last. "The old regulator. I told Finch in 'ninety-one that if he didn't let me re-bush that third wheel it would fail him inside a decade." A ghost of his usual dryness surfaced and sank again. "Men never listen about clocks. They think because a clock tells them the time, it must also tell them the truth."

"Can you go?" Tobias asked. "Should I fetch the doctor first? Because I can fetch the doctor, I don't mind, it's only—"

"I cannot cross the room, Tobias, let alone the city and a hill." Dominic took off the spectacles and folded them with terrible care. "No. Someone will have to go in my place."

The kitchen went quiet. Forty clocks ticked into the silence, forty slightly different opinions about what time it was.

"Finch will expect—" Tobias started.

"Finch wrote to *the Master Clockmaker*," said Dominic, "and in this shop, that is what the letter says it is. Wren finishes her piece this week. Her work is sound. Her hands are steady." He looked at her then, straight on, and Wren felt something turn over in her chest — that old, familiar sensation from her first days in the house, aged ten, when he had lifted her half-finished practice gear out of her grubby fists and said *huh*, in the tone other men reserved for finding money in the street. Nine years, and he could still produce that sensation at will, like a conjurer with a coin.

"The Observatory," Marisol said. "Alone."

"She is twenty-two years old, Marisol, and a better mechanic than I am at thirty-five, which I say with full knowledge of what it costs me."

"That is not what I—"

"It is precisely what you meant, and it does her no kindness to pretend otherwise." But he said it gently, and put a hand briefly on Marisol's arm, and Wren saw the two of them conduct an entire argument in the space of a glance — an argument with years of practice behind it, worn smooth as an old coin. Marisol lost, as she usually did in matters concerning Wren's advancement, because losing those arguments was the price of having helped win them in the first place.

"When do I leave?" Wren asked.

"Tomorrow, on the early tram. Take the tools you trust and the ones you don't; you'll want both. And Wren—" He hesitated. Dominic Vale did not hesitate. In nine years she had seen him hesitate perhaps three times, and each instance had preceded an event she still thought about. "The regulator is a fine old machine but temperamental. Whatever you find wrong with it — whatever you *find* — send word before you touch anything beyond the fault."

"Whatever I find?"

"A figure of speech." He smiled, and it was his ordinary smile, warm and crooked, and it settled nothing at all. "Go and eat. Marisol has made enough lamb for six, and we are only four, and Ezra will manage five portions regardless of arithmetic."

* * *

Supper was loud and ordinary and did its work. Ezra told the story of the Greek steward who had tried to pay him in octopuses, which improved in the telling as always, until there were eleven octopuses and the steward had developed a lisp. Tobias recounted, with wounded dignity, the saga of the solicitor's office cat, which had sat upon his briefs and declined all appeals. Marisol refilled plates nobody had empty and pretended not to watch Wren eat, which was how she watched everyone eat, always.

Dominic picked at his food and laughed in the right places and twice asked Wren to repeat things she'd already said. At half past eight he pleaded fatigue and went up, leaning on the rail the whole way, and Marisol watched the ceiling after him as though her eyes could follow through the plaster.

It was near midnight when Wren saw the light.

She kept late hours by trade; a clockmaker's best thinking is done at night, when the shop's forty voices stop arguing and settle into consensus. She had come down for her notebook, meaning to sketch the regulator's escapement from memory so as not to walk in ignorant tomorrow, and she was halfway across the dark workshop when she noticed a thin line of orange under Dominic's door at the end of the passage.

Everyone else was abed. The fire in the kitchen grate was banked. There should have been no light up here but moonlight, and there was precious little of that.

Wren stood in the cold passage, in her nightdress and stocking feet, and had a debate with herself that lasted perhaps two seconds and lost comprehensively.

She did not mean to spy. She told herself this sincerely, and it was even mostly true. She meant only to knock — to check on him, to offer tea, to say something light about the tram timetable. Her hand was raised to do exactly that when the door swung inward on unlatched hinges, and there was Dominic at the hearth in his dressing gown, feeding papers to the dying fire, and the light lay across his face and made him a stranger.

Not in feature. In *manner*. His expression was the one he wore at the bench over a difficult repair — total concentration, a kind of grim tenderness — but bent on documents, and the documents were burning, and one of them—

One of them was sealed. Had been sealed. He broke it as she watched, cracked the wax with his thumbnail the way a man cracks a pistachio, and the wax was black. Not red, not the brown-black of cheap sealing sticks, but black — a dense, glossy black, and where the flame caught the broken halves a moment later, the seal gleamed like a beetle's back before it curled.

Dominic read whatever was inside quickly. Then he crumpled it, fed it to the fire, and stood looking at the flames with his hands pressed flat together behind his back, and Wren — who knew every posture of the man, who could have drawn his silhouette from memory in the dark — could not name what the posture meant. Grief, maybe. Or a man standing at attention before something.

The floorboard betrayed her.

It was a small sound, one board among hundreds, but Dominic turned his head like a wren herself, quick and exact, and found her in the doorway. For a heartbeat neither of them moved. The fire popped. Somewhere below, the shop's regulators ticked on, indifferent.

"Wren." His voice was perfectly level. "Can't you sleep?"

"You left your door open," she said, which was true and explained nothing. "I came for my notebook. I wanted to sketch the escapement before morning."

"Thorough," he said. "Good. Thoroughness will serve you tomorrow." And he crossed to the hearth and set the poker against the last of the papers, stirring them down to ash with the unhurried competence of a banked fire, and the gesture was so ordinary, so entirely the gesture of a man disposing of nothing more than correspondence and receipts, that Wren almost believed it.

Almost. But the seal had been black, and black wax was not used by astronomers' clerks, and she had watched him break it like a man breaking bread rather than a man opening mail.

"What was that?" she asked. "The letters?"

"Old correspondence. Nothing that concerns the Observatory." He smiled — the crooked, warm smile — and it occurred to Wren, standing cold-footed in the doorway with her notebook forgotten under her arm, that she had never before noticed how much a smile could conceal while showing so much. "You'll take the seven o'clock tram. Marisol will pack you a parcel that could feed the tram's entire complement. Eat some of it. Wear your good coat — Finch is particular. And Wren—"

"Yes?"

"If anything at the tower seems strange to you — anything at all — you will send me word before you act. Promise me."

There it was again. *Before you touch anything.* *Before you act.* Twice in one day, from a man who had spent nine years teaching her the opposite lesson — that a mechanic's judgment was her own, that hesitation past the point of evidence was cowardice, that she was to trust her hands because he trusted them.

"I promise," she said, because he was ill, and old, and asking, and because she loved him, and love in that house had always outranked curiosity.

Though not by much.

"Off to bed, then," said Dominic, kindly, and turned back to the fire, and Wren went — back down the stairs, through the sleeping shop with its forty ticking witnesses, to her narrow room under the eaves where her apprenticeship piece waited on the bench, one wheel short of done.

She lay in the dark and listened to the clocks and turned the day over like a movement in the hand. Pride first, and it was real: *the Observatory*, the master regulator itself, and her name signed for it, her hands chosen. Nine years of small gears and borrowed belonging, and tomorrow the city's best clock would know her name.

But under the pride, thin and cold as a hairline crack across a crystal, ran another thought entirely, and she could not file it smooth: that Dominic Vale burned letters at midnight; that one had borne a black seal; and that he had sent her, alone, to the top of a hill full of clocks —

and asked her to promise not to look too closely at what she found there.
