## 3. The Observatory at the Edge of the City

The tram let Wren off at the bottom of Observatory Hill at half past seven in the morning, and by half past eight she had concluded that whoever designed the road up had done so with a personal grudge against clockmakers' apprentices.

It wasn't the cold, though the December wind came off the estuary sharp enough to shave with. It wasn't even the ice, which lay across the cobbles in long silver panes that her boots found with unerring accuracy. It was the case — Dominic's old leather tool case, heavier than sin and twice as disapproving, banging against her hip with every step as though it too objected to the climb.

"You could have said no," she told it, breath fogging. "You could have said, *Wren, you are nineteen years old and you have never once been anywhere alone except the fishmonger's.*"

The case said nothing, being a case. But it had belonged to Dominic for forty years before it belonged to her, and it had a way of making silence feel like commentary.

She stopped halfway up to look back at the city, because Marisol would have wanted her to — *you always regret not looking, niña* — and there it was, spread out below like something spilled from a very grand pocket: slate roofs smoking with morning fires, the river stitched through it all in grey silk, masts along the quay like needles waiting for thread. Somewhere down there was the workshop, where Tobias was presumably already at his desk pretending he'd slept, where Ezra was presumably still asleep in fact, and where Marisol was almost certainly wrapping bread and cheese into a parcel Wren had sworn she didn't need and had accepted anyway.

And somewhere down there, in bed under three quilts and a hot brick, was Dominic Vale, coughing in that new wet way he tried to hide whenever she came into the room.

"He sent me because I'm good," she reminded herself, "not because he's dying."

The two possibilities were not, she knew, mutually exclusive. She put her shoulder to the case and climbed.

The observatory, when she finally crested the hill, looked less like a building than like an argument between an onion and a cathedral. A great stone drum sat squat in the center, crowned with its copper dome gone green with weather, and around its ankles clustered lower wings of offices and lodgings, all of it ringed by a black iron fence with spear-tips that caught the light like punctuation. The whole thing stood on the highest ground for miles, which was, Wren supposed, rather the point. Astronomers and clockmakers shared a vice: both were forever craning their necks toward something just out of reach.

The gate was open. That should have been her first unease. Institutions like this did not leave gates open; institutions like this believed, deep in their institutional bones, that open gates led directly to scandal, ruin, and letters to the newspapers.

She let herself through anyway, crossed a courtyard scoured bare by wind, and pulled the bell rope at the main door. Somewhere inside, a bell rang — and rang, and kept on ringing far longer than any sensible bell, until Wren winced and the door finally opened.

The man in the doorway was sixty-odd, tall in the way of things built to be leaned on, with white hair cropped close and a cardigan worn over what had once been a uniform. His eyes, when they found her face, did something peculiar. They widened, then narrowed, then went carefully, deliberately blank, like a ledger being closed before an auditor walked in.

"Miss Brightwater," he said.

Not *good morning*. Not *may I help you*. He knew her name, or thought he did.

"From Vale's workshop," she said. "Dominic Vale couldn't come himself. He's unwell. I'm his apprentice — nearly finished." She heard herself add the last part and wished she hadn't. Nineteen years of being the smallest person in every room had taught her to say *nearly finished* the way other people said *I have references*.

"Yes," the man said slowly. "Yes, I imagine you are." And then he stepped back from the doorway and inclined his head — actually inclined it, a small formal bow, the kind given to bishops and creditors — and said, "Aldous Finch. Keeper of instruments. Come in out of the wind, Miss Brightwater. We've been expecting you."

"We?"

"The clock," Finch said, which was not an answer, and led her inside.

* * *

The interior smelled of lamp oil, cold stone, and old paper — the smell of every serious institution Wren had ever imagined, mixed with one she hadn't: tea. Strong tea, brewed too long, left standing. There was a pot on a tray in the corridor outside what Finch called the transit room, with two cups beside it.

Two cups.

"Are you expecting someone else today?" she asked.

Finch's stride hitched — barely, but Wren spent her life watching mechanisms, and men were mechanisms of a sort. "No," he said. "Habit."

But the second cup had no dust on it, and the tea in the pot was fresh, and when they passed the tray again twenty minutes later the second cup was gone.

The transit room itself she forgot about entirely the moment she entered it, because the master clock was there, and the master clock was magnificent.

It stood floor to ceiling against the north wall, a walnut case taller than Ezra (which was saying something, as Ezra measured six feet before his boots) and wider than Dominic's workbench, with glass panels let into its doors so that the whole beating heart of it was visible: brass wheels stacked in columns, escapement gleaming, pendulum swinging below with the slow patience of something that had never once hurried and never once been late. Dials of painted enamel faced the room — hours, minutes, seconds, each hand moving with that particular quality Wren loved best in fine clocks, the quality of inevitability. This was not a clock that suggested the time. This was a clock that *ruled* it.

Every telescope in the building, Finch explained, took its marching orders from this machine. Every star observed, every position logged, every almanac printed under the observatory's name traced back to these hands. Ships navigated by what this clock decided. Insurance rates were calculated by it. If it lost two seconds, somewhere across the world a captain would turn his wheel two seconds' worth of degrees wrong.

"And it has begun losing more than that," Finch said. "Eleven seconds in four days. Then yesterday—" he pressed his lips together "—yesterday it stopped. At 3:17 in the afternoon. In front of the Astronomer Royal."

"Stopped dead? Or slowed?"

"Stopped. Mid-beat. Like a man struck by lightning." Finch's jaw worked. "We got it running again within the hour, but it hasn't held true since. Our own people have been through it twice. They found nothing. So I sent for Vale."

Wren set her case down on the cloth-covered table Finch indicated and opened it, taking comfort in the ritual of it: loupe out, screwdrivers laid in order of size, the roll of soft brass shims, the little jar of oil Dominic mixed himself and guarded like a relic. Her hands steadied as they worked. They usually did.

"May I?" she asked, nodding at the case door.

Finch hesitated for exactly one heartbeat too long. "Of course."

The lock was simple — a cabinet lock, chosen for dignity rather than security, and Wren was through it in ninety seconds while Finch watched her do it with an expression she couldn't read at all. Relief? She'd have guessed relief, except that underneath the relief there was something tighter, something that looked like a man watching a fuse burn toward a charge he'd forgotten he'd planted.

Then the movement swung out toward her on its hinges, and she stopped thinking about Finch altogether.

For ten full minutes she didn't touch anything. She just looked. This is what Dominic had drilled into her from the age of nine, before he'd let her hold so much as a file: *Diagnosis before treatment, Wren. Any fool can take a clock apart. The repair is in the looking.*

She looked.

The movement was a beauty — a Graham deadbeat escapement, six-foot mercury-compensated pendulum, train wheels cut and crossed with a delicacy that made her fingers itch with envy. Whoever had built it had loved it, and whoever had maintained it had loved it too; the oiling was correct, the wear patterns honest and even, everything tended the way you tend a garden or a child.

Everything except one thing.

"There," she said quietly.

"Where?" Finch was at her shoulder.

"Third wheel of the going train. Counting from the escapement." She pointed with a screwdriver, not touching. "That wheel's been replaced. Recently. See how the crossings are filed — the spokes, I mean? Slightly different profile. And the teeth." She leaned closer, loupe screwed into her eye socket. "The teeth are wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"They're cut half a degree off profile. Maybe less. You'd never see it with your eye. You'd barely measure it with a good caliper." Wren straightened up and looked at the wheel the way one looks at a snake discovered in a laundry basket. "But over a few thousand beats, those teeth fight the pinion ever so slightly. Not enough to stop it. Enough to slow it. And enough to make the pallets land badly, and the impulse get ragged, and eventually — after days of the mechanism tiring itself out like a horse driven uphill — it stumbles."

Finch had gone very pale. "An error in manufacture, surely. A bad wheel from the supplier—"

"No." She said it flatly, because there was no kind way to say it. "Look at the pivot holes. Someone re-bushed them to fit this wheel. That's deliberate — that's skilled work, done with a graver, by hand, from inside the case. And see here—" she touched the edge of the wheel plate with a fingertip "—the screw heads have been burnished afterward. Someone polished them to hide the tool marks. This wasn't a mistake that got installed. This was surgery."

The room was very quiet. Out in the dome overhead, something creaked as the sun warmed the copper.

"You're certain," Finch said. It was not really a question.

"I'm certain someone did this on purpose, with skill, with access, and with patience." Wren wiped her hands on her apron and found they were not entirely steady anymore. "Whoever it was wanted the clock sick, not dead. Dead gets you a new clock, and a new clock keeps perfect time. Sick gets you months of doubt. Months of people asking whether the observatory can be trusted. Months of—" she paused "—of sending for outside help."

She looked up at Finch and found him looking at her with that strange careful blankness again, and the thought arrived fully formed, cold as the courtyard flagstones:

*He expected someone else. He expected Dominic. And whoever did this knew Dominic would come.*

"Keeper Finch," she said, "who else knew the clock was failing?"

His mouth opened. Closed. He turned away and busied himself straightening a tray of tools that needed no straightening. "The staff, naturally. The Astronomer Royal. Myself." A pause. "One or two others."

"One or two others" being the universal human phrase for *names I am not going to give you*, Wren folded her arms and waited. She had learned waiting from Dominic too, who could out-wait a rusted screw.

Finch broke first, as most mechanisms do under steady load.

"Miss Brightwater." He turned back, and what was in his face now was neither blankness nor relief but something rawer — fear, maybe, or guilt wearing fear's coat. "Before I tell you anything else, I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer plainly. Has Dominic Vale ever spoken to you about the year 1869?"

The question landed like a dropped weight on a workbench. "1869? No. Why would he—"

"And nothing unusual has happened at the workshop lately? No strangers asking questions, no letters out of the ordinary, nothing missing?"

Marisol's voice floated up from somewhere in her memory — *a man came round Tuesday asking about our Dominic, said he owed him money, I sent him off with a rolling pin* — and Ezra's, quieter — *seen a ship's boat loitering by the coal wharf, girl. Twice now.* — and her own certainty wobbled like a plumb line in a draft.

"No," she lied, and was surprised at how easily it came. "Nothing unusual."

Finch studied her for a long moment, and she had the uncomfortable sense that he weighed her lie and found it wanting, and decided to pretend otherwise. Then he nodded, once, briskly, as if crossing some internal threshold.

"Very well," he said. "Repair the clock, Miss Brightwater. Repair it properly — better than properly. And while you're in that case..." He stopped. Started again. "Take your time. Look closely. Look at everything."

"At everything," Wren repeated. "Why?"

But Finch was already walking to the door, and the light behind him, and the set of his shoulders, told her he would say no more just yet. At the threshold he paused, one hand on the frame.

"When you write to Mr. Vale tonight," he said, without turning around, "tell him Aldous remembers. He'll know what it means."

Then he was gone, leaving Wren alone in the transit room with the greatest clock she had ever seen, a tray of abandoned tea, and eleven seconds of stolen time ticking somewhere in her chest.

* * *

She worked until the light failed.

It was easier than thinking, which was exactly why she did it — Dominic called it *hiding inside the mechanism*, and accused her of doing it whenever a problem was too big to face with her hands empty. She stripped the third wheel, re-cut the tooth profiles on Finch's own lathe with cuts so fine she held her breath for the length of each one, re-bushed the plate true, adjusted the depthings, oiled sparingly and perfectly. By four o'clock the clock's beat had gone from a limping stumble to the long level tick of a healthy heart. By five she had it holding time to a quarter-second over the hour, and by six she was simply listening to it, the way you listen to someone breathing easily again after a fever breaks.

And then, because there was nothing left to adjust and she could not bear to leave, and because Finch had said *look at everything* in a voice that had not been idle, she took up her lamp and began examining the inside of the case itself.

Walnut, panel by panel. Brass hinges, screw by screw. The pendulum crutch, the suspension spring, the counterweights. She worked methodically, bottom to top, running her fingertips along joints, tapping panels and listening to the sound they returned — solid, solid, solid—

*Hollow.*

High up in the case, behind the trunk of the movement where no maintenance would ever require access, one panel gave back a note slightly different from its brothers. Wren stood on a stool, heart thudding in her ears louder than the escapement, and examined it by lamplight. No hinges. No handle. Only, along the bottom edge, a seam so fine she'd have missed it entirely if she hadn't been looking for seams — and at one corner, almost invisible, a small brass boss the size of a shirt button.

She pressed it. Nothing. She pressed and turned. Something clicked, deep in the wood, with the sweet exact voice of well-made detent — and the panel shifted a quarter inch outward.

Behind it, in a cavity lined with oiled silk, lay papers.

Wren did not take them out. Some instinct older than her training stopped her hand — the same instinct that says *do not read the letter addressed to someone else*, that says *this is bigger than you and you are standing in the middle of it*. But through the gap she could see the topmost document: heavy paper, folded in thirds, and upon it, in ink gone brown with age, handwriting she knew better than her own.

She would have known it anywhere. She had watched it write her first wage book, her birthday cards, the labels on Marisol's preserve jars.

Dominic's hand. On a letter hidden in the observatory's master clock, sealed beneath a panel with a secret catch, dated — she could just make it out, tilted in the lamplight — the twelfth of March, 1869.

Thirty years ago.

The clock ticked on above her, steady now, healed, keeping perfect time for an empire that navigated by it. And Wren Brightwater, apprentice clockmaker, nineteen years old, three days from her graduation piece, stood on a stool in a freezing tower with her hand pressed flat against the case and understood, with absolute clarity, two things.

First: that Finch had known. That *Aldous remembers* meant remember this, remember where, remember why — and that the two teacups had been set out for a man who never came.

Second: that whatever Dominic Vale had hidden here thirty years ago, he had burned something in the grate last night with a look on his face she had never seen before, and had then sent *her* — small, borrowed, nearly-finished Wren — to fetch it home.

The lamp guttered. Far off across the dark city, the workshops and warehouses of the Harrow Shipping Company glowed with their own lamps, and Wren thought, quite suddenly, of Ezra's ship's boat loitering by the coal wharf.

She took the letter out after all.

Some things, once started, cannot be stopped mid-beat.
