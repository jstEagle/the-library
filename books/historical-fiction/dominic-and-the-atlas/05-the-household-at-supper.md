## 5. The Household at Supper

Marisol Ferro had learned to read a household the way other women read letters — by the weight of the paper, the slant of the hand, what was pressed between the lines and what was blotted out entirely.

She had kept Dominic Vale's household for nineteen years, and in that time she had developed opinions about supper the way generals develop opinions about terrain. Tobias could not abide onions before a day of ledgers; Ezra ate anything set before him but grew suspicious of anything too fine, as though luxury were a trick played by the sea; Wren forgot meals entirely when she had a movement to finish, and had to be fed like a watch that needed winding at regular intervals — soup at seven, bread at nine, whether or not the girl remembered to be hungry.

And Dominic — Dominic ate whatever was put in front of him, and always, always looked at his plate first, as if checking it for something.

Tonight the table was laid for five and set for four.

"Wren will be back by nine," Tobias said, not looking up from the napkin he was folding into increasingly improbable shapes. He folded things when he was nervous. Marisol had watched him turn an entire dinner service into a flock of paper swans the winter the bailiffs came sniffing about the debt he'd fled from before any of them knew him. "The observatory's only over the hill. She said nine."

"She said nine three hours ago," Ezra said, sawing at his bread with a knife meant for beef. He was a large man gone soft at the edges, like a hull that had taken on just enough water to sit lower in the water than it used to, but there was still iron under the softness. "Hills don't eat people."

"Clocks do," said Tobias. "Clockmakers get eaten by clocks all the time. It's practically the trade."

Marisol put the tureen down harder than strictly necessary, and both men straightened like schoolboys. Nineteen years of ladling had given her the authority of a ship's captain and the aim of a ship's gunner, and they both knew it.

"Ezra, fetch her plate down warm. Tobias, if you fold that napkin into another bird I'll make you eat standing." She sat, untied her apron, and let herself have two full breaths before she touched her spoon. That was the ritual. Whatever the day had done to them, they sat, they breathed, they began with soup.

It was a fragile thing, this ritual. She had spent nineteen years learning exactly how fragile.

* * *

The house had been loud once, in the early years, when Dominic collected strays faster than Marisol could feed them. There had been others — a girl who ran off to the theatre, a boy apprenticed out to Chandlers' Row, a pair of twins who stayed one winter and vanished like weather. But the four of them had stuck, the way burrs stick, and somewhere along the way the burrs became a family, though nobody had ever said so aloud, because saying so aloud felt like inviting someone to take it away.

Dominic had come down for supper tonight. That itself was notable. For three weeks he had taken his meals on a tray in the workshop, claiming work, and Marisol had carried the trays up and carried most of them down again untouched, and had begun composing in her head the speech she would deliver when he finally admitted how bad the cough was. She had been drafting versions of that speech since October. It had grown footnotes.

But tonight he came down, grey as February and twice as cold to the touch, and took his seat at the head of the table, and thanked her for the soup in that low courteous voice of his that made even gratitude sound like a secret being kept.

"You've colour," she told him, which was a lie, and he thanked her for that too, which proved he knew it.

They were halfway through the fish course when the knock came.

Three raps, evenly spaced, deliberate. Not the fist of a neighbour, not the patter of the boy who delivered ice. Marisol's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth, and she watched three other spoons stop at the same moment, four people moving as one body without meaning to, and thought: *there it is.* That was the thing about a family built like theirs. Everyone at this table had heard a knock mean trouble before. They'd simply never heard it mean trouble to all of them at once.

"I'll go," said Dominic, beginning to rise.

"Sit," said Marisol, and she was already up. "You'll strain something. I'll see who it is. If it's the vicar wanting money for the roof, he can want it out here in the dark where we all want things."

It was not the vicar.

* * *

The man on the step was everything the knock had promised: young, but dressed old, in a coat cut for a much larger inheritance, gloved, hat held correctly. He had the smile of a man who has practised smiling in a mirror and been told it works.

"Good evening. Mr. Vale's residence?" He pronounced *residence* the way other men pronounced a sum of money.

"It's a house," Marisol said. "People live in it."

"Quite." The smile recalibrated. "My name is Pell. Pell & Sons, solicitors, acting for Harrow Shipping. We're conducting an inquiry — a formality, nothing more — regarding certain assets connected to a Mr. Dominic Vale. Perhaps you're the housekeeper?"

"Cook," said Marisol, and let the word stand there doing all the work a lesser word couldn't.

"Ah. Well. Cook." He produced a card from an inner pocket with the flourish of a man producing a warrant. She did not take it. After a moment he set it on the ledge beside the door, where it lay looking foolish. "We understand Mr. Vale has been unwell. A tragedy. In cases of prolonged illness, questions of *administration* arise, you understand. Records. Correspondence. We'd hoped to speak with him briefly."

"He's eating his supper."

"At this hour?"

"That's generally when supper happens, sir, yes."

Behind her, inside the house, there came the distinct sound of a chair scraping — Ezra, she guessed, rising without meaning to, the way big men do when their blood decides something before their manners do. Pell's eyes flicked past her shoulder, quick as a rat's, and came back.

"Perhaps another time. Might I ask—" and here the smile returned, wider, warmer, worse, "—how long you've been in Mr. Vale's employ? Only, we're compiling particulars of the household. Names, dates of arrival. For our records."

And there it was. Marisol looked at this young man with his mirror-practised smile and his firm's name like a knife laid flat on the table, and understood precisely what he had come to do: count them. Put names and dates against each of them, the way you inventory a hold before deciding what to jettison.

Nineteen years ago, Marisol Ferro had arrived at this door with a suitcase, a widow's black going grey at the seams, and a talent for feeding people that was the only thing anyone had ever wanted from her. Dominic had asked her one question — *"Can you keep a kitchen, Mrs. Ferro? I find I'm collecting people, and people insist on eating"* — and she had said yes, and that had been the whole of the contract, unwritten, unsigned, worth everything.

"I've been here since before you had a firm, likely," she said. "And my name's on no record you'll be compiling, because I'm a cook, and cooks are recorded in pies."

Pell blinked. "I... beg your pardon?"

"Good night, sir. Mind the step, it's older than me and meaner."

She closed the door on his second smile, which had been starting, and leaned against it, and listened to his boots descend the steps and fade toward the lane. Then she stood there a moment longer, hand flat on the wood, breathing.

When she turned, all four of them were in the hall.

Ezra filled the stairway like furniture that had always been there. Tobias stood with his hands hidden in his sleeves, which was what he did with his hands whenever they might betray him. Dominic sat wrapped in a blanket Marisol did not remember fetching for him, looking small and amused and terribly awake. And Wren's place at the table stood empty and candlelit, and somehow that emptiness was the loudest person in the room.

"Well," said Marisol. "He knows we're here. Now we decide what he knows about us."

* * *

Supper resumed, because supper always resumed; that was its entire theology. But it went on differently now, and Marisol watched it happen the way she watched a sauce she suspected was about to break.

Tobias spoke first, and spoke too much, which was his tell. "Perfectly routine," he said, addressing the fish. "Firms like that send inquiries out wholesale. Probate gossip, asset mapping. I've seen it done to half the businesses on Fetter Lane. It doesn't signify." He refolded a napkin. It became, despite all threats, a bird. "They compile, they cross-reference, and ninety times in a hundred the file closes and nobody hears of it again."

"And the other ten?" Ezra asked.

"The other ten times somebody's already decided what they want and is using the paperwork to walk up the garden path." Tobias set the bird down. His fingers had gone white at the tips. "I'd know. I used to file the walking papers myself."

That was more than Tobias usually said about Before, and Marisol caught Dominic's eye flick toward him — quick, cataloguing, concerned — and filed that away.

Ezra, for his part, said nothing at all about Pell, which was unlike him; Ezra commented on everything, weather, ships, the moral character of mackerel. Instead he ate in silence, and kept glancing at the window that faced the lane, and when he rose to fetch the bread he contrived to pass that window slowly, twice, looking out. A man watching the street. A man who had spent thirty years watching horizons for sails and knew exactly what a stranger's silhouette looked like lingering where it shouldn't.

And Dominic — Dominic watched all of them.

Marisol had seen him do it for years and told herself it was affection, and mostly it was. But tonight she saw it clearly for the first time as something else as well: a man taking attendance. Counting his strays while the door was still shut. And beneath the blanket, his hand rested against his chest, over the pocket of his dressing gown, where for three days running there had been a small rectangular weight she'd noticed when she laundered it. Something he carried now that he hadn't carried before. Something that had not left his person even to be washed.

He caught her looking. He didn't look away, and neither did she, and between them passed the entire conversation they would have later, condensed:

*You know something.*

*I know several somethings.*

*You'll tell me when it endangers them.*

*It already endangers them, Marisol.*

*Then sooner than later.*

He inclined his head a fraction, which from Dominic was a signature on a document, and turned to Tobias and asked after some harmless matter at Fetter Lane, and the table eased by degrees, the way a sea eases, without ever quite becoming safe.

* * *

Later, with the kitchen banked and the good dishes drying, Marisol found Ezra sitting on the back step in the cold, smoking his pipe, which he was supposed to do nowhere near her flour and never did anywhere else. She sat down beside him because it was cold and because she wanted to, and handed him half a cold dumpling without comment, because that was their particular friendship, conducted largely in food.

"Pell," she said.

"Aye."

"You've seen his kind before."

"On ships, you mean." Ezra tamped the pipe, unhurried. Firelight from the kitchen window caught the white in his beard. "Aye. Every crew's got one. Friendly cove, comes aboard at port, asks about cargo, asks about crew, wants to know who signed articles and who was shanghaied. Never does anything himself." He struck a match. "He's the fellow who comes *before* the fellows."

"How many fellows come after?"

"Enough." He drew on the pipe, and the ember lit his eyes. "Mari. You know I don't pry at Dominic. Thirty years I've sailed under quiet captains, and the quiet ones have their reasons. But there's a smell off this week I know from the forecastle. He's burning papers. Seen the ash in the grate, Tuesday. And now Harrow's men counting heads at our own door." He paused. "Whatever's coming, it's coming on a schedule. And we're not on it."

Marisol pulled her shawl tighter and looked out at the dark garden, at the shed where Wren had her first bench, at the crooked line of laundry wire, at every small domestic thing she had spent two decades nailing down against the wind.

"They're not here yet," she said.

"No."

"Then there's supper tomorrow to think of, and Wren coming home, and Dominic eating an actual meal for the first time in a month. One catastrophe per evening. That's the rule."

Ezra laughed, low, the rumble she liked. "One per evening. I can sail under that."

They sat until the pipe went out. Inside, faintly, they could hear Tobias moving about the parlour, putting the room to rights with that terrible careful tidiness of his, and above it, from the workshop, the uneven tread of a man crossing a floor toward sleep — and then stopping, and not sleeping.

Marisol rose, brushed off her skirt, and made herself the same promise she made every night: that whatever this family was built on — and she had known since the first year it was built on something, because no man collects orphans and sailors and ruined clerks out of simple kindness, kindness doesn't keep accounts that carefully — whatever it was built on, the walls would hold, because she would stand in the doorway holding them.

She was halfway through washing the tureen when Dominic came down.

He stood in the kitchen doorway in his dressing gown and blanket, grey, upright, formal as a summons, and in his hand was the letter — the small rectangular weight from his pocket — sealed in black wax, held out toward her like something that had gotten too heavy to carry alone.

"Marisol," he said quietly. "There's something you should read before that firm sends anyone else to this door. And there's something I must ask you to do, tomorrow, that I should have asked years ago."

She dried her hands.
