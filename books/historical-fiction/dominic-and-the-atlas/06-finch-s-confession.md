## 6. Finch's Confession

The master clock ticked on through the afternoon like a patient who has been told the worst news and decided to be cheerful about it anyway. Wren sat on the floor of its case with her legs folded beneath her and her sleeve rolled past her elbow, and she listened to it think.

She had spent three hours undoing what someone else had done. That was the part that still sat wrong in her chest, like a gear tooth cut a half-degree off true — not broken, merely *wrong*, and all the more maddening for how nearly right it looked. The misfiled wheel had been beautiful work. Anyone less fussy than an apprentice raised by Dominic Vale would have oiled it, adjusted it, and gone home believing the clock had simply grown old.

But she had been raised by Dominic Vale, and fussiness was the family religion.

"You've been in there a long while," said Keeper Finch from behind her.

Wren emerged backwards from the case, blinking into the amber light of the clock room. The observatory's great telescope rose through the dome above them like a church spire in reverse — pointing not up at heaven but out at everything else. Dust hung in the low sun. Finch stood by the door with two cups of tea balanced on a small tray, and he was watching her the way people watch a kettle they are not entirely sure about.

"I found your saboteur's handiwork," she said. "Third wheel train. Filed the arbor down by half a hair and swapped in a wheel from a different movement. It would have run for weeks before anyone noticed."

"Would it?" Finch set the tray on a stool. "Then he was better than the last one."

Wren took the cup he offered because refusing tea seemed rude even in a house full of mysteries. "The last one?"

Finch did not answer immediately. He crossed to the clock instead, laid his palm flat against the case — walnut, dark with age, carved along its crown with stars and a fat crescent moon that some Victorian carpenter had clearly enjoyed himself making — and stood there listening as though the mechanism were speaking to him in a language he had once known and half forgotten.

"Thirty years ago," he said, "I was not a keeper of telescopes. I was a clerk. A junior under-clerk in the offices of Vale & Marchand, shipping brokers, down by the pool."

Wren's cup paused halfway to her mouth. "Dominic owned ships?"

"Dominic owned *everything*." Finch smiled, but it was the kind of smile that has to be carried up a long staircase. "Or he was going to. Patents for a new hull design. Shares in six companies. Deeds, charters, letters of credit. Enough paper to buy a street. And then in the spring of 1869, he walked into my office, put his hat on my desk, and asked me if I trusted banks."

"Did you say yes?"

"I was twenty-two and wanted to keep my position, so yes." Finch shook his head slowly. "He laughed. He had a laugh like a door swinging open — you never knew whether you were being invited or thrown out. Then he said: *good, because I don't, and I need someone who can keep a secret inside something that never stops moving.*"

Wren set her tea down on the floor beside her knee. Her hands had gone cold, which was absurd, because the clock room was warm and the tea was hot and none of this was her business. Except that it was, apparently, and had been all along.

"He trusted no vault," Finch went on. "Vaults have ledgers. Ledgers have clerks. Clerks have debts, and debts have owners. No — Dominic wanted his inheritance somewhere no lawyer would ever think to look, and no thief would ever trouble to steal, because no thief alive can carry off a building." He patted the clock case the way one pats an old horse. "So we hid it here. Inside the thing that keeps the time."

"The documents are in the clock." Wren heard her own voice come out very flat and very steady, which was how her voice always went when her heart was doing something unseemly. "In this clock. Now."

"Have been since before you were born, Miss Brightwater."

*Miss Brightwater.* She turned the name over and found it suddenly strange — a coat that fit perfectly and yet might, she now suspected, have been tailored by someone else's tailor.

"How?" she managed. "Where? The case is solid—"

"Nothing is solid. That is the first lesson of clocks and the last lesson of families." Finch knelt with a grunt — his knees announced themselves like ship's horns — and pressed a carved star near the base of the case. It gave with a click that Wren felt in her own teeth, and a shallow drawer slid out, no thicker than a hymnal, invisible against the shadow line of the moulding.

Inside lay a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord gone brown with age. And on top of the packet, a letter sealed in black wax.

Black wax. The same black wax she had seen glowing in Dominic's grate three nights ago, curling and blistering as it burned.

"Is that—" she began.

"There were copies," Finch said quietly. "One for the trust itself. One letter of instruction. And one letter to whoever came in Dominic's place when the clock needed mending. He always knew it would need mending eventually. Clocks do. It's rather their whole argument." He held the letter out to her. "He said if it was ever you who climbed this hill, I should know you'd been told nothing, and that I was to tell you everything."

"Everything," Wren repeated. It did not feel like a word. It felt like a cliff edge.

"Everything," said Finch, and sat down heavily on the stool, and drank his tea, and waited for her to read.

* * *

She read it twice. The first time her eyes skated over the words without gripping them, the way ice skates grip nothing; the second time the words went in deep enough to leave marks.

*To the keeper of this house of time: the Brightwater household holds in trust what is written below. Not by blood. By choice, which is the stronger claim, though the law does not know it.*

There followed an inventory that made her dizzy to look at: patents registered in three countries; shares; a deed for a vessel called the *Atlas*, laid up in ordinary; accounts at banks whose names she only knew from the labels on other people's luggage. And beneath it, in Dominic's unmistakable hand — the hand she had watched every evening of her life, writing orders for brass stock and recipes for clock oil and once, memorably, a birthday poem for Marisol that scanned terribly and meant everything —

*Beware the Harrows. They will come for this before the century turns, because after the century turns, it is theirs to take from whoever holds it. The trust expires at midnight on the thirty-first of December, 1899. At that hour, the estate passes entire to the bearer of this letter who signs alone. One signature. One heir. This is the law's price for trusting love with money, and I could not pay it and protect them all. Forgive me. Choose well. — D.V.*

Wren read the last paragraph four times. Somewhere outside, gulls were arguing about something. The clock ticked. The tea went cold in her cup.

"One signature," she said aloud.

"That is the shape of it," said Finch. "A century trust — a device for keeping fortunes safe across a hundred years. When it expires, the estate must pass to a single named hand. If the household presents the letter together and cannot agree on whose name goes on the transfer..." He spread his fingers apologetically. "...the estate falls to the Crown, or to any claimant with a better case. And the Harrows have been building their case for thirty years. They have lawyers older than I am."

"But there are five of us." Her voice cracked on the number. "Six, counting you. There are five of us and one berth. That's not an inheritance, that's a—" She stopped, because she could not think of a word terrible enough, and because the word *lifeboat* arrived unbidden and stayed.

"Yes," said Finch simply. "It's a lifeboat. And I am sorry for it. I have been sorry for thirty years, which is why I became a keeper of telescopes instead. Stars don't ask you to choose between people."

Wren stood up. Her knees were steadier than she deserved. She walked to the clock and looked at it properly — really looked, the way she had been taught to look at mechanisms, seeing not the case but the intention underneath. All those years. All those suppers. Marisol's kitchen, Tobias's ledgers, Ezra's stories, Dominic at the head of the table like a lighthouse that had decided to raise children instead of warn ships away. And threaded through all of it, hidden in a hilltop clock like a seed in winter soil, this.

"He built us around a secret," she said. "All of us. None of us knew."

"He built you around a *treasure*, and let you be a family while he waited to see what would grow." Finch rinsed his cup in the small sink with the fussiness of a man who had lived alone too long. "I have often thought those may be the same act. Though I admit the distinction escapes me at present."

"Why tell me?" The question came out harder than she intended. "Why not Tobias — he keeps accounts, he'd understand the legal side. Why not Ezra, he's eldest. Why me? I'm an apprentice. I'm the youngest person in that house including the cat."

Finch looked at her for a long moment. In the low sun his face was all lines and kindness, a map of a journey she knew nothing about.

"Because when you opened the movement this morning," he said, "you didn't start by fixing what was broken. You started by asking what had been *done*, and by whom, and why. Because you measured the misfiled wheel before you touched it, so you'd know what truth looked like when you found it." He smiled his carried-up-the-staircase smile. "And because Dominic said, if it's the girl, tell her everything. He said the others would each want the inheritance for reasons, and that was right and proper — reasons are human. But you, he said, would want to know what it *cost*. And that is the only question worth asking before you sign anything."

Wren looked down at the letter in her hands. Black wax, unbroken. Thirty years old and heavier than any gear she had ever lifted.

"When does it expire?" she asked, though she already knew.

"Midnight, December the thirty-first." Finch glanced at the clock's dial, where the hands moved with their old serene indifference. "Nineteen days."

*Nineteen days.* Nineteen days to learn what a life was worth at auction. Nineteen days to go home and sit at supper with Marisol's bread and Ezra's salt-stained jokes and Tobias's careful arithmetic and Dominic's quiet at the head of the table, and know that every one of them had a berth and there was only one boat.

"Does anyone else know?" she asked. "About the expiry. About any of it."

"Only me. And now you." Finch hesitated, and Wren saw — with the particular clarity that dread lends the young — that the hesitation was the true answer trying to get out.

"And the Harrows know it exists," she said.

"The Harrows know it exists," Finch agreed. "They have known for years. That is why the clock was sabotaged, Miss Brightwater. Not to stop time. To summon a clockmaker. They have been waiting for this letter to come out of hiding, because a secret in a vault is untouchable, but a secret in a young woman's pocket on a dark road down a hill..."

He did not finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Wren folded the letter closed, felt the black wax press its cold round seal against her palm, and understood with perfect certainty that the walk back down the hill was going to be the longest of her life.

"Keeper Finch," she said. "How many roads lead down from this hill?"

"Just the one, I'm afraid." He was already fetching his coat and a lantern, moving with more urgency than he had shown all day. "But it's a good long one, and dark early this time of year, and I don't care for your chances on it alone." He held the door open for her, and the wind came in smelling of salt and coming trouble. "Come along, then. You'll stay for supper, and at first light I'll walk you down myself. I've thirty years of sins to answer for, and I'd rather like to start repaying them by delivering the post."

* * *

Supper at the observatory was fish pie, eaten mostly in silence while the wind tested the dome above them like a burglar working a lock. Finch talked a little — about Dominic as a young man, impossibly dashing, impossibly certain; about the night in 1869 when the two of them had carried the packet up this hill in a hatbox, pretending it was a hat — but Wren heard it all at one remove, through the ringing of her own thoughts.

Once, passing her the salt, Finch said quietly: "You're wondering whether to tell them."

"I'm wondering what telling them costs," she said, "and what not telling them costs, and which bill comes due first."

"Dominic said you'd ask exactly that." Finch's eyes crinkled, though not happily. "He also said — and I quote, because I have had thirty years to memorize it — *tell her the household was never the disguise. It was always the point.*"

Wren looked at her plate and blinked hard and pretended the wind had got in her eyes, and Finch, being a gentleman, refilled her glass and said nothing at all.

At first light they came down the hill together, the lantern swinging between them, the letter riding in an inner pocket of her coat where she had sewn it flat against the lining with the neatness of a girl who measures her whole life in small portable things. The city lay grey and smoking below, and somewhere in it, on a street called Candlewick Row, her family was waking up to kettles and quarrels and ordinary morning noise, not knowing they were rich, not knowing they were hunted, not knowing that in nineteen days the law would ask them to eat their own hearts.

The walk passed a cooperage, a chapel, a shuttered chandler's. Nothing stirred. It was almost — almost — disappointing.

Then, at the corner where the hill road bent toward the bridge, Finch stopped and caught her arm, and Wren saw it at the same moment he did: the door of the workshop standing open. Not broken. Merely *wrong* — ajar by exactly four inches, neat as a filed arbor, deliberate as everything else the Harrows touched.

And from inside, faintly, came the sound of Tobias shouting, and beneath his voice, lower and colder, the sound of men who did not live there, turning over drawers.
