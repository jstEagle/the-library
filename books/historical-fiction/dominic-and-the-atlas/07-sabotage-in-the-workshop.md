## 7. Sabotage in the Workshop

Tobias Fenwick had kept ledgers long enough to know that every disaster announced itself in advance — a column that refused to sum, an entry made twice, a debt that grew a shilling fatter each week like something fed by hand. Disasters were orderly things. They arrived on schedule.

Which was why, standing in the doorway of the workshop at half past six in the morning with his hat still on and his breath still outside him, he could not make sense of what he saw. This disaster had not been entered in any book he kept.

The drawers of Dominic's great workbench had been pulled out and turned upside down, their contents scattered across the floorboards like a spilled purse of bones. Gears lay everywhere, brass and steel, winking up at him from between the planks with a terrible innocence. The cabinet of watch glasses had been opened without its key — the door hung at a wrong angle, one hinge weeping a small silver screw. Even the jars of oil and jeweler's rouge had been rearranged, set back in the wrong order, which struck Tobias as somehow the most violent act of all. In this house, everything had its place, and everyone knew the places, and the knowing was the whole point.

"Hello?" he said, uselessly, to nobody. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

He stepped inside carefully, the way one steps into a church after a robbery, and heard himself crunch. He looked down. Glass. A drawer's worth of empty glass vials, crushed beneath someone's boot heel — boots bigger than his, bigger than Marisol's, bigger than Wren's neat little shoes. He crouched and picked a shard out of the tread of his own boot with a fingernail, as if evidence might explain itself to him if only he held it correctly.

It did not.

Tobias Fenwick had come to the Brightwater household seven years ago with one trunk, a forged letter of reference, and eleven pounds nine shillings he had not entirely earned. He had fled a position at Cornish & Meld, import brokers, where he had made a small arithmetic of his own desperation: three hundred pounds owed to men whose names he had never learned because the men themselves never appeared, only their clerks, only their letters, only the interest compounding politely month upon month like ivy over a window. He had told Dominic Vale that he was seeking quieter work. Dominic had looked at him for a long moment — that look of his, patient as a pendulum — and said, "You'll want supper first. Books keep till morning."

That was the whole of the interview. Tobias had spent seven years waiting for the rest of it and it had never come.

Now he stood among the wreckage of the workshop and felt the ivy close over the glass.

* * *

By seven o'clock the whole household had assembled in the wreckage like witnesses to an accident none of them would admit to having seen.

Marisol arrived first, in her apron, flour still on her knuckles, and stopped so abruptly that Ezra walked into her back. The old sailor peered around her bulk, took in the scene, and whistled — low and mournful, the sound he otherwise reserved for funerals and bad weather.

"Somebody wanted finding," Ezra said. "Not taking. Finding." He nodded at the floor, where a small fortune in gears and springs lay glittering, untouched. A thief with any sense would have filled his pockets. These pockets, apparently, had been searching for something particular.

"Wren's not down yet?" Marisol asked.

"She went to the observatory yesterday," Tobias said. "The summons. Didn't she tell you—"

"She left a note," Marisol said, too quickly, and Tobias understood that the note had been left for Dominic, and that the question of whether anyone else had read it was a door Marisol preferred not to open before breakfast.

And then came the sound that Tobias would hear for the rest of his life whenever he thought of this room: the sound of Dominic Vale's cane on the stairs, slower than it had been even a fortnight ago, each step placed like a man crossing ice.

Dominic appeared in the doorway. He was seventy-one years old and had looked sixty for twenty years, but illness had caught him at last and closed the gap in a single season, folding him inward like a instrument put away damp. He surveyed the room. His face did not change — it almost never changed — but his hand tightened on the head of his cane until Tobias could see the tendons stand out like cables under the skin.

"The green ledger," Dominic said.

"I'll look," Tobias said at once, because looking was his gift; give him chaos and he would impose an index upon it. He began righting drawers, sorting, restoring. "The green one with the brass corners? The accounts?"

"All of them," Dominic said quietly. "But the green one especially."

There was something in his voice Tobias had never heard before, and he had spent seven years listening to this man the way sailors listen to weather. It was fear.

Dominic crossed the room — no cane-planting now, just quick uneven strides, careless of the glass — and pulled open the false bottom of the tallboy in the corner, the one everyone pretended not to know about. Empty. He went to the shelf above the hearth. Empty. He stood a moment in the middle of the floor, turning slowly, a man reading a room the way Tobias read a page, and then his face went grey, actually grey, the color of pewter, and his knees simply declined their further service.

Tobias reached him before he struck the floor — barely. Marisol was there a heartbeat later, and Ezra, and for a confused minute the three of them lowered Dominic onto the good chair by the cold stove while Marisol barked for salts and Ezra barked for brandy and Tobias, absurdly, held his employer's wrist and counted the pulse the way he counted anything, gratefully, because counting was solid ground.

"He's breathing," Tobias said. "He's breathing fine."

"Fine," Marisol muttered, forcing the brandy between Dominic's lips when his eyes fluttered. "Men who breathe fine don't fold up like laundry."

* * *

They got him upstairs to bed, all four of them — though it said something about the household's proportions that Marisol bore half his weight herself without complaint or visible effort — and settled him under quilts, and then Marisol stationed herself in the sickroom like a garrison and would not be moved. Ezra took the kitchen and the front windows. Which left Tobias alone, at nine o'clock in the morning, with the wreckage downstairs and a single instruction delivered from the stairs in a voice gone thin:

"Tobias. A word."

He climbed. Dominic lay propped against pillows, looking out the window at the street below, where the ordinary traffic of Fetter's Lane went about its business: the milk cart, the knife-grinder, two boys racing hoops. Nothing out of place. That was the trouble with the world; most of its disasters were private ones.

"You'll take inventory," Dominic said. "Of what's missing. You're the only one I trust to know what was there."

Tobias felt something twist pleasantly and painfully in his chest at *the only one I trust*, and then twist differently at what followed.

"And Tobias." Dominic's eyes came off the window and settled on him, dark and unhurried. "If there is anything you have been meaning to tell me — about yourself, your history, your debts, your name — today is an excellent day for telling it."

The floor seemed to tilt. Tobias heard himself say, with perfect clerkly composure, "I don't know what you mean, sir," and heard how the lie sounded — smooth, practiced, the reference letter of it audible in every syllable.

"No," Dominic agreed mildly. "I expect you don't." And he closed his eyes, and within moments his breathing deepened into sleep, leaving Tobias standing there holding the sentence like a live coal.

* * *

Inventory took him until mid-afternoon.

He worked methodically, as was his nature and his armor. Tools: present, all of them, even the good loupe. Stock of movements: present. Cash box behind the loose brick in the chimney breast: present, and unopened, forty-one pounds sitting there like bait nobody had wanted. What was missing was paper. Ledgers — eleven of them, going back to the founding of the household, the complete financial and personal history of Dominic Vale's strange little kingdom. Correspondence from the locked writing desk. And one more thing, which Tobias found only because he knew to look: the deed-box under the floorboards beneath the workbench, a hiding place so old that Tobias had discovered it as a boy might discover buried treasure, years ago, and never told a soul, and never touched it either, because some things in a house are not yours even to know about.

The box was there. The box was empty except for a folded sheet of newspaper and, tucked into it, a single strip of black wax, broken, bearing the impress of a seal Tobias did not recognize: a ship under full sail.

Harrow Shipping.

He sat down hard on the workbench stool. His hands were not steady, so he folded them, the way he did when the sums frightened him, and made himself think.

He knew that name. Of course he knew that name. Harrow & Sons appeared in the old ledgers — he had entered invoices against them himself in the early days, freight and passage and one memorable entry, *consolidation of account, see Mr. V.*, that Dominic had never explained. And Tobias knew another thing about the name Harrow, a thing he had told no one: the men to whom he had owed three hundred pounds seven years ago, the men whose clerks had written him such patient, escalating letters — their stationery had carried a small printed crest at the top. A ship under full sail. He had told himself for seven years that many firms used ships in their crests. He had told himself this with the discipline of a man balancing a book that would not balance.

The ransackers had taken ledgers, letters, and a deed. They had left money, tools, and stock. Whoever they were, they were not thieves. They were researchers. And they had known precisely which papers mattered, which meant somewhere in the past seven years, somebody had read those ledgers who should not have, and copied what mattered, and waited.

Somebody like a clerk in desperate debt, who once, for six months' forbearance and thirty pounds, had let a stranger copy pages from his employer's books late at night when the workshop slept.

Tobias had done it at Cornish & Meld, before Brightwater — that was the story he had kept polished and ready, and it was true, and it was also not the whole truth, because the whole truth included the fact that the copying had not stopped being offered when he changed employers. It had stopped being accepted. Mostly. There had been one letter, four years ago, which he had answered with one page torn from the green ledger, and thirty pounds, and a promise that the account was settled and the arrangement ended forever. Thirty pounds. He had used it to buy Wren her first set of jeweler's screwdrivers, and had never once, in four years, allowed himself to think of where the money came from, and now he thought of nothing else, the memory arriving with the awful clarity of a misfiled gear finally found.

He looked down at the black wax in his palm, the little ship riding its broken sea.

The chimes of the mantel clock — Wren's repair, keeping stubborn time — marked four o'clock. Somewhere out there, a courier was delivering a sealed letter to the observatory, or had already delivered it; somewhere out there, Wren was climbing a hill toward a master clock, carrying nothing but her tools and the household's entire unexamined faith in her. She didn't know. None of them knew, except Dominic, who had feared exactly this for God knew how long, and had said *today is an excellent day for telling it* to the one person in the house whose confession could burn the whole place down.

Tobias sat in the ruined workshop until the light went amber, doing the only thing he knew how to do. He made lists. What was taken. When. By whom, probably. And on a second sheet, in a hand less steady than his ledger hand, he wrote: *What I owe.* Underneath it he wrote the figure — not three hundred pounds, not thirty, but the true figure, which had no numbers in it at all — and stared at it until the words swam.

Then Ezra put his head round the door and said, quietly, for Ezra, "Post's come," and held out a letter.

It was addressed to Mr. Tobias Fenwick, in a clerk's hand he had hoped never to see again, on paper whose crest rode a full-sailed ship. The wax was unbroken. The date was today.

He turned it over twice before his fingers would obey him, and broke the seal, and read:

*Mr. Fenwick — Your former account has been purchased by new parties, who instruct us to advise you that settlement may still be arranged without inconvenience to yourself or to your associates. The green ledger, returned intact, will be accepted in full discharge of all sums outstanding. Kindly consider the brevity of the remaining term. — Yrs., per proc., Harrow & Sons.*

Below it, in different ink, someone had added a single line, unhurried and terrible in its patience:

*Six days remain in the century, Mr. Fenwick. We know you know what that means.*

Downstairs, the mantel clock ticked on, faithful as ever, marking out the shrinking inventory of his days. Tobias folded the letter along its original creases — he was, whatever else, tidy — and understood with perfect bookkeeping clarity that he stood at a fork from which every road was betrayal: of the household, or of himself, and that whichever he chose, he would be choosing it alone, in a house where he had never once been asked to choose anything harder than supper.

He put on his coat.
