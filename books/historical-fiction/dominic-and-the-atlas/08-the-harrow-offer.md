## 8. The Harrow Offer

He came on a Tuesday, because of course he did. Tuesdays were the day the workshop smelled most of solder and least of Marisol's cinnamon buns — a fact I would later realize Edmund Harrow had probably calculated down to the minute. A man who arrives when the biscuits are gone and the defenses are low is not a man who has stumbled in by accident.

I was at the bench, hunched over a lady's pocket watch with a mainspring so tired it had essentially retired, when the bell above the door gave its little cough. Tobias looked up from his ledgers, went pale in the way of a man recognizing trouble from an old nightmare, and said, in a voice pitched for me alone:

"That's him."

"Him who?"

"Harrow. The *Harrow*."

I set down my tweezers. I will confess — since this account is for no one but the register and whoever keeps it after me — that my first thought was not of inheritance or patents or black wax. It was: *he's better dressed than the door.*

And he was. Edmund Harrow wore a coat the color of good sherry and carried his hat as though it had never once known rain. He was perhaps fifty, with the sort of handsome that comes from being photographed often, and grey at the temples in a way that suggested he had arranged for it. His eyes did the arithmetic of the workshop in four seconds flat: the bench, the tools, Tobias, me.

"You must be Miss Brightwater," he said. "The apprentice."

"The journeyman," I said, "as of spring." I do not know why I corrected him. Perhaps because it was the only thing in the room I owned outright.

"Journeyman," he repeated, tasting the word like a wine he had decided to be generous about. "Even better. May I?" He gestured at the stool by the door, and — the man had a talent for this — somehow made it seem as though *we* were hosting *him*, though he'd let himself in ahead of his own knock.

"Mr. Harrow," Tobias said, finding his spine somewhere around his shoulder blades, "Mr. Vale is indisposed."

"So I hear. My condolences to his lungs." Harrow sat anyway. "It's Miss Brightwater I've come to see. What I have to say won't take long, and then you can both go back to pretending I don't exist, which I understand is house policy."

"We don't pretend you don't exist," I said. "We simply haven't needed you."

Something flickered behind the photograph-handsome face. Respect, possibly. Or the recalibration of a man discovering the price had gone up.

* * *

Marisol emerged from the kitchen wiping her hands on her apron, which is Marisol's version of drawing a sword. Ezra appeared in the doorway to the back room with a lag of several seconds, smelling faintly of tar, wearing the expression of a man who had heard the word *shipping* from two rooms away and come to loom. He was very good at looming. Thirty years before the mast will teach a man to fill a doorway like weather.

"Tea, Mr. Harrow?" Marisol asked, in the tone of a woman offering either tea or scalding water, his choice.

"Thank you, no. I shan't stay." He folded his hands. "Miss Brightwater. I'll be plain, because I respect plainness in a workshop. My family's firm has a claim on certain property currently held in trust — property connected to this household through Mr. Vale. There is a document. When the century turns, that document becomes, let us say, actionable. Whoever holds it holds everything. I would very much like it to be held by someone sensible."

"And if it's held by nobody sensible?"

"Then we are all of us poorer and wiser." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on better people than me. "Here is what I propose. Come with me. Not as an employee — as a partner. The Harrow firm opens a horological division; you run it. Your name over the door beside mine. Premises in the City, apprentices under you, wages your father-figure couldn't dream of. Legitimacy, Miss Brightwater. A place at the table instead of a stool by it."

The workshop went very quiet. Even the regulator clock on the wall seemed to tick more softly, listening.

And here is the part I am not proud of: for one whole breath, I wanted it.

Not the money. Well — some of the money. But the *name over the door*. I had spent nineteen years measuring my life in small portable things, and here was a man offering me something enormous and bolted down, something with foundations and a brass plaque, something that could not be packed into a carpetbag when the borrowing ran out. He had seen the exact shape of the ache in me the way I can see a stripped gear through a closed case back — by knowing where to look.

"A partnership," I said carefully.

"Equal terms. Well." He permitted himself a small correction, as though honesty were a garment he adjusted in public now and then. "Proportionate terms. You bring the letter and the skills; I bring the capital and the name. You'd be a Harrow concern. Protected. Permanent."

"Permanent," said Ezra from the doorway, in a voice like an anchor dragging.

"An unfamiliar concept aboard ship, perhaps," Harrow said pleasantly, without looking at him. "But ashore we do our best."

Tobias had gone from pale to the color of ledger paper. I understood why, later — understood it fully — but in that moment I only saw a man watching a debt walk through the door wearing expensive boots.

"Mr. Harrow," I said, "may I ask you something honest? Since you came all this way for honest answers."

"I insist on them."

"What happens to the others? If I sign with you. Marisol, Tobias, Ezra — and Dominic. Does your proportionate partnership have room for a sick old clockmaker, a cook, a clerk, and a sailor? Because the premises sound lovely, but I notice they were sized for one."

Harrow didn't flinch. That was the worst of it — he didn't flinch, because he'd already done this sum too.

"Severance would be generous," he said.

"Severance," Marisol repeated, from behind her apron, the way another woman might say *blasphemy*.

"They are not your responsibility, Miss Brightwater. They are Mr. Vale's collection. And collections disperse. It's the nature of them." He rose, smoothed his coat, and delivered the rest directly to me, gently, which somehow made it worse. "You've been given a family the way orphans are given coats. Warm, useful, secondhand. I'm offering you one that fits. One that's *yours*. Blood, Miss Brightwater. Name. A place that exists before you arrive in it and goes on after you leave. No borrowed father, no assembled brothers. Yours."

The room tilted, just slightly. I put my hand flat on the bench to steady it and felt the worn grain of the wood under my palm — wood polished by twenty years of Dominic's hands and five of mine, layered together like lacquer.

"My name is Brightwater," I said.

"Yes," said Harrow. "That is rather my point."

"It isn't a point, it's a mistake." I stood. I'm not tall; standing up doesn't usually win arguments. But I had learned posture from a man who repairs things for admirals, and it turns out that standing up slowly, while holding a pair of tweezers, has a dignity all its own. "Brightwater was the name Dominic gave me, and it fit better the day he gave it than any name cut to measure would fit now. You talk about family like it's a deed — something recorded, something that precedes you. Mine is something we built. We built it out of nothing, Mr. Harrow, which means nobody holds the title to it but us. And you can't buy what we've already paid for."

The silence afterward had a texture to it. Tobias was staring at me as if I'd balanced the national debt. Ezra made a low sound in his chest that on a ship would have been a cheer.

Edmund Harrow, to give him his due, took the blow like a man who had expected it and filed it under useful information anyway.

"Well said," he murmured. "Rehearsed?"

"Lived."

He put on his hat with two hands, unhurried. At the door he paused, and turned, and this is where I must record honestly that the man finally found the soft place in me, the way a pick finds a ward.

"One piece of advice, freely given," he said. "Ask your Mr. Vale about the *Cormorant*. Ask him about the year 'seventy-one, and whose signature was on the manifest. Ask him why a man with a fortune to guard spends thirty years collecting strays instead of heirs." The grey temples caught the lamplight. "You believe you know what Dominic Vale is protecting, Miss Brightwater. I promise you, you do not yet know what he's *atoning* for."

And he was gone, the bell coughing behind him, leaving only the smell of bay rum and cold air and a silence with teeth in it.

* * *

We stood there a while. Marisol broke it first, the way she breaks everything — with practicality.

"He's wrong about the biscuits," she said. "Tuesday or not, I always keep back a tray."

"You weren't here for the biscuits," Tobias said faintly.

"Everyone is here for the biscuits. It's how I know when a person means harm. Harm never takes the tray." She untied her apron with great decision. "Wren. Whatever he said at the end — whatever word he dropped like a hook in a stream —"

"*Cormorant,*" Ezra said quietly.

We all turned. Ezra had come fully into the room at last, and his face had changed. Not frightened. Worse. *Remembering.*

"You know it," I said.

"I know the name." He lowered himself onto Harrow's abandoned stool, which creaked in protest, and stared at his hands as if reading them. "Thirty years back, give or take. Before I crewed for Dominic proper. There was a vessel — fast, ugly lines, built for running cargo that didn't care to be declared. The *Cormorant*. She went down off the southern coast in the winter of seventy-one. All hands." He looked up. "Nearly all hands. There was a survivor. And the manifest she sailed under..." He shook his head slowly. "Dominic had me burn papers once, years ago. Ship's papers. I never asked whose name was on them. A man who doesn't ask keeps his berth. But I remember the shape of the question."

The regulator clock ticked into the silence, patient as ever, keeping count of something none of us could see.

"So it's true, then," Tobias said. "Whatever Harrow wants — whatever the letter says — there's something underneath it. Something older."

"There's always something underneath," Marisol said grimly. "With men and with houses. Doesn't mean you stop living in them."

But I wasn't listening anymore, not properly. I was thinking about last week, and the night before that, and every small strangeness I had filed away the way one files a misbehaving escapement — noted, deferred, *to be repaired later*. Dominic burning correspondence by candlelight. Black wax. The way he watched me measure things, with an attention that sometimes felt less like pride than like accounting.

A man with a fortune to guard, collecting strays instead of heirs.

Or — Harrow's version — a man with a debt to pay, collecting witnesses.

"I have to ask him," I said.

"Wren." Marisol's hand landed warm on my shoulder. "He's ill. Whatever you ask, ask gently."

"I'll ask gently." I was already reaching for my coat. "But I'm going to ask."

Ezra rose and, unasked, fetched the lantern and lit it, because Ezra had spent a lifetime reading weather and evidently felt some coming. "I'll walk you as far as the hill road," he said. "Night like this, a girl carrying questions shouldn't walk alone. Questions weigh more after dark. Everyone knows that."

Outside, the wind had come round from the east, and the first hard flakes of the season were coming down with it — small, dry, precise, like filings from some vast workshop above the city. Halfway up the hill road, breath steaming, lantern swinging, I asked Ezra the thing that had been sitting in my chest since the door closed.

"Ezra. If the letter names us — all of us — and everything Harrow says about Dominic is true... does it change what we are to each other?"

Ezra walked several paces before answering, snow gathering on his shoulders like a man collecting a debt of his own.

"No," he said at last. "It changes what we're *from*. Nothing changes what we are. A crew's a crew once she sails, whatever port signed the articles." Then he stopped, and lifted the lantern, and nodded at the dark shape of the observatory hill ahead, where one light still burned in the keeper's window — and, higher up, in the tower itself, a light that had no business burning at that hour, moving slow across the clock face like a man searching for something among the gears.

"Though I'll tell you what else everyone knows," Ezra said quietly. "Nothing brings old debts to the door like a turning century. And somebody," he squinted against the snow, "is already inside the clock."

The tower light went out.

I started to run.
