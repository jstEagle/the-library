## 10. Dominic Speaks

Dominic Vale had never once, in fourteen years, let anyone see him sleep.

He was a man who rose before the kettle sang and retired after the last lamp was turned down, who appeared at breakfast already fully assembled — waistcoat buttoned, hair combed to the left, opinions arranged in neat rows behind his spectacles. Wren had privately suspected for years that he wound himself like one of his own regulators each morning, and that somewhere in his chest there was a mainspring he would rather die than let run down in public.

Now he lay propped against three pillows in the narrow room above the workshop, gray as old pewter, his hands folded on the coverlet like instruments put away for the night, and Wren understood that the spring had come very near its end.

The ransacking of the workshop had done it. Three days since Tobias's scream had brought them all downstairs — three days since Dominic had seen his ledgers gone from the locked drawer, since he'd gripped the doorframe so hard Wren heard the wood creak, since his legs had simply stopped agreeing with the rest of him. Dr. Pemberton came, pronounced it his heart, prescribed rest and quiet, and left with a fee that made Marisol mutter darkly about doctors and their arithmetic.

Rest and quiet, in a household holding its breath for December the thirty-first.

Wren sat by his bed with her mending in her lap, not mending anything. She had been sitting there an hour while the winter light moved slowly across the floorboards, and Dominic had slept, if sleep it was — his eyes moving beneath the lids like something being sought in a dark room.

When he woke, he did not startle or apologize, as some men would have. He simply looked at her for a long moment, as though confirming a sum, and said:

"You've been there since Marisol brought up the tray."

"The tray's cold," Wren admitted.

"Then you've earned your reputation as the worst apprentice in Southwark." His voice was thin but dry as ever, and hearing it, Wren felt her chest loosen for the first time in days. "Pour me a little of that tea anyway. Cold tea builds character. That's what my mother said, and look how I turned out."

He drank a mouthful without flinching and set the cup down with the exactness of habit. Then he was quiet again, watching the window, where the frost had grown ferns along the lower panes.

"Wren," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I'm going to tell you a story. And when I'm done, you may be angry with me, and I want you to know in advance that I will deserve it." He paused. "But I'd ask you to hear the whole of it before you decide how much."

Her hands went still in her lap. "Is this about the letter?"

"So Finch told you about the letter." Something moved across his face — not quite surprise; more the weary satisfaction of a man whose last arrangement has worked. "Good. Old Finch. He always could keep a secret longer than he could keep his conscience. Tell me — did he tell you *what* the letter says?"

"He said it names the Brightwater household as custodians. Of an inheritance. Patents, shares, a deed. He said Harrow Shipping wants it, and that when the century turns, whoever holds the letter holds everything."

Dominic nodded slowly, once, like a clock acknowledging the hour. "Finch always summarized beautifully. It's why I trusted him with the hiding place and never with the whole truth." He drew a breath, and it cost him visibly — Wren saw the effort of it in his throat — and went on.

"Then here is the part he couldn't tell you, because he doesn't know it. The inheritance is real. The patents are real — escapement mechanisms, two of them, invented by my father and improved by me, worth more than this whole street. The shares are real. The deed is real, and Ezra could draw you a map to what it covers faster than any lawyer could find it in his files." A ghost of a smile. "And every piece of it is willed, in black and white, under seal, witnessed by a Keeper of the Royal Observatory, to the Brightwater household."

"To the household," Wren repeated carefully.

"To all of it. To Marisol Ferro, cook. To Tobias Quill, clerk. To Ezra Dunn, sailor. To Wren Brightwater, apprentice. To Dominic Vale, lately of nowhere in particular." He recited it like a lesson learned long ago and rehearsed nightly. "Five names. One trust. Expiring at the turn of the century, because lawyers are poets in their way and thought an expiration date added drama."

Wren sat very still. Outside, a cart rattled past on the frozen cobbles, and someone shouted a price, and the ordinary world went on as if the floor had not just tilted beneath her chair.

"You wrote our names," she said. "All of us. In a legal document."

"I did."

"Before—" She stopped, recalculated. "Dominic. Marisol came eleven years ago. Tobias came nine years ago. I came fourteen—"

"Fifteen in March," he said. "You came in March, in the rain, carrying a broken carriage clock in a flour sack and refusing to let go of it even to shake my hand."

"—and you wrote us all into a fortune as if we were — as if we were *family*—"

"As if," Dominic agreed mildly.

"But we're not!" It came out louder than she meant, and she lowered her voice fiercely, glancing at the door through which Marisol might appear at any footstep. "We're not family, not the kind that goes in documents. Marisol needed wages. Tobias needed a bed and a locked door between him and his creditors. Ezra needed a harbor. I needed — I don't even know what I needed, I was seven. We're strays. You took us in. That's charity, that's kindness, it's — it's *good*, Dominic, it's the best thing that ever happened to any of us, but it isn't — nobody writes a *fortune* over strays."

She ran out of breath. Dominic waited until she had finished the way he used to wait for a pendulum to settle before regulating it — patient, unhurried, letting the oscillations die of their own accord.

"May I tell my story now?" he asked.

She folded her arms. It was not permission, exactly, but it was not refusal, and he took it as such.

* * *

"My father," Dominic began, "was an inventor who hated merchants, which is a fatal combination. He built things of genuine beauty — you've handled the '87 escapement, you know. It loses half a second a week in damp weather, and no one has beaten it in twelve years, and they won't." He said it without vanity, the way one states the boiling point of water. "Harrow Shipping wanted those patents. My father refused to sell. So they did what powerful firms do: they waited, and they sued, and they buried him in paper until his heart gave out at fifty-one, and then they offered me a fraction of the worth, and I refused, and I learned that a man alone is very easy to ruin."

His voice stayed level throughout, in the tone he used for explaining the lever escapement to first-years, and somehow that made it worse.

"They took the workshop. They took the house. What they could not take — because my father hid it, and I moved it, and eventually Finch and I sealed it inside a clock, because a vault can be bought and a judge can be bought but a gear-train frightens lawyers — was the proof of ownership. The patents. The shares in three shipping concerns Harrow assumed he'd swallowed. And the Atlas."

Wren's head came up. "*The Atlas?*"

"A ship," Dominic said, enjoying — she could tell, even now, even here — her expression. "Half-built when my father died. Registered, paid for, moored up the estuary these twenty-two years under a caretaker's name. Harrow thinks it was scrapped in 'ninety-one. Harrow is wrong about a great many things, but that one I've treasured."

"A ship," Wren said faintly. "Ezra knows. Doesn't he. That's why he went strange when the letter came up."

"Ezra crewed for me thirty years ago, before either of us had gray hairs worth counting. He helped move the papers twice, once in a storm, once at gunpoint, and never asked a question either time. When I opened this household, I didn't advertise the fact. But he knew what he was signing on for." Dominic's gaze drifted to the ceiling. "Which brings me, reluctantly, to the part where you get to be angry."

"You mean the part where you admit you collected us *on purpose*."

The words fell into the room like a dropped tool, and neither of them reached for them for a moment.

"I had a fortune," Dominic said quietly, "and no heir, and enemies, and a deadline — my father's trust, like mine, expires with the century; the lawyers of that generation had a mania for it. If I died without lawful heirs, Harrow would pick the estate clean within the month. If I named a single heir, I painted a target on them and invited the rest of the world to despise them as a favorite. And I was — Wren, I was *lonely*. There is no clause in any trust document for that, but it weighed as heavily as the rest."

He shifted against the pillows, and Wren, moving before she decided to, adjusted them behind his shoulders. His hand caught hers briefly — a touch light as a fingerprint — and released.

"So I began to gather people. Deliberately. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. Marisol: I found her cooking for men who called her 'girl' at forty-five, and I offered her a kitchen of her own and a name in a document she hadn't read. Tobias came fleeing debtors' prison, and yes — I knew his debts were entangled with Harrow's factors, and I took him in partly out of mercy and partly because a man inside Harrow's ledgers sees Harrow's habits. Ezra I've described. And you—"

"Don't," Wren said.

"—you were seven," he went on, inexorable, gentle, "and you were guarding a broken clock like a dragon guards gold, and you looked at my workshop the way starving people look through bakery windows, and I thought: *there*. She measures the world in small portable things, that one. She'll understand exactly what it costs to carry everything you own and call it nothing." He paused. "I chose you first. Before the others. I'll deny having favorites until my dying day, but I chose you first."

Wren discovered she was gripping her mending hard enough to snap the thread. She made herself set it down.

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why write our names at all? You could have kept it simple. One heir. A cousin. Anyone off the street with a signature. Why build a — a *household* around it? Why make us think—" She stopped, because the next word was *home*, and saying it aloud in this room, to this man, was suddenly more than she could do.

Dominic was silent for a long time. Downstairs, faintly, came the percussion of Marisol's knife against the board, steady as a seconds-hand, and the sound seemed to give him courage.

"Because a name on paper is bait," he said finally. "And I refused — do you hear me, Wren, I *refused* — to hand Harrow a lonely heir to devour. Four heirs are harder to intimidate than one. Five are harder still. Every person under this roof makes the others safer. That was the lawyer's reason, and I'll own it."

"And the other reason?"

"The other reason," he said, "is that I wanted a family, and I was too proud to simply ask, so I built one the only way I knew how — mechanically. With springs and balances and careful weights." A rueful twist of the mouth. "It is not a method I'd recommend. Families made deliberately have all the vulnerabilities of families made by accident, plus documentation."

Wren laughed — a wet, unexpected sound, halfway to something else — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"The letter names all five of us," she managed. "But Finch said the law—"

"Recognizes one signature." Dominic's face went grim, and ten years seemed to settle onto it between one breath and the next. "That is the trap in the works, and I did not build it, but I cannot take it apart either. The trust instrument allows for joint custody of the assets but requires a *single principal heir* to execute the claim — one signer, chosen by the household itself, before midnight on the thirty-first. One berth, one name, one person who inherits everything and thereby holds the legal power to protect the rest — or sell them out, or cast them adrift. The solicitors assure me this clause is unimpeachable. Solicitors love clauses that force people to choose between the people they love. It keeps their fees up."

"And Harrow knows this."

"Harrow has known the terms for months. It's why he stole the ledgers instead of the letter — he doesn't need the letter, Wren. He needs *us divided*. One signer means one point of failure. He needs only to find the weakest of the five, or the proudest, or the most frightened, and the estate walks out the door wearing a legal smile." Dominic's hands tightened on the coverlet. "I built a fortress out of people, and now everyone in England with an interest is trying to find the loose brick."

The fire in the little grate had burned low. Wren got up without asking and fed it another coal, because her hands needed occupation and because Dominic had taught her, years ago, that tending a fire was simply clockwork with flame — draft and fuel and timing — and doing clockwork calmed her when nothing else would.

"Marisol," she said, her back to him. "Does she know?"

"She knows enough. She's known since the second year, when she found the draft of the trust in my desk while looking for a recipe. She never said a word. Eleven years, Wren. Think of that. She carried it alone so the rest of you wouldn't have to." His voice roughened. "Tobias suspects. Ezra knows the shape of it if not the letters of it. And none of them have ever once asked you, have they? Not one of them has tried to find out whether you were the chosen signer."

Wren turned around slowly. "How do you know?"

"Because you didn't know until Tuesday," Dominic said, "and this household tells each other everything except its secrets. It's our chief virtue and our entire problem."

He held out his hand to her then — the thin one, the one that used to guide hers over a wheel-cutting engine until the fear left her fingers — and she crossed the cold boards and took it, and sat on the edge of the bed like a girl of seven with a clock in a sack.

"I am sorry," he said. "For the engineering of it. For the choosing. For every year you believed you belonged here by accident when in fact you belonged here by design — and I need you to hear both halves of that sentence, because I cannot tell which of them is the confession and which is the gift. Perhaps there's no difference. Perhaps that's what I most have to answer for."

"Dominic." She looked at their hands, his papery over her callused. "If you tell me — honestly — that none of it was real to you. The suppers. The arguments about the good scissors. Teaching me to cut gears. Any of it. Then I'll be angry, and I'll have the right."

"None of it was real," he repeated softly, wondering. "Child, the fortune was the forgery. The paperwork, the patents, the ship — those were the mechanism. You were the escapement. All of you were. The only true thing I ever built is eating supper downstairs right now, arguing about whether Ezra is allowed to salt the porridge."

Above them, right on cue, came a distant crash, Marisol's voice raised in Gaelic-flavored outrage, and Ezra's laugh rolling up through the floorboards like distant surf. Tobias's voice threaded through it, apologizing about something.

Wren laughed and cried at the same time, without ceremony, and Dominic patted her hand twice, precisely, which was his version of weeping.

"There is one more thing," he said, when she had found her handkerchief. "And then I'll let you go, because Marisol will be up with soup directly and she takes prisoners." His eyes, above the gray exhaustion, were sharp and clear and sorry. "Midnight on the thirty-first, one signature claims everything, and the household must choose who signs. I wrote that clause thirty years ago believing I would be alive to veto it, or dead before it mattered. I was wrong twice." His grip tightened, brief and fierce. "So I am asking you — not as your master, not as the man who chose you first, but as the man who loves you best — to help them choose fairly. Whatever it costs. Because the trap was built to divide you, Wren, and the only way to beat a trap built to divide a family—"

"—is not to divide," Wren finished.

"That," said Dominic, settling back into his pillows, closing his eyes, "and to check what the law actually requires of a signature before you sign it. Ask Finch. Ask him about the witnessing requirements. Ask him what happens if the document presented at midnight differs from the one he sealed." A pause, long enough that she thought he'd drifted off. "Ask him why a man who distrusts vaults would trust a clock."

His breathing evened. Wren sat a moment longer, watching the frost-ferns on the window glow with the last of the daylight, feeling grief and gratitude grind against each other in her chest like meshed gears — imperfect, noisy, but turning.

Then she stood, and straightened her apron, and went down toward the noise and the porridge and the smell of burnt salt, and outside, far off across the darkening rooftops, the observatory clock began to strike six.

She counted the chimes. All six, clean and true — Finch had repaired it well, or well enough.

Two nights left, she thought. And a question to ask a keeper.
