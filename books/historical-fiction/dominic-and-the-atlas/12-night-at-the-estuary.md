## 12. Night at the Estuary

**Wren**

The map Ezra drew was a work of art and a lie at the same time. It showed the estuary as a neat blue ribbon, the mudflats as tidy crosshatching, the derelict dry dock as a confident rectangle labeled *ATLAS — berth three*. What it did not show was that in December the mud swallowed boots to the knee, that the tide came in like an ambush, or that the fog off the water had weight and opinions.

"Cheerful place," Wren said, picking her way along the rotting boards of the old coaling pier. Her tool bag hung at her hip, heavier than usual because she'd packed for every disaster she could imagine. "Reminds me of home."

"Home smells better," said Ezra behind her. "Usually."

He carried the lantern low, shaded by his coat so its light wouldn't carry across the black water. He moved like a man walking a deck in rough weather even on solid ground — knees soft, weight centered — and Wren found herself copying his gait without deciding to. Thirty years of sailing lived in his bones and leaked out through his feet.

The Atlas appeared out of the fog all at once, the way ships do: not gradually but suddenly, as if the mist had been holding her and then changed its mind. A barque, masts bare, hull streaked with tar and neglect, riding low and patient at her moorings like a dog tied up too long who has stopped barking but not stopped watching the gate.

Wren stopped. Something in her chest turned over.

"She's real," she said. "I half thought you'd invented her."

"I invented plenty of stories in my day, miss, but never a ship." Ezra's voice had gone quiet and strange. "She carried us through two typhoons and one war scare. Dominic bought her outright in '78 and then did the maddest thing I ever saw a man do with a fortune."

"What's that?"

"He sailed her into this mud hole and walked away." Ezra shook his head slowly. "I asked him why, once. He said, *Because the safest place to hide something is in plain sight, wearing rust.*"

Wren looked at the rust. At the tar streaks. At the careful, deliberate shabbiness of her. Plain sight, indeed. The whole city passed this hulk every day and saw nothing but scrap.

They crossed the gangway — a plank, really, worn smooth down its middle — and stepped aboard. The deck creaked a long complaint. Under their feet, the ship felt less dead than sleeping; Wren could feel it in the way a clockmaker feels a stopped movement, the difference between absence and suspension.

"Cabin's under the quarterdeck," Ezra whispered. "Master's cabin. There's a strongbox bolted under the floorboards, and inside—"

Lantern light bloomed above them.

Not theirs.

* * *

**Ezra**

Ezra put out his own lantern without thinking, the old reflex of a man who'd smuggled things worth smuggling, and pulled Wren back against the mainmast. Above them, on the quarterdeck, two figures moved with lanterns of their own, and their light swung down into the open companionway where a third man's voice echoed, muffled and profane.

Three. Maybe four.

Harrow's men. It could be no one else. Ezra felt the knowledge settle over him cold as spray. He should have known the ledger theft was only the opening hand. Harrow wasn't a man who sent one card at a time.

"—told you there'd be papers," the voice from below was saying. "Harrow says the register proves ownership. Burn the register, burn the claim. Simple."

*Aye,* Ezra thought, *and simple men make simple mistakes.* They were searching aft first, tearing through the master's cabin, which meant they hadn't found the strongbox yet — or if they had, they didn't know what they were holding.

He leaned close to Wren. In the dark he could just see her face, pale smudge, jaw set. Brave girl. Foolish girl. Both at once, which was the family condition.

"The register's not in the cabin," he breathed. "Dominic kept it in the chart room, false back of the drawer, port side. Those fools are ripping apart starboard cabins."

"How do you know which side they're on?"

"I can hear the swearing. Sailors swear port-side first. Habit." He almost smiled. "Listen. When I say go, you go. Low, quick, past the galley. Chart room's twenty steps. You're small, you're quiet, you've spent your life being overlooked — no offense."

"Some taken," she whispered. "And you?"

"I'm going to give them something to look at." He patted the great iron bell housing amidships. "A ship at night makes noises, lads'll think. But a bell that rings itself? That they'll want to investigate. Away from the chart room."

"Ezra—"

"Miss Wren." He used the name gently, the way he'd once used it about her to Marisol, with pride. "I've been wrecked twice, shot once, and married briefly. I know how to take a knock. Get the register. That paper is the whole family standing up straight. Go on my count."

Above, a lantern passed. Below, more cursing, a splintering of wood.

"Three," Ezra murmured. "Two. Go."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren went.

Twenty steps, Ezra had said, and she counted them the way she counted the teeth of an escapement wheel, each footfall released only when the ship groaned to cover it. Past the galley, where the smell of forty-year-old salt and old coffee still clung. Past a coil of rope stiff as wire. The chart room door stood ajar — someone had already searched here and given up — and she slipped inside and eased it nearly shut behind her.

The drawer was exactly where Ezra said. False back, exactly as promised. Wren had grown up around men who hid things; she knew the species of joint, the tell-tale thickness of a board that held more than board. Her fingernail found the seam. The panel came away with a whisper.

Inside lay a oilcloth packet, tied with tarred string.

She untied it with fingers that wanted to shake and refused to be allowed. Inside: a ship's register, leather-bound, stamped with the Admiralty mark. She opened it to the flyleaf and there it was, in ink gone brown with age:

*S.S. Atlas. Registered owner: Dominic Vale.*

And beneath it, in a clerk's later hand, the notation she hadn't dared hope for: *Vessel held in trust per instruments of 1878. See estate filings.*

Proof. Not just of the ship. Of the trust itself. Harrow couldn't burn what was already written into law — but if this vanished, the fight would become their word against a shipping empire's lawyers.

She wrapped it back in oilcloth, tucked it inside her shirt against her heart where her heartbeat suddenly seemed very loud, and turned to go.

The door slammed open.

* * *

**Ezra**

The bell trick had worked for ninety glorious seconds. Ezra had struck it twice with the heel of his hand — *clang, pause, clang* — and heard the searchers aft freeze, confer, and come pounding forward with lanterns swinging, exactly like rats toward a better smell.

What he had not accounted for was the fourth man. There is always a fourth man. Thirty years at sea had taught him this and he had somehow forgotten it in the excitement.

The fourth man had stayed at the gangway, sensible fellow, and now came up behind Ezra quiet as tide.

Ezra heard him at the last moment — boot on wet deck — and turned into the blow instead of away from it, taking it on the shoulder instead of the temple. They went down together in a tangle of rope and ill temper. The man was young and strong and fought like someone who'd learned in a back alley rather than a forecastle, all elbows and panic, and Ezra fought like an old sailor, which meant dirty, economically, and while complaining.

"That's," — he caught an elbow across the ear — "*my* ship, lad, technically, and you're trespassing—"

Someone seized his collar from behind. Two of them now. The deck tilted — no, *he* tilted — and there was a moment, brief and clear as a navigational fix, when Ezra understood several things at once: that he was going to lose this fight; that Wren was already at the rail on the far side, a shadow with a bundle pressed to her chest; and that this was entirely acceptable arithmetic.

One old salt traded for one future. Any captain in history would sign off on that.

He stopped fighting to win and started fighting to be loud. He roared — a proper bosun's roar, the kind that had once carried across a whole deck in a gale — and kicked over the lantern, and in the sudden scrambling dark he heard, faint and beautiful, the soft thud of girl's boots hitting the pier planking.

Then something hard met the side of his head, and the estuary went sideways, and the last thing Ezra Oldsalt — Ezra Marsh, retired, of the Brightwater household — thought before the dark took him was:

*Don't let go of the wheel, boy. Never let go of the wheel.*

* * *

**Wren**

She ran. The mud took her to the shin, sucked at her boots, tried its level best to keep her, and Wren discovered that terror plus fury produces a kind of strength — she hauled her legs free as though the mud owed her money. Behind her, on the ship, shouting; a crash; then, horribly, silence except for the water.

She reached the pier. She could go. Every survival instinct she owned screamed *go, run, get it home, get help.*

Her feet stopped anyway.

Ezra was still on that ship. Ezra, who had drawn her a map with a smiley face next to the label *you are here*, because gentle humor was how he told you he loved you. Ezra, who had taken a beating so she could walk twenty steps.

Wren stood in the freezing mud, seventeen years old and carrying the entire inheritance of the Vale estate against her hammering heart, and understood with terrible clarity that this was the moment everything before had been practice for. You could keep time for yourself, or you could keep it for everyone. There was no third option. Dominic had chosen the second thirty years ago on this same estuary, and now it was her turn.

She went back.

* * *

**Ezra**

Coming to was like surfacing. Cold air, blood in his eye, hands dragging him — and then a voice he knew, hissing furiously:

"If you die on this deck I will never repair your watch again. Do you hear me? Never. I'll let it run down and stop and I'll bury it next to you."

"Wren." His mouth tasted of copper. "That's the cruelest threat I ever heard."

"Good. Stay awake and be offended."

She had him under one arm, impossibly, absurdly, this slip of a girl hauling a man twice her tonnage along the deck, and behind them the searchers were still coughing lantern-oil out of their eyes. She'd gone back through the dark, found him, and — he would piece this together later, marveling — she had jammed the capstan bar through the wheel spokes so the ship couldn't be moved, and led his attackers on a short chase aft, and doubled back. All in the time it took him to bleed.

"You're a menace," he wheezed, as they lurched down the gangplank together, three-legged, into the mud.

"I'm a Brightwater," she said. "Same thing."

* * *

**Wren**

The fog took them in like family and hid them all the way back. Twice Wren thought she heard pursuit; both times it was only the tide coming in, patient as a debt. Ezra grew heavier with every step, and she grew lighter, which made no sense physically and perfect sense otherwise — the register rode warm against her chest, and with every yard the fear in her cooled and hardened into something with edges. Something she could build with.

At the top of the pier she stopped and looked back. The Atlas sat black on black water, masts against the sky like a signature on nothing yet.

"They'll try again," Ezra rasped beside her. "Harrow won't stop. And come midnight New Year's Eve—"

"I know what comes at midnight." Wren touched the oilcloth packet through her shirt. "Then we'll simply have to be quicker than midnight, won't we?"

Ezra laughed, which turned into a cough, which turned into him sitting down abruptly on a bollard.

"Home," Wren said firmly, shouldering under his arm again. "Marisol will feed you until you're sorry, and Tobias will apologize for existing, and Dominic—" She paused. Dominic would understand. Dominic had always understood that a family isn't what keeps you safe; it's what you refuse to abandon in the mud.

"Home," Ezra agreed.

They went up the hill together through the fog, slow, leaving one set of tangled footprints, and behind them the estuary kept its counsel, and somewhere out on the black water the Atlas rocked at her moorings — no longer forgotten, no longer alone, waiting for the rest of her crew.

But when they reached the workshop door, lamplit and golden through the frosted glass, Wren stopped dead on the step.

Every window was dark — except one. And moving behind that one, silhouetted against the curtain, stood a tall figure in a fine coat, patiently waiting to be let in.

Edmund Harrow. Sitting in Dominic's own chair, by the look of it, drinking from Marisol's best teacup.

"Well," said Ezra softly. "So much for arriving home first."
