## 13. The Century Turns Tomorrow

The last morning of the nineteenth century arrived the way bad news does — quietly, and already inside the house.

Wren knew it before she opened her eyes. She had slept with the letter from the clock case beneath her pillow for three nights now, wrapped first in oilcloth and then in one of Marisol's tea towels, because oilcloth alone seemed insufficiently reverent for a document that could unmake her family. Every time she rolled over, the paper crinkled against her ear like a small dry voice saying *remember, remember, remember*.

She lay still in the grey light and listened to the workshop wake up around her. Pipes knocking in the wall — that was Tobias, up early to warm his hands over the stove and pretend he wasn't rehearsing speeches. The particular creak of the third stair, which only Ezra's boots could produce, a sound like a ship complaining in a swell. Below it all, the tick of forty-one clocks, none of them agreeing on the hour, all of them insisting anyway.

Her whole life, she had measured time in small things. The width of a pallet. The turn of an escapement. Now the century itself had become a measurement, and everything she loved was packed inside it like works inside a case, waiting for someone to wind or break them.

She got up. She dressed. She put the letter in her coat's inner pocket instead of under her pillow, because today, of all days, it belonged against her heart where she could feel its weight and account for it.

Then she went down to face the household.

* * *

Marisol had made too much food.

This was how Wren knew, walking into the kitchen, that everyone understood exactly what day it was. On ordinary mornings there was porridge, bread, sometimes an egg if the hens were feeling generous. This morning the table groaned: fried tomatoes and mushrooms, a whole bacon collar glazed with honey, a loaf still steaming from the oven, a dish of preserved plums that Marisol had been hoarding for an occasion so dire she had never once named it.

"Sit," Marisol said, in the voice that meant *sit or be sat*.

Wren sat. Ezra was already at his place, hat on the table beside him like a guest at his own funeral. Tobias sat very straight, hair combed flat, looking as though he had prepared for a deposition. And at the head of the table, wrapped in a blanket despite the stove's roaring, sat Dominic Vale.

He was thinner than he had been a month ago. The illness had taken him down like a tide going out, leaving strange objects exposed — the sharp architecture of his cheekbones, the tendons of his hands. But his eyes were the same eyes that had watched Wren file her first gear tooth at nine years old and said only, *"Again. Slower."*

"Good morning, Wren," he said. "You slept."

"I did."

"Good. Today will want a steady hand."

Tobias's fork stopped halfway to his mouth. Ezra looked at the ceiling. Marisol set down a teapot with slightly more force than porcelain strictly deserved.

"You might have waited until after breakfast," Marisol said, "before talking about it."

"The trouble with waiting," Dominic said, "is that it starts to feel like living. And we cannot live this morning. Not yet." He folded his blanket back with deliberate care, as though preparing to demonstrate something at the bench. "Midnight tonight. The trust expires at the turning of the year, and the terms are simple enough that even Mr. Harrow's lawyers cannot make them complicated. Whoever holds the letter presents it, signs, and inherits the whole of it — patents, shares, deed, and vessel. Everyone else becomes, in the eyes of the law, a stranger at the door."

"And out the door," Ezra said quietly.

"That too," Dominic agreed. "Harrow has been generous with hints on that point."

The fire popped. One of the forty-one clocks, upstairs and out of sight, chose that moment to strike the quarter-hour with tremendous confidence and no accuracy whatsoever. Tobias laughed — one startled bark — and then pressed his hand over his mouth as though he'd sworn in church.

"Forgive me," he said. "It's only — that clock has been wrong since I arrived. Eight years. It strikes eleven minutes late every single day, and every single day I mean to fix it, and every single day something more urgent—"

"Tobias." Marisol put a hand over his. "Eat your eggs."

He ate his eggs. They were excellent eggs. Nobody tasted them.

* * *

Afterward, while Marisol washed dishes with a violence that suggested the plates had personally wronged her, Dominic asked Wren to walk with him.

They went out into the yard behind the workshop, where the cold was clean and the sky hung low and white as unwashed linen. Dominic moved slowly, one hand on the fence rail, and Wren matched her pace to his without being asked. She had learned years ago that this was half of what caring for someone meant: adjusting your own clock to theirs.

"I've been thinking about clocks," Dominic said.

"You usually are."

"Humour an old man. A clock is honest, Wren. Every gear does exactly one job, and does it in full view of every other gear, and if one fails, the whole mechanism stops and announces the failure to the entire street." He paused at the gate, breath fogging. "People are not honest that way. People fail silently. People hide their worn teeth. And then you discover, thirty years on, that the whole machine has been running on parts that never should have meshed."

"Is that what we are?" Wren asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Parts that don't mesh?"

Dominic turned and looked at her for a long moment. The wind moved his thin hair. Behind his eyes, some enormous calculation was taking place — she recognized the look; she wore it herself whenever a movement wouldn't come right.

"You are the finest thing I ever built," he said. "And I never built you at all. Do you see the difference? A maker chooses his parts. I only kept my door open, and the right people walked through it. That's not building. That's—" He searched for it. "That's navigation. You read the weather and you hold your course and you thank God for the crew."

"Dominic." She made herself say it plainly, because the morning was short and the letter was heavy in her pocket. "Ask me what you brought me out here to ask."

Something moved across his face — pain, or pride, or both wearing each other's coats.

"Very well." He straightened as much as his body allowed. "The law gives me no vote and you no guidance. Only a signature line and a deadline. So I am asking nothing, Wren. I am asking *everything*, which is worse. I am asking you to choose fairly. Not generously — fairly. To weigh each of us honestly, including yourself, including me, and to do it without letting love tip the scales. Because if you choose from love alone, you will choose wrong, and if you choose from duty alone, you will choose worse, and either way Harrow wins and this household ends at midnight."

"And how," Wren said, "am I supposed to weigh a person? Weigh Marisol? Weigh Ezra? Put them on a jeweller's scale next to a patent and read the beam?"

"I don't know." It cost him visibly to say it. "I have spent thirty years avoiding the question by refusing to answer it. That is the sum of my cowardice, and I am handing it to you on the last morning of a century. Forgive me. Or don't, and do the thing anyway. Both are permitted."

Wren looked out past the fence, past the yard, toward the city, where somewhere on a hilltop the observatory master clock was ticking through the final hours of its long testimony. She thought of the letter's careful legal language. *The Brightwater household.* As if a household were one thing. As if it could be handed over like a parcel.

"I'll try," she said.

"That's all any clock does," said Dominic. "Try. And announce it loudly when it fails."

* * *

By afternoon the household had gathered in the front room without anyone calling a meeting, the way iron filings gather around a magnet, or perhaps the way witnesses gather around an accident.

Wren sat at the workbench with the letter laid out before her on a square of green baize she used for delicate assembly. The seal — black wax, stamped with the Vale anchor-and-star — was broken now; it had been broken since the observatory, but somehow it looked more broken today, in front of everyone, than it ever had in private.

Marisol stood by the mantel with a dish towel she was wringing like a confession. Tobias sat on the piano stool with a ledger on his knees that he was not reading. Ezra leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, hat on, as if ready to be summoned to a ship at any moment. Dominic occupied the good chair by the window like a king who had abdicated but not yet left the palace.

"The way I read it," Wren said, "and Mr. Pellowe the solicitor confirmed it Tuesday, though he charged me a full guinea to say almost nothing — the instrument requires presentation before midnight. At the observatory. The master clock is named as witness. Its chimes mark the moment of transfer. Whoever stands there at the twelfth stroke of the year's last hour, with this letter and their signature, takes everything. If nobody stands there, or if the document is found voided, contested, or destroyed—" she heard her own voice go dry, "—the estate escheats, and Harrow's claim ripens like rot."

"Cheerful lad, this Pellowe," said Ezra.

"He charges extra for cheerfulness."

Silence took the room and held it a while. The fire worked at its logs. Upstairs, the dishonest clock struck four, eleven minutes late, faithful to its own private hour.

"I'll say it, then," Marisol said at last, and her chin came up the way it did when a fishmonger tried to shortchange her. "Since we're all pretending we haven't each spent the week composing speeches in our heads. Somebody in this room ought to take it. Somebody *will* take it, because the alternative is Edmund Harrow, and I would sooner see the whole estate burn. So let's not sit here performing modesty at one another until the clock runs out. Let's talk like family."

"We *are* talking like family," said Ezra. "Nobody's said anything true yet."

A surprised laugh escaped Tobias, quickly strangled. Even Marisol's mouth twitched.

"All right," Wren said. She looked down at the letter, at the five names she knew were written into its preamble like a crew manifest — *guardians, jointly, of that which followeth* — and felt the vertigo rise again, the same dizzy drop she'd felt in the clock tower when the paper first told her that her whole life had been a clause in someone else's contract. "All right. Fairness. Then we do it properly. Tonight, before we go — because we *are* going, all of us, I'm not sitting home like a pawn while the rest of you freeze on that hill — we each say our piece. What the inheritance means to us. What we'd do with it. No arguing, no scoring points. And then I decide."

"And if your decision breaks us?" Tobias asked. His voice was very quiet. He had gone pale in the way he did whenever debt was mentioned, which was often enough that they had all learned not to look at him when it happened. Wren looked at him now.

"Then it breaks us honestly," she said, "instead of letting Harrow break us lying."

Dominic closed his eyes briefly — approval, or grief, or both. "There's my girl," he murmured, and Wren felt the words land somewhere under her ribs and stay there, glowing and awful.

They began to disperse, slowly, the way a congregation does. But at the door Ezra stopped, and turned, and looked at Wren with those sea-grey eyes that had seen forty years of weather.

"One thing, lass," he said. "Whatever you decide — decide it before you climb that tower. Don't let the clock decide for you. Machines'll always take the choice away if you stand still long enough. I've watched men wait out their whole lives on a dock, letting the tide do their deciding." He tapped the doorframe twice, an old sailor's knock. "Don't be a man on a dock."

Then he was gone into the blue evening, and the house settled into its terrible waiting quiet.

* * *

Wren spent the hours before supper at her bench, ostensibly oiling a carriage clock for Mrs. Pemberton on Chandler Lane, actually listening to the house.

Through the floorboards came the sounds of a family rehearsing its own dispersal. From the kitchen, the rhythmic thump of Marisol kneading bread with unnecessary passion, pausing at intervals — Wren would have bet her best tweezers — to stand with floury hands and stare at nothing. From the parlour, the scratch of Tobias writing and crossing out, writing and crossing out, paper after paper, a man drafting a will in reverse. From the yard, the slow circuit of Ezra's boots, out and back, out and back, pacing a deck only he could see.

And from the good chair by the window, occasionally, a cough.

Wren set down her oiler. She thought about fairness. She thought about what it actually was, stripped of lawyers' language — whether it was giving every gear its due, or whether it was refusing, root and branch, to build a machine whose design required one part to be sacrificed so the others could turn.

Her father — the first one, the blood one, the one she barely remembered — had been a clockmaker too, before the fever took him and Dominic took her. She had a memory like a pressed flower: being lifted up to see the insides of a great clock, and a voice saying, *see how each wheel serves the next, and the next serves all.* She had spent twenty years believing that was the truest sentence in the world.

Now she wondered whether the lawyers had ever looked inside a clock. Whether they understood that a mechanism designed so that one wheel must fall out for the rest to keep turning was not a mechanism at all. It was a trap with a pendulum.

The bell over the shop door rang.

Everyone in the house heard it. Wren heard everyone in the house hear it — the sudden triple stillness of kitchen, parlour, and yard, like instruments falling silent mid-bar. Visitors on the eve of the century's end were not visitors at all.

She put the letter in her pocket, wiped her hands, and went out into the shop.

The man standing between the regulator clocks was broad, well-furred, and expensively gloved, and he was examining Wren's apprenticeship piece — the little eight-day movement she'd finished in Chapter the first of all this — with the air of a man appraising a horse he intended to buy cheap.

"Miss Brightwater," said Edmund Harrow. "I do hope you'll forgive the hour. But then, you of all people know the value of these last few hours." He smiled. His teeth were excellent. "I've come with a proposal I think you'll want to hear before you go climbing any hills tonight."

Behind her, at the top of the stairs, Wren heard the dishonest clock begin to strike five — eleven minutes late, right on schedule — and felt, for the first time in her life, genuine sympathy for a machine that couldn't help being out of step with the world.

"You have ten minutes," she said.

"I'll need twelve," said Harrow pleasantly, and drew off one glove, finger by finger.
