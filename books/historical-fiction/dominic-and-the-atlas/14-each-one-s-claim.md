## 14. Each One's Claim

The thirtieth of December arrived wearing grey, like a mourner who had come early to the funeral of the year. Snow had fallen in the night and lay thin and grudging on the workshop roofs, too little to beautify anything and just enough to make the walk to the pump treacherous. Wren carried the letter everywhere now — inside her waistcoat, against her breastbone, where she could feel its corner with every breath like a splinter she could not reach.

Dominic had asked her to choose fairly. She had spent the whole of the morning discovering that *fairly* was a word with no mechanism inside it. She kept opening it up, as it were, looking for the escapement, the teeth, the spring — and finding only air.

So she did what she always did with a clock that would not run. She took it apart and examined each piece.

* * *

Marisol found her first, because Marisol found everything first. It was her gift and her tyranny. Wren was in the scullery, ostensibly scrubbing a pan that did not need it, when the cook came through the door with a sack of onions on her hip, set it down with a thump, and said, "Well. You may as well ask me while my hands are wet, because I'll answer quicker before I start crying."

Wren turned around, pan dripping. "I didn't say anything."

"You've been looking at me all morning like I'm a ledger you have to balance." Marisol plunged her own hands into the wash-basin, though there was nothing in it but cold water, and worked them together as if kneading invisible dough. "Go on, then. Ask me why it should be me."

"I wasn't going to—"

"Wren." Her voice gentled in a way it almost never did. "Child. You will have to hear it from each of us eventually, and better from us than from your own imagination, which I know for a fact is worse than the truth. I raised you on burnt porridge and second helpings. Let me speak."

Wren set down the pan. The water dripped. Somewhere above them Tobias's floorboard creaked his pacing.

"I was sixteen," Marisol said, "when I came through that door, and I had a baby dead in Valencia and a husband dead with her, and no trade but cooking for other people's children while mine went in the ground. I thought I had come to this country to disappear. And Dominic — that impossible man — he put a knife in my hand and said, *the household eats what you make of it*, and do you know what he meant by that? He meant everything. He meant that I would not be a servant here. I would be the one who made the family, daily, out of flour and salt. Twenty-two years of making it." Her hands stilled in the water. "I am fifty-eight years old, Wren. My knuckles ache in winter. If you give the berth to Ezra, he sails away and takes half my heart with him; if you give it to Tobias, God help us, he'll invest it. But if it is mine, it is a roof. It is the guarantee that this table never empties. That no one here is ever again the person standing outside a door with nowhere to be taken in."

She lifted her hands from the basin and gripped Wren's, wet and cold and strong.

"I'm not asking you to choose me," she said. "I'm asking you to understand what it is I'd be choosing *for*. Not a fortune. A door that locks from the inside, and everyone I love behind it."

Then she picked up the onion sack, because grief in Marisol's world never excused anyone from dinner preparation, and left Wren alone with the dripping pan and the terrible arithmetic beginning in her head.

* * *

Tobias chose the library, which was like Tobias. He waited until evening, sent Wren a note in his small upright hand — *might you spare me a quarter hour? T.* — as if requesting an appointment about coal delivery. When she came down he had laid out the whole scene: two chairs, the lamp trimmed, even a plate of the shortbread Marisol pretended she hadn't made for exactly this purpose.

"Sit, sit," he said, flustered. "This is — I want to do it properly."

"Tobias, it isn't a hearing."

"It is rather, though, isn't it?" He laughed, a thin sound. "That's the devil of it. Sit."

He sat opposite her, knees together, hands clasped, a thin man assembled entirely of angles and apology. For a long moment he looked at the fire instead of at her.

"I came to this house eleven years ago owing four hundred and sixty pounds to men who break fingers as a sort of hobby," he said. "You knew that much?"

"Everyone knows that much."

"Everyone knows the shape of it. Nobody knows the substance." He drew a breath. "It wasn't cards. It was worse — it was *ambition*. I was a junior clerk at an insurance house and I saw how the partners lived and I wanted it overnight instead of over forty years, and I forged endorsements on promissory notes. Cleverly! That's the shameful part, Wren, I was *good* at it. Until I wasn't." He finally looked at her. "When the debtors found me the second time, Dominic paid them off. All of it. Didn't ask a question except whether I'd do the household's books honestly, and I have, eleven years, every entry, and I want you to understand what I'm about to say in light of that: those ledgers are the first clean thing I have ever owned."

The fire popped. In the walls, the workshop clock ticked with the patience of something that had seen every confession ever made in this house.

"So when people say I should take the berth — redemption, they mean, a fresh start — I want to shake them," Tobias said. "A berth doesn't redeem a man. Work redeems him. Showing up at six every morning redeems him. I don't need the inheritance, Wren. What I need is to stay in the room where I became someone worth feeding." He smiled, wretched and honest. "But here is my shame tonight, since we're being thorough: when Harrow's man came asking questions last month, I recognized his voice. He dunned me once, years ago. And I said nothing. Because part of me — the old forged-promissory part of me — hoped that if Harrow won, my debts might burn with the trust, and I could begin again unowned." His voice cracked. "That's my claim and my confession both. Choose whoever you choose. Only — if you choose me, choose me knowing I'm a man still being repaired. Not finished goods."

He pushed the plate toward her. "Shortbread?"

She took a piece. Her eyes were burning and she ate it anyway, because refusing Marisol's shortbread was a sin, and Tobias watched her chew with the expression of a man who had handed over a ledgers' worth of guilt and found the auditor merely hungry.

* * *

Ezra caught her at dawn the next day, which was fitting; dawn belonged to him the way supper belonged to Marisol and figures to Tobias. He was on the back step with tea he had already let go cold, watching the light come up over the estuary haze, and he didn't turn when she sat beside him. Between them, unremarked, he laid a folded square of oilcloth.

Inside was the map. She had seen it once before, the night he'd shown them the Atlas existed — the coastline drawn from memory, the little hook of the estuary, the ship marked with a bold X and, inexplicably, a fish.

"The fish means good mussels," Ezra said, following her glance. "Never mind the fish." He wrapped his scarred hands around the cold cup. "You've heard the others. Cook wants a door. Clerk wants a room. So here's mine, plain: I want the sea."

"Ezra—"

"No, let me get it out; I've rehearsed." He grinned, but the grin didn't hold. "Forty-one years I went down to ships, Wren. Forty-one years, and I never owned so much as a rope on any of them. I crewed for Dominic on the Atlas run in the seventies — I've told you some of it and lied about the rest, and I'll correct the lies another day. Point is, when I retired — when my knee retired me, more honestly — I came here because he asked, and I told myself it was temporary. Thirty years is a long temporary." He nodded at the map. "There's a ship sitting in that mud that has my name in her articles. Has all ours, likely, if the register says what I think it says. And every morning I wake up and I can smell the water from this step and it's like being invited to a feast through a window."

He turned to her at last, and his eyes, so often crinkled with jokes, were grave.

"I won't pretend I don't want the berth. I want it like breathing. But hear the rest: if you give it to me, I'll sail her, and I'll pay wages to every soul in this house, and I'll die happy — and if you give it to someone else, I'll crew for *them*, and I'll die nearly as happy, because a sailor knows better than most that you don't get to own the wind. You only get to sail in it." He tapped the X on the map. "One thing I'll say for my claim over the others'. Whatever happens to papers and trusts and Harrows — a ship keeps people fed. Real food. Real work. It's the only inheritance in that clock that floats."

He refolded the oilcloth with seaman's neatness and put it in her hands. "Hold onto that. Not as a claim. As evidence that the mussel spot is real."

* * *

She had saved Dominic for last without deciding to. Perhaps it was cowardice; perhaps it was sense, the way you leave the mainspring for the end because everything else depends on it.

He received her in his room upstairs, propped in the chair by the window with a blanket over his knees, and the winter light made him look transparent, a man becoming more sketch than portrait. He knew why she'd come before she opened her mouth. He always had.

"You've collected the others," he said. "Marisol's door, Tobias's room, Ezra's wind. Say it — you want mine."

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I'm supposed to be dying, and I intend to be difficult about the schedule." He patted the stool beside him. "Sit, girl. Let an old fraud finish being honest."

She sat. Up close she could see the tremor in his hands that he hid from the others, and it frightened her more than Harrow did.

"They will tell you I gathered you all deliberately," Dominic said, looking out the window at the snow-grey roofs. "That's true, and it sounds monstrous, so let me give you the monstrous version plainly. Thirty years ago I learned that everything my father built — the patents, the shares, the Atlas herself — would pass, on the turn of the century, to whoever held one letter and signed one line. One heir. The lawyers designed it that way to force certainty on an uncertain family, and the Harrows have been circling it like gulls ever since. And I looked at that clause and I thought: then I will build a family so strange that no lawyer can guess who the heir will be. Orphans. Strays. Runaways with false names and true debts. People nobody else would fight for." He smiled faintly. "Camouflage, at first. I will not lie to you. Love came later. Love always does the astonishing thing of arriving after its excuse."

"And the letter names all of us," Wren said quietly. "But the law hears one signature."

"The law is a clockmaker who builds movements and forgets to ask what time is for." He turned to her then, and his eyes were fierce and tired. "Yes, I have a claim. Legacy — they'll call it. Every founder wants his name kept. But listen to me, Wren, and mark it, because this is the only inheritance I have that matters: I do not want the berth. I am seventy-nine years old; what would I do with protection? I have had my life. What I want—" his hand closed on hers, light as paper, "—is what any maker wants at the end. To see the movement keep running after he closes the case. Choose whoever you must. But if you choose *me*, I shall be extremely annoyed, and I will haunt you, and I will be insufferable about it for eternity."

She laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob, and he pretended not to notice, which was his greatest kindness.

* * *

That night she sat alone in the workshop with all their claims spread across her bench like components waiting for assembly. Marisol's door. Tobias's room. Ezra's wind. Dominic's running movement. Four claims, one berth, and every single one of them, she realized, had asked to be understood before they asked to be chosen — had, in fact, quietly refused the thing the letter demanded they fight over.

She turned the document under the lamp and read the clause again. *To the holder of this instrument, duly signed before the last stroke of the century's final hour, passes the whole.*

And it hit her the way a gear hits the wrong tooth — a small sound, and then everything stops.

One signature. One holder. One berth. Whoever signed inherited everything, and everyone else got nothing — got, possibly, cast out, since Harrow's lawyers had already hinted that unclaimed dependents of an expired trust forfeited even the workshop's lease. The choice didn't divide the inheritance. It divided the *family*. That was its function. Some drafter thirty years ago had built a trap into the very heart of Dominic's fortune — and Harrow needed only to stand back and let them spring it on themselves. No sabotage required. No midnight raid. Just four people who loved each other, and one line for a signature, and midnight coming.

"They didn't design a lock," she said aloud to the empty workshop, to the clocks ticking all around her in the dark like a jury. "They designed a key that only fits if you break it turning."

She sat very still. And slowly, the way panic becomes engineering in a mind raised on escapements and pallets, the heartbreak in her chest cooled into something with edges. A trap built by lawyers was still only a mechanism. Mechanisms had assumptions. Assumptions could be violated — politely, precisely, with the right tool at the right moment.

The clause required a signature before the last stroke of midnight. It required *one* holder. It had never once said the document itself could not be amended — if amended before execution, witnessed, sealed, entered into the case of the legal witness itself...

Wren stood up so fast her stool skidded.

From the yard below came the crunch of boots on frost — several pairs, moving with a purpose no late visitor should have — and then Finch's voice, high and strained, calling her name from the gate.

"Wren! Miss Brightwater! They're at the tower — Harrow's men are *in the tower* — and they mean to stop the clock before the year does!"
