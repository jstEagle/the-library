## 15. Harrow Moves at Midnight Eve

The last day of 1899 began, in the Brightwater workshop, with Marisol burning the porridge.

This was not in itself remarkable — Marisol's relationship with the stove had always been one of passionate misunderstanding — but the smoke brought Tobias down from his ledgers with his collar askew, and Ezra up from the cellar where he'd been sharpening things that did not need sharpening, and for a moment we all stood in the kitchen together coughing and laughing and fanning the window, and I thought: whatever happens tonight, remember this. The smell of burnt oats. Tobias's collar. Ezra laughing so hard he had to sit on the stairs.

Dominic took his tea by the fire without comment. He was better — sitting upright again, color in his face — but better was a relative word, like "nearly," like "almost midnight."

"Tonight," he said, when the smoke had cleared, "the trust expires at the twelfth chime. The letter must be presented and signed before then, at the observatory, before witnesses, with the master clock as the legal mark of the hour." He looked at me. "I asked you to choose fairly, Wren. Have you chosen?"

I had not. I had spent three days turning the question over like a gear that would not seat — Marisol, Tobias, Ezra, Dominic, myself — five names on a letter, one signature allowed, four people left out in the cold of a new century. Every arrangement I imagined had teeth in it somewhere.

"I've chosen to be thorough," I said, which is what clockmakers say when they haven't solved the problem.

Dominic almost smiled. "Thoroughness has saved worse situations than ours."

It had not yet saved ours, but I didn't know that then. What I knew was this: Finch's letter said the transfer must be witnessed by the chime of the master clock, because the trust's own language named the observatory timepiece as its arbiter — an eccentricity of the original lawyers, who had trusted no man's pocket watch and no vault, only brass and steel and gravity, things that could not be bribed. Whoever stood beneath that clock at midnight, letter in hand, held everything. Everyone else held nothing.

We were to go up together at eleven. All five of us, so that whichever of us signed, the others would at least be present — a family gathered around its own amputation.

At half past ten, Tobias came into the workshop where I was packing my tool roll, and he was white as paper.

"Wren," he said. "You should look at the hill."

* * *

From the workshop door, the observatory was a dark thumbprint against a darker sky, except that it wasn't dark tonight. The clock tower's lantern room glowed — it should have glowed, the keeper kept it lit — but there were lights below it too, moving light, lantern-light in the wrong places, down near the base where no honest person needs a lantern at half past ten at night.

Ezra appeared at my shoulder with the field glasses he swore he'd left behind at sea twenty years ago and had used every week since. He looked a long moment.

"That's Harrow's blue ensign on the carriage," he said quietly. "And those are Harrow's men, because nobody else stands like that — like they're being paid by the hour and resentful about it." He lowered the glasses. "They're in the tower already, girl. They went in through the boathouse door while the hill was empty."

Marisol had come up behind us with a bread knife still in her hand from the morning's work, which she seemed only then to notice, and set down with great dignity on the windowsill.

"They'll void it," Dominic said. His voice was very calm, the way it went calm before weather. "If the documents are destroyed before midnight — if the letter never reaches the clock — the trust lapses unclaimed, and the estate passes under the old deed. To Harrow Shipping." He closed his eyes. "They are not here to steal, Wren. Stealing leaves evidence. They're here to burn."

"The chimes," I said, and even as I said it my heart was going like a flywheel, because I had spent two days inside that mechanism and I understood something that I don't think even Finch had fully articulated, something that had been sitting in me since the night I found the letter, waiting to matter.

Everyone looked at me.

"The clock isn't just where the transfer happens," I said. "The clock *is* the witness. That's why the lawyers wrote it that way — 'the moment of transfer shall be marked by the master clock.' Not a registrar. Not a magistrate. The *chime*. The trust exists in law because the clock says it does. Which means—" My voice cracked and I pushed through it. "Which means if the chime never sounds at midnight, the moment never arrives. The transfer can't complete. And if the clock sounds *wrong*—"

"If the clock is stopped," Dominic finished softly, "time itself, for the purposes of that trust, stands still."

Tobias, who had spent thirty years making other people's chaos legible, said faintly: "That can't be legal."

"It doesn't have to be legal," said Ezra. "It has to be done before midnight. Legal is for January."

* * *

Here is what happened in the next four minutes, which I recount because afterward no one believed we had managed it so quickly, and because I want it recorded that Marisol was the one who thought of the trapdoor:

Tobias wrote a note in his beautiful clerk's hand — *delayed on the road, come at once, bring the letter* — addressed to no one, meant to be found. We left it pinned to the workshop door, so that if Harrow's men sent anyone back down the hill they would believe the household was straggling up after them, scattered and late, no threat.

Marisol packed food as though we were going to war, which is to say she packed enough for a siege: cold potatoes, bread, a jar of pickled onions, and — I will never understand this — a bottle of the good sherry. "For afterwards," she said, daring any of us to laugh.

Ezra took down the shotgun, considered it, and put it back. "No," he said, mostly to himself. "Not tonight. Tonight we want no bodies, only witnesses." Instead he took the boathook, on the grounds that it was long and discouraging without being permanent.

Dominic tried to stand and his legs betrayed him at the second step. He sat back down in his chair with a terrible patience, and I saw for the first time what it cost him not to rage.

"You stay," I told him.

"Wren—"

"You *stay*." I knelt by his chair. "If we all go up that hill and it goes wrong, there's no one left to send help, no one left to know, no one left to keep the shop. Someone has to keep the shop, Dominic. That's the whole point of a shop." I pressed my hand flat on the armrest. "Keep the shop. Leave the clock to me."

He looked at me for a long moment — the look I knew from my apprenticeship, the one that weighed and measured and found, usually, that I was smaller than the job. Tonight the scales tipped the other way.

"Eleven forty," he said. "The chime must fall at twelve exactly, or not at all. Do you understand what you're claiming?"

"Yes," I lied, and kissed the top of his head, and went out into the cold.

* * *

The hill road was glazed with ice and the wind came off the estuary with knives in it. We went up the back way, the goat path Ezra swore by, which was less a path than an argument the hill was having with itself. Below us, the tower rose black against the stars, and I could hear — faintly, horribly — the tick of the great clock carrying down through still night air. Ten-forty-five. Fifteen minutes of the nineteenth century left.

There was a service door at the tower's base, and a stair inside it, ninety-one steps — I knew because I had counted them twice during the repair, once going up and once going down, because counting is how I pray. Ezra tried the door. Locked, of course.

"The boathouse," he breathed. "They came in through the boathouse. There's a hatch off the coal store, runs up into the cellar of the tower. Old smuggling route — this hill was a Catholic house once, before the observatory, and Catholics build like sailors." He was already moving. "Everything old is a secret if you keep it long enough."

The coal store smelled of a hundred years of fires. The hatch was heavy as sin and opened without a sound, which told me someone had oiled it recently, and my stomach dropped, because recently-oiled hinges meant Harrow's men had come this way, and might come again.

We emerged into the tower's cellar, where the clock's great weights hung in their channels like sleeping serpents — two tons of iron, falling slowly, all night, every night, to keep the hands moving. Above us, through floor after floor, I could feel the tick coming down through the stonework, through my boot soles, into my teeth.

And above that, footsteps. Several sets. And a voice I knew, saying pleasantly, "Search the case thoroughly. Paper burns; gears don't matter. And for God's sake mind the mechanism — I'm told it's worth more than the building."

Edmund Harrow had come himself.

* * *

We stood in the dark among the sleeping weights and I made the plan in the space of six ticks, and the plan was bad, and I made it anyway.

"Tobias," I whispered. "The letter. You carry it. You and Marisol get out the way we came in and go around to the front door and knock like honest citizens. You have until quarter-to. If they let you in, present the letter. If they don't—"

"Don't what?"

"Then they're obstructing a legal transfer in front of a witness, which Finch will swear to, and Harrow's whole game collapses in court in February instead of tonight." I looked at him. "Can you argue with a lawyer, Tobias?"

"I was nearly a lawyer," he said, with the ghost of his old shame and none of its weight. "Before the debts. I remember how they think. They think everyone is frightened."

"Good. Go be frightening."

"And you?" Marisol caught my sleeve. Her face was grey in the dark, and older than I ever let myself see. "Wren, what will you do?"

"I'm going up the inside of the clock," I said, "because somebody has to make sure midnight happens properly."

She gripped my hand hard enough to hurt, said something in her mother's tongue that I chose to believe was a blessing, and pushed me toward the ladder.

Because here is the thing about a tower clock that no one tells you: between the outside of the walls and the inside of the works there is a gap — a crawl-space of timber and dust and pigeon leavings that runs the whole height of the shaft, built by the original makers so their men could reach the hands and the bell without climbing over the escapement. I had found it on my second day, following a draught. It was foul, and dark, and it went all the way up.

Ninety-one steps by stair. Perhaps sixty feet by ladder and crawl.

Ten fifty-two.

I climbed.

* * *

I will pass over most of that climb, because my hands remember it better than my mind does and my hands are not writers. There were rungs missing. There was a place where pigeons had been, recently, and I forgave them nothing. At each landing-slit the wind came through the arrow of the stone and tried to take the lamp from my teeth, and each time I hung in the dark with both arms shaking and thought about small things: the weight of a pallet stone, three grains. The thickness of a mainspring, the width of a hair. My whole life measured in portable things, and now every gram of it needed at the top of a tower.

Below me, muffled by floors, I heard the front door open — voices, Tobias's clerk-calm rising above Harrow's pleasant menace — and then, some minutes later, boots on the main stair, many boots, coming up. They were bringing the confrontation to the clock room, which meant they were bringing it to *me*, and I pressed myself into the framing of the tower and stopped breathing and listened to Edmund Harrow say, directly beneath my dangling foot:

"—a simple matter, Keeper. The letter, the fire, and by January no one is any the wiser. Your part is small. Your part, in fact, is already done — you wrote to me in October, do you recall? *Come and see what Vale has hidden.*"

And Finch's voice, cracked and low: "I wrote to tell you to leave it alone. You read it however suited you."

"Semantics," said Harrow comfortably. "Gentlemen, the case."

I heard iron tools on old wood. I heard the paneling — the paneling I had repaired, whose joints I knew like my own knuckles — begin to come apart.

Ten fifty-nine by the watch in my pocket, which I no longer trusted, because from up here the only true clock in the world was the one I was inside of.

I went up.

* * *

The clock room at the top was a cathedral turned inside out: the great drum of the barrel overhead, the pendulum swinging its slow metronome below the floor, and the whole frame trembling with the effort of being correct. Through the glass of the lantern room the snow-light came down blue. The chime train's hammers stood raised above the bells like executioners at rest.

And wedged behind the fourth wheel of the striking train, where I had found it and left it, because the safest vault in England had proved to be the one everyone forgot to check: the letter.

Harrow's men reached the clock room door at the same moment I reached the ladder-hatch, which meant we saw each other across sixty feet of ticking machinery at precisely the same instant, and one of them shouted, and I did the only thing I could think of.

I dropped the safety catch on the great wheel.

Now — a tower clock's going train, properly governed, releases its power one tooth at a time, delicately, forever. But there is a maintenance mode, and every apprentice learns it first because it is also the fastest way to lose a finger: disengage the escapement, and two tons of falling weight become two tons of *falling*. I did not let it fall. I let it *run* — the winding crank spun free in my hands, screaming, and the noise in that stone room was the end of the world, and three grown men who had been paid by the hour and resentful about it decided simultaneously that the money was insufficient.

They ran. Down the stair, past Harrow, past Finch, gone.

Which left, in the clock room, at eleven o'clock and perhaps fifty seconds by the great hands themselves: me, the letter clutched in my teeth like a pirate's knife, the crank tearing at my palms — and Edmund Harrow, standing very still by the case, watching me with an expression I had last seen on Dominic's face over a well-made gear. Appreciation. It was the worst thing I had ever seen on him.

"You could stop," he said reasonably, over the roar. "You could simply stop, Miss Brightwater, and we could discuss—"

The crank bucked. The minute hand, visible through the dial aperture, shuddered forward — 11:01, 11:02, the weight spending itself in seconds instead of hours, the whole century running out early — and I understood with perfect clarity that I had maybe four minutes before the weights hit the cellar floor and the clock died, and that if the clock died before twelve, the trust expired unwitnessed, and Harrow won everything by my own hand.

Every skill my family taught me converged, right there, in my ruined hands.

Marisol taught me that haste spoils food and hands. Tobias taught me that every disaster is a filing error looking for a home. Ezra taught me that a ship in a storm is saved not by strength but by giving way at the right instant, riding the load instead of fighting it. And Dominic taught me the rest: *listen to the machine, Wren. It always tells you what it wants.*

The machine wanted a brake.

I let go of the crank, dove across the frame, and jammed my wooden tool handle — my own, the one with my name burned into it, the one Marisol had given me at the start — into the spokes of the great wheel.

Wood shrieked. Iron sang. The wheel slowed, caught, groaned —

— and stopped.

The pendulum swung once more, twice, and hung still.

In the sudden silence, snow ticked against the lantern glass, and somewhere below, faintly, I heard the church bells of the city below beginning to ring eleven — the slow, honest, ordinary bells, keeping the real hour for the real world, while above them the master clock of the Royal Observatory stood frozen at 11:03, and the twentieth century arrived to find the door locked and no one keeping time.

Harrow straightened slowly. For a moment neither of us moved. Then he smiled, and it was almost warm.

"Well," he said. "You've stopped the clock, Miss Brightwater. No chime at midnight, no witnessed transfer. The trust dies at twelve regardless — unless you mean to restart it, and restart it *early*, and sign before an incorrect hour, which invalidates it just as surely." He gestured at the dead hands with something like courtesy. "You've destroyed your own inheritance more thoroughly than I ever could. I'll want a full account, when you're sensible. It's rare to meet someone who understands that the clock was always the whole game."

"It isn't the whole game," I said.

"Oh?"

"No." I spat the letter out of my teeth into my palm and unfolded it, shaking, and looked him full in the face. "The clock is the *witness*. I'm the game."

Behind Harrow, in the doorway, Finch stepped into the clock room holding a candle, an inkpot, and the calm of a man thirty years late and finally on time.

"Miss Brightwater," he said. "What is it you intend to write?"
