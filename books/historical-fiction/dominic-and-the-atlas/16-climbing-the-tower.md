## 16. Climbing the Tower

The wind at the top of Observatory Hill had teeth.

Wren felt them first on her knuckles, then her ears, then through the seams of her coat as she came around the eastern wall of the observatory grounds at a dead run, her tool bag bouncing against her hip like a stubborn child refusing to be carried properly. Below the hill, London lay under a blue-black dusk, gas lamps pricking through the haze in their thousands, and somewhere down there — she did not let herself think about it too precisely or her legs would stop — Marisol was standing over Dominic's bed with a rolling pin, Tobias was barricading doors with furniture he was too slight to lift, and Ezra had gone for the constable who would almost certainly not come in time.

Above her, the clock tower rose out of the frozen dark like a black chess piece, its four faces glowing a sickly amber against the clouds. Eleven minutes past eleven. She could see the hands from here. She had been able to see them since the bottom of the hill, which meant everyone else could too, including the three men currently climbing the tower's internal stair with a crate of tools and, if Finch's frantic note was to be believed, enough lamp oil to make the whole legal question of the Vale inheritance moot by way of arson.

*They mean to burn it where it sits,* Finch had written, in a hand so unlike his usual copperplate that Wren had read the note three times before believing it. *Inside the case. They know now. I told them where. God forgive me, I told them. Come quickly, girl. Come up the outside. The stair is lost.*

He had signed it with a small, wretched drawing of a gear, which was somehow more damning than any signature.

"Right," Wren said aloud, because saying things out loud made them true, and what she needed true right now was that this was possible. "Up the outside."

The outside of a clock tower is not built for climbing. It is built, rather deliberately, for *not* being climbed — smooth ashlar stone, ledges the width of a good loaf, and a clock face jutting out at each quarter like a shelf designed by someone with a grudge against humanity. But Wren Brightwater had spent eleven years learning how machines thought, and towers were machines. A tower was a machine for holding a clock up against the sky, and every machine had its service points, its maintenance paths, its places where a person with small hands and no sense whatsoever could find purchase.

She had helped Finch oil the external pulley mounts two months ago. She remembered where they were.

The first twelve feet were the worst — a drainpipe that groaned like a dying whale when she tested it, then a buttress corner where the mortar had crumbled into handholds of exactly the wrong size: too shallow to trust, too deep to ignore. She went up anyway, bag straps crossed tight over her chest the way Ezra had taught her when they'd climbed the rigging of the *Atlas* in the estuary fog ten days ago, him behind her, murmuring *three points of contact, lass, always three*, his breath smelling of tar and courage.

"Three points," she whispered to the wind, and the wind answered by trying to remove all of them at once.

Her left boot found the pulley mount. Her right found a gap between stones that might have been a mason's oversight or a mason's apology. She hauled herself onto the first ledge below the south face, pressed her back flat against the freezing stone, and looked down only once — a mistake. The hill fell away beneath her like the world tilting, lamplight swimming far below, and her stomach performed an operation she had previously only read about in Tobias's medical pamphlets.

"Don't look down," she instructed herself. "You're a clockmaker. Clockmakers look *at* things, not *past* them."

Above her, muffled by stone, came the sound of voices and boots — the men inside, passing the second-floor landing. One of them laughed. The laugh had the particular lazy cruelty of a man who believes he has already won, and it did more for Wren's nerve than all her self-instruction. Her anger, which had been simmering since the workshop had been ransacked, since Dominic had collapsed among his scattered ledgers, since Edmund Harrow had smiled at her in her own parlor and called her *nobody's daughter* as though it were an argument rather than an insult — her anger finished the climb for her.

There was a maintenance hatch on the north side, just below the clock room floor. She'd asked Finch about it once, idly, the way she asked about everything, and he had said, *It's rusted shut, my dear, nobody's opened it since the old keeper's day.* Nobody, Wren had reflected at the time, except whoever had oiled its hinge recently enough that the oil hadn't yet gummed — she'd noticed it on their last visit, a bright thread of fresh lubricant on ancient iron, and filed the observation away without understanding it.

Now she understood it. Finch had kept himself a door. He had kept himself a door the whole time he was selling them out, which meant some part of him had always known he would need to open it again.

The hatch lifted with a shriek that she felt in her spine and prayed the wind would swallow. Cold air rushed down past her, carrying torchlight and voices.

"—Harlow says the letter's in the weight case, behind the strike train—"

"Harlow also said the old man kept it in his desk, and we've torn that desk to splinters. Check everything. We have until midnight, no longer. Whatever that madman Finch is playing at—"

"He's playing at keeping his neck out of a rope, same as us. Mr. Harrow bought him thirty years of silence and he's spent the last of it."

Wren pulled herself through the hatch into the darkness beneath the clock room, eased it closed behind her, and stood in the narrow space between the tower's inner wall and the great timber frame that carried the movement above — a forest of beams, wheels, and hanging weights, all of it shuddering faintly with each tick, as though the tower itself had a heartbeat and was running scared.

She knew this heartbeat. She had nursed it back to health in October, lying on her back in the dust of the clock room while Finch handed her tools and talked nervously about weather. She knew the escapement's particular limping tick, the one she'd never quite cured. She knew the strike train's habit of hesitating before the hour as though gathering its courage.

Somewhere above her, gathering its courage, was the last chime of 1899.

And somewhere above *that* — no. She corrected herself. Above her, in the clock room itself, was the case, the weight compartment, and the hidden cavity where Aldous Finch had sealed away the Vale inheritance thirty years ago, trusting no vault but this one. And standing between her and it were three of Harrow's men with a crowbar and a can of lamp oil.

* * *

She climbed the last ladder slowly, testing each rung, listening. The voices had moved to the far side of the clock room, near the east face. Good. The mechanism housing stood center-room, a cathedral of brass and steel taller than two men, and the weight case ran up its spine like a tower within the tower. If she circled wide along the wall, in the shadow of the west face's works—

A hand closed on her arm.

She nearly screamed. The hand belonged to Aldous Finch, who clapped his other palm over her mouth and drew her back into the recess behind the west going-train, and for one absurd moment Wren found herself cataloguing, with the detached calm of terror, that his hand smelled of beeswax and gunpowder, which was a combination she had not previously encountered on a human being.

"Hush," he breathed. "Hush, girl, it's me."

She peeled his hand away. "I know it's you. You wrote me a note shaped like a confession."

"I wrote you a note shaped like a prayer." His eyes were terrible in the gloom — red-rimmed, exhausted, lit from below by the lantern the men had left on the floor across the room. "I don't pray, as a rule. Never saw the use. But I have been these past three days, and every prayer had your face on it."

"Flattering. Where are the others?"

"Where they should be — nowhere near this tower." He glanced toward the voices. "Harrow took six men to the estuary tonight, thinking you and the sailor would run for the ship again. These three came here with me. He thinks I'm delivering the location of the documents and then walking away whistling."

"And instead?"

Finch's jaw worked. For a long moment he looked less like the fussy, deferential keeper she'd known for months and more like the young man who had once, thirty years ago, hidden a fortune inside a clock because he trusted gears more than people.

"Instead," he said quietly, "I am standing in front of the weight case, and behind me is a crowbar, and I have told these gentlemen that the mechanism is rigged — that any attempt to force the case will drop the striking weight forty feet onto whichever of them is standing closest. Which is a lie." He produced, from inside his coat, the crowbar, and held it like a man who had never held one before, which he plainly had not. "But it is a lie they must test to disprove, and none of them wishes to be the tester."

"You're holding them off. Alone. With a lie."

"With a very well-machined lie." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite humor; the ghost of where humor used to live. "I served Dominic Vale for thirty years, Miss Brightwater. Lying well is the least of what I owe him. And you—" He stopped. Started again. "What I told Harrow about your household. What I gave him. There isn't a night I'll sleep soundly again for the rest of my life, whatever happens in the next forty minutes. But perhaps if you get up those stairs of time—" he gestured vaguely at the whole trembling machine around them, "—perhaps I sleep badly in a warm house instead of a cell."

"That," Wren said, "is the most honest thing anyone's said to me all week."

Across the clock room, a voice barked: "Oi! Finch! Who else is up here?"

Nobody moved. The wind filled the silence, moaning through the louvres of the east face. Then footsteps, coming around the mechanism, and a lantern's glow swung toward them, and Finch did the bravest stupid thing Wren had ever witnessed: he stepped out of the shadows, raised the crowbar over his head like a man threatening to conduct an orchestra, and bellowed:

"The weight! Touch the case and the weight comes down!"

Which was true of nothing, and convinced nobody, and bought Wren exactly four seconds.

She used them. She dropped low, darted along the base of the mechanism housing, and slipped into the gap between the weight case and the west wall just as the nearest man rounded the corner, lantern high, shouting. The clock room erupted — boots, cursing, Finch's voice cracking heroically on threats he could not keep — and Wren pressed herself flat against cold iron and thought, with a clarity that frightened her, *I know this machine better than any of them will ever know anything.*

Because she did. That was the whole of it. That was the secret the last eleven years had been building toward, the thing she had never put into words because it had seemed too small: she knew this machine. She knew it the way Marisol knew when bread was ready by smell alone, the way Ezra knew a squall was coming by the taste of the air, the way Tobias knew a ledger was cooked by the shape of a column of numbers, the way Dominic knew a person's whole history by watching their hands. Every skill her family had ever taught her converged here in the dark — Marisol's patience, Ezra's nerve, Tobias's precision, Dominic's stubborn faith in things that could be mended — and it turned out that *knowing a machine* was simply another name for being loved by people until you became capable of this.

The weight case was locked — no, not locked, *latched*, and latches were opinions, and opinions could be changed. Her picks were already in her hand. She had re-pinned this latch in October after finding it sprung; she knew its temperament, its little trick of wanting pressure upward-left while the tumbler gave. Behind the case, above, the great weights hung on their cables, patient as executioners. To her left, through a slit in the housing, she could see lantern light jerking and hear Finch's voice rising toward genuine panic.

The latch gave with a click she felt more than heard.

She slid into the weight compartment — a vertical shaft barely wider than her shoulders, iron on three sides, the counterweight cable humming faintly beside her like a harp string — and climbed. Rungs were set into the inner wall, ancient, rust-flaked, each one an act of faith. Above her, the documents waited in their cavity behind a false plate: the letter, the patent, the deed, the shares. Everything. The whole borrowed shape of her life, folded up in paper, twenty feet above a floor where men were now physically dragging Aldous Finch away from a case they no longer believed was trapped.

"Pull him off!" someone snarled. "Get the oil! We fire the case and be damned to the trap!"

Below her, Wren reached the false plate, got her fingertips into its seam, and pulled. It came free with a grinding whisper, and there they were, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with lawyer's ribbon, exactly where a frightened young keeper had hidden them three decades ago: the inheritance of Dominic Vale, and with it, in eleven minutes, the future of everyone she loved.

Boots rang on iron. Someone had found the ladder.

She had the bundle in her coat, buttoned against her chest, before the lantern light reached her — and then she made the decision that would have made Ezra proud and Marisol furious: instead of retreating down the shaft toward the men, she climbed *up*, faster, higher, past the weights, into the upper works where the going-train spun in the dark like machinery in a dream, until the ladder ended and she emerged, blinking, into the clock room proper — directly behind the three men, with the full height of the mechanism between them.

All three turned. Finch, half-pinned against the wall, stared at her in horror. The biggest of the men, the one with the oil can, began to smile.

"Well," he said. "The little clockmaker delivers herself."

"Actually," said Wren Brightwater, backing toward the east face with her hand finding the release lever she had greased with her own hands in October, "I'm here to set the time."

And she pulled it — the lever that disengaged the entire striking train from the movement, the safety every clockmaker knows and no shipping thug does — and above their heads the great bell of the observatory, primed and waiting for midnight, went suddenly, utterly silent. The hands froze at seventeen minutes to twelve.

Every face in the room turned upward in superstitious dread, because a stopped clock at the top of a tower in a freezing wind sounds like the end of the world.

"It's broken," Wren announced into the ringing quiet, with the serene confidence of a liar who has excellent craftsmanship behind her. "Completely. And unless one of you happens to know which wheel goes where — that's the third wheel, mind, not the second, everyone gets it wrong — this clock will not strike again tonight. Not at midnight. Not ever, without me."

The big man's smile died. Somewhere below them, faint through the stone, the city bells began to ring the three-quarters.

And Wren, heart hammering against the stolen papers, thought: *Now I just have to hold the last quarter-hour — and build a new document out of an old lie, before the century turns.*
