## 17. The Last Chime of 1899

The clock room smelled of oil and cold iron and, faintly, of the century that was dying on the other side of the glass.

Wren had imagined this moment a hundred ways in the climbing of it — in the stairwell where the wind came through the stone like breath through teeth, in the last ladder where the ice had turned every rung into an argument. In none of those hundred imaginings had she pictured Edmund Harrow's men already there, and in none of them had she pictured Aldous Finch standing between them with a fire axe.

He held it badly. That was the first thing she noticed — not the absurdity of a Keeper of the Royal Observatory brandishing an axe in his shirtsleeves at eleven minutes to midnight, but the *badly*, elbows locked, weight on his heels. Thirty years winding a clock taught a man many things; axe-work was not among them.

"Gentlemen," Finch said, in the voice he probably used for telling astronomers their calculations were wrong, "you are trespassing on His Majesty's time."

"It's the Queen's time," said the taller of the two men, "and it's about to be Mr. Harrow's."

"Her time," Finch conceded, without moving. "Still. Mine to keep."

Wren stayed in the shadow of the ladder hatch, heart going like a flywheel. There were three of them, not two — the third stood by the clock case itself, and he was doing something to the great door with a crowbar that made her whole body flinch, because you did not put iron to a movement like that, you simply did not, it was like prying open a ribcage to get at a heart—

"Finch," she said.

Everyone turned. The crowbar man paused mid-heave, which she supposed was one small mercy to the mechanism.

"Miss Brightwater." Something moved across Finch's face — relief and shame tangled together, neither winning. "I told them you'd come."

"You told them?" The taller man laughed. "You've been telling us everything for a month, old man."

"Yes." Finch did not look at him. He looked at Wren, and his voice dropped to something meant only for her, though the room was small enough that it reached everyone. "A month ago I would have let them break that clock and burn what's inside it, and I'd have told myself it was practicality. A month ago I thought the letter in there was a dead thing, and Dominic Vale a fool for trusting paper to iron instead of iron to stone." He shifted his grip on the axe — no better, but more decided. "Then they sent a girl to fix it. And she fixed it. And I remembered what it is we're actually keeping here."

"Step aside, Finch," the tall man said. "Mr. Harrow's instructions are clear. At midnight the trust lapses. All we need is for nothing to be presented before then. One smashed case. One burned letter. Simplest job in London."

"And if the clock stops wrong," Wren said quietly, "the chime doesn't mark the moment at all, does it? No legal witness. No transfer. The estate goes to — whoever the courts decide. Which, after thirty years of Harrow lawyers buying judges, would be Harrow." She had pieced it together on the stairs, gear by gear, the way she pieced everything. "You're not destroying evidence. You're breaking the witness."

The crowbar man smiled at her, and it was not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Clever little thing. Mr. Harrow said you would be."

"He also said," Wren went on, and now she stepped out of the hatch fully, into the lamplight, into the room where her life had changed three weeks ago, "that family is blood and name. He said it like it was mathematics. Like there's only one way to sum a person up." She walked past all of them — past the axe, past the smile, past the crowbar — and laid her palm flat against the cold brass of the clock case, and felt through it the enormous patient heartbeat of the machine. Eleven minutes to midnight. The escapement ticked against her hand like a pulse answering a pulse. "My father — not the one who made me, the one who *chose* me — taught me that any mechanism can be beaten. Not by force. By understanding it better than the man who built it."

"The man who built this one," said Finch, "built it to be honest. That's its weakness and its virtue."

"No," said Wren. "Its weakness is that everyone assumes a clock can only tell one kind of truth."

She opened her satchel.

* * *

They had argued about it until dawn, the night before, in the workshop that smelled of solder and Marisol's panic-bread. Tobias pacing, Marisol gripping the table edge, Ezra turning his cap in his hands like a man at prayer, Dominic propped in his chair looking, for the first time in Wren's memory, simply old.

"The document must be signed and sealed inside the case before the twelfth chime completes," Finch had said, when Wren had run home from the observatory with the idea half-formed and entirely mad. "The original trust names the clock as its witness. The chime marks the instant of transfer. If a valid instrument is within the case at the moment of marking, the law recognizes it — I have spent thirty years making sure of it, reading every clause until I could recite them in my sleep."

"One berth," Dominic had said heavily. "That was the term. One signature recognized. I begged them for more, thirty years ago. Lawyers are generous with everything except arithmetic."

"Then we don't ask their permission," Wren had said. "We amend it. Five signatures. Equal shares. It's still one instrument, still one letter, still one berth — but the berth holds all of us."

Tobias had been the one to find the flaw first, because Tobias found flaws the way other men found trouble: instinctively, remorselessly. "Amendments require consent of the grantor and attestation of the witness. Both alive. Both present." He'd looked at Dominic. Then at Finch, who wasn't there yet.

"I'll sign," Dominic had said simply. "It's my estate. It should have been five shares from the beginning. I was a coward about it — I thought dividing it evenly would invite them to pick us off one by one, so I built a fortress around a single heir instead, and look what the fortress did. It nearly divided the only thing I ever built that mattered."

And Marisol had cried, briefly and furiously, and then threatened to feed everyone until they were too heavy to climb towers, and Ezra had said nothing at all except to take Wren's hands in his two ruined ones and hold them, which from Ezra was a speech.

Now, in the clock room, with nine minutes left, Wren took out the document.

It was written in Tobias's best clerk's hand on Marisol's good linen paper — "if it's worth fighting for it's worth writing properly" — amended and fair, five names for five shares, and beneath the amendment clause, four signatures already dried: Dominic Vale's spidery flourish, Marisol's careful loops, Tobias's cramped precision, Ezra's laborious capitals, learned late and never trusted. The fifth line waited for her.

The sixth space waited for a witness.

"All right, Finch," she said. "Time to choose again. Last chance this century."

The crowbar man took a step toward her. Finch swung the axe — not at anyone; he brought it down across the hatch ladder, jamming it, splintering the top rungs into a bristle of wood and iron. It was, Wren thought, exactly the sort of thing a clockmaker-swordsman would do: not violence, just removal of options.

"That," Finch said, breathing hard, "was thirty years of pent-up frustration, gentlemen, and I have another decade's worth. Sit down."

To Wren's lasting astonishment — and, by their faces, to their own — they sat. Perhaps it was the axe. Perhaps it was the sound of the tower above them, the great wheels turning overhead, the whole sky full of ticking. Or perhaps, she thought later, it was simpler than that: men hired to break a thing will hesitate when the thing looks back at them and refuses.

Eight minutes.

She worked.

The clock case opened along seams most people never saw, because Dominic had designed them and only a Brightwater hand knew where to press. Inside, in the little iron cradle Finch had installed three decades ago, lay the original letter in its oilcloth shroud — black wax seal intact, the wax gone dark as dried blood. She did not read it. She knew what it said. She had known since the day she found it, and knowing had cracked her world open and never quite closed the seam.

Beside it, she set the new document.

Then the real work began, because a legal instrument inside the case was not enough — Finch's clauses required it to be *kept*, and kept meant secured, and secured meant, in the language of clocks, mounted so that the mechanism itself acknowledged it. She had built the fitting herself, over four sleepless nights, out of brass and spring steel: a small cradle that would take both letters side by side, wired to the striking train so that at each chime the mechanism pressed them — gently, the way you press a hand — confirming their presence. Twelve confirmations. Twelve witnesses in one.

Her fingers were numb. The lamp guttered. Below her, distantly, the city was gathering itself in the streets, and she thought of Marisol and Ezra and Dominic waiting at the foot of the hill, and Tobias — Tobias had insisted on coming partway, and was presumably at this moment standing in the stairwell being brave at a door, which for Tobias was heroism of the highest order.

Six minutes.

"Miss Brightwater." Finch crouched beside her, watching her seat the cradle with the expression of a man watching surgery on his own child. "When this is done — whatever happens after — I want you to know I'm sorry. For the month I spent on their side of it."

"You weren't on their side," Wren said, fitting the last screw. "You were on nobody's side. That's different. Lonelier." She glanced up. "Sign."

He signed with the stub of pencil she handed him, his hand shaking only a little, and she blew on the ink and slotted the paper home into the cradle, and the mechanism took it — a soft click, precise as a heartbeat, the clock accepting its burden.

Four minutes.

"Now," said the tall man, rising, "you stop it. That's the plan, yes? Freeze the moment? Because if the clock doesn't strike, there's no transfer at all, and the courts—"

"The courts will find an unexpired trust naming five heirs and a documented witness," Wren said, "which Mr. Harrow cannot contest, because contesting requires an opposing claimant, and there isn't one. There's no sole heir to argue with. There are five, and they agree with each other." She stood, wiping her hands on her apron, and allowed herself the smile she usually saved for a stubborn mainspring. "You've been trying to break a game with two players. I've added four more and rewritten the board. Go tell Mr. Harrow."

Two minutes.

The men looked at one another. Then — she would wonder about this for years — they simply left. Down through the hatch, around Finch's wreckage, gone into the cold, because men paid to smash clocks do not stay to be defeated by them; there was no percentage in it. Only the tall man paused at the ladder, and said, almost respectfully: "He'll try something else. He always does."

"Good," said Wren, who did not entirely feel it. "I'll be keeping time."

One minute.

Finch stood beside her at the case. Through the glass, the observatory hill fell away in white terraces, and beyond it the whole city lay under a thin skin of snow and gaslight, ten thousand windows, ten thousand lives, none of them aware that the next sixty seconds contained theirs.

"I wound her this morning," Finch said softly. "She hasn't missed a midnight in forty years. Don't be gentle with her. She hates that."

The final seconds gathered. Wren put her hand on the great wheel, feeling the energy coiled in it, a year of falling weight ready to spend itself in twelve strokes — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what she was about to do and what it would cost and what it would make.

"She won't be gentle either," Wren said. "Neither am I."

Midnight came.

The first chime went through the floor and up through her boots and into her teeth. The second. The third, and somewhere below, faint as memory, the city began to answer — church bells, ship horns on the river, a foghorn with a cold. Fifth. Sixth. Each stroke, she felt the mechanism press the letters in their cradle, click, click, confirming, witnessing: *they are here, they are here, all of them are here.*

Eighth. Ninth. Tenth.

On the eleventh, she moved.

Not to stop the clock — not yet, not quite. On the eleventh chime, with the twelfth already gathering in the striking train, she slipped the detent. Just so. Half a tooth of delay. The smallest intervention of her life, measured in fractions of a second, and it stretched the pause between the eleventh stroke and the twelfth into something long and silver and suspended — a moment hung on a hook outside of time, the legal instant frozen at its very hinge while the world waited for the last blow to fall.

In that stretched moment — three seconds, five, seven, the city below gone strangely quiet, even the bells seeming to lean forward — Wren Brightwater did not inherit anything, and did not refuse anything. She simply stood in the gap between two notes of a bell and held the door of the century open.

Then she released the detent, and the twelfth chime fell like an axe the other way, like a gavel, like a door closing gently on one hundred years and opening onto another — and the mechanism clicked twice more against the cradle, confirming, confirming, and it was done.

The trust had transferred at the stroke of midnight. To five names. Equally. Witnessed by the clock, by Finch, and by the girl who kept it.

Silence, afterward. Real silence, the kind a clockroom never keeps. Snow ticked against the glass. Finch was laughing quietly, helplessly, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand.

"Thirty years," he said. "Thirty years I guarded that letter thinking it was a bomb. And it turns out it was a door."

"It was both," Wren said. "Most things are." Her legs abruptly informed her they were done pretending, and she sat down on the floor of the clock room, in the oil and the dust and the settling cold, and laughed too — a shaky, undignified, entirely happy laugh, the kind you could only laugh when the worst thing had already happened and hadn't, after all, happened at all.

Far below, muffled by stone and snow, she heard the workshop door bang open and Marisol's voice rise joyfully over the hill — shouting something about midnight soup and if anyone froze to death on her doorstep she'd never forgive them — and Ezra's deep laugh, and Tobias's thinner one, and underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, Dominic coughing and then calling her name up into the dark, once, the way you call a child in from the weather.

Wren got up. She checked the escapement, oiled the bearing Finch always forgot, and left the clock running true.

At the ladder she paused, and looked back once at the case — at the two letters lying side by side in their brass cradle, old and new, the past and the amendment, keeping company in the dark the way families do.

Then she climbed down toward the light and the shouting and the soup, already composing in her head the sentence she would say to Edmund Harrow if he ever worked up the nerve to come in person:

*You can't contest five hands holding on to each other. You can only wish them happy.*

She was halfway down the tower when she heard, rising from the streets below, the first sounds of the new century — and among them, unmistakably, horses. Many horses, moving fast up the observatory road, with a carriage behind them, and a voice she knew.
