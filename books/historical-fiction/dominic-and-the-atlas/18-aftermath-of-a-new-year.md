## 18. Aftermath of a New Year

The trouble with winning, Tobias Pell had learned by the second of January, was that nobody let you rest afterwards. Victory, it turned out, arrived the way debt collectors did: loudly, in numbers, and demanding to be received in the front room.

He counted three of them at breakfast — lawyers, not collectors, though he could no longer always tell the difference in his dreams. They sat around Marisol's table drinking her coffee and eating her seed cake as if they'd paid for either, and they spoke of the Brightwater trust the way men speak of weather that has inconvenienced them personally.

"The amendment is irregular," said the tall one, whose name was Bramwell and whose collar seemed to be attempting to strangle him on his client's behalf. "Five equal signatures. There is no precedent."

"There is now," said Wren, who had her hands wrapped around her own cup and an expression Tobias recognized from the workshop bench: the look she wore when a mechanism had defeated her twice and she intended it not to defeat her a third time. "I filed it before midnight, witnessed, sealed, and inside the clock itself. You may contest the law, Mr. Bramwell, but you'll have to contest the clock first, and I've seen what happens to people who try."

Bramwell opened his mouth, considered, and closed it again. Tobias, watching from his usual corner with his ledger — the new ledger, the one with pages that had never been sold to anyone — made a small mark in the margin. He kept notes now on everything: every visitor, every challenge, every letter with a Harrow crest. If his past had taught him anything, it was that paper was where the danger lived, and so paper was where he would stand guard.

* * *

By the fourth of January the household had developed routines for siege. Marisol cooked as though feeding an army expecting to be starved out; Ezra took to walking the perimeter of the property each evening with a lantern and, Tobias suspected, nothing more dangerous than a marlinspike and a great deal of confidence. Dominic came down to the parlour for an hour a day, wrapped in two blankets, and received visitors like a king in winter quarters — courteous, faintly amused, and utterly immovable.

Tobias kept the accounts, answered the letters, and waited for the axe.

It was a reasonable expectation. He had done the arithmetic years ago, alone, in the dark of his narrow room above the workshop: someday the debts would surface, or the men he'd sold information to in those desperate first months — names, ledgers, the whereabouts of a shipping manifest or two — would come calling, wearing better coats this time. And when they did, they would not come for him alone. They would come through him, the weak seam, the patched hull, and everyone he loved would take water.

He simply hadn't expected them to arrive by way of a legal challenge.

The letter came on a grey Tuesday, delivered by a clerk with apologetic eyebrows. Tobias read it standing in the hall, hat still in his hands, and read it again, because the words refused to hold still.

*In re the Vale Trust:* the solicitors for certain interested parties wished to establish whether all five signatures on the amended instrument were given freely and without coercion, and in particular wished to examine the history of one Tobias Pell, formerly of Fennick & Sons, whose known associations with creditors of record might constitute grounds for challenge to his capacity and good faith.

They were going to use him. Not even against the family directly — worse than that. They were going to use him as proof that the whole thing was rotten, that a trust built on clerks and cooks and sailors and clockmakers was no trust at all, and his name was the crowbar they meant to insert.

He folded the letter with great care, which was what he did when he wanted very much to tear something, and went upstairs to pack.

* * *

He got as far as the second shirt before Marisol knocked — which meant, in the grammar of that household, that she had seen him pass the kitchen door with a valise and had given him exactly four minutes to lose his nerve before intervening.

"You're packing," she said from the doorway. She did not enter. Marisol never entered a room she intended to lay siege to; she held the ground outside it.

"I'm tidying," Tobias said. "Aggressively. With luggage."

"Tobias."

"Marisol." He set the shirt down. His hands were not entirely steady, and he resented them for it. "You've seen the letter. Or you will. They're going to drag Fennick's through whatever court will have it, and my name attached, and when the dust settles the Harrows won't care about me — I'm a means to an end, they'll discard me like anyone else — but you'll all have spent a year and a fortune defending a man who sold your secrets once already." He made himself look at her. It was harder than arithmetic. "I can spare you that. It's the one sum I can actually pay."

For a long moment Marisol said nothing, and Tobias braced for agreement, because agreement was what he deserved and the world had rarely shortchanged him on that particular commodity.

Instead she said, "Sit down."

"Marisol—"

"That's not a request, clerk." But there was no iron in it; there was something worse, something gentle, and it undid him more thoroughly than shouting would have. He sat on the edge of the bed. The valise gaped between them like an accusation.

"Do you know," she said, leaning on the doorframe, "what I thought the first month you were here? That you would leave. Not dramatically — you're incapable of drama, don't argue — but quietly, some night, the way you arrived. I thought it because you flinched every time someone laughed too near you, and you counted things. Spoons, coins, days. People who count like that are people who expect to be taken from."

"I count because counting is safe," Tobias admitted. "Numbers don't turn on you."

"And yet," said Marisol, "you stayed. Nine years. So here is what I know that you seem to have missed, you ridiculous man: we are not being *defended*. We are being *challenged*, and challenges are answered, and we answer them together, because that is the entire point of the document Wren put inside that clock. Five signatures. Equal. Yours is one of them, and it isn't worth less because your ledger has some ugly pages. Every ledger does. Mine has pages you'll never see."

Tobias looked at his hands. "Mine can hurt you."

"So can Ezra's temper, and Wren's stubbornness, and Dominic's secrets, and my tongue. We are a household of weapons, Tobias. That's why we keep each other." She straightened. "Supper is at seven. Bring yourself. Leave the valise; I'll find a use for the shirts."

She left. Tobias sat a while longer in the cold little room, listening to the sounds of the house below — pans, laughter, Ezra singing something nautical and mostly wrong — and understood, slowly, the way he understood most things, by checking the figures twice, that he had offered to pay a debt that no longer existed, to people who had never once presented the bill.

He unpacked the shirts.

* * *

But the letter did not go away, and neither did the solicitors, and by the following week the challenge had a date attached: a hearing, in February, before a man whose reputation for strictness was exceeded only by his reputation for naps. The household met it in the manner they now met everything — around the supper table, under Marisol's lamps, passing dishes and arguments in equal measure.

"They'll call me as a witness," Tobias said, pushing potatoes around his plate. "Against us. They'll ask me to confirm my own bad character, and I'll have to, under oath, because lying is the one debt I've never taken on since."

"Then you'll confirm it," said Dominic, from his chair at the head of the table. His voice was thin these days but precise, a clock that had been wound carefully its whole life. "And then you'll tell them what you did after. Nine years of honest books. A man who mends is worth ten who never tore."

"That's not how courts work, sir."

"No," Dominic agreed pleasantly. "But it is how juries work, and how judges work, when they are awake. And it is how *families* work, which is the jurisdiction that concerns me."

Ezra, who had been uncharacteristically quiet, set down his fork. "There's another thing," he said. "Been thinking on it since the estuary. The *Atlas*'s register — the one Wren pulled off her — it lists original crew. Nineteen names. One of them's mine." He paused, and Tobias saw the old sailor choosing his words the way he chose his footing on wet deck. "Another one of them is a fellow called Quill. Mate back then. These days, I hear, he keeps books for the Harrow offices."

The table went still.

"He'd know the old ownership papers," Ezra went on. "Know them well enough to forge around them. If they mean to challenge the trust, they'll want a man who can swear the *Atlas* was never really ours — and Quill's the sort who'd swear the sea was dry for the right wage."

"Then we get to the register first," said Wren.

"It *is* ours," Tobias said, before he could think. "We hold it. Wren carried it off the ship herself."

"We hold the copy we found," Wren corrected gently. "Not the only one. Registers were filed with the port authority, Tobias. In triplicate." She looked at him across the table, and something in her expression — apology mixed with request — told him precisely what was coming before she said it. "And the port authority keeps records through clerks. Good ones. Trusted ones. Ones with references."

Everyone was looking at him now. Tobias felt the familiar vertigo, the old sensation of standing on a ledge over his own past — except that this time, when he looked down, there was no darkness waiting. There was a table full of people who had not let him pack.

"You want me to go to the port authority," he said slowly, "in my professional capacity. Where the Harrows' solicitors will certainly see me. Where they will say, *there goes the compromised witness, tampering with records.*"

"I want you," said Wren, "to do the thing you're best at in the world, in plain sight, because hiding is what got us here. Bramwell expects a frightened man. Let's introduce him to our clerk instead."

Marisol passed him the bread, which in that household constituted a formal motion, seconded by all present.

Tobias took a piece. His hands were steady after all.

"All right," he said. "But I want it noted in the minutes that I objected, thoroughly, and was overruled by dinner rolls."

"Noted," said Dominic, smiling faintly. "Minuted. Filed."

* * *

He went to the port authority the next morning, ledger under his arm and his heart somewhere in his throat, and discovered two things before noon. The first was that he was, in fact, very good — that nine years of honest work had made him fluent in a language his younger self had only spoken badly, and that the registrar's own clerks greeted him almost as a colleague. The second was that the triplicate file for the *Atlas*, year of original registration, had been requested and withdrawn eleven days earlier by a firm of solicitors whose letterhead Tobias now knew better than his own handwriting.

Someone had reached the records first.

He stood in the marble corridor of the port authority with the empty space on the shelf where the file should have been, and felt the old fear rise — and then, remarkably, felt it settle, the way a compass settles when you stop walking in circles. Because the Harrows taking the file was not the trap closing. It was information. It told them where the blow would fall, and blows foreseen were blows a body could brace for.

He wrote it all down, carefully, in the new ledger, and walked out through the front doors with his head up.

At the top of the steps, a carriage waited that he did not recognize — and did, at the same time, recognize completely, because it was exactly the kind of carriage a man like Edmund Harrow would ride in: expensive without warmth, polished without light. The window lowered an inch.

"Mr. Pell," said a voice from within, unhurried and pleasant. "A word, before your hearing. About what happened at Fennick & Sons — and about what *didn't* happen. I think you'll find I know rather more of the story than you do."

The door of the carriage swung open.
