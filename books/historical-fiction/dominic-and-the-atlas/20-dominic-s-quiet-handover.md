## 20. Dominic's Quiet Handover

The first frost of January came on a Tuesday, and with it came Dominic Vale down the workshop stairs without his cane.

Wren noticed the absence before she noticed the man — she had spent eleven years learning to read the small machinery of him, the way a clockmaker reads an escapement, and the cane had been part of the mechanism since the autumn. He descended slowly, one hand flat against the wall where generations of palms had worn the plaster smooth, and he was dressed not in his work apron but in the good grey coat he saved for lawyers and funerals. Wren's stomach performed a small, cold maneuver at the sight of it.

"You're not dying," she said. It came out as an accusation.

"Not today," Dominic agreed. "I checked."

He crossed to his bench — *his* bench, the long one by the north window where the light came in clean and unbroken from half past eight until noon — and ran a finger along its edge as though taking leave of an old colleague. On the bench sat a wooden box Wren had never seen before. It was made of walnut, small enough to hold with two hands, and it had no lock.

"Sit down, Wren."

"There's the Hollis regulator to finish, and Marisol wants the kitchen clock before—"

"Sit down anyway. The Hollis regulator has waited three weeks for you already; it can wait twenty minutes more. That is one of the great discoveries of middle age: nearly everything can wait."

She sat on the apprentice stool, which she had outgrown in height but never in habit, and folded her hands in her lap like a girl about to be scolded. Dominic lifted the box, considered it, and set it in front of her.

"Open it."

Inside, on a bed of flannel gone soft and grey with age, lay the workshop keys.

There were seven of them. Wren knew each one the way she knew her own teeth. The great iron key for the street door, its bow stamped with the Brightwater mark that predated all of them. The key to the stock room, small and brass and slightly bent, so that it only turned if you lifted the door by its handle first — Tobias had complained about it for nine years rather than fix it, and now even his complaints were part of the furniture. The key to the safe that no longer held a fortune but still held the deeds. The key to the cabinet of customer pieces, entrusted only to journeymen. Two keys whose locks Dominic had removed years ago but which he had kept because, as he put it, a keyring should have some mysteries left in it. And last, hanging apart from the others on its own loop of cord, the smallest key of all — the one to Dominic's private drawer, the drawer from which he took his tea, his correspondence, and once, memorably, a pistol he had never fired at a man he had never named.

Wren did not touch them.

"Take them out," Dominic said. "They weigh almost nothing. That is the point of keys; they are the lightest things in the world and the hardest to carry."

"Dominic." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "You're seventy-three. You recovered from a collapse in December. You are allowed to retire. You are allowed to hand things over. But you don't get to do it like this, all at once, in the good coat, as if—" She stopped, because she heard herself, and what she heard was a girl listening for the sound of being sent away.

"As if I'm clearing my accounts," Dominic finished gently. "No. Sit. Let me tell you a thing properly, since I appear to be doing everything properly today. God willing it doesn't become a habit."

He lowered himself onto the stool across from her, and for a moment the workshop was very quiet — just the tick of the six clocks that were always kept running, out of phase with one another, so that the room breathed in layers. Wren had grown up thinking every house sounded like this. It had been one of the shocks of her life, at age fourteen, to learn otherwise.

"Do you remember the night you came?" Dominic asked.

"I remember the rain."

"I remember more than the rain." He smiled, and the smile did something complicated at one corner, the way it did when he was about to confess to something. "I remember a girl of ten standing in my doorway holding a biscuit tin against her chest like it contained the crown jewels, refusing to let go of it, refusing to come in until I assured her — in writing, if necessary — that the tin would not be confiscated. Do you know what was in the tin?"

"My tools," Wren said. "What was left of them."

"Eleven files, a pair of pliers with one grip, and a screwdriver worn down to a nub. Worth perhaps fourpence altogether. And you stood in the doorway prepared to defend fourpence worth of broken tools against a grown man, because they were yours, because they were what you had, because—" he tapped the box "—because you measured the world in what could be carried. That is why I chose you first, Wren. Before Ezra jumped ship into my harbor. Before Tobias turned up owing everyone money and apologizing for the weather. Before Marisol answered my advertisement with a letter so insolent I hired her on the spot. You were the first. I saw a child who understood exactly what things cost and exactly what they were worth, and understood — this is the rare part — that those are different sums."

"Dominic—"

"And then," he went on, merciless, in the tone of a man finishing a proof, "you stayed. That is the part I want written down somewhere. Choosing is common. Staying is the whole art. You could have carried that tin out the door any night for eleven years, and some nights, I know, you thought about it. Instead you unpacked it. You put your fourpence of tools on a bench in my shop and you built a life around them, gear by gear, until the tin was empty and the house was full." He closed the lid over the keys and pushed it an inch toward her. "So take them. Not because I am done — I intend to be an intolerable presence on that staircase for another decade at least, haunting your customers and correcting your filings — but because a household should never have only one person who knows how all the doors open. We learned that in December, didn't we? We learned it the hard way, at the top of a tower, in the wind."

Wren looked at the keys. Eleven years ago she had stood in a doorway clutching a biscuit tin, and the man across from her had knelt down to her height and said, *Nothing you carry will ever be taken from you here.* She had assumed at the time that it was the sort of thing adults said. It had turned out to be the only promise anyone had ever made her that was kept in full.

Her eyes were doing something embarrassing. She blinked it back down.

"If I take these," she said, "you're taking wages."

"I am taking *fewer* wages, and only for the quiet hours, and Marisol has already agreed to enforce it, and you have seen Marisol enforce things."

"That's a conspiracy."

"It's a household," Dominic said. "Same thing, better organized."

Wren reached into the box. She took out the seven keys first and threaded them onto her own ring, where they clicked against her loupe-case and her small punch set, settling in among her tools as though they had always meant to be there. Then she lifted the seventh key, the little one on its cord, and held it up.

"This one I'll wear," she said. "The drawer key. If I'm keeping your secrets now, I might as well keep them where I can find them."

Dominic laughed — a real laugh, from the chest, the one Marisol said could be heard through two floors and used to frighten the cats — and Wren realized she had not heard it since before December. Some knot behind her sternum, wound tight for weeks, gave a quarter turn and loosened.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"The private drawer gets cleaned out. Today. Whatever's in there — old letters, black wax, unpaid debts, love notes, pistol ammunition — I want it inventoried while you watch. No more locked rooms in this house. We're done with sealed documents in this family. We keep our papers where the whole household can read them, or we don't keep them at all."

For a moment something moved behind Dominic's eyes — the old instinct to hold a card close, thirty years deep — and then, deliberately, consciously, he let it go. She watched him let it go. It was, she thought, the most difficult piece of craftsmanship she would see that day.

"Agreed," he said. "Though I warn you, half of it is receipts from 1881 and the other half is letters from a woman in Lisbon whom Marisol already knows all about."

"Marisol knows *everything*. That's why the household runs."

* * *

The inventory of the drawer took until midafternoon and produced, as promised, forty-one receipts, twelve letters smelling faintly of salt and orange blossom, one unfinished letter to a Lisbon address that Wren pretended very hard not to read upside down, a spare mainspring, and a photograph she had never seen.

It showed a ship. A three-masted barque at anchor, her rails crowded with crew, and standing at the foot of the mainmast, thirty years younger and already going grey at the temples, Dominic Vale — beside a younger Ezra, both laughing at something off-camera, one of Ezra's enormous hands gripping Dominic's shoulder like a man holding down a hatch in weather.

Wren set it on the bench between them and looked at it a long time.

"You're going to visit her," Dominic said. Not a question.

"At spring tide. Ezra's got the refit list down to a science and Tobias has found cargo — honest cargo, tea and machine parts, nothing that requires a false name in any port. She sails under five names now, that ship. Registered to all of us." Wren touched the edge of the photograph. "Come with us. Just to the estuary. She won't sail without seeing you, and neither will he."

Dominic picked up the photograph and looked at it, and Wren watched thirty years move across his face like weather — grief and guilt and something older than either, which she finally recognized as longing, plain and undecorated.

"In April," he said. "When the ice is off the roads. I'll stand on the quay and criticize his rigging, and he'll call me an old woman, and we shall both be perfectly happy." He slid the photograph across the bench to her. "Keep this. It belongs on your wall, next to the register."

And that was when the bell over the street door rang, and boots came down the stairs two at a time, and Ezra's head appeared around the doorframe followed by the smell of cold river air.

"Wren — sorry, Dom, need to steal her — there's a man at the door from the *Shipping Gazette*, wanting words about the Atlas, and Tobias is trying to answer him with lawyer words, which means inside a quarter hour we'll have promised the man an exclusive, an apology, and our firstborn. Thought you'd want to be the one who talks to pressmen. Being as you're the clockmaker of record and all."

Wren looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at the keys on her belt, and his eyes crinkled.

"You heard the man," he said. "Clockmaker of record. Go and be recorded."

She was halfway up the stairs, the drawer key swinging cold against her collarbone, before she realized she was smiling — and that above her, faint through the floorboards, she could hear Dominic humming to himself as he swept out the old drawer for good.
