## 21. What Can Be Carried

The observatory clock had been lying to the heavens for a year, and Wren had come to make it tell the truth.

That was how she thought of it now, in her own head, though she'd never say it aloud to Finch, who took his timekeeping with the solemnity of a priest taking confession. The great regulator at the heart of the tower — the one whose chimes had marked the transfer of an empire's worth of patents and shares and one stubborn ship — had been running since that midnight on the last day of 1899. Running well enough. Running honestly enough for lawyers.

But not properly. Not the way it deserved.

Wren stood before the open case with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her tools laid out on a square of flannel, as Dominic had taught her before she could reach the workbench without a stool. The January light came through the tower windows in pale slabs, full of drifting dust, and the pendulum swung its slow metronome behind her like a patient heartbeat.

The misfiled gear was long gone — she had found it a year and a lifetime ago, its teeth cut a half-degree wrong so that the train bound and slipped, bound and slipped, wearing itself toward ruin with every hour. Harrow's sabotage, elegant and mean. She had replaced it then with whatever the tower's stores offered, a serviceable piece, nothing more. A splint on a broken leg.

Today she had brought the bone.

It sat on the flannel beside her pliers: a wheel she had cut herself over eleven evenings in the workshop, brass from the same stock Dominic used for his finest pieces, each tooth filed and polished under the loupe until it caught the light like a row of tiny suns. Her hands had known what to do even when her mind was elsewhere — which was, she had learned, the whole secret of craft, and possibly of family too. The hands remember what you choose. They keep working on it while you sleep.

"Tea," Finch announced from the doorway, in the tone of a man who considered tea less a beverage than a legal requirement. He carried the tray himself, which still startled her; a Keeper of the Royal Observatory, hauling refreshment for an apprentice clockmaker half his age. But Finch had spent thirty years deferring to no one and one year making up for it, and he did everything now with a kind of careful penance, as if good manners could balance old ledgers. Perhaps they could. She had seen stranger arithmetic.

"Thank you." She cleared a space among the oil bottles. "How is she running?"

"Fast," said Finch, with disgust. "Two seconds a week."

"Two seconds a week is within tolerance."

"Two seconds a week," Finch repeated, "is a lie told slowly." He poured. "You sound like him, you know."

"Like Dominic?"

"Like your master." He handed her a cup and looked into the clock case with the expression of a man regarding an old friend who had once disappointed him. "I told you once I trusted no vault. I'll tell you the rest of it now, since we're both of us too old to pretend otherwise: I didn't hide those documents in this clock only because vaults can be bought. I hid them here because I knew someone would have to come and repair her someday. Clocks are honest things. They demand attention. Sooner or later, whoever tends them must open the case." He sipped. "I gambled that the person who opened it would be worth trusting."

"And if it hadn't been me? If it had been some Harrow man with a wrench?"

"Then I should have had to become considerably braver than I am," said Finch placidly, "and I am grateful daily that it never came to that."

Wren laughed and set down her cup. The truth was she had missed this room — missed it the way you miss a place where something enormous happened to you, which is to say with a complicated ache, half grief and half gratitude. A year ago she had climbed these stairs with a letter burning in her pocket like a coal, believing her whole life was borrowed furniture in someone else's house. A year ago she had stopped a clock at midnight and started history instead.

Now the tower was just a tower, and the clock was just a clock, and the work was just work, and there was no feeling in the world she loved more.

"Right," she said. "Hold the weights."

They worked through the morning. She lifted out the replacement wheel — the splint — and set the new one on its arbor, seating it with a click so soft she felt it more than heard it, the sound of a mechanism accepting its own missing part home. Then the escapement, cleaned and jeweled. Then the pallets, dressed by hand. Finch held lamps and passed tools and asked questions he mostly knew the answers to, because asking questions was how Finch showed affection, and by noon the great clock's tick had changed pitch entirely — deeper, rounder, the tick of a thing with nothing left to prove.

"There," Wren murmured. "Now you're telling the truth."

She reached for her punch and graver to mark the repair, and paused.

Inside the case, low on the left-hand frame where a conscientious maker signs his work, were two sets of initials already. F.A.F., struck neat and shallow in 1869, the year Finch and Dominic had hidden the world inside this machine. And below it, older, bolder, worn nearly smooth by a century of dust and polish: D.V.

Dominic Vale. She traced it with one fingertip. Somewhere south of the city, an old man was napping in his chair after lunch and pretending he wasn't pleased when she woke him with tea, and his initials had been waiting in this brass for thirty years for someone to find them.

Wren set her graver against the metal, beneath the others, and carved W.B. in small, clean letters. Then, because she was her father's daughter in every way except the ones that involved paperwork, she added a date, and then — after a moment's argument with herself that she lost — a single small gear stamped beside the initials, four teeth, perfectly round.

Finch peered over her shoulder. "A flower?"

"A gear."

"Ah." He considered it. "In the dark they look much the same."

"That's the idea, sir. Everything looks like a flower if you're hopeful enough."

Finch made the small huffing sound that served him for laughter, and together they closed the case, and the clock began the long patient business of being right.

* * *

Packing, Wren had decided, was a moral discipline.

This was not a conclusion she had come to easily. There had been a time — she could admit it now, alone in her room with the trunk open on the bed — when she had measured her whole life in small portable things precisely because she expected to be sent away from it. Tools first, always. A change of clothes rolled tight. Letters she couldn't part with, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. One bag, packed in ten minutes, ready by the door for whenever belonging ran out. She had been doing it since she was eight years old, since the night Dominic found her on the workshop step measuring the cold in shivers and the street in paving stones.

The bag had lived under her bed for nine years. Even last winter, through the whole affair of the letter and the ledger and the men in Harrow's livery, it had stayed there, stuffed and waiting, like a coward's parachute.

Yesterday she had taken it out and emptied it onto the bed, and discovered what nine years of readiness actually amounted to: three screwdrivers, a spare apron, a hairbrush, forty-one letters, and a biscuit tin containing — inexplicably — a single thimble and half a crown. She had sat on the floor and laughed until Marisol put her head round the door to ask if she was quite well.

Because here was the thing the empty bag had shown her: none of it was home. It was only evidence of home. And she had been carrying evidence all those years the way a thief carries stolen goods, ready to run, keeping the proof close so that no one could take from her the fact that she had once been loved somewhere.

She was done carrying evidence.

What went into the trunk now, on this bright Saturday in early spring, was a different inventory entirely. Tools, yes — but chosen for the job ahead rather than hoarded against flight. Her best loupe. The new oil bottle with the stopper that didn't leak, Tobias's birthday gift to her, purchased with his first wages from the shipping office where he now kept accounts so scrupulous that the owner had twice checked them for jokes. Letters, yes — but only six of them, the ones she meant to reread on the crossing, including the latest from Ezra aboard the Atlas, written in a hand like rigging knots and describing the Bay of Biscay in terms that suggested he wanted to fight it personally.

And at the bottom of the trunk, wrapped in the flannel she'd carried under her bed for nine years and never once needed: nothing. Empty space. Room, if she wanted it, for shells or stones or a rope knot or anything else the estuary might offer up. Or room for nothing at all. Both possibilities pleased her equally.

"You'll want the heavy coat," Marisol called up the stairs, in the voice she used for facts. "The wind off the water in March comes straight through wool like it's gossiping about you."

"I have a coat."

"You have a *coat*. You haven't got *the* coat." Marisol appeared in the doorway holding a bundle the size of a small sheep, smelling of camphor and kitchen smoke, and deposited it on the bed without negotiation. "Ezra says the Atlas makes port Tuesday and sails Friday with the tide, and you're to be aboard for the trip up the coast, and if you catch your death of cold he will bring you back to me frozen solid and I will not be blamed for it."

"Marisol, it's three days."

"It's March on the North Sea," said Marisol, which ended the discussion. She surveyed the trunk, the folded shirts, the tidy rows of tools, with the critical eye of a woman who judged households the way other people judged horses — by teeth and temperament. "Well. You pack like a person going somewhere, anyway."

"Was there another way I packed?"

"Like a person leaving somewhere," said Marisol. "There's a difference. Took me twenty years and a dead husband to learn it, so don't go smug about it." She softened then, the way she did, all at once and against her will, and pressed something small into Wren's hand. A parcel, waxed paper, warm from the oven. "For the voyage. Don't eat them all on the pier like a seagull."

At the door, pausing, Marisol looked back. Her eyes went around the little room — the workbench under the window, the shelf of clocks Wren had mended and kept ticking, the nail by the door where a packed bag had hung for nine years and now hung nothing at all.

"You're coming back Thursday week," Marisol said. Not quite a question.

"I'm coming back Thursday week," Wren agreed. "Someone has to mind the shop."

"Mind you don't mind it too well. Dominic's taken to rearranging the drawers when you're gone. Says he's looking for things. He knows exactly where things are. He just likes the drawers complaining at him." Marisol sniffed, blinked hard at the window, and departed downstairs to the kitchen, where love was best disguised as bread.

Wren closed the trunk and buckled it. Light. Everything she owned of consequence fit in one trunk and one coat pocket, and the pocket held only a key.

The workshop key. Dominic had given it to her in February, formally, with Tobias as witness and Marisol pretending not to watch through the kitchen door, and he had made a small speech about steadiness and trust that he abandoned halfway through because his voice betrayed him, and finished by shoving the key across the bench and saying gruffly, "Don't let anyone file the wrong gears." She wore it on a cord now, next to her skin, where it had warmed itself into the ordinary temperature of her body. That was the strange discovery of this past year: the things that mattered weighed nothing. A key. Six letters. A name carved in brass on a hilltop. Home, she understood now, was not the emergency bundle under the bed. Home was the place that repaired you when you ran down, that opened your case and seated the missing wheel and closed you up again ticking true. You couldn't carry it. You could only keep it wound, and be kept.

She took up the trunk, shrugged into the terrible heavy coat, and went down through the house that smelled of bread and lamp oil and solder — Tobias's boots in the hall, Ezra's pipe on the sill, Dominic humming in the back room over a movement he'd been "just finishing" since November — and out the front door into the bright loud spring street.

The estuary lay east, past the docks and the salt-smell and the gulls conducting their eternal grievances. Down at the quay the Atlas waited, refitted and renamed in paint no prouder than any working cargo vessel, which was exactly as proud as Ezra wanted her. His letter had ended, in letters large enough to be read across a table:

*Fair winds Thursday. Bring nothing you can't set down.*

Wren walked east with the trunk bumping her knee and the key warm at her throat, and she was smiling before she knew it, because she had finally understood the instruction. She was bringing almost nothing at all.

Which was, of course, everything.

From the end of the pier, she could see the Atlas at her moorings — and something else besides: a small figure at her rail, waving both arms like a man signaling a rescue, and beside the gangway a second figure in a good coat who had absolutely no business being out of the city, leaning on a cane and trying to look as though climbing a pier were a thing he did daily.

"Dominic?" she called, when she was near enough. "What on earth — you're supposed to be resting—"

"I am rested," said Dominic Vale, with the dignity of a man who had walked to the end of one pier and intended to be praised for crossing an ocean. "I've come to see you off. Also—" he produced an envelope from his coat, sealed plainly in brown wax, and held it out with a hand that was not entirely steady, "—there's a berth opening in the crew list. Joint ownership means joint decisions, and the household voted last night while you were carving your name into Finch's clock. We require a keeper of time aboard, and we are informed you're available."

Wren took the envelope. Inside was a ship's article, a working agreement, five signatures already at the bottom — and a blank line waiting, drawn neat and true, exactly wide enough for hers.
