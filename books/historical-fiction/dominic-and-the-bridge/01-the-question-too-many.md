## 1. The Question Too Many

The census man arrived on a Sunday, which was the first wrong thing about him.

Enumerators came on Mondays. Everyone knew that — the whole machinery of the census turned on the Monday night, the forms filled in by the head of household before midnight, the enumerator collecting them Tuesday like a milkman collecting bottles. But this one came to Croft House on Sunday afternoon, while the household was at church except for me and the cook, and he stood on the step with his leather satchel and his rain-spotted hat and said he'd been asked to take particulars early, there being a difficulty with the schedule in this district.

I had been in service long enough to know that difficulties with schedules were usually difficulties of someone's invention.

"You'll be the housekeeper, then," he said. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with a way of looking at you that was not rude exactly but was not the look a man gives a servant either. It was the look a man gives a ledger entry he means to audit.

"Under-housekeeper," I said. "Mrs. Prewitt keeps house."

"Then I'll want her particulars too. And the driver's." He opened his satchel and took out a printed schedule, smoothing it against the doorframe as though my doorstep were a desk. "Name and surname. Relation to head of household. Age last birthday. Occupation. Birthplace." His pencil hovered. "And for married women, the number of years married, children living, children died."

"Mrs. Prewitt will give you all that herself."

"I'm sure she will." He smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "But I'll start with you, if you don't mind. Name?"

"Mirabel Finch."

The lie came out smooth as poured cream. I had used it for six years. Elias Finch was no relation of mine; I had taken the name off a gravestone in a Berkshire churchyard when I was nineteen years old and needed to become nobody. A name off a stone belongs to no one living, and no one living comes asking after it.

"Mirabel Finch," he repeated, writing. "Age?"

"Twenty-two."

"Occupation?"

"Domestic servant."

"Birthplace?"

"Berkshire."

He wrote that too, and then he looked up, and the pencil stopped moving, and something in the quality of his stillness made the skin go cold along my arms.

"And your mother's maiden name?"

I had filled in three census schedules before this one — 1891 as an infant in my mother's keeping, 1901 in the schoolroom of a family whose name I have made a point of forgetting. No enumerator had ever asked me my mother's maiden name. It is not a question the form asks of servants. The form asks what the head of household requires it to ask, and heads of household do not require their under-housekeepers' mothers' maiden names, because servants do not have lineages. Servants have references.

"That's not on the schedule," I said.

"It's an addition." He tapped the margin, where indeed some fresh column had been ruled in, in different ink from the rest of the printing. "New instructions this year. For verification purposes."

"Verification of what?"

"Of particulars." He said it mildly, reasonably, the way a man says something he has said many times and does not intend to vary. "It's a simple question, miss. Your mother's maiden name."

Miss. Not a slip. Servants are not miss.

Through the open door behind him I could see the square, the plane trees just coming into leaf, a woman crossing with a pram, the ordinary Sunday world going about its business as though nothing were happening on this step but a census. Behind me, in the hall, the clock over the coat stand ticked with the particular loudness clocks achieve when one is trying to think.

My mother's name. Eleanor Ravensworth. Eleanor Ravensworth, who had been dead eleven years, who had died in a place I had never been allowed to visit, whose grave I had found only because Mrs. Tallow wept the location out of herself one afternoon over tea. Eleanor Ravensworth, whose name I had not spoken aloud in six years, because names are things that can be traced, and I had built my life out of untraceable materials.

"Eleanor," I said. "Eleanor Smith."

He wrote it. I watched him write it, and I watched the way he wrote it — unhurried, neat, a clerk's hand, taking down the answer without any visible opinion of it. And yet I felt, with a certainty I could not have argued for in a court or even to Adeline over dinner, that he knew it was false. That he had known it would be false before he climbed our steps. That the question was not a question at all but a test, and I had just failed it, and the failure was being recorded in that neat clerk's hand in triplicate somewhere.

"Father's occupation?" he asked.

"Gardener."

"Dead?"

"Both my parents are dead."

"Any brothers or sisters?"

"One brother. Dead also." This was true, or true enough; the brother had been born dead between me and the child after me who never came, and my mother had grieved for it in ways I understood only now, at twenty-two, in retrospect, standing on a doorstep lying to a government official.

He closed the schedule. "That'll be all for today. Mrs. Prewitt can expect me tomorrow for the household particulars." He put on his hat. And then, at the bottom of the steps, he turned around, and he said, in a tone of perfect civility:

"Ravensworth is a Hampshire name, isn't it? Old family. Big estates."

The square went very quiet. Or I went very quiet inside it; I cannot say which.

"I wouldn't know," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I suppose you wouldn't." And he touched his hat brim and walked away down the street, unhurried, a grey man with a satchel, indistinguishable from a hundred other grey men with satchels, and I stood in the doorway of Croft House with my heart going like a trapped bird and the taste of my own lie sitting in my mouth like a coin.

* * *

Adeline came home at six, smelling of cold air and other people's cigarette smoke, her cheeks bright from the argument she had plainly been having with someone since lunchtime.

"The census," she announced, dropping her gloves on the hall table, "is an outrage, and I intend to make it one publicly. They've added questions designed to count women as furniture. Married women aren't persons under this schedule, Mirabel, they're appendages — years married, children borne, as though a woman were a mare in a stud book." She pulled off her hat and shook out her hair, forty-one years old and entirely unaware of how much like a girl she looked doing it. "Well. Let them count us. We shall make ourselves so very countable they choke on the figures."

"Yes, ma'am."

She paused. She had that trick of pausing that made you feel the pause was your fault. "You're pale. Has Mrs. Prewitt worked you to death already?"

"The census man came early. I gave him my particulars."

"Ah." She hung her hat herself, which she always did, refusing on principle to let a servant perform small offices for her — one of the hundred small hypocrisies of Adeline Croft, who paid wages and called it sisterhood. "Did he trouble you?"

"He asked after my mother's maiden name."

Something crossed her face. It was quick — quicker than a blink, quicker than thought, gone before it could be named — and if I had not spent six years learning to read faces the way other people read newspapers, I would have missed it altogether. I did not miss it.

"What an odd question," she said.

"He said it was a new instruction."

"They're adding instructions all over the place this year. Bureaucracy breeds like rabbits." She swept past me toward the study. "Supper at eight. And Mirabel — the post came while I was out. There's a letter on the tray that goes to Mr. Gerald first thing tomorrow. Don't forget it."

Gerald. Mr. Gerald Ravensworth, of the Hampshire Ravensworths, old family, big estates, whose letters arrived at Croft House twice a week and were carried by me to the post or from the post with the regularity of tides. I carried Mr. Gerald's correspondence the way I carried everything: carefully, punctually, and after reading it.

I want to be honest here, in whatever private ledger I am keeping by writing this down, because honesty is going to matter later — I learned that year that honesty delayed is honesty denied — and so I will say it plainly: I read Adeline's letters. All of them. Had done for six years. I told myself it was devotion. I told myself that a woman driving the movement's most wanted speaker through police cordons and hostile crowds ought to know what she was carrying, in case it was a warrant or a warning or a trap. I told myself that Adeline would forgive it, that Adeline, who preached that all doors should be opened and all secrets dragged into daylight, would understand that her own chauffeuse had simply applied the principle at home.

These were lies. They were good lies, well-tailored lies, lies I wore so long they fit me like my own skin, but they were lies, and underneath them was the simpler truth: I read the letters because reading them was the only power I had. A servant is invisible. An invisible woman who reads becomes something else — a ghost with eyes. I had convinced myself that ghosts with eyes served the cause.

The letter lay on the silver tray where she'd said. Heavy paper, solicitor's fold, addressed in a hand I didn't know to Gerald Ravensworth Esquire, care of Mrs. A. Croft — which was itself odd, because Gerald's letters usually came direct to his townhouse and Adeline merely forwarded her replies. This one had come to her. Marked Private and Confidential in the corner, twice, the second time underlined.

I took it upstairs to my room, which was a proper room, with a window over the mews, because Adeline insisted her staff sleep like human beings — another of the hundred kindnesses that made leaving her unthinkable, back when leaving her was unthinkable.

I slit it with the bone folder I kept for the purpose, along the fold, so it could be resealed with paste and no one the wiser. Six years of practice. Forty seconds' work.

The paper was good stock, Chancery Lane stock. The letterhead read HALLOWAY & SON, SOLICITORS, 14 CHANCERY LANE, LONDON. The date was four days old. And the body of the letter, written in a dry precise hand that wasted not one word more than the law required, said this:

Dear Sir,

Further to our correspondence of the 3rd inst., we write to confirm receipt of your instructions. Our client's inquiries concern one Mirabel Ravensworth, born 1889, daughter of Eleanor née Ravensworth, believed to be residing in or near London under another name. You will appreciate that matters touching the validity of certain instruments executed in 1893 render it necessary to establish the present whereabouts and circumstances of the above-named person with some urgency.

We are instructed that the census now being compiled may prove of assistance in this connection, and that arrangements have been made accordingly.

We remain, sir, your obedient servants,
Halloway & Son

I read it three times. Then I sat down on my narrow bed with the letter flat in my lap and listened to my own blood moving.

Born 1889. My birth, exact to the year. Daughter of Eleanor née Ravensworth — my mother's name, her true name, the name I had buried under Eleanor Smith twelve hours earlier, set down in black ink in a stranger's hand. Believed to be residing in or near London under another name. Me. Me, located, described, hunted — hunted was the word my body chose, hunted, with the blood-drumming animal certainty of prey — and found, or nearly found, or arranged-to-be-found, through the census. Through the question too many. Through the neat clerk's hand writing *Eleanor Smith* while knowing it was a lie, because the question had never been a question; it had been a net, drawn slow across the water, and I had swum straight into it and called it a Sunday errand.

And the rest of it. *Matters touching the validity of certain instruments executed in 1893.* Instruments. Wills, settlements, deeds — the dry furniture of other people's dynasties. 1893. I was four years old in 1893. My mother was alive in 1893, and unhappy in 1893, unhappy in a way I remembered as weather rather than event, a low pressure that settled over our rooms, and my father walking the floor at night, and then the men coming, two men in dark coats, and my mother going with them because she was told she must, and my father not stopping them, and after that the weather never lifted again until the day in 1900 when Mrs. Tallow came to tell me my mother had died of congestion of the lungs in a place whose name Mrs. Tallow would not say.

Certain instruments executed in 1893. Validity. As though the thing done to my mother had paperwork. As though the weather of my childhood had been drafted by a solicitor and witnessed in duplicate.

Someone was looking for Mirabel Ravensworth. Someone had hired Halloway & Son, and Halloway & Son had written to Gerald Ravensworth, and Gerald Ravensworth's mail passed through my hands twice a week, and arrangements had been made with the census — arrangements, plural, funded, organized, the full weight of somebody's money bent toward finding a servant girl who had spent six years making herself into nobody.

I resealed the letter. Paste, press, forty seconds. I carried it downstairs and set it on the hall table for the morning post, and I stood looking at it for a long moment, this ordinary envelope, this little white boat about to carry its cargo of my own name across London to the uncle I had not seen since I was nine years old.

Adeline's voice came from the study, calling me to bring the evening's papers, warm and easy and utterly unknowing — or knowing; I would think about that later, that flicker on her face on the step, that half-second of weather behind her eyes.

"In a moment, ma'am," I called back.

And I stood there in the hall with the clock ticking and the envelope on the tray, and I understood, with the cold clean clarity that arrives sometimes and changes everything, that the invisibility I had worn for six years like a coat had just developed a tear in it — and that through the tear, someone was looking in.
