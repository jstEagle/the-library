## 3. The Governess Who Vanished

Mrs. Tallow lived above a greengrocer's in Camden, in two rooms that smelled of cabbage and lamp oil, and she had been expecting me for years. I understood this the moment she opened the door—before I said a word, before the light reached my face—because her hand went to her mouth and she said, "Oh, Miss Mirabel," as though I were a debt come due at last.

She was smaller than I remembered. Everyone is smaller when you have stopped being six years old. But her eyes were the same, quick and brown and wet-prone, and her hands still had the look of hands that had done a great deal of washing for other people.

"I don't suppose you'd come in out of the cold," she said, though she was already stepping back to let me pass.

The room held a horsehair sofa, a table with a doily on it that had been crocheted by someone with more patience than skill, and a photograph of a young woman in a high-collared dress. I knew the photograph. I had known it existed somewhere all my life without knowing where, the way you know a tooth is loose.

"That's her," I said.

"That's your mother," Mrs. Tallow said. "Eleanor Ravensworth, when there was such a thing as an Eleanor Ravensworth."

I sat down because my legs told me to. She fussed with the kettle, and I watched the photograph while her back was turned—the direct gaze, the chin lifted slightly, the expression of a woman who had been photographed once and intended it to count. I had spent twenty-two years assembling my mother from fragments: a lullaby no one else sang, a smell of violet water that came to me unbidden in church, the way my own hands looked sometimes and seemed suddenly unfamiliar. Now here was the whole of her, reduced to silver nitrate and card stock, looking at me as if she had been waiting too.

"Mrs. Tallow." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You were her nurse."

"Her maid, then her nurse, then her only friend, and then nobody at all, which is what they arrange for you when they've finished." She set a cup before me without asking whether I wanted tea. In that household of my childhood, Mrs. Tallow had never once asked whether I wanted tea. "Sit by the fire, Miss Mirabel. This will take some telling, and you've got hands that shake when you're holding things, same as your mother had."

I hadn't known that about myself. It is a strange thing, to learn your own body from another person's memory of your dead mother.

* * *

She told it plainly, the way working people tell hard stories, because they have learned that ornament is for people who can afford grief as a leisure activity.

Eleanor Ravensworth had been born to money in 1861, third child and only daughter of Josiah Ravensworth, wool and shipping, one of those fortunes so large and so dull that no one ever asks how it was made. She had grown up in the very house in Belgravia where I now parked a motor car every Tuesday and carried in trays of correspondence. She had been educated past the ordinary lot of daughters—French, drawing, enough mathematics to read her father's ledgers, which was apparently considered eccentric of her and rather fine.

"And she could drive," Mrs. Tallow said, with sudden fierceness. "Not horses. Engines. Her brothers called it a vulgar mania but she drove that motor of hers all over Hertfordshire before most gentlemen knew what a crankshaft was. That's who you take after, Miss Mirabel. Not them. Her."

Something in my chest turned over. All my life I had believed I chose the motor work; it had felt like choice, like the one piece of my history I had authored myself. I had not known I was repeating it.

"She met your father at Harrogate. Mr. Ravensworth's clerk—no, worse than that, he was under the steward, which even the servants counted as marrying down twice. Thomas Mirabel's father was a schoolmaster. Good family in the way poor families are good, which is to say honest and tired." Mrs. Tallow's hands folded themselves in her lap, knuckles like walnuts. "Your grandfather Josiah was dead by then or he might have allowed it. Your uncles did not allow anything. Gerald especially. He was always the one with the ledger-heart."

Gerald. The name went into me like a splinter and I sat very still around it.

"They married anyway. Eloped, near enough. And for seven years they were happy in a small house in Kentish Town, and your father rose—he was clever—and Eleanor had you, and Mrs. Tallow came along as nurse because Eleanor wouldn't have anyone else near you. Seven years." She repeated it as if the number needed establishing as fact before the rest could be believed. "And then your father died. Fever, quick, no fault in it. And Eleanor was left with a daughter of two years old and no income worth naming, and she did what proud women do. She wrote to her brothers."

* * *

I had drunk my tea without noticing. Mrs. Tallow refilled the cup, and her hands were shaking now, not mine, and I understood that she had rehearsed this alone in this room for twenty years, waiting for someone to come and require it of her.

"They took you in, you understand. You won't remember, but you were kept in the nursery at Belgravia same as their own children, dressed same, taught same. And Eleanor they put in a cottage on the estate, at first, with an allowance. Small. Insulting-small, but an allowance. And then—" Mrs. Tallow stopped. She looked at the fire. She looked at the photograph. "Miss Mirabel, I want you to hear me say the next part properly, because there are people alive today who would prefer you heard it wrong."

"I'm listening."

"In 1893 your mother began writing letters. To friends, to a solicitor, to a newspaper once. Asking after her dower rights. Asking why the allowance had shrunk. Asking, in the last of them, why her sons—that is, your Uncle Gerald and Uncle Maurice—had altered the terms of a trust that should have come to her when your father died. She was not mad, whatever they told people after. She was inconvenient."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below us the greengrocer was pulling down his shutters, iron on iron.

"They got two doctors to sign a certificate. One of them never met her—I've seen the paper, or I saw it once, through a keyhole of circumstance I won't go into now. They got witnesses to attest to things no one witnessed. And in November of 1893 they took her to a private asylum in Hertfordshire, and the admission was written up as voluntary, which it was not, and the cause was written up as acute mania with delusions regarding her family, which it was not. She had delusions regarding her family, mind." Mrs. Tallow's voice cracked and she mastered it. "She believed they would come for her. That was the delusion. That her brothers would relent and she would see her daughter again."

I did not remember my mother. I want to be exact about this, because everything afterward depended on precision: I did not remember her. I had been two years old when she vanished, and the family story—told to me at intervals, in the tone reserved for sad weather—was that my mother had been delicate, that she had gone away for her health, that she had died abroad in 1897 of a consumption. I had built my whole self on that soft foundation and never once thought to press on it, because children believe the stories they are given, and I had been given mine so early it felt like memory.

"How did she die?" I asked. "Truly."

"Quietly," Mrs. Tallow said. "That's the cruelty of it. No drama at the end. She just wore out, the way they do in those places when no one comes. Four years. She was thirty-six."

* * *

We sat with that a while. I am not going to pretend I took it in with any grace. There is a kind of knowledge that enters the body before the mind consents to it, and I sat on Mrs. Tallow's horsehair sofa with my cup in my shaking hands and understood that I had been raised by the people who destroyed my mother. Fed at their table. Taught my letters by governesses whose wages came from a fortune that had been assembled partly out of her erasure. And loved—or I had believed myself loved—by a great-aunt and uncles who spoke of Eleanor, when they spoke of her at all, in the regretful murmuring tones one uses for a relative who died of something shameful.

"Why?" I said finally. "An allowance is cheap. An asylum costs money. Why not simply keep paying her to be quiet?"

"Because she wasn't quiet anymore," Mrs. Tallow said. "And because of the trust. Your father's family had property—small, but real, and there was a marriage settlement besides. When your father died it ought to have come to Eleanor, and through her, in time, to you. If she was of sound mind she could compel an accounting. If she was of sound mind she could write to lawyers, which she was doing. A lunacy order stops all of that, dear. A lunacy order makes a married woman into nobody. Her property goes to trustees, and the trustees were her loving brothers." She said the word *loving* flatly, without irony, which was somehow worse. "It wasn't passion, Miss Mirabel. It was bookkeeping."

Bookkeeping. I thought of Gerald Ravensworth's beautiful hands, the way he handled a pen, the pleasure he took in neat columns. I thought of him at Christmas, carving meat, saying grace.

"And me," I said. "Where did I fit in their bookkeeping?"

"You were a child. Children get absorbed." Mrs. Tallow looked at me with an old servant's directness. "But you were also a complication, because your mother's claim didn't die with her. It passed down. Do you understand what I'm telling you? You are the remainder of the estate, Miss Mirabel. You're the clause they couldn't strike out, only defer. And deferred claims have a habit of coming due."

"The census," I said.

She nodded slowly, unsurprised, and I realized she had been following the whole thread before I arrived, the way she had always followed the household's threads before anyone told her anything.

"A man came round Thursday," she said. "Asking questions up and down this street. Not the regular enumerator—I know Mr. Pettifer by sight, he's taken this street ten years. This one was a tall gentleman, soft-spoken, very correct, with a leather case. Asking after households that had taken in nurses or children between 'ninety and nineteen hundred. Asking particularly—" her voice dropped, not for drama but because the truth itself was heavy and had to be lowered carefully, "—asking particularly whether a Mrs. Tallow, formerly nurse to the Ravensworth family, might recall the whereabouts of any daughter of the late Mrs. Eleanor Mirabel, born 1889."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd nursed half of Camden and couldn't recall which children belonged to which griefs." Something moved in her face that was almost a smile. "I've lied to better men than him, love, and to some much worse. But he wrote my name in his book regardless. And he gave me his card, in case I recalled anything further." She leaned over, opened a drawer in the table, and produced a visiting card, handling it with two fingers as though it were something that had died. "There's no name printed on it. Just a set of chambers. Lincoln's Inn."

Lincoln's Inn. Not Gerald's world exactly, but adjacent to it—the legal quarter, where documents lived and were made to live differently than they should.

"He wasn't hunting you to harm you," Mrs. Tallow said, watching my face. "Or not only. Think, Miss Mirabel. Nobody sends a discreet man round with questions unless somebody wants something found—or wants something found first. Either your uncle knows the claim survived and means to smother it proper this time. Or—" and here she took my hand, her grip surprisingly strong, "—or there's a solicitor somewhere who's found the same irregularities you're about to find, and he's looking for the heir to make the claim on behalf of."

"Find the heir," I repeated. The words sat strangely. Twenty-two years invisible, a driver, a shadow, a girl who read letters in parked cars because her own life contained nothing worth reading—and now, possibly, two parties racing to locate me, one to erase and one to enact.

"There's something else," Mrs. Tallow said. "And I'll give it to you because I promised your mother, God rest her, that if it ever came to this, you'd not go blind into it. Before they took her, Eleanor hid papers. She trusted me with the knowing of it, though not the keeping. A bundle, wrapped in oilcloth. She said—if it ever mattered—her daughter would find the way to find them. She said her daughter was a reader." Mrs. Tallow's eyes were bright and terrible. "Are you still a reader, Miss Mirabel?"

The question landed with a weight I could not show her. I thought of Adeline's letters, slit and steamed and resealed, hundreds of them, years of them—my shameful, secret education in how power writes to itself. I thought of the letter from Halloway I had read two nights ago, and its phrase about irregularities in a will of 1893, and the way my heart had recognized its own name before my mind caught up.

"Yes," I said. "I'm still a reader."

"Then listen close, because I'll only say this once. The bundle isn't at Belgravia and it isn't at the Kentish Town house, long gone. Eleanor hid it where she hid everything that mattered—in plain sight, in the last place her brothers' sort would ever stoop to look. She hid it among the servants, dear. Ask yourself what survives in a great house when the master dies and the lawyers descend. Not the portraits—they stay put, don't they, and get inventoried. Not the plate. What survives is what some faithful woman carried home in her apron when the household broke up, because she loved somebody and kept her things." Mrs. Tallow squeezed my hand. "There was a housekeeper at the Hertfordshire place. Ellen Bray. She was dismissed in 'ninety-seven, the month your mother died, and she took service after with a family named Croft. Croft, of all names. She's dead now herself, but she talked, the way we all talk at the end. Talked about a bundle in oilcloth she was paid to burn and didn't."

Croft. The name entered the room like cold air.

I stood, because sitting had become impossible. On the table the photograph regarded me with its level chin and direct gaze, and for the first time in my life I looked at my mother's face and saw not a loss but an instruction.

"Mrs. Tallow," I said, "what was the housekeeper's position with the Crofts?"

"Companion-housekeeper to the mother. Dead now, the both of them, but the daughter kept the connection—kept the woman on in some capacity till the end, people said, out of sentiment. The daughter would be about your age." Mrs. Tallow rose too, laboriously, and took both my hands in her cracked ones. "Miss Mirabel. Whatever face you're making right now, stop it. Because I've known you since you were two days old and I can see you thinking of a name."

I was thinking of a motor car. I was thinking of Tuesdays, and sealed envelopes, and the warm specific authority of Adeline Croft's handwriting, which I knew better than my own. I was thinking of a house in Belgravia where I parked every week, and a brother named Gerald who visited there with a leather portfolio, and a movement I had served for four years in the belief that history happened elsewhere and I was lucky to hold its coats.

"I don't know any Crofts," I said, and heard my mother's own gift for the necessary lie arrive in my mouth as naturally as breathing.

Mrs. Tallow looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I had never seen her do in all my childhood: she curtsied. Deep, formal, absurd in that cabbage-scented room—a curtsy owed not to me, I understood, but to whatever was standing in me now that hadn't been standing there an hour ago.

"Then you'd best go and not-know them properly," she said. "And Miss Mirabel—you take care. Men who send tall gentlemen with leather cases to ask after girls born in 1889 are men who have already decided what happens to the girl when they find her."

* * *

I walked back across London in the dark instead of taking the tram, three miles, because I could not bear to sit still and could not yet afford to be seen arriving anywhere with my face in its current state. Grief and fury take turns holding the wheel, and neither of them drives well.

By the time I reached the mews where the motor was garaged, I had stopped shaking and started planning, which is the family inheritance, I now understood, on both sides—the reading, the driving, the hiding of things in plain sight.

Tomorrow was Tuesday. Tomorrow I would drive to Belgravia as always, and carry in the post as always, and be invisible as always. Except that invisibility, I now knew, was not absence. It was access. And somewhere upstairs in that glass-bright house, in a desk or a drawer or a locked case that Gerald Ravensworth visited too often, there might be a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, hidden by a housekeeper who was paid to burn it and refused—

—and I had just remembered, with a clarity that stopped my breath on the dark street, exactly which drawer in Adeline Croft's study I had seen, last spring, locked with a small brass key that Adeline wore on a ribbon at her wrist, and had never once, in all my years of reading her letters, been able to open.
