## 4. Adeline's House of Glass

Adeline Croft had learned long ago that a household, like an argument, could be held together with nothing but confidence and good lighting.

She stood in the morning room at Wexford House—her house, though the lease said otherwise, though the lease was itself a small act of fiction she maintained the way other women maintained their complexions—and watched the light come through the tall windows and gild everything that needed gilding. The worn patches on the Turkish carpet. The water ring on the rosewood table where she'd set a wet tumbler three years ago and never had the heart to confess it to Mrs. Prewitt. The stack of unopened bills she kept in a drawer shaped exactly for the purpose of not looking like a stack of bills.

From the street, Wexford House looked like what it pretended to be: the headquarters of a movement with momentum, funded by serious money, staffed by serious women. From inside, it was a machine Adeline ran on charm, credit, and the particular alchemy of making people feel that giving her their last five pounds was their own idea.

It was working. God help her, it was still working.

The evening meetings drew forty where they had once drawn nine. The newspaper clippings came in faster than Nellie could paste them into the scrapbook. Only last week a woman from Manchester had written to say she'd named her daughter Adeline, which was flattering and slightly terrible, because the woman in the newspapers—the bold one, the quotable one, the one who stood on platforms and made hecklers look foolish—was a version of herself that required the morning room, the bills in their drawer, and above all the money to keep the lights on while she rehearsed being fearless.

Fearless cost money. Nobody printed that.

"Mrs. Croft." Mirabel at the door, with the particular stillness she carried like luggage. "The post."

Adeline took the tray without examining it too closely. That was the arrangement, and it was a good one: Mirabel sorted, answered what could be answered, sealed what must be sealed, and asked nothing. A driver who could read and write and keep her mouth shut was worth her weight in motor oil. Adeline had found her two years ago through a cousin of Nellie's—a quiet girl with steady hands, an uncanny memory for streets, and eyes that took in everything and gave back almost none of it.

"Anything that looks like a creditor?" Adeline said lightly.

"Nothing that looks like anything," Mirabel said. Which was Mirabel for *yes*, and Adeline chose not to pursue it, because pursuing it would mean knowing, and knowing would mean the fear got a chair at the table.

"Thank you. And Mirabel—if Mr. Ravensworth calls today, I'm at the dressmaker's until four."

A pause. Half a heartbeat. "You'll be at the dressmaker's."

"I'll be somewhere that isn't here when he asks about the accounts. Yes."

"Yes, ma'am."

She went, and Adeline sat with the post and did not open the envelope with the solicitor's crest, because she knew what it would say. It would say what the last one said. It would use words like *arrears* and *foreclosure proceedings* and *our client's patience*. She put it in the drawer with its brothers and closed the drawer and thought, as she always did, about Gerald Ravensworth's money, which was coming—which had been promised, which was practically hers—and how everything would be different when it arrived.

Everything would be different. She believed this the way she believed in the vote: absolutely, and against all daily evidence.

* * *

Gerald Ravensworth arrived at half past four, which meant he arrived when he knew Adeline would be cornered, available, and grateful. He filled the morning room doorway the way he filled every doorway—not large, exactly, but *assured*, a man who had never once entered a room wondering whether he was wanted there. Grey suit, grey gloves, a stick with a silver head he tapped against his boot when he was thinking, which was often, because Gerald enjoyed the appearance of deliberation even when the decision had been made in the motor on the way over.

"Addie." He used her married name's first syllable like a man handling something he owned. "You look harassed."

"You look expensive. Sit down and tell me you've brought good news."

He sat, crossing his legs, laying the stick across his knee. "I've brought news. Whether it's good depends on your temperament."

"My temperament is famous."

"Your temperament," Gerald said, "is why my brother-in-law's club has started asking after you by name. You're becoming visible, Addie. Visible is expensive."

*There it is.* Adeline kept her face pleasant and her hands folded. This was the ritual, and she knew the steps: he would express concern for the cause, concern for her, concern for appearances; he would let her understand that the money existed but was contingent; and she would perform just enough contrition to unlock the next tranche without surrendering the thing that made her worth funding, which was that she frightened people.

"The accounts are sound," she said. "We're lean. We've always been lean."

"The accounts are a sieve with a hat on." He said it mildly, almost fondly, the way one comments on weather. "The motor alone—you know what that machine costs per mile? And your driver takes it out how often? Every day?"

"The motor wins us coverage. Coverage wins us members. Members win us—" She stopped herself before saying *money*, because they both knew the members won nothing of the kind. The members won meetings, and meetings cost money, and the money came from Gerald.

"Mm." He tapped the stick. "Here is my position. My position is that I believe in you. Not—" a raised hand— "not in the cause, Addie, do let's not insult each other with that. In *you*. You are the only person in this whole shrieking parade who understands that a movement is a business with better music. So I am going to advance you another quarter's sum against the autumn fund, and I am going to ask you, as a kindness between friends, to let me have a look at the books myself this time. My man could sit with your girl. The quiet one. What's her name."

The question landed somewhere below Adeline's ribs, cold and specific, though she couldn't have said why.

"Miss Vasquez. Mirabel."

"Vasquez." He turned the name over like a coin he suspected of being foreign. "Odd sort for the work. Where did you find her?"

"A friend of Nellie's. She's excellent. Discreet."

"They usually are," Gerald said, "until they aren't." Then he smiled, and the smile rearranged his whole face into something almost warm, and Adeline hated how much relief she felt at it, how cheaply bought her own loyalty apparently was. "Well. Send her round Thursday with the ledgers. Nothing sinister, Addie. A tidy set of books makes everyone sleep better. Especially me."

He rose, collected his stick and gloves, paused at the window—the light doing him the same favor it did the carpet, gilding what needed gilding—and said, without turning around, in a voice entirely changed:

"Oh. One more thing. Have you ever heard the family speak of a sister? Eleanor."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere upstairs Nellie dropped a shoe.

"Eleanor," Adeline repeated carefully. "No. Should I have?"

"No," Gerald said. "No, I don't suppose you should. Forget I mentioned it. A legal curiosity, nothing more. Some paperwork surfacing from the old days. It'll come to nothing." He settled his hat. "Thursday, then."

And he was gone, leaving behind the smell of good tobacco and a silence with edges on it.

* * *

That night Adeline sat in the morning room with one lamp lit and tried to think about Eleanor Ravensworth and found, to her genuine unease, that she *had* heard the name before—years ago, in the first flush of her engagement, at a dinner at the Ravensworths' where old Mr. Ravensworth senior had said something cutting about a daughter who had married "a clerk with a pretty face," and the whole table had performed the synchronized silence of people stepping around a grave. She'd asked Gerald afterwards, in bed, drowsy, brave. He had said: *There is no sister. There was an incident. We don't discuss it.*

She hadn't asked again. She had been twenty-three and in love with the house the marriage came with, and there were questions you didn't ask when you were busy becoming someone new.

But now she sat with the lamp and understood, with the cold clarity that sometimes visited her between performances, that Gerald did not mention names idly. Gerald mentioned things for reasons. A legal curiosity. Paperwork surfacing. *It'll come to nothing*—which, from Gerald, meant the opposite of nothing, or he wouldn't have bothered carrying it across London to deposit in her morning room.

Was it a warning? A test? Was someone asking questions about Eleanor Ravensworth, and did Gerald suspect the questions were coming from—or through—Wexford House?

Her mind went to Mirabel, the way it increasingly did, and she examined the thought the way she examined the bills in the drawer: reluctantly, quickly, closing it again before it could become knowledge. The quiet girl with the steady hands and the memory for streets. The odd sort for the work. Who *was* she? Adeline had hired competence and never asked for a history, and now a history might be arriving whether she wanted it or not, wearing a grey suit and carrying a silver-headed stick.

"No," she said aloud to the empty room. "Coincidence."

But she said it in the tone she used on hecklers, and she knew exactly how much those tones were worth.

* * *

Thursday, Mirabel took the ledgers to Ravensworth's man and returned at dusk with them bound in brown paper and her face, as ever, giving back nothing. Adeline watched her set them on the table and thought: *she reads everything I leave out. Of course she does. I would, in her place.* The thought was not alarming so much as clarifying, and it settled something in her—some small debt of symmetry, since Adeline read everyone too, everyone, it was the whole trade.

"Gerald's man give you trouble?" she asked.

"No, ma'am. He mostly wanted to talk about horses." A beat. "He asked whether you kept papers at the house. Personal papers, older ones. I said I wouldn't know."

Adeline's hands went still on the arm of her chair. "And what did you say he concluded?"

"He didn't conclude anything out loud." Mirabel straightened the ledgers so their edges matched, precisely, unnecessarily. "But he wrote something down when he thought I'd gone."

When she had left the room, Adeline poured herself the sherry she'd been avoiding all week and stood at the window watching the lamplighter work his way down the square. Papers. Older ones. A dead woman's name surfacing in two houses in one week like a body rising in a pond.

Gerald was searching for something, or searching for someone, and the search had begun circling her household—her glass house, with all its windows facing out.

She drank the sherry and told herself the most useful lie in her considerable repertoire: that whatever was coming, she would manage it. She had managed creditors and constables and husbands' clubs and her own creeping terror that the whole edifice was built on borrowed sand. She would manage this.

Upstairs, in the servants' attic under the eaves, a light burned late. Adeline saw it from the square, a thin gold line under Mirabel's door, and stood a moment longer than she meant to, looking up at it, feeling—for the first time in years—the distinct sensation of being the one read, rather than the one reading.

The next morning's post contained a letter addressed to *Miss M. Ravensworth*, care of Wexford House, franked from a solicitor's office in Chancery Lane.

Adeline held it a long time before she put it on the tray, unopened, face down, and called for the car.
