## 5. The Forged Lunacy Papers

The Ravensworth townhouse in Belgravia kept eleven rooms on the three upper floors and a housekeeper named Mrs. Prew who believed, with religious fervour, that no gentleman's home should be cleaned by anyone he had ever met. This was the belief I was counting on. Gerald Ravensworth had last seen me at my mother's funeral, when I was nine years old and crying into a black veil. Grief ages a face differently than service does. A woman in a grey uniform, cap pulled low, hands chapped from the soda she carried in a pail—no one looks twice at her. That is the whole architecture of our invisibility, we who serve: not that we are hidden, but that we are beneath notice.

Mrs. Prew had hired me off a card left at the Catholic women's registry near Victoria. My references were forged, my name was Alice Merrick, and my story—that my husband had gone to Glasgow for work and stopped writing—was the sort of sad arithmetic that made matrons sigh and sign papers without reading them too closely. The suffrage movement had taught me this much: how paperwork moves through institutions, how a convincing lie wears the same clothes as the truth.

I had been cleaning Gerald Ravensworth's house for six days.

* * *

Six days of learning its rhythms. Gerald rose late, took his breakfast at half past nine, and left for the City at ten. His man, Voss, accompanied him. The undermaids worked the ground floor; the first floor belonged to me—the drawing room, the morning room, the library. The library was locked between cleanings, which told me everything I needed to know about where a man keeps what he doesn't want found.

On the sixth day, Thursday, Gerald did not go to the City. He received visitors instead: two men in good coats who stayed an hour and a half in the library, and whose voices never rose above a murmur. Through the keyhole of the adjoining morning room, where I knelt with my polishing cloth like a woman with nothing in her head but brass fittings, I heard fragments. *Enrollment.* *Chancery Lane.* *The old register copy should have been destroyed years ago.*

Then, after they left, Gerald called down for his hat and coat and went out alone, and Mrs. Prew said I could do the library while it was empty.

He had left in a hurry. Men like Gerald always believe the world will wait for them.

* * *

The lock on the library door was a Bramah—good, but old, and old locks remember their habits. I had watched Voss open it twice from the end of the corridor, pretending to carry linens. There is a trick to it: the key must be withdrawn with a slight upward turn, or the bolt catches. But I had no key. What I had was a hairpin, a stub of tallow candle, and six days of patience, and what the Bramah had was a worn warden that had been jemmied once before—there were bright scratches around the plate that no locksmith's work would leave. Someone else had wanted into this room. Perhaps someone hired by Halloway, whose second letter had reached me four days earlier and now sat folded against my ribs like a coal.

*I have reason to examine certain irregularities attending the will of the late Josiah Ravensworth, deceased 1893,* Halloway had written. *Irregularities of enrollment. If you are the Mirabel Ravensworth born 1889—and I believe you are—you should know that your claim, if claim there be, grows weaker the longer it sleeps.*

My claim. As if I wanted the money. As if that was what any of this was about. It took me a long time that night, lying in my narrow bed in Pimlico, to admit that some part of it was exactly about that—not greed but arithmetic: every pound that had clothed and fed and schooled Adeline Croft's beautiful causes had come out of an estate that should have been my mother's, and my mother had died in a lunacy ward with her name struck from her own life. The money was only the shape the injustice took. The injustice itself was older and colder than coin.

The hairpin took eleven minutes. I had never picked a lock in my life, and my hands shook so badly at the fourth minute that I had to stop and press them flat against my skirts and think of my mother's hands—how they smelled of clove oil when she unstopped her sewing box, how they went still and wrong in the last photograph anyone took of her. Then the bolt gave with a sound no louder than a swallowed word, and I was inside.

* * *

Gerald Ravensworth's library was a museum of himself. Leather-bound sets of everything, spines uncracked. A globe with the Empire's territories picked out in red. Photographs in silver frames: Gerald at Oxford, Gerald with his hounds, Gerald standing beside his brother Josiah's portrait like a man standing beside a business asset he had successfully liquidated. And on the wall behind the desk, in a heavy gilt frame that had once hung somewhere grander, a family tree, hand-inked on vellum, going back to a Ravensworth knight who had done something forgettable under Charles II.

I found my mother on it. Or rather, I did not find her.

There was Josiah, born 1851. There was Gerald, born 1856. There was a sister, Constance, dead in infancy. The vellum was immaculate. Eleanor Ravensworth, born 1862, married 1887 to one Thomas Vasquez, engineer, died 1899—she was nowhere. Not crossed out. Never entered. Two hundred years of names and mine was the mother-shaped absence at the bottom of it, erased so early and so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased.

That was the moment I stopped shaking.

I had expected to feel fear in that room, and I did—it sat in my throat like a stone the whole time. But underneath the fear was something flatter and harder, a kind of cold that was almost restful, because it asked nothing of me except to keep working. I have thought since about how people talk about rage as heat, fire, burning. Mine came in cold. It made my hands steady. It made me methodical.

I started with the desk.

* * *

Locked drawers, all of them, but desk locks are vanity locks—they exist to be seen locking things. The letter tray held nothing but invitations and a bill from a tailor whose prices could have kept Ruth's workhouse ward in bread for a year. The centre drawer, however, sat shallow in its runners, shallower than its neighbours, and when I pulled it fully out and tipped it, something shifted in the space below—a false bottom, sitting loose because whoever installed it had been a carpenter and not a conspirator.

Underneath: a bundle of papers tied in legal tape, and a bank book.

I did not look at the bank book first, though I registered it—registered the figures, the transfers marked *A.C. Committee*, *Movement acct*, dates going back years, sums that made Adeline's rented headquarters and her motor car and her three secretaries suddenly legible. Gerald had been funding the cause. Generously. Repeatedly. I filed it away the way you file away a blow you haven't finished feeling.

The papers in the tape were older. The topmost document was a solicitor's cover sheet, and beneath it, on paper gone the colour of weak tea, was a certificate.

*County Lunatic Asylum at Wellsbourne.*

*Certificate of Insanity and Order for Reception.*

I read my mother's name in a stranger's handwriting. *Eleanor Vasquez, née Ravensworth, of Clapham, aged thirty-one years.* Read the findings: *delusions of persecution; asserts that her husband's family conspire against her; violent language toward her brothers; refuses sustenance offered; general derangement of mind of some months' duration.* Each phrase a small, tidy tombstone. She had asserted her husband's family conspired against her. Her husband's family had conspired against her. The delusion and the truth were the same sentence, and the doctors had written both down without irony, because doctors write what they are paid to find.

Her brothers. I looked for the signatures.

Josiah's signature I knew—he had died before I could form memories of him, but his name was on every document that had ever touched my life obliquely, the invisible gravity around which my childhood orbited. There it was, certifying his sister's madness, dated the 14th of March, 1893. Eleven days after Josiah's father's death, I would learn later; eleven days after the will that changed everything had been read.

And beneath Josiah's signature, in the space marked *Witness to the Order*, a second name.

Croft.

Henry Croft.

I stood very still in Gerald Ravensworth's library with the dust settling around me and the clock on the mantel ticking like something being counted down, and I read the name until it stopped being ink and became a man. Henry Croft, Adeline's father, the sainted radical, the man whose portrait hung in the movement's headquarters, whose memoir Adeline quoted the way other women quoted scripture—*the law is a fence built by the strong, and we are the ones who must pull it down.* Henry Croft, Q.C., champion of friendless women, had stood in a room in 1893 and witnessed the commitment of a thirty-one-year-old heiress to an asylum, freeing her share of the Ravensworth estate to flow to her brothers and, eventually, into the coffers of every righteous cause his daughter would ever lead.

The movement's sainted family. Built on my mother's ruin. Literally, arithmetically, floor by funded floor.

I want to say I wept. I didn't. The cold in me had passed beyond weeping, the way deep water passes beyond light. I copied the date, the asylum's name, the certificate reference number onto the flyleaf of my cleaning ledger, in the shorthand I use for linen inventories—anyone inspecting it would see laundry counts. Then I wrapped the certificate and its attached order in my spare apron, which I had brought upstairs rolled inside my cleaning cloths for exactly this purpose, and I put the false bottom back, and the drawer, and I wiped every surface I had touched.

At the door I stopped. On the desk, in the silver frame, young Gerald grinned beside his brother's portrait. I turned the frame forty degrees, so that Josiah's painted eyes looked slightly off into the distance, at nothing.

It was a small thing. It was the only honest thing I did in that house.

* * *

Mrs. Prew met me on the stairs with her arms full of napkins and her suspicions full of nothing. "Alice. The study's done, then?"

"Done, ma'am. The master's back Monday?"

"Sunday night, likely." She squinted at me. "You've a colour on you. Not coming down with something?"

"The heat, ma'am," I said. The house was freezing. "I'll be better when I've had air."

I carried my pail down three flights and out the area door into the April street, and I walked twelve blocks before I let myself stop, in the doorway of a shuttered chapel, and unwrap the apron and look at the paper again. The name had not changed. Henry Croft had not un-witnessed anything in the hour since I'd last read it. Somewhere across the city, Adeline was speaking at a hall in Battersea—I had driven her there myself that morning, had adjusted the windscreen for her, had heard her rehearse the peroration under her breath: *my father taught me that no woman's liberty should ever depend on another's convenience.*

No woman's liberty. He had certified my mother mad in an afternoon.

And Adeline—Adeline who pressed my shoulder when I lifted her down from the motor, who called me *the best of lieutenants*, who had taken Gerald's money for twenty years without asking, apparently, where it came from—Adeline either knew or did not know, and I could not decide which possibility was worse. If she knew, then every kindness had been purchased with my mother's blood-money and seasoned with contempt for my ignorance. If she did not know, then she was a fool leading thousands of women, and fools make poor allies in the fight I now understood I was entering.

Halloway's letter needed answering. The certificate needed copying by a proper clerk, witnessed, lodged somewhere Gerald's reach couldn't burn. And there was a third thing, the thing the cold voice in me had already begun planning around: the certificate proved the lunacy order existed. It did not prove it was forged. For that I would need the underlying papers—the medical certificates signed by doctors who had never examined her, the enrollment records, whatever version of the 1893 will Gerald's men had been whispering about preserving or destroying. Those lived elsewhere. Those would cost more than a hairpin.

I rewrapped the papers, straightened my cap, and walked north toward the telegraph office and the registry where a woman named Alice Merrick would, by Friday, give notice of a sick mother in Manchester.

Behind me Belgravia glittered its grey glitter, every window a frame around somebody's respectable thefts. Ahead of me, across the river, the movement's halls were lighting up for evening meetings, and I thought of Adeline at the Battersea rostrum, borrowing her father's halo, and I felt the cold settle into the place where my loyalty used to live.

I had one ally in the world I hadn't chosen yet, and her name was Adeline Croft, and tomorrow morning I would drive her wherever she asked to go, and watch her hands on her gloves, and wait to see if she flinched when Gerald's name was spoken.

She had asked me, just last week, to drive her to Chancery Lane.

I had assumed it was for the Cause.

Now I intended to find out.
