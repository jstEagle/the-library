## 6. What Loyalty Weighs

Adeline Croft kept a ledger of small betrayals, and she told herself it was for the cause.

Not a real ledger—she had enough of those, stacked in the drawer beneath the telephone, their columns of red ink marching toward numbers her brother-in-law insisted were temporary. No. This was a mental one: a catalogue of every lie she'd told in service of the movement, each one weighed and found light as ash. The dinner parties where she'd smiled at men whose votes she meant to take. The letters burned before they could be read by anyone's wife or maid. The cheque Gerald had pressed into her hand last spring, which she'd deposited under an account name that would not survive scrutiny.

She had stopped counting after forty. It seemed morbid.

What she could not stop noticing was Mirabel.

The girl had been driving her for three years, and Adeline trusted her the way she trusted her own hands—without thought, without examination, which was itself a kind of luxury Adeline knew she couldn't afford. Mirabel was quiet in the way that made people mistake her for empty space. Ministers' wives said things in front of her they'd never say before a footman. Gerald, God help him, had once discussed the movement's finances at length within earshot of her, in the motor, on the very road to his house in Kensington, and Adeline had thought nothing of it because Mirabel was furniture, Mirabel was weather, Mirabel was a pair of steady hands and a face that gave away nothing.

Except now it gave something away. That was the trouble.

Adeline noticed it first on the Tuesday—she dated everything by Tuesdays now, since that was when the WSPU committee met and her whole life arranged itself around the meeting like iron filings around a magnet. They were idling outside the Caxton Hall annex in the rain, wipers ticking, and Adeline came down the steps with her collar up and her speech still hot in her mouth, and there was Mirabel in the driver's seat with a letter open in her lap.

Open. Not sealed against the rain, not tucked in the dispatch case, but open, her thumb resting at the crease as if she'd forgotten herself entirely. She looked—Adeline would search later for the right word and keep coming back to this one—*caught*. Not startled. Caught. The difference mattered. Startled was a flinch; caught was a calculation about how much had been seen.

Then Mirabel folded the letter, slid it into her coat, and said, "Miss Croft. You'll want the rug over your knees, it's turned foul," and her voice was perfectly level, and Adeline got in, and the moment should have died there.

It didn't die. Moments like that never do; they go underground and put out roots.

* * *

By Thursday Adeline had constructed three explanations and discarded two.

The first—that Mirabel was reading her post out of idle curiosity—she threw out within the hour. Three years, and the girl had never once betrayed a confidence, never repeated a name, never so much as glanced sideways at Adeline across a drawing room in a way that suggested shared knowledge. Idle readers leaked. Mirabel didn't leak.

The second—that she was a spy, some Blackwell or Home Office creature planted among them—lasted until midnight, when Adeline lay awake cataloguing everything Mirabel could have sold and hadn't. There had been arrests. There had been raids. If Mirabel were police, half the committee would be in Holloway by now and Adeline with them.

Which left the third explanation, and it was this: Mirabel was reading the letters for something personal. Something in the correspondence touched her own life, and she had been quietly, methodically, hunting it through other people's mail.

The thought should have frightened her. It did frighten her. But underneath the fear—and this was the part Adeline could not confess to anyone, the part she carried alone to bed each night like a stone under the pillow—underneath it was a terrible, vertiginous relief.

Because if Mirabel was searching, then Mirabel might find things. And if Mirabel found things, then someone else in the world would finally know what Adeline herself only half-knew, only suspected in the small hours, only felt as a cold draft under the door of her conscience whenever Gerald Ravensworth crossed her threshold with his hat in his hand and his smile arranged just so.

She decided to test her. Not cruelly—Adeline was capable of cruelty but tried to ration it—but carefully, the way you tap a wall to learn where the hollow places are.

* * *

Friday morning, fog thick enough to drink. The garage behind the Cromwell Road house smelled of petrol and hay and the particular cold-metal damp of motors in winter, and Mirabel was under the bonnet with a spanner when Adeline came out wrapped in Gerald's Christmas scarf, feeling absurd and determined.

"You'll ruin your gloves," Mirabel said, without surfacing.

"They're your problem when I do." Adeline perched on the workbench, which she knew Mirabel hated—the oil, the dust—and watched her work. Strong forearms, sleeves rolled, hair pinned back with what appeared to be a pencil. There was something steadying about watching her, always had been. In a movement made entirely of noise—pamphlets and platforms and the endless braying argument of it—Mirabel was the single silent thing, and silence, Adeline was discovering at forty-one, becomes precious precisely in proportion to its scarcity.

"I want to tell you something," Adeline said, "and then I'm going to ask you not to repeat it, and you're going to think the second part is ridiculous because you've never once repeated anything."

The spanner paused. Mirabel straightened up and looked at her, and her eyes in the grey light were the colour of the fog itself.

"Miss Croft—"

"Adeline. For God's sake, we've been over this."

"Adeline." The girl said it like a confession. "You don't have to ask me not to repeat things. I've heard four years of committee secrets and none of them have gone further than my own head."

*I've heard.* Not *you've told me*. Adeline let it pass, or pretended to, and took a breath that tasted of engine oil and did the thing she'd come out to do.

"It's about the accounts," she said. "The movement's accounts. And about Gerald."

Something moved in Mirabel's face—something fast, quickly shuttered, like a bird crossing a window. Adeline filed it and went on.

"You know he handles our treasury. Has done since '08, when he offered—generously, everyone said—to take the burden off Mrs. Pethick's shoulders." She picked at a thread on the scarf. "And I know what people say about me and Gerald, which is that I married into comfort and bit the hand that funds me, and I can live with that. What I can't live with is arithmetic."

"Arithmetic?"

"The subscriptions come in. The donations come in—I raise half of them myself, I know their size, I remember faces and figures the way you remember roads. And yet every quarter the balance is thinner than it ought to be, and every quarter Gerald has an explanation, and every explanation involves some delay, some investment pending clearance, some *temporary* arrangement." She heard her own voice sharpen and made herself stop. "Three years of temporary arrangements, Mirabel. Do you know what I think? I think my husband's brother is robbing us. I think he's been robbing us since the beginning, and I think he believes I'm too much of a lady—or too much of a coward—to add the columns myself."

The garage was very quiet. Rain ticked somewhere above the skylight.

"And you haven't," Mirabel said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I haven't checked, no." Adeline laughed, short and ugly. "There's a word for a woman who audits her husband's brother without proof. Several words. None of them printable. And if I'm wrong—" she spread her hands "—I've handed the newspapers the story of the year. *Suffragist Splits Over Money.* They'd run it beside the prison photographs."

"So why tell me?"

And there it was: the question, clean as a scalpel, delivered without accusation. Adeline looked at her—at this woman she had hired on a whim because she drove beautifully and asked for almost nothing, this woman whose file in Adeline's mind contained a mother dead young, a childhood passed among indifferent cousins, and otherwise a blank so complete it had begun, lately, to feel deliberate.

"Because you're the only person I know who doesn't want anything from me," Adeline said. "Everyone else wants money, or speeches, or my name on their letterhead. You want a wage and dry boots. It makes you the only honest audience I have." She slid off the bench. "And because I need someone to drive me to Chancery Lane on Monday, and I'd rather it were you than a cabman who talks."

"Chancery Lane," Mirabel repeated. Her voice had gone very careful, the way it did on icy roads.

"Solicitors. There's a firm there—Halloway & Sons—who handled some old family business years ago, before my marriage. I've had a note from them asking me to call. I don't know yet whether it concerns the estate or the movement, and frankly I don't trust either possibility." She managed a smile. "But if Gerald is stealing from us, there may be documents. Records. Things that live in solicitors' offices and nowhere else. I want to know what Halloway knows before I decide what Gerald knows I know. You follow?"

Mirabel followed. Adeline could see her following—the slight forward lean, the spanner turning over and over in her fingers like a worry stone.

"Monday morning," Mirabel said. "I'll have the car ready by nine."

* * *

Saturday, Gerald came to lunch.

He arrived in his Rolls with the hood down despite the cold, because Gerald believed display was a form of argument, and he kissed Adeline's cheek and praised the soup and told two stories about backbenchers, and all the while Adeline watched him the way she had learned to watch cabinet ministers: not for what they said but for what they avoided.

He avoided the subject of money entirely, which was unlike him. He talked instead about the census—about the great national count coming in April, about the government's insolence in counting women as though they existed, and how the movement's boycott would make the returns a farce. He was expansive on it. Jovial. Twice he mentioned, apropos of nothing, that the enumerators were asking unusually thorough questions this year. Family histories. Maiden names. Lines of descent.

"Why," Adeline said lightly, "planning to disown someone?"

Gerald's laugh was a beat late. "Merely observing, my dear, that governments collect more than they're owed. As do families, occasionally." Then he turned to ask Mirabel—through Adeline, in the third person, as he always did—whether the Daimler could be spared Tuesday week, as he had engagements in Kensington.

"She'll manage her diary," Adeline said, and saw, across the table, Mirabel's hands go still around the coffee pot.

That night Adeline sat at her desk with the household accounts and, for the first time in three years, actually read them. It took her until two in the morning. When she finished, she sat in the dark with the figures swimming and thought: *it isn't only the movement.* Some of these payments had nothing to do with the WSPU at all. Disbursements to names she didn't recognise. A standing order to a private asylum in Surrey that drew against family money, not movement money—an asylum whose name she had heard exactly once before, years ago, in her husband's study, spoken by Gerald in a tone she had never heard him use again.

Eleanor House. That was the name.

She wrote it on a slip of paper, considered it, and then—because she was Adeline Croft, because there were already forty betrayals in her ledger and she could feel the weight of another gathering—did not burn it. She folded it into her purse instead, next to the note from Halloway & Sons, and went to bed, and lay awake listening to the house settle, thinking about Mirabel's still hands and Gerald's late laugh and the strange, growing certainty that the two of them stood at opposite ends of the same secret, and that she, Adeline, was the only person in England who couldn't see what it was.

* * *

Sunday evening she found Mirabel in the motor, of all places—sitting in the parked Daimler in the dark of the mews, hands on the wheel, going nowhere. Adeline opened the passenger door and climbed in without asking, and the cold closed around them both, and neither of them started the engine. There was a lamplight through the windscreen and the smell of leather and rain.

"Rehearsing?" Adeline asked.

"Resting." A pause. "You shouldn't be out without a coat."

"You sound like my late governess." Adeline tucked her hands under her arms and looked through the glass at the wet cobbles. "I used to sit in my mother's carriage the same way, before my presentation. Not going anywhere. Just—somewhere that belonged to me. Somewhere with a door." She hadn't meant to say that. The cold made her loose. Everything made her loose lately; she suspected it was the beginning of honesty, which she understood to be terminal. "Mirabel. Whatever it is you're carrying—and I've driven behind enough secrets to know the posture—you should know that when you set it down, I'd rather you set it down near me than anywhere else."

Mirabel didn't answer for a long moment. In the lamplight her profile was all clean line and restraint, and Adeline thought, not for the first time: *there is a person in there I have never once troubled to meet, and I have let her carry me for three years.*

"If I ever do set it down," Mirabel said quietly, "I hope you'll forgive me for how long I held it."

"That's a cryptic answer."

"It's the only kind I can afford this week."

They sat with that, whatever it was, sitting between them like a third passenger. Adeline reached over and covered Mirabel's hand on the gearstick—a gesture she would not have permitted herself in daylight, in town, in any of the rooms where she was somebody—and Mirabel let her, and turned her hand over so that for a moment they were palm to palm, and the fog pressed close around the motor like something listening.

"Monday, nine o'clock," Adeline said at last, withdrawing her hand, making her voice brisk again, buttoning herself back into Adeline Croft. "Chancery Lane. Wear something plain; I don't want to be remarked at."

"Adeline." Mirabel's eyes came round to her, and there was something in them Adeline would spend many nights afterwards trying to name—warning, maybe, or grief arriving early, the way grief does. "When we get there. Whatever the clerks show you, whoever asks you questions—remember that I drove you. Remember that part first."

"Is that loyalty or a warning?"

"Yes," said Mirabel, and started the engine.

They pulled out of the mews into the fog, headlamps carving two short tunnels of yellow that gave out metres from the bumper, and Adeline settled back against the seat with Halloway's letter in her purse and Eleanor House written on a slip of paper and forty betrayals in her invisible ledger—and thought, with the peculiar clarity that comes at the edge of sleep or disaster, that tomorrow she was going to Chancery Lane to look for answers, and that the truest answer in the car had been sitting beside her for three years, holding the wheel, waiting to be asked.

She just didn't yet know the question would cost her everything.

Nine o'clock Monday came grey and early, and Mirabel had the car warm and waiting, and the roads into the City were slick with the tail end of the night's rain. It was not until they turned onto Chancery Lane itself, past the great iron gates of the Public Record Office, that Adeline noticed Mirabel's eyes fixed on the brass plate they were passing—**HALLOWAY & SONS, SOLICITORS**—with an expression Adeline had seen only once before, on the face of a woman being arrested on the pavement at Westminster: the expression of someone who has already decided, and knows what the deciding will cost.

The car drew up at the kerb. Somewhere inside that building, behind frosted glass, waited the first page of an answer.

Adeline reached for her purse, and the slip of paper inside it—Eleanor House—was warmer than it should have been, as if it knew.
