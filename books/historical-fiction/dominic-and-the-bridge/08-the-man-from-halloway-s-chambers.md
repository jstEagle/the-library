## 8. The Man from Halloway's Chambers

Chancery Lane had its own weather. The fog off the Thames came up the valley of Holborn and settled between the law offices like an old tenant who would not be moved, and the clerks and barristers moved through it in black rows, collars high, briefcases clutched to their chests as if the papers inside might fly free if loosened for a moment. I fitted among them better than I had expected to. A woman of thirty-one, plainly dressed, carrying a cushion tied with string: nobody's daughter, nobody's driver, nobody's problem. I had spent six years being invisible on purpose. That morning, for the first time, invisibility felt like a tool rather than a wound.

Halloway's chambers were on the first floor above a stationer's shop, up a staircase that smelled of pipe smoke and India rubber. The brass plate said HOLLOWAY — read it twice, I told myself, Halloway with an *a*, the solicitor who wrote to me about my mother when she was eleven years dead — and beneath it, smaller: *Enquiries by appointment.*

I had no appointment.

I knocked anyway.

The door was opened by a young man with ink on three fingers and his collar slightly crooked, the sort of young man whose whole face is a question mark until he decides what you are. He looked at me. He looked at the cushion. He looked past me into the stairwell, which was empty, and something in his posture changed—not warmer, exactly. More awake.

"You're the railway one," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Mr. Halloway's out. There was a telephone call this morning from a correspondent in Berkshire—a Mrs.—" he consulted some internal ledger— "a party who said correspondence should come through her only for the present. And then a porter from Paddington telephoned an hour ago to a number he wasn't supposed to have, asking whether we expected a lady passenger." His eyes came back to mine. "So either you are expected and watched, or you are not expected and followed. Which is it?"

"Both," I said. "I'm Mirabel Ravensworth."

I had not said the name aloud to a stranger in years. It sat in the foggy little hallway between us like something set down on a table.

The young man—Finch, I would learn, Elias Finch, junior clerk, twenty-four years old, underpaid and precise as a watch mechanism—stood aside and let me in.

* * *

Halloway's private room was small and crowded, every surface stacked, one window giving onto a light-well where the fog pooled like milk. Finch cleared a chair without asking whether I wanted to sit, which I took as a form of respect. Then he locked the door, drew the blind over the window, and held out his hand for the cushion.

"First," I said. "Who is your correspondent in Berkshire?"

"A client. Or the widow of one." He did not blink. "Mr. Halloway acted for a Mrs. Eleanor Ravensworth's trustees before the war of paperwork swallowed everything. He has written four letters since January to an address in Berkshire asking after a Mirabel Ravensworth, born 1889, daughter of the above. Three came back unclaimed. The fourth—" he nodded at the cushion— "I take it the fourth found its way to the right hands eventually, even if by the wrong post."

"You knew someone was reading the mail?"

"I know that when letters go missing on a route, either the route is broken or someone is standing on it." He pulled a letter-knife from the desk. "May I?"

I set the cushion down and untied the string myself. Sixty miles I had carried the thing, sewn up with documents against my own hip the night before, and now that it came time to open it my fingers went clumsy as a girl's. Finch waited. He was good at waiting; I could see that already. It was going to be one of the things I liked about him.

The seam opened. Out came the oilcloth packet, and out of the oilcloth, in the fog-coloured light of that room, the papers I had taken from my uncle's townhouse while wearing another woman's apron:

The commitment order of Eleanor Ravensworth, dated the 14th of March, 1893. Two physicians' certificates, each certifying lunacy without ever having troubled to write more than a line of observation apiece. And the letter—the private letter—that had made me steal them, in which my uncle Gerald thanked a Mr. Croft for his "discretion in a family matter most delicately concluded."

Finch read all of it twice. He read the way Mrs. Tallow knitted: fast, without seeming to look, missing nothing. When he finished the second pass he laid the pages out on the desk in a row, edge to edge, and stood over them with his hands behind his back like a man looking down into a grave he had dug himself.

"Say what you came here to say," I said, because he was taking too long.

"I'm deciding how to put it." He glanced up. "You'll want it plain, I think. You've the look of someone who has been lied to politely for a long time."

"Plainly, then."

"The commitment order is forged." He tapped the paper without touching it. "Not irregular—forged. There are two kinds of bad document, Miss Ravensworth. The kind that bends the rules, and the kind that couldn't have existed at all in any lawful universe. This is the second kind. Lunacy commitments require two medical certificates from two physicians who have *examined* the patient. These certificates are dated within a day of each other, but they were written by different hands on different paper stock—one of these sheets has an 1894 watermark." A thin smile, the first. "Somebody ordered stationery in advance of the madness it documented. That happens more often than you'd think, which is why we can prove it: the mills kept records."

"And the signature?"

"Dr. Ambrose Pell died in 1891." He let that sit. "Two years before he certified your mother insane."

The fog pressed against the window. I heard myself breathe. Somewhere below in the light-well, a cat was complaining about the weather, absurdly, ordinarily, while the floor of the world rearranged itself under my feet.

"Then it could be undone," I said. "Tomorrow. You walk into a court with a dead man's signature and a watermarked certificate and—"

"No." Finch sat down at last, across from me, and folded his hands. "Now I must stop being the bearer of gifts. What you have proves the *commitment* was forged. That is a scandal, and if Mr. Gerald Ravensworth's name were attached to nothing else, it might ruin him. But it does not return one farthing, because the estate did not pass through the lunacy order alone. It passed through the will. Your grandfather's will of 1893 left Ravenmoor and the greater part of the personal estate in trust—to use his phrase—for 'all issue of my daughter Eleanor.' Gerald's version of events, filed and enrolled, is that there were no such issue living at probate. That you did not exist, or that your existence had been extinguished with your mother's. To break that, we need the *original* enrollment of the 1893 will—the registry copy as it was actually deposited, before someone paid to have the register amended."

"Amended," I repeated. "Can a register be amended?"

"A register can be amended the way a certificate can be forged: by men with money and no imagination. It leaves scars." He drew a sheet of blotting paper toward him and began, absently, to sketch on it—a square, a smaller square inside it, arrows. "Here is our difficulty. The registry copies of private settlements and wills from that period—the ones that would show the amendment, show the scar—are not all in London. Your grandfather, being the sort of man who trusted nobody, deposited a duplicate enrolment with his own stewards at the country seat. Ravenmoor. If the London register has been cleaned, the Ravenmoor copy will still carry the true text—and, more usefully, the steward's attestation, signed and dated, which no one thought to forge because no one imagined a steward's signature would matter."

"It matters."

"It is everything." He looked up. "And it is sitting in the archive of a house belonging to the man who has already shown, via a railway porter and a Berkshire widow, that he knows exactly where you are and what you are carrying."

* * *

I want to record, because it is the honest thing to record, that my first feeling was not fear. It was anger of so clean and cold a kind that it felt almost like happiness. All those years of driving through English counties with sealed envelopes on my lap, telling myself history happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms I would never enter—and here was a room, and I was in it, and the history was mine, and it had been stolen with a watermark and a dead man's name.

"How long," I said, "has Halloway known?"

Finch hesitated. It was the first hesitation he had shown, and I marked it the way you mark a loose stone on a staircase.

"My principal has known since January that something in the Ravensworth probate smelled wrong. He wrote to you because the trust deed named you, and because a solicitor's first duty is to find the beneficiary before the beneficiaries' enemies do. Whether he knew *everything*—" Finch spread his hands. "Mr. Halloway is seventy-three. He keeps his older clients' secrets in the same drawer as his teeth. But he also kept your mother's file, which is why the file exists, which is why I am talking to you instead of showing you the door."

"Your principal is not here."

"No."

"Then I am talking to a junior clerk."

"Yes." He didn't flinch. "Aged twenty-four, called nothing, paid worse than a governess, and the only person in these chambers today who knows what a 1894 watermark means. You may wait for Mr. Halloway to return Thursday. Or you may deal with me. I'd counsel dealing with me. Thursday is two days during which your uncle's solicitors can be doing arithmetic of their own."

Through the blind, the fog was lifting off the light-well; a rectangle of grey daylight crawled slowly up the wall. I thought of Adeline—I thought of her the way you think of a lit window when you're out in the rain, helplessly, gratefully, and with the beginning of dread. Adeline, whose father's name sat on the thank-you letter in my pocket like a coal. Not yet. I could not hold both truths in one hand yet. One fire at a time.

"Tell me about checkpoints," I said. "You said—no, you didn't say it, but your face did. Getting into Ravenmoor is not a matter of walking in."

"It isn't," Finch said. "Ravenmoor is a house party house. Gerald fills it with county families and London people twice a year—the next occasion, I understand, is in a fortnight, for his daughter's engagement. The gates are the first checkpoint, and they are trivial compared to what follows: a household staff he personally selected after the last scandal, an archive room with a new lock, and—" he paused— "a guest list that includes several gentlemen whose profession is being seen places. Witnesses, in both senses. You cannot slip into Ravenmoor. You can only *belong* there, briefly, under a false enough skin."

"And the second checkpoint?"

"That one is harder." He met my eyes. "Even if you reach the archive, a stolen register copy proves nothing. It becomes evidence only if it comes back here, is sworn, and is matched against the court's own records—which means it must survive the journey, and you must survive being the woman who took it. Your uncle has demonstrated he can buy porters at Paddington. At the estate gates he will not need to buy anyone. They will simply be his."

"So we need the copy, and we need a way for the copy to travel, and we need a person who cannot be bought standing behind it when it arrives."

"Yes." He smiled properly then, quick and rueful. "You see the shape of it already. I suspected you would. People who spend years reading other people's letters generally learn the shape of things."

I stood. My knees informed me that the train, the porter, the night of sewing needles had not been free, and I declined to listen to them. On the desk, the papers lay in their neat row: my mother, certified mad by a dead man, erased by amended ink, survived by a daughter who had just learned her own name again in a fog-bound room in Chancery Lane.

"Miss Ravensworth." Finch rose too, and gathered the papers, and rewrapped them in the oilcloth with a clerk's economy—every fold exact, every motion twice-checked. He held the packet out, then did not quite release it. "One thing more. Hope, in my trade, always comes with a price attached, and I'd rather name it now than have you discover it later. What we do next cannot be done quietly. Papers can travel in cushions. Affidavits require witnesses. Court orders require names, in daylight, on the record. Sooner or later—sooner, if my instinct is worth anything—you will have to stand up in a room full of strangers and say who you are and what was done to your mother, and from that moment you will never be invisible again. Not to your uncle. Not to the newspapers. Not—" he paused, delicately, "—to your friends in the movement, if any of them would rather this stayed quiet."

I thought of a drive through Richmond Park, Adeline laughing at something I'd said, the wind taking her hat. I thought of a letter thanking a Mr. Croft for his discretion.

"I stopped being invisible on Tuesday," I said. "A census talked me out of it. Take the packet or leave it, Mr. Finch—but I'm going to Ravenmoor."

"Not with that, you aren't." He drew the oilcloth bundle back and unlocked a drawer of the desk—deep, iron-bound, the kind of drawer that had swallowed more secrets than most confessor. "Duplicates stay in London. I'll make facsimiles tonight; you'll carry copies, worthless in law but useful for persuasion. The originals remain here, behind Mr. Halloway's best lock, where your uncle's money cannot reach and his porters cannot search."

"And if they come here?"

Finch pushed the drawer shut. The lock turned with a small, final sound, like a full stop.

"Then," he said, "we shall find out very quickly whether Chancery Lane is still made of anything besides paper."

He walked me down. On the stairs he gave me an address—a stationer's near the Strand, ask for Mr. Finch, say it concerns watermarks—and I gave him the date of Gerald's house party, gleaned from an invitation I had steamed open eleven months ago and never, until this week, understood.

The fog had thinned to a pale wash. Down at the corner of the lane, a newsboy was shouting something about the Parliament, and two men in dark coats stood a little too long outside the stationer's opposite, and neither of them bought anything.

Finch saw them too. He adjusted his collar, became instantly, perfectly ordinary, and murmured, for me alone:

"A fortnight. Two checkpoints. Don't take the direct train."

Then he touched his hat, and went back up the stairs, and I stepped into the street with a cushion full of nothing, carrying everything that mattered in my head.
