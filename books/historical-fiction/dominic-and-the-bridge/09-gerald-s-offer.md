## 9. Gerald's Offer

There was a particular pleasure Gerald Ravensworth took in the early hour—the city not yet awake, the coal smoke lying blue over the rooftops, his club's morning room empty except for him and The Times. At fifty-eight he had earned his mornings. He had earned everything, if one counted the cost honestly, and Gerald made a point of never counting it at all.

He was on his second cup of coffee when the letter came.

It was not the letter itself that unsettled him—letters arrived by the dozen—but the hand on the envelope. A clerk's hand, careful and anonymous, from a firm of inquiry agents he had retained in March and half forgotten. *Re: the girl.* That was all the covering note said. The girl. As though Mirabel Ravensworth, twenty-two years old, driver of motor cars and reader of other people's post, could be reduced to a phrase in a report.

He read the report standing up. Then he sat down, then stood again, which annoyed him, because a man who had faced down two bank crises and one very nearly fatal scandal involving a racehorse ought to be able to read a page of paper without his legs taking a vote.

The substance was simple. Miss Mirabel Ravensworth had called at the offices of Halloway & Finch, Chancery Lane, posing—it was supposed—as a cleaner seeking references for previous employment. She had asked questions about files dated 1893. She had given the name Tallow as a character reference, and Mrs. Tallow, née's nursemaid's widow, whatever she was, had been traced to a cottage in Camden where she received callers on Thursdays.

Gerald folded the report along its crease, once, precisely.

So. The girl knew.

He had wondered, these past weeks, watching her at Adeline's house—that rigid set of her shoulders when he entered a room, the way her eyes went to his hands rather than his face, as though hands were what did things in this world. He had told himself it was the ordinary resentment of the poor kin toward the comfortable ones. Every family had its Mirabels: cousins once removed with claims they didn't know how to press and pride that kept them from trying.

But she wasn't pressing a claim. She was digging up a grave.

His coffee had gone cold. He poured it out the window into the area below—a habit his wife had scolded him for thirty years ago and had long since stopped noticing—and rang for his hat.

* * *

Finding her was not difficult. That was the thing about the girl nobody seemed to have remarked upon: she was always somewhere visible. A woman who drove a motor car through the streets of London wearing goggles and a dust coat, ferrying suffragists to their rallies, could no more disappear than St Paul's could. If you wanted to find Mirabel, you found the movement, and if you wanted to find the movement, you followed the noise.

He caught up with her on the Thursday, at the garage off the Euston Road where the Croft committee kept their battered Humber. She was alone under the bonnet, sleeves rolled, a smear of grease along one forearm like a brushstroke, and for a moment—one unguarded moment—Gerald saw Eleanor in the line of her neck. His sister, bent over the same angle over her books at Ashcourt thirty-five years ago, before the family had decided that books were bad for her health.

He put the thought away the way he put away all inconvenient thoughts: carefully, in the dark, face down.

"Miss Ravensworth," he said.

She came up fast, wrench in hand, and he watched her register three things in order: who he was, that they were alone, and that the garage door at her back opened onto a yard with two exits and neither of them quick enough to matter. Good. He wanted her thinking clearly, but cornered-clearly, which was a different instrument altogether.

"Mr. Ravensworth." She did not wipe her hand before extending it, which was either insolence or a test, so he shook it, grease and all. Her grip was stronger than his wife's. Stronger than some men's. "You've come to donate to the cause. I can fetch a collection tin."

"I've come to talk."

"How novel. Most of your kind telegrams."

*Your kind.* He let that sit. Anger was useful, but only his own, rationed like brandy. "Somewhere with chairs," he said. "And tea I'd be willing to drink."

"There's a Lyons around the corner. It's full of clerks and they listen to everything."

"Perfect," said Gerald. "Clerks who listen are clerks who can be quoted later. Come along."

* * *

She chose a table against the wall with her back to the bricks—old habit, or new training; the movement taught its women to mind sightlines—and ordered nothing, which told him she expected this to be short. He ordered tea for himself anyway, and waited until the waitress had gone, and laid his cards on the table one at a time, because he had learned long ago that cards played all at once looked like panic.

"You visited Chancery Lane," he said. "On Tuesday. In a grey coat, telling a junior clerk your name was Smith."

He had expected her to flinch. She didn't flinch; she went still, which was worse, and which reminded him unpleasantly that this creature spent her days carrying secrets worth hanging over and delivering them intact. Stillness was her professional condition.

"And if I did?"

"Then we understand each other, and I haven't wasted a morning." He stirred his tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "I'm going to make you an offer. Before I do, I want you to hear me fully, because you will be tempted to mistake this for a threat, and it is not a threat. Threats are for people whose opposition costs less than their compliance. You, Mirabel, are not worth threatening. You are worth buying."

"That's flattering."

"It's arithmetic." He set down the spoon. "Five hundred pounds. Paid in consols, in your own name, tomorrow if you want it that way. Plus passage to Toronto, third class upgraded to second—I'm not a monster—and a letter of introduction to a firm out there that places English girls in respectable situations. Governesses, companions, ladies' secretaries. You'd take the boat inside a fortnight and you would write to no one in England ever again."

Silence. Behind them a clerk at the next table laughed at something in a newspaper, and the laugh sounded enormous.

"Or," Mirabel said slowly, "I refuse, and you do what? Have me followed? Followed is old news, Uncle Gerald. I believe your man and I have already been introduced, though he doesn't know I noticed. You'll need better than a follower. You'll need a lunacy order, won't you." Her voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep, tectonic. "That seems to be the family remedy. One signature, two witnesses, and an inconvenient woman vanishes into a corridor with locked doors at both ends. It worked on my mother."

There it was. Out in the open, in a Lyons' in broad daylight, over untouched tea.

Gerald had prepared for rage. Rage he could manage; rage was weather, and he had shelter. What he had not prepared for was how much it cost him to hear Eleanor spoken of as *my mother*, in that flat, unforgiving tone, by a daughter who had been nine years old at the funeral and had stood at the graveside in a coat two sizes too big.

"Eleanor was ill," he said.

"Don't." Quiet. Terrible. "Don't you dare give me the word *ill*. I've seen the papers. I've held them. I know what 'ill' meant in 1893 and who signed what and whose name was on the witness line, and if you say *ill* to me one more time I will stand up in this café and repeat every word of it at a volume the clerks will enjoy."

He believed her. That was the trouble with the modern girl—you couldn't call a bluff she hadn't agreed to hold.

He changed instruments. This had always been his gift: knowing when the violin wouldn't carry and reaching for the drum.

"You imagine," he said, "that you have discovered something. A secret, hidden away, known to a guilty few. Let me tell you what you've actually discovered. You've discovered that everyone already knows—or knew, or guessed—and chose, sensibly, to let it lie. There is no conspiracy, Mirabel. There is only a consensus. Do you know why the papers were never challenged? Because challenging them would have ruined four families and benefited one dead woman. Because the money built hospitals and endowed scholarships and kept two hundred people in employment at Ashcourt. Because what's done is done, and the past is a country it is expensive to invade." He leaned forward. "And because your friends know."

Her eyes didn't move from his face, but he saw the words land. Saw them land exactly where he aimed them.

"Adeline Croft," Gerald said softly, "has known since 1906."

"That's a lie."

"Is it? Think. Think hard, since you're so devoted to evidence. Where does the movement's money come from? Who bailed the WSPU out in the winter of '07 when Christabel was in prison and the bailiffs were at the door? Whose cheque has paid for every motor car that woman has driven since motor cars came within her reach?" He spread his hands. "I am the movement's banker, my dear. Adeline and I have an arrangement older than your employment. She asked me once—1906, at a garden party at Richmond, I remember it because it rained—she asked me, quietly, over the strawberries, whether the stories about Ashcourt were true. About your mother. And I told her they were regrettable, and she said—" he paused, savouring it, hating himself for savouring it—"she said, *then it's fortunate the cause has more urgent injustices to attend to.* Those were her words. More urgent. You may verify the date with anyone who was on that lawn."

"You're lying now," Mirabel said, but her voice had changed. He had heard that change before, in boardrooms, in the moment a director realizes the accounts will not bear inspection. The sound of a person auditing their own memory and finding entries they cannot explain away.

Every kindness Adeline had shown the girl. Every confidence. Three years of Sunday dinners and taken-into-trust and *Mirabel, dear, you drive wonderfully* —all of it suddenly legible in a new hand. Gerald watched the reinterpretation happen behind her eyes and felt something he refused to name, because naming it might have been remorse and he had no budget for remorse.

"She uses you," he said. "You read her letters, don't you. Don't bother denying it; a woman who opens sealed correspondence for the cause tells herself it's devotion, and Adeline has known about that too, by the way, for at least a year. Why do you think she lets you? A driver who reads is a liability. A driver who reads and says nothing is an asset. She has never corrected you because you are useful twice over." He sat back. "You are surrounded by people, Mirabel, who have all decided you are small enough to use and loyal enough not to notice. I am the only one offering you the truth and five hundred pounds in the same breath."

The tea had gone cold in front of him. He drank it anyway.

"The offer stands fourteen days," he said. "After that the terms worsen, and I promise you, the worsening will not look like a threat. It will look like misfortune. Girls who drive motor cars have accidents. Cleaners who wander into offices have their characters blackened. Nurses in Camden cottages—" he saw her whole body go rigid at that, and noted it, filed it, *the nurse matters to her, why does the nurse matter* —"nurses come to unfortunate ends, or get moved to establishments where visitors are discouraged. None of it will touch me. That is the entire point of being me."

Mirabel stood. She stood the way soldiers stand up out of trenches, he thought—all at once, braced for what comes next.

"My mother," she said, "sat in that asylum for eleven years writing letters nobody posted, and every single person who knew—including, apparently, my sainted employer—decided she was a price worth paying. So here is my counter-offer, Uncle Gerald. Take your five hundred pounds and your consensus and your urgent injustices, and spend the fourteen days deciding where you'd like to be standing when it all comes down. Because I am going to finish what I started, and I am not going to Canada, and I am not going to be small." She pulled on her driving gloves, finger by finger, unhurried, and the smear of grease on her forearm caught the light. "You should know one more thing. You've just told me Adeline knew. Which means I can't trust her, and I can't trust you, and I can't trust anyone I've trusted for three years. Do you understand what you've done? You've made me free."

She walked out. The door swung behind her, and through the glass he watched her cross the pavement to the Humber, crank it herself, and pull out into the traffic with the particular competence of a woman who had decided that competence was all the family she required.

Gerald sat for a long while among the clerks.

He had come intending to buy her, and he had, in a sense, succeeded—he had purchased her distrust of everyone else. But a cold thought had lodged itself under his ribs, the kind of thought that grew. A girl with nothing left to lose, who knew the shape of the forgery, who had already reached Chancery Lane once, and who had just announced her freedom in a café full of eavesdropping clerks—

She hadn't gone to Chancery Lane for the 1893 papers, he realized. Those were copies; copies could be contested. She had gone looking for the enrollment. The registry. And if the registry copy differed from the family's version—if some conscientious clerk forty years of quiet had buried the discrepancy instead of destroying it—

Gerald signalled the waitress and ordered brandy, though it was barely noon.

Then he put on his hat and went to find out what, exactly, was filed under Ravensworth in the rolls of the Court of Chancery—and to move the nurse first.
