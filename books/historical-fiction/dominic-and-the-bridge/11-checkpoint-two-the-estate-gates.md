## 11. Checkpoint Two: The Estate Gates

Mrs. Tallow arrived at the lodger's door in Ilkley with a carpet bag, a hatpin like a fencing foil, and the expression of a woman who had buried three husbands and outlived every one of them on purpose.

"You look terrible," she said, looking me over. "Good. Nobody suspects a woman who looks terrible. They pity her, and pity opens doors faster than money."

"You said you knew the house."

"I said I'd been in it." She set the carpet bag down and began unpacking it with the brisk economy of a field surgeon. "Fourteen years, before your mother married. I was under-housekeeper at Ashdene before your uncle turned it into a place for showing off motorcars to men who can't drive them. I know which doors stick, which servants talk, and which of the family drink before noon." She looked up. "I know where the archive is, too. Old library, east wing, behind the shelving your grandfather had built to hide the family papers from the tax man. Pride and concealment, the two great Ravensworth virtues."

"And the house party?"

"Three days. Shooting, mostly. Gerald's way of collecting debts—he invites men who owe him and lets the cold finish the job. There'll be forty guests and twice as many hired staff, all strangers to each other. Nobody questions a new face when there are too many faces already." She held up a folded paper. "I've written to the housekeeper at Ashdene—Mrs. Pryce was my junior, and she owes me a favor older than you are. You'll be on the list as temporary staff, engaged for the party. A parlourmaid. Name of—"

She paused, and I understood she was waiting for me to say it.

"Mirabel Shaw," I said. My mother's name before she was a Ravensworth. Before she was anything the family allowed her to be.

Mrs. Tallow's eyes softened for exactly one second, which for her was a public display of weeping. "Fitting," she said. "Now. The archive is locked, but the key lives in the housekeeper's room, in a drawer with the spare keys to the wine cellar, because your uncle thinks nobody would ever look for a library key among the claret. Mrs. Pryce will leave the drawer unlocked on the second night, when the party's at its noisiest. You'll have perhaps two hours. The register copy Halloway needs is a leather-bound volume, marbled edges, the year 1893 stamped on the spine. You'll know it when you see it."

"And if I'm caught?"

"Then you're a thief and a liar, and I'm an old woman who hired a stranger and was deceived." She said it without a flicker. "I've been a Ravensworth servant long enough to know how the family treats its own. A servant they'll simply dismiss. It's the family they destroy."

I thought of Gerald in the motor yard, his voice gentle as a hand on a shoulder: *Canada is a lovely country in the summer.* I thought of Ruth, hidden somewhere in a parish I hadn't yet found, waiting for me to come for her. I thought of my mother, whose face I had to work to remember, and hated myself a little for it.

"Then we'd better not be caught," I said.

* * *

Ashdene at night was a different house than Ashdene by day.

By day, arriving in the servants' cart among six other hired girls, I saw it as the guests were meant to see it: the long gravel sweep, the cedar on the lawn that had been old when Victoria was young, the windows throwing back the September light like a row of held breath. Gerald stood on the steps greeting his guests, and I kept my head down and my hands folded, one parlourmaid among many, invisible in the way I had spent years learning to be invisible. It should have been easy. It had been my profession, in a sense—reading letters no one thought a chauffeuse could read, standing behind Adeline's shoulder while history was made in other people's voices.

But invisibility, I was discovering, is a kind of exposure. To be unseen you must first make yourself nothing, and nothing can be looked through.

The house at night was all corridors and cooling stone, the party's noise reduced to a distant animal sound—the gramophone, the laughter that comes too easily after the fourth glass, a man singing badly and being applauded for it. I had changed out of my uniform into dark clothes that Mrs. Tallow had thoughtfully packed, and I moved through the service passages with a candle I didn't light, counting doors by the feel of the air.

The east wing smelled of cold leather and dust and, faintly, of apples—the orchard wall ran close beneath the windows. Mrs. Tallow's instructions had been exact: past the billiard room, through the door with the swollen frame that needed a shoulder, along the gallery where the Ravensworth portraits hung in their gilt and their certainty. I passed my great-grandfather and my great-uncles in the dark, their faces catching what little light came through the long windows, and I felt nothing. That frightened me more than the risk. I had expected to feel something—rage, grief, the pull of blood. Instead there was only the work, and the work was a door at the end of the gallery with a brass handle worn bright by a century of hands.

It was unlocked. Mrs. Pryce had earned her favor.

Inside, the old library was a cavern of shelving built to deceive, and behind it, as promised, the archive: a low room lined with tin boxes and ledger shelves, the family's memory filed and dust-sheeted like furniture in a dead man's house. I found the housekeeper's key ring in my pocket and tried the first lock. It turned. The second. Also.

I let myself breathe.

The ledgers ran along the east wall in chronological order, and I went to 1893 the way you go to a grave you have promised someone you'll visit—knowing what's there, dreading it, needing to see it anyway. My fingers walked the spines. *1891. 1892. 1893.*

The space where it should have been held a leather-bound volume with marbled edges, the year stamped in gold on the spine.

I pulled it out and opened it on the reading table, and the candle—I lit it now, there was no help for it—showed me within ten seconds that I was holding 1894.

The 1893 volume was gone.

I told myself it was misfiled. I went through the shelf twice, then the whole wall, then the tin boxes marked *Legal*, then the boxes marked *Estate*, then, in a kind of madness, every box in the room, my hands faster and less careful, dust rising in the candlelight like the house itself exhaling. Nothing. The register for 1893 had been lifted out of its sequence with the precision of a surgeon taking a tumor—no gaps in the shelving, no disturbed dust, nothing to show it had ever stood there at all except the absence, which I could feel the way you feel a tooth missing.

He knew.

He had known since the motor yard, or before it—since the railway porter, since Halloway's first letter, perhaps since the census taker with his too-many questions, that patient, professional curiosity that I had mistaken for bureaucratic diligence. Every step I had taken, he had walked ahead of me and tidied the room. The porter at the station hadn't been searching my luggage; he had been confirming my departure. Gerald hadn't been trying to stop me from reaching London. He had been letting me run, so that he could close each door behind me and watch me find it shut.

I sat down on the reading table's chair in the dark of my family's archive, among the records of everything my family had ever owned or owed or buried, and I put my face in my hands.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above me, the party laughed at something I would never hear.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the candle had burned down to a stub and my eyes had adjusted to the dark enough to see the shapes of the shelves, which is, I think, the only reason I noticed it.

A box on the legal shelf, third from the top, sat slightly forward of its neighbors. Not enough for anyone but a woman who had just handled every box in the room. I took it down. Inside: conveyances, a marriage settlement, a bundle of correspondence tied in ribbon gone brittle as straw—and at the bottom, a single loose sheet, folded twice, in a hand I knew.

Not Gerald's. Not my grandfather's.

My mother's.

Eleanor Ravensworth's writing—Eleanor Shaw's writing, small and upright, each letter formed with the care of a girl taught penmanship by someone who believed neatness was a moral quality. I had seen it once, on the flyleaf of her Bible, which Mrs. Tallow had kept for me all these years. I knew it the way you know a voice heard through a wall.

It was dated March 1891. Two years before the will. Two years before the asylum.

*I have signed what they put before me because they tell me it is the only way to keep Mirabel with me. I do not understand half of it. I am writing this and hiding it here because I do not know who else to remember it. If I am ever not myself, it will be because they have made me so. E.R.*

The paper trembled in my hands, and I understood that the trembling was mine.

She had known. Not afterward, not in the fog of the laudanum they gave her—before. She had sat in this house, in this wing, perhaps in this room, and understood what was being assembled around her, and she had done the only thing available to a woman with no standing and no allies: she had written it down and hidden it in the family's own memory, trusting the paper to outlast their patience.

They had taken the register. They had not thought to search for a dead woman's hand in a box of conveyances, because to them she had never been a person, only a problem, and problems are filed, not remembered.

I folded the letter small and put it inside my bodice, against my heart, which was the nearest thing to a grave she had.

Then I put out the candle, and in the dark I heard, very distinctly, the gallery door at the end of the corridor close.

Not slam. Close—the careful, controlled sound of a door handled by someone who did not want to be heard, which meant someone who knew I was here, and did not want the party to know it.

Footsteps. Unhurried. Coming along the gallery past the portraits, past my great-grandfather's gilt certainty, toward the one door in the house that stood between me and being caught.

Mrs. Tallow had told me the swollen door needed a shoulder. I gave it mine, and ran.
