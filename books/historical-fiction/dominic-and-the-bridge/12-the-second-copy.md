## 12. The Second Copy

Elias Finch had been a junior clerk in Halloway's chambers for eleven years, and in that time he had learned three things about the law. First, that it moved at the speed of treacle. Second, that the men who shouted loudest in the corridors of Chancery Lane were usually the ones with the weakest cases. And third — the lesson he had learned the hard way, at the cost of a blistering reprimand in his second year — that the truth about any document lived not in the document itself but in its neighbours: the papers filed before it, the papers filed after it, the small clerical ghosts that no one thought to burn because no one thought of them at all.

He was thinking about ghosts on the morning of the fourth of June, 1911, because the room smelled of dust and old vellum, and because the woman who had come to them three days ago with a cushion full of stolen papers was still sitting in his head like a splinter.

Her name was Mirabel Ravensworth. She had arrived at the chambers in a plain grey coat with rain on her shoulders and a look in her eyes that Finch had seen perhaps four times in eleven years — the look of a person who had stopped being afraid of the consequences and started being afraid only of failing. She had laid the documents out on Mr. Halloway's baize with hands that barely shook, and she had said, in a voice like a woman reading a shopping list: *My mother was declared insane so my uncles could take her inheritance. I want it undone.*

Halloway had examined the commitment order, the letters of administration, the correspondence between the brothers and their solicitors, and had pronounced, with the gravity he reserved for good news: *The forgery is provable. The lunacy commission of 1893 was signed by a witness who, by the records of this office, was in Calcutta on the date of signature.*

It should have been enough. It was not enough.

Because Gerald Ravensworth was not a stupid man, and stupid men do not hold estates for eighteen years on forged foundations. Somewhere there was a true copy of the 1893 will — the original enrollment, the version Eleanor Ravensworth had actually signed — and Finch would have wagered his salary that Gerald had spent the past week making sure it was the only version left in the world.

* * *

The chambers of Halloway & Son occupied the second and third floors of a narrow building on Chancery Lane, and the third floor was Finch's kingdom: the register copies, the enrollment books, the decades of filed memoranda that no barrister ever asked to see because barristers believed, with a faith that bordered on the religious, that everything important had already been extracted and summarized by someone like him.

On the fourth of June, a Tuesday, he climbed the stairs at seven in the morning, an hour before the office boy arrived, and pulled down the enrollment registers for 1893.

The system was simple and, like most simple things, almost perfectly neglected. When a will was proved, a copy was enrolled. When a will was amended, contested, or re-enrolled, a further copy was filed. And when — as happened more often than the Inland Revenue would have liked — a will was enrolled twice, the second enrollment was supposed to supersede the first, and the first was supposed to be marked as superseded, and the marking was supposed to be checked, and the checking was supposed to be checked, and in practice the whole apparatus rested on the diligence of clerks, and clerks were mortal.

Finch found the 1893 enrollment for the Ravensworth estate by half past eight.

It was exactly what Halloway had predicted Gerald's version would be: the estate settled in trust to the brothers, Edmund and Gerald Ravensworth, with provision for the testator's sister Eleanor's *maintenance* — that word doing its quiet, monstrous work, maintenance being what you gave to a person whose judgment you had already removed. Dated the fourteenth of March, 1893. Signed, witnessed, enrolled.

Finch sat back and rubbed his eyes. If this was all there was, then the papers in the cushion were a dead end. The commitment order proved the lunacy proceedings were forged, yes — but forged to what end? The brothers' counsel would say the will was genuine, that poor Eleanor had been ill, that the family had acted from compassion, and the maintenance provision would sit there in black ink like a saint's alms, and the whole case would collapse into a sad story about a sick woman and her dutiful brothers.

Unless.

He had learned the third lesson about documents the hard way, and he applied it now with the particular tenderness of a man avenging an old wound. He did not close the register. He turned the pages backward, and then forward, and then — because the enrollment books for 1893 were bound in two volumes, and the second volume covered enrollments filed late, corrections, and *registrations of instruments enrolled out of time* — he pulled down Volume Two.

And there it was.

* * *

It was a thin entry. Three lines. It would have been easy to miss, and it had plainly been missed for eighteen years, because it sat at the bottom of a page in a volume that recorded, for the most part, the dull administrative sediment of the Chancery system: late stamp duties, corrected spellings, a solicitor's apology for a misfiled affidavit.

But the entry was dated the ninth of January, 1893 — two months *before* the March will — and it recorded the enrollment of an instrument described as *the last will and testament of Edmund Ravensworth the elder, of Ravensworth Hall, deceased, together with a codicil of the said testator bearing upon the disposition of the estate to his daughter Eleanor.*

Eleanor. Not *maintenance.* Not *sisters and brothers jointly.* Eleanor, by name, with a disposition — a share, an outright bequest, a thing with weight and edges.

Finch read the entry four times. Then he went and fetched the cross-reference ledger, and then the fee book, and then, at half past ten, he climbed down to the second floor and knocked on Halloway's door with a knuckle that was not entirely steady.

* * *

Halloway was sixty-three years old and had the temperament of a man who had spent four decades being right in rooms where nobody wanted him to be. He read the entry twice, put on his spectacles, read it a third time, and then did something Finch had seen him do only twice before: he took off his spectacles and sat in silence for a full minute.

"You understand what this is," Halloway said at last.

"An older enrollment. January 1893. A codicil in Eleanor's favour, enrolled before the March will was ever proved."

"Which means the March will — Gerald's will — either supersedes it lawfully, in which case the codicil is dead paper, or it does not, in which case the whole edifice of the estate since 1893 has been built on a suppressed instrument." Halloway tapped the page. "And here is the beauty of it, Finch, and the horror of it. The codicil is *enrolled*. It is in the public register. It has been in the public register for eighteen years. Gerald Ravensworth could not burn it, because he does not know it exists — or, if he knows, he cannot reach it, because this register is not his to reach into. It is mine. Ours. The Crown's."

Finch felt the thing he always felt at such moments, the cold bright lift of it, the sense of standing at the edge of a well and hearing, far down, the sound of water. "Then we file. We apply to inspect the enrolled instrument, we obtain a certified copy, and we—"

"No," said Halloway.

The word dropped into the room like a stone.

"No," Halloway repeated, "because I have already asked. Yesterday evening, after your Miss Ravensworth left. I put the application in myself, under my own hand." He paused. "It was refused this morning. Refused pending resolution of a *disputed question of custody* — which is Chancery for *someone with better lawyers than yours has telephoned the registrar*."

Finch stared at him. "Gerald knows."

"Gerald's solicitors know that we know *something*. They do not know what. But they have moved to seal the register pending a formal application, and a formal application requires—" Halloway's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "A court order. And a court order requires an application on affidavit. And an affidavit requires—"

"A witness," Finch said slowly. The water at the bottom of the well had gone quiet. "Someone who can swear, on oath and in the open, to the facts. To the circumstances of the 1893 enrollment. To the codicil's existence and execution."

"To the *forgery*," Halloway said. "Because that is what it will come to, Finch, and do not let us pretend otherwise. A court will not overturn eighteen years of possession on a register entry alone. It will want a living person who can stand in the box and say: *I saw the codicil signed. I saw the March will made in its place. I know why.*" He laid his hand flat on the ledger. "The evidence must be a person now. Not a paper. Papers can be sealed, stolen, burned. A person—" he paused, and Finch watched the old man weigh the words and find them true — "a person can be frightened. But a person can also be found."

* * *

Finch spent the afternoon doing what he did best, which was the unglamorous arithmetic of other people's lives. He pulled the probate file for Edmund Ravensworth the elder, dead these eighteen years. He pulled the file on the lunacy commission of 1893 — the forged one, the one with the witness who had been in Calcutta. He made lists.

The witnesses to the codicil, per the enrollment entry: a solicitor's clerk named Prewitt, dead, per the Law Society's records of 1904. And a name that made Finch stop with his pen above the page:

*Ruth Amble.*

Not Calcutta. Not the Calcutta name on the forged commission — that had been a different witness entirely, a doctor, a man bought for a morning's work. Ruth Amble was something else. She appeared again, three months after the enrollment, in the records of the Ravensworth household as a *nurse, engaged for the care of Mrs. Eleanor Ravensworth* — engaged, Finch noted, in April of 1893, one month after the March will was proved and the codicil buried.

Engaged to watch her.

The thought settled over Finch with the weight of cold water. They had enrolled the codicil lawfully — the old man's solicitor had done his duty, filed it, done everything right — and then the brothers had simply waited, and forged, and buried, and hired a nurse to sit with the one person who knew there had been another paper. And the nurse had stayed. For how long? He turned pages. Years. Ruth Amble, nurse, appears in the household accounts until 1899, and then she vanishes — no reference, no pension, no letter of character. Vanishes the way servants vanish, which is to say: the way people vanish when someone powerful wants them gone.

Finch sat in the lamplight of the third floor and looked at his lists, and understood the shape of what he had found, and did not like it.

The case now rested on a woman who had been employed to guard a prisoner and had perhaps, in eighteen years of silence, made her peace with it. A woman who would have to stand in a courtroom and say aloud: *I watched them do it, and I said nothing.* He had been a clerk long enough to know that such testimonies were the hardest to win and the most expensive to buy — not with money, but with something rarer. With the promise that the silence had not been for nothing.

He copied the enrollment entry by hand, twice, and locked one copy in the chambers safe and put the other inside his waistcoat, against his chest, where he could feel it when he breathed.

Then he went downstairs to write to Miss Mirabel Ravensworth, care of the suffrage rooms in Bloomsbury, and found Halloway standing at the bottom of the stairs with his hat already on.

"You'll tell her in person," the old man said. "Not in a letter. Letters can be read." He settled the hat with two fingers. "And tell her this, Finch, and tell her exactly: we no longer need her to find a paper. We need her to find a person. The nurse. Ruth Amble. And she should be quick about it — because if the brothers have discovered the codicil's existence, they will have reached the same arithmetic we have. And a man like Gerald Ravensworth does not leave a witness standing."

Finch walked out into Chancery Lane at dusk, the copy warm against his ribs, and thought about the well, and the water, and the long climb down that was about to begin.
