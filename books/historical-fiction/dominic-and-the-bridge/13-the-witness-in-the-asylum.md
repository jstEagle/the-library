## 13. The Witness in the Asylum

The name arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a note from Finch that smelled of lamp oil and haste: *Ruth Amble. Formerly nurse, Stonebridge Lunatic Asylum. Last known address, a cousin's house in Bermondsey — 1904.*

Nine years gone.

I sat in the motor outside the library at Kensington and read it four times, because the arithmetic would not settle. Nine years. My mother had died in 1896. Ruth Amble had watched her committed in '93 and then she had walked out of that story and kept walking for fifteen years, and I was meant to find her before two brothers' worth of money found her first.

Finch's note ended with a line underlined twice: *If we reach her after they do, there will be nothing to reach.*

I put the note in my glove — I had taken to keeping everything that mattered against my own skin now, papers and names and fears, all sewn into the lining of my life the way the documents had been sewn into that cushion — and I drove to Bermondsey.

* * *

Bermondsey in February was a study in brown. Brown river, brown fog, brown-faced men trudging home from the tanneries with their collars up. The cousin's house on Corbett Street had been split into lodgings; the landlady, a woman with ink-stained fingers and the flat patience of someone who had heard every kind of question, told me Ruth Amble had left in 1905 owing three weeks' rent.

"Left where?"

"That's the thing." She looked me over — my plain coat, my driving gloves, my accent, which I had learned to flatten when I needed to. "She didn't leave like a woman going somewhere. She left like a woman being erased. Bit at a time. First she gave up the front room, then the back room, then she was sleeping in the kitchen, and then one morning the kitchen was empty too."

"Family?"

"A brother, I think. Or no one. She used to say—" The landlady paused, choosing. "She used to say she'd been a witness once and it had cost her the price of a house. I never knew what she meant. She drank a bit, toward the end. Women who drink are hiding something or grieving something, and she wasn't the hiding sort."

Parish records were not hard to come by when you knew how to ask, and Adeline had taught me how to ask — how to stand in a vestry doorway looking official and slightly impatient, how to let a curate's own terror of bureaucracy do the work. Ruth Amble surfaced in the relief books of three parishes, each entry sadder than the last: casual ward, casual ward, *medical ward — fits*, then a long blank gap, then, eighteen months ago, a name scratched into the register of St. Aldhelm's workhouse in Southwark, in the hand of someone writing quickly:

*Amble, R. — infirmary ward — refuses chapel — harmless.*

*Harmless.* As if harmlessness were the qualification for being allowed to exist.

* * *

St. Aldhelm's stood behind a brick wall the colour of old teeth, its windows too high to see out of, which I suppose was the point. A beadle took my card — I had cards now, printed with a fiction about parish visiting on behalf of a ladies' charitable society, which was at least half true — and led me down a corridor that smelled of carbolic and boiled cabbage and underneath both, faintly, of despair.

"She's in the third alcove," he said. "But I'll tell you now, miss, you won't get much. She doesn't speak. Hasn't, properly, since the winter. Doctor says it's the age. I say—" he lowered his voice as if the walls might testify against him— "I say some people get talked at so long they forget anyone might want to hear them."

The infirmary ward was long and dim, thirty beds in two rows, most of them occupied by women asleep or pretending to sleep. In the third alcove sat a small woman upright against her pillows, hands folded on the blanket like folded linen, eyes open and fixed on the middle distance.

She must have been past sixty but she looked older, the way the very poor do — worn down to essentials. Her hair was white and cropped short, prison-fashion or hospital-fashion, and her wrists above the grey blanket were thin as a child's.

"Mrs. Amble?" I said.

Nothing.

"Ruth Amble. Of Stonebridge."

At *Stonebridge* something happened. Not much. A flicker at the corner of her mouth, a small contraction of the hands. But it was enough, and my heart went off like a starting pistol, because flickers can be worked with. Adeline had taught me that too, though she'd meant it for crowds.

I pulled the chair close and sat.

"My name is Mirabel Ravensworth," I said quietly. "My mother was Eleanor Ravensworth. She was a patient at Stonebridge between 1893 and 1896."

The middle-distance stare did not move. But the hands did — they turned over, palms up, an unconscious little gesture of supplication or surrender, and I thought: *she remembers the beds. She remembers turning patients over.*

"I've come a very long way," I said, "to ask you about her."

* * *

We sat through the whole of visiting hour without another word from her. I talked anyway — about the fog, about the motor car I'd left outside, about anything — and near the end I made a mistake that turned out not to be a mistake. I said, "They told me she was mad. They showed me the papers. And I have since learned the papers were lies, and I am going to prove it, and I need you to help me."

Her head turned.

It turned slowly, the way a door turns on a rusted hinge, and for the first time she looked at me directly, and her eyes were wet.

"You're Eleanor's girl," she said. Her voice was rust and disuse. "You have her chin."

"Yes," I said, though no one had ever told me which of my features was hers. I had spent my whole life not knowing. It was unbearable and wonderful to be identified by it.

"He wrote to me," Ruth whispered. "After. After she died. He wrote and said I was to forget what I'd signed, for my own good. And I forgot. I got very good at forgetting. You see how good."

"Who wrote? Mr. Gerald?"

"No." Her eyes closed. "The other one. Mr. Hugh. He came to my lodging with a man from the asylum and a paper already written, and he said, sign here, Ruth, and here, and it would be forty pounds and no questions asked, and I had a mother to keep, and—" The sentence collapsed into her chest. When she surfaced again she said, "I read it. Before I signed. I know they think I didn't, but I read it, and it said she was of unsound mind, dangerous to herself, and she wasn't. She was sad. She'd had a baby die before you, did you know that? Before you. And then your father died, and she was sad, and sad isn't mad, but there was no word for sad in those papers except madness, so I signed, and God has been collecting the debt ever since."

I could not speak for a moment. In the next bed an old woman muttered to the ceiling. Somewhere a trolley rattled.

"Ruth," I said at last. "There's a court case coming. There are lawyers, real ones, and they can overturn what was done to her. But they need a witness. They need the person who saw her, who knows she was not mad. They need you."

"No." It came out instantly, flatly, the way a burned hand withdraws. "No. I can't go into a courtroom. I can't say it out loud. I've not said it out loud in twenty years, and saying it—" she pressed her hands to her mouth— "saying it makes it true that I did it."

"It was true before you spoke."

"Then let it stay buried with her!" Her voice cracked upward and the beadle's head turned at the end of the ward. She dropped to a whisper, urgent, gripping my wrist with a strength that surprised me. "Listen. Listen to me, Eleanor's girl. Men came asking about me. Three weeks ago. Well-dressed man and another one taking notes. Asked the master if there was any Amble on the books. The master said no, because I'm entered under the matron's maiden name since the fever — they mix us up when we're moved, you see, the paperwork loses us — and the master genuinely didn't know. So they went away. But they'll come back. Paperwork always gets found. That's what I am now, do you understand? I'm not a person anymore. I'm paperwork that hasn't been found yet."

The words went through me like cold water. Because I had been the same — a name surfacing in a solicitor's letter, an anomaly in a census column, paperwork that somebody wanted found. Gerald's people had hunted me the same way, and only luck and Mrs. Tallow had kept me unfound. Ruth had no Mrs. Tallow. Ruth had a wall and high windows and a maiden name that wasn't hers.

"If they find me," she said, "they'll move me. There's a private place they use — the well-dressed ones always have a private place — and once you're in a private place, nobody visits, nobody asks, and whatever you were going to say in a courtroom comes out of you like water out of a cracked jug. Slowly, and then all at once, and then never."

I looked at her small hands still gripping my wrist, at the cropped hair, the folded linen calm of her, and I understood something Finch's notes had never managed to teach me.

For months I had thought of this as a matter of documents. Find the paper, carry the paper, deliver the paper. Papers were light. Papers could be sewn into cushions and talked past porters. I had imagined justice as a thing you smuggled.

But justice, it turned out, weighed eleven stone and had a bad heart and was terrified of closed carriages. Justice was a frightened old woman in a parish ward, entered under the wrong name, who had sold her signature for forty pounds in 1893 and had been paying it back at a pound a day ever since. You could not smuggle a person past three checkpoints in a cushion. You had to persuade her. You had to carry her. And carrying was a different craft entirely from smuggling — slower, gentler, and impossible to do alone.

"They'll come back for you," I said. "Soon. Days, maybe."

"Then I'd best be quick about dying," she said, with the terrible matter-of-factness of the very poor, and I gripped her hands back.

"No," I said. "You'd best be quick about living. Ruth — listen to me. I'm going to come back. Two days, perhaps three. When I come, I'll bring clothes and a story and someone I trust, and we'll get you out of here before they find the paperwork. But I need you to decide, now, while I'm sitting in front of you, whether when the time comes you'll sign the affidavit. Whether you'll say out loud what you read in that paper."

She was quiet a long time. The muttering woman in the next bed fell silent as if listening. Down the ward a nurse's skirts hissed past.

"I'm frightened of three things," Ruth said finally, counting them off on her fingers like a child reciting a lesson. "Courtrooms. Closed doors. And meeting her again, after. Your mother."

"And yet you'd do it?"

"I signed her away for forty pounds." Her chin came up, and for a moment under the ruin of her face I saw the young nurse she must have been — steady-eyed, the kind who stays when others go. "Forty pounds and a dead baby and a husband in the churchyard, that was my reasoning, and it seemed sound at the time, the way wickedness always does. If God gives me one hour of courage left in this body, I'd rather spend it on her than hoard it for myself." Her grip tightened. "But hear me, girl. If they take me first — if you come back and I'm gone — don't waste yourself hunting me. Go on without. Promise me. A witness lost is a grief; a case lost is her whole grave dug up again."

"I promise nothing of the kind," I said.

Something moved across her face that might have been the ghost of a smile. "No," she said. "I don't suppose you do. She was the same. Stubborn as a mule and twice as likely to bite." She settled back against the pillows, exhausted by so many words, and closed her eyes. "Come on the Thursday. Matron's day off. The evening porter drinks. Thursday."

I stood, and arranged my coat, and turned to go, and her voice caught me at the alcove's edge, barely audible:

"Eleanor's girl. Tell me one thing before you go. Did she ever—" the pause went on so long I turned back— "did anyone ever tell you what she called you? Her name for you? Because she had one. She'd say it at the window when I brushed her hair."

The ward tilted. Twenty-six years of not-knowing shifted on its foundations.

"What did she call me?" I asked.

Ruth smiled at the ceiling, eyes still shut, and said a single word — and I carried it out of St. Aldhelm's and down the fog-bound street and did not let go of it the whole way to the motor, holding it against my ribs with the rest of the evidence, warm as a coal.

Thursday. Two days. I had a refuge to arrange, clothes to find, and Mrs. Tallow to convince.

What I did not know — could not know, standing there in the brown fog with my mother's name for me hidden in my coat — was that even then a well-dressed man was showing a photograph around the vestry of a rectory seven miles east, asking after a small white-haired woman who answered, sometimes, to the name of Amble.
