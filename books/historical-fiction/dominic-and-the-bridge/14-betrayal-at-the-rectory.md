## 14. Betrayal at the Rectory

Ruth Penny had spent forty years learning to read the sound of footsteps.

In the asylum it had been a survival skill, the first thing they taught you without meaning to: the flat slap of the attendants' boots meant routine; the quick, light tread of a doctor meant questions; and the slow, deliberate pace of two men walking together in step meant someone was being taken somewhere. She had lain awake through thirty years of nights cataloguing footsteps, and so when she heard them coming up the rectory path on that grey Thursday morning—two men, unhurried, in no step at all, which was worse than marching because it meant they were confident—her body knew before her mind did.

She set down the teacup she'd been washing and stood very still in Mrs. Harkness's narrow kitchen, listening.

The rectory of St. Jude's-in-the-Marsh sat a mile outside the village, at the end of a lane that flooded each winter and dried into ruts each summer. It belonged to the Church, but the living was poor and the parish smaller every year, and the Reverend Mr. Selby had long since stopped asking awkward questions about the quiet widow his housekeeper had taken in as a favour to a cousin. Ruth paid nothing. Ruth asked nothing. That was the arrangement, and for eleven weeks it had held.

Eleven weeks. She had begun, God forgive her, to feel safe.

The knock came at the front door—not the kitchen door, where tradesmen knocked, but the front door, which nobody used. Three knocks, evenly spaced. A man who knocks like that has been told exactly how many times to knock.

Mrs. Harkness appeared in the kitchen doorway with a dishcloth still in her hand, her face already folding into the particular worry she wore whenever anything from the world beyond the lane arrived. "You're not to answer," she said. "Mr. Selby said if anyone came asking after you—"

"Then he knew," Ruth said quietly. "He said if anyone *came*. Which means he thought they might."

She heard the reverend's voice in the hall, pitched low and careful, and then another voice, smooth as poured cream, saying words she couldn't make out and didn't need to. There was only one category of visitor whose voice sounded like that in her experience: men who had been sent, and were accustomed to being obeyed when they arrived.

"Back door," Ruth said. Her legs felt like someone else's legs, old woman's legs, and she hated them for it. The garden gate gave onto a footpath across two fields to the workhouse road. If she could reach the hedgerow before—

But even as she moved toward it, the kitchen window darkened. A third man. They had covered the back. Of course they had covered the back; she would have covered the back herself, once, on the other side of this transaction.

*It ends here,* she thought, with a strange calm. *Thirty years late, but it ends here.*

* * *

They did not drag her. That was the thing people never understood about how it worked, then or now: there was almost never dragging. The man in the hall—tall, greying, with the soft hands of someone who had never done a day's labour and the patient eyes of a fisherman—introduced himself simply as Mr. Corben, and said he represented a private establishment that had taken an interest in her welfare, and that Dr. Lyle at Marlowe House had reviewed her circumstances and recommended a period of rest.

"What circumstances," Ruth said, "would those be."

"Your history, Mrs. Penny." He smiled gently. "Your confinement. Nineteen-ought-one, I believe. Ashbourne. It's all in your file."

Her file. Thirty years and they still had her file. She looked past him at the Reverend Selby, who was studying his own hands as though he'd found something written on them, and she saw that he had already agreed—that the conversation in the hall had not been a negotiation but a notification—and she understood, in one cold drop, how it had happened to Eleanor Ravensworth too. Not with violence. With paperwork. With reasonable men in good coats assuring other reasonable men that everything was entirely proper.

"I'm not unwell," Ruth said.

"No one says you are, Mrs. Penny." Corben's voice stayed kind, which was somehow the most frightening part. "Marlowe House is not Ashbourne. It's private, comfortable. Ladies take the waters. You'll have your own room."

Behind him, the second man shifted his weight by the door—a younger man, broad, hired for his shoulders rather than his manners—and Ruth read the whole architecture of the thing in that shift. Comfortable rooms. Own room. A door that locked from the outside.

Because she was the witness. Because she had signed her name on an order of lunacy in 1893 for a wage of eleven shillings a week and a reference letter, and somewhere in London a young woman was collecting the paper trail of that signature, and somewhere else a man named Gerald Ravensworth had decided that the signature should be kept where signatures could not testify.

They hadn't come for her mind. They'd come for her mouth.

"I'll need to pack," Ruth said.

"We've brought what you require," Corben said.

Of course they had.

* * *

They put her in a carriage—not an ambulance, which might draw notice, but a plain closed brougham with good horses—and the younger man sat opposite her with his hat on his knees, and Mr. Corben rode up beside the driver, and they went out along the flooded lane at a decorous pace, because everything about it must look like nothing at all.

Ruth watched the fields go by. The marsh beyond St. Jude's was silver under a thin sun, and the reeds stood in their thousands, and a heron lifted off from the ditch with that slow prehistoric effort of hers, and Ruth thought: *I am sixty-eight years old and I am going to disappear into one of those comfortable rooms and no one will ever find me, because I taught myself thirty years ago that the way to survive is to be where no one looks.*

She had survived Ashbourne by making herself small. Quiet hands, quiet mouth, never a favourite and never a target, moving through those corridors like furniture. It had worked. It had kept her alive while women around her went under—the ones who fought, the ones who wept, the ones who believed somebody was coming. Nobody came. She had learned that lesson so thoroughly it had become her spine: *nobody comes.*

And then, three weeks ago, a motor car had come down the workhouse road with a young woman at the wheel, and the young woman had walked into the laundry yard where Ruth was mangle-work, and had said, "You signed a certificate in 1893. For Eleanor Ravensworth." And before Ruth could deny it or faint or run, the girl had said the extraordinary thing, the thing that had turned Ruth's whole interior architecture upside down:

"I'm her daughter. And I'm not angry at you. I want to know what they made you sign."

Not angry. Ruth had braced for fury and received instead a question, and underneath the question something she had not heard directed at her in thirty years: the assumption that what Ruth had to say mattered.

"They told me she was violent," Ruth had said, in the end, sitting on an upturned crate in the yard with her mangle-hands in her lap. "They showed me her file before I met her. Violent tendencies, delusions, destruction of property. And then they took me in to see her, and she was—" Her voice had gone, briefly. "She was sitting by the window doing hemming, and she asked me very politely whether I thought the matron would let her have a Bible. Politest creature you ever saw. And I knew. I knew in five minutes that file was fiction. But I'd signed my name in the book downstairs before I ever laid eyes on her, because the doctor put the page in front of me and the brothers were standing there and one of them mentioned my sister's situation, she was in a similar place, and how it would go easier for everyone if the paperwork matched." She had looked up. "I've signed my name to a great many cowardices, miss. Hers was the worst."

Now, in the swaying brougham, Ruth counted the turns. Out of the lane, left onto the workhouse road—they were not taking her to the station, then; Marlowe House was south and east, over two counties—and she began, methodically, the way she had once memorized ward rounds, to build a map of what she could leave behind her. She had no money. But she had a tongue.

"Young man," she said to the shoulders opposite.

He glanced up, wary.

"If you've children," Ruth said, "you'll want to think about what's written against your name someday. I was an attendant thirty years. I know how files grow. Yours will say you assisted in the unlawful detention of a witness to fraud, if this goes wrong. And it will go wrong, because the young woman looking for me doesn't frighten."

"I don't know anything about any of that," the man said.

"No," Ruth agreed. "That's usually how it's done."

He looked away, and she marked it: the jaw working, the eyes fixed on the window. Not an ally. But a crack. She had spent her life finding cracks.

* * *

Mirabel reached the rectory at twenty past eleven, having driven the last four miles faster than any motor car had a right to travel a rutted lane, and she knew before she got down from the seat that she was too late. You could tell from a distance, she would think later, from the way the house stood. Houses that have been searched have a particular stillness, doors standing open onto emptiness, curtains askew, the whole building turned slightly inside out like a pocket.

Mrs. Harkness was in the garden, wringing the dishcloth as though it had personally offended her.

"Gone?" Mirabel said, though the word wasn't needed.

"Half an hour," Mrs. Harkness said. "Perhaps less. A brougham, plain, good horses. Mr. Corben, he called himself—he gave a card, but it's only a name on a card, isn't it, and Mr. Selby took it and won't say more than that the lady's welfare is provided for, which is what they *told* him, and he wanted to believe it, poor man, wanting to believe things is half the wickedness in the world." Her voice cracked. "They said she was going for rest. They had a trunk packed for her. Who packs a trunk for a person who's supposed to be choosing to go?"

Mirabel stood in the ransacked parlour. Drawers pulled out and set on the floor, upside down, neat as an inventory. Mattress stripped. The floorboards near the hearth pried at—one had been levered up badly and not replaced. They had been looking for something. Papers, presumably; whatever they feared Ruth had passed to Mirabel, or might yet.

But Ruth had passed everything on. Two days ago, in the workhouse laundry, wrapped in oilcloth inside a flour sack: her copy of the 1893 attendance ledger page, the original of her own affidavit draft, unsigned, and—the piece Mirabel had not dared hope for—a letter, dated October 1893, from Dr. Lyle himself to Gerald Ravensworth, discussing the fee and, in a sentence that had made Mirabel's hands shake on the train home, noting that "the certification was arranged to your specification and the attendant's signature secured as discussed."

Specification. As though Eleanor were a coat.

So they had not found what they came for. Which meant the brougham was not the end of it. Which meant wherever Ruth was going—to a room with a lock on the outside, to comfortable ladies taking the waters—the danger was not that she would be hidden, but that once hidden she could be made safe to destroy. Files grew. Diagnoses accumulated. A woman certified once could be certified again, deeper, further from help, until her testimony was legally worthless and practically unreachable.

"She trusted this place," Mirabel said. "I put her here."

Mrs. Harkness looked at her—at the mud on her boots, the motor goggles pushed up, the smell of petrol and fear—and something in the older woman's face softened. "You can't stand guard over a person every hour of their life, miss. Nobody can. That's how they do it—they wait for the hours."

"The workhouse women," Mirabel said. "Did anyone come from the village? Any of the laundry girls?"

"Not yet."

"Then I'll go to them." Mirabel was already turning. "If they inform me—if one of them saw the direction—"

"And what will you do?" Mrs. Harkness asked, and it wasn't unkind; it was the question of a woman who had watched powerful men walk out of her master's hall and had learned exactly how much weight names carried. "Follow a brougham across two counties?"

"No," Mirabel said. "I'm going to telephone."

* * *

The vicarage had the parish's only telephone, an instrument the Reverend Selby regarded much as he regarded the Book of Revelation—necessary, occasionally useful, and best approached with caution. He surrendered the hall to Mirabel without protest; whatever conscience was working in him had made him pliable.

The exchange at Market Drover took four minutes and two operators to connect her to the garage off Gray's Inn Road where Elias Finch kept, among other things, a second telephone and a talent for sounding like six different officials. When his voice came down the line, thin and crackling across the counties, Mirabel discovered her hands were steady and her voice was not.

"Finch. They've taken her. Corben, a card with just a name, a private carriage, heading—I don't know the direction yet, the laundry girls may have seen. South or east, I think, they didn't go toward the station."

A silence on the line. Then Finch, very level: "Corben. I know the name. He does removals for Marlowe House. Private asylum, near Harmondsworth. Miss Mirabel—listen to me. If she reaches intake, she becomes a patient. Certified on arrival, on papers that are already drawn, I promise you. After that, getting her out takes months and a magistrate, and Gerald owns better magistrates than we do."

"Then we get her out before intake."

"With what? You cannot storm a private asylum."

"No," Mirabel said, and looked out the vicarage window at the silver marsh, at the heron coming down again into the reeds, patient, exact. "I can't. But I know people who storm things. Bigger things than one carriage. Every Saturday, in every town in England, with banners."

There was a pause, and then she heard it in Finch's silence: the moment the plan stopped being hers alone and became real.

"The march," he said slowly. "The Harmondsworth route passes within a quarter mile of the Marlowe gates. Half of the movement owes Adeline Croft favours, and half of *those*"—he hesitated—"half of those don't know what Adeline knows."

"Then don't tell them what she knows," Mirabel said. "Tell them a sick old woman needs protecting from men who move people in trunks. Tell them the truth in the shape they'll fight for." She gripped the receiver. "Finch, they were half an hour ahead of me. I will not be half an hour behind again. Get me the march order. Get me Adeline's motor and Adeline's name, because whatever she is, whatever she's kept from me, no one in England mobilizes women faster than she does—and I am going to drive to her house right now and ask her to choose."

"And if she chooses Gerald?"

Mirabel watched the heron strike the water and come up with nothing, and try again.

"Then I'll know," she said, "exactly what I'm carrying into Harmondsworth. Goodbye, Finch."

She set down the receiver, thanked the Reverend Selby, who could not meet her eyes, and went out to the motor car, where the engine ticked as it cooled, faithful and ordinary in the grey morning. Behind her the rectory stood open and emptied, a pocket turned out; ahead of her lay ninety miles of road and one question wearing a human face.

She cranked the engine, took the wheel, and drove.
