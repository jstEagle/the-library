## 17. The Affidavit

We came into London by the early train, Ruth and I, before the city had finished rubbing its eyes.

She had never ridden third class before—she said so twice on the platform at Croydon, apologetically, as though poverty were a manners problem she could correct if only she tried harder. Her hands would not keep still. She folded and unfolded a handkerchief until it wore soft as cloth paper, and when the train lurched out of the station she gripped the seat's edge with both hands and looked, for all the world, like a woman being carried to her own trial.

"It isn't a courtroom," I told her. "It's a set of chambers. There'll be ink and a desk and a man who says thank you."

"I know what a set of chambers is, Miss Mirabel. I sat in enough of them in 1893, saying what I was told to say." She smiled then, a small ruined thing. "That's the trouble. I know exactly what they are."

I had no answer for that, so I gave her the truth instead, which is what I should have given her from the beginning. "You don't have to do this. Halloway can build a case on the register alone. It will be slower. It may fail. But you have spent forty years paying for one lie, and nobody—not me, not the law, not my mother's ghost—has the right to spend you again."

Ruth looked out the window a long while. Cottages gave way to terraces; terraces gave way to soot and girders and the backs of factories where the day shift was already filing in, women mostly, heads down against the wind.

"Do you know what I did after," she said, not really asking. "After they took your mother away and paid me off. I went into service in Richmond. I married a bootmaker. He died of his lungs in '99 and I went back into service, and every place I went I watched the ladies of the house, and I thought: which one of you would sign a paper like that? Which one of you, if your brother asked? And I never once thought about the woman on the other side of the door." She turned to me. "The signing won't undo it. I know that. But if I die in that workhouse with the truth still sitting quiet inside me, then I've kept their secret my whole life, and I won't. That's all it is. Not courage. Just—I won't."

The train ran on toward the smoke.

* * *

Halloway's chambers occupied the second floor of a narrow building off Chancery Lane, up a staircase that smelled of floor wax and cold stone, past a brass plate polished thin by decades of thumbs. Elias Finch met us at the landing. He had dressed for the occasion—a collar so stiff it looked capable of independent thought—and he bowed over Ruth's hand as though she were arriving at Ascot, which I could see startled her more than any roughness would have done.

"Miss Finch is expecting—" I began.

"Mr. Halloway is in conference with the instructing solicitor until nine," Finch said. "Which is to say he is at his club eating kippers, and will be here by half past. In the meantime—" he opened the inner door "—everything is prepared."

The room beyond held a long table, a window with grey light falling through it, and papers. Rows upon rows of them, squared and flagged and cross-referenced in Finch's small furious hand: the forged commitment order photographed and enlarged; comparisons of hands, Gerald's signature traced beside itself in red ink where the loops disagreed; affidavits from the parish registry; and at the centre, alone on its own folio, the copy of the 1893 will enrollment as it appeared in the Chancery register—the older record, the true one, the document Gerald had never known existed because he had never imagined the law keeps its own memory even when men pay it to forget.

Ruth stopped in the doorway.

"That's his writing," she said quietly. "Mr. Ravensworth. On the order. He signed it himself, in his own study, and I stood behind him and signed where his finger pointed, and I never read the whole page because I didn't think—" She pressed her lips together. "I was nineteen. You don't think at nineteen that a gentleman's finger is pointing you at perjury."

"Then we shall say precisely that," Finch said, pulling back her chair. "In those words, if you like. Juries believe plain speech far faster than they believe mine."

He laid the affidavit before her, clause by clause, and read each aloud in a voice pitched for a sickroom, patient and unhurried, and asked after each whether it was true and whether she wished it altered. Twice she corrected him—once on a date, once because he had written *was directed* where she wanted *was told*, and she would not yield on it. *Told*, she said. Directed was what happened to soldiers. Told was what happened to nurses.

And then there was nothing left but the pen.

Her hand shook so badly at first that the nib chattered against the blotter like a small animal. Finch said nothing. I said nothing. We let the silence be hers. And slowly, by some discipline I will never understand, she mastered it—not the shaking, which continued, but the fear underneath it—and she wrote *Ruth Ellery* across the foot of the page in letters that wavered and did not stop, and dated it, and looked up.

"Well," she said. "Forty years late."

"On the contrary," said a voice from the doorway. Halloway stood there in his coat still buttoned wrong, kipper-smelling and magnificent, reading the room in one sweep. "Punctual to the minute. The law has simply been slow catching up to you."

* * *

It was Finch, going to the window to adjust the blind against the strengthening light, who saw them first.

"Mr. Halloway," he said. "We have company."

Three men across the lane, standing beneath the plane tree opposite the building. They were not pretending otherwise—they were solicitors' men, you could tell by their boots and their bowsers' patience—but they were positioned one at the lane's mouth, one by the corner, one directly beneath our windows, and a fourth was climbing back into a waiting brougham, no doubt to report that the quarry was at home.

"Gerald's people," I said.

"His solicitors filed an application at ten o'clock last night," Halloway said, unbuttoning his coat with terrible calm. "An interim injunction, ex parte, alleging that certain documents now in these chambers were unlawfully removed from Mr. Ravensworth's townhouse and seeking their seizure pending determination of ownership." He smiled without warmth. "They were clever about the timing. Filed after the offices closed, served this morning. The bailiffs will have the order in hand within the hour."

"Can they take them?" My voice sounded far away to me. "The originals?"

"The injunction covers the documents. It does not—" and here Halloway raised his voice slightly, addressing the room at large as much as me "—cover a sworn affidavit, which is testimony, not property. Nor does it cover copies. Nor does it cover me." He crossed to the table and began sorting papers with quick, economical movements. "Finch. The application for the court order—we file it now, this morning, before their injunction is returnable. Once the order issues, the register copy becomes evidence in a matter properly before the court, and their little fishing expedition dies in committee. Get your hat."

"The filing office opens at ten," Finch said, already moving.

"Then we have fifty-seven minutes," said Halloway, consulting his watch, "to move a sworn witness, an original affidavit, and an application for a court order past three hired men and a barrister's clerk who has been paid to be inconvenient. Miss Ravensworth. How are you at driving?"

"In London?" Ruth said faintly.

"She means me," I said. "I'm excellent at driving. It's everyone else in London who worries me."

* * *

We went down the back stair, which was less a stair than a ladder with pretensions, emerging through the yard behind the building into Fetter Lane. Finch carried the affidavit inside his coat, flat against his chest, and Ruth walked between us holding my arm like any niece escorting an aunt to morning service, and if the men at the front of the building noticed us at all, we were nobody worth noticing. That is the whole trick of invisibility. I had practised it for years. I had simply been practising on the wrong side.

A cabstand stood at the corner. I had Adeline's motor garaged two streets over—brought up from the country the night before, along with everything else I had set in motion while the house slept—and I sent Finch ahead to bring it round to the cab rank's shadow, and stood with Ruth in the doorway of a tobacconist's, watching the reflections in the glass.

"There," Ruth murmured. "Under the tree. He's seen you before, that one. At the townhouse. The night you went in with the cleaning women."

She was right. He had the particular blankness of a man working hard not to look at something. I turned us both away, laughing at some joke neither of us had made, a niece and aunt admiring a shop window full of pipes, and the motor came growling around the corner with Finch rigid at the crank-side like a man transporting nitroglycerine, and we were in and moving before the man under the tree had finished deciding we were no one.

"Chancery Lane filing office," Finch said from beside me. "Side entrance. And Miss Ravensworth—you should know, the clerk at the counter, Hobbes, he's been got at. Gerald's solicitors use him. If we go to his window—"

"Then we don't go to his window." I took the corner hard enough to make Ruth gasp and Finch clutch his hat. "Who else takes filings?"

"Anyone on duty. But Hobbes controls the queue book—"

"Then someone else will simply have to be first in it." The filing office rose ahead of us, soot-dark and indifferent, its steps already busy with clerks trailing the smell of breakfast. I pulled up at the kerb. "How long, once it's filed?"

"If the duty master signs it today—an hour. Perhaps two. If Hobbes delays it in the book, days, and by then they'll have found a judge who owes them."

"Then it won't sit in any book." I turned in my seat and looked at Ruth Ellery, who had ridden in a motor car for the first time in her life and had not complained, and who was holding her handbag with both hands as though it contained everything she had. "Mrs.—Ruth. When we go in, you will walk to whichever window has the shortest queue, and you will ask, loudly, for directions to the ladies' convenience. Loudly. Can you do that?"

Something moved behind her eyes—the old nurse, the woman who had held my mother's hand in a locked room, surfacing for a moment through forty years of rubble. "Loudly," she said, "is the one thing I've come here to be."

* * *

It worked, and it worked because it was simple. Ruth's voice, cracked and carrying, cut across the hall like a dropped plate, and every head—including Hobbes's—turned toward the elderly lady demanding to know where a person went in this establishment, and while they turned, Finch stepped to the adjacent window and put down the application, and the fee, and the affidavit, and said *filed this morning, madam's instructions, time-stamped please*, to a junior clerk who wanted nothing more than to be helpful and get back to his sandwich.

The stamp came down at eleven minutes past ten.

Finch wrote the time in his notebook with a hand that trembled just once, and I understood then that he had been afraid this whole time—all those months of lamplight and registers—afraid every single day, and had simply decided that fear was another item on the list of things to do. I liked him better for it than for anything else.

We came out onto the steps, and the morning was bright and cold, and for perhaps thirty seconds I allowed myself to believe we had done it.

Then I saw the brougham.

It stood at the bottom of the steps, horses steaming, and beside it stood a man I knew—not one of the hired watchers but a principal, Gerald's own solicitor, Mr. Prewitt, grey and smooth as river stone, flanked by two bailiffs with the broad, bored build of men whose profession is other people's furniture. Prewitt consulted his watch, ascended three steps, and inclined his head to me with perfect courtesy.

"Miss Ravensworth. How pleasant. I wonder if I might have a word before my colleagues make this tiresome for everyone."

"You may have the word," I said, "and I'll decide afterward whether anyone was tiresome."

"The injunction is being served on Mr. Halloway's chambers at this moment. The bailiffs will enter and remove the documents removed from my client's house. That is settled law and you know it." He paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before setting down the thing he actually came to say. "What is not settled—and what I am authorised to make generous provision for—is your own position. My client's regard for family feeling remains, despite everything, intact."

Behind me, Ruth's breath had gone very quiet. Beside me, Finch had gone very still, his hand flat against his coat, over the notebook, over the time-stamp written there in ordinary blue-black ink.

"You're offering me money," I said, "while your bailiffs walk up those stairs."

"I am offering you a future, Miss Ravensworth. My client is offering you a future." Prewitt smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the worst thing about it. "The papers they seize will be copies, or near enough. The affidavit of one demented old workhouse woman will be attacked as unreliable by Friday. The court order you have just obtained—" and here he glanced, briefly, at Finch, and I saw that they had known, that Hobbes or someone like him had already sent word, that we had perhaps twenty minutes' head start and they had spent nineteen of them letting us feel victorious "—will be set aside as irregularly procured. All of this happens today, whether you stand here or not. The only variable is you. Walk away now, with five hundred pounds and a passage booked, and in a year nobody will remember there ever was a question about the 1893 will. Stay, and you will be in court tomorrow, publicly, as the servant girl who stole her employer's letters."

The bailiffs had started up the steps. Their boots rang on the stone, unhurried, certain.

And I found—to my own surprise—that I was not afraid. I was angry, which is a different engine entirely, and it turns the wheels faster.

"Mr. Prewitt," I said, "you've made one error in an otherwise competent morning's work. You've assumed the papers are the case. They never were. Papers can be seized, burned, lost, misfiled by a helpful clerk named Hobbes. But the affidavit was signed at twenty past nine this morning by a living witness, in front of counsel, and it is now part of a court file, in the custody of the court, and no bailiff on your client's payroll can touch it without committing an offence the newspapers will find extremely interesting." I took a step down toward him, close enough to see his composure flicker. "My mother was declared mad so that two brothers could take her inheritance, and one of your client's family signed the order, and one of my friends' fathers witnessed it, and I have spent months learning that the law protects men like your client right up until the moment a woman stands in front of it and refuses to move. I am standing here. I am not moving. Tell Gerald that."

Prewitt's face did something complicated and brief, like weather over water.

"Very well," he said softly. "Tomorrow, then. Court Four, before Master Ellison. I hope you sleep well tonight, Miss Ravensworth."

The bailiffs passed us going up. Through the tall windows behind them, I could see Halloway already in the hallway of his chambers, arms folded, waiting for them with the serenity of a man who has hidden everything that matters and intends to enjoy the conversation.

Finch touched my elbow. "The order's issued. It's entered. Whatever they take upstairs will be copies." His voice shook slightly on the last word, and steadied. "It's real now, Miss Ravensworth. The law has it. The law is moving."

Below us, in the street, Gerald's brougham waited with its horses steaming, and somewhere across the city Adeline Croft was waking to an empty envelope on her hall table, and somewhere in a private asylum outside Guildford, I would learn later, a telephone was already ringing.

Tomorrow, Court Four.

Tonight, whatever Gerald had left.
