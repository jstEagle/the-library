## 18. Chancery, Third Day

The third day of the hearing opened with rain, and with Mr. Petherbridge smiling at me as though I were something he'd found on his boot.

I had learned by then to read counsel the way I read letters—the set of the shoulders, the angle of the head, the small economies of gesture that told you what a man intended before he spoke. Petherbridge came into court that morning like a man who had been handed a gift. He arranged his papers with unusual care. He was pleasant to Halloway in the corridor, which frightened me more than any sneer would have done.

"He's going for me," I said quietly.

Halloway did not look up from his brief. "He's been going for you since Tuesday. Today he means to land it."

"How?"

"By telling the truth about you," Halloway said. "It's always worse than a lie. Lies can be caught out."

* * *

The Master of the Rolls took his seat at ten minutes past ten, and the rain worked at the high windows of the Royal Courts of Justice like fingers trying the glass. The public gallery had filled overnight—word travels strangely through Chancery Lane, and something about this case had caught at people: an heiress nobody had heard of, a will forty years old, a lunacy order with a forged signature. Ruth sat two rows behind me between Finch and Mrs. Tallow, her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral, which in a sense she was. She had signed her affidavit on Monday. What we were doing today was defending it—and defending me.

Petherbridge rose early, and he wasted no time on ceremony.

"My lord," he said, "before my friend continues to parade documents before this honourable court, there is a matter of character that must be addressed." He turned, and found me, and smiled again. "The court has heard much of Miss Mirabel Ravensworth. It has heard her described as the daughter of Eleanor Ravensworth, the wronged sister of my client. It has heard her called an heir. I must ask the court to consider what else she is—or rather, what else she has been, these six years past."

He paused. He was good; I gave him that. He let the silence build the way a surgeon lets the ether build.

"She has been," he said, "a servant. A paid driver and general factotum in the household of Mrs. Adeline Croft. And she stands here today claiming a fortune of some eighty thousand pounds on the strength of documents she obtained"—he permitted himself a small, fastidious horror—"by opening correspondence entrusted to her. By rifling the private papers of a house that paid her wages. By impersonating a domestic servant to burgle my client's townhouse in Belgravia."

A stir ran along the gallery. The Master of the Rolls did not look up from his notes.

"We have heard," Petherbridge went on, "of the stolen cushion. Of luggage inspected at railway stations. Of affidavits extracted from a workhouse pauper of uncertain memory and doubtful motive. My lord, strip away the Latin and what remains is this: a maidservant who read her betters' letters, liked what she found, and has spent a year turning other people's secrets into a claim upon their property. If she will open one letter, why not another? If she will search one house, whose house is safe?"

He turned to the bench fully now.

"I do not say she is not sincere, my lord. Sincerity is cheap. I say that everything this court has been asked to admire about her—the discovery of the will, the exposure of the commitment order, the whole apparatus of her case—rests upon acts which, committed by any person without a grievance, would be prosecuted as theft and burglary. She comes to equity with hands that are not clean. And equity, your lordship will remember, does not come to the aid of those who come with unclean hands."

He sat down. The gallery breathed. Behind me I heard Ruth's breath catch—a small, terrified sound—and Finch murmur something to steady her.

I looked at Adeline.

She sat at the back, where she had sat every day, dressed in grey, taking no part. Gerald's people had wanted her to stay away entirely; she had refused, and then refused equally to sit with them. She occupied a middle pew like a woman at a service for someone whose life she had once shared. When our eyes met, she did not look away, and she did not smile. Six months ago I would have needed a smile. Now I only needed her not to flinch, and she didn't.

Halloway rose unhurriedly, the way he did everything except when he was filing papers against the clock.

"My lord, I shall deal with my friend's submission in a moment, and I shall deal with it briefly, because it deserves brevity. But first, with your lordship's leave, I would like to examine Miss Ravensworth—not upon the documents, which my friend has made much of, but upon the reading of them."

"Very well," said the Master of the Rolls. "Briefly."

I went into the box.

The oath, the Bible, the little ritual of it. Then Halloway's voice, level as a ruled line.

"Miss Ravensworth, you have heard my friend describe you as a maidservant who read her betters' letters. Is that description accurate?"

"It is accurate in its facts," I said, "and false in everything else. I was employed as a driver. I read the correspondence I carried. I did so for six years."

"Why?"

The courtroom waited. This was the question I had rehearsed a hundred answers to, and Halloway had warned me against all of them. Do not be clever, he had said. Be exact.

"Because no one watched me," I said. "Because I drove a lady who moved among powerful people, and I carried the paper they wrote to each other, and I discovered that the world I had been taught was happening somewhere above me was in fact happening in my own two hands, in envelopes, in my keeping. I read because I could, and because after a while I understood that the difference between me and the people whose letters I carried was not intelligence or honesty. It was that they had never once needed to hide anything from a servant, and so never troubled to."

"And did you profit from this reading? Before last year?"

"No, sir."

"You sold nothing. You blackmailed no one. You kept no secrets for leverage."

"No. I read, mostly, things about meetings and money subscriptions and who was angry with whom. It was—" I stopped, because the true word had arrived, and Halloway had also told me never to refuse the true word. "It was how I learned history. I had believed history was made elsewhere. I learned it was made in the post."

Petherbridge was on his feet. "My lord, if my learned friend proposes to make literature of this examination—"

"I am making evidence of it," said Halloway mildly. "Your lordship will forgive me if I take it slowly, since my friend has rested his entire case upon it. Miss Ravensworth—in March of last year, did you open a letter addressed to Mrs. Croft?"

"Yes."

"What was it?"

"A letter from a solicitor named Halloway, inquiring after one Mirabel Ravensworth, born 1889, daughter of Eleanor Ravensworth, deceased."

"Had you ever before heard that you had a legal claim?"

"No. Until that letter I did not know my mother's maiden name had ever mattered to anyone."

"And when you later searched your uncle's townhouse, and found the commitment order of 1893—did you steal it?"

"I copied it. I left it where it lay. I stole nothing but the knowledge."

"And the will register copy from the estate archive at Fenn House—you failed even to obtain that."

"I did. Gerald removed it before I arrived."

"So the document upon which this claim principally rests—the 1893 enrollment—was obtained not by you at all, but by my junior, Mr. Finch, lawfully, from the records of this very court."

"That is my understanding, sir."

Halloway turned to the bench. "My lord, my friend says my client comes to equity with unclean hands. Let us examine the hands. She read letters that were given into her custody. Wrongful—I concede it at once, and she has never pretended otherwise; indeed, she confessed it to me before she hired me, which I suggest is more than most litigants do. But what flowed from that wrongful act? Not theft. Not blackmail. Not one farthing of gain until this court should decide she is owed it. From a maidservant's forbidden reading has come this: proof that in 1893 a sane woman of sound testamentary capacity was declared a lunatic on papers signed by a witness who swore before God he had seen her, when he was, on his own diary—which my friend has not challenged—one hundred and forty miles away in Norfolk on the day in question."

I heard the gallery take that in. We had held the diary back until this morning; Finch had found it in a bookseller's auction catalogue, of all places, bought for four shillings, and Petherbridge's face when Halloway laid it on the bench had been worth the whole terrible year.

"Now my friend asks the court," Halloway continued, "whose house is safe if servants may read letters. It is a fine question, and I will answer it plainly: no house built upon a forgery is safe from anything. But consider, my lord, what my friend's argument requires. It requires that the only lawful route to this truth was none—for the law itself was the instrument of the wrong. The order committing Eleanor Ravensworth was lawful in form. The asylum received her lawfully. Her brothers administered her estate lawfully, under seal, for nineteen years. Every gear of the machine turned precisely as the law provided, and the machine consumed a living woman and returned nothing. There existed no petition she could sign, for she was legally incapable of signing. No court she could approach, for the law held her mad. My client's mother had, in the eyes of the law, no hands at all."

He paused, and his voice dropped half a tone.

"The truth survived those nineteen years in exactly one place, my lord: in a child too young to forget her mother's face, and in the forbidden reading of a servant nobody thought worth watching. Justice outside the law prepared the way for justice within it. That is not my client's shame. It is the reason this court is able to hear this case at all."

Silence, of the heavy kind. The Master of the Rolls wrote three lines in his notebook, unhurried.

Then Petherbridge rose for cross-examination, and I understood why he had smiled that morning.

"Miss Ravensworth," he said, softly, reasonably, a man almost pained by what he must do, "you tell the court you read hundreds of letters over six years and profited by none. Yet within weeks of reading the Halloway letter, you had retained solicitors. Within months, you had burgled my client's home. Within the year, you had smuggled a vulnerable elderly witness across a police cordon in a stolen motor car—"

"Borrowed," I said.

"—in a borrowed motor car," he agreed smoothly, "under forged passes bearing the name of the Women's Social and Political Union. Forged passes, Miss Ravensworth. You are familiar with forgery as a practice."

"In the way one becomes familiar with anything," I said, "by seeing it done to one's family first."

A titter from the gallery, quickly suppressed. The Master of the Rolls looked over his spectacles at me and did not reprove me, which I counted as a mercy.

"Let us speak of family," Petherbridge said. "Mrs. Tallow. Your old nurse. You sought her out, questioned her, shaped her recollections—"

"Mrs. Tallow remembered my mother perfectly well before I ever reached her door."

"—and then Mrs. Ruth Ellery, the witness. A destitute workhouse pauper, seventy-three years of age, of failing mind—"

"Her mind is better than yours," I said, "and she remembers my mother's face, and she has remembered it for thirty years because remembering it was the only penance available to her."

"Miss Ravensworth—"

"You ask why she testifies," I said, and my voice surprised me by going quiet instead of loud. "She testifies because men came to move her to a locked asylum three days before she signed, the same way they moved my mother, using the same powers, the same forms, the same lawful machine. She has seen what happens to women the machine wants. That is her motive, sir. It is not doubtful memory. It is memory with teeth."

Petherbridge studied me for a long moment, and I watched him decide—correctly—that every further question would hand me the knife again. He bowed to the bench and sat down.

* * *

They adjourned at four. Verdict reserved until the morrow.

In the corridor outside the court, the year pressed in on me—all of it at once, the cushion with its sewn seam, the porter's eyes at the station, the empty drawer at Fenn House, the rectory with its chairs overturned and its floor scattered with Ruth's poor possessions, the night crossing with her hands cold in mine across the gearbox while I talked us past a constable who wanted to know why two women were driving to London at two in the morning. Everything unlawful. Everything necessary. I had spent twelve months learning that the law and justice were different countries, and here I stood inside the law's great marble palace asking it to bless the road I'd taken between them.

"You did well," Halloway said beside me. "Don't relive it tonight. Sleep if you can."

"Will it be enough?"

He considered before answering, which was why I trusted him. "The forgery is proven. The diary settles it beyond argument. Tomorrow he rules on the will and on you. Petherbridge's last card is your character, and today you showed him the deck."

Adeline was waiting by the great doors, alone. Rain had followed us all day and now came down in earnest, silvering the lamps of the Strand.

"I want you to know," she said, without preamble, "that whatever he rules tomorrow—about the will, about the money—I'm glad it was said in the open. About the reading. About all of it. You've spent years being invisible on my behalf, and I let you, because it was convenient, and convenient is the most respectable word for cowardice I know."

I looked at her. Six months ago that sentence would have undone me. Now it simply landed, honest and late, like weather.

"Gerald's man spoke to my solicitor this afternoon," she went on. "There's to be an offer before judgment. You should hear it from me, not from him. He means to settle—and the terms, Mirabel, are not what you'll expect. They're not money."

"What then?"

She hesitated, and I realized, with a strange lurch, that she was afraid for me.

"He isn't buying silence anymore," Adeline said. "He's buying the story. Full withdrawal of the claim in exchange for the one thing Halloway's case still needs tomorrow—Ruth Ellery's affidavit, withdrawn. Quietly. He'll give you everything else: the estate, the acknowledgment, Eleanor's name restored to the church window at Fenn. Everything but the finding of fraud. Because a verdict of forgery doesn't ruin Gerald, Mirabel. It ruins the machine. And the machine has friends in rooms neither of us will ever see."

Above us, the clock over the great doors struck five, and somewhere behind the oak panels, the law settled for the night into its shelves and ledgers, holding our future in a copybook hand—while out in the wet street, a hansom drew up, and a small grey-haired woman stepped down from it alone, looking up at the Royal Courts of Justice as though she had come, at last, to keep a promise.

Ruth had left the workhouse that morning without telling anyone where she was going.

She saw me across the pavement, and raised one hand, and smiled.
