## 1. The Weight of What Stays

A year teaches you the weight of things.

Not their price — I could not tell you what a pound of tea costs in town anymore, or a yard of flannel, and I have stopped being embarrassed by it. No. A year on this headland teaches you what things weigh when you have to decide whether to carry them.

I learned this the hard way, in the weeks after the sea gave back Dominic's coat but not Dominic. There was a morning — I remember it was raining, because everything that first month was raining — when I stood in the bedroom with his spare waistcoat in my hands and could not put it down. It weighed nothing. A few ounces of good wool, a handful of bone buttons. And I stood there like a woman holding an anvil, arms shaking, because my hands had decided something my head hadn't agreed to yet: that some objects are heavier than they look, and grief is measured not in tears but in tonnage.

So I developed a system. I am a keeper's wife — widow, I correct myself, though the word still fits like someone else's boots — and keepers are system people. The lamp must be trimmed at dusk and dawn. The log must be kept. The lens must be wiped of salt every day the sea is generous enough to throw it at us, which is every day. Systems keep you upright when nothing else will.

The system for Dominic's things was this: anything I could carry without my knees buckling stayed. Anything I couldn't went into the sea chest at the foot of the bed, and the chest stayed shut, and I did not think about the chest, and if you believe that last part I have a bridge to sell you, or I did, until the storms took that too.

* * *

The twelfth of November. One year. The anniversary arrived the way most weather does on this coast — announced for days beforehand by everyone except me, who had chosen not to mark the calendar and then woke at four in the morning anyway, wide-eyed as a guilty child, knowing exactly what day it was.

I got up. That is the whole secret of widowing, as far as I can tell. You get up.

I lit the stove, put the kettle on, and went up the ninety-one steps to the lamp room to begin the evening's work early, because work early is better than thinking early. Tomas slept on in the little room off the kitchen, ten years old and sprawled like a starfish, one arm hanging off the cot. He sleeps the way his father did — completely, gratefully, as though sleep were a country you could emigrate to. I envied him and loved him and closed the door softly.

Ninety-one steps. Dominic counted them once for a visiting inspector and made it a joke between us: *ninety-one reasons to have strong legs, Pet.* Now they are ninety-one reasons to be alone with my own thoughts twice a night, which the doctor on the mainland would probably call unhealthy and which I call Tuesday.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and cold glass, the way it always does, the way it did when he was alive, and I stood there a moment with my rag in my hand letting that smell do what it does. Grief is mostly smell, I've decided. Nobody warns you. You can go whole days thinking you're mended, and then the wind shifts and brings woodsmoke and salt tar and something underneath that was just *him*, and you have to lean against the railing until your legs remember their business.

I polished. I wound the clockwork. I checked the wicks and the oil reservoirs and the spare burners lined up like patient soldiers. Outside, the sea worked at the rocks below with the patience of a creditor, and the light swept its long arm across the water and gathered nothing, as it had gathered nothing for a year, because the season's wrecks were done and the fishing boats knew better than to trust the November dark.

That is the discipline of it, the part I hold onto when everything else floats loose: the light is not for me. It never was. It burns for strangers — for the deckhand three hundred yards from disaster, for the merchantman's mate who can't read these waters, for people whose names I will never know and who will never know mine. Dominic used to say that was the whole trade, keeping a light for people who pass you by in the dark. He said it laughing. He said most things laughing, which is why it took everyone so long to learn how serious he was underneath.

For the first months after he drowned, I resented those strangers. There, I've said it, if only to the inside of my own skull where confessions are cheap. Every ship I saved felt like a ship that hadn't saved him. Every grateful signal flag some passing schooner ran up her rigging felt like a coin tossed to the widow at the funeral — well-meant and useless. Keeping the light while your own world has gone dark is a peculiar cruelty, tending a flame for other people's safety when no light anywhere had been tended for yours.

But resentment, I found, does not trim wicks. Resentment lets the mechanism run down at three in the morning and then a Norwegian brig goes onto the Blackwater shoal with the loss of nine men, and nine more women somewhere learn what you learned, and the arithmetic of it is unbearable. So you do the work. You do the work and eventually — this is the strange mercy nobody tells you either — the work starts doing you back. The routine holds your shape when your shape won't hold itself.

* * *

At noon I took tea down to the workshop, out of habit, and caught myself at the door with two cups.

This is another thing about the first year of widowing: the habits outlive their reason. His tea, steeped too strong the way he liked it, going cold on the bench beside a vice he'd never turn again. I drank my own cup standing in the doorway, looking at the room the way you look at a sleeping animal — carefully, so as not to wake anything.

Dominic's workshop is the warmest room on this rock and the coldest, depending on the hour and my courage. It is a low stone building attached to the tower's base by a covered walk, built by his grandfather, who built everything here including, according to family legend, several mistakes that were later mistaken for features. Inside: the bench under the south window, scarred and stained dark with a century of use. Tools on the wall in their painted silhouettes, so precise a household that even a stranger could see at a glance if something were missing. Nothing is missing. I have checked the silhouettes the way other women check faces in photographs, and every tool hangs exactly where he left it, which is either comforting or terrible, and some days both before breakfast.

He was a maker, Dominic. Not a keeper who dabbled — a maker who kept a light to pay for the making. Clocks repaired for half the farms on the mainland coast. A music box for Tomas's fourth birthday that played three tunes badly and was beloved absolutely. Barometers, sextant fittings, a hundred small brass kindnesses shipped across the water in straw. When he died, letters came from people I'd never heard of, saying they still used something he'd made every day of their lives. I read those letters maybe forty times each. They were proof of something, though I couldn't have said what — that he had existed, perhaps. That existence could leave useful marks.

I set his tea down on the bench, out of habit, out of idiocy, out of whatever it is, and then stood there hating myself gently for it, and that was when I saw the oilcloth bundle.

* * *

It sat on the shelf above the bench, behind the tin of polishing rags, wrapped and tied with cord in that particular double knot he used for everything — the knot I have untied a thousand times on parcels and sail ties and Tomas's boots, a knot so familiar it might as well have been his signature. I had looked at that shelf a hundred times in a year. I had inventoried it, in my way, catalogued it among the things-I-can-carry. And the bundle had been there the whole time, hidden in plain sight the way only familiar things can hide, and until this morning my eyes had simply declined to report it.

Or my eyes reported it and the rest of me overruled them. I honestly don't know which. A year in, you learn that your own mind is a crew with opinions of its own, and some mornings it refuses orders.

I untied the knot.

My hands knew the work and did it without consulting me, and the oilcloth fell open like a petal, and there it was.

A compass. Half-made.

The case was done — a beautiful thing, brass gone soft amber with age and handling, the bezel threaded true, the glass fitted and gleaming. The card lay beside it, unfinished: a disc of paper shellacked to a mother-of-pearl base, the north point painted, the rest blank. A compass rose with all but one of its petals unpainted. Waiting.

And under the card, folded small, a slip of paper in his hand — the quick slanted hand I knew better than my own handwriting by now, having spent a year comparing them. Three words only:

*For Tomas. Finish.*

I sat down on the stool, which was fortunate, because my legs had resigned.

Here is what I want to explain, and I will fail, because English doesn't have the words and neither do I. A dead man's letter breaks your heart. A dead man's *unfinished work* breaks something else entirely — something structural, a keel timber, a thing you didn't know was holding you upright. A letter says goodbye. An unfinished compass says *not yet*. It says there was a morning when he stood at this bench and set this down, meaning to come back, meaning there would be a next time, and the sea said otherwise, and now the next time belongs to whoever finds it.

Me. Apparently the next time belonged to me.

I picked up the card. It was lighter than a bird's wing. It weighed nothing at all, this disc of shellac and pearl, and it nearly went through the floor with me, through the stone, down through the headland into the grey water where the heavy things go.

I measured it against my system. Could I carry it?

No. Obviously not.

I wrapped it back in the oilcloth anyway, and tied the knot — his knot, the double one, my hands doing their old faithful work — and I did not put it back on the shelf. I carried it into the house, up the stairs, past Tomas's door, and set it on the mantel in the front room where I would have to look at it every day. Which was either very brave or very stupid, and a year has taught me those are frequently the same decision viewed from different windows.

* * *

That evening, dusk came down early and mean, the way it has started to lately. The sky to the west went the colour of a bruise deciding whether to commit, and the barometer in the hall — his barometer, naturally, another thing he made, another thing I dust around rather than look at — had begun its slow slide toward *stormy* with the smooth inevitability of something that has done this many times and intends to do it many more.

I lit the lamp. Ninety-one steps down, supper made and eaten, homework argued over and abandoned, Tomas packed off to bed with the music box playing its three bad tunes. Then up again for the midnight trim, because the system holds, because the system is all.

On the landing at step sixty-something — I lost count, which never happens — I stopped and looked out the seaward window and thought about the compass sitting on the mantel below me, one floor down, wrapped in its oilcloth like a wound. *Finish*, he'd written. As if finishing a compass were like finishing a fence. As if the man who wrote it hadn't spent his whole life hiding the difficult parts of himself in plain sight, on shelves, behind tins of rags, where a loving wife's eyes could decline to report them for a full year.

Because here is the thing I haven't let myself think until tonight, and now that I've started I apparently can't stop: Dominic Kavanagh finished everything. Every clock, every fitting, every commission. His workbench was a point of pride precisely because nothing lingered there. And yet the compass — the one object meant for his own son, the inheritance — sat unfinished on a shelf, wrapped up like something he wanted found by someone, someday, but not by him, not while he could help it.

People wrap things to protect them. Or to hide them. Sometimes, on this coast, both.

The wind has come around to the northeast while I stood here woolgathering, and it has teeth in it now, and the barometer is still falling, and the first great storms of the season are meant to be gathering offshore like relatives before a funeral. The supply boat ran yesterday; tomorrow it may not. We have flour and oil and lamp wicks enough, and a winter ahead of exactly the kind I have survived before, alone but for a boy and a ghost and ninety-one steps.

Tomorrow I'll take the compass back down to the workshop. Tomorrow I'll look at what my husband left undone and try to understand why.

Tonight I have a light to keep.
