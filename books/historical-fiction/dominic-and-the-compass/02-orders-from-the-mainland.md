## 2. Orders from the Mainland

The ferryman came on a Tuesday, which was wrong already. Tam Beattie only ran the crossing on Thursdays and Sundays, and he never came ashore in February unless somebody had died or somebody had sent money, and in my experience the two arrived in roughly equal measure.

I saw his boat from the lamp room while I was cleaning the lens — the daily ritual of wiping salt from brass and glass that could not be allowed to know how tired I was of it — and by the time I got down the ninety-one steps to the shingle, he was hauling his dinghy above the tideline with the air of a man who hoped the sea would take it back before he had to row home.

"Mrs. Kavanagh," he said, tugging his cap.

"Tam."

"Ferry's running special," he said. "Bridge side wants papers put in your hand. Proper hand, they said. Yours." He produced an oilskin packet from inside his coat as though it were a fish he regretted catching.

"You've come all this way for paper?"

"And a cup of tea, if you're offering. And I'll not lie to you, Petra, the wind on the way over had opinions about me."

I made him tea because that is what one does, and because the house was very quiet in the hours between Tomas's schoolwork and the lighting of the lamp, and quiet is a thing I have learned to fear slightly more than storms. Storms at least announce themselves.

Tomas sat at the table with his slate, pretending to do sums and actually doing something far more interesting with the chalk dust, which he was arranging into what appeared to be a fleet of tiny ships. I did not stop him. His father drew ships in margins too; some inheritances need no paperwork.

"Open it, then," Tam said, wrapping both hands around his mug. "You'll want to be sitting."

"I'm always sitting. That's mostly what keeping a light is. Sitting near a fire other people lit."

But I opened it, because Tam had the particular stillness of a man delivering bad news who has decided it is not his job to soften it, only to survive the receiving of it.

There were three documents. The first was from the mainland magistrate's office, typed, which always strikes me as a small arrogance — as though a machine's certainty could settle anything. The second was a copy of a will, or part of one, with clauses underlined in someone else's ink. The third was a single page in a solicitor's careful hand, addressed properly this time, to *Mrs. Petra Kavanagh, Keeper, Cairnholm Light*.

I read the magistrate's letter twice. Then I read it aloud, because there are things that stay unreal until they have been said in your own voice in your own kitchen.

"...whereas the parcel of land known as the workshop plot, comprising the outbuilding and appurtenant ground at the seaward point of Cairnholm, has been willed into dispute by the late Mr. Dominic Kavanagh, and whereas competing claims have been lodged by the estate of Mr. Alistair Kavanagh and by Messrs. Harrow & Vane, shipping factors, of the mainland..."

"Harrow and Vane," Tam said, into his tea. It was not quite a question.

"You know them?"

"Everyone knows them. Everyone knows them the way you know weather coming. Ships factor. They buy cargo space, charter vessels, insure things." He turned his mug a quarter turn on the table. "Never heard them mentioned in connection with this rock before, mind."

The letter went on, in its machine-certainty, to inform me that unless the estate were settled — claims reconciled, title determined, the matter *resolved to the satisfaction of the court* — surveyors would arrive at spring thaw to value and prepare the workshop plot "for clearance and sale."

Clearance. As in emptied. As in cleared.

"They want the workshop," I said. It came out flatly, which surprised me. I had expected my voice to shake.

"Looks like."

"It's a shed and forty feet of gorse. There's nothing in it worth a surveyor's boot leather. Dominic's tools, a bench, half a stove. Whoever's fighting over it isn't fighting over the land."

Tam looked at me with the mild, patient eyes of a man who ferries people and their news back and forth and has long ago stopped being astonished by either. "Then what are they fighting over?"

I didn't answer, because I didn't know, and because Tomas had gone still at the table in the way he goes still when adults begin speaking in the lowered voices he has learned means the conversation has become worth listening to.

"Finish your sums," I told him, and he bent obediently over the slate, and the little chalk ships waited for him.

* * *

After Tam left — refusing payment, accepting bread, promising to look in again Thursday if the sea allowed, which is ferryman language for *I will be thinking about you* — I took the letters up to the workshop.

I don't go up there often. That is not sentiment, or not only sentiment; it is logistics. A lighthouse demands everything upward — oil, glass, attention, the whole posture of the place is vertical — and a widow learns to ration her descents into the horizontal parts of grief. The workshop sits at the point where the headland gives up and becomes rock and spray, and it holds everything of Dominic's hands. His tools in their racks. His apron on its hook, still shaped faintly to a body that has been in the water a year. And on the bench, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine, the compass.

I had found it in the autumn after we buried him. Or rather, Tomas had found it, climbing on things as usual, and had come down the path holding it like something rescued from a fire. *Is this Papa's? Can I have it when I'm bigger?* I had said yes, because you say yes to children about their dead parents' belongings; it is the only currency you have. Then I had carried it back upstairs myself and set it where it lives and mostly not looked at it, the way you mostly do not press a bruise.

Now I untied the twine.

The compass was perhaps two-thirds finished. Dominic had been a meticulous craftsman — he'd apprenticed to a watchmaker in his youth, before the sea took him the way the sea takes everyone eventually, one trade at a time — and even incomplete, the thing was beautiful. The bowl was done, spun and polished, the gimbal rings fitted so they sang faintly when tilted. The card, though — the compass card, the rose of directions itself — lay beside the bowl as a disc of paper-thin brass, blank but for faint pencil divisions, unengraved, unfinished. The needle sat in a folded square of tissue like a sleeping bird. And the case, the hinged brass case that should have held everything together and kept it honest against the pitching of any deck in any weather — the case existed only as pieces. Side plates cut. Lid roughed out. Hinges in a twist of cloth.

He had been close. Weeks, maybe, from finishing. And he had stopped.

I had assumed, in the first raw year, that he had simply run out of time. Men who work the water do not get to schedule their endings. But now I stood in the cold workshop with a magistrate's letter in my hand and a shipping firm's name in my mouth — *Harrow and Vane* — and I asked the bench, the apron, the oilcloth, the question I had not thought to ask before, because grief makes a very effective darkness:

Why would a shipping firm fight over a shed full of an unfinished compass?

Because wills are read. Because probate inventories list things. Because a disputed estate gets opened, itemized, valued, and examined by strangers in good coats. Because if you wanted something of Dominic Kavanagh's to disappear quietly, and he had inconveniently died without disposing of it, the cleanest road to it ran through a courtroom — and the second-cleanest through surveyors with a writ and a wagon.

They didn't want the land. They wanted what the land contained. They wanted this — I picked up the blank brass card, and it was lighter than the truth ought to be — and whatever this was, it was enough that two parties were willing to quarrel over a dead man's bench within weeks of the funeral.

Unless. Unless it was simpler than that. Unless Alistair — and I knew from the will's language that the competing claim was Alistair's, my father-in-law, who had spoken to me perhaps forty words since the burial, each one correctly constructed and none of them warm — unless Alistair simply wanted his son's last work kept in the family, and Harrow and Vane were opportunists circling any estate with brass in it.

That was the kind version. I held it up to the grey window light and tried to make it fit.

It didn't. Firms like Harrow and Vane do not send letters through magistrates over watchmaker's scrap. I have dealt with enough mainland men to know that their attention follows money the way gulls follow the plough, and there is no money in gorse and a half-built compass. Unless the compass itself is the money. Unless what it is — what it was commissioned to be, who paid for it, what it was meant to do once finished — matters more than what it weighs.

Commissioned. The word arrived in my head without an invitation, and I stood there turning it over. Because that was another thing I had not let myself think about: the compass was not begun on a whim. I remembered — suddenly, entirely, the memory stepping out of wherever I had shelved it — Dominic at this bench the summer before he drowned, measuring something small with calipers, and Tomas asking what it was for, and Dominic saying, easily enough, *It's a commission, lad.* And when Tomas asked who commissions a compass, Dominic had smiled the particular smile he used for changing the subject and said, *A firm that knows the value of knowing where you are.*

I had thought nothing of it. A wife hears a hundred sentences a day and files them all under *husband being himself*, and later, when one of them turns out to have been load-bearing, the whole structure of what you thought you knew sags.

A firm that knows the value of knowing where you are.

I set the card down. My hands were steady, which seemed dishonest of them.

* * *

That evening I lit the lamp, because the sea does not care what you have learned about your husband's correspondence. I trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that turns the lens, watched the great light gather itself and begin its slow revolution — white flash every seven and a half seconds, the pulse this coast has steamed and sailed and occasionally wrecked by for sixty years — and I stood in the lamp room with the wind leaning on the glass and let myself think the thoughts I'd been postponing since morning.

Here is the arithmetic of my mourning, the tidy version I had kept for a year: my husband went out in his boat on a night the barometer had warned about, to check lines he need not have checked, and the sea took him, and the sea is nobody's enemy, it is only the sea. Grief like that is terrible but clean. You can carry it. It has a shape.

But a disputed will is not a shape. A shipping firm's name on a magistrate's letterhead is not a shape. A man who stops three-quarters of the way through his masterwork is not a shape — or he is a different shape than the one I had, and I did not yet know its outline, and I confess I stood in that lamp room feeling the first fine crack run through the whole careful edifice of what I had decided happened to Dominic Kavanagh.

Not suspicion. Not yet. I want to be fair to myself, and to him; suspicion is a debt you cannot unowe once incurred. What I felt was smaller and worse: a misalignment. The sensation a keeper knows best — the needle a half-point off true, the light sweeping across a horizon where the chart says there should be a rock and shows instead open water. Nothing you can report. Everything you must watch.

Tomas came up while I stood there, in his nightshirt, hair wild, on the pretext of asking whether he could keep the lamp room warm for me, which is his way of saying he doesn't like going to sleep while the light is turning.

"One more minute," I said, which is always the answer, and he leaned against me, and below us the workshop sat dark at the point of the headland like a closed fist.

"Mam," he said. "When the surveyors come — that's the men who measure things, isn't it? Cooky at school says they're coming to the point in spring."

News travels fast when the news travels by schoolboy. "It says so in the letter," I admitted.

"Will they throw Papa's things away?"

"No," I said. And then, because I am apparently capable of making promises I have no power to keep, I put my hand on his head and said it again, firmer, the way you speak into a wind. "No. Whatever else happens, his things stay ours."

He nodded, satisfied, the way children are satisfied, trusting the adult economy of promises, and went down to bed. I stayed in the lamp room until his door clicked shut below me.

Then I went down the ninety-one steps, past the kitchen where the magistrate's letter lay on the table, and out into the dark, and walked the path to the workshop with the storm lantern swinging, and unlocked it, and sat at my husband's bench with his unfinished inheritance in front of me and his father's claim and a stranger firm's claim folded in my pocket.

The estate had to be settled before spring thaw. Settled meant claimed, inventoried, argued over in rooms I would never be permitted to enter — unless someone on this rock settled it first. Unless someone understood exactly what it was Dominic had been building, and for whom, and why he had stopped.

In the morning I would write to the mainland. Tonight I did something less sensible.

I reached for the letter I hadn't yet fully read — the solicitor's page, the one addressed to me personally — and smoothed it flat under the lamplight, and found, at the bottom, beneath the signature, a postscript in smaller ink that I had skimmed past once already:

*P.S. — Be advised that Mr. Alistair Kavanagh, having no crossing available since the closure of the bridge, has engaged passage by fishing boat and intends to present himself at Cairnholm within the fortnight, to oversee his interests in the workshop plot directly.*

I sat very still and listened to the sea working the rocks below, patient as a creditor.

My father-in-law was coming. The man who had shaken my hand at the funeral as though completing a transaction was coming up my path, into my house, to stand at his dead son's bench — and somewhere behind his correct manners, I now suspected, was either the answer to all of it or the reason for it.

The lamp turned above me, white flash, seven and a half seconds, faithful as ever, showing every ship on this coast exactly where it was.

If only it worked on the truth.
