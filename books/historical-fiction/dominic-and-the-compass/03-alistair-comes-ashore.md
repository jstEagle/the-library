## 3. Alistair Comes Ashore

I had rehearsed the crossing eleven times in my head, and the sea refused to cooperate with any of them. The ferryman — a squat, salt-cured man named Brannigan who spoke to God and the outboard engine with equal familiarity — had asked me twice whether I was certain about the trip. Twice I had said yes. A third time he opened his mouth, and I informed him that I had buried a wife, a son was dead to me in the only way that mattered, and that I would thank him to mind his engine.

He minded his engine.

The headland came up out of the grey the way I remembered it: sudden, black-toothed, indifferent. The lighthouse stood at its crown like an exclamation mark at the end of a sentence nobody wanted finished. Below it, the keeper's house crouched behind a windbreak of stunted pines, and beyond that, half-hidden, the long low roof of the workshop where my son had spent thirty years making things that floated, turned, measured, and kept people honest. Where he had left one thing unfinished.

One thing. It is remarkable, the arithmetic of grief. A whole life summed as a remainder.

Brannigan ran the bow onto the shingle below the house and did not offer to help with my case. I had not asked. A man who has packed his own bag since 1861 does not require assistance from someone named after a brand of Irish whiskey, though I confess the thought occurred to me and was not uncharitable. The wind took my hat brim the moment I stepped down, as if the island itself were going through my pockets.

The path up was stone and root, worn slick by forty years of Kavanagh boots. Mine were the third generation to make this climb. Dominic's had been the last, and they had gone into the sea with him. I thought about that the entire way up, which is to say I thought about nothing else and told myself I was thinking about the weather.

She was waiting on the step.

Petra Kavanagh — Petra Amberdale, born Halloway; I have never once used her married name aloud without irony and she knows it — stood with her arms folded against the wind, wearing a coat that had been Dominic's and a face that gave away precisely nothing. Her hair was pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided that vanity was another item the sea had taken, and she looked at me the way a keeper looks at weather coming in off the horizon: noting it, measuring it, deciding whether it warranted shutting the shutters.

"Mr. Amberdale," she said.

"Mrs. Kavanagh."

"You'll want tea."

It was not a question, and it was not hospitality. It was tactics. She was informing me that the ritual existed and would be observed, and that within its forms we would conduct whatever skirmish the afternoon required. I respected her for it immediately, which annoyed me.

"I'll want tea," I agreed.

* * *

The house smelled the way it always had — lamp oil, bread, salt — but under it there was a new note, or rather an absence of one. A year of a man's absence has a smell. Things stop being warmed by him. His coat stops taking on his shape and begins merely hanging there, and eventually someone moves it, and then the house smells like itself again, which is worse.

Tomas was at the table doing sums when we came in, and he rose halfway out of his chair the way boys do when they cannot decide whether an adult is an occasion. He had Dominic's jaw and Halloway coloring, and when he saw me he went very still, the way one goes still around something either precious or dangerous. I have been both in my life and have stopped pretending to know which occasions called for which.

"Grandfather," he said.

"Tomas." I set my hand on his head because some transactions must be honored regardless of what they cost, and his hair was thick and dark under my palm and I removed the hand perhaps too quickly. He sat back down and bent over his figures with the exaggerated diligence of a child fleeing a room while remaining seated in it.

Petra filled the kettle without asking how I took my tea. She knew. That was the trouble with the woman — she knew everything about me worth knowing and deployed the knowledge like ammunition she declined to fire. Ten years of Sunday dinners, two funerals, one wedding. You accumulate debts of observation across a table that no amount of formality can discharge.

"The ferryman said the bridge is due to close," I said. "Early this year."

"He said." She set the cup in front of me. "The engineer's boy came round Tuesday. Cracked pier. They'll condemn it until spring thaw."

"Then I arrived none too soon."

Her eyes came up. Grey, Dominic's grey, or rather his were hers — he got everything from her side, which I mean as a compliment to her family and could never bring myself to deliver as one.

"Soon for what, Mr. Amberdale?"

"For the purpose stated in my letter."

"Your letter said you wished to pay your respects."

"My letter was written by a solicitor. Solicitors mistake courtesy for meaning. My purpose is the workshop."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Tomas's pencil scratched. Outside, the wind found the gap under the door and played it like a reed instrument, badly.

"The workshop," she repeated.

"Which is to be cleared in spring, per the orders your solicitor received and mine also, and which will be cleared over my body if necessary, but preferably over my supervision instead. I built that bench. I taught the man who died at it. Whatever is in that room leaves it in an order I determine, or it does not leave at all."

"And the surveyors?"

"Come at thaw. As do I leave at thaw. We need not see each other past April, Mrs. Kavanagh, which I anticipate will suit us both admirably."

She considered me across the table for a long moment. There are women who deliberate with their faces; Petra deliberates somewhere behind them, in a room I have never been shown. When she finally spoke, her voice had the pleasant, lethal mildness of cream poured over broken glass.

"Dinner is at six. You'll take the front room. Tomas, finish the sums."

And that was the whole of the negotiation. I had come ashore armed with a solicitor's letter, a father's grief, and forty years of being right, and I had been billeted, fed, and managed inside of four minutes. I drank my tea. It was strong enough to stand a spoon in, which I chose to interpret as respect.

* * *

I did not go to the workshop that first evening. This requires explanation, because every instinct in me pointed there the way a compass needle points north — an image I would come to regret — but there is a discipline to entering a dead man's room, and the discipline is that you do not do it tired, hungry, and full of the crossing. Grief is like drink in that regard. It makes a man confident he can handle tools he cannot.

So I sat through dinner discussing herring prices, the school correspondence course, and the structural integrity of the bridge with the thoroughness of a man building a wall out of small talk, and at half past eight I retired to the front room and did not sleep.

It is a strange thing, lying in a bed in a house your son helped raise the roof beams for, listening to the sea work the rocks below like a debt collector. I had made that crossing perhaps two hundred times in my life, and I had always known, in the dull animal way one knows things, that the water was the enemy and the light was the argument against it. Dominic had kept the argument going for nine years. Then one November night the enemy collected, and the argument continued anyway, lit by his widow, and I did not know whether that was devotion or defiance or simply the fact that a lamp, once lit, does not much care whose hand holds the match.

At some hour past midnight I heard the floorboards in the hall take weight. Light footsteps, pause outside my door, move on. Then the stair, the outer door, the wind's brief intrusion. I went to the window and watched, by the sweep of the lamp above, a figure cross the yard to the workshop, let herself in, and — this was the part that held me — not strike a light. Petra Kavanagh stood in her dead husband's workshop in the dark for the better part of a quarter hour, and then came back to the house and slept, apparently, the sleep of the just, because I heard nothing further.

A woman who visits a room in the dark is a woman who cannot bear to visit it in the light, or a woman checking that something is still where she left it. I have found in my long experience that these are frequently the same woman.

* * *

Morning granted us a grudging brightness, the kind the coast issues on approval only, and I presented myself at the workshop door at eight o'clock sharp with my collar buttoned and my case of tools, because if I was to oversee the clearing of a shop I would do it with my own dividers, thank you, and not the bent-handled relics of another man's sentimentality.

The door was unlocked. It should not have been. A workshop containing a dead man's unfinished commission ought to be locked like a reliquary, and the fact that it wasn't told me something about how Petra had spent the year that I filed away and did not like the shape of.

Inside, the room had been kept. That was my second surprise, and it undid me more than the first. Tools on their shadows — he'd painted silhouettes on the board so each chisel had its ghost, a trick I taught him at eleven years old and he had carried into his own shop like a heresy of mine made doctrine. Sawdust swept. The stove blackened and ready. And on the bench, centered, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine in a bowline tied the way I tie a bowline, sat the compass.

I did not touch it. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger more honest than the ones men keep: I stood in the doorway with my tool case dripping rain on the sill, and I looked at that oilcloth bundle, and I did not touch it, because touching it meant beginning, and beginning meant learning why he had stopped, and I had crossed a strait in November to avoid exactly that learning.

"You'll want to inventory before anything's moved."

Petra stood behind me with a ledger of her own and a pencil tucked through her bun. She had dressed for battle in an apron and an expression of perfect cooperation, which is the most alarming outfit a woman can wear.

"That's my intention," I said.

"Then we agree on the procedure. Tools first, papers second, materials last. Nothing leaves the room. Nothing gets finished." She said the last flatly, watching me. "You're here to order the clearing, Mr. Amberdale. Not to complete his work. Those are different permissions, and you have the first one only."

"On the contrary, I—"

"You wrote *oversee*. I read it three times. Overseeing is not finishing." She stepped past me into the room, and here is the thing I have not forgiven her for and never shall: she walked to that bench and untied the bowline — my bowline, my son's fingers taught by my fingers — and folded the oilcloth back with the unhurried competence of a nurse changing a dressing, and she let me look at the brass, the half-cut card, the pillar plates clamped and waiting, and her face never moved at all.

"Do you know why he never finished it?" I asked. The question escaped before I could dress it in etiquette, and I hated myself for the nakedness of it, the way a man hates a cough in church.

"No," said Petra, folding the cloth back over the works with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a coat over a sleeping child. "Do you?"

And because I am seventy-one years old and have survived my wife, my trade, and most of my illusions by knowing exactly which questions to answer slowly, I said: "I intend to find out what he was working on when he died. That is all my overseeing means. If you insist on making an enemy of a grieving old man, Mrs. Kavanagh, you'll find I'm very good at it, but I'd hoped we might be colleagues instead. The bridge closes soon. Neither of us can leave. It seems poor strategy to spend a winter at war over a bench."

The wind took that moment to shove hard against the eastern wall, and somewhere overhead a shutter banged twice, like applause from a gallery that had heard better arguments.

Petra studied me for the length of one full turn of the lamp above the headland, her face lit and darkened and lit again. Then she held out the ledger and the pencil.

"Tools first," she said. "You take the wall rack. I'll do the lathe. We'll compare lists at noon."

It was not peace. Do not imagine peace. It was two armies agreeing on a field, and I took the pencil because refusing it would cost more than taking it, and I began reading my dead son's tools like a letter I already suspected contained bad news.

We worked until the middle of the morning, calling measurements to each other in voices pitched carefully neutral, and it was almost tolerable, which frightened me more than hostility would have. Somewhere near eleven, moving the plane till aside, my knuckles brushed the back panel of the tool cabinet — and found it loose. Not broken. Loose in the particular way of wood that has been opened often and closed carefully, a panel that swings on a secret, and behind the faintest breath of movement I heard paper shift against paper.

Petra saw my hand stop. Across the room, over the bench, our eyes met, and for the first time since I'd come ashore, neither of us was armed with manners.

"What's behind there, Mr. Amberdale?" she asked quietly.

"I was about to ask you the same thing."
