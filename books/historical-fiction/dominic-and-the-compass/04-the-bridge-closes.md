## 4. The Bridge Closes

The storms of our coast keep a calendar the way churchgoers keep Lent — you can mark the date, but they arrive when they please, and they always overstay.

In the eleven winters I had kept the light at Kavanagh Head, first as Dominic's wife and then, God help me, as his widow, the season's first true gale had never come before the third week of November. That year it came on the fourth of October, arriving like a creditor who has learned where you actually live. The barometer had been falling for two days — falling the way Alistair's opinion of me fell, steadily and without any expectation that it would ever stop — and by dusk the sea had gone from iron to black glass to something with teeth in it.

I lit the lamp early. There is no ceremony in it anymore; there hadn't been for years, even before Dominic drowned. You trim the wick, you wind the clockwork that turns the lens, you wipe the glass with vinegar and newspaper because your father-in-law's grandfather insisted on it and somehow the insistence outlived everyone who insisted. Then you sit in the lamp room with the great eye turning beside you and you watch it sweep the water, saying *here, here, here* to ships that will never say thank you, and you think about supper.

That night I thought instead about the bridge.

The bridge to the mainland was not much of a bridge. It had been built in 1861 by a contractor who, by all accounts, bid low and delivered lower, and every winter since it had taken its beating from the strait and given back a certain amount of itself. We inspected it the way you inspect an old dog — hopefully, knowing the answer. But it had held through eleven winters, and I had made the mistake, common among widows and lighthouse keepers, of confusing endurance with permanence.

At half past nine the wind came around to the southeast and stopped being weather and became intention.

I went down. Alistair was in the workshop, which he had claimed on his second evening ashore the way empires claim coastlines: by standing on it and looking offended when anyone objected. He sat at Dominic's bench with a jeweler's loupe screwed into his eye, examining the half-made compass as though it were a suspect he intended to break by morning. He had not asked my permission to touch it. I had not asked his permission to resent it. We were even, more or less, which is as close to peace as two people in one house should hope to get.

"The barometer's dropped thirty points since noon," I said.

"Has it." He did not remove the loupe. Conversations with Alistair were conducted largely through the fiction that the other person was not present.

"There's a boat at the mooring that says otherwise, and a bridge between us and the mainland that was old when Victoria was young. If this blows the way I think it will—"

"If you intend to lecture me on weather," he said, "I'll remind you that I have been coming to this headland since before your father owned boots."

"Then you'll remember what happened to the Carnscarrow road in '71."

That landed. He took the loupe out of his eye slowly, the way other men set down a glass they'd rather finish. In '71 he had lost a brother — or a cousin; Alistair's griefs were filed away so tidily I never learned which — to a storm surge that took the low road whole. It was the only key I had ever found to him, and I hated using it, and I used it anyway. Grief makes opportunists of us all.

"I remember '71," he said quietly.

"Good. Then help me batten the workshop. If the water comes up over the sill we'll lose the lumber, and if we lose the lumber—" I stopped. I had almost said *we cannot finish it*, and finishing the compass was not yet something we had agreed to do together; it was something each of us intended to do alone while pretending the other wasn't there.

He rose without argument, which frightened me more than the wind did. Alistair agreeing with me was a sign of the times ending.

* * *

We spent two hours battening, and if you have never worked alongside a man you dislike in a rising gale, let me tell you: it clarifies things. There is no room for etiquette in hauling storm shutters. You learn whether a person pulls his weight, and Alistair pulled his, grumbling in a dialect of single syllables, moving around the workshop with a sureness that told me he had known this room long before I had. He knew which boards creaked. He knew that the third shutter pin needed a tap with the mallet first. He had taught all of it to Dominic, presumably, and now the knowledge was orphaned, passed to me sideways and against both our wills.

By midnight the rain came horizontally. By two the strait sounded like a train that never passed. Tomas slept through most of it — children do, the little barbarians — though once I checked on him and found him awake, listening with his hands behind his head, ten years old and entirely delighted.

"Is it the big one?" he asked.

"It's a big one."

"Grandfather says the bridge won't last five more years."

"Your grandfather says many things."

"Did he tell you the bridge is why he came now? Before the season?" And then he rolled over and slept, having planted his small grenade and walked away whistling, which is a family trait on both sides.

I stood in his doorway longer than I meant to. The bridge is why he came now. Of course it was. Alistair had arrived three weeks ahead of the storms with papers in his case and spring surveyors already spoken of, because he wanted the workshop cleared and the land settled before anything — weather, conscience, or inconvenient daughter-in-law — could stand in the way. I had assumed haste was greed. Standing there in the dark of my son's room, with the house groaning around me like a ship, it occurred to me for the first time that haste might be fear. The thought did not make me warmer toward him. It made me more careful, which is a different thing entirely.

* * *

The bridge died at dawn, though we didn't confirm it until noon.

The gale blew itself out in the morning the way they do — suddenly, shamelessly, leaving wreckage everywhere and a sky so clean blue you could scream. I walked the cliff path with the glass, and Alistair followed without being invited, because the bridge mattered to him too, whatever his reasons.

From the headland we could see it. Or rather, we could see where it had been. The central span was simply gone — not collapsed into picturesque ruin but *gone*, taken whole by the water, and the near approach ended in splinters like a broken chair leg. Two of the pilings stood naked downstream, leaning. The channel beneath ran fast and brown with torn kelp.

"Well," Alistair said.

"Yes," I said.

Neither of us said the rest of it: that the ferry wouldn't run reliably once the season started in earnest; that the supply boat would try when it could and fail when it couldn't; that we were, for all practical purposes, on an island until spring thaw. Four months. Four months of sharing a kitchen with a man who spoke to me the way one reads terms and conditions.

"We should get the boy's winter stores sorted," I said. "If the boat can't run—"

"The boat will run. It always runs for this coast." He said it as if daring the weather to contradict him.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we eat what's here and we don't waste candles." He was still looking at the gap in the bridge. Something crossed his face — I want to say relief, and I have thought about it since, and I still want to say relief — there and gone like a bird's shadow. Then he turned and walked back toward the house without another word, and I stayed a moment longer, looking at the broken span, thinking the thought I would take back to bed with me for months:

*Whatever he came here to bury, the sea just agreed to help him.*

* * *

We struck the bargain four days later, over mended china, which tells you how well the week had gone.

It began badly, as everything between us did. He had spent those days in the workshop behind a closed door — my door, in my late husband's workspace, on my land, a fact he treated as an administrative error — and I had spent them deciding not to knock. On the fourth evening, curiosity and loneliness got the better of pride in roughly equal measure, and I knocked.

He opened the door partway, saw it was me, and opened it fully anyway, which from Alistair was practically an embrace.

The bench was covered in the compass's scattered organs. The brass bowl, half-polished, caught the lamplight. The card — the delicate card that would carry the needle's truth around its circle — lay under a weight, blank, waiting for someone with steady hands and patience, and I recognized with a jolt that neither of us qualified, and that we would have to make do regardless.

"You've done nothing," I said. "Four days, and nothing."

"I've been *deciding*," he said.

"People drown while you decide, in my experience."

Something flickered there too — I was beginning to collect his flickers the way beachcombers collect sea glass, not knowing what any piece was worth but unable to leave them be — and he turned away and put his hand flat on the bench, next to the compass bowl.

"This was to be Tomas's," he said. It was not a question, and it was the first thing he had ever said to me that acknowledged we loved the same person, or had.

"It was."

"Dominic was not a finisher." He said it harshly, but I heard underneath it what it cost him. "My son could begin anything. Brass, timber, arguments. He had ten projects going the day he married you, and nine unfinished the day—" He stopped. Started again. "The compass he began the year the boy was born. Twelve years. Twelve years, Petra, and it sits here like a wound."

"He was busy keeping your light."

"My light," he repeated softly, and looked at me, and for one unguarded moment we simply were two people who missed the same man, and it was terrible, and I nearly forgave him everything right then. Fortunately the moment passed. Forgiveness, like the supply boat, does not run on schedule.

"Here is my proposition," I said, before sentiment could get further than it should. "You said yourself the surveyors come at thaw. You want the estate settled, the workshop cleared, this business finished. I want the compass finished for Tomas before anyone touches this room. Neither of us can do it alone — you need someone to mind the light and the house and the boy, and I need someone who knows the craft, because I watched you file that pivot yesterday and I cannot do it. So."

"So," he said.

"We finish it together. Before the thaw. Before the surveyors. You teach me, I keep everything else standing. When the bridge opens, the compass goes to Tomas, the workshop clears, and you go back to wherever it is you live, and we are done with one another. Completely. Permanently."

I had rehearsed it and it still came out faster than intended, the way confessions do. Alistair considered me for a long moment. Outside, the sea was making its usual arguments against human arrangements.

"You understand," he said slowly, "that some of what is in this room ought not to be finished at all."

I have thought about that sentence many times since, and what I understood at that moment was only this: that he was not talking about craftsmanship. But I was tired, and proud, and grieving, and I chose to hear it as an old man's fussiness about tolerances.

"I understand that my son inherits his father's compass," I said, "or I will know the reason why."

A silence. Then Alistair extended his hand across Dominic's bench, over the scattered parts of the thing that had brought us to this, and I shook it. His grip was dry and strong and cold, like handling a fitting left out overnight.

"To spring, then," he said.

"To spring."

I collected the lamp and left him to his deciding, and it was only halfway down the corridor that I realized what we had done. We had made a pact the way people make pacts in stories, and in stories pacts are always made by people who are each keeping one secret from the other. I had mine — the ledger page I had folded into the back of my Bible, with its payments from a shipping firm whose name I could not find in any register on the mainland. And he had his, whatever it was, sitting behind his eyes like a stone behind ice.

Still. The bridge was gone. The compass waited. Winter had us both, and there is a strange comfort in being trapped with purpose rather than without one.

I climbed to the lamp room that night and lit the flame, and the great lens turned, and the beam went out over the black water saying *here, here, here*. And below me in the workshop a light still burned, and an old man bent over brass, and I thought: we will finish what he started, Dominic, whatever it was you started, and may God forgive us both for what we find.

It was the last easy week we would have for a long time. Within the month, the ledgers would open the whole business like a body on a table — but that night I didn't know it. That night I only knew that the bridge was down, the bargain was struck, and somewhere offshore the next storm was already gathering itself, patient as anything, waiting for its turn.
