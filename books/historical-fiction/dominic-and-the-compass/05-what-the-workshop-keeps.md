## 5. What the Workshop Keeps

*Petra*

My father-in-law knocked on the workshop door the way a magistrate knocks — three precise raps, as though the door owed him an answer. He did not wait for one. He never had, in the four days since the bridge closed and stranded us with each other, and I had stopped expecting him to.

"We will do this properly," Alistair said. "An inventory. Every tool catalogued, every drawer accounted for, so that when the surveyors come in spring there is nothing left to argue about."

"Nothing left to argue about," I repeated, because echoing him had become a kind of sport. He disapproved of sport on principle, which made it sweeter.

He shot me a look over his spectacles — the look he reserved for when I was being flippant about estate matters, which was to say, most mornings before tea. "You may find this difficult to believe, Petra, but order is a kindness. Chaos is merely grief that has been allowed to breed."

"And what has order ever done for you, besides keep you company?"

I regretted it the moment it left my mouth, mostly because it was true and he knew it was true, and there are cruelties that work only as accidents. Alistair's jaw tightened, and for a moment he looked very much like Dominic — same bones, same stubborn architecture of the face — and then he didn't, because Dominic would have laughed at me and Alistair reached instead for the inventory book tucked under his arm.

"The chisels first," he said. "We begin with the chisels."

So we began with the chisels.

The workshop smelled the way it always had: linseed oil and brass filings and the ghost of pipe smoke, though Dominic had been dead fourteen months and the pipes longer than that. I had gone in perhaps six times since the funeral, each visit shorter than the last, each time leaving with some small object I could justify carrying back to the house — a spare wick trimmer, a coil of copper wire, once a single screw I could not account for and still kept in my apron pocket like a relic no one would pray to.

Now I had to stay. That was the cruelty of an inventory: it required you to look at everything, name everything, and decide what everything was worth. Grief by the pound.

Alistair worked down one wall and I worked down the other, calling out items in a flat liturgy. *Half-inch firmer chisel, handle cracked. Dividers, brass, 6 inch. Mallet, beech, head loose.* He had beautiful handwriting for a man who disapproved of sentiment — small, upright, economical letters, the kind that took up no more room than necessary, like himself.

"He sharpened these the week before," Alistair said suddenly. He was holding one of the chisels up to the grey window light, turning it. "I told him a man who keeps his edge tools ready in October is either a craftsman or a fool."

"Which did you decide?"

"Neither. Both." He set the chisel down with exaggerated care, aligning it with its brothers in the rack. "He was going to teach Tomas. That was the whole point of the compass, he said. Not the object. The teaching of it."

I wrote *chisels, set of nine, good condition* in my own hand, which is not beautiful but is legible, and pretended my eyes were not stinging. This was our arrangement, Alistair's and mine: we took turns being the one who did not cry, and neither of us acknowledged the arrangement existed.

The morning went on like that. Drawers of screws sorted by size into paper twists. Auger bits nested in their wooden case like sleeping birds. And everywhere, everywhere, the half-finished evidence of a mind that planned ten projects ahead and expected forty years to finish them in. He got eleven.

It was near noon, I think — the light through the salt-hazed windows had gone from pewter to dull silver — when I opened the bottom drawer of the planing bench and found it did not slide smoothly. It caught, hesitated, and gave with a small scrape, the sound of something underneath that should not have been there.

"Alistair."

"Mm." He did not look up from counting gimlets.

"There's a false bottom in this drawer."

That brought him around. He crossed the room faster than a man his age ought to cross any room, and stood over my shoulder, and for a moment neither of us touched anything. The workshop, which all morning had been a room full of answers — *this is a spokeshave, this is a burnisher, this is what your husband valued, this is what he left* — became abruptly a room full of questions.

"Lift it," Alistair said. His voice had changed. Flatter. Careful.

I lifted the thin board free. Beneath it lay a packet wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, the knot done in that particular doubled hitch Dominic used for everything, the one I had never learned properly because I always had him to untie things. Beneath the packet, flat and square, a ledger bound in blue cloth, its corners worn soft.

"You take the book," Alistair said. "I'll take the packet."

"No." I heard myself say it and was mildly surprised. "Together. Whatever it is, together."

He looked at me then — really looked, the first time in days — and something moved behind his face like weather far out at sea. "Very well," he said. "Together."

* * *

*Alistair*

I knew what the ledger was before she opened it. God help me, I knew, and I let her open it anyway, because stopping her would have required an explanation, and explanations were precisely the cargo I had not come ashore carrying.

I had told myself, on the ferry over and in the sleepless nights since, that I came to oversee the clearing of the workshop. There was truth enough in that to pass inspection. But a man who lies to others must first practice on himself, and I had grown fluent.

Dominic had written to me in September of last year. The letter is burned now; I fed it to the stove myself, page by page, which I have found is the only way to destroy a letter from your own son without reading it again as it dies. What remained of it lived behind my ribs. *Father, I've found something in the firm's accounts. Come in the spring and I'll show you. It touches Grandfather's old commissions. I mean to put it right.* Something in those lines. I have turned the words over so many times the edges have worn smooth, and still I cannot make them mean anything except that he knew, and that knowing killed him, and that I had spent fourteen months hoping the sea did it instead.

And now Petra sat at his bench with his ledger open in front of her, and I watched her read her husband's secret, and I understood that there are worse things than drowning. There is watching someone else read the water level.

"It's accounts," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; I saw the tremor start at the wrist. "Payments. Dates going back years. But it's not his handwriting."

"It's a fair copy," I said, and could have bitten my tongue off. She looked up sharply.

"You know that how?"

"Because I can see it. A fair copy is made from another document, tidier than the original, entered later. Anyone who has kept books knows the difference." This was true, and also a deflection, and she accepted it the way one accepts change from a stranger — without pleasure, but without argument.

She began reading entries aloud, quietly, the way one reads in a sickroom. "Fourteen pounds, tenth of March 1881. Twenty-two pounds, November the same year. Thirty pounds — thirty pounds, Alistair — for 'attendance upon lights.'" She frowned. "What lights? We're the only light within thirty miles, and no one pays us attendance."

"Shipping firms pay for all manner of services. Pilotage. Salvage. Repairs to moorings." My own voice sounded to me like someone else's, reasonable and hollow. "Coastal trade runs on informal payments. Always has. Half the firms on this coast couldn't survive an honest audit and they know it, and everyone knows they know it, and the ships go out and come in regardless."

"This isn't a shipping firm." She had turned to the inside cover, where Dominic had noted the payer in his careful hand, the hand I had taught him myself at a bench very like this one, thirty-odd years ago, when my wife was alive and the world was a place where ledgers held only money. "*Meridian Line Shipping,*" she read. "I've never heard of them. Have you?"

I am not, on the whole, a liar. I am a man who arranges truths, the way one arranges tools in a rack — nothing hidden, merely not all displayed at once. "No," I said, which was true. I had heard of the men behind the name, which was different.

"Look at the dates against the wrecks." She was doing sums in her head, quick as her mother had been, quick as Dominic. "The *Cormorant* went down on the Carrick shoal in March '81. Here's a payment the ninth of March. The *Ellen May*, November '81 — payment the twelfth. The *Providence Girl*—" She stopped. She had gone pale in the particular way of a person who has just discovered that arithmetic can wound.

"Coincidence of the season," I said. "Storms come in clusters. Wrecks come with storms. Payments come after wrecks, because salvage follows wrecking. It's a grim trade but it's a trade, and it doesn't make criminals of—"

"Of whom, Alistair?" Very quiet. "Finish the sentence. It doesn't make criminals of whom?"

Of your husband. Of my son. Of the firm that bears our family's mark in its very name if one knows where to look, Meridian Line, meridian, the line a compass card swings to true north along — I built that connection with my own counsel twenty years ago and thought it clever, and I have hated my cleverness ever since.

"Of anyone," I said. "Bookkeeping is not crime. If it were, half the respectable world would hang."

She was silent a long moment. Then she did the thing I feared most, which was not shouting and not accusation but something quieter and more dangerous: she tore — neatly, along the fold, a woman who has hemmed sheets all her life knows how to tear cloth straight — the last leaf of entries free of the binding, folded it in quarters, and put it in her apron pocket, where I happen to know she carries a spare lamp key and a dead man's single screw.

"That's evidence," I said, too quickly.

"Of what? You've just spent ten minutes telling me it's nothing." She rose, and folded her arms, and looked at me across the bench that had been Dominic's and was now, apparently, a battlefield. "If it's nothing, you won't mind me keeping a page of nothing."

There was no answer to that which did not dig the hole deeper, so I gave none. I am told silence reads as dignity. In this case I suspect it read exactly as what it was.

"We'll continue the inventory tomorrow," she said. "I want the rest of the afternoon with the light."

She gathered the ledger, the packet, the torn page's twin absence in the binding, and went to the door, and paused there with her hand on the frame.

"Alistair. You knew that drawer stuck."

"I suspected the drawer held something. Dominic was secretive toward the end. Fathers notice."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You knew *what* it held. I watched your face when I lifted the board. You weren't surprised. You were braced." She let that sit between us, cold and patient as the sea. "When you're ready to tell me what you were braced for, I'll be in the lamp room. It's the only room in this house where I know what I'm keeping."

The door closed behind her, gently, which from Petra is the loudest thing she does.

I stood alone in my son's workshop among his ordered tools, and I picked up the brown-paper packet and felt through it the shape of what I already knew was inside — papers, correspondence, the commission letter, the whole poisoned inheritance — and I understood that I had perhaps a fortnight, perhaps less, before the woman downstairs decided she deserved the truth more than the dead deserved their privacy.

The trouble with rooms full of questions, I thought, replacing the false bottom with hands that were not quite steady, is that sooner or later someone comes looking for answers. And the trouble with fathers is that we are always, always the likeliest suspects.

Upstairs, faintly, I heard the lamp room hatch open, and the small sounds of Petra beginning the lighting of the light. I wrapped the packet again in its string and its doubled hitch, and I put it in my coat, inside pocket, over the heart — the place where I keep everything I cannot bear to read and cannot bear to burn.

Tomorrow we would inventory the augers. Tonight I would decide how much of September's letter I could confess without destroying the last two people on earth I had any power to protect.

Both decisions, I already knew, would come too late.
