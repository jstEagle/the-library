## 6. Brass and Memory

Alistair Kavanagh taught the way winter teaches: by withholding, by example, and by making you feel foolish for asking twice.

We had struck our bargain three days earlier — finish the compass before the surveyors came, then part forever — and I had imagined, in the way one imagines things at two in the morning, that "finish the compass" meant a week of pleasant puttering with files and polish until the thing assembled itself out of goodwill. What it actually meant was standing in Dominic's workshop at seven in the morning, sleeves pinned to the elbow, while my father-in-law held a dividers up to the light and said, "No," for what I calculated was the eleventh time.

"Your scribe angle is wrong. You're dragging it like you'd drag a plough."

"I'm holding it exactly as you told me."

"You're holding it as I *demonstrated*. Holding and doing are different countries, girl, and you haven't applied for passage."

I bit down on the reply that rose — something about how I had kept a lighthouse alone through two winters while he wrote letters from a warm parlor on the mainland — and instead adjusted my grip. The brass blank sat on the bench between us, a disc the width of a dinner plate, soft as butter compared to steel and stubborn as sin all the same. On it, in faint scribed arcs that were supposed to become compass points but currently resembled a spider's dying argument, lay my first attempts.

Alistair leaned over, examined the mess, and — this was the part that unsettled me more than any scolding — did not sneer.

"Scrape it back," he said. "Brass forgives. That's why we start with brass and not with pride."

* * *

You should understand what the workshop was to me before that week, because what it became afterward is the whole of this story's hinge.

Dominic's workshop had been, for a year, the room I did not enter. Not out of superstition exactly — I am the keeper of a light; superstition drowned somewhere around my second winter here — but out of a plainer economy of grief. There are only so many rooms a heart can walk through in a day, and I had allocated mine to the kitchen where I cooked for my son, the lamp room where I worked for strangers' ships, and the bedroom where I did neither and therefore could afford to feel whatever came. The workshop asked too much. It asked me to see his apron on its hook. It asked me to smell him — oil and shavings and the particular sweetness of hot brass — and to know that the smell was fading year by year into the smell of mere disuse, which is the smell of death wearing a room's clothing.

Now I was in it six hours a day, and the smell was coming back. Alistair lit the stove the first morning the way Dominic must have learned to light it from him — kindling laid in a lattice, never a heap, the flue opened wide before the match — and the warmth woke the resin in the shelving and the oil in the cans, and for one vertiginous moment I was certain Dominic had just stepped out to check the weather and would come back in stamping the wet off his boots.

He didn't come back. But the room, at least, had decided to stay alive, and I found I was glad of it in a way that felt faintly like betrayal and mostly like breathing.

* * *

The compass itself, I should describe, since I had spent a year not looking at it.

Dominic had wrapped it in oilcloth with the care of a man wrapping something he meant to return to soon, and inside the cloth the work lay open like a sentence stopped midway. The case: done, beautiful, a drum of brass with a glass-topped lid whose hinge moved so smoothly it made no sound at all, which struck me as an extravagance — surely a thing could be allowed to announce its own movement? The bowl, gimbaled so it would stay level in any sea: done. The needle: done, and when Alistair lifted it near the lodestone he kept in his coat pocket — he carried a lodestone in his coat pocket the way other men carry tobacco, a fact that told me more about his childhood than any story would have — the needle swung and settled and pointed home with a decisiveness I envied.

What remained was the card: the graduated disc beneath the needle, divided into thirty-two points, each one named, each one engraved. Thirty-two points. Four hundred and some individual letters and degree marks, each cut by hand, each wrong if it was wrong by the width of a hair, because a compass that lies even slightly will lie catastrophically eventually, at night, in weather, with rocks underneath.

"A man's life sits under that needle," Alistair said, the first morning, tapping the blank. "Some stranger we'll never meet, in a sea he can't see the bottom of. He'll believe whatever this card tells him. So." He looked at me over the top of it, and his eyes, which are Dominic's eyes gone grey as old rope, held no sentiment whatsoever. "We do not do it quickly. We do it rightly."

"And if I do it wrongly?"

"Then we scrape it back and begin again, however many times it takes. The brass forgives. The sea doesn't."

* * *

So I learned.

I learned to sharpen the graver on an Arkansas stone, circling, circling, checking the edge against my thumbnail until it just caught. I learned to hold it upright against the T-rest of his little engraving pad — he had brought Dominic's pad from the mainland in his luggage, wrapped in the same shirt he'd worn on the ferry, which I chose not to think about — and to push with the whole arm and not the wrist, because the wrist trembles and the arm remembers. I learned that brass cuts brightest in the middle of a stroke and goes dull at the ends unless you ease off like a boat easing into a berth.

And I learned Alistair, which was harder.

My father-in-law had come ashore cold as a ballast stone, all formalities and freight, claiming rights over a dead son's tools. For three days we had circled each other through the house with the elaborate courtesy of two nations negotiating while their fleets sail. He called me *Mrs. Kavanagh*, which was absurd, since I had been born a Murchie and married into the name and could hardly be my own mother-in-law; I called him *Mr. Kavanagh*, which was equally absurd, and we both persisted, and there was a kind of honor in the persistence, the way there is honor in not being the first to lower a sail in rising wind.

But hands busy with the same work cannot keep up full ceremony. By the second day he was saying "left hand higher" instead of "if you please, Mrs. Kavanagh, your left hand." By the third he said "girl" again, which I had decided to take as promotion rather than insult, since he said it in exactly the tone Dominic used when correcting my knots — exasperated, exacting, and underneath both, a current of something warmer that neither of us would have survived naming aloud.

He was a better teacher than I expected and a worse-tempered one than I feared, and both surprises rearranged themselves in me daily. He never once said *Dominic could do this at fourteen.* I had braced myself for it the way you brace for a wave you know is coming, and it never came, and I understood eventually that this restraint cost him something, and that his paying it was his version of tenderness.

* * *

On the fourth afternoon, working the east points of the card — ENE, NE, NNE, the letters getting smaller and the divisions finer as they crowded toward north — my mind went where minds go when hands are occupied and hearts are unguarded.

Tomas was six. It was April, the light lasting longer each evening, and Dominic had him in the workshop making — nominally — a boat. In practice, Tomas was making a boat-shaped collection of enthusiasm, and Dominic was guiding it with the patience of a saint who had specifically trained for this miracle.

"Hold it like a sparrow," Dominic said. "Firm enough it doesn't fly off. Gentle enough nothing breaks."

"That's a chisel, Papa. Sparrows don't hold chisels."

"All the good ones do."

And Tomas laughed the way he laughs — still laughs, thank God, though the laugh has deepened a register since — and gripped the chisel with his whole small fist, white-knuckled, and pushed, and the chisel skidded, and instead of scolding Dominic put his own hand over his son's and said, "There. Now it knows you're serious."

*I'm serious,* six-year-old Tomas announced to the workshop, to the boat, to his father, to the world entire, and cut a groove in the pine that ruined the hull and delighted everyone, and Dominic swept him up and set him on his shoulder and said the boat had died a hero's death and would be buried at sea with full honors, and the burial took place in the rain barrel with a eulogy I heard through the window while I trimmed the lamp wicks, and I remember standing there in the lamp room with the scissors in my hand thinking: *this. This is the richest I will ever be.*

I came back to the workshop with wet eyes and a dry face — a keeper's trick, crying without the inconvenience of tears, since salt air and lamp optics punish moisture — and found Alistair watching me.

"What," I said.

"Your ENE is the finest stroke you've cut yet," he said. "Whatever you were thinking about, think it every time."

* * *

That evening he stayed at the bench after supper. I had expected him to retreat to the parlor with his book as he did, punctual as tide tables, but instead he turned the lamp low and drew the needle assembly toward him and began, without preamble, to show me how the pivot seated, how the needle balanced true only if the card's weight was distributed evenly, how a compass out of balance lies not by much — a degree, perhaps two — but lies always in the same direction, and a man steering by it will trust it precisely because it fails consistently.

"Dominic balanced this already," I said.

"He did." Alistair tilted the little assembly so the lamplight ran along the needle. "Better than I could have. And I taught him." He set it down with a click that seemed, in the quiet, very loud. "There's a thing people don't grasp about teaching a trade. You spend years putting yourself into another person's hands — literally into their hands, correcting their grip, moving their fingers. And then one day their hands are better than yours. And you discover you're glad. Nobody warns you how strange glad feels."

I wiped the card clean of filings and did not say anything, because he was not really talking to me, or rather he was talking to me only in the sense that I was the person in the room, and rooms require someone.

"He'd have finished this card in a fortnight," Alistair went on. "His letters were full of it. *The card, Father, wait till you see the card.*" His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Every letter, the card. Never once said what it was for. I assumed a client. Merchantmen commission them for their best helmsmen. A gift piece. I assumed—"

He stopped.

"Assumed?" I prompted, because the silence had a shape, and the shape had edges, and I wanted to know what was inside it.

"Assumed it was simple," he said, after a moment, in a voice carefully emptied of everything. "It isn't simple. Nothing commissioned at that price is simple." And then, briskly, before I could follow the thread: "Your NNE is drifting half a point. Scrape it back."

* * *

I scraped it back. I cut it again. It came out true.

But I lay awake that night turning his sentence over like a coin I suspected of being clipped. *Nothing commissioned at that price is simple.* Commissioned. Who commissions a compass from a lighthouse keeper's husband on a headland at the end of the world? What client pays enough that the price itself becomes a warning?

And why — this was the question that sat up with me past two, staring at the ceiling — why had Alistair truly come? He claimed duty: oversee the workshop, protect Dominic's tools from the surveyors' carts. But he had brought Dominic's engraving pad in his luggage, wrapped against harm like a relic. Men do not pack relics for a house clearance. Men pack relics for a vigil.

I thought of the ledgers we had found, the payments from the shipping firm whose name meant nothing to me, the page I had folded into my apron pocket and had not yet shown him that I kept. I thought of how quickly he had said *it's nothing* when I'd asked — too quickly, the way a helmsman answers too quickly when you ask whether he's seen the rock.

No. That was uncharitable. That was exhaustion and grief talking, and an old man mourning his son badly at close quarters, in a house too small for two kinds of silence.

I fell asleep telling myself that, and nearly believed it, and dreamed anyway of needles swinging, settling, pointing — always, in the dream, at something below the horizon that none of us had thought to look for.

In the morning, Alistair was at the bench early, and there was a letter in his coat pocket that he kept touching, the way you touch a bruise to learn whether it still hurts.

It did. I could see it across the room.
