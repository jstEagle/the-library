## 7. The Wrecker's Light

I have never slept well in other people's houses, and my son's house — my son's *lighthouse*, if I am honest, though the deed said otherwise — was no exception. The wind found every seam in the stonework and played them like a fiddle badly tuned, and beneath that there was the sea itself, working the rocks below with the patience of a debt collector.

So I lay awake in the narrow guest room and listened, and did what old men do when they cannot sleep: I lied to myself. I told myself I had come ashore to oversee the workshop. To catalogue Dominic's tools properly, as a craftsman's estate deserves. To spare Petra the indignity of strangers' hands on his things before the surveyors came at thaw.

It was a serviceable lie. I had used it on the ferryman, on myself on the crossing, and now, hourly, on the widow downstairs, who accepted it with the flat courtesy of a woman who has decided not to look too closely at a gift horse because she suspects its teeth.

The truth was simpler and uglier: I had come to bury something.

* * *

In the winter of 1861, I was twenty-six and already half-deaf from the hammer, working brass in my father's shop in Wicklow, when a man named Erasmus Quill came in with an order that paid three times what it should have.

I remember thinking, even then, *no one pays honest money for dishonest hours.* But I was young, and my father was ill, and Quill had the manner of a man doing you a favour by letting you take his coin.

He wanted lantern housings. Not lamps — housings. Iron-framed, glass-fronted cases built to hold a common ship's lantern, but with one peculiarity: the back panel was hinged, and the front glass was of a peculiar red-amber tint he supplied himself. He wanted them heavy enough to be carried up a cliff path by one man, bright enough to be seen from four miles out in bad weather, and — this was the detail I should have understood forty years sooner — arranged so the light could be shown or shuttered in an instant.

"Coastal signalling," Quill said. "Fishing fleets. Very hush-hush, very profitable for all concerned." He smiled with teeth like a picket fence. "You'll not speak of the order."

I did not speak of the order. I made eleven of them over two winters, each one finer than the last, because shame and craftsmanship are not mutually exclusive; indeed, in my experience, they frequently share a bench.

What were they for? You will have guessed already, and you will hate yourself for guessing, as I do. A ship coming home in fog or foul weather looks for a light. A lighthouse says *here are the rocks, keep off.* But a light on a cliff that appears where no charted light should be — that says *here is harbour, here is safety, come in toward the light.* And a master exhausted, half-blind with salt spray, trusting a warm amber gleam where his heart tells him shelter lies—

The rocks off that headland did not need my help killing men. They merely needed the help of a light shown in exactly the wrong place, at exactly the right time. The firm — Hollis & Vane, Shipping and Salvage, though the salvage always seemed to precede the shipping — paid Quill, and Quill paid me, and eleven houses went out into the dark carrying eleven lies.

Three wrecks that I know of. The *Cormorant* in '62, with six drowned. The *Marguerite* in '64, nine souls, among them a woman whose body they brought up two tides later still holding a child's shoe. And the *Providence* in '67 — oh, the name alone should have been warning enough — which broke apart within sight of the false light while her crew signalled frantically toward what they believed was rescue.

I stopped taking Quill's money after the *Marguerite*. That is the sentence I have polished and carried and set on the mantelpiece of my conscience for thirty years: *I stopped after the second wreck.* It is true. It is also arranged, as carefully as any light in a housing I ever built, to show only the part of the room you're meant to see. I did not stop sooner. I did not go to the magistrates. I made five more housings after the *Marguerite* before I found the courage — no, call it what it was, the *disgust* — to quit, and I kept every shilling of Quill's wages, and I built a respectable life on the proceeds, and raised a son in a house bought partly with the drowning of strangers.

When Dominic was born, I looked at his small fists and swore he would never know. That is the whole of my fatherhood in one gesture, perhaps: a man standing between his child and the truth, and calling it protection.

* * *

Petra knocked at my door on the third morning, a tray in her hands, because the woman could not so much as suffer my presence without feeding it.

"There's tea," she said. "And porridge, though I've been told you consider porridge an insult."

"Who told you that?"

"You did. Tuesday. At breakfast. You said, and I quote, 'In my day we fed this to horses and called the rest of us lucky.'"

"I stand by it. Porridge is a punishment oats devised for mankind."

The ghost of a smile crossed her and was gone, which is how smiles travelled through that house in those weeks — briefly, and never stopping long enough to be caught. She set the tray down and did not leave. She stood at the window looking out at the grey water, arms folded, and I watched her and thought, not for the first time since coming ashore, that my son had married better than he knew, and worse than she deserved.

"The supply boat didn't run yesterday," she said. "Sea's building. Tam McAllister flagged the crossing from the mainland side. It'll be weeks, likely, before anyone comes or goes."

"Then we'd best make the workshop efficient," I said. "No sense wasting trapped time."

"Mm," said Petra, in the tone of a woman filing something away. She turned from the window. "Alistair. Why does a man who hates porridge and ferries and, I suspect, me, volunteer to spend a winter cataloguing screwdrivers?"

"Because screwdrivers don't talk back."

She let that sit, and left, and I sat with my tea going cold and understood that my serviceable lie had developed a leak. Widows notice leaks. They live beside the sea; it is a professional qualification.

* * *

Here is what I actually intended, those first days in the workshop, while Petra measured the compass card and I made a great show of inventorying chisels: I intended to find the ledger pages and burn them.

Dominic had found things. I did not know how much, or how thoroughly, until I stood in his workshop surrounded by his tools and understood, with a slow cold settling, that a man who works brass as finely as Dominic worked it does not leave a mystery alone once he has put his calipers to it.

He had come to visit me in the spring of '92 — his last visit, God forgive me — and he had stayed three days, and somewhere in the second evening he had asked, casually, over whisky, whether I had ever done work for a firm called Hollis & Vane.

I am told my face went very still. My face has been going very still at inconvenient moments since before Dominic was born; it is the Kavanagh inheritance, along with weak eyes and strong opinions.

"Lantern housings," I said. "Long ago. Coastal signalling work."

"Coastal signalling," Dominic repeated, and turned his glass, and did not press. That was his way. He was a patient craftsman, my son. He believed every joint would tell you its secret if you held it to the light long enough.

He had been working, that season, on a commission. He told me about it with something like pride: a fine compass, marine grade, commissioned through an intermediary, generous terms. And a strange clause — the client wanted the case unusually deep, with a sealed inner compartment, and wanted the maker to deliver it personally to a named party on the mainland, no intermediaries.

"A deep case," I said. "With a sealed compartment."

"For the gimbal arrangement," Dominic said.

I nodded. There is a legitimate gimbal arrangement that wants depth. There is also a compartment deep enough for papers, or gold, or a confession. When you have spent your youth building lying lights, you develop an eye for furniture that hides things.

That night I barely slept, and in the morning I did the cowardly thing I have done at every crossroads of my life: I said nothing. I shook my son's hand at the pier, told him to mind the child and the wife, and watched his boat diminish, and felt — I swear this to whatever is listening — a presentiment like a hand laid flat on the back of my neck.

Eight months later the sea sent him home to me as driftwood.

* * *

Or did it? I want to be careful here, because this is the reef I have been wrecking myself on since the telegram came. The sea took Dominic. That is what the inquiry concluded, what the village believed, what Petra believed, what I chose to believe because the alternative required believing that men I had taken money from, decades ago, might still be in the business of arranging drownings — and that they might have learned, from my son's questions or his commission or both, precisely what he knew.

Quill was dead; I had danced, privately and disgracefully, on that grave years ago. But Quill had principals. Firms do not die when their agents do. Hollis & Vane became Hollis & Sons became, I gathered from shipping notices, simply *the Firm* among people who spoke of it in lowered voices. Wrecks still happened along that coast at a rate slightly exceeding statistical manners. Salvage firms still profited. False lights were an old trade, and old trades do not die; they retire, rebrand, and hire younger men.

And my son — my careful, patient, joint-inspecting son — had asked me about them eight months before he drowned.

So: I came ashore under cover of duty. I told myself, on the crossing, that I would find whatever Dominic had found — notes, ledger copies, correspondence — and I would destroy it, and thereby protect Petra and Tomas from the attention of men who solved problems with cut fittings and convenient storms.

I told myself this was love. Some nights, in that guest room with the wind fiddling in the stones, I could almost hold the shape of it. Most nights it slipped through my fingers like the thing it was: guilt, wearing duty's coat, hoping to pass inspection.

* * *

The trouble with burying a thing in a dead man's workshop is that the dead man's widow lives ten steps away, is neither stupid nor blind, and grieves in an investigative manner.

On the fifth day I found her at Dominic's desk with the ledger open — the ledger I had already searched twice and failed to find, because Dominic, with a craftsman's slyness I can only admire posthumously, had shelved it spine-inward behind a row of pattern books. She had a page turned down and her finger on a column of figures, and she looked up at me with an expression I recognized, horribly, because it was the one I wore in mirrors.

"This firm," she said. "Hollis & Vane. Do you know them?"

The name struck me the way a wave strikes a man turned sideways — not head-on, but taking the legs out from under everything. I heard myself say, "Shipping outfit. Before your time. They folded."

"They didn't fold. They're paying Dominic. Here—" she turned the ledger toward me, and her finger was steady, steadier than mine would have been, "—and here, and here. Regular payments, over a year. Alistair, why would a shipping firm pay a compass-maker monthly, like a wage?"

"Retainers happen. Instruments need servicing."

"Instruments he never delivered," she said quietly. "There's no delivery record. No completion entry. Just payments, month after month, and then—" her finger moved to the final line, dated eleven days before his boat went out, "—this. Twice the usual amount."

The wind chose that moment to lean on the tower, and the whole house groaned, and I used the pause like a curtain.

"Business settlements run ahead of deaths sometimes," I said. "Grief makes patterns, Petra. Grief is a pattern-seeking animal. It sees conspiracy in arithmetic."

"Does it," said Petra, and tore the page neatly out of the ledger, and folded it, and put it in her apron pocket, and looked at me with eyes the colour of the January sea. "Then grief and I will get along fine. I've arithmetic enough here to count on."

She left me alone with the tools of my son and the wreckage of my purpose, and I stood a long while among the chisels and the files, in the smell of oil and brass shavings that is the only church I have ever truly entered, and I admitted it at last, in the silence where no light could dress it up:

I had not come to clear the workshop. I had come to bury the evidence. And I was too late, because Dominic had already buried it — carefully, spine-inward, in his own way — and his widow had already dug it up, and she was holding a torn page of my oldest sin in her apron pocket like a lit lamp she hadn't yet decided where to shine.

* * *

We struck our bargain that evening — finish the compass before the surveyors come, then part forever — and she shook my hand across the kitchen table like a woman closing a trade, and I went up to bed almost relieved, because a bargain is a container, and containers feel like safety to men who have spent their lives trying to keep things shut.

I should have known better. I built enough housings in my day to know that anything with a hinge will eventually be opened by someone.

I lay listening to the wind rise, and thought of the boy asleep down the hall. Tomas. Ten years old, with Dominic's patience and, God help him, Dominic's habit of asking quiet questions and then going away to answer them himself with a chisel and a lamp and no supervision whatsoever.

I had spent three days keeping Petra from the ledgers, and I had counted it a success that she had only the one page.

It occurred to me, somewhere past midnight, as the storm found its full voice against the tower, that I had not given one moment's thought to where a fatherless boy goes looking for his father — or to the fact that a child who finds a ledger full of a dead man's secrets does not fold one page neatly into an apron pocket.

He reads all of it. And then he asks.
