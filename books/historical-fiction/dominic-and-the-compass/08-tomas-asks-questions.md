## 8. Tomas Asks Questions

The boy found the ledger on a Tuesday, because boys of ten have an unerring instinct for the one drawer in a house that matters. I have since decided that children are less like children than like compass needles — they swing, they settle, and they point, without any instruction from us, at whatever truth we are most anxious to hide.

I was in the kitchen when it happened, up to my wrists in bread dough, which is how nearly all catastrophes at this light find me: flour-dusted and entirely unprepared.

He came into the kitchen carrying the ledger the way he carried everything now that he was ten — carefully casual, as though he had not been waiting days for an excuse to hold it. The book looked absurdly large under his arm, all its corners soft with salt damp, its spine cracked where Dominic used to lay it open across his knee.

"Mama," Tomas said. "What's a wrecker?"

I want to tell you that my hands kept working. They did not. There is a particular quality to the silence after your child says a word you hoped he'd never learn, and in that silence I heard the dough sigh, the stove tick, the sea going about its business against the rocks below as if none of it were any concern of mine.

"Where did you hear that word?" I asked, which is what mothers say when they need three more seconds to decide how to lie.

"It's written in here." He set the ledger on the scrubbed table, open — of course open; he would have opened it before he'd so much as wiped his boots. "Mr. Harrow Shipping Firm. Payment rendered. Payment received." He read it out in the flattened, careful voice he used for scripture, and then he looked up at me with Dominic's grey eyes, which I have never once appreciated, because they see too much. "And there's a word next to the amounts. Wrecker light, 1881. Wrecker light, 1884. What did Papa do, Mama?"

*What did Papa do.* Four words, and every one of them a small knife.

Here is what I knew by then, and what I had told no one: I knew the payments ran through eleven years of ledgers. I knew Alistair had gone grey around the mouth when I showed him the page, and had said *it's nothing, girl, shipping firms pay odd invoices all the time,* in the voice of a man rehearsing. I knew I had torn the worst leaf out and folded it into my bodice like a woman hiding a letter from a lover, except what I was hiding was worse — it was doubt. And I knew that my son deserved better than what I was about to give him.

But knowing a thing and being equal to it are two different countries, and there was no bridge between them that morning either.

"A wrecker," I said, wiping my hands on my apron, buying another second, "was a man who — well. In the old days, along some coasts, there were men who put false lights up. To make ships think the harbour was somewhere it wasn't."

Tomas absorbed this the way he absorbs everything: completely, and then sideways. "They wanted the ships to crash."

"Yes."

"So the ships would break and they could take things off them."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly. Then his finger came down on the ledger line, and my clever, terrible boy said, "So why is Papa's name in a book full of money from men like that?"

And here is the moment I will answer for. Not at the magistrate's table in spring, not before God if such an office exists, but here, in my own accounting, where it counts. My son asked me a true question, and I looked at his face — at the face that is half mine and half a drowned man's — and I chose the gentler lie over the hard truth, the way you choose a shawl over a coat because the coat means admitting winter has come.

"He wasn't," I said. "Taking money. Your father was — " and God help me, the invention came quickly, fluently, the way lies do when they are needed. "A man who kept accounts can be made to keep accounts for anyone, Tom. A firm pays a workshop to do work, and the work gets written down. It doesn't mean your father knew what the work was *for*. He made instruments. Instruments go places. He couldn't follow them."

It was a good lie. That is the shameful part — it was a *good* lie. It had the shape of plausibility, it protected the dead, and it required nothing of my son but to go on loving his father, which is the least I could think of to ask of anyone that morning.

Tomas considered it. I watched him weigh it, watched the needle swing.

"But why would Mr. Harrow's firm keep paying," he said, "if Papa didn't know?"

Because children do not accept the first answer the way adults do. Adults take the first answer because we have learned the price of the second. Ten-year-olds are still rich enough to ask.

"Papa was the best instrument-maker on this coast," I said. "Men put up with a great deal for the best."

"You always say Papa never did anything he didn't understand all the way through."

"I know what I say."

"You say he was honest."

"He was." Too fast. I heard it come out too fast, the way a wave comes in higher than the last, and I saw my son hear it too.

I could stop even now, I told myself. I could sit down at the table, fold my floured hands, and say, *Tom, sit down, there are things in this book I don't understand yet either, and we're going to understand them together, because that's what your father would have wanted — not protection, understanding.* I could have done it. I have replayed that doorway a hundred times since, and in the better versions I always walk through it.

In the actual version, I took the ledger off the table and closed it.

"This is grown-up business," I said. "Estate business. Papers and solicitors and your grandfather fussing. It has nothing to do with your father's heart, do you hear me? Nothing. Your father loved this coast and everyone on it, and he kept this light with me for nine years, and if the only thing you ever learn about him is that, you'll have learned enough."

The words came out with such force that Tomas stepped back half an inch. I had not meant to shout. I had shouted at my son for asking about his own father, in our own kitchen, over a book that had every right to be on our table — and the moment the last word landed I felt the lie go into me like a swallowed hook. Not painful yet. Just set. Just there, barb-down, waiting for someone to pull the line.

"Am I in trouble?" Tomas asked quietly.

"No." I came around the table and knelt, which put my face level with his and my shame at exactly eye height. "No, my love. You're not in trouble. You asked a fair question and I gave you a poor answer, and that's my failing, not yours."

He put his arms around my neck. He smelled of woodsmoke and the workshop, because Alistair had let him sweep shavings that morning, and I held him and felt the hook set deeper with every heartbeat.

"Did Papa ever lie to you?" he asked, into my shoulder.

Children should come with warning bells. Where had he even got such a question? From me — from watching my face, that's where; ten-year-olds read faces the way sailors read weather, and mine had been giving away squalls all morning.

"Not to me," I said, which was technically true and morally bankrupt, because the ledger suggested my husband had lied to everyone else, including possibly the both of us, for eleven years.

* * *

Afterward I sent him out to check the gull stakes — a nonsense errand, invented on the spot, accepted with the terrible politeness of a child who knows he is being managed — and I stood alone in the kitchen with my hands still faintly floury and understood that something had changed shape in the house.

Grief, I had learned this year, is mostly a matter of housekeeping. You fold it, you shelve it, you dust around it. I had become expert. I could pass Dominic's coat on its peg ten times a day without touching it. But a lie is not grief. A lie does not stay folded. A lie is a living thing you've let into the house, and it walks around at night and rearranges the furniture, and by morning nothing is where you left it and you can't remember what the room originally looked like.

By noon I couldn't remember what I'd told Tomas about his father before this. Which stories were true? Which had I embellished? *Your papa was the steadiest hand on this coast* — true. *Your papa feared nothing but shallow water* — was that true? Was it true before Tuesday? Would it survive the ledgers?

That afternoon I carried the torn leaf out of my bodice, where it had spent two days warming against my ribs like a second heart, and I read it again in the workshop while Alistair filed brass across the room and pretended not to watch me.

Harrow Shipping Firm. Eleven entries. The last dated six weeks before Dominic drowned.

"He asked me what a wrecker was," I said finally.

Alistair's file stopped. "Who asked."

"Tomas. He found the ledger in the bench drawer. He read it, Alistair — front to back, or near enough. He asked me why his father took money from wreckers."

The old man set the file down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when your hands want to do otherwise. "And what did you tell him?"

"That his father didn't know."

The silence that followed had texture. Wind found the chimney and hummed down it.

"That may yet prove to be true," Alistair said.

"Don't." I laid the leaf on the bench between us. "Don't build me a little bridge of maybe. I've just crossed one and it dropped me in the water. Either you believe what you're saying or you don't, and I watched your face when you said it, and your face doesn't believe it."

"My face is seventy-one years old," he said, "and entitled to its opinions." But he wouldn't look at the ledger leaf, and he picked the file back up, and the scratch of brass resumed too quickly, and I understood that I had just watched a man decline a door that was open to him.

I should note what else I understood, standing there among Dominic's tools with the wind getting under my skin: that I had defended my husband harder to my son than I had ever defended him to myself. That love, unexamined, curdles into something like perjury. And that I had lied — truly lied, knowingly, to the one person on this earth I'd sworn never to lie to — not for Tomas's sake. For Dominic's. For a memory. For a dead man's good name, which he might not even have deserved, while my living boy stood in front of me deserving everything.

The lie cost me something immediately, as I say. Here is the itemized bill: it cost me the easy way Tomas used to look at me, which lasted four more minutes before he started looking *carefully*, the way you look at a light you suspect flickers. It cost me my own reflection in the kitchen window that evening. And it cost me this — I could no longer claim ignorance. Whatever the ledgers meant, whatever Dominic had done or been made party to, I now had a stake in the answer that was not clean. I had invested in the gentle version. And a woman who has invested in a story will defend it past all evidence, unless she is very careful, or very frightened.

I intended to be very careful.

The sea had other plans. At dusk Alistair came in from the gallery shaking spray off his collar and stood a long moment at the window, looking north, where the horizon had gone the colour of a bruise and the swell was running longer than it had any business running in November.

"Barometer's falling fast," he said. "Faster than I like."

"Storms fall fast this time of year."

"Not like this one." He turned from the window, and for a moment — I am certain of it — he looked at me the way a man looks at someone he owes an apology to. "Petra. Before this weather breaks, there's something you'll need to hear from me. Something about Dominic, and why he — "

The lamp above us dimmed, steadied, dimmed again. Somewhere out in the dark, the first long swell of the great storm struck the headland with a sound like a door closing.

"— what," he finished, and did not say anything else at all.
